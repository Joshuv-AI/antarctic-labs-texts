Crystallization in Poly(Ethylene Oxide) Thin Films: Roles of Shear, Confinement, and Homoepitaxy
Authors: Cong Liu, Sinan Feng, Shuntaro Uenuma, Atsushi Takahara, Kohzo Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLammelar orientation during crystallization in poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) thin films is governed by three competing mechanisms, which we elucidate using pseudo‐polyrotaxane nanosheets (PPRNSs) and their end‐capped analogues (cPPRNSs) as model fillers. Shear forces during processing favor edge‐on lamellae, while confinement at high filler loadings promotes flat‐on orientation. PPRNSs introduce a third mechanism‐homoepitaxial templating‐that directs flat‐on crystallization through specific matrix‐filler interactions absent in cPPRNSs. Systematic orientation analysis before and after thermal recrystallization reveals distinct switching behaviors. cPPRNS composites exhibit edge‐on lamellae initially, which transition to mixed or flat‐on orientations upon recrystallization depending on filler concentration. In contrast, PPRNSs impose flat‐on orientation directly during processing at sufficiently high filler loading and maintain robust flat‐on orientation after thermal recrystallization across the investigated concentration range. These findings establish predictive guidelines for tuning lamellar orientation in ultrathin polymer films, with implications for flexible electronics, optical devices, and barrier materials.Keywords: crystal orientation, crystallization, poly(ethylene oxide), pseudo‐polyrotaxane, thin films

Introduction

Controlling polymer crystallization orientation in thin films represents a fundamental challenge with critical implications for flexible electronics, optical devices, and barrier materials [1, 2, 3]. Specifically, edge‐on orientations enable efficient in‐plane charge transport for flexible electronics [4]; flat‐on lamellar arrangements increase gas diffusion tortuosity to enhance barrier performance [5]; and for optical devices, crystal orientation serves as the primary control parameter for tuning birefringence and optical anisotropy [6]. The confined geometry of films thinner than 200 nm creates a complex competition between processing‐induced forces, geometric constraints, and surface interactions that ultimately determines crystal orientation [7, 8, 9]. While conventional approaches including epitaxial crystallization [10, 11] and stress‐induced orientation [12] have achieved limited success, the development of predictive frameworks for orientation control remains elusive due to the complex interplay of competing mechanisms.

The physics of crystallization in thin films differs fundamentally from bulk behavior [13, 14]. In these quasi‐two‐dimensional environments, conventional thermodynamic driving forces are often overwhelmed by processing‐induced effects. For example, spin coating—a ubiquitous thin film preparation method—introduces significant shear forces during film formation that can override intrinsic crystallization preferences [15]. Understanding and controlling this balance between kinetic and thermodynamic factors represents a key challenge for the field.

Poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) serves as an ideal model system for mechanistic studies due to its well‐characterized crystallization behavior and broad technological relevance. In solid electrolyte applications, crystal orientation directly impacts ionic conductivity, as Li+ transport occurs through amorphous regions and oriented structures enhance ion mobility [16, 17, 18]. For gas barrier films, orientation controls permeability by determining the tortuosity of diffusion paths, since crystalline domains act as impermeable obstacles to gas transport [19, 20]. In biomedical applications, crystallization behavior influences the mechanical properties critical for drug delivery and tissue engineering [21, 22, 23]. However, achieving reproducible orientation control in PEO thin films remains challenging due to the delicate balance of forces under confinement.

Two‐dimensional nanofillers have emerged as promising candidates for crystallization control, with studies on graphene [24] and clay nanosheets [25, 26] demonstrating orientation effects at high loadings. The fundamental limitation lies in their reliance solely on physical confinement without specific surface interactions that can direct crystallization. Recent advances in polymer brush‐grafted nanoparticles have shown enhanced crystallization control through surface‐mediated nucleation effects [27, 28]. However, the extension of these concepts to two‐dimensional systems and their integration into comprehensive mechanistic frameworks for thin film crystallization remains unexplored.

We recently demonstrated that pseudo‐polyrotaxane nanosheets (PPRNSs) can control PEO crystallization in thick films through combined effects [29]. PPRNSs are formed through the self‐assembly of β‐cyclodextrin (β‐CD) with PEO76‐PPO29‐PEO76 triblock copolymers, where β‐CD selectively encapsulates the poly(propylene oxide) (PPO) segments to form crystalline sheets, leaving PEO chains as surface‐grafted brushes [30, 31, 32]. This unique architecture provides both two‐dimensional confinement and specific homoepitaxy interactions that facilitate flat‐on PEO crystallization. However, a systematic investigation is required because the physics governing thin films differs substantially from that in thick films, particularly the kinetic dominance of processing‐induced shear forces over thermodynamic preferences, may fundamentally alter how these PPRNS mechanisms translate under strong confinement.

Herein, we establish a three‐mechanism framework for predicting polymer crystallization orientation in thin films. We achieve this through systematic comparison of PPRNSs (Figure 1a), which feature tethered PEO brushes providing homoepitaxial functionality, and capped PPRNSs (cPPRNSs) with end‐capped PEO brushes that mimic conventional two‐dimensional fillers. Our experimental approach employs a two‐step processing protocol: spin coating to form thin films followed by melt recrystallization to reveal thermodynamic preferences (Figure 1b). The three competing mechanisms (Figure 1c) are: (1) shear forces from spin coating processing, which promote edge‐on orientation through chain alignment parallel to the substrate; (2) geometric confinement effects from two‐dimensional nanosheets, which favor flat‐on orientation when present at sufficient density; and (3) homoepitaxial effects from surface polymer brushes, which provide specific nucleation sites for flat‐on crystallization. Using Grazing Incidence Wide‐Angle X‐ray Scattering (GIWAXS) and Polarized Modulation‐Infrared Reflection Absorption Spectroscopy (PM‐IRRAS) to analyze PEO lamellar geometry and molecular orientation both after spin coating and after melt recrystallization, we quantify the individual contributions and competitive interactions of these mechanisms. This framework provides predictive capabilities for achieving precise orientation control across diverse thin film applications and offers fundamental insights into the interplay between polymer dynamics, processing forces, and interfacial interactions in confined systems.

Results and Discussion

Morphological Characterization of Isolated Nanosheet Fillers

Figure 2a demonstrates that both PPRNS and cPPRNS exhibit similar rhombus‐shaped platelet structures with lateral dimensions of approximately 2 µm, as confirmed by SEM. The discrete rhombic geometry is preserved in both cases, indicating that the chemical modification to endcap the PEO brushes maintains the overall nanosheet architecture. This morphological similarity is critical for comparative studies, as it ensures that any differences in crystallization behavior stem from surface chemistry rather than variations in filler geometry.

Morphological Characterization of Thin Films

The resultant composite form macroscopically continuous semicrystalline thin films fabricated by spin coating, with thicknesses ranging from 150 to 250 nm. The GIWAXS profiles in Figure 2b confirm that the composite fabrication process preserves both the PEO matrix crystallinity and the nanosheet structure. The PEO/PPRNS and PEO/cPPRNS composites both display characteristic PEO crystalline reflections at (120) and (032), along with additional peaks at (024) and (224), demonstrating successful crystallization of the polymer matrix. Critically, the dotted reference patterns at the bottom show that the characteristic β‐CD diffraction peaks from both PPRNS and cPPRNS fillers—including reflections at (200), (002), (040), (022¯), (132¯), (240), (330), (241), (060), (260), and (170)—remain detectable in the composite films [33]. This coexistence of PEO and β‐CD crystalline signatures confirms that the spin coating and thermal processing steps do not destroy the nanosheet structure, ensuring that both filler types can exert their respective confinement and homoepitaxial effects on PEO crystallization orientation.

The β‐CD (001) and (002) reflections (labeled in Figures 3 and 5) are concentrated at χ ≈ 0° in all composite films, confirming that both PPRNS and cPPRNS nanosheet platelets lie parallel to the substrate surface. This orientation remains unchanged after melt recrystallization, validating the premise that cPPRNS exerts consistent geometric confinement and PPRNS exerts consistent confinement and homoepitaxial effects throughout all processing steps.

GIWAXS analysis provides quantitative assessment of crystalline orientation through examination of characteristic diffraction peaks and their azimuthal distribution. The PEO crystal structure exhibits prominent reflection peaks at q = 13.53 and 16.51 nm−1, corresponding to the (120) and (032) crystallographic planes, respectively [34]. Edge‐on orientation is characterized by maxima of the (120) and (032) reflections at azimuthal angles (χ) of 0° and 28°, respectively (theoretical value: 23°, based on the published PEO crystal structure [34]), indicating c‐axis (chain direction) parallel to the surface [35]. Conversely, flat‐on orientation displays maxima of (120) and (032) reflections at χ = 90° and 67°, respectively, with polymer chain axes oriented perpendicular to the surface. Analysis of the azimuthal integration profiles of these characteristic reflections provides quantitative measures of orientation preference, with sharp, well‐defined peaks at specific angles indicating strong preferential orientation and broad, flat distributions suggesting random or mixed orientations.

Kinetic Dominance: Shear Effects in As‐Spun cPPRNS Films

The As‐Spun GIWAXS patterns (Figure 3a–c) reveal that shear forces dominate across all concentrations (1‐12 wt.%), producing consistent edge‐on orientation, as (120) and (032) reflections appear at χ = 0° and 28° (Figure 4), respectively. This behavior confirms that processing‐induced alignment overwhelms any geometric constraints during high‐speed spin coating, even at substantial filler concentrations. Notably, pure PEO films do not exhibit strong edge‐on orientation under these conditions (Figure S1), indicating that the presence of two‐dimensional fillers enhances shear‐induced allignment during processing. The higher loading of cPPRNS induces a higher degree of edge‐on orientation, demonstrating that 2D fillers amplify shear‐induced alignment effects [36].

Thermodynamic Control: Confinement Threshold in Recrystallized cPPRNS Films

The recrystallization results (Figure 3d–f) reveal the intrinsic orientation preferences when shear memory is eliminated through thermal treatment. At low concentrations (1 wt.%), cPPRNS films exhibited mixed orientations after thermal treatment; this was evidenced by the (032) reflection being distributed across multiple azimuthal positions (Figure 4). This result indicates that weak confinement effects at insufficient filler concentration cannot provide directional constraints for preferential crystallization. However, at high concentrations (6–12 wt.%), a dramatic transition to flat‐on orientation occurs, as evidenced by the shift of (120) and (032) reflections to χ = 90° and 67°, respectively. This demonstrates that confinement effects inherently favor flat‐on crystallization when present at sufficient filler loading to create strong geometric constraints. The orientation factor for 12 wt.% loading is higher than that of 6 wt.%, confirming the concentration‐dependent strengthening of confinement effects.

This concentration‐dependent behavior establishes a critical threshold for confinement mechanism activation and confirms that two‐dimensional fillers can achieve orientation control, but only under specific conditions: high loading and elimination of competing processing effects. Figure 4 shows the systematic transition from shear‐dominated (edge‐on) to confinement‐dominated (flat‐on) behavior through azimuthal integration profiles of the (120) and (032) reflections. The evolution of peak positions and intensities with concentration provides direct evidence for mechanism switching through thermal treatment.

Shear vs. Homoepitaxy: Concentration‐Dependent Orientation in As‐Spun PPRNS Films

Figure 5 reveals the significantly more complex behavior of PPRNS systems where homoepitaxy effects compete with both shear force and confinement mechanisms. The As‐Spun state (Figure 5a–c) demonstrates concentration‐dependent orientation transitions that reflect the evolving balance between competing mechanisms. At low loadings (1 wt.%), shear forces dominate despite the presence of polymer brush homoepitaxy, yielding edge‐on orientation similar to cPPRNS systems. However, the orientation parameter values show subtle differences that indicate emerging homoepitaxy contributions.

At high concentrations (6–12 wt.%), the homoepitaxial effect, enhanced by confinement contributions, successfully overcomes the shear induced orientaion to achieve flat‐on orientation even during processing. This represents a key distinction from cPPRNS systems, where flat‐on orientation requires elimination of shear forces through recrystallization. The PPRNS systems demonstrate the robustness of homoepitaxy effects in competing with processing‐induced alignment. The orientation degree at 12 wt.% loading is higher than that of 6 wt.%, confirming the concentration‐dependent strengthening of template‐confinement synergy.

Homoepitaxy‐Confinement Synergy: Robust Flat‐on Orientation After Recrystallization

The recrystallization behavior of PPRNS systems (Figure 5d–f) provides crucial insights into the synergistic interactions between homoepitaxy and confinement effects. When shear memory is eliminated, the system is governed by homoepitaxy vs. confinement mechanisms, both of which favor flat‐on orientation. The synergistic combination of strong homoepitaxy and confinement effects maintains robust flat‐on orientation across all concentrations, even in 1 wt.% loading. This persistence of orientation control after thermal treatment demonstrates the thermodynamic stability of homoepitaxy‐directed crystallization.

The azimuthal integration analysis of PPRNS systems (Figure 6) reveals the quantitative differences in mechanism competition compared to cPPRNS behavior. For As‐Spun samples, the concentration‐dependent evolution is more gradual, with 1 wt.% showing broader distributions that progressively sharpen with increasing loading, reflecting the emerging dominance of homoepitaxy over shear forces. After recrystallization, all PPRNS concentrations exhibit sharp, well‐defined peaks characteristic of strong flat‐on orientation, demonstrating the universal effectiveness of homoepitaxy‐confinement synergy across the entire concentration range studied.

Molecular‐Level Validation: PM‐IRRAS Corroboration of Mechanistic Transitions

The PM‐IRRAS technique provides a complementary molecular‐level validation of crystallization orientation mechanisms identified by GIWAXS analysis. This method exploits a surface selection rule that maximally enhances vibrations with a transition dipole moment perpendicular to the substrate [37]. By analyzing the PM‐IRRAS spectra in the 1500–800 cm−1 region, the orientation of the PEO chains relative to the substrate can be inferred from the direction of the transition dipole moments with respect to the PEO helical axis. (Figure 7), enabling discrimination between flat‐on and edge‐on crystal orientations [28, 38].

PM‐IRRAS spectra of cPPRNS‐x% (a) and PPRNS‐x% (b) in the 1500–800 cm−1 region. Dashed lines and solid lines represent As‐Spun and recrystallized samples, respectively. Vibration mode: ω = wag, τ = twist, r = rock.

The spectroscopic evolution observed for both cPPRNS and PPRNS systems directly corroborates the orientation trends revealed by GIWAXS measurements. In As‐Spun films, the dominance of modes with transition dipole moments perpendicular (⊥) to the helix axis is confirmed by prominent bands at 1360, 1280, and 843 cm−1, attributed to various CH2 vibrations as detailed in Figure 7. This spectral signature indicates preferential alignment of PEO helices with their axes parallel to the substrate surface, providing molecular‐level evidence for the shear‐induced edge‐on lamellar orientation observed in diffraction patterns. Vibration bands at 1120 and 1467 cm−1 remain relatively insensitive to orientation changes and serve as internal references across all samples.

Thermal recrystallization induces systematic spectral transformations that reveal concentration‐dependent molecular reorientation behavior. For cPPRNS systems, low concentrations (1%) maintain the spectral dominance of modes perpendicular to the helix axis, consistent with mixed orientation states where insufficient confinement prevents complete reorientation. At higher concentrations (6%–12%), significant intensity enhancement occurs for modes with transition dipole moments parallel (∥) to the helix axis at 1344, 1242, and 964 cm−1, indicating successful transition to a perpendicular orientation of the PEO helices that corresponds to flat‐on lamellar alignment achieved through confinement mechanisms.

PPRNS systems demonstrate markedly different behavior, exhibiting pronounced enhancement of parallel transition dipole bands (1344, 1242 cm−1) across all concentrations upon recrystallization. This concentration‐independent spectroscopic response highlights the superior orientation control achieved through template‐confinement synergy, where surface‐grafted polymer brushes provide consistent nucleation sites that direct flat‐on crystallization regardless of loading level. Consistent with this homoepitaxial interpretation, isothermal crystallization measurements in our prior work [29] showed that, within the PPRNS series, increasing filler content from 1 to 12 wt.% progressively shortens the crystallization half‐time, and that at matched 12 wt.% loading, PEO/PPRNS crystallizes substantially faster than PEO/cPPRNS bearing end‐capped brushes, supporting a role for the free surface‐tethered PEO brushes in promoting nucleation relative to the end‐capped (cPPRNS‐type) analogue. The molecular‐level evidence directly supports the mechanistic framework established through diffraction analysis, demonstrating that template effects operate at the fundamental level of chain alignment during crystallization. These combined GIWAXS and PM‐IRRAS results, which quantitatively define the competition and synergy between shear, confinement, and homoepitaxy, now allow us to establish a predictive mechanistic phase diagram.

Design Principles for Orientation Control

Figure 8 provides a comprehensive phase diagram that establishes clear design principles for achieving targeted crystallization orientations through systematic control of processing conditions and filler selection. The diagram reveals distinct pathways for orientation control based on the interplay between filler type, concentration, and thermal treatment, offering predictive capabilities for materials design across diverse applications.

Crystallization orientation mechanism phase diagram as a function of processing conditions and filler content, showing distinct regions for shear‐dominated, confinement‐only, homoepitaxy‐confinement synergy, and three‐mechanism competition behaviors.

For applications requiring edge‐on orientation, As‐Spun cPPRNS composites provide reliable shear‐dominated allignment over the investigated concentration. Low loading PPRNS composites can also exibit edge‐on orientation when shear dominates over homeoepitaxial and confinement effects. The shear‐induced alignment is the most straightforward approach for applications such as certain electronic devices where in‐plane chain alignment enhances charge transport properties. The robustness of this mechanism across different filler types provides processing flexibility while maintaining consistent orientation outcomes.

Mixed orientations can be systematically accessed through the three‐mechanism competition region at low‐to‐medium PPRNS concentrations in the As‐Spun state. This intermediate behavior offers tunable crystallization characteristics where partial orientation control is desired, enabling fine‐tuning of material properties through precise filler loading optimization. The ability to achieve controlled mixed orientations provides opportunities for applications requiring intermediate mechanical or optical properties that benefit from neither purely edge‐on nor flat‐on crystallization.

Flat‐on orientation can be achieved through two distinct pathways depending on processing constraints and performance requirements. The confinement‐only region demonstrates that high‐loading cPPRNS systems can achieve flat‐on orientation through pure geometric constraints after thermal treatment, providing a cost‐effective approach when post‐processing annealing is acceptable. This pathway is particularly suitable for applications where thermal treatment is already part of the manufacturing process. Alternatively, the homoepitaxy‐confinement synergy region shows that PPRNS systems achieve flat‐on orientation directly during spin coating at high loadings, eliminating thermal treatment requirements entirely. These systems maintain consistent flat‐on orientation across all concentrations after recrystallization, offering superior processing flexibility and robustness for applications where processing conditions may vary or where consistent performance across different loading levels is critical.

The homoepitaxy effect boundary clearly delineates the fundamental advantage of surface‐functionalized fillers, revealing that PPRNS systems demonstrate enhanced orientation control capabilities that extend across broader concentration ranges compared to conventional two‐dimensional fillers. This mechanistic understanding enables optimization of filler design, where the incorporation of surface‐grafted polymer brushes provides access to homoepitaxy‐mediated crystallization that can overcome processing‐induced effects and maintain orientation control under diverse conditions.

The orientation transition boundaries identify critical thresholds where mechanism switching occurs, providing quantitative guidelines for selecting optimal filler concentrations and thermal treatment conditions to achieve targeted orientations. These boundaries enable predictive design of processing protocols, allowing for systematic manipulation of crystallization behavior through controlled variation of processing parameters.

Conclusion

We have established a three‐mechanism framework—shear, confinement, and homoepitaxy—that governs lamellar orientation in PEO thin films processed with PPRNS and cPPRNS fillers. Systematic GIWAXS and PM‐IRRAS analysis reveals distinct orientation switching behaviors that provide predictive guidelines for processing‐condition selection. In particular, at sufficiently high loadings, PPRNS systems leverage homoepitaxial templating to achieve flat‐on orientation directly during spin coating, without requiring post‐processing thermal treatment. These findings offer a mechanistic basis for orientation control in ultrathin polymer films relevant to applications in flexible electronics, optical devices, and barrier materials.

Experimental Section

Materials

Beta‐cyclodextrin (β‐CD) and tetrahydrofuran were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation. HO‐PEO76‐b‐PPO29‐b‐PEO76‐OH (Pluronic F68), carbonyldiimidazole (CDI), ethylenediamine, and trimethylolpropane triglycidyl ether (TMPTG) were procured from Sigma–Aldrich. PEO with a weight‐average molecular weight (M w) of 330 000 g/mol and polydispersity (M w/M n) of 1.18 was purchased from Polymer Source. All chemicals were used as received.

Preparation of Water‐Dispersed Samples of PPRNSs and cPPRNSs

Pluronic F68 was converted into its diamine‐terminated derivative, H2N‐PEO76‐b‐PPO29‐b‐PEO76‐NH2, via carbonyldiimidazole (CDI)mediated coupling with ethylenediamine as reported previously [32, 39]. H2N‐PEO76‐b‐PPO29‐b‐PEO76‐NH2 (1.08 g) was added to a β‐CD aqueous solution (100 mL, 18 g/L). The mixed solution was stirred for two weeks and gradually formed PPRNS by self‐assembly. For cPPRNS synthesis, TMPTG (1–200 equiv relative to axis polymer [mol/mol]) was added to the PPRNS aqueous dispersion and reacted for 1 week at 25°C following the established procedure [40]. This modification end‐capped the surface PEO brushes, creating conventional 2D filler behavior. The synthesis scheme is shown in Scheme 1.

Synthesis of PPRNS and cPPRNS. Self‐assembly of β‐cyclodextrin (β‐CD) with H2N‐ PEO76‐b‐PPO29‐b‐PEO76‐NH2 triblock copolymer forms PPRNS with tethered PEO brushes. A subsequent end‐capping of the PEO brushes with TMPTG produces cPPRNS, eliminating their homoepitaxy functionality and allowing them to serve as conventional two‐dimensional fillers.

Fabrication of PPRNS and cPPRNS‐Based Thin Films

Silicon substrates (10 × 10 × 0.65 mm3) were cleaned successively with distilled water and acetone, prior to 10‐min UV‐ozone treatment (ASM401 OZ, ASUMI GIKEN). Thin films were prepared by spin coating aqueous solutions containing PEO (50 mg/mL) and varying filler concentrations (1–12 wt.%) at 3000 rpm for 60 s on silicon substrates. The resulting films had thicknesses of 150–250 nm as summarized in Table 1. Film thickness was measured using a laser microscope (VK‐X250, KEYENCE) afrer scratching the films with a razor blade to expose the substrate surface. The As‐Spun thin films were heated at 100°C for 5 min and cooled to 25°C to induce PEO recrystallization.

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a JEOL JSM‐7800F field‐emission scanning electron microscope.

Grazing Incidence Wide‐Angle X‐Ray Scattering (GIWAXS)

GIWAXS measurements were performed at the BL05XU beamline of SPring‐8, using a monochromatic X‐ray beam with a wavelength of λ = 1.0 Å (12.4 keV). Samples were mounted on a high‐precision 6‐axis goniometer under ambient conditions. The incident angle (a i) was set at 0.11°, above the critical angle of PEO (0.108°), to ensure penetration into the film's bulk. A 2D hybrid pixel detector (Pilatus3 S 1 m, Dectris Ltd.) was positioned at a sample‐to‐detector distance of 302 mm, calibrated using a silver behenate (AgBh) standard. Scattering patterns were acquired with 0.7 s exposure per frame to mitigate radiation damage. Data reduction, including background subtraction and geometric corrections, was performed using GIXSGUI [41]. Remapped images are displayed in the (q xy, q z) projection: q z is the out‐of‐plane component normal to the film surface, q y lies in‐plane one orthogonal to the beam, and q x along the beam. The scattering intensity as a function of the in‐plane angle χ was analyzed. We define χ as the angle between q z direction and q, χ = 0° aligns with q z (vertical), and χ = 90° with q x (horizontal).

Polarization‐Modulation Infrared Reflection Absorption Spectroscopy (PM‐IRRAS)

PM‐IRRAS measurements were performed using a Bruker VERTEX 70 FTIR spectrometer with the polarization modulation accessory (PMA 50) equipped with a photoelastic modulator (PEM) operating at 50 kHz (Hinds Instruments PEM‐200) and a liquid‐nitrogen‐cooled mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. The incident IR beam was polarized using a ZnSe wire‐grid polarizer (0°) and modulated between p‐ and s‐polarization states by the PEM (45°) set to generate a half‐wave retardation at 1000 cm−1. This beam was then focused on the sample at an incidence angle of 75° to maximize surface sensitivity. Samples were deposited on Au‐coated glass substrates (ST Japan Inc.) and dried under vacuum. Spectra were acquired in a nitrogen atmosphere over 3600–600 cm−1 at a resolution of 2 cm−1, with 512 co‐added scans per measurement to ensure sufficient signal‐to‐noise ratio.
