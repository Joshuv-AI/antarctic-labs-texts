Supramolecular coordination nanoplatform amplifies oxidative stress to overcome ferroptosis resistance in gallbladder cancer
Authors: Yuhao Zhao, Jingyun Zhang, Chen Zhou, Mao Yang, Zhigang Zheng, Yixiang Gu, Yang Zhang, Xingqi Chen, Zhengjun Zhou, Jin Gu, Lijin Zhao, Wenguang Wu, Meidong Lang, Yingbin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is a highly aggressive malignancy, and its robust antioxidant defense system frequently leads to profound therapeutic resistance. Targeted disruption of redox homeostasis offers a potential strategy to overcome such resistance. In this study, we developed ICFe@D, a supramolecular nanomedicine co-assembled from iso-liquiritigenin (ISL), Fe³⁺, and chlorin e6 (Ce6) within a DSPE-PEG matrix, to trigger combined ferroptosis and apoptosis. As a tumor microenvironment (TME)-responsive platform, ICFe@D systematically dismantles the antioxidant defenses of GBC cells through a “supply-and-exhaust” mechanism. Specifically, Fe3+-derived Fe2+ and laser-activated Ce6 act as an efficient reactive oxygen species (ROS) generator via Fenton and photodynamic reactions, while ISL severely impairs the intracellular antioxidant capacity by downregulating GPX4 and activating the p62/Keap1/Nrf2/HMOX1 signaling pathway. We evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of this platform in patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and patient-derived organoid xenograft (PDOX) models. ICFe@D demonstrated potent and consistent tumor regression across heterogeneous patient-specific models with minimal systemic toxicity. This study presents a highly translatable and precise nanotherapeutic strategy that effectively sensitizes GBC to ferroptosis, providing a valuable preclinical framework for biliary tract cancer therapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04625-6.

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is an aggressive malignancy and the most common biliary tract cancer [1–3]. Its asymptomatic progression and non-specific early symptoms often result in late-stage diagnoses, with fewer than 30% of patients qualifying for surgical resection [4]. Even among those undergoing surgery, recurrence rates exceed 60%, leading to a dismal five-year survival rate of less than 5% for advanced-stage disease [5]. Current therapeutic approaches, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, have shown limited efficacy in improving survival outcomes, underscoring the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies [6, 7].

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death characterized by reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction and lipid peroxidation (LPO), has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in cancer [8–11]. Increasing evidence highlights its potential to overcome drug resistance and enhance antitumor activity across multiple cancer types [12–14]. Our previous research demonstrated that iso-liquiritigenin (ISL), a natural chalcone compound, effectively induces ferroptosis in GBC cells by continuously hyperactivating the p62/Keap1/Nrf2/HMOX1 signaling pathway and downregulating GPX4 [15]. While the Nrf2/HMOX1 axis is traditionally recognized as a cytoprotective antioxidant mechanism, its sustained hyperactivation by ISL excessively degrades intracellular heme. This process releases massive amounts of endogenous labile iron, transforming a protective pathway into a potent pro-ferroptotic trigger [16–18]. However, the clinical application of ISL is limited by its poor aqueous solubility, low bioavailability, and potential systemic toxicity [19, 20]. Moreover, most studies on ISL remain preclinical, focusing on cell lines or animal models, with a lack of validation in physiologically relevant systems such as patient-derived organoids (PDOs) [15, 21].

To overcome these limitations and maximize the therapeutic potential of ISL, we rationally designed a supramolecular nanomedicine, ISL-Ce6-Fe-DSPE (ICFe@D). Encapsulated within a 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE) matrix, ICFe@D facilitates efficient tumor targeting via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [22]. Upon reaching the acidic tumor microenvironment (TME), ICFe@D dissociates to deliver a combined therapy involving ISL, Fe3+-derived Fe2+-driven Fenton reactions, and chlorin e6 (Ce6)-driven photodynamic therapy (PDT) [23, 24]. Under 666 nm laser irradiation, Ce6 generates singlet oxygen (¹O₂), while Fe3+-derived Fe2+ drives the production of cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH). Concurrently, the released ISL severely impairs the cell’s ability to clear these accumulated lipid peroxides by downregulating GPX4 and depleting glutathione (GSH) [25].

We hypothesize that combining continuous ROS generation (supply) with GPX4/GSH depletion (exhaust) thoroughly disrupts the intracellular redox balance, triggering synergistic ferroptosis and apoptosis to overcome the intrinsic therapeutic resistance of GBC cells. Furthermore, to bridge the critical gap between preclinical research and clinical application, we evaluated this therapeutic paradigm using patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and PDO-xenograft (PDOX) models. Compared to traditional cell lines, these models better preserve the histological architecture and heterogeneity of primary human tumors, and thus provide a more reliable framework for assessing the translational potential of ICFe@D in GBC treatment [26–28] (Scheme 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

Iron (III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl₃·6 H₂O, ≥ 99.0%, Analytical grade) and DSPE-PEG (> 95.0%, Reagent grade) were purchased from Shanghai Titan Technology Co., Ltd. Isoliquiritigenin (ISL, ≥ 98.0%) was obtained from Shanghai Dibai Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., while chlorin e6 (Ce6, ≥ 94.0%) was sourced from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, ≥ 99.0%, HPLC grade) and methanol (≥ 99.8%, HPLC grade) were supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was prepared in-house following standard protocols. All chemicals and reagents were used without further purification.

Characterization

The morphological features of nanoparticles were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-1400, JEOL) with point and line resolutions of 0.38 nm and 0.20 nm, respectively. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential measurements were performed using a Zetasizer (Malvern ZEN3600). UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded on a UV-Vis 765 spectrophotometer. The fluorescence emission spectra were acquired using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (PE LS-55) with an excitation wavelength of 403 nm. The chemical composition and oxidation states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Mg Kα radiation (1253.6 eV) as the excitation source. The iron content was quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Agilent 5800 VDV). The drug loading of ISL was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Shimadzu LC-20 A) equipped with a C18 column (Agela, 250 mm × 4 mm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and 0.04% formic acid, with detection performed at 367 nm.

Synthesis of IFe@D nanoparticles

The IFe@D nanoparticles were synthesized using a self-assembly approach. First, 3 mg of ISL was dissolved in 0.5 mL of DMF under ultrasonication (200 W, 25 kHz) at room temperature for 5 min. Separately, 5 mg of FeCl₃·6H₂O was dissolved in 7 mL of deionized water. The ISL-DMF solution was then added dropwise to the FeCl₃ aqueous solution under continuous stirring (500 rpm). After complete addition, the mixture was sonicated for 10 min to promote coordination between ISL and Fe³⁺ ions. Subsequently, 500 µL of a 10 mg/mL DSPE-PEG methanol solution was introduced dropwise into the mixture, followed by another 10-minute sonication. The solution was stirred for an additional 2 h to ensure complete self-assembly. Finally, the resulting nanoparticles were purified by dialysis using a membrane with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 3500 Da against deionized water for 12 h to obtain IFe@D nanoparticles.

Synthesis of ICFe@D nanoparticles

ICFe@D nanoparticles were prepared using a similar self-assembly method. Specifically, 3 mg of ISL and 1 mg of Ce6 were co-dissolved in 0.5 mL of DMF and sonicated for 5 min to form a homogeneous solution. Separately, 5 mg of FeCl₃·6 H₂O was dissolved in 7 mL of deionized water. The ISL-Ce6-DMF solution was added dropwise into the FeCl₃ aqueous solution under continuous stirring. After complete addition, the mixture was sonicated for 10 min. Next, 500 µL of a 20 mg/mL DSPE-PEG methanol solution was introduced dropwise, followed by another 10-minute sonication. The reaction mixture was stirred continuously for 2 h. ICFe@D was purified by dialysis (MWCO = 3500 Da) against deionized water for 12 h.

The test of ISL and Ce6 release behavior from ICFe@D

3 mL of ICFe@D nanoparticle suspension was placed in a dialysis membrane (MWCO = 3500 Da) and immersed in 20 mL of PBS at different pH values (7.4 and 5.7) in 50 mL centrifuge tubes. The release study was conducted at 37 °C under continuous orbital shaking. At predetermined time intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 24, 36, and 48 h), 3 mL of release medium was collected for analysis and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The concentration of ISL in the collected samples was quantified using HPLC, while the concentration of Ce6 was determined by UV-vis spectroscopy.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human gallbladder cancer cell line SGC996 was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Science (Shanghai, China), and the NOZ cell line was obtained from the Health Science Research Resource Bank (Osaka, Japan). The NOZ and SGC996 cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Hyclone, USA), with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Yeasen, China). The cells were maintained in a 5% CO₂ incubator at 37 °C.

Cellular uptake of free Ce6 and ICFe@D

For the assessment of cellular uptake, both NOZ and SGC996 cells were cultured in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well in 1 mL of medium and incubated for 24 h. Free Ce6 (0.3 µg/mL) and ICFe@D (3.5 µg/mL) were then added to the wells, and the cells were incubated for 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h, respectively. After washing three times with PBS, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min in the dark and stained with DAPI. Cellular uptake was monitored using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV3000, Japan) at the specified time points.

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxic effects of ICFe@D, ISL, IFe@D, and free Ce6 on GBC cell lines (NOZ and SGC996) were assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Yeasen, China) assay. The process included determining the IC50 values for each treatment group and subsequently assessing cell viability under conditions with or without 666 nm laser irradiation. All experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

NOZ and SGC996 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well in 100 µL of complete medium and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to allow cell adherence. Afterward, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different concentrations of ICFe@D, IFe@D (ranging from 0 to 50 µg/mL of ISL) or ISL (ranging from 0 to 100 µg/mL of ISL) for various incubation periods (24, 48, and 72 h) to determine the optimal concentration and time. After the treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). The IC50 values were calculated using dose-response curves generated by GraphPad Prism software.

Based on the IC50 results, NOZ and SGC996 cells were treated with optimized concentrations of ICFe@D, IFe@D, ISL (3.5 µg/mL of ISL), or Ce6(0.3 µg/mL of Ce6) for 48 h. The experimental groups were divided into the following ten groups (NC, ISL, Ce6, IFe@D, and ICFe@D, with or without laser irradiation). For the laser-irradiated groups, 8 h post-treatment, the cells were subjected to laser irradiation (666 nm, 20 mW/cm²) for 5 min, as appropriate. Following irradiation, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for an additional 40 h. The CCK-8 assay, as described above, was used to assess cell viability for all groups.

Colony formation assay

The colony formation assay was conducted to evaluate the long-term proliferative potential of NOZ and SGC996 cells after treatment. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well in 2 mL of complete medium and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were divided into ten groups. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. After treatment, the cells were cultured for an additional 10 days, with the medium replaced every four days. At the end of the incubation period, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.05% crystal violet for visualization. Plates were washed three times with PBS to remove excess stain, and then captured.

Wound healing assay

The wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the migratory ability of GBC cells following treatment. NOZ and SGC996 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. The cells were divided into ten groups. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. After treatment, the cells were incubated for an additional 48 h to allow migration into the wound area. Photomicrographs of the initial wound (0 h) and at the end of the incubation period (48 h) were taken under an inverted microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Intracellular reactive oxygen species measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the 2',7'-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) ROS assay kit (Beyotime, China) in both NOZ and SGC996 cell lines and GBC organoids. Cells were divided into ten groups. Organoids were divided into two groups: NC, ICFe@D + L. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. After treatment, DCFH-DA (0.1%) diluted in serum-free medium was added to each well. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min to allow for ROS detection. After incubation, the DCFH-DA solution was removed, and fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Intracellular lipid peroxidation accumulation assay

Intracellular LPO levels were assessed using the fluorescent probe C11-BODIPY581/591 (GLPBIO, USA). NOZ and SGC996 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. The cells were divided into ten groups. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. After treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium containing 10 µM C11-BODIPY581/591 and incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV3000, Japan) with excitation wavelengths of 488 nm for the oxidized form (green fluorescence) and 561 nm for the reduced form (red fluorescence).

Intracellular lipid peroxidation MDA content assay

Intracellular lipid peroxidation was assessed using a malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (Beyotime, China). NOZ and SGC996 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were divided into ten groups. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. After treatment, the cells were harvested by trypsinization and lysed using ice-cold lysis buffer. The lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was mixed with the MDA assay working solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath (95 °C) for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature, the samples were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy™ Mx Microplate Spectrophotometer). Finally, MDA levels were normalized to protein concentration determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China).

Evaluation of intracellular GSH/GSSG level

The intracellular GSH/GSSG ratio was determined using a GSH/GSSG assay kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. NOZ and SGC996 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were divided into ten groups. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. After treatment, the cells were harvested by trypsinization and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets were lysed using the protein removal reagent provided in the kit and incubated on ice for 5 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected to measure total GSH and oxidized GSSG levels. The GSH and GSSG contents were quantified using a microplate reader (Synergy™ Mx Microplate Spectrophotometer) at an absorbance wavelength of 412 nm.

Western blot analysis

For cell lines, NOZ and SGC996 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were divided into ten groups. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. For organoids, similar experiments were conducted using the NC and ICFe@D + L groups. Organoids were cultured in Matrigel and treated for 48 h with specific concentrations of ICFe@D based on their IC50 values (7 µg/mL for P1 and P2 PDOs, and 12.5 µg/mL for P3 PDO). After treatment, both cells and organoids were washed with PBS and lysed using ice-cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Protein concentrations in the supernatant were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with loading buffer, denatured at 100 °C for 10 min, separated by SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad) on a 10% gel, and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to p62, Nrf2, p-Nrf2, Keap1, HMOX1 (Proteintech, 1:1000), GPX4 (Abcam, 1:1000) and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000). After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature and visualized using the BIO-RAD ChemiDoc system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Mitochondrial morphological analysis by transmission electron microscopy

Mitochondrial morphology was analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) in the NC and ICFe@D + L groups. After treatment for 48 h, the NOZ and SGC996 cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C overnight. The fixed cells were washed three times with PBS and post-fixed in a solution containing 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO₄) and 1.5% potassium ferricyanide for 2 h at 4 °C. After washing with deionized water, the samples were dehydrated using a graded series of acetone solutions. The dehydrated samples were infiltrated with a mixture of acetone and Epon 812 epoxy resin, followed by embedding in pure resin containing 1.5% benzyl dimethylamine (BDMA). Polymerization was performed at 45 °C for 12 h, followed by 48 h at 60 °C. Ultrathin sections (~ 70 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7) and mounted on copper grids. The sections were double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate for contrast enhancement. Mitochondrial ultrastructure was visualized using a Hitachi HT7700 transmission electron microscope operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.

PI/Annexin V apoptosis assay

The apoptosis of NOZ and SGC996 cells was evaluated using the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences, USA). The cells were divided into ten groups. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. After treatment for 48 h, both adherent and floating cells were collected and washed twice with cold PBS. The cells were resuspended in 1× binding buffer at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Apoptosis analysis was performed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis assay kit, and the results were quantified using a flow cytometer (BD LSRFortessa™, USA).

Human GBC tissues

Fresh tumor tissues were surgically obtained from three patients diagnosed with gallbladder adenocarcinoma at Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to tissue collection.

Establishment of GBC organoids

Fresh tumor specimens were collected and immediately placed on ice in tissue storage solution (Miltenyi Biotech, Germany). The tissues were minced into small fragments and digested in a solution containing 1 mg/mL Collagenase IV and 1 mg/mL DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 30–60 min with gentle agitation. The digested tissue was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting cell pellet was washed thoroughly with cold PBS. The isolated cells were resuspended in 20 µL of Matrigel (Corning, USA) and seeded into prewarmed 48-well plates at a density of 10,000–20,000 cells per well. After the Matrigel solidified at 37 °C, 250 µL of OneCULTar™ organoid culture medium (OneTar Biomedicine, China) was added to each well. The culture medium was replenished every 3 days and organoids were passaged in about a week using TrypLE Express (Gibco, USA), followed by reseeding into fresh Matrigel. Optical images of the organoids were captured throughout the culture process to monitor growth and morphology.

Hematoxylin and eosin and Immunohistochemistry Staining

Tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were excised from all CDX (n = 5 per group, 50 mice in total) and PDOX (n = 3 per group, 18 mice in total) model mice at the end of the treatment period for histological and immunohistochemical analyses. Organoids embedded in Matrigel were carefully removed from culture plates and subsequently embedded in agarose gel for structural support. Both tissues and organoid samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature overnight. Following fixation, samples were sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices for subsequent analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed according to standard histological protocols to assess overall tissue architecture and morphology. Tumors and major organs from all groups were examined using a digital microscope to evaluate tumor cell density, structural damage, and any pathological alterations in the organs. Immunohistochemical staining was conducted using anti-HMOX1, anti-GPX4, and anti-Ki-67 antibodies following the standard protocol.

Organoid viability assay

The cytotoxic effects of ICFe@D, IFe@D, ISL, and free Ce6 on GBC organoids were evaluated using the CellTiter-Lumi™ 3D (CTG) assay (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Organoids were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well, mixed with 3µL of Matrigel in 100 µL of organoid culture medium. The plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for four days to allow organoid formation. Afterwards, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different concentrations of ICFe@D (ranging from 0 to 1,200 µg/mL of ISL) and incubated for 48 h to determine the IC50 values. Following treatment, 100 µL of CellTiter-Lumi™ 3D reagent was added to each well. The plates were gently shaken for 5 min at room temperature to promote cell lysis and then incubated for an additional 25 min. Luminescence was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Based on the IC50 values obtained, organoids were treated with specific concentrations of ICFe@D (7 µg/mL for P1 and P2 PDOs, and 12.5 µg/mL for P3 PDO) for 48 h. To further investigate treatment effects, the organoids were divided into ten groups. Treatments were applied as described in the cell viability assay. Organoid viability in all treatment groups was assessed using the CellTiter-Lumi™ 3D assay as described above. Each treatment group included three replicate wells to ensure reproducibility and reliability.

Cell-derived xenograft and patient-derived organoid xenograft models

Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (22–24 g) were used for both models. For the cell-derived xenograft (CDX) model, 1 × 10⁶ NOZ cells suspended in 100 µL of PBS were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse (n = 50). For the patient-derived organoid xenograft (PDOX) model, GBC organoids were dissociated from matrigel using TrypLE Express, centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min, and the resulting cell pellet was resuspended in Matrigel. The organoid-matrigel mixture was subcutaneously implanted into the right flank of four-week-old BALB/c nude mice (n = 18). Once tumors reached a measurable size, further experimental treatments were initiated.

In vivo biodistribution of ICFe@D

Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (22–24 g) were used to establish subcutaneous tumors by implanting NOZ cells into the right flank. Tumors were allowed to grow to approximately 40 mm³ before experimentation. Mice were divided into two groups, each receiving an intravenous injection of either ICFe@D (10 mg/kg) or free Ce6 (2 mg/kg). In vivo fluorescence imaging was conducted at designated time points (4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h post-injection) using AniView100 Multi-mode In Vivo Animal Imaging System-BLT to monitor the biodistribution of the nanomaterials. At each time point, tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested for analysis. The collected tissues were weighed and their fluorescence intensity was measured to evaluate the distribution and retention of ICFe@D compared to free Ce6.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of ICFe@D

In the CDX model, tumors were allowed to reach approximately 80 mm³ before treatment initiation. Mice were randomized into ten groups (n = 5 per group, 50 mice in total): PBS, ISL, Ce6, IFe@D, ICFe@D, PBS + L, ISL + L, Ce6 + L, IFe@D + L, ICFe@D + L. Each group was intravenously injected once every two days, for a total of five injections. The equivalent dosage of ISL was 10 mg/kg, and the dosage of Ce6 was 2 mg/kg. Mice underwent laser irradiation (666 nm, 20 mW/cm²) for 5 min at 12 h post-injection. In the PDOX model, mice bearing tumors from 3 distinct patients were used. For each patient model, mice were randomized into two groups (n = 3 per group, 18 mice in total): PBS and ICFe@D + L, following the same treatment protocol as described above. Tumor volumes for both models were measured every two days using a digital caliper, with the tumor volume calculated using the formula: (width² × length) / 2. Body weights were also recorded at the same intervals to assess systemic toxicity. At the conclusion of the treatment period, in strict accordance with the ‘3Rs’ principles and standard operating procedures, the animals were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation following pentobarbital injection. Subsequently, tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from all mice in their respective cohorts were meticulously harvested and allocated for further analyses.

Blood biochemical analysis

To evaluate systemic toxicity at the end of the treatment period, blood samples were randomly collected from a subset of mice in each CDX model group (n = 3 per group, 30 mice in total), whereas blood was collected from all mice in each PDOX model group. Mice received intravenous injections of their respective formulations every two days for a total of five doses, as described previously. Approximately 600 µL of blood was obtained via cardiac puncture and serum was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 15 min. Biochemical analyses were performed using an automated biochemical analyzer (Rayto, Shenzhen) to evaluate key markers. Liver function was assessed by measuring alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyltransferase (γ-GT), albumin (ALB), and total protein (TP). Renal function was evaluated by quantifying urea (UREA) levels, while serum glucose (GLU) levels were monitored to assess metabolic health.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Multiple comparisons of data were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way ANOVA or two-tailed Student’s t tests. In all cases, the differences of statistics were considered at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of ICFe@D

Leveraging the dual coordination mechanisms of phenolic hydroxyl groups in ISL with Fe³⁺ ions and the carboxyl-Fe³⁺ electrostatic interactions in chlorin e6 (Ce6), we successfully synthesized two self-assembled nanostructures: ISL-Fe-DSPE (IFe@D) and ICFe@D. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. 1a) revealed that IFe@D adopted an irregular spherical morphology, with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 69.4 nm and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.13. In contrast, ICFe@D exhibited a relatively stable spherical structure, characterized by a slightly smaller hydrodynamic diameter of 66.1 nm and a PDI of 0.29, as measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The colloidal stability of these nanoparticles was assessed over a 14-day period (Fig. 1b), during which both IFe@D and ICFe@D maintained consistent particle sizes, demonstrating exceptional stability. Notably, the incorporation of Ce6 significantly influenced the zeta potential, shifting from − 6.4 mV for IFe@D to -16.7 mV for ICFe@D (Fig. 1c), suggesting enhanced electrostatic stability due to the integration of Ce6.

The UV-vis spectra of ISL, ISL-Fe, ISL-Fe-Ce6, and Ce6 in DMSO solutions were measured to determine whether the drug loading of ISL and Ce6 could be detected using UV-vis spectroscopy (Fig. 1d). ISL exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 375 nm, which intensified upon complexation with Fe³⁺, indicating strong coordination between ISL and Fe³⁺. Ce6 displayed a distinct absorption peak at 666 nm, which underwent a slight redshift in ISL-Fe-Ce6 without significant changes in absorbance intensity. It is confirmed that ISL cannot be analyzed by this method due to its complexation with Fe3+, while Ce6 remains unaffected.

The mass drug loading capacities for ISL and Ce6 were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and UV-vis spectroscopy, respectively. Based on their respective standard calibration curves (Fig. S1), the mass drug loading values were calculated to be 8.7% ± 0.3% for ISL and 2.8% ± 0.2% for Ce6 (n = 3). The UV-vis spectrum of ICFe@D further confirmed the presence of Ce6, displaying a characteristic absorption peak at 670 nm (Fig. 1e). Additionally, fluorescence emission spectroscopy under 403 nm excitation (Fig. 1f) demonstrated a pronounced redshift in the maximum emission peak of ICFe@D compared to free Ce6. High-resolution Fe 2p spectrum detected by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 1g) confirmed the presence of Fe³⁺ within ICFe@D, with characteristic peaks observed at binding energies of 712.2 eV (Fe 2p₃/₂) and 725.4 eV (Fe 2p₁/₂), accompanied by a satellite peak. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) quantified the iron content in ICFe@D at approximately 4.2%. All the above results collectively demonstrated the successful preparation of ICFe@D.

Finally, the pH-responsive drug release profiles of ISL and Ce6 from ICFe@D were evaluated under acidic (pH = 5.7) and neutral (pH = 7.4) conditions (Fig. 1h, i). At pH = 5.7, both drugs exhibited cumulative release rates of approximately 50% within 48 h, while less than 20% was released at pH = 7.4 over the same period. This accelerated release at pH 5.7 reflects the highly acidic environment of intracellular endolysosomes (pH 5.0–6.0) following cellular internalization, effectively minimizing premature drug leakage in the neutral systemic circulation (pH 7.4). Mechanistically, the acidic environment protonates the phenolic hydroxyls of ISL and carboxyls of Ce6, which significantly weakens their coordination bonds with Fe³⁺. This triggers the rapid disassembly of ICFe@D, enabling targeted intracellular drug release.

In vitro cytotoxicity of ICFe@D on gallbladder cancer

Next, we first investigated the in vitro antitumor effects of ICFe@D on GBC cell lines. To determine the optimal working concentration and incubation time for ICFe@D, we conducted CCK-8 assays on NOZ and SGC996 cells treated with ICFe@D, IFe@D, or ISL at different concentrations for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h. The IC50 values for ICFe@D in NOZ cells were 9.31 µg/mL, 3.40 µg/mL, and 2.52 µg/mL at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, respectively. Similar dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity was observed in SGC996 cells (Fig. 2a). Notably, the IC50 values for the ISL were consistent with those reported in previous studies [15], while the IC50 values for the IFe@D were significantly lower, and further reduced in the ICFe@D, indicating that Fe3+-derived Fe2+ drives oxidative stress via the Fenton reaction [23], which ultimately led to cell death (Fig. S2a).

Based on these findings, we selected an ICFe@D concentration of 3.5 µg/mL with a treatment duration of 48 h to evaluate its cytotoxic effects under conditions with or without 666 nm laser irradiation. To assess cellular uptake, ICFe@D was incubated with NOZ and SGC996 cell lines for various time points (2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h), after which the fluorescence signals were evaluated using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The red fluorescence signal of Ce6 in cells treated with ICFe@D increased over time and peaked at 8–12 h. In contrast, cells treated with free Ce6 alone exhibited no significant fluorescence, indicating that ICFe@D was taken up more effectively by cells at the same concentration of Ce6 and was retained longer within tumor cells (Fig. 2b; Fig. S2b, c). Therefore, we chose to perform 666 nm laser irradiation after 8 h of incubation.

Cell viability assays were performed to assess the effects of ISL, free Ce6, IFe@D, and ICFe@D (Fig. 2c). After 666 nm laser irradiation, the cell viability of free Ce6 and ICFe@D treated cells significantly decreased, while no notable changes were observed in the NC, ISL, or IFe@D groups, demonstrating that Ce6 generates cytotoxic effects upon 666 nm laser activation. Notably, ISL did not exhibit significant anti-proliferative effects, likely due to the insufficient concentration to achieve an effective therapeutic dose. In contrast, IFe@D exhibited a significantly enhanced anti-proliferative effect compared to free ISL, demonstrating the advantages of the EPR effect and the intrinsic synergy between ISL and Fe³⁺. While ICFe@D also showed a notable reduction in cell viability without irradiation, the most profound inhibition was observed in the ICFe@D + L group, which significantly outperformed all other treatment groups.

Furthermore, after 666 nm laser irradiation, ICFe@D significantly inhibited both proliferation and migration abilities of tumor cells, as observed in NOZ and SGC996 cell lines (Fig. 2d-g; Fig. S2d, e). These results provide additional evidence supporting the antitumor effects of ICFe@D and highlight its potential to suppress tumor progression.

ICFe@D enhances ferroptosis in gallbladder cancer in vitro

To investigate whether ICFe@D further promotes ferroptosis-related metabolic changes in GBC, we first measured intracellular ROS levels after different treatments. In both NOZ and SGC996 cells, treatment with Ce6 + L, IFe@D, IFe@D + L, and ICFe@D resulted in significant green fluorescence, which represents ROS accumulation. The strongest fluorescence was exhibited by ICFe@D + L-treated cells, indicating that the Fe2+-mediated Fenton reaction, combined with the Ce6-based PDT, and the effect of ISL synergistically contributed to the excessive accumulation of ROS (Fig. 3a; Fig. S3a, b).

To confirm that ICFe@D triggers genuine ferroptosis rather than standard PDT-induced cytotoxicity, we further investigated specific downstream hallmarks, particularly the unrepairable accumulation of lipid peroxides (LPO) and the collapse of the antioxidant defense system [29]. To assess LPO levels, we used the fluorescence probe C11-BODIPY581/591 and measured the cellular malondialdehyde (MDA) content. The results of CLSM revealed a shift from red fluorescence to predominant green fluorescence in the ICFe@D + L group (Fig. 3b; Fig. S3c). Similarly, MDA levels were significantly higher in the ICFe@D + L group compared to other treatments (Fig. 3c; Fig. S3d), confirming enhanced LPO accumulation and intensified ferroptosis in tumor cells.

Next, the GSH/GSSG ratio was assessed as an indicator of cellular redox status. Ce6 + L, IFe@D, IFe@D + L and ICFe@D groups exhibited notable reductions in GSH/GSSG levels, and the ICFe@D + L group showed the most pronounced GSH depletion (Fig. 3d; Fig. S3e). This could be attributed to the ROS generated by PDT and Fenton reactions further exacerbating redox imbalance, depleting endogenous GSH on top of ISL-mediated GPX4 inhibition.

To further elucidate the mechanism underlying ICFe@D-induced ferroptosis, we analyzed the expression of p62/Keap1/Nrf2/HMOX1 signaling components and GPX4 (Fig. 3e; Fig. S3f). Compared to ISL alone, IFe@D and ICFe@D upregulated p62, Nrf2, and HMOX1 expression while downregulating Keap1. This indicates that the p62/Keap1/Nrf2/HMOX1 pathway is activated, which is particularly evident in the ICFe@D + L group. Meanwhile, GPX4, a key regulator of ferroptosis, was markedly reduced in the ICFe@D + L group. Together, these results suggested that low-dose ISL, combined with high oxidative stress induced by Fe³⁺ and Ce6, effectively triggers ferroptosis in cancer cells.

Additionally, transmission electron microscopy revealed shrunken and ruptured mitochondria with increased membrane density and reduced cristae, which are hallmark features of ferroptosis [30], in ICFe@D + L-treated GBC cells (Fig. 3f; Fig. S3g).

Finally, to evaluate whether ICFe@D synergistically induces apoptosis alongside ferroptosis, we performed Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry analysis. The proportion of apoptotic cells was significantly higher in the ICFe@D + L group compared to other groups (Fig. 3g; Fig. S3h). This finding highlights that ISL synergizes with Fe3+-derived Fe2+-driven Fenton reactions and PDT to enhance apoptosis in GBC cells.

Therapeutic effects of ICFe@D in patient-derived gallbladder cancer organoids

With the advancement of 3D culture systems, PDOs have emerged as valuable tools for disease modeling and drug efficacy evaluation, accurately replicating the structural and histopathological features of primary tumors [31]. Building on previous studies conducted in cell lines, we investigated the therapeutic efficacy of ICFe@D against GBC using PDO models. Tumor specimens were obtained from three patients diagnosed with GBC to establish PDO models (Fig. 4a). Bright-field microscopy revealed that all three PDOs exhibited similar morphologies, characterized by irregular cystic or solid structures. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining further confirmed that the PDOs closely mirrored the histopathological features of their corresponding primary tumors (Fig. 4b).

To explore the drug sensitivity of GBC PDOs to ICFe@D, we treated the three organoids with varying concentrations of ICFe@D and assessed cell viability using the CellTiter-Lumi™ 3D (CTG) assay (Fig. 4c). The results showed the IC50 values for ICFe@D in PDOs were 6.79 µg/mL, 7.44 µg/mL, and 12.76 µg/mL at 48 h. Notably, the IC50 value for the P3 PDO was higher than that of the other two PDOs, possibly due to the heterogeneity among GBC patients. These findings confirmed that GBC PDOs, as physiologically relevant in vitro models, are also susceptible to ICFe@D treatment.

Given the heterogeneity among GBC patients [32], we treated the PDOs with specific ICFe@D concentrations based on their respective IC50 values (7 µg/mL for P1 and P2 PDOs, and 12.5 µg/mL for P3 PDO) (Fig. 4d). The antitumor effects were further compared in different treatment groups (NC, ISL, Ce6, IFe@D, and ICFe@D, with or without laser irradiation). Consistent with cell line results, significant tumor-killing effects were observed in the ICFe@D + L group across all PDOs, further validating the potent antitumor activity of ICFe@D and its potential as a therapeutic option for GBC.

To further validate that ICFe@D exerts its antitumor effects by promoting ferroptosis in GBC organoids, we assessed ROS levels in the three PDOs after various treatments (Fig. 4e). Significant green fluorescence was observed in all ICFe@D-treated PDOs under laser irradiation, aligning with results from cell line studies. Moreover, western blot analysis confirmed activation of the p62/Keap1/Nrf2/HMOX1 signaling pathway and a notable reduction in GPX4 protein expression following ICFe@D treatment (Fig. 4f). Collectively, these results demonstrated that ferroptosis plays a critical role in the antitumor effects of ICFe@D in GBC PDOs.

Evaluation of tumor targeting of ICFe@D in vivo

To determine whether the antitumor effects of ICFe@D observed in GBC cell lines and PDO models could be replicated in vivo, we established a subcutaneous tumor model using NOZ cells in mice. The tumor-targeting capability of ICFe@D was evaluated by intravenously injecting the mice with either ICFe@D or free Ce6, followed by in vivo imaging to track biodistribution (Fig. 5a, b). Compared to free Ce6, ICFe@D showed significant fluorescence accumulation at the tumor site within 4 h post-injection. The fluorescence intensity increased over time, peaking at 12 h, and remained around the tumor for up to 24 h, indicating sustained accumulation. Tumors and major organs (heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, and spleen) were harvested at specific time points (4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h) following injection. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging demonstrated that ICFe@D predominantly localized to tumor, exhibiting stronger fluorescence and prolonged retention compared to free Ce6 (Fig. 5c-e). These findings suggest that ICFe@D, facilitated by DSPE-mediated endocytosis, has strong tumor-targeting and retention capabilities, enhancing its potential for effective in vivo antitumor therapy.

In vivo antitumor activity and safety evaluation of ICFe@D

Building on its strong tumor-targeting and retention properties, we further investigated the in vivo antitumor activity of ICFe@D in a NOZ tumor xenograft mouse model. Mice were randomized into 10 groups consistent with the in vitro experiments and received intravenous treatments every two days for a total of five injections (Fig. 6a). Tumor growth inhibition was evaluated through measurements of tumor volume, tumor weight, and histological analysis of tumor tissues.

Compared with the PBS control, treatments including Ce6+L, IFe@D, IFe@D+L, ICFe@D, and ICFe@D+L significantly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 6b, c). Specifically, ICFe@D+L induced a significantly greater reduction in tumor volume than ICFe@D alone, decreasing tumor size by nearly 88% relative to the PBS group. This volumetric inhibition aligned directly with the tumor weight analysis, where the ICFe@D+L group yielded the lowest average tumor weight among all conditions (Fig. 6d). Notably, body weight remained stable across all treatment groups, indicating minimal systemic toxicity (Fig. 6e).

Histological analysis revealed compelling evidence of ICFe@D’s therapeutic potential at the tissue level. Sections of the tumors in each group were stained with H&E (Fig. 6f). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining showed increased HMOX1 expression and decreased GPX4 levels in tumors treated with ICFe@D + L, confirming enhanced ferroptosis induction in tumor tissues. Additionally, Ki-67 staining indicated markedly reduced tumor cell proliferation following ICFe@D + L treatment, reinforcing its potent antitumor efficacy in vivo (Fig. 6g).

To evaluate safety, we performed H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) and blood biochemical analyses post-treatment. No pathological abnormalities were detected in any major organs across treatment groups, with tissue morphology comparable to that of controls (Fig. 7a). Biochemical assessments of liver function markers (AST, ALT, γ-GT, ALB, TP), renal function marker (UREA), and blood glucose levels (GLU) revealed no significant deviations, indicating that ICFe@D caused no detectable toxicity compared to the PBS group (Fig. 7b–h). These results demonstrate that ICFe@D exerts potent in vivo antitumor effects through ferroptosis induction while maintaining an excellent safety profile.

Validation of ICFe@D antitumor activity in PDOX models

Patient-derived organoid xenograft (PDOX) models provide an advanced platform for preclinical cancer research, offering superior clinical relevance compared to conventional CDX models by closely recapitulating the genetic, histological, and microenvironmental features of primary tumors [33]. To further assess the clinical translational potential of ICFe@D, PDOX models were generated using organoids derived from three GBC patients and randomized into two groups: PBS and ICFe@D + L (Fig. 8a). ICFe@D + L treatment significantly reduced tumor volume (Fig. 8b, c) and tumor weight (Fig. 8d) across all PDOX models, without causing notable weight loss or organ toxicity (Fig. 8e; Fig. S4a-h). Histological analyses further confirmed consistent therapeutic efficacy of ICFe@D + L across all PDOX models (Fig. 8f, g).

These findings confirm that the antitumor effects of ICFe@D observed in organoids are reproducible in vivo using PDOX models. This reinforces its therapeutic efficacy, driven by ferroptosis induction, and underscores its potential as a promising candidate for clinical application in GBC therapy.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed ICFe@D, a multifunctional nanoparticle for the targeted treatment of GBC. This nanoparticle integrates the synergistic effects of ISL, Fe3+-derived Fe2+-driven Fenton reactions, and Ce6-based PDT to induce ferroptosis and apoptosis in tumor cells. It demonstrated enhanced cellular uptake and prolonged tumor retention under 666 nm laser irradiation. Mechanistically, ICFe@D orchestrated ferroptosis through a profound disruption of redox homeostasis: ISL-mediated hyperactivation of the p62/Keap1/Nrf2/HMOX1 pathway drove severe intracellular heme degradation, releasing endogenous labile iron. This endogenous iron synergized directly with the exogenous iron from ICFe@D, fueling massive ROS overproduction and LPO accumulation via the Fenton reaction. Concurrently, the depletion of GSH and downregulation of GPX4 completely collapsed the cellular antioxidant defenses. These effects were validated in vitro using GBC cell lines and PDOs, as well as in vivo models, where ICFe@D exhibited potent tumor-targeting capabilities, remarkable antitumor efficacy, and minimal systemic toxicity. Furthermore, ICFe@D possesses clear physiological clearance pathways to ensure long-term safety: released iron enters the endogenous pool for safe storage or utilization, while the DSPE-PEG matrix is effectively cleared via standard lipid metabolism and renal/hepatobiliary excretion. Notably, its robust therapeutic effects in PDOX models underscore its clinical translational potential. The ability of ICFe@D to synergistically induce ferroptosis and apoptosis provides a promising strategy to overcome the limitations of conventional therapies for GBC.

To successfully translate this therapeutic paradigm into the clinic, future research must address both nanoplatform optimization and the challenges of deep-tissue light delivery. On one hand, incorporating ROS-sensitive cleavable crosslinkers or advanced pH-responsive polymers could further ‘lock’ the nanoparticles during systemic circulation, ensuring a cascaded, accelerated payload release specifically within the TME. On the other hand, the deep anatomical location of GBC poses an inherent limitation for conventional PDT light penetration. In future clinical practice, ICFe@D could be integrated with Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). This minimally invasive technique would enable direct, localized laser irradiation within the biliary tract, overcoming tissue penetration depth limits and thereby maximizing the precise photodynamic therapeutic efficacy against GBC.

Beyond the direct synergistic cytotoxicity, the profound lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial collapse induced by ICFe@D hold potential implications for remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment. Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptotic cancer cells can release specific damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and lipid mediators, which may serve as triggers for immunogenic cell death (ICD). Although our current in vivo evaluations were purposefully conducted in immunodeficient PDOX models to faithfully preserve the patient-specific tumor architecture and heterogeneity, the extensive ferroptosis observed implies that ICFe@D might possess underlying immune-priming capabilities. We anticipate that future studies in immunocompetent models will be highly valuable to explore whether this ferroptosis-sensitizing nanomedicine can be integrated with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, aiming to overcome the immunosuppressive microenvironment and elicit durable systemic antitumor immunity in GBC.
