Risk factors for preterm birth and prolonged pregnancy following cervical cerclage in women with cervical insufficiency: a retrospective analysis and predictive model
Authors: Jing Wang, Mengmeng Wu, Huijuan Rong, Guoyuan Shi, Huixia Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical insufficiency is one of the major risk factors for preterm birth. The efficacy of cervical cerclage (CC) in improving pregnancy outcomes remains controversial. This study aimed to compare obstetric outcomes between patients with cervical insufficiency who underwent CC and those who did not to determine the optimal cutoff point for preterm birth on the basis of fetal survival outcomes and to identify risk factors influencing preterm birth. Furthermore, we sought to analyze the determinants of pregnancy prolongation after CC and to develop predictive models using pregnancy prolongation as the endpoint.MethodsPatients who underwent CC intervention and those who declined it, were included. Following 1:1 propensity score matching, the duration from cervical insufficiency diagnosis to delivery and fetal survival outcomes were compared. On the basis of fetal survival status, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified 30.5 weeks as an exploratory cutoff for discriminating fetal survival within this cohort. Using pregnancy prolongation after CC as a continuous endpoint, linear regression analysis was conducted, and nomograms were constructed to assess predictive performance.Results133 pregnant women with cervical insufficiency who underwent CC were included and matched with 133 women who did not receive the procedure. CC significantly prolonged the gestational duration (P < 0.01) but did not improve fetal survival (P = 0.45). The neutrophil percentage was significantly greater in mothers who delivered preterm at the 30.5-week cutoff (P = 0.022), and this difference persisted at 32 and 37 weeks. Multivariate analysis indicated that both neutrophil and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were significantly associated with prolonged pregnancy (P < 0.05 for both). The nomogram model, which uses prolonged pregnancy as the endpoint, demonstrated good predictive performance (R²=0.49).ConclusionCerclage performance was associated with a longer gestational duration but was not significantly related to fetal survival outcomes. Inflammatory markers such as the neutrophil count and CRP level may help predict preterm labor and pregnancy prolongation following CC. The nomogram model based on preoperative characteristics demonstrated accurate predictive ability for fetal retention time. However, the ROC-derived gestational threshold and predictive models should be regarded strictly as exploratory, proof-of-concept findings requiring mandatory external validation before any clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09012-5.Keywords: Cervical cerclage, Preterm birth, Cervical insufficiency, Retrospective, Prediction model

Introduction

Premature birth (before 37 weeks of gestation) is the leading cause of neonatal death in those under five years of age and accounts for approximately 9.9% of all births worldwide [1, 2]. Cervical insufficiency is a recognized risk factor for preterm birth and is characterized by painless cervical dilatation with prolapse of the lower pole of the gestational sac into the vagina. This condition reflects a structural defect of the cervix that prevents maintenance of a full-term pregnancy [3–5].

Cervical cerclage (CC) has been used to treat cervical insufficiency to prevent preterm delivery, extend the pregnancy period, and improve pregnancy outcomes [6, 7]. International guidelines recommend that if there are no signs of bleeding or infection, women with cervical dilatation and membrane prolapse during 16–28 weeks of pregnancy should consider emergency or salvage CC to prevent preterm delivery or uterine activity [8]. Owing to the need for suturing around the remaining cervix, the CC procedure carries the risk of complications such as rupture of membranes, maternal or fetal infection, sepsis, cervical trauma, and clinical deterioration, and is contraindicated in conditions such as placenta previa [8].

A systematic review concluded that, compared with expectant management, emergency CC can significantly prolong the gestational age and improve the neonatal survival rate [9]. Moreover, both laparoscopic and open CCs have positive effects on pregnancy preservation [10]. The results of a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) using individual patient-level data revealed that CC did not reduce or increase the preterm birth rate in singleton pregnancies with short cervical lengths after 24 weeks of gestation [11]. The results of a previous RCT-based meta-analysis revealed that the role of CC in preventing miscarriage and preterm birth in women with a short cervix on ultrasound remains uncertain [12].

The effectiveness of CC in improving pregnancy outcomes among women with cervical insufficiency remains controversial. This controversy primarily stems from variations in surgical indications, definitions of outcomes, and risk stratification criteria [13–15]. Our recent meta-analysis identified physical examination findings, cervical length, cervical dilatation, and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels as key risk factors for preterm birth after CC and further indicated that differences in defining preterm birth thresholds may substantially influence study conclusions [16].

The threshold defining preterm birth varies considerably across studies, with commonly used gestational cutoffs at 24, 28, 30, 32, 35, and 37 weeks [16]. Such heterogeneity may introduce selection bias in reported outcomes and contribute to inconsistencies across studies. We posit that variations in the optimal cutoff among studies primarily reflect differences in healthcare capacity and neonatal care quality. As medical standards advance, the survival rate of premature infants continues to improve, leading institutions to redefine the gestational threshold for preterm birth progressively earlier. Accordingly, our study aims to address the variability in preterm birth classification that arises under differing clinical conditions. The localized cutoff for preterm birth was derived by integrating local delivery timing data with corresponding fetal survival outcomes. This approach also allows for personalized and dynamic determination of preterm birth thresholds, tailored to the underlying clinical characteristics of different patient populations.

Accordingly, this study first compared obstetric outcomes between women with cervical insufficiency who received CC and those who did not and then employed the fetal survival curve derived from our hospital data to determine a “localized” cutoff, replacing traditional fixed gestational age thresholds, to identify risk factors associated with preterm birth. Finally, using the interval from surgery to delivery as a continuous endpoint, a nomogram prediction model for postoperative fetal retention time (the cerclage-to-delivery interval) was constructed to provide a quantitative framework for CC decision-making and postoperative risk stratification. In addition, pregnant women who have undergone trachelectomy for cervical cancer are recognized as a high-risk population for cervical insufficiency and should be screened as early as possible [17, 18]. This group warrants separate investigation and was therefore excluded from the present analysis.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shijiazhuang Fourth Hospital (approval no. 20230061). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Pregnant women who were diagnosed with cervical insufficiency and hospitalized at Shijiazhuang Fourth Hospital between November 2021 and March 2023 were retrospectively enrolled. The participants included patients who underwent McDonald’s CC during the study period and those who did not undergo CC due to personal preference. A study flowchart is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Diagnostic criteria for cervical insufficiency

The diagnosis of cervical insufficiency was based on medical history, gynecological signs and transvaginal ultrasound. The diagnostic criteria included the following: 1, a history of 2 or more painless second- and third-trimester miscarriages or premature deliveries. 2, There are old lacerations or surgical scars on the cervix, and the cervical canal is painlessly softened, shortened or even dilated. 3, Transvaginal ultrasonography reveals a cervical canal length of less than 2.5 cm from 16 to 24 weeks of gestation, separation of the cervical intima, or wedge-shaped or funnel-shaped changes in the cervical intima. Two investigators (JW and MW) applied standardized criteria for diagnostic review and screening for cervical insufficiency. In cases of diagnostic disagreement, resolution and final adjudication were conducted by the corresponding author (HY) to ensure consistency within our cohort. We acknowledge that cervical insufficiency remains a partially clinical diagnosis without universally standardized diagnostic criteria. Although we applied predefined institutional criteria and independent review to enhance internal consistency, inter-center variability in diagnostic thresholds—particularly regarding cervical length cutoffs, degree of dilation, and interpretation of historical obstetric events—may influence case classification across different clinical settings.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1, pregnant woman who met the diagnosis of cervical insufficiency (based on medical history, gynecological signs, and transvaginal ultrasound, as detailed above) and who received or did not receive CC treatment; 2, singleton pregnancy; and 3, complete clinical medical records, including demographic data, laboratory and ultrasound findings, and pregnancy outcomes. The exclusion criteria included the following: 1, positive for human immunodeficiency virus; 2, history of cold knife conization; 3, congenital uterine malformation; 4, confirmed chorioamnionitis or intrauterine infection prior to surgery; 5, severe pregnancy complications (e.g., placental abruption, diabetic ketoacidosis); 6, severe organ dysfunction (e.g., cardiac, hepatic, or renal failure); 7, fetal congenital or chromosomal abnormalities requiring termination of pregnancy; and 8, patients who were lost to follow-up or whose medical records were incomplete.

Data sources

Patient data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and operating room logs, with a focus on the following basic characteristics: admission body mass index (BMI), weight, gravida/para/abortions/living-children (GPAL) history, comorbid diagnoses (e.g., scarred uterus, hypothyroidism, polycystic ovary syndrome, diabetes, anemia), gestational age at the time of CC, cervical length and dilation status (measured via transvaginal ultrasound, cervical morphology was classified as U-, V-, or Y-shaped based on the dilation pattern of the internal cervical os), results of vaginal secretion culture(performed within 3 days of admission, identifying pathogenic organisms such as Group B Streptococcus and Escherichia coli), preoperative routine blood tests (e.g., peripheral white blood cell count (WBC), CRP) and electrolyte tests. For patients undergoing CC, blood samples were collected 1–2 days prior to surgery. Therefore, inflammatory biomarkers included in the present analysis reflect preoperative laboratory status obtained shortly before cerclage placement and prior to mechanical cervical reinforcement. For patients who did not undergo cerclage, laboratory indices were collected at the time of diagnosis and hospital admission. Consequently, biomarker levels represent baseline clinical status at presentation rather than post-intervention inflammatory responses. All samples were obtained under comparable clinical conditions, irrespective of patients’ symptomatic status. For patients who did not undergo CC, routine blood test results were collected at the time of hospital admission. The McDonald CC procedure is shown in the supplementary file. Patients with cervical insufficiency who did not receive CC intervention during the same period were selected for the extraction of the above information. The preoperative indicators mentioned above were substituted with laboratory indicators collected at the time of the cervical insufficiency diagnosis.

Pregnancy outcome assessment

Fetal delivery status and timing after CC were recorded. Outcome measures were defined as follows: Stillbirth: Fetal death occurring between CC surgery and delivery. Preterm birth: Delivery occurring before a specified gestational age. Outcomes were classified according to gestational age thresholds at 28, 32, and 37 weeks and cutoff values on the basis of child outcomes. The cerclage-to-delivery interval was defined as the time from completion of the CC procedure (recorded at the end of surgery) to pregnancy termination, whether iatrogenic or spontaneous, reflecting how effectively cerclage prolonged gestation and potentially improved perinatal outcomes.

Sample size calculation

Based on institutional clinical records, the incidence of delivery before 32 weeks of gestation was approximately 30% among patients who received CC and 45% among those who did not. Sample size estimation was performed via G*Power version 3.1.9, with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power (1 − β) of 0.80. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 128 participants.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared via the independent-samples t test. Continuous variables not conforming to a normal distribution are expressed as medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs) and were compared via the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages) and were compared via Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

A 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) approach was applied to construct a control group comparable to the cerclage group among women diagnosed with cervical insufficiency who did not undergo the procedure. The matching covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included maternal age, obstetric history (gravida, para, abortion history, living children), cervical characteristics (cervical length, dilation status, and ultrasound morphology), and non-inflammatory laboratory parameters obtained at admission (e.g., hemoglobin, electrolytes including sodium, potassium, and calcium). In the present cohort, 133 patients underwent cerclage and 133 did not, yielding 266 observations available for propensity score estimation. A total of 10 covariates were included in the propensity model. Because propensity score modeling predicts treatment allocation rather than outcome events, conventional outcome-based “events-per-variable” rules are not directly applicable. Nevertheless, the sample size relative to the number of covariates (approximately 26 observations per variable) was considered adequate to support stable estimation of the propensity scores and to minimize model overfitting.

Inflammatory markers—specifically C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil percentage, white blood cell count (WBC), and procalcitonin (PCT)—were intentionally excluded from the propensity score model. Although these markers were available at baseline, they may reflect acute inflammatory status at admission or perioperative conditions rather than stable pre-treatment characteristics influencing the initial decision to perform cerclage. Furthermore, from a causal inference perspective, inflammatory activation may lie on the potential causal pathway between cervical insufficiency severity and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Adjusting for such variables in the matching process could introduce overadjustment bias by controlling for potential mediators rather than true confounders. Therefore, these markers were excluded from matching and were instead analyzed separately in regression models to explore their prognostic associations with pregnancy prolongation and preterm birth. Detailed data on key covariates before and after PSM are provided in Supplementary Table 1. To assess the impact of CC on gestational age at delivery and fetal survival outcomes, Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were generated. Separate KM models were constructed for gestational duration (delivery as the event) and fetal survival (stillbirth as the event) to reflect distinct clinical endpoints. When gestational duration was analyzed, delivery was defined as the event, and ongoing pregnancy was considered survival (event-free status). When fetal survival was analyzed, fetal death was defined as the event, and continued fetal viability was considered survival. These definitions were applied consistently in all survival analyses to avoid conceptual ambiguity. We tested the proportional hazards assumption using the Schoenfeld residuals test.

Using fetal death as the outcome indicator, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal time point for defining preterm birth under local conditions. Given the limited number of fetal death events and the retrospective nature of the cohort, this ROC-derived threshold was considered exploratory and subject to potential overfitting and optimism bias. Accordingly, the analysis was performed for descriptive and hypothesis-generating purposes rather than to establish a clinically actionable cutoff. Using this cutoff as the main criterion, outcomes between groups were compared, and potential risk factors were analyzed via univariate and multivariate analyses. Patients who undergo CC inherently face a greater risk of preterm birth. The optimal cutoff point—the ROC-derived optimal threshold for predicting fetal death—was identified as the threshold that best discriminates between fetal survival and fetal death. This value corresponds to the gestational age yielding the highest combined sensitivity and specificity for predicting fetal survival outcomes. The purpose of this analysis was not only to identify an average gestational duration but also to determine the time threshold that most effectively differentiates fetal survival from fetal death. Importantly, this threshold is not fixed and may vary across populations with different demographic or healthcare characteristics. Additionally, preterm delivery thresholds at 28, 32, and 37 weeks, as commonly reported in previous studies, were also analyzed and compared between groups.

This study also examined the cerclage-to-delivery interval. Correlation analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between the cerclage-to-delivery interval and maternal characteristics. Univariate and multivariate linear regression models were subsequently applied to identify risk factors associated with the cerclage-to-delivery interval, and a nomogram model was constructed to evaluate the agreement between the predicted and observed outcomes. Bootstrap optimization and 10-fold cross-validation were used to internally validate model robustness and assess potential variable collinearity. Prior to model construction, key assumptions of linear regression were formally evaluated. Linearity between continuous predictors and the cerclage-to-delivery interval was assessed using scatter plots and partial residual plots. No substantial deviations from linearity or homoscedasticity were observed, and residual distributions were considered acceptable for regression modeling. This study included only participants with complete clinical data, and a complete-case analysis was performed without imputation. Model performance was evaluated in terms of both discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was quantified using the coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE). Calibration was assessed primarily through visual inspection of calibration plots comparing predicted and observed cerclage-to-delivery intervals, as well as examination of residual distributions. Formal statistical goodness-of-fit tests were not performed, given the continuous outcome framework and exploratory nature of the model. Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling and 10-fold cross-validation to assess model stability and to reduce optimism bias. All performance metrics derived from these procedures reflect internal validation within the derivation cohort and do not constitute external validation. All the statistical analyses were conducted via R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For clarity, we distinguished between exploratory and confirmatory analyses. Confirmatory analyses included comparisons of gestational duration and fetal survival between the CC and non-CC groups after PSM, as well as multivariate regression analyses to identify independent risk factors for preterm birth and for the cerclage-to-delivery interval. These were predefined study objectives. In contrast, exploratory analyses encompassed the ROC-derived preterm birth threshold, subgroup analyses stratified by gestational age (28, 32, and 37 weeks), and the construction of predictive nomogram models.

Results

Comparisons between CCs and non-CCs

A total of 133 pregnant women who were diagnosed with cervical insufficiency and who underwent CC were included in this study. A 1:1 PSM approach was applied to select patients with cervical insufficiency who did not undergo CC as the control group. The baseline characteristics were not significantly different between the two groups (Table 1). In particular, cervical dilation status at presentation, cervical length, ultrasound morphology (U-, V-, and Y-shape), and the rate of positive vaginal secretion cultures were comparable between groups after matching (all P > 0.05). Standardized mean differences for all matched covariates were below conventional thresholds for meaningful imbalance, indicating adequate covariate balance following PSM (Table 1). Before and after PSM, the distributions of key baseline demographic, obstetric, and clinical variables were comparable (Supplementary Table 1), which shows no statistically meaningful residual differences across matched covariates.

KM curves were generated for both groups to evaluate two distinct time-to-event outcomes. In the first analysis (gestational-duration analysis), delivery—regardless of neonatal status—was defined as the event, and ongoing pregnancy was considered event-free survival. In the second analysis (fetal survival analysis), stillbirth was defined as the event, and continued fetal viability was considered survival. Although both analyses are presented as KM curves, they address conceptually different endpoints: one reflects time to delivery (gestational prolongation), whereas the other reflects time to fetal death (fetal survival). These definitions were applied consistently to avoid conceptual ambiguity. Thus, separation of curves in the gestational-duration analysis reflects differences in timing of delivery rather than intrinsic fetal survival probability. Accordingly, separation of the curves primarily reflects differences in event timing, particularly gestational prolongation, rather than a direct causal effect of CC on intrinsic fetal viability. The results indicated that when delivery was used as the endpoint, a significant difference in gestational duration was observed between the two groups (p < 0.001; Schoenfeld residuals test: p = 0.091). However, when stillbirth was used as the outcome, no significant difference was observed between the groups (p = 0.45; Schoenfeld residuals test: p = 0.060) (Fig. 1). For clarity, the term “survival” in these models refers strictly to the absence of the defined event and does not imply equivalence between gestational prolongation and fetal survival outcomes.

Comparisons using each gestational cutoff

Because the optimal cutoff point for defining preterm birth remains controversial, this study determined the ROC-derived optimal threshold for predicting fetal death. ROC curve analysis identified 30.5 weeks (213.5 days) as the optimal cutoff for discriminating fetal survival status within this cohort. This analysis was based on 18 fetal death events among 133 patients who underwent CC. At this threshold, the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.989, with a sensitivity of 0.922 and a specificity of 1.000. However, given the limited number of outcome events relative to the evaluated time points, this high discriminatory performance may reflect overfitting, optimism bias, or quasi-complete separation inherent to small-event retrospective analyses. Therefore, the ROC-derived cutoff should be interpreted strictly as an exploratory, cohort-specific finding and not as a clinically actionable gestational age threshold. External validation in independent populations is essential before any consideration of clinical implementation (Supplementary Fig. 2). ROC curve analysis identified 30.5 weeks as the optimal cutoff for discriminating fetal survival status in this cohort; however, this threshold should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory, data-driven finding derived from a limited number of fetal death events. All fetuses delivered after 30.5 weeks survived, whereas among those delivered before this threshold, 9 survived and 18 died. Patients were stratified into two groups according to this cutoff, and intergroup differences were analyzed. A significant difference was observed in the percentage of neutrophils (neutrophil [%]) between the groups, with higher values among mothers who delivered preterm (p = 0.022) (Table 2). Notably, this cohort specific, data-driven threshold is exploratory in nature and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Consequently, it was not suitable for guiding clinical decision-making until it was validated in independent populations.

Predictors of prolonged pregnancy after CC

We defined the cerclage-to-delivery interval as an additional reference outcome. Correlation analysis suggested that factors such as the timing of surgery and the presence of a short cervix were associated with the cerclage-to-delivery interval (Fig. 2). Furthermore, a scatter plot was constructed to illustrate the relationship between surgical timing and the cerclage-to-delivery interval. Among surviving fetuses, a significant negative correlation was observed between surgical timing and the cerclage-to-delivery interval (Fig. 3). Additionally, a nomogram prediction model incorporating multiple factors was developed to estimate the cerclage-to-delivery interval. Three versions were evaluated: one including the timing of CC surgery (Fig. 4, A), one excluding it (Fig. 4, B), and one comprising only variables significant in multivariate regression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3). The results indicated that the R² values of the three linear regression models were 0.49 (0.425 after adjustment), 0.45 (0.385 after adjustment), and 0.101 (0.087 after adjustment). The residual plots and internal calibration plots for each model are presented in Supplementary Figs. 4–9. Visual inspection of the calibration plots demonstrated reasonable agreement between predicted and observed values within the derivation cohort. However, these findings reflect internal validation only and do not establish external generalizability. These findings support the appropriateness of the linear regression framework for modeling the cerclage-to-delivery interval within this cohort. The distributions of the MAE and RMSE obtained through 10-fold cross-validation for the three models are provided in Supplementary Table 6. The results of the multicollinearity analysis, expressed as variance inflation factors (VIFs), are presented in Supplementary Table 7, confirming the absence of significant collinearity and supporting model robustness. Details of the univariate and multivariate models are presented in Supplementary Table 8. Multivariate regression further demonstrated that the neutrophil percentage and CRP level were significantly associated with the cerclage-to-delivery interval.

Discussion

This study retrospectively analyzed the obstetric outcomes of patients with cervical insufficiency who either underwent or did not undergo CC. Following expert recommendations, we clarified the rationale underlying the study’s main findings. First, the 30.5-week threshold is a strictly data-driven, exploratory finding derived from our single-center retrospective cohort, rather than a clinically validated standard. Although the ROC analysis demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance for fetal survival at this gestational age threshold (AUC 0.989), this cutoff should be interpreted strictly as an exploratory, data-driven finding. The apparently high discriminatory performance may partially reflect overfitting in a small-event setting, reinforcing the need for cautious interpretation. It is not intended to represent a clinically actionable or prescriptive gestational age threshold. Rather, this observation should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires validation in independent cohorts with different clinical settings, patient populations, and levels of neonatal care before any potential clinical application can be considered. Its primary value is hypothesis generation. It provides a preliminary framework for re-evaluating preterm birth thresholds in relation to local neonatal care capacity rather than serving as clinical guidance. We explicitly emphasize that this ROC-derived gestational threshold should not be used to guide clinical decision-making, determine intervention timing, or counsel individual patients regarding fetal prognosis at this stage. Any application beyond exploratory research would be premature without robust external validation in larger, independent, and preferably multicenter cohorts. Although the AUC was high (0.989) for distinguishing fetal survival, the clinical significance of this threshold remains limited by several factors: (1) strong context dependency, as it reflects our institution’s neonatal intensive care capabilities and may not apply to centers with different resources; (2) absence of external validation, leaving reproducibility and generalizability unconfirmed; and (3) potential retrospective bias, including unmeasured confounders such as subclinical infection. Consequently, this threshold cannot currently guide clinical decision-making regarding intervention timing or individual fetal prognosis. In addition to its single-center and retrospective design, this analysis is susceptible to optimism bias due to the small number of fetal death events relative to the number of evaluated time points. Specifically, only 18 fetal death events were available for ROC modeling in the cerclage cohort, which limits the stability of the derived threshold and increases the likelihood of model overfitting. In small-event settings, even minor variations in event timing may substantially influence ROC performance metrics, potentially inflating the apparent discriminatory ability. As such, the apparent discriminatory performance of the 30.5-week threshold may be overestimated and requires confirmation in independent cohorts. Despite limited immediate clinical utility, the 30.5-week threshold provides meaningful research direction by underscoring the importance of developing ‘localized preterm birth definitions’ tailored to regional healthcare capacities, thereby challenging universal, one-size-fits-all cutoffs. Second, although an association exists between inflammatory markers and preterm delivery or pregnancy prolongation, causality cannot be inferred. The lack of standardized timing for laboratory measurements (relative to admission, symptoms, or surgery) represents a potential source of bias. These biological indicators may fluctuate across different stages of disease progression and clinical management, thereby influencing the observed associations. Accordingly, we caution that these biomarkers should be regarded as associative indicators rather than predictive tools for clinical application. Third, both the predictive and nomogram models should be considered proof-of-concept frameworks intended to guide future research, rather than instruments for immediate clinical application. Fourth, as a single-center study, the external validity of our findings is inherently limited by local clinical characteristics, and reproducibility across different regions may be influenced by several factors: (1) Differences in patient populations: The study cohort was drawn primarily from Hebei, China, where demographic characteristics and etiological patterns of cervical insufficiency may differ from those in other regions; (2) Variations in clinical practice: Heterogeneity in surgical timing and neonatal care levels among institutions may influence the applicability of the 30.5-week preterm birth threshold and reduce the predictive model’s generalizability; (3) Differences in laboratory standards: Variability in testing methods, reference ranges, and timing of sample collection (e.g., preoperative blood sampling) for inflammatory markers such as neutrophil percentage and CRP may also affect result comparability. These differences highlight the need for external validation through multicenter studies conducted in diverse clinical settings. Readers are encouraged to interpret the present findings with these contextual limitations in mind. In particular, the combination of a very small number of fetal death events and near-perfect classification at the derived threshold raises the possibility of quasi-complete separation, which can artificially inflate AUC estimates in retrospective ROC modeling. Under such conditions, performance metrics may appear exceptionally high despite limited generalizability. Accordingly, this threshold should not be interpreted as defining a biological boundary of fetal viability or as a prescriptive clinical decision point. Rather, it represents a data-driven observation within a specific cohort and healthcare context, requiring validation in larger, independent, and preferably multicenter studies before any translational consideration. Given the limited number of fetal death events relative to evaluated gestational time points, the ROC-derived cutoff is susceptible to model optimism and potential overfitting. The near-perfect discrimination observed in this cohort may therefore overestimate true performance in external populations.

Patients who underwent CC were categorized based on different preterm birth time points related to fetal survival. Furthermore, prolonged pregnancy after CC was used as the primary outcome to analyze relevant risk factors and develop a predictive model. These findings indicated that CC was associated with a longer cerclage-to-delivery interval but was not statistically associated with improved fetal survival in this cohort. Importantly, gestational prolongation should not be interpreted as clinically trivial solely because a statistically significant survival benefit was not detected. Even modest increases in gestational age may confer substantial neonatal advantages, particularly near viability thresholds where each additional week—or even days—of intrauterine development can significantly influence survival probability and reduce severe neonatal morbidity. However, this observation warrants careful interpretation. First, fetal death was a relatively rare outcome in this cohort, and the study was likely underpowered to detect modest differences in survival between groups. Second, the timing of cerclage placement may play a critical role. Interventions performed later in gestation or in the presence of advanced cervical dilation, membrane exposure, or early inflammatory activation may biologically limit the potential for survival benefit. When cervical remodeling has progressed substantially, the underlying processes driving preterm birth—such as extracellular matrix degradation, ascending microbial invasion, or cytokine-mediated uterine activation—may already be well established. Under these conditions, cerclage may still prolong gestation by providing mechanical support, yet the magnitude of prolongation may be insufficient to overcome pre-existing fetal compromise or to shift delivery beyond a clinically meaningful viability threshold. This gestational age–specific biological window may partially explain why prolongation did not uniformly translate into improved fetal survival in the matched cohort. Third, in extremely preterm gestations, competing risks—such as intrauterine infection, placental dysfunction, or fetal compromise—may limit the extent to which gestational prolongation alone can translate into improved survival. Fourth, fetal survival is influenced by multiple factors beyond gestational age alone, including intrauterine infection, placental pathology, fetal condition, and the quality of neonatal care. Together, these factors may explain why an apparent benefit in pregnancy prolongation did not result in a statistically significant survival advantage. An additional consideration relates to gestational age–specific viability thresholds. In extremely early gestations (e.g., < 24–26 weeks), even modest prolongation of pregnancy may not necessarily translate into measurable survival benefit if delivery still occurs below the threshold of neonatal viability or within a gestational window associated with high mortality despite intensive care. Conversely, in later preterm periods, incremental prolongation may reduce neonatal morbidity without substantially altering survival probability, particularly in tertiary centers where baseline survival rates are already high. Furthermore, subclinical intrauterine infection or inflammatory activation may persist despite mechanical cervical reinforcement, thereby limiting the survival benefit of pregnancy prolongation. In such cases, cerclage may delay delivery without fully reversing the underlying biological processes contributing to fetal compromise. These gestational age–dependent and pathophysiological factors may partially account for the observed dissociation between prolonged gestation and fetal survival in the present cohort. From a mechanistic perspective, the dissociation between gestational prolongation and fetal survival likely reflects the multifactorial nature of fetal compromise in cervical insufficiency. Cerclage primarily provides mechanical reinforcement of the cervix; however, fetal survival is determined not only by gestational duration but also by the intrauterine biological environment. Subclinical intrauterine infection or sterile inflammatory activation may persist despite mechanical cervical closure. In such cases, cerclage may delay delivery without fully reversing inflammatory-mediated fetal injury, placental dysfunction, or cytokine-driven fetal stress. Moreover, if cerclage is performed at a relatively advanced stage of cervical remodeling or inflammatory activation, the biological trajectory toward adverse outcome may already be established. Therefore, prolongation achieved under these circumstances may shift the timing of delivery without substantially modifying the underlying pathophysiology that contributes to fetal demise.

In addition, residual confounding inherent to observational studies may contribute to this finding. Although propensity score matching balanced measured clinical covariates, unmeasured factors—such as subtle differences in inflammatory burden, microbial colonization patterns, physician judgment regarding disease severity, or patient-level biological heterogeneity—may have influenced both the likelihood of cerclage placement and subsequent survival outcomes. Consequently, the absence of a statistically significant survival benefit should be interpreted as reflecting complex biological and methodological interactions rather than as evidence that gestational prolongation lacks clinical relevance.

In particular, subclinical intrauterine inflammation or occult infection may represent a central unmeasured biological modifier influencing both the effectiveness of cerclage and fetal survival outcomes. Even if cerclage mechanically prolongs gestation, persistent inflammatory activation may independently impair fetal viability, increase susceptibility to infection-related complications, or precipitate adverse neonatal outcomes. Consequently, pregnancy prolongation achieved in the context of ongoing inflammatory processes may not necessarily translate into improved survival. Because inflammatory markers were not incorporated into the matching algorithm, residual differences in baseline inflammatory burden between groups may have attenuated the observed survival effect. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant survival benefit does not necessarily imply a lack of clinical value of gestational prolongation, particularly given the multifactorial nature of fetal death and the limited number of outcome events in this study. Importantly, the clinical significance of gestational prolongation is highly dependent on the specific gestational window in which it occurs. In extremely preterm periods (e.g., 23–26 weeks), even a prolongation of several days may substantially improve neonatal survival probability and reduce severe morbidity, particularly in centers with advanced neonatal intensive care capabilities. In contrast, at later gestational ages (e.g., 28–32 weeks), incremental prolongation may primarily reduce complications such as respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage, or long-term neurodevelopmental impairment rather than dramatically altering survival rates. Therefore, the clinical benefit of cerclage-related pregnancy prolongation cannot be interpreted uniformly across all gestational ages and should be contextualized according to local viability thresholds, neonatal care capacity, and patient-specific risk profiles. The absence of statistical significance may also reflect limited statistical power. Stillbirth is a rare outcome in current clinical practice, which limits the ability to detect differences between groups. In the present cohort, the stillbirth rates were 11% and 14% in the cerclage and noncerclage groups, respectively. A power calculation (α = 0.05, 1–β = 0.80) suggests that about 1,500 cases per group would be required to achieve statistical significance. The estimated number needed to treat (NNT) suggests that, statistically, one additional fetal survival might occur for every 34 patients treated with CC. However, this estimate does not reflect a confirmed clinical effect. We reiterate that this study may be underpowered to detect differences in rare outcomes, such as fetal death. The estimated NNT is illustrative and hypothetical, and should not be interpreted as a clinically applicable indicator.

According to local fetal survival statistics, 30.5 weeks was selected as the cutoff point to describe preterm birth outcomes. Importantly, this threshold is highly cohort-specific and subject to optimism bias due to the small number of fetal death events, and it should not be interpreted as a clinically actionable gestational age threshold without external validation. The excellent discriminatory performance observed in the ROC analysis may therefore reflect overfitting, underscoring the need for validation in independent, larger cohorts before any clinical interpretation. The results revealed that the maternal neutrophil percentage was significantly elevated in cases of preterm birth. This trend persisted in analyses using 32- and 37-week thresholds, with neutrophil percentages remaining significantly associated with preterm birth. The cerclage-to-delivery interval was associated with several maternal baseline characteristics. The nomogram prediction model constructed in this study demonstrated good predictive performance for estimating the cerclage-to-delivery interval within the derivation cohort. However, this performance reflects internal validation only and does not imply clinical readiness. External validation in independent cohorts is essential before any consideration of clinical implementation. Accordingly, the present model should be regarded as a proof-of-concept decision-support framework intended to generate hypotheses and guide future prospective validation, rather than as a tool for direct clinical decision-making.

At present, multiple perspectives remain regarding the clinical utility of CCs. A recent evidence-based study demonstrated that CC reduces the risk of perinatal miscarriage, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and preterm delivery in women with twin pregnancies and a short or dilated cervix. However, a proportion of stillborn and preterm delivery cases persist [19]. The findings of the present study indicate that, among patients with cervical insufficiency, CC is beneficial for prolonging pregnancy duration. Although the fetal survival rate was relatively low, the difference did not reach statistical significance. Nevertheless, the retrospective design of this study introduces inherent bias in CC intervention, and the level of evidence is consequently lower than that of evidence-based prospective research. A recent retrospective study examining pregnancies with a sonographically measured cervical length ≤ 25 mm and no prior preterm birth reported that cerclage did not significantly affect clinical outcomes. The authors therefore concluded that ultrasound-guided cerclage should be used with caution in low-risk women [20]. This finding underscores the importance of analyzing risk factors and implementing patient risk stratification.

The results of this study indicate that higher neutrophil percentages and elevated CRP levels were statistically associated with shorter gestational duration after cerclage. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not imply causality. Elevated inflammatory markers may reflect several non-mutually exclusive biological processes, including subclinical intrauterine infection, systemic inflammatory activation, stress-related leukocyte redistribution, or advanced cervical remodeling. Because these mechanisms cannot be distinguished within the present retrospective dataset, the biomarkers should be regarded as nonspecific indicators of inflammatory or biological activation rather than mechanistic determinants of cerclage failure. Erkan Sağlam and colleagues conducted a study analyzing the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), a neutrophil-related marker of systemic inflammation, to examine its associations with gestational age, birth weight, and the Apgar score. These findings suggest that markers reflecting systemic inflammatory status may serve as potential predictors of preterm birth and adverse neonatal outcomes [21]. H Minakami et al. reported the relationship between preoperative CRP and WBC levels and the efficacy of CC emergence. These results suggest that CRP is associated with preterm delivery. However, the sample size was also small [22]. Li-Qiong Zhu et al. also retrospectively analyzed the risk factors for pregnancy outcomes after emergency CC and reported that the CRP level was associated with poor pregnancy outcomes [23]. In Ying-Yu Chen’s study, CRP was significantly associated with the risk of preterm delivery and low birth weight [24]. However, K Taghavi et al. reported that increased serum CRP was not significantly associated with preterm delivery in patients with prophylactic CC [25]. Therefore, the relationships between neutrophil and CRP levels and pregnancy outcomes remain controversial. This inconsistency may partly stem from variations in sampling time points, including differences in blood collection before and after surgery and in the timing of vaginal culture acquisition. Given the retrospective design and the lack of standardized timing for laboratory assessments, these markers should be interpreted as associative rather than predictive. From a pathophysiological perspective, the association between neutrophil percentage, CRP levels, and the cerclage-to-delivery interval is biologically plausible. Cervical insufficiency and spontaneous preterm birth are increasingly understood within the framework of inflammatory-mediated cervical remodeling. Subclinical intrauterine or systemic inflammatory activation can stimulate the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, which promote extracellular matrix degradation, collagen disorganization, and premature cervical softening. Neutrophil activation represents an early component of this inflammatory cascade, contributing to protease release and local tissue remodeling. Elevated CRP levels may reflect systemic inflammatory signaling secondary to localized intrauterine processes. In this context, heightened inflammatory activity may compromise the mechanical integrity of the cervix and attenuate the effectiveness of cerclage in maintaining pregnancy. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted as reflective of an inflammatory milieu associated with adverse outcomes rather than evidence of direct causality, as the retrospective design precludes mechanistic inference. Inflammatory activation can compromise the structural integrity of cervical connective tissue, stimulate uterine contractility, and consequently shorten the duration of pregnancy. However, causality cannot be inferred from the present retrospective design. Elevated inflammatory markers may reflect an underlying inflammatory milieu or subclinical infection rather than acting as direct drivers of cerclage failure. Such inflammatory states could serve as markers of disease severity or ongoing pathological processes that predispose to preterm delivery, rather than mechanistic determinants themselves. It should be noted that several potential confounding factors—such as subclinical infection (e.g., undiagnosed chorioamnionitis), variability in the timing of laboratory assessment (1–2 days before surgery or at admission), and intercurrent conditions (e.g., diabetes)—may influence the strength of the observed association between inflammatory markers and clinical outcomes. Further prospective studies with standardized timing of laboratory assessments and careful control for potential confounders are warranted to validate these findings.

In addition to neutrophil percentage and CRP, other inflammatory biomarkers may also show statistical associations with pregnancy outcomes. For example, WBC count, PCT level, and positive vaginal secretion culture results were also statistically associated with preterm birth before 37 weeks of gestation. We emphasized that these findings represent statistical correlations rather than causal relationships, and that the observed associations likely reflect an underlying inflammatory milieu rather than direct causal mechanisms. Studies have suggested that interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, interleukin-8, and tumor necrosis factor alpha might be related to preterm delivery [26–28]. A proteomic study by Kisoon Dan et al. revealed that latexin is related to fetal outcomes in patients with singleton pregnancies who received rescue CC [29]. Therefore, Yeon Kim et al. reported that the plasma inflammatory factors IGFBP-3, MIP-1α, and S100A8/A9 can be used as noninvasive independent biomarkers to identify spontaneous preterm birth risk in women with cervical insufficiency after salvage CC [30]. However, WBC and CRP are the most commonly used inflammatory biomarkers in clinical practice. In addition, whether antibiotics can provide clinical benefits to patients with high inflammation levels is worthy of further study [31]. Recent studies have also investigated risk factors for preterm birth following cerclage and identified postcerclage transvaginal ultrasound findings, progressive leukocytosis, cervical shortening, and intraamniotic sludge as predictive indicators for preterm birth before 34 weeks [32, 33].

This study developed a nomogram to predict the interval between the time of surgery and delivery. From a conceptual perspective, this nomogram provides a preliminary framework for individualized risk stratification in tertiary-care settings. By integrating readily available preoperative laboratory and clinical parameters, it may assist in estimating the expected duration of pregnancy prolongation following cerclage within the derivation cohort; however, all predictive performance metrics reported in this study—including R², MAE, RMSE, calibration plots, and cross-validation results—refer exclusively to the derivation cohort. Although internal validation techniques such as bootstrap resampling and 10-fold cross-validation were applied to reduce optimism bias, these approaches do not substitute for independent external validation. Decision curve analysis was not performed; therefore, the net clinical benefit of applying this model in real-world decision-making remains undetermined. Future prospective studies incorporating clinical utility assessment would be necessary to evaluate whether model-guided management meaningfully improves outcomes. Therefore, the reported performance reflects model behavior within the current dataset only and should not be interpreted as evidence of generalizability or transportability to other clinical settings or populations. For example, patients with shorter predicted retention intervals may warrant closer monitoring. However, such applications remain exploratory and require prospective validation. Conversely, patients with longer predicted intervals may be managed with standard surveillance protocols. Importantly, such implementation should be considered exploratory and supportive rather than directive, as the model has undergone internal validation only. To facilitate individualized prognostic assessment and support clinical decision-making, the postoperative fetal preservation time can be predicted via relevant pre- and post-CC risk factors, thereby stratifying patient risk levels. For pregnant women with a shorter predicted preservation time, more intensive monitoring and timely intervention are recommended, whereas those with a longer predicted preservation time may undergo a more conservative management approach to minimize patient burden and optimize medical resource utilization. Given the single-center retrospective design and absence of external validation, the nomogram should be regarded strictly as an internally derived, exploratory framework intended to support hypothesis generation and future validation efforts rather than immediate clinical implementation.

This study has the following limitations that need to be stated

Methodological limitations: First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and external validation could not be performed. Consequently, the external validity of these findings should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, cervical insufficiency lacks a universally accepted diagnostic standard and is often defined based on a combination of historical, clinical, and ultrasonographic findings. Differences in how institutions operationalize diagnostic criteria—such as the required number of prior second-trimester losses, cervical length thresholds, or the role of physical examination findings—may affect patient selection and baseline disease severity. Therefore, variations in diagnostic interpretation across centers could influence both the observed effect size of cerclage and the reproducibility of the predictive model. As this study was conducted under a specific clinical management protocol for CC—including perioperative antibiotic use, postoperative monitoring, and standardized patient selection criteria—the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Pregnancy prolongation and fetal survival outcomes may differ in settings with alternative cerclage techniques (e.g., Shirodkar vs. McDonald), perioperative management strategies, infection screening practices, or patient populations with distinct demographic or clinical characteristics. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to institutions with differing clinical protocols or healthcare resources. As a single-center study conducted within a specific healthcare system, the observed associations—including the ROC-derived preterm birth threshold and the predictive performance of inflammatory markers—are likely influenced by local clinical protocols, patient characteristics, and neonatal intensive care capacity. Specifically, this cohort was derived from a tertiary-care hospital in Hebei Province, China, and therefore reflects the demographic composition, obstetric risk profile, and healthcare delivery structure of this regional population. Ethnic background, baseline obstetric characteristics, access to prenatal care, and thresholds for neonatal intensive care intervention may differ substantially across countries and healthcare systems. Consequently, the observed gestational prolongation patterns, fetal survival rates, and inflammatory marker distributions may not be directly generalizable to populations with different ethnic compositions or healthcare infrastructures. In addition, perioperative management—including antibiotic administration, cervical monitoring protocols, hospitalization criteria, and neonatal resuscitation practices—followed institutional standards specific to our center. Variations in surgical technique, infection screening strategies, and postoperative surveillance intensity across institutions may influence both gestational prolongation and survival outcomes. Therefore, extrapolation of these findings to centers operating under different clinical protocols should be undertaken cautiously. Furthermore, because treatment allocation was based partly on patient preference rather than randomization, selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Decisions to undergo cerclage may have been influenced by socioeconomic status, health literacy, perceived disease severity, or counseling differences, which may vary across healthcare systems and cultural contexts. These contextual factors may limit transportability of the results. Consequently, while the general direction of the findings may be informative for similar tertiary-care settings, their direct applicability to institutions with different resource levels, patient populations, or management strategies may be limited, underscoring the need for external validation in diverse healthcare contexts. Second, systematic differences between women who underwent cerclage and those who did not remain a potential source of bias inherent to observational studies. Importantly, treatment allocation in this study was influenced by patient preference rather than randomization. Although propensity score matching balanced measured baseline characteristics, preference-based decision-making may reflect unmeasured factors such as perceived disease severity, risk tolerance, socioeconomic status, access to care, physician counseling style, and subtle clinical findings not fully captured in electronic records. These factors may be associated with both treatment selection and pregnancy outcomes. Consequently, residual and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded and may have attenuated or exaggerated the observed associations between cerclage and fetal survival. The non-randomized, preference-based allocation design therefore limits causal inference. Although PSM improved balance across measured covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Importantly, propensity score matching can only balance measured and included covariates and cannot account for unmeasured confounders or nuanced clinical decision-making factors that may have influenced the indication for cerclage placement. Of particular concern is residual biological heterogeneity related to subclinical infection, inflammatory profile nuances not fully captured by routine laboratory markers, as well as subtle clinical findings, physician perception of risk, and patient anxiety or preference, all of which may have influenced both treatment allocation and outcomes. Although routine laboratory markers (e.g., WBC, CRP, neutrophil percentage) were available, they do not fully capture intraamniotic inflammatory activity or occult infection. Such unmeasured inflammatory variation may have influenced both clinical decision-making regarding cerclage placement and subsequent outcomes. Therefore, even after PSM, differences in underlying inflammatory burden between groups may have persisted and partially contributed to the dissociation observed between gestational prolongation and fetal survival. Unmeasured or incompletely captured factors, such as subclinical infection, timing of laboratory assessments, or clinician judgment, may still have influenced the results. In addition, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, as cases in which CC declined or was not performed could have included a greater proportion of patients with less severe diseases. Moreover, the reasons for declining cerclage may reflect unmeasured clinical, psychological, or sociocultural factors that could have influenced the study outcomes. Among the women who received CC, some may have demonstrated a stronger motivation for diagnosis and treatment, greater disease awareness, and relatively higher socioeconomic status. These potential confounding factors may still have influenced the matching results. However, all patients were diagnosed with cervical incompetence, and conventional preoperative inflammatory markers and the expected date of delivery were not major determinants in the decision to undergo CC. Although inflammatory markers were intentionally excluded from the propensity score model to avoid adjustment for variables that may reflect acute inflammatory states or lie on the causal pathway, we acknowledge the possibility of residual confounding related to subclinical infection or inflammation. Given the well-established role of subclinical intrauterine infection and systemic inflammatory activation in the pathogenesis of cervical insufficiency and spontaneous preterm birth, exclusion of inflammatory markers from the PSM model may have allowed residual biological heterogeneity to persist between groups. Although this approach was conceptually intended to avoid overadjustment for potential mediators, it cannot eliminate the possibility that baseline inflammatory status influenced both the likelihood of cerclage placement and subsequent pregnancy outcomes. In particular, undetected or subclinical intrauterine or systemic inflammatory processes—insufficiently captured by routine laboratory tests or clinical diagnosis—may have influenced both the likelihood of cerclage placement and subsequent pregnancy outcomes. Such residual inflammatory confounding may also partly explain the apparent disconnect between gestational prolongation and fetal survival: occult intrauterine inflammation could independently compromise fetal viability regardless of gestational age at delivery, thereby attenuating the potential survival benefit of prolonged pregnancy. As a result, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded despite PSM, and the observed associations between inflammatory markers and pregnancy prolongation should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Causality cannot be inferred from the present retrospective design. In summary, the clinical decision to perform cerclage may be influenced by a range of subtle factors that are difficult to capture in existing electronic medical record data—such as physicians’ subjective clinical judgment, individual medical preferences, patients’ acceptance of surgical risks, and socioeconomic status. These factors cannot be fully controlled through PSM, potentially leading to residual intergroup differences and minor interference in the correlation analysis between cerclage and clinical outcomes. The decision to exclude inflammatory markers from the PSM model was based on the conceptual distinction between confounders and potential mediators; however, we acknowledge that this approach may also permit residual confounding if baseline inflammatory status influenced both treatment allocation and outcome. Third, the number of outcome events—particularly fetal death—was limited. This constraint is especially relevant for interpreting the ROC-derived cutoff and the multivariable predictive modeling, as small event counts increase the risk of overfitting and optimistic performance estimates. Accordingly, both the ROC analysis and the nomogram should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Although internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling and cross-validation techniques, no external validation cohort was available; therefore, model transportability and generalizability remain unproven. As external validation was not conducted, these models cannot yet be recommended for routine clinical use and should be regarded as exploratory, proof-of-concept tools consistent with their current level of evidence. Therefore, causal inference regarding the effect of cerclage on gestational prolongation or fetal survival should be interpreted with caution, and the observed associations should be regarded as adjusted comparisons rather than definitive causal estimates.

Clinical limitations: First, although our findings suggest that CC may prolong pregnancy duration, this extension appears insufficient to significantly improve neonatal survival rates, possibly because the non-CC group included patients with milder clinical presentations. Second, the neutrophil percentage and CRP level were significantly associated with preterm delivery; however, these results represent statistical associations rather than causal relationships. Further mechanistic research is needed to elucidate the underlying biological pathways involved. Given the inherent constraints of the retrospective design, causality between inflammation and preterm birth cannot be definitively established. Third, the absence of microbiological and histopathological infection data limits the ability to identify specific infection types and classify related risk factors for preterm birth. Fourth, the collection of potential confounding variables was not comprehensive, particularly regarding vaginal bleeding, uterine contractions at admission, cervical dilation, and cervical angle characteristics. Undiagnosed cases of chorioamnionitis may also have affected neonatal outcomes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, although perioperative management generally followed standardized institutional protocols, subtle variations in adjunctive treatments (e.g., antibiotic duration or postoperative surveillance intensity) cannot be entirely excluded and may have contributed to residual heterogeneity in outcomes. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these results. External validation in larger, multicenter cohorts with a greater number of outcome events is therefore essential before these findings can be generalized or applied in clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, the cohort-specific, data-driven 30.5-week threshold is exploratory and subject to potential overfitting due to the small number of fetal death events. It should be regarded strictly as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, and must not be interpreted as a clinically actionable gestational age threshold without rigorous external validation. Given the limited number of fetal death events and the inherent risk of overfitting in retrospective ROC modeling, this cutoff cannot be considered clinically validated. External validation in larger, independent, and preferably multicenter cohorts is mandatory before any potential clinical application. Although cerclage placement was associated with prolonged gestational duration, it was not statistically associated with improved fetal survival within this cohort. Although cerclage placement was associated with prolonged gestational duration and did not demonstrate a statistically significant survival advantage in this matched cohort, the clinical relevance of gestational prolongation must be interpreted within the context of gestational age windows and local neonatal care capacity. Gains achieved near the threshold of viability may translate into meaningful improvements in neonatal survival and morbidity profiles, even when overall survival differences do not reach statistical significance in limited sample sizes. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence against the clinical utility of cerclage. Rather, they underscore that gestational prolongation and fetal survival represent related but non-interchangeable outcomes. Pregnancy prolongation may confer clinically meaningful benefits—particularly within specific gestational windows—even when statistical differences in survival are not detected. The absence of a significant survival advantage in this study may reflect limited statistical power, residual confounding, heterogeneity in inflammatory status, and context-specific neonatal care capacity. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as highlighting the complexity of outcome evaluation in cervical insufficiency rather than negating the potential value of cerclage. All proposed predictive frameworks require rigorous external validation before any clinical implementation. Therefore, gestational prolongation and fetal survival should be considered complementary but distinct outcome domains, and absence of statistical survival benefit should not be equated with lack of clinical utility.
