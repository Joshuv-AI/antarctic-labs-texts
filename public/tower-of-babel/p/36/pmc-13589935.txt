The polymeric nanovectors landscape for cancer immunotherapies towards advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs)
Authors: Ilaria Elena Palamà, Alessandra Invidia, Claudia De Stradis, Gabriella Leccese, Clara Baldari, Barbara Cortese, Clara Guido, Andrea Giglio, Concetta Quintarelli, Biagio De Angelis, Gabriele Maiorano, Franco Locatelli, Giuseppe Gigli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite major advances in oncology, cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, underscoring the need for more effective and personalized therapeutic solutions. Advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs), including gene and cell-based therapies, have emerged as a promising frontier for immune cell engineering in oncology. However, current approaches still face several technical/safety issues, mainly associated with the delivery strategies used and their complex manufacturing processes. In this context, polymeric nanovectors (pNVs) have gained attention as a promising tool for developing efficient therapeutic delivery systems. The possibility of customizing pNVs’ composition/physicochemical properties, exploiting natural/synthetic polymers, enables more precise and controlled cargo delivery to immune cells, paving the way for the development of future ATMPs-oriented strategies for cancer immunotherapy. This review highlights the recent advancements in the application of pNVs for the editing of immune cells, focusing on innovative nanotechnology-based strategies to enhance the precision and efficacy of immunotherapy with a particular emphasis on their prospective role in next-generation ATMP development.

Introduction

Gold standard treatments for cancer include surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, with immunotherapy emerging more recently as a key therapeutic strategy. Immunotherapy acts by strengthening the activity of immune cells, thereby promoting a targeted response against cancer. Malignant cells exert several mechanisms to escape the immune system: lower immunogenicity, inhibition of T cell functions through immune checkpoints, recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, and generation of an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME)1. In recent years, immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative approach through different strategies, including cancer vaccines, immune checkpoint inhibition, and immune cell engineering. However, their efficacy is often contrasted by factors such as the specific tumour type, the presence of metastases, and severe side effects2. Moreover, interpatient variability within the TME represents a challenging issue in limiting the effectiveness of current clinical approaches, underscoring the need to develop more personalized treatments. In this evolving landscape, the development of ATMPs offers an innovative and promising window in cancer treatment. Their manufacture is a complex procedure by which a research-grade technology must be translated into a clinical-grade product. Unlike classical drugs (small molecules), ATMPs are considered ‘living drugs’, which means live cells or tissues equipped with new therapeutic tools to fight malignant cells, particularly in cancer applications. Among the most cutting-edge approaches, gene therapy and somatic cell therapy have gained significant attention over recent decades.

Defined as the direct transfer of genetic material into cells or tissue, gene therapy is supported by approved clinical trials to treat several diseases3,4 with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) therapy representing a constantly improving strategy for cancer treatment5. Notwithstanding hopeful outcomes, the utmost critical challenge is the delivery of specific genetic information into human cells, most often achieved using engineered viruses as carriers6. Physical methods are almost completely overtaken by viral carriers, which are employed in Food and Drug Administration (FDA) -approved immunotherapies. However, this viral system involves risks such as insertional mutagenesis, toxicity, and immunogenicity7.

Somatic cell therapy also holds promise for cancer treatment. Defined as a biological medicinal product composed of cells or tissues subjected to substantial manipulation, this approach aims to improve current immunotherapies exploiting antigens, adjuvants, or cytokines to modulate immune cells’ functions relevant to clinical outcomes8. Among somatic cell therapies applied in oncology, dendritic cells (DC) vaccines and tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy are widely explored, with some achieving FDA approvals. However, their clinical impact remains limited by suboptimal antigen presentation, complex patient-specific manufacturing, poor migration and survival in vivo, immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment, and off-target effects9,10.

To overcome these limitations, nanotechnology-based strategies have been developed, exploiting different organic and inorganic nanomaterials, with polymers emerging as a promising alternative to the conventional lipid nanovectors (NVs). Indeed, even though lipid NVs have gained popularity as a drug/gene delivery system due to their good biocompatibility and payload capacity, they still lack some important features for application in precision medicine. On the contrary, polymers fill these gaps, providing long-term stability, higher versatility in payload types, highly tunable release kinetics, enhanced targeting, lower immunogenicity, good biodegradability, and significantly lower manufacturing costs11,12. Their application for gene delivery represents a smaller portion of their overall use due to challenges such as intracellular delivery, endosomal escape, and limiting their clinical translation13. Among polymers, synthetic ones, in particular cationic polymers, are widely engineered for their easy tunability and abilities to condense genetic material, facilitating endosomal escape14, 15, 16, 17. On the contrary, natural polymers, particularly appealing for their accessibility, low immunogenicity, and biocompatibility, are less used for gene delivery due to the required chemical modification to improve stability and cellular uptake18.

This review critically examines recent progress in the use of pNVs as gene tools to empower ATMPs development. We discuss the design principles and functionalization strategies of natural, synthetic, and hybrid polymers for targeted delivery, and their application in the engineering of immune cells in the tumour microenvironment (TME) and in secondary lymphoid organs. We also highlight integrated approaches that combine pNVs with immunomodulators, cytokines, and genome editing technologies, and summarize the emerging clinical data on polymer-based ATMP candidates. Conclusively, we outline current translational barriers, including endosomal escape, biodistribution, and regulatory challenges, and highlight opportunities for pNVs to enable translational, in vivo immune cell reprogramming for next-generation cancer immunotherapies.

Polymeric nanovectors for therapeutic agent delivery

pNVs are reshaping the landscape of ATMPs in cancer immunotherapy by enabling a precise and controlled delivery of genetic or immunomodulatory payloads to immune cells, although their use remains at a preclinical or early translational stage and should therefore be considered as a prospective ATMP-oriented strategy rather than an established clinical modality.

An overview of the natural and synthetic polymers most widely used for gene delivery, together with representative structural motifs, is reported in Fig. 1. These materials are promising candidates for engineering functional applications, due to high loading capacity, tunable stability, and controlled interactions with nucleic acids19. These polymer features must also be observed in relation to pNV architecture, which strongly influences their ability to overcome biological barriers and engineer immune cells. The diverse architectures of pNVs critically determine biodistribution, cellular uptake, endosomal escape, and ultimate transfection efficiency20. In particular, size governs lymphatic drainage and organ retention, shape influences margination and internalization, and surface charge modulates protein corona formation and interactions with immune cells-factors that are especially relevant for targeting secondary lymphoid organs and overcoming physiological barriers to gene delivery. At the same time, pNVs present trade-offs. For instance, natural polymers exhibit great biocompatibility, bioactivity, and biodegradability, but can suffer from batch-to-batch variation and higher immunogenicity21. Additionally, their high bioactivity could result in a strong immunogenic response, which may act as a double-edged sword, depending on the intended immunotherapeutic application. Synthetic polymers, on the other hand, are characterized by biological inert property and precisely controlled chemical structure, which confers high batch-to-batch uniformity and great chemical versatility. Natural polymers employed in the delivery of therapeutic agents

Natural polymers, comprising large molecular compounds ubiquitous in nature or derived from botanical and zoological sources, have been extensively applied for drug and gene delivery, as well as generating interesting immune-stimulatory effects45. Based on chemical composition, natural polymers can be classified into polysaccharides, polypeptides, and proteins.

Polysaccharides are quite advantageous due to their relatively low immunogenicity, cell targeting ability, and the presence of reactive groups on the glucose units, which allows the synthesis of derivatives with tailored biomedical functions. Typical examples of biodegradable polysaccharides employed in cancer immunotherapy include animal-derived compounds, as chitosan (CS), hyaluronic acid (HA), and plant-derived compounds, such as β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) and cellulose. CS, derived from chitin, a natural polymer mainly found in the exoskeletons of crustaceans, is a linear amino polysaccharide and the only natural positively charged polymer with bio-adhesive properties. Several studies reported the use of CS for gene delivery in the last decade, despite many efforts being made to improve its effectiveness, due to its poor stability in physiological conditions46,47. Indeed, its positive charge improves the encapsulation of negatively charged nucleic acids and cellular internalization rate (50%–70%)46,48, however, the presence of primary amine groups makes CS insoluble at physiological pH and prone to aggregation. In addition, CS exerts potent immunoadjuvant effects, promoting DC maturation via DNA Sensor cGAS–STING-dependent induction of type one interferons (IFNs) and enhancing antigen-specific T cell immunity, highlighting its unique potential in the design of cancer vaccines49,50. In this context, a notable study of Shi and colleagues demonstrated the efficacy of CS NVs for delivering tumour cell lysate (TCL) to develop a vaccine against melanoma, exploiting the immunoadjuvant properties of the nanomaterial along with mannose as a DC-targeting moiety51.

Another key natural polymer employed in pNVs’ formulation is HA, which is a polysaccharide consisting of repeated disaccharide units of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine and D-glucuronic acid, classified into small (10–30 kDa), medium (30–100 kDa), and large (≥100 kDa) molecular weight polymers (SMW, MMW, and HMW). Due to its natural presence in human tissues, HA exhibits excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, and good water solubility. Moreover, thanks to its ability to specifically target CD44 receptors, which are overexpressed in various solid tumours, HA polymers have been widely used for drug delivery, but also for gene delivery52. HA is a widely recognized size-dependent mediator of inflammation, as HA with different molecular weights can bind the same receptors expressed on cancer cells (e.g., CD44) yet activate different pathways and exert opposite functions. For instance, LMW HA polymers are known for promoting angiogenesis and inflammatory cascades in the body through the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and heat shock protein (HSP) synthesis, enhancing tumour development. On the other hand, HMW HA polymers primarily exhibit immune-suppressive and anti-angiogenesis functions, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production and suppressing endothelial cell growth through CD44 signalling stabilization, toll-like receptor (TLR) inhibition, and modulating macrophages polarization53,54. However, the use of HMW-HA in NVs synthesis is limited, due to its very long polymer chains, which create high viscosity and difficult chemical modification, often leading to oversized or unstable NVs with reduced tumour penetration. In contrast, MMW-HA provides a better balance between immunological properties and NVs design, exhibiting a mildly stimulatory and partly anti-inflammatory profile, and allowing tighter control over NVs size, which enhances intratumoural diffusion55.

Another natural polymer of interest is represented by β-CD, which is a cyclic oligosaccharide composed of multiple glucose molecules linked by 1,4-α-glycosidic bonds, derived from enzymatic degradation of starch. Due to their special toroidal shape, they can form inclusion complexes, which stabilize and improve the solubility of compounds that are unstable or insoluble in water, finding extensive applications in both drug and gene delivery56. Among β-CD derivatives, hydroxypropyl-β-CD (HβCD) and methyl-β-CD (MβCD) have shown particularly interesting immunological properties. HβCD, an FDA-approved excipient widely known for enhancing the solubility, bioavailability, and chemical stability of lipophilic drugs, can inhibit immunosuppressive functions in the TME, inducing macrophage polarization into M1 phenotype, as reported in recent years57,58. Similar properties were reported for MβCD NVs59.

Finally, one of the most important commercial natural polymers is cellulose, a linear polysaccharide made of repeating units of β-D-glucose. Thanks to its biocompatibility and tunable surface chemistry, cellulose represents a promising platform for drug and gene delivery. Considered immunologically inert in many in vitro and in vivo settings, cellulose can be retained in the bloodstream for extended periods, but also exhibits low biodegradability, which can compromise the release kinetics of therapeutic cargo. Therefore, the chemical structure of cellulose is often modified to enhance its degradation rate, aiming to obtain a balance between immunogenicity and biodegradability60. Indeed, the introduction of chemical moieties on native cellulose could increase its immunogenicity, conferring an excess of positive or negative charges that activate the mononuclear phagocyte system. For these reasons, several cellulose derivatives have been synthesized, with carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC), hydroxypropyl cellulose (HPC), and hydroxypropylmethyl cellulose (HPMC) representing the most routinely used in medical and pharmaceutical applications61. Among immunomodulatory mechanisms, it is worth mentioning polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid stabilized with poly-L-lysine and carboxymethylcellulose (poly-ICLC), a synthetic virus-mimicking double-stranded RNA that acts as an adjuvant, stimulating TLR3, producing cytokines and other molecules that recruit and boost T cells, potentially resulting in tumour regression and even abscopal effects on tumour metastasis62. These properties paved the way for the development of multiple cellulose-based nanoformulations designed to function as efficient, vaccine-like drug or gene delivery systems63. Among natural polymers, proteins, derived from animal or human tissues, as well as from blood plasma and microbial fermentation, are widely employed for drug and gene delivery, with gelatin and albumin representing the most used64.

Albumin, a water-soluble globular protein from blood plasma, thanks to its amphiphilic nature, has been widely used to bind and solubilize poorly water-soluble drugs—getting FDA approval—but exhibits low nucleic acid loading. Furthermore, albumin coating increases NV's half-life and tumour targeting, escaping the mononuclear phagocyte system and binding several overexpressed receptors or proteins in vascular endothelial cells and on cancer cell surfaces, as gp60 (endothelial albumin-binding receptor), secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC), and receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE)65,66. By binding these molecules, albumin NVs are internalized by cancer cells and act as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP)67, inducing cell stress and increasing macrophage-mediated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines68.

Gelatin, derived from collagen, is also a promising candidate for the synthesis of NVs as it easily degrades through enzymatic hydrolysis into non-toxic peptides and amino acids that are naturally metabolized and are well-tolerated in vivo, with minimal immune response. However, this polymer lacks specific tumour targeting motifs, thus resulting in off-target toxicity69,70.

Lastly, among natural polymers, it is worth mentioning peptides as well, due to their growing applications in drug and gene delivery. Peptides, defined as comprising 2 to 50 amino acids linked by peptide bonds, exhibit unique structural and functional properties compared to proteins, particularly in modulating immune responses. Therefore, engineering peptide-based NVs, either through direct self-assembly or post-modification of NVs, has been widely considered as a promising drug/gene delivery approach, focusing on amphiphilic and cationic peptides71.

Amphiphilic peptides, such as glutamic acid-alanine-leucine-alanine (GALA) and arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD), are widely used for NVs functionalization to increase nucleic acids transfection capabilities72. RGD can bind to integrin receptors on the surface of cells, particularly those overexpressed on cancer cells, leading to increased cellular uptake of NVs73. However, several studies reported the production of anti-RGD antibodies in some patients, reducing the effectiveness of the therapy or causing collateral effects. Therefore, some strategies have been developed to overcome this limitation, including peptide structure modifications or incorporation of RGD peptides into stealth lipids, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG)-lipids74. GALA is a pH-sensitive fusogenic peptide: following cellular uptake by endocytosis, endosomal acidification increases the helical content of the GALA peptide, leading to NVs fusion with the endosomal membrane and cytosolic release of the cargo. This mechanism protects the cargo from endosomal degradation while enhancing its intracellular delivery75. Several studies also reported the ability of GALA peptides to target sialic acid-terminated sugar chains on different cell types76, including immune cells, highlighting an interesting application of these peptides in the development of cancer vaccines77. In this context, a relevant study of Bo Lou and colleagues reported an increased activation of DCs upon administration of antigenic mRNA NVs functionalized with GALA peptides to a variety of tumour cell lines (murine melanoma, mouse colorectal cancer, and human lung carcinoma)78. Cationic peptides are characterized by amino-derived functional groups with positive charges, enabling interactions with negatively charged nucleic acids and cell membranes within biological systems. One of the most used cationic peptides for gene delivery is protamine (PRM), a small peptide rich in arginine and histidine residues, with a molecular weight of ∼4.5 kDa, typically derived from fish sperm. Due to its multiple positively charged residues, PRM possesses strong cationic properties and has been utilized in gene delivery systems as early as 199779. Similarly to PRM, non-viral NVs based on melittin, a cationic peptide from bee venom, possess the ability to enhance the endosomal escape of DNA and other nucleic acids, increasing gene transfection efficiency, forming hydrophilic pores in cell membranes80.

Synthetic polymers for non-viral nanovector production

Synthetic polymers are composed of repeating monomeric units, chemically synthesized through different polymerization processes in laboratories or industrial settings to define polymer topology, polydispersity, and end-group functionality, which are essential for tailoring polymers to specific biomedical applications. This class of polymers generally possesses hydrolytically labile chemical bonds in their polymer backbone, such as esters, anhydrides, carbonates, amides, and urethanes64. Among these classes of polymers, polyesters, such as polylactic acid (PLA), polycaprolactone (PCL), and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), represent the most frequently employed nanomaterials for the synthesis of non-viral NVs. The presence of carboxyl groups confers a negative charge and helps with the colloidal stability of pNVs in aqueous environments, preventing aggregation81,82. Moreover, they exhibit extensive preclinical and clinical data due to their higher biocompatibility28, easier control than polyamides (slow hydrolytic degradation), and are more stable than polyanhydrides (fast hydrolytic degradation)83. Among polyesters, PLGA, a biodegradable polymer composed via the catalysed ring-opening copolymerization of glycolic acid (GA) and lactic acid (LA) monomers, offers a better balance between mechanical properties and biocompatibility for therapeutic agent delivery, due to its rapid degradation rate and release profile, which can be tuned by adjusting the lactic acid to glycolic acid ratio, which are both natural metabolites in the body. Furthermore, a recent study by Ma and colleagues40 demonstrated that acidic degradation products of PLGA NVs can drive a pro-inflammatory macrophage response, providing a new mechanism of PLGA-induced inflammation and paving the way for the design of next-generation PLGA-based biomaterials with tailored immunomodulatory properties. However, negatively charged NVs, as polyesters-derived, often face low cargo loading efficiency for gene delivery, due to electrostatic repulsion with negatively charged nucleic acids. To overcome this intrinsic limitation, cationic synthetic polymers, such as polyethyleneimine (PEI), poly(L-lysine) (PLL), poly(β-amino esters) (PBAEs), and poly(amidoamine) dendrimers (PAMAM), have been widely exploited83. Among them, branched and linear PEI are the most widely known and used cationic polymers for gene delivery29,84. The abundant amine groups in PEI confer high solubility in water and other polar solvents and promote strong electrostatic interactions with negatively charged molecules, such as nucleic acids and antigenic proteins85. These strong interactions generate polyelectrolyte complexes (polyplex) that protect nucleic acids from enzymatic degradation, promote cellular internalization, and enhance nucleic acid transfection efficiency (50%–80%), escaping endosomal degradation (20%–30%)86,87. Among the mechanisms proposed to explain the improved endosomal release associated with PEI, the “proton sponge effect” remains one of the most discussed and widely considered. This effect relies on the ability of polymers rich in protonatable amines—such as PEI and other cationic polymers—to buffer the acidic endosomal compartment after cellular uptake. As endosomes acidify, these amines become progressively protonated, causing the influx of protons, chloride ions, and water. The resulting osmotic swelling leads to endosomal membrane destabilization or rupture, and the cargo is efficiently released into the cytosol88. Thanks to its enhanced transfection efficiency, PEI has been widely investigated for vaccine development, and several studies documented the efficacy of PEI-based vaccines both in vitro and in vivo. However, its immunological properties remain poorly defined. Indeed, these studies do not clarify whether the improved efficiency of these vaccines was solely due to cargo protection or to intrinsic adjuvant properties of PEI31,33, 34, 35. Moreover, the applications of PEI for gene delivery still raise concerns in the scientific community, mainly due to its high toxicity and poor biocompatibility, compromising a safer translation from in vitro to in vivo studies in immune cells manufacturing, such as CAR-T therapy89. Indeed, highly positively charged PEI NVs have been shown to interact with biological molecules, such as serum proteins, cell membranes, and other cellular components, interfering with numerous physiological processes89. To address key limitations of PEI, alternative cationic polymers have been considered, such as PLL, poly(cyclohexane-1,4-diylacetone dimethylene ketal (PC7A), and PBAE.

PLL is a synthetic homopolymer of the naturally occurring amino acid L-lysine. Contrary to CS, at physiological pH, the primary amine groups on the lysine side chains are protonated, giving PLL a strong positive charge32. Despite its superior biocompatibility due to its natural derivation, this polymer shows lower nucleic-acid encapsulation and transfection efficiency than PEI, due to less stable polyplex formation and a weaker buffering capacity90. For this reason, many studies have explored gene delivery systems that combine PLL with other polymers, some of which exhibit immunological properties. However, these properties cannot be attributed to PLL alone, and further studies are needed to clarify the intrinsic immunological properties of PLL37,91.

PC7A is an amphiphilic block copolymer, composed of cyclic seven-membered ring 2-hexamethyleneiminoethyl units, which endow it with unique pH-responsive characteristics, exhibiting hydrophobicity under neutral and alkaline conditions and hydrophilicity in acidic environments. Such pH responsiveness allows PC7A to rapidly release cargo within the typically acidic TME36. For instance, when copolymerized with cationic polymers like PEI, PC7A improves endosomal escape while reducing cytotoxicity, thanks to its higher biocompatibility in both in vivo and in vitro settings and resistance to degradation38. Another important function of PC7A is acting as a stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonist through non-competitive binding, thereby triggering the activation of downstream immune cells and finding potential applications for vaccine development92.

PBAE is a linear or branched polymer composed of ester linkages and amino groups. PBAEs exhibit the same advantages of PEI for gene delivery, as well as strong affinity for nucleic acids and pH-mediated endosomal escape, while keeping a low toxicity, due to its easier degradation into biocompatible byproducts39. Furthermore, hyperbranched PBAE forms enhance the polymer's pH buffering capacity and result in higher gene transfection (40%–90%) and endosomal escape (20%–45%) rates than PEI93. In soluble form, PBAE does not show significant immunogenicity, but can activate innate immunity in a molecular weight-specific manner when condensed into a particulate form. Like other cationic polymers, its immunological properties remain unclear, as the studies reporting immunoadjuvant activity do not examine PBAE in isolation41.

Finally, PAMAM dendrimers are synthetic, highly branched, tree-like macromolecules with a well-defined symmetrical architecture. This class of molecules is typically used for the synthesis of polyelectrolyte complexes, thanks to the presence of primary amine (–NH2) terminal groups40. PAMAM dendrimers provide safer, more controllable, and tunable gene-delivery systems than PEI owing to their higher biocompatibility and chemical structure. Although their transfection efficiency is usually lower than PEI-based NVs (50%–70%), proper chemical modification—such as the incorporation of tumour-targeting moieties—can yield comparable gene expression levels while maintaining a superior safety profile, as demonstrated in several studies94,95. Lastly, these compounds need further studies to confirm an immunoadjuvant profile95. Once these factors have been considered, pNVs’ translation into in vivo studies could still present some challenges, including their relative instability in physiological fluids and their potential to cause toxicity or immunogenicity. Therefore, different covalent and non-covalent modifications of these polymers have been evaluated to broaden their applicability. In this context, a synthetic polymer that deserves a mention for its remarkable properties is PEG43. PEG is a highly biocompatible and non-toxic synthetic polymer, widely used for its stealth effect29,43. PEGylation to nanoparticles or polymers creates a hydrophilic shield that resists non-specific binding of proteins by opsonization, which reduces clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) and consequently increases circulation time in the bloodstream43. However, many studies documented the induction of anti-PEG antibodies following repeated or even single exposure96,97. These antibodies, both pre-existing and treatment-induced, can bind PEGylated NVs and promote opsonization, thus accelerating their clearance and therapeutic efficacy. To overcome this limitation, several strategies are being explored at a, including the use of alternative hydrophilic polymers (e.g., polysarcosine coatings)98, reduction of PEG density, or the use of cleavable PEG layers that detach after administration to minimize immune recognition44.

Similarly, the combination of polymers, such as PLL-PEG-based NVs, was utilized for their ability to condense DNA in the inner core and their increased pharmacological half-life43,99. Triblock copolymers consisting of hyper-branched PEI grafted-PCL-block PEG (hy-PEI-g-PCL-b-PEG) with and without a FA targeting ligand have been developed for siRNA delivery100. Copolymers have also demonstrated suitable efficacy for gene delivery in cells that are hard to transfect, like immune cells. Indeed, specific copolymers have recently been studied for gene delivery to macrophages100,101. In this regard, targeting CD206 mannose receptors, which are highly expressed in the TME, represents a promising strategy for cancer therapy102. For example, mannose PLL conjugates, to produce polyplexes with plasmidic (P)DNA for macrophage transfection, were developed, as well as a mannose-conjugated branched PEI. Likewise, mannosylated PEI-coated liposomes can be used for gene delivery in macrophages103,104, as well as mannosylated CS-graft-PEI polymer. Notably, Raviv and colleagues delivered in vivo DNA conjugated with PEG-PEI block-copolymers to DCs by targeting mannose105.

Advanced nanoformulations for innovative immune cell engineering

T cells, NK cells, macrophages, and DCs are critical immune cell populations that shape the TME and are central to the development of current and next-generation ATMPs. Each contributes uniquely to anti-tumour immunity and presents specific challenges for clinical translation. T cells, particularly cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, support most approved and emerging ATMPs, such as TIL therapies and engineered T cell products (e.g., CAR-T and T cell receptor (TCR)-T), but suffer from exhaustion, poor persistence, and suboptimal trafficking in solid tumours. DCs, essential for antigen presentation and T cell priming, are used in DC-based vaccines but are limited by poor migration and short lifespan. NK cells and macrophages are increasingly explored in the ATMP landscape due to their innate tumour-killing potential and ability to modulate the TME, with CAR-NK and CAR macrophages (CAR-M) representing a new appealing strategy for the treatment of solid tumours.

Non-viral polymeric nanovectors for T cell engineering

The integration of polymeric nanomaterials into ATMPs is rapidly redefining the landscape of T cell engineering, offering a next-generation toolbox for safe, flexible, and scalable delivery of genetic cargo.

T cells develop in the thymus and are then released into the peripheral blood circulation as naïve T cells, which, upon antigen stimulation, differentiate into helper (CD4+), cytotoxic (CD8+), regulatory, or memory subsets. TCR complexed with the CD3 complex allows antigen recognition: CD4+ T subset recognizes peptides bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class 2, while CD8+ T cells engage MHC class 1120, 121, 122.

In the context of ATMPs, these biological distinctions are crucial for designing next-generation gene delivery systems for subset-specific modulation, such as CAR and TCR therapies, which have emerged as one of the most relevant breakthrough approaches for cancer treatment in recent years. Despite their similarities, these two approaches differ significantly. CAR-T, through its chimeric antigen receptor, may directly identify and attach to cancer cell antigens without requiring the presentation of the major histocompatibility complex. On the other hand, designed TCR treatment is MHC-dependent as it improves the natural TCRs’ ability to identify cancer antigens when they are displayed by the MHC on tumour cells. TCR-based T cells can only be utilized to treat patients with certain MHC types because of their MHC reliance120,121.

However, despite its remarkable specificity and therapeutic potential, several limitations continue to raise concerns in the scientific community. The two biggest issues are represented by safety concerns relative to the use of viral vectors and the extensive ex vivo manipulation of the T cells for the manufacturing process5,6,123,124. The standard production process for adoptive T cell therapies typically involves expensive and specialized ex vivo culture steps to activate and expand cytotoxic T cells (Fig. 4). Moreover, standard manufacturing strategies require ex vivo expansion of several days to reach clinically effective doses. Strategies that preserve less differentiated T cell subsets, modulate the cytokine and metabolic environment, and incorporate pharmacological agents can collectively enhance the efficacy of CAR T cell therapies. Regarding safety considerations, the transduction process used during T cell manufacturing - whether employing viral vectors (primarily retroviral or lentiviral) or transposon systems - results in random integration of the CAR or TCR construct in the T cell genome, thereby raising concerns about potential genotoxicity120,123,126. Moreover, the production of viral vectors represents one of the most significant cost drivers in the manufacturing of T cell-based therapies. For instance, since the FDA's and European Medicinal Agency's (EMA) landmark approval of the first CAR T cell product, Tisagenlecleucel, was approved in 2017, for the treatment of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (B-ALL), the exceptionally high manufacturing cost raised several concerns. These controversies include the affordability and equitable access to CAR-T cell therapies within the public healthcare systems127,128.

For these reasons, research efforts have focused on developing alternative carriers that could be effective as viral vectors, but safer and less expensive. Among these, non-viral NVs, including inorganic and organic, represent a promising option, although they still present several challenges before being recognized as a valid alternative for actual gene transfer. Above all, biodegradable polymers are a particularly versatile platform for the delivery of signals to the immune system because they can be easily surface-modified to target specific receptors and engineered to release encapsulated cargo in a precise, sustained, and stimuli-responsive manner. Perhaps, pNVs can facilitate translation of successful ex vivo T cell therapies by enhancing cellular manufacturing and the phenotypic properties of T cells for in vivo therapeutic use.

In the following paragraph, selected examples of ex vivo and in vivo T cell engineering using polymeric nanocarriers to deliver different genetic materials are reported.

In a recent study, Parayath et al.106 developed and proved the efficacy and safety of an injectable nanocarrier that delivers in vitro transcribed CAR or TCR mRNA for transient reprogramming of circulating T cells to recognize disease-relevant antigens in mouse models of human leukemia, prostate cancer, and hepatitis B-induced hepatocellular carcinoma, causing disease regression at levels similar to bolus infusions of ex vivo engineered lymphocytes (Fig. 5). A biodegradable PBAE polymer formulation was used as a carrier matrix to load and deliver mRNA encoding disease-specific receptor genes into human lymphocytes. In addition, an anti-CD8 antibody coupled to a PGA polymer was employed for cell targeting. Specifically, the mRNA-charged nanocarriers present four functional components (Fig. 5A): (i) surface-anchored targeting ligands (anti-CD8 antibodies) that selectively bind the T cells and initiate rapid receptor-induced endocytosis to internalize them; (ii) a negatively charged coating with PGA, to minimize off-target binding; (iii) an inner matrix made of PBAE, acting as mRNA protection from enzymatic degradation in the endosome, and releasing them into the cytoplasm, thereby enabling translation of the encoded protein; (iv) mRNA producing transient expression of the disease-pecific CAR or TCR. After incubation with NVs, T cells show a high level of surface expression of CAR (Fig. 5B) or TCR (Fig. 5C) with a peak on Day 2. In a recent study, Nguyen et al.14 synthesized polymer-stabilized Cas9 with PGA, containing truncated Cas9 target sequence (tCTSs) added at the ends of the homology-directed repair template, which assists the template entry to the nucleus. This system, along with the employment of polymeric nanomaterials, improved the editing efficiency by approximately twofold and reduced toxicity, across multiple genomic loci in different cell types, such as bulk CD3+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), γδ T cells, B cells, NK cells, and primary and induced pluripotent stem cell-derived hematopoietic stem progenitor cells (HSPCs). This study paved the way for the synthesis of new formulations exploiting the characteristics of polymeric nanomaterials for DNA delivery in T cell engineering.

Yu Q. et al.15 developed self-assembled nanoparticles (SNPs) charged with plasmid encoding for epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)v3-CAR for transfection of T cells. These SNPs were produced by self-assembly of PEG with cationic polymers PAMAM and PEI. The SNPs showed high efficacy in Jurkat T cell gene transfection and exhibited low cytotoxicity, thereby resulting in transient EGFRv3-CAR expression on the cell membrane and enabling Jurkat T cells to recognize and bind specifically with EGFRv3-positive tumour cells, such as glioblastoma.

To avoid all the problems associated with the ex vivo engineering of CARs and their infusion, the research moved to the in vivo engineering of CAR-T to simplify their manufacturing process. To engineer T cells in vivo, a platform capable of safely transferring genetic material to the target T cells is required. One of the most significant achievements in this field is the study of Smith et al.129, which developed synthetic polymer-based nanostructures to deliver genes encoding disease-specific CARs directly into circulating T cells in vivo. These DNA nanocarriers have a biodegradable PBAE core, modified with peptides containing microtubule-associated sequences and nuclear localization signal (NLS) to enhance nuclear import of plasmid DNA. NVs selectively deliver CAR pDNA to T cells in vivo thanks to a functionalization with anti-CD3ε F (ab′)2 antibody fragments, leading to sustained anti-tumour activity and long-term disease remission in preclinical models. This approach is among the first that use synthetic polymeric NVs to demonstrate in vivo programming of circulating T cells, marking a critical step toward broader clinical implementation of personalized cancer immunotherapies129. Over the years, an increasing number of studies have exploited polymeric materials for CAR-T cell generation, demonstrating their applicability not only in NV-based gene delivery but also in more complex polymeric architectures. A notable example is the supramolecular hydrogel developed by Zhu et al., assembled from the cationic polymer mPEG-PCL-PEI and α-cyclodextrin to enable localized and biocompatible gene delivery within tumours. This platform was later adapted to directly target T cells through anti-CD3-conjugated PGA, allowing in situ CAR plasmid delivery. Beyond locally engineering T cells, this system is able to stimulate T cell activation, promote pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhance T cell CD8+ infiltration130.

Another promising approach involves the use of siRNA to improve T cells’ functionality in vivo. Xie et al.109 exploited a transferrin-PEI-based NVs (Tf-PEI) to deliver in vitro and in vivo a siGAPDH, demonstrating the capability of these NVs to selectively target the activated T cells in vivo, due to the presence of Tf receptors, which are expressed exclusively on activated T cells.

Beyond these approaches, other research groups have investigated applications of non-viral NVs, focusing on combinatorial strategies designed to synergize with CAR T cell treatment. Indeed, it has been widely demonstrated that several pleiotropic pro-inflammatory cytokines elicit a specific immune response, delivering pro-inflammatory cytokines in combination with the adoptive T cell therapy. However, some studies131,132 have revealed shortcomings in this approach, due to the systemic immune response to artificial stimuli, with severe side effects. To overcome these limitations, a strategy that combines a nanodelivery system coupled with protein nanogels (NGs) has been developed133. Tang and coworkers, combining these two technologies, not only provided a system that used non-viral NVs efficiently bound to the plasma membrane of T cells to carry specific drugs, but also ensured a consistent T cell expansion and sustained persistence in vivo thanks to the bounds with the NGs133. NG administration enabled an 8-fold increase in cytokine dosing without toxicity, resulting in a 16-fold enhancement of T cell expansion within tumours compared to systemic delivery of free cytokines133.

In this context, Lin et al. designed and validated a multifunctional modular iron-oxide-based nanoplatform for the sustained release of different cytokines necessary to improve the fitness of CAR-T produced with conventional viral methods134. This innovative strategy, in combination with non-viral polymeric delivery employed for CAR construct transfer in T cells, can open the door to send specific signals essential to obtain functional CAR-T ex vivo and improve their manufacturing process.

An innovative approach for combinatorial therapy employing CAR was established by Yaman et al.135 who developed a biomimetic drug carrier based on CAR-T cell membranes for the targeted delivery of cisplatin. CAR-T cells engineered with anti-human epidermal growth factor 2 (HER2) were engineered via lentiviral transduction of anti-HER2 CAR coding plasmids and used for cell membrane extraction. Cisplatin-loaded PLGA nanoparticles were synthesized and coated with anti-HER2-specific CAR-T cell membranes, showing a sustained drug release over 21 days in physiological conditions, increased uptake, and inhibited growth of multiple HER2+ cancer cells in vitro. These results were also confirmed by in vivo studies using a subcutaneous lung cancer model in nude mice, exhibiting a preferential localization in tumour areas and a significant reduction in tumour growth135. Therefore, CAR-engineered membrane-coated NVs could be a promising cell-mimicking drug carrier that could improve the therapeutic outcomes of CAR-T therapies.

In summary, polymeric NVs are positioned as a key element in the clinical translation of T cell-based therapy, bypassing the labour-intensive virus-dependent manufacturing process and enabling off-the-shelf ATMP pipelines. Furthermore, the addition of smart functionalization, such as ligands, endosomal escape moieties, and NLS, allows pNVs to aid not only as a gene delivery system, but also as multifunctional monitoring modules in ATMPs, thus enabling dynamic control over T cell function in vivo and addressing key criteria for EMA and FDA approval.

NK cell engineering employed non-viral polymer-based nanocarriers

The successful results obtained with CAR-T therapy paved the way for engineering other immune cells136, i.e., NK cells that have the potential to be used as effector cells in CAR therapy137. NK cells are specialized lymphocytes capable of detecting and eliminating infected or abnormal cells without the need for prior antigen-specific activation and represent a critical component of the innate immune system. Unlike adaptive lymphocytes, NK cells do not possess unique antigen receptors. Instead, they rely on a diverse array of germline-encoded receptors to distinguish between healthy and diseased cells. Their phenotype is characterized by the absence of CD3 and the presence of CD56 and CD16 in most human NK cells138, 139, 140. NK cells exert their effector functions primarily through killing of damaged cells and the production of IFNG, a cytokine that, in turn, activates macrophages141. Moreover, the recognition of a target cell induces the exocytosis of granules that release perforin near the damaged site. Perforins facilitate the entrance of other proteins, granzymes, into the cytoplasm of target cells, which in turn initiate a cascade of events that induce apoptosis142. NK cells can also kill cells in a perforin-independent manner since they are provided with FAS ligand (FasL) and tumour necrosis factor (TNF)- related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)143.

NK cells are equipped with activating and inhibitory receptors, whose functions depend on the binding to infected or healthy cells, respectively. Activating receptors recognize a heterogeneous array of ligands principally expressed on the surface of damaged and/or infected cells and, consequently, activate tyrosine-kinases (PTKs) that induce the killing of target cells144. On the other hand, inhibitory receptors bind MHC1 molecules145 that are expressed by nucleated cells; this binding activates tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) that obstacle the activation pathway, and NK cells cannot trigger the killing of target cells139,140.

Thanks to their natural cytotoxic status and the ability to eliminate damaged cells, NK cells are potent tools for cancer immunotherapy, improving their effector functions or redirecting their target cells through genetic modifications146.

One of the main challenges in utilizing NK cells as effector cells for CAR therapy is the efficient delivery of genetic carriers into these cells147. For primary NK cells, electroporation remains the most used non-viral transfection method, capable of transfecting cells with both DNA and RNA. However, this technique is often associated with irreversible membrane damage and high cell death rates148,149. PDA-coated complex has been designed to engineer NK cells, but also to monitor their trafficking in vivo16. This was achieved by incorporating a magnetic core, resulting in a cellular uptake of 41.59%. To evaluate the genetic material delivery, they labelled the complexes with rhodamine B isothiocyanate and bound them to GFP-plasmid, and fluorescent images confirmed the transfection in the NK-92 cell line16.

In another study, Wilk et al.111 formulated a system named charge-altering releasable transporters (CARTs) to engineer NK primary cells composed of a lipid block and a charge-altering block. This system is based on a charge switch: to bind nucleic acids, CARTs are initially cationic, becoming neutral under physiological conditions, with the consequent release of the cargo. The delivery mechanism is based on a pH-sensitive ester-to-amide rearrangement of the cationic poly (α-aminoster) backbone into neutral small molecules (diketopiperazine) at pH 7.4. Using these lipoplexes, a transfection efficiency of approximately 27 % was achieved.

Moreover, the research group of Zhuo et al.112 proposed a complex composed of dendrimer-entrapped gold nanoparticles (AuDENPs) entrapped in concentric shells of polyamidoamine dendrimers modified PEG (mPEG) to deliver the human ferritin heavy chain (hFTH1) in NK cells. hFTH1 was used as a molecular tracer of immune cells by using magnetic resonance imaging to evaluate the therapeutic effects and to monitor the cellular distribution in a non-invasive way. Through this system, a transfection efficiency of around 80% was obtained.

Engineering NK cells represents an intricate challenge, but nanomaterials offer significant advantages for addressing multiple objectives simultaneously. In addition to efficiently transfecting genetic materials, NVs act to improve NKs’ functionalities and performances, to label and trace the cells over time, and to immunomodulate NK cells’ activity148,150.

Macrophage: engineering by nanostructured polymeric materials

Beyond the engineering of T and NK cells with NVs, macrophages represent an additional key target, as their plasticity and pivotal role in shaping the tumour immune landscape make them amenable to nanotechnology-driven modulation. Macrophages are one of the primary immune cell types that infiltrate tumours, and which become tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), and play a dual role in the TME depending on their phenotypes, either promoting or suppressing cancer growth151. Despite the classification of macrophages being outdated, to simplify their role in TME, they are usually subdivided into two main subclasses, i.e., M1 and M2, whose differentiation is driven by extracellular stimuli. M1-like macrophages are associated with antitumour activity, and are characterized by antigen-presenting, phagocytic, and cytotoxic tasks, inducing the secretion and secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines, like interleukin (IL)23 and IL12. Instead, M2-like macrophages are related to pro-tumoural functions, hampering immune response and promoting angiogenesis. The M1/M2 ratio depends on concentration and the type of signals152. Considering the high plasticity of macrophages and their different role in tumourigenesis, several studies have focused on the repolarization of TAMs and the inhibition of their recruitment and survival153. In this regard, macrophages play a central role in the TME and are increasingly being explored as vehicles for targeted cancer therapy. NVs, due to their customizable physicochemical properties and biocompatibility, offer a promising platform for engineering macrophages. Moreover, NVs can leverage the natural tumour-tropic behaviour of macrophages, driven by chemokine gradients and inflammation-associated signals within the TME, to achieve targeted delivery to tumour sites154. The entry of NVs into macrophages occurs through multiple pathways. In general, particles of 20–100 nm are internalized via caveolin-mediated endocytosis, while the uptake of particles of 100–350 nm follows the clathrin-mediated endocytosis155,156. Macropinocytosis is a form of fluid-phase endocytosis involving extensive membrane ruffling and is capable of internalizing particles in the 0.5–5 μm range157. Nevertheless, phagocytosis remains the primary and most efficient mechanism for nanoparticle uptake by macrophages158,159. This mechanism of internalization starts with the interaction between ligands present on the particles and pattern-recognition receptors expressed on the surface of macrophages, which induces a reorganization of the cytoskeleton and the formation of a phagolysosome. Once internalized, however, most nanoparticles are rapidly cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system and directed to lysosomal degradation, which can restrict therapeutic bioavailability and induce inflammatory or cytotoxic responses160,161. In this context, polymeric nanomaterials provide a powerful tool for delivering cargo directly into cells, offering high versatility depending on the receptor they engage, the intracellular signalling pathway they activate, and the cytokine they induce. Additionally, they can be used to functionalize NVs in a way that reduces clearance by macrophages, to enable controlled intracellular release of the cargo, and facilitate macrophage engineering161, 162, 163.

Different nanomaterials have been found to polarize macrophages toward different functional states, sometimes pushing them from an inflammatory, M1-like profile to a more anti-inflammatory, tissue-repairing M2 state, and sometimes the opposite164. This ability to steer immune responses gives NVs an extra level of value, making them useful for far more than just carrying therapeutic drugs or genetic material.

Indeed, macrophages can be reprogrammed using empty NVs that are composed of materials with specific properties. For instance, amine-modified (PS–COOH) and carboxyl-modified polystyrene NVs (PS–NH2) hamper the expression of CD163, a scavenger receptor that counteracts inflammation, the immunosuppressive receptor CD200R, and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL10. The ability of both PS–COOH and PS–NH2 to counteract the expression of these receptors and cytokines prevents the polarization of M2, making them an attractive strategy for antitumour therapy165.

On the contrary, polyurethane (PU) NVs have been shown to interfere with the proinflammatory function of M1 macrophages166. Due to the good biocompatibility and elastic properties, PU is an interesting material for NVs synthesis. However, the functionalization of PU-NVs affected the phagocytic activity of macrophages, showing inhibitory activity against the proinflammatory cytokines tumour necrosis factor (TNF)A and IL1B, a critical aspect to consider for cancer treatment166.

Combining the intrinsic properties of NVs with a specific cargo is an effective strategy to target macrophages and repolarize them. For instance, TLR agonists have proven activity in the reprogramming of M2 toward M1-like macrophages; however, they show limited use due to the shortage of effective delivery methods. Rodell and colleagues167 encapsulated R848, an agonist of TLR7/8, to activate the transcription factors NF-κB and interferon regulatory factor, in β-CD NVs. By combining high loading capacity with macrophage affinity, these NVs demonstrated high and rapid uptake and antitumour activity through repolarization of M2 in M1.

This effect was also investigated by Li et al.168, by delivering R848 in liposome NVs and observing a quick accumulation in the tumour site with an enhancement of phagocytosis in lymphoma cell lines. The authors used pH-responsive NVs to induce drug release specifically in the TME and tissue resident antigen-presenting cell (APC) to promote T-cell infiltration168. The delivery of R848 in macrophages was also enhanced by the NVs/bacteria complex. R848 and doxorubicin (DOX) were separately encapsulated by PLGA, obtaining PR848 and PDOX, respectively, and subsequently, E. coli MG1655 was linked to PR848 by glycol CS (Ec-PR848). With this approach, bacteria were used specifically in hypoxic tumour tissue, taking advantage of their anaerobic features. Thus, Ec-PR848 NVs were shown to induce polarization of M2 in M1 after phagocytosis and R848 release, resulting in the secretion of TNFA and IL6 and the activation of the antitumour immune response. Moreover, PDOX triggered immunogenic cell death (ICD) contributed to the activation of the antitumour immune response169.

Chang et al.170 used PLGA for the coating of MnO2 NVs loaded with an antiangiogenic drug (sorafenib) to rescue the hypoxic condition in TME of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Within an acidic TME, MnO2-NVs reacted with H2O2 and H+ to produce oxygen and reduce hypoxia. The MnO2 NVs broke down in the acidic environment of TME to produce Mn2+. This consequently promoted M1 polarization, promoted by reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced from Mn2+ and H2O2, resulting also in CD8+ T cells penetration into HCCs. This strategy not only improved the accumulation of sorafenib in hepatocarcinoma and facilitated oxygen creation by lowering hypoxia through the catalytic impact of MnO2 but also triggered the reprogramming of M2 macrophages toward M1170.

Another strategy to promote M2 trans-differentiation into M1 consisted of covering NVs with mannose, which specifically binds the CD206 receptor on the surface of M2 macrophages. Thanks to this stratagem, manno-encapsulated NVs were preferentially taken up by M2 macrophages, but not M1, which showed significantly lower CD206 expression levels171.

Recently, NVs functionalized with mannose were used to deliver small interfering RNA (siRNA), to silence colony-stimulating factor-1 receptor (CSF-1R), whose overexpression is one of the main causes of the poor tumour prognosis, for hampering M2 differentiation and consequently promoting tumour death172. In addition to siRNA, macrophages were also engineered with NVs carrying the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Tu et al.114 developed a co-delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knock down the cyclin-dependent kinase five gene (Cas9-Cdk5), involved in the regulation of PD-1/PD-L1 pathway and PTX, which induced ICD and macrophages repolarization. The pH-responsive NVs composed of an inner core of cationic copolymer (PEI-PLGA) were bound at the PEG corona through an acid-cleavable bond. Immune clearance was shown to be reduced due to the PEG layer in the blood circulation, while the acid environment of TME induced NVs disassembly with the consequent drug and gene release. With this system, the downregulation of PD-L1 due to Cas9-Cdk5 and the re-polarization of M2 to M1 due to the effect of PTX were obtained simultaneously114.

Another recent approach used pH-responsive PEG-conjugated phospholipid derivatives and phospholipid targeting TAM to functionalize E. coli protoplast-derived nanovesicles. This approach aimed to target, at the same time, TAMs, PIK3CG, a pivotal modulator of macrophage polarization, and CpG-rich genomic DNA fragments, which are TLR9 ligands, allowing specific delivery (Fig. 6A). Together, these two targeted stratagems allowed an efficient uptake by macrophages (Fig. 6B and C), with a consequent TAM repolarization, toward an anti-tumour M1 activity (Fig. 6D). The immunostimulatory effect of CpG oligonucleotides was also used to functionalize several formulations of NVs, such as PEGylated liposomes, liposomes together with the immunomodulator anti-CD40174, biomimetic red blood cell (RBC) membrane-coated NVs loaded with the glucocorticoid baicalin175, and Graphene oxide (GO)–PEI–PEG nanocomplex. All of these formulations show high efficacy in TAM reprogramming17. Finally, as previously described for T and NK cells, macrophages also represent an attractive model for CAR engineering. CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) technology, obtained by engineering macrophages with specific CAR genes for enhancing their binding to tumour cells, has shown a strong anti-tumour effect regardless of the M1/M2 state176.

Moreover, CAR-M has a good potential for the treatment of solid tumours, since macrophages are the most abundant immune cells at the tumour site. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that CAR-M displayed high antigen-specific phagocytosis and cytotoxicity, resulting in the killing of tumour cells and high T cell recruitment177.

Viral vectors are generally used to produce CAR-M in vitro, but Kang and colleagues107 showed an in vivo reprogramming of M2 using mannose-conjugated PEI NVs (MPEI) carrying genes encoding CAR and Interferon-γ (CAR-IFNG). The CAR-IFNG was delivered by employing an associated non-viral system, resulting in persistent expression of the gene within MPEI NVs that provided targeted delivery to M1. Cancer cells were phagocytosed by CAR-M, which in turn activated naïve T cells by tumour antigen presentation, resulting in increased anti-tumour activity and decreased tumour growth in mice107.

Additionally, CAR-M technology offers a valid alternative for the treatment of non-responding tumours, as demonstrated by Gao and colleagues108, which takes advantage of the infiltration properties of macrophages within TME in the brain. In their work, the RP-182 peptide, involved in the activation of phagocytosis and autophagy in M2-like macrophages, was conjugated onto the surface of poly (β-amino ester)-based NVs, which in turn were functionalized with the NLS to ensure nuclear delivery of a plasmid encoding Erb-B2-specific CAR. ErbB2 is a human epidermal growth factor that is abnormally expressed in patients with brain stem glioma (BSG). Briefly, RP-182 peptide triggered the polarization of M2 in M1 macrophages, inducing phagocytosis of cancer cells and cytokine storm, which in turn recruited CD8+ T cells within TME. Finally, macrophages in vivo engineered with CAR–ErbB2 recognize and eliminate tumour cells, amplifying the immune response108.

To date, NVs represent a wide resource for enhancing the efficacy of cancer treatments by engineering macrophages. Ongoing research in this field will set the ground for the development of innovative NVs-based approaches for cancer immunotherapy.

Dendritic cells engineering: The rise of polymeric nanostructured platforms

At the interface between the innate and adaptive immune system, DCs play key roles in the TME and hold unexplored potential for cancer immunotherapy. DCs consist of multiple distinct subsets, including four major populations: plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), conventional DCs (cDCs), which are further split into type 1 (cDC1) and type 2 (cDC2), and monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs). Each subset possesses a different capacity for antigen presentation, migration, and cytokine secretion178.

moDCs comprise a heterogeneous population of APCs, mainly arising from monocyte precursors in response to inflammation. According to ex vivo studies, moDCs secrete a variety of cytokines, including IL1, TNFA, IL12, and IL23, that in turn stimulate CD4 and CD8 T cells polarization and activation179. However, prevailing models suggest that moDCs function mainly at a site of inflammation rather than migrating to lymph nodes. Therefore, despite the possibility of deriving potent moDC in vitro from peripheral blood-derived CD14+ monocytes with granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and IL4, the use of such preparations in immunotherapy is declining in favour of ‘naturally occurring’ DC, such as pDC or cDCs180.

pDCs are a specialized subset of dendritic cells derived from HSCs in the bone marrow, best known for their ability to sense and respond to viral infection through several mechanisms by the rapid production of high quantities of type one and type 3 IFN and other cytokines181. In the context of cancer, pDCs are a double-edged sword. On one side, pDCs have been implicated in cancer-associated immunosuppression, mainly through stimulation of pro-tumoural Tregs via secretion of inducible costimulatory ligand, which in turn suppresses anti-tumour immunity. On the other hand, pDCs can detect tumour cells mainly through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as TLR7 and TLR9, that recognize DAMPs or nucleic acid fragments released by stressed, dying, or necrotic cancer cells182.

cDC1, derived from HSCs in the bone marrow, are found in blood and among resident DC of lymph node, tonsil, spleen, bone marrow, and non-lymphoid tissues, skin, lung, intestine, and liver. For instance, cDC1s are known to be highly efficient in the cross-presentation on MHC1 molecules of exogenous antigens to CD8+ T cells, which, together with the high levels of expression of TLR3, TLR9, and TLR10, is crucial for anti-tumour immune response183. Moreover, a key marker of cDC1 is C-type lectin domain family nine member A (CLEC9A), a unique receptor that recognizes bare actin filaments exposed upon necrotic cell death and directs cell-associated antigens into the cross-presentation pathway. Expression of the XCR1 chemokine receptor is another conserved feature of mature cDC1, enabling close communication with activated T cells and NK cells producing the chemokine ligands. Lastly, cDC1 promotes T helper type 1 (Th1) and NK responses through IL12 production, leading to enhanced immune responses for clearing cancer cells184.

The major population of cDCs in human blood, tissues, and lymphoid organs is characterized as cDC2. In vitro studies highlighted the ability of cDC2 in priming CD4+ T cells via antigen presentation on MHC2 and consequently polarizing them toward anti-tumour Th1 or Th17 phenotypes185. Moreover, a vaccination study demonstrated that Th17 polarization of CD4+ T cells induced a downstream reprogramming of TAMs from a pro-tumoural M2-like phenotype to an anti-tumoural M1-like phenotype. Lastly, cDC2 has also demonstrated the capacity to cross-present antigen to CD8+ T cells, and its ability to secrete IL12 is greater than that of cDC1. All these features could be attributed to the wide range of PPRs equipping cDC2, as lectins, TLRs, nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors, and retinoic acid-inducible gene-1 (RIG1)-like receptors186.

Based on the above, there is increasing interest in modulating DC function to improve cancer immunotherapy, and many strategies have been developed to target DCs, such as the administration of tumour-associated antigens (TAAs) and adjuvants that mobilize and activate endogenous DCs, as well as the generation of DC-based vaccines, exploiting both in vivo and ex vivo strategies.

As CD8+ T cells are often the main effectors of antitumour immunity, promoting cross-presentation of TAAs by DCs is considered paramount. TAAs frequently used in the clinic for DC loading in vivo or in vitro to elicit antitumour T cell activation include differentiation antigens, overexpressed antigens, viral antigens, cancer-germ-line antigens, mutated neoantigens, and whole tumour antigens. However, antigen presentation can be impaired in cancer through various tumour-intrinsic and tumour-extrinsic mechanisms. Therefore, efforts combining adjuvants with antigens for in vivo provision are on the rise, exploiting both natural and synthetic molecules. Among natural molecules, some cytokines are worth mentioning, as GM-CSF, involved in cDC mobilization, attraction, and maturation, and FMS-like Tyrosine Kinase 3 Ligand, promoting cDC1 and cDC2 mobilization/expansion187. As synthetic adjuvants, several studies highlighted the efficacy of poly (I:C), a duplex of polyinosinic acid (poly I) and polycytidylic acid (poly C) mimicking viral dsRNA and acting as a TLR3 agonist, and unmethylated CpG oligodeoxynucleotides, acting as a TLR9 agonist. Lastly, several imidazoquinoline-based compounds have shown a strong immunomodulatory action, as R848 (Resiquimod), acting as a TLR7/8 agonist188.

Moreover, targeted delivery of antigens and adjuvants to DCs in vivo can further improve antitumour immunity, as demonstrated through preclinical efficacy and first clinical trials ongoing189. C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) have been used as preferential target receptors due to their diverse expression pattern on DCs. For instance, DEC205, CLEC9A, and langerin ligands have been used to target cDC1s, CLEC4A4 to target cDC2s, CLEC7A to target cDC2s and moDCs, CD209, mannose receptor, and macrophage galactose-type lectin to target predominantly cDC2s and moDCs190.

However, while the amount of TAAs and adjuvants that can be fused to these targeting molecules could be limited, the use of polymeric NVs is an appealing approach. Concerning DCs, medium-sized NVs (5–100 nm) most efficiently reach the lymph node, and negatively charged adjuvants, as poly (I:C) and unmethylated CpG oligodeoxynucleotides, are easily internalized in cationic NVs191. Notably, negatively charged NVs such as the FDA-approved PLGA promote DC maturation, cross-presentation, and TH1 cell polarization. For instance, Hanlon et al.192 demonstrated that human moDCs efficiently internalize anti-DEC205 antibody-coated PLGA NVs loaded with MART1 peptide, a melanocyte differentiation antigen that is commonly expressed in melanoma cells, displaying enhanced cross-priming activity compared with exposure to untargeted NVs. In another study191, anti-CLEC9A-coated PLGA NVs carrying a GP100 synthetic long peptide induced a robust CD8+ T cell priming ex vivo by human primary blood cDC1s. Kim et al.193 selected two TLR7/8 bi-specific imidazoquinoline-based esters for encapsulation in PLGA NVs and investigated their effectiveness in multiple tumour models (e.g., melanoma, bladder, and renal cell carcinoma), different vaccine designs (e.g., peptide-based, whole tumour cell lysate-based, and in situ vaccines), and vaccine modalities (prophylactic and therapeutic), showing that PLGA NVs trigger a robust immune response due to secretion of high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Lastly, Liu et al.194, synthesized CS-poly(acrylic acid) NVs loaded with R848 and MnCl2 to treat melanoma cells. Results achieved showed NVs accumulation at the tumour site, sustained release of MnCl2 and R848 at low pH, significantly increased infiltration of M1 macrophages and CD8+ T cells, and maturation of DCs by STING and NF-κB pathways.

DC vaccines comprise patient-derived DCs manipulated ex vivo, generally by isolating monocytes or HSPCs from peripheral blood. These cells are subsequently treated with adjuvant molecules or genetically engineered to induce differentiation, stimulated to induce maturation, and loaded with different TAAs. Although multiple clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and immunogenicity of DC-based vaccines, clinical responses are largely disappointing, with only one clinically approved DC-based vaccine, sipuleucel-T (Provenge), which consists of autologous blood DCs loaded with a recombinant fusion protein antigen composed of prostatic acid phosphatase and GM-CSF195. This limited success can be attributed in part to poor standardization and reproducibility of vaccines, as autologous DCs produced from patients’ monocytes ex vivo could present batch-to-batch inconsistency, and DC quality and function can be highly variable, presenting insufficient antigen presentation, migratory capacity, and cytokine release. On the other side, the immunosuppressive TME could inhibit DC function, promote DC tolerogenic phenotypes, or suppress T cell activation, down-streaming DC vaccination efficiency196. In this context, polymeric NVs offer a promising in vivo alternative to overcome the limitations of ex vivo manipulation. Here, we report some strategies adopted to produce in vivo DC vaccines. Rajput et al.197 have rationally engineered an NV-based delivery system for vaccines (InAc-NVs) using inulin acetate (InAc) as a polymer to target DCs with TAAs from melanoma. As a potent vaccine adjuvant, InAc-NVs activated TLR4 on multiple immune cells, including DCs, to secrete various cytokines and promoted DCs maturation as observed by a decreased phagocytic ability and enhanced capability to express increased levels of various maturation markers (MHC1, MHC2, CD40, and CD80). Importantly, InAc-NVs provided complete protection in 100% of the vaccinated mice from metastasis of intravenously injected melanoma cells (B16-F10) to lungs197.

In another study, Heo and colleagues117 synthesized multifunctional PLGA NVs that can deliver TAAs and siRNA for immunosuppressive genes SOCS1 (suppressor of cytokine signaling 1) to DCs simultaneously. The encapsulation efficiency of hydrophilic SOCS1 siRNA into hydrophobic PLGA matrix was drastically enhanced by adding a tumour model antigen such as ovalbumin (OVA), and the knockdown of SOCS1 in DCs enhanced expression of TNFA, IL6, IL12, and IL2 pro-inflammatory cytokines and elicited cross-presentation in OVA-specific CD8+ T cells117.

Lastly, Ouyang and colleagues118 genetically engineered DCs through silencing their YTHDF1 protein (an important reader protein responsible for RNA m6A methylation) expression via a dendrimeric NV. PAMAM dendrimers of V generation were partially decorated with mannose and 1,3-propanesultone and then entrapped with Au nanoparticles, enabling targeted gene silencing of DCs overexpressing mannose receptors to upregulate the expression of CD80 and CD86, markers of DC maturation, potentially leading to tumour antigen cross-presentation. In conclusion, pNVs could overcome key limitations of traditional DC manipulation strategies by improving the delivery of antigens, adjuvants, or genetic material towards the development of innovative DC-based ATMPs for clinical cancer treatment.

Nanovectors delivery strategies for immune engineering in secondary lymphoid organs

All the immune cell populations previously described—and widely exploited in immunotherapeutic strategies—not only reside within the tumour microenvironment (TME) but are also highly enriched in secondary lymphoid organs such as lymph nodes (LNs) and the spleen. These organs serve as central sites for the initiation of rapid and coordinated immune responses, making in situ engineering of immune cells within them an attractive strategy to boost antitumour immunity. However, achieving selective and efficient delivery of nanomaterials to these immunological hubs remains challenging, due to substantial off-target clearance and the complex anatomical barriers that regulate lymphoid trafficking198.

LNs are strategically distributed throughout the body and present multiple physiological barriers to nanocarrier delivery199. First, lymphatic vessels function as molecular sieves: NVs <10 nm rapidly diffuse through capillaries and undergo fast clearance; particles between 10 and 100 nm can passively drain through inter-endothelial junctions; NVs >100 nm are generally retained within the interstitium and are transported into LNs predominantly via antigen-presenting cells (APCs). Surface charge further affects uptake, with positively charged NVs showing more efficient LN entry than neutral or negatively charged ones200. NVs deformability also facilitates lymphatic access.

A second barrier is the macrophage-rich subcapsular sinus, which efficiently removes large NVs. PEGylation and the use of APC-targeted larger NVs have been proposed to overcome this limitation. A third challenge lies in the narrow diameter of the LN conduits, where T and B cells reside, restricting internal nanoparticles penetration199.

To address these obstacles, Xu et al.156 developed mannan-decorated, pathogen-like PLA-PEI NVs loaded with tumour antigens and the TLR9 agonist CpG, improving DC recognition and LN accumulation157. Surface functionalization is one of the most common strategies to enhance LN targeting. PEGylation increases lymphatic drainage by reducing stromal interactions, although it may reduce LN retention201. Wang et al. 202 engineered mesoporous polydopamine NVs loaded with the TLR7 agonist imiquimod and coated with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), preventing stromal binding and enabling slow cargo release.

The same group developed PLGA NVs coated with dendritic cell membranes, functionalized with anti-CD3ε antibodies, and loaded with imiquimod, generating artificial APCs that showed enhanced LN accumulation and potent polyclonal and antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell activation203. Additional approaches include ROS-responsive PEG-b-PAsp-PBE NVs carrying the TRP2 antigen, which promote DC uptake, LN retention, and T-cell priming204, as well as oxidation-sensitive PEG-bl-PPS filomicelles that switch to spherical micelles under oxidative conditions, optimizing lymphatic transport and sustained antigen release205. PEG-PE micelles have also been shown to directly bind CD8α+ DCs in LNs and enhance antigen presentation206. Finally, a thiolated PPS platform based on oxanorbornadiene (OND) linkers has been developed to deliver small- and medium-sized molecules to lymphocyte subsets via a controlled two-stage fragmentation process207.

The spleen represents another essential immunological hub, filtering blood-borne antigens and orchestrating adaptive immune responses. Its complex architecture—comprising trabeculae supporting vasculature and highly organized red and white pulp—makes it a natural accumulator of nanoparticles208. Yet, splenic targeting is also hindered by three primary barriers: formation of a protein corona (leading to macrophage-mediated clearance), size- and charge-dependent sequestration in the liver or kidneys, and macrophage interception in the marginal zone before NVs can reach T and B cells209. NVs <5 nm are typically cleared by the kidneys, whereas hepatic capture decreases with increasing NV size. NVs of 100–200 nm exhibit improved ability to avoid marginal-zone macrophages. Non-spherical shapes and deformable materials further enhance traversal of splenic sinuses209. To minimize protein corona formation, surface modification with PEG or other hydrophilic polymers is commonly employed. Although cationic NVs exhibit high cellular uptake due to interactions with negatively charged membranes, they also bind more serum proteins. Targeting ligands such as anti-CD205 (for DCs) or anti-CD3 (for T cells) can promote selective engagement of splenic immune cells210.

Recently, emerging nanoparticle platforms have shown the ability to selectively deliver mRNA or other nucleic acids to splenic DCs following systemic administration, even without ligand-mediated targeting. Ben-Akiva et al.119 developed biodegradable lipophilic PBAE NVs carrying antigen-encoding mRNA, achieving high antigen expression and potent DC-mediated CD8+ T-cell priming. Zwitterionic functionalization of cationic polymers has likewise yielded improved serum stability, uptake, and endosomal escape, enabling efficient mRNA delivery to spleen and LNs with high protein expression211.

Taken together, current evidence highlights that while secondary lymphoid organs represent ideal anatomical sites for immune-cell engineering, their unique structural and cellular features impose significant barriers to NVs trafficking. Emerging strategies, such as adaptive or stimuli-responsive nanomaterials, membrane-coated carriers, and platforms capable of delivering nucleic acids directly to resident APCs, illustrate a promising path forward.

Clinical trials on ATMP for cancer immunotherapy involving polymeric nanovectors

While several studies on cancer models have shown promising results about pNVs for drug/gene delivery, a deep understanding of the action mechanisms exerted by polymeric nanomaterials in vivo is essential to achieve clinical translation. Currently, over 50 polymer-based formulations are undergoing clinical trials for cancer treatment212, providing compelling evidence of the potential of pNVs as ATMPs for cancer immunotherapy. The first polymer-based formulations developed for cancer treatment were primarily drug--polymer conjugates, where therapeutic agents were covalently linked to the polymer to improve solubility, stability, and circulation time. For instance, PEG-conjugated IL15 agonist (NKTR-255) is being tested in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) patients post-CAR-T therapy to enhance immune efficacy (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT05664217)213, and a phase one trial is ongoing to assess the safety and efficacy of NKTR-255 combined with TIL therapy in refractory non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT05676749)214.

Later advancements led to the development of polymeric NVs, in which drugs, adjuvant molecules, or nucleic acids were physically encapsulated or adsorbed within nanoscale polymeric carriers, to offer a more controlled release beyond targeted delivery, marking a distinct evolution in polymer-based cancer therapeutics. An example is represented by PLGA NVs encapsulating threitolceramide-6 (IMM60), which activates the invariant NKT (iNKT) cells, and the NY-ESO-1 (oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma-1) peptide to treat NY-ESO-1 positive cancer patients. This vaccine is currently evaluated in a phase one clinical study (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT04751786)215.

Beyond peptide/protein vaccines, cationic polymers like PEI can enhance DNA vaccines by boosting antigen expression and immune responses. In an early phase one trial (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT04049864), a PEI-DNA vaccine showed promising safety and immunogenicity in relapsed neuroblastoma patients’ post-chemotherapy and stem cell transplant216.

While some polymer-based immunotherapies have shown strong promise in preclinical and early clinical studies, other trials, as β-CD NVs delivering siRNA for solid tumours, have been terminated, underscoring the challenges in translating these therapies into clinical success (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT00689065)217.

Poly-ICLC CMC, a TLR3 agonist made of CMC, polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid, and PLL dsRNA, mimics microbial dsRNA, stimulating DCs and inflammasome pathways against various cancers and achieving preliminary results in glioblastoma (GBM) (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT02078648)218.

A phase 2 trial (ClinicalTrial.gov ID: NCT01204684) demonstrated that combining autologous tumour lysate-pulsed DC (ATL-DC) with poly-ICLC enhanced IFN responses in CD8+ T cells and monocytes, effectively delaying malignant glioma progression219.

Although polymeric nanomaterials have shown great promise in preclinical and clinical studies, their potential toxicity and biosafety must be thoroughly assessed before they can be widely used in clinical practice. Toxicity remains one of the main barriers to the FDA approval of polymer-based nanodrugs. Once administered, pNVs can interact with various organs, tissues, cells, and biomolecules, potentially triggering harmful biological responses such as oxidative stress and the activation of apoptotic signalling pathways. Comprehensive evaluation of the safety, tolerability, and therapeutic effectiveness of these innovative polymeric nanoformulations in cancer patients through clinical trials is essential to enable their adoption as part of standard cancer treatment.

Future directions and perspectives

Immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment, providing a more targeted approach with fewer side effects. FDA-approved CAR-T cell therapy, achieved by T cell engineering, represents one of the main successful examples of ATMPs128. However, researchers still face several challenges to enhance immunotherapy effectiveness also in solid tumours, and to simplify and ensure the safety of the treatment220. The versatility of nanomaterials paved the way for novel approaches in precision medicine, especially in oncology, representing a promising tool to improve current immunotherapies based on ATMPs. Indeed, their use underscores the advantages of non-viral vector-based immune-cell engineering over traditional viral gene-delivery systems, while also expanding the principles of precision medicine to advanced drug-delivery systems13.

This review provides an overview of current advances in the application of pNVs in immunotherapy with their relative benefits and drawbacks, deepening the recent progress of drug and gene delivery by pNVs. Despite rapid advances in preclinical research, their translation into clinical practice remains far from successful. To fully harness their therapeutic potential, future research must strategically address several key scientific, technological, and regulatory challenges.

The use of non-viral vectors for gene delivery minimizes risks associated with immunogenicity, insertional mutagenesis, limited cargo loading, and complexity of manufacturing while offering advantages in scalability, manufacturing, and cost–effectiveness. pNVs can be loaded with a broad range of genetic cargo, such as pDNA, siRNA, and mRNA for gene editing and functionalized to optimize immune targeting abilities and to reduce off-target effects. Several studies suggest that combining natural and synthetic polymers for non-viral vectors synthesis may offer synergic effects for a more effective gene delivery strategy. For instance, CAR-T therapies based on viral vectors are notably successful, but innovation is necessary to address key challenges and enhance the long-term sustainability of this approach. Perhaps, the use of non-viral vectors exploiting the wide range of polymeric nanomaterials could represent a suitable alternative for developing more effective CAR-T therapies. Following the success of CAR-T therapy, this strategy has been expanded to engineer other immune cells, such as NK and macrophages. Indeed, pNVs could provide a promising platform for modulating TAM behaviour by delivering immunomodulators, for instance, for their repolarization toward antitumour phenotypes. In the case of NK engineering, the traditional electroporation method could be bypassed by nanotechnology-based delivery systems. Similarly, dendritic cells have emerged as an appealing target for non-viral vector-mediated engineering owing to their central role in orchestrating antitumour immune responses. Indeed, pNV-based strategies can enhance DCs activation and antigen-presentation capacity by delivering nucleic acids encoding tumour antigens, co-stimulatory molecules, or modulators of DC maturation, generating more potent and durable T-cell priming. Although the above-mentioned clinical trials with different polymeric materials, the obstacles in clinical translation of pNVs for immune cells engineering include: i) Biological barriers — Immune cells have robust mechanisms to protect themselves from foreign materials; thus, NVs used for gene delivery can cause toxicity, undesired cell activation, compromising cell viability and function. Moreover, the various cell types of the immune system (i.e., T cells, macrophages) show different phenotypes, surface markers, and activation states, making common targeting tricky. Concerning T cells, NV-mediated gene delivery is hampered by their quiescent state and limited capacity for endocytosis, leading to low transfection efficiency85,221. NK cells are difficult to manipulate due to their limited proliferation and their inherent resistance to genetic modification, making controlled gene expression challenging, while macrophages, which easily phagocytose NVs, just as easily degrade the delivered genetic material222. Comparably, the limited success of DCs engineering can be attributed in part to poor standardization and reproducibility of vaccines, as autologous DCs produced from patients’ monocytes ex vivo could present batch-to-batch inconsistency, and the immunosuppressive TME could promote DC tolerogenic phenotypes; ii) Manufacturing process standardization — The main difficulties remain reproducible manufacturing, scale-up, and maintaining batch-to-batch consistency under good manufacturing practice (GMP) conditions, in addition to storage stability of polymer formulations. Standardizing production pipelines, implementing modular manufacturing platforms, and developing robust analytical tools for characterization and release testing will be critical steps in bridging this gap. The establishment of clear safety and efficacy benchmarks for non-viral systems can aid in accelerating clinical usage5; iii) Technical and clinical evaluation — Lack of standardized characterization methods and limited clinical data on safety and efficacy in gene delivery immunotherapy restricts adoption and investment. ATMPs require highly specialized work to optimize the transition from research to clinical application, improving all procedures, and clinical trials serve as good examples to follow.

Therefore, careful tuning of nanomaterials' physicochemical properties is essential to mitigate these adverse effects and ensure patient safety. Nanomaterial-based therapies require standardized protocols and collaborative efforts between researchers and clinicians, essentially to address the transition from research to clinical application. Computational approaches through machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven modelling could predict polymer structure-function relationships, optimize formulation parameters for specific immune cell targets, and design delivery systems with enhanced therapeutic indices. Such approaches will be particularly valuable in the context of personalized medicine, where patient-specific tumour profiles and immune landscapes may require tailored NV designs. With advancements in nanotechnology, the ability to create ‘universal’ or ‘off-the-shelf’ NVs, or to integrate multifunctionalities in one platform, will extend the range of patients who could be effectively treated by cell immunotherapy, heralding a new era of precision medicine.

Achieving their full clinical potential will require interdisciplinary collaboration, technological innovation, and a deliberate focus on translational science. As these challenges are met, pNVs are poised to redefine the future of ATMPs, enabling safer, scalable, and truly personalized cancer treatments.
