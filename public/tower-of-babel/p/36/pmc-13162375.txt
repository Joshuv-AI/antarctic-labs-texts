Impacts of opioid use, postoperative pain, pain interference, and side effects on postoperative psychological distress: a retrospective study of the PAIN OUT registry in seven Asian regions
Authors: Yuchang Bao, Chi Wai Cheung, Yulin Huang, Ruth Zaslansky, Painout ASPIRE Network, Winfried Meissner, Hung Chak Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative psychological distress is highly prevalent in Asian surgical populations, but the relationships among opioid exposure at different perioperative stages, pain characteristics, side effects, and postoperative anxiety and helplessness remain poorly understood.MethodsA total of 6,105 patients from seven Asian regions in the PAIN OUT registry were included. Opioid use during the preoperative, intraoperative, recovery room, and ward stages was converted to oral morphine equivalents (OMEs). Four dimensions of postoperative outcomes– psychological distress (anxiety, helplessness), pain intensity, pain interference, and side effects– were assessed using the International Pain Outcomes Questionnaire (IPO-Q). Multinomial logistic regressions were used to examine the associations.ResultsModerate-to-severe anxiety and helplessness were reported by 42.6% and 27.9% of patients, respectively. Time spent in severe pain showed the strongest associations with anxiety (moderate phase: OR = 2.711 [2.238, 3.285] for moderate anxiety; OR = 2.295 [1.72, 3.071] for severe anxiety), while maximum pain severity was less predictive. Sleep interference was most strongly associated with helplessness (severe interference: OR = 3.307 [2.317, 4.720] for severe helplessness; OR = 2.738 [1.94, 3.86] for severe anxiety). Common side effects– dizziness (ORs: 1.535–2.643), drowsiness (ORs: 1.426–1.881), and itching (ORs: 1.428–1.917)—were significantly associated with both anxiety and helplessness. For opioid exposure, higher intraoperative and ward OMEs were modestly associated with lower distress (ORs: 0.969–0.982 per 10-mg increment), while preoperative and recovery-room OMEs showed no significant associations. Counterintuitively, moderate-to-severe maximum pain was associated with lower odds of moderate helplessness (ORs: 0.679–0.687).ConclusionIn this Asian cohort, pain duration and sleep disruption were more strongly associated with postoperative psychological distress than pain severity. The timing of opioid administration (intraoperative and ward) showed modest associations, suggesting that when opioids are used may be as important as the total dose. Integrated postoperative care should prioritize rapid resolution of prolonged pain episodes, proactive nighttime symptom management, and structured monitoring of common opioid-related side effects to reduce early postoperative distress.Keywords: Postoperative psychological distress, Anxiety, Helpless, Opioids, Postoperative pain

Background

In postoperative patient care, the importance of addressing both physical and psychological aspects of recovery is increasingly recognized. Postoperative emotional disturbances, such as irritability, and psychological distress, including anxiety and insomnia, are highly prevalent among surgical patients, even after uncomplicated procedures [1]. Common manifestations include stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms [1–3]. A systematic review found that over 20% of patients experienced clinically significant postoperative stress and anxiety symptoms [4]. Even with rigorous diagnostic criteria, nearly 9% of participants exhibited distress symptoms following surgery [5, 6]. These issues may be equally or more prevalent in Asian regions. Systematic reviews indicate that Asian surgical populations have elevated rates of perioperative psychological distress. A meta-analysis of studies from low- and middle-income countries found that Asia had the highest pooled prevalence of preoperative anxiety at 62.59%, significantly exceeding rates in other regions [7]. Similarly, postoperative psychological distress has been reported as substantial in some Chinese surgical cohorts; for example, a Chinese cohort study reported postoperative anxiety in 56.8% of patients on postoperative day 1 following orthognathic surgery [8]. These elevated rates may reflect differences in healthcare system structures, limited integration of psychological support services, and cultural factors influencing the expression of distress and help-seeking behaviors [9–11]. These psychological symptoms can persist long-term and negatively affect postoperative health outcomes and complications [12].

Despite advances in surgical and analgesic techniques, postoperative pain remains a significant concern. A substantial proportion of patients experience moderate to severe pain after surgery [13–15]. Postoperative pain is a critical factor linked to negative psychological responses after surgery [16–18]. The relationship between postoperative pain and psychological distress involves several interconnected factors. Pain can contribute to anxiety and helplessness, while neurobiological mechanisms, such as activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, may intensify pain sensitivity [19, 20]. Inadequate pain control can lead to pain catastrophizing and exacerbate anxiety, creating a self-reinforcing cycle [21]. Physiological responses to both pain and anxiety, such as increased heart rate, blood pressure, and cortisol levels, can also mutually amplify each other [22]. Given these complexities, effective pain management with analgesic drugs, such as opioids, may play a significant role in modifying postoperative psychological distress. Adequate pain control can reduce anxiety, break the pain-anxiety cycle, and help patients feel more in control during recovery [23]. This can improve the overall postoperative experience and mental well-being. However, opioids may also have adverse effects that contribute to psychological distress, such as nausea, dizziness, and sedation, complicating the overall recovery experience [24].

Understanding the relationship among postoperative pain, opioid use, side effects, and postoperative psychological outcomes is essential for improving patients’ pain management. While previous research has established links between postoperative pain and psychological distress, few studies have examined how opioids used at different stages of the operative process and the side effects experienced by patients may jointly influence postoperative psychological distress. More importantly, no studies have focused on Asian regions. These regions typically have health systems and patterns of opioid use that are markedly different from those in Europe and North America. A study comparing opioid use in the United States and the rest of the world found that U.S. patients had an extremely high prevalence of prescribed opioid use (91%) compared with non-U.S. patients (5%) [25]. In contrast, patients in Asian countries (e.g., China) had a high rate of opioid use during and immediately after surgery (within 24 h), although health professionals in Asian countries may have varying levels of opioid-related knowledge [26]. A recent study in China found that 68% of 26,193 adult patients with systemic opioid use received opioids on the first day after surgery, mostly through intravenous patient-controlled analgesia [23]. Another study in China found that 48.33% of hospitalized patients with acute pain were administered analgesics [27]. However, health professionals (e.g., fellows and residents) may have limited knowledge of certain opioids, such as oxycodone, despite being familiar with common opioids like morphine and fentanyl [27]. This knowledge may influence their opioid use during operations, as suggested above. More importantly, a study reported a high prevalence of moderate-to-severe pain on the first day after surgery (48.7%) in China and found a strong association among pain severity, poor recovery, and patient satisfaction [23]. These differences may affect the impact of postoperative pain on psychological distress.

This study aims to address these gaps by examining the associations between opioid use at different stages of the operative process, postoperative pain, and side effects with postoperative psychological distress in seven Asian regions, based on self-reported patient questionnaires.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study used anonymous secondary data from the PAIN OUT ASPIRE registry [28]. PAIN OUT ASPIRE is a one-year project of the PAIN OUT registry conducted in 2018 that collected anonymous information on pain outcomes and management from 25 tertiary hospitals across seven Asian regions: Indonesia, Singapore, Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (China), Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, the Philippines, and Chinese Mainland [28]. The project aimed to enhance clinicians’ decision-making and improve postoperative outcomes for patients in Asian regions. Patients were eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) on postoperative day 1 (POD 1) and returned to the ward for at least 6 h; (3) underwent intraoperative procedures; (4) had no cognitive impairment; and (5) provided consent to participate in the survey assessing pain-related outcomes. Patients were approached once by a trained surveyor on the first postoperative day. Exclusion criteria for the current analysis included: (1) missing or unrated pain-related evaluations on the International Pain Outcomes Questionnaire (IPO-Q); (2) missing information on opioid dosage at any of the four perioperative stages (preoperative, intraoperative, recovery room, or ward); and (3) missing demographic information (e.g., age, sex, or BMI). Of the 9,130 patients initially recorded in the PAIN OUT ASPIRE registry, 6,105 were retained after removing those with missing values.

We declare that this study met the requirements and standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. The analysis of anonymous secondary data for this study was approved by the Human and Artefacts Ethics Sub-Committee, City University of Hong Kong (Code: HU-STA-00001478). Consent was obtained from each participant at the participating hospitals of PAIN OUT ASPIRE. The use of anonymous secondary data for this study was also approved by the administrative team of the PAIN OUT registry.

Oral morphine equivalents (OMEs)

We assessed opioid use with oral morphine equivalents (OMEs). Four OME measures were used in this study: preoperative OMEs, intraoperative OMEs, recovery room OMEs, and ward OMEs. These measures captured opioid use during the preoperative, intraoperative, recovery room, and ward stages. For each OME measure, opioid doses were converted to oral OMEs using conversion factors adopted from previous studies [28, 29]. This conversion table was developed by systematically collecting and synthesizing conversion factors from clinical guidelines, peer-reviewed literature, and professional societies across Europe, Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States. The derived conversion factors reflect broad international consensus rather than relying on a single institutional table or regional guidelines, and they account for diverse opioid formulations and routes of administration, making them suitable for the multinational settings of our study. This standardization also enables more accurate comparisons of opioid use across patients and over time, especially given the multinational nature of the dataset. Additionally, the use of OMEs normalizes differences in prescribing practices and accounts for the varying potencies of opioids, providing a more precise representation of the association between total analgesic effect and postoperative psychological distress [29]. Postoperative outcomes

Four dimensions of postoperative outcomes, assessed by the International Pain Outcomes Questionnaire (IPO-Q) [30], were used in this study: pain-related psychological distress, pain intensity, pain interference, and side effects. Pain-related psychological distress included postoperative anxiety and postoperative helplessness. Pain intensity included maximum pain, minimum pain, and time spent in severe pain. Side effects included nausea, drowsiness, itching, and dizziness. Pain interference included interference with in-bed activities, interference with out-of-bed activities, sleep interference, and interference with deep breathing or coughing. All items were measured on an 11-point scale. We adopted the classification scheme from [30], which used a computed ABC analysis to categorize all items as mild/none, moderate, or severe. Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables to summarize the sample characteristics. Bivariate Pearson correlations among study variables were computed. To address the multiple-testing problem and control the family-wise Type I error rate, p-values were adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method.

To examine the associations between opioid use and postoperative psychological distress, as well as the influence of pain and side effects, multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted. Separate multinomial logistic regression models were constructed for two key health outcomes: postoperative anxiety and postoperative helplessness.

In each model, we assumed that the interaction between postoperative anxiety and postoperative helplessness was the primary factor that could interfere with each other, while other determinants were considered additional factors. When postoperative anxiety was the key outcome, postoperative helplessness was included as a covariate. The same approach was used to estimate effects on postoperative helplessness.

Based on this assumption, we used the following factors as independent variables: preoperative OMEs, intraoperative OMEs, recovery room OMEs, ward OMEs, maximum pain, minimum pain, time spent in severe pain, interference with in-bed activities, interference with out-of-bed activities, sleep interference, deep breathing or coughing, nausea, drowsiness, itching, and dizziness. All OME-related variables were continuous, with OME intervals set at 10 mg. Other variables were categorical, derived from the cut-offs listed in Table 2. The “mild / none” level served as the reference category. The following variables were included as covariates: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and type of surgery. These were selected for their clinical relevance and prior literature demonstrating their association with postoperative pain outcomes and opioid use [31, 32].

The results of the multinomial logistic regression analyses were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). An odds ratio greater than 1 indicates an increased likelihood of the outcome, while an odds ratio less than 1 indicates a decreased likelihood. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. Following previous studies, we categorized multicollinearity as follows: (1) VIF < 5: no evidence of multicollinearity; (2) VIF between 5 and 10: moderate multicollinearity; (3) VIF > 10: serious multicollinearity [33].

Sensitivity analysis

Postoperative anxiety and postoperative helplessness were not only factors that may interfere with each other but also highly correlated variables. Therefore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted as follows: (1) to compare the correlation between postoperative anxiety and postoperative helplessness using our analytic data; and (2) to conduct multinomial logistic regression analyses without including postoperative anxiety or helplessness as a covariate for comparison.

Multicollinearity tests indicated that all variables had VIF < 5, although the VIFs for maximum pain were marginal (postoperative anxiety model: 4.963; postoperative helplessness model: 4.770). This indicated that, despite relatively high correlations among several variables in this study (Table 4), our statistical analyses were not affected by significant multicollinearity.

Postoperative anxiety

A significant association was found between maximum pain intensity and severe postoperative anxiety, but not with moderate anxiety (Table 5). Patients with moderate maximum pain were 90.6% more likely to have severe postoperative anxiety than those who reported “mild/none” (OR: 1.906 [1.358, 2.676]). For patients reporting severe maximum pain, the OR for postoperative anxiety increased to 2.164 [1.457, 3.214]. The increasing ORs from moderate to severe maximum pain indicate that higher maximum pain is associated with higher odds of severe postoperative anxiety. Minimum pain showed a different pattern. Moderate minimum pain was associated with higher odds of both moderate anxiety (OR = 1.469 [1.208, 1.785]) and severe anxiety (OR = 1.378 [1.056, 1.799]). Interestingly, severe minimum pain was not significantly associated with postoperative anxiety at any level. Regarding time spent in severe pain, significant effects were found during the moderate phase, and the effect sizes were large. Specifically, the moderate phase of time spent in severe pain was associated with higher odds of both moderate and severe postoperative anxiety, with ORs of 2.711 [2.238, 3.285] and 2.295 [1.715, 3.071], respectively.

Postoperative helplessness

Pain intensity showed mixed results regarding postoperative helplessness (Table 5). Both moderate minimum pain (OR = 1.409, 95% CI [1.110, 1.789]) and severe minimum pain (OR = 1.497, 95% CI [1.053, 2.129]) were associated with higher odds of moderate postoperative helplessness. However, only severe minimum pain was associated with higher odds of severe postoperative helplessness (OR = 1.855, 95% CI [1.278, 2.693]). A moderate phase of time spent in severe pain was associated with higher odds of moderate postoperative helplessness (OR = 1.446, 95% CI [1.106, 1.890]). Surprisingly, moderate and severe maximum pain were associated with lower odds of moderate postoperative helplessness (OR = 0.679, 95% CI [0.516, 0.893] and OR = 0.687, 95% CI [0.489, 0.967]).

OMEs showed patterns similar to those observed for postoperative anxiety (Table 6). Higher intraoperative and ward OMEs were associated with lower odds of moderate postoperative helplessness, with a marginal effect size (OR = 0.982 [0.968, 0.997]; OR = 0.982 [0.965, 0.999]). Higher intraoperative OMEs were also associated with lower odds of severe postoperative helplessness, with a marginal effect size (OR = 0.969 [0.952, 0.985]).

Our analysis showed that greater severity of side effects was associated with higher odds of postoperative helplessness (Table 7). Severe dizziness was linked to increased odds of both moderate and severe postoperative helplessness, with a stronger association for severe helplessness (OR = 1.507 [1.069, 2.125]; OR = 2.643 [1.847, 3.783]). Similarly, moderate drowsiness and itchiness were associated with higher odds of moderate helplessness (OR = 1.491 [1.164, 1.911]; OR = 1.459 [1.065, 2.000]), while severe drowsiness and itchiness were associated with higher odds of severe postoperative helplessness (OR = 1.881 [1.338, 2.645]; OR = 1.917 [1.293, 2.840]). Interestingly, moderate nausea was associated with lower odds of severe postoperative helplessness (OR = 0.555 [0.395, 0.780]).

We also found significant associations between pain interference and postoperative helplessness (Table 8). Sleep interference showed a strong relationship with postoperative helplessness. Both moderate and severe sleep interference were associated with higher odds of moderate helplessness (OR = 2.406 [1.875, 3.087]; OR = 1.818 [1.299, 2.544]) and severe helplessness (OR = 1.703 [1.214, 2.388]; OR = 3.307 [2.317, 4.720]), with the strongest association observed between severe sleep interference and severe helplessness. Moderate interference with in-bed activities increased the odds of moderate helplessness (OR = 1.796, 95% CI [1.346, 2.395]), while severe interference with in-bed activities was linked to both moderate (OR = 1.768 [1.233, 2.534]) and severe helplessness (OR = 1.708 [1.137, 2.567]). For interference with out-of-bed activities, moderate interference was associated with both moderate and severe helplessness (OR = 1.504 [1.103, 2.051]; OR = 1.814 [1.185, 2.775]), but severe interference with out-of-bed activities increased only the odds of severe postoperative helplessness (OR = 2.122 [1.304, 3.453]). Moderate or severe interference due to deep breathing or coughing also increased the odds of experiencing moderate postoperative helplessness (OR = 1.360 [1.033, 1.791]; OR = 1.599 [1.153, 2.217]) and severe helplessness (OR = 1.422 [1.010, 2.004]; OR = 2.229 [1.538, 3.230]).

Sensitivity analysis

Postoperative anxiety and postoperative helplessness were highly correlated (r = 0.78). After removing postoperative helplessness as a covariate (Tables 9, 10, 11 and 12), moderate nausea, moderate or severe interference with deep breathing or coughing, moderate interference with out-of-bed activities, and severe interference with in-bed activities were significantly associated with severe postoperative anxiety. However, the association between moderate interference with in-bed activities and moderate postoperative anxiety was no longer significant.

Discussion

Interpretation of results

This study examined the association between postoperative pain, side effects, and opioid use at different stages of the operative process, and their effects on postoperative anxiety and helplessness in seven East Asian regions. Our findings showed that postoperative distress was prevalent in these regions. Over 40% of patients reported moderate to severe postoperative anxiety, and 28.5% reported moderate to severe postoperative helplessness. The self-reported rate of postoperative psychological distress among our participants was higher than that reported in previous studies conducted primarily in Western populations [34–36]. For example, a large retrospective cohort study found that the prevalence of postoperative depressive symptoms ranged from 8.6% to 18.8% between 2004 and 2021 across different types of surgery [35]. Additionally, Sveinsdóttir and Zoëga [36] found in a prospective study that 20.2% and 5.6% of patients reported moderate to severe symptoms of anxiety and depressive symptoms immediately after discharge. The high prevalence of postoperative psychological distress may be due to differences in healthcare practices. Unlike Western settings, where multidisciplinary psychological support is increasingly integrated into postoperative care [9], Asian healthcare systems prioritize physical recovery, with limited resources for mental health screening or intervention [10]. The difference between our study and others suggests a potential influence of local health systems, underscoring the need for targeted interventions to address these psychological challenges in postoperative care across Asian regions.

Building on previous studies, we examined the association between opioid use at different stages of the operative process and postoperative psychological distress in seven East Asian regions. In our study, higher intraoperative and ward OMEs were associated with lower odds of postoperative anxiety and helplessness. Specifically, each 10 mg increase in intraoperative or ward opioid administration was associated with a 1.8% to 3.1% reduction in the odds of postoperative anxiety and helplessness. This finding is somewhat consistent with previous studies indicating that opioid use may be linked to reduced psychological distress due to their chemical properties and interactions with neurological systems [37]. However, the effect sizes were marginal. Additionally, we did not observe any significant relationships between opioid use in the preoperative period or recovery room and postoperative anxiety and helplessness. These results suggest that (1) postoperative management should address multiple aspects of care and treatment rather than relying solely on opioid use, and (2) the effects of opioids may depend on the timing of anesthetic administration [38, 39]. The duration of opioid treatment in the recovery room may be too brief to significantly affect postoperative anxiety. Specifically, the timing of opioid administration may be more important than the total amount used, as excessive opioid use can also lead to negative effects and increased mortality [40].

Another interesting finding in our study is the variability in the relationships between different pain intensity levels and postoperative anxiety. Specifically, time spent in severe pain was significantly associated with postoperative anxiety, with large effect sizes (ORs = 2.295–3.442). However, only maximum pain showed a significant association with severe postoperative anxiety, with greater pain linked to higher odds. Only moderate minimum pain was associated with higher odds of moderate and severe anxiety. These results indicate that not all types of pain intensities are good indicators of postoperative anxiety. Based on our findings, the duration of severe pain may be the key determinant of postoperative anxiety, rather than pain severity (maximum and minimum pain).

Compared with postoperative anxiety, the associations between pain intensity and postoperative helplessness showed mixed results in this study. Although previous studies suggested that greater pain intensity is associated with more psychological distress, our results indicated that moderate and severe maximum pain were associated with lower odds of moderate postoperative helplessness. Only moderate and severe minimum pain, as well as moderate time spent in severe pain, were associated with higher odds of moderate postoperative helplessness. Only severe minimum pain was positively associated with severe helplessness. Although the mechanism is not clear, these results may be due to (1) the priority of postoperative care and (2) interference from the interaction between postoperative anxiety and helplessness. Specifically, our results were based on retrospective survey information at POD 1. All patients received 24h of postoperative care, including opioid use, before completing the survey. The question about helplessness was intended to assess how pain may have affected a patient since their surgery. It is also important to note that average ward OME use was relatively high in this study (mean = 45.4 mg; SD = 80.5 mg). Therefore, we may expect our results to align with the fear-avoidance model, emphasizing appraisals of controllability [41]. Given the high level of opioid use, even the chemical components of OMEs may have only a marginal effect on postoperative helplessness; a “placebo” effect may arise from frequent opioid use and related postoperative care (e.g., clinical checks), which may further enhance patients’ perceptions of active management for prioritized pain care [23]. Such “placebo” effects from interventions may lead to externalized control beliefs (e.g., “my pain is being managed by opioids”), which ultimately reduce postoperative helplessness [42]. Therefore, only minimum pain, an indicator of less severe pain, was associated with higher odds of postoperative helplessness.

The interference from the interaction between postoperative anxiety and helplessness is also evident in sensitivity analyses. When postoperative anxiety was removed as a covariate, the association between severe maximum pain and lower odds of moderate helplessness became insignificant. Notably, a significant association with a large effect size was found between severe maximum pain and severe helplessness (OR = 2.400, 95% CI [1.715–3.357]). This suggests a potential failure in fear-avoidance conditions due to the interconnected relationship among anxiety, helplessness, and opioid use.

The results above further highlight the importance of investigating associations between side effects and psychological distress. Although our findings indicated large effect sizes of side-effect-related variables on both postoperative anxiety and helplessness, the patterns varied. Results for postoperative helplessness showed a progressive, gradual increase in risk with severe side effects. Severe side effects were generally associated with higher ORs for severe helplessness than for moderate helplessness. These findings suggest a potential mechanism within 24h after surgery that may be influenced by the interplay among patient care, anxiety, and helplessness. Specifically, severe dizziness can be associated with a temporary loss of self-care ability, necessitating considerable nursing support. This dependency may diminish functional autonomy and, in turn, heighten feelings of helplessness [43]. However, postoperative care is often constrained by limited resources. Although most postoperative care protocols include clear antiemetic guidelines, nurses and other medical staff may not be able to monitor each patient in a timely or frequent manner unless serious issues are identified. As our results were independent of the effect of postoperative anxiety, underlying factors related to delays in patient care and fear-avoidant conditions may explain the dose-response relationship between severe dizziness and postoperative helplessness. In comparison, although severe side effects usually had a stronger impact on moderate postoperative anxiety (as determined by OR), the difference in ORs between moderate and severe anxiety from side effects was small. Some groups (e.g., dizziness) even had lower ORs for severe anxiety than for moderate anxiety. However, sensitivity analysis indicated dose-response relationships, with a gradual increase between severe side effects and anxiety when we did not remove the effects or interferences of postoperative helplessness. The corresponding ORs also increased dramatically. These findings suggest that side effects may not affect postoperative anxiety unless patients also experience helplessness. The observation that moderate nausea was associated with lower odds of severe helplessness further supports these conditions. Specifically, nausea is often accompanied by objective signs, such as vomiting, making it easier for nursing staff to recognize symptoms and provide earlier intervention. Timely and standardized intervention from medical staff, due to clear signs and significant symptoms, may explain why moderate nausea was associated with lower odds of severe helplessness.

Regarding pain interference, associations between sleep interference and psychological distress have been widely observed in other studies. For example, a previous study indicated that surgery may suppress slow-wave sleep, leading to sleep deprivation [44]. Postoperative pain and the ward environment may further exacerbate sleep disruption [44, 45]. Our results extend previous findings by controlling for OMEs, pain, anxiety/helplessness, and other side effects or pain interferences in the model. These results further indicate the independent associations between sleep interference and psychological distress. Notably, effect sizes were extremely high in this study. The OR for severe sleep interference on severe helplessness was 3.307. Therefore, the effects of sleep interference should not be overlooked. Similar to side effects, the comparison between the main analysis and the sensitivity analysis indicated that associations between other types of pain interference and anxiety were affected by helplessness, further implying the roles of patient care and the fear-avoidance condition.

Implications for pain management and postoperative care

Our findings suggest several actionable directions for refining postoperative pain management protocols in East Asian settings, which should be evaluated prospectively.

First, the strong association between time spent in severe pain and POD 1 anxiety suggests that protocols may benefit from prioritizing the rapid resolution of prolonged severe pain episodes rather than relying on isolated pain score snapshots. One potential approach is to implement structured reassessment at fixed intervals (e.g., every 2–4 h) with predefined escalation steps within multimodal analgesia. However, prospective trials are needed to determine whether this approach effectively reduces prolonged pain episodes and associated anxiety. This may be especially relevant in East Asian populations, where cultural norms and higher pain tolerance can delay pain reporting and inadvertently prolong untreated severe pain [46, 47].

Second, the contrasting associations across perioperative time windows– where intraoperative and ward-administered opioids were linked to lower psychological distress, while preoperative and recovery-room opioids were not– highlight a potentially important temporal dimension of opioid use. Although our retrospective design cannot establish causality, these patterns suggest that ensuring adequate intraoperative opioid coverage and maintaining consistent ward-based administration may represent a critical window for intervention. This interpretation aligns with the preventive-analgesia framework, which emphasizes the duration and effectiveness of perioperative analgesia, rather than total opioid exposure, in reducing pain-related sensitization and subsequent adverse outcomes [48]. Prospective studies are needed to confirm whether this timing-based approach effectively reduces pain intensity and psychological distress.

Third, the strong association between sleep interference and psychological distress underscores the potential importance of preserving sleep during postoperative recovery. These findings indicate that nighttime pain management protocols deserve focused attention, especially in settings with limited nocturnal staffing. Strategies such as scheduled analgesics, environmental modifications to reduce sleep disruption, and improved nocturnal reassessment thresholds could be evaluated in prospective studies to determine their effects on sleep quality and psychological outcomes. Additionally, symptoms often considered minor, such as dizziness and drowsiness, showed significant associations with psychological distress in our data. Whether routine, standardized assessment of these symptoms (e.g., using the IPO-Q), followed by structured management protocols, can improve distress outcomes remains to be determined. Similarly, postoperative nausea and vomiting assessment using validated instruments, combined with evidence-based management strategies, could be incorporated into integrated postoperative care pathways.

Fourth, given the high prevalence of moderate-to-severe anxiety and helplessness, we recommend integrating a brief psychological distress screening into routine POD 1 assessments using validated tools (e.g., HADS). Future studies should examine whether implementing brief validated screening tools (e.g., Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale) can identify high-risk patients and whether subsequent interventions improve recovery trajectories. For patients identified with moderate distress, stepped-care approaches ranging from low-intensity bedside strategies (e.g., guided breathing, brief psychoeducation) to specialist mental health referrals for severe or persistent symptoms could be tested for feasibility and effectiveness in diverse Asian healthcare settings.

Finally, the differing associations of activity interference with anxiety and helplessness suggest that recovery pathways may need to address functional and psychological needs at the same time. Early mobilization programs may be most effective when combined with supportive communication and structured assistance that promote autonomy, potentially reducing dependency-related helplessness while preserving functional independence.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting our findings. The retrospective design and single-point data collection (postoperative day 1) preclude causal inferences and limit the evaluation of temporal dynamics among pain, opioid use, and psychological distress across the perioperative period. The PAIN OUT registry relies on voluntary hospital participation, which may introduce selection bias if participating institutions differ systematically in resources, pain management protocols, or patient case mix. Our cohort likely underrepresents patients with severe complications, cognitive impairment, or critical illness who were unable to complete questionnaires, potentially biasing results toward less vulnerable populations.

Although we used validated IPO-Q items, single-item measures of anxiety and helplessness may not capture the full multidimensional complexity of these constructs as effectively as validated multi-item scales (e.g., HADS-A, GAD-7). The IPO-Q items have been specifically validated for research on postoperative pain outcomes and demonstrate clinical relevance; however, comprehensive psychological batteries would provide a richer characterization of distress dimensions. Additionally, these constructs are subjective and highly culture-dependent, which may introduce measurement variability across our multiregional sample.

We assessed only four side effects – nausea, drowsiness, itching, and dizziness – using 11-point numerical rating scales and categorized them by severity. Important opioid-related adverse effects, such as constipation, respiratory depression, delirium, and cognitive impairment, were not included in the dataset. We cannot definitively attribute the observed side effects to opioids rather than other medications, anesthetic agents, or surgical factors, although the symptoms assessed (drowsiness, itching, dizziness) are strongly associated with opioid use in the literature. In this study, we used standardized OME conversion factors, which enable comparisons but may not adequately account for individual pharmacokinetic variability and opioid-specific receptor-binding profiles.

An important limitation is that the de-identified dataset available for this secondary analysis included opioid dosing information but lacked detailed data on non-opioid analgesics (e.g., NSAIDs, acetaminophen), regional anesthesia or analgesia techniques, and psychiatric medications. Consequently, we could not account for multimodal analgesia patterns or baseline psychiatric treatment, which may confound the observed associations between opioid exposure and postoperative psychological distress. Future prospective studies with comprehensive perioperative medication data are needed to disentangle the effects of opioid timing and dose from the broader quality and composition of analgesic and psychological care. Additionally, standardized OME conversion factors, while enabling comparisons, may not adequately account for individual pharmacokinetic variability and opioid-specific receptor-binding profiles, which could influence both analgesic efficacy and psychological effects.

Although we adjusted for broad surgical categories, the registry does not capture procedure duration or validated indicators of surgical invasiveness or complexity (e.g., operative complexity scores, extent of tissue trauma), nor does it provide procedure-specific risk stratification. More invasive or longer surgeries may require higher opioid doses and may independently increase psychological distress, potentially confounding the observed associations between intraoperative or ward OMEs and psychological outcomes. Substantial heterogeneity exists within surgical categories; for example, both laparoscopic cholecystectomy and complex hepatobiliary resection are classified as ‘general surgery’ despite markedly different pain and psychological risk profiles. Additionally, we lacked data on baseline psychological status, preoperative pain chronicity, ASA physical status classification, and psychiatric comorbidities– factors that may predispose patients to both higher analgesic requirements and greater postoperative psychological distress. Collectively, these unmeasured factors may affect both the magnitude and direction of the estimated associations.

Cultural factors such as pain stoicism and shame around expressing distress, which are prevalent in many Asian societies, may systematically influence self-reported outcomes and lead to underreporting compared to Western populations. The registry did not collect individual-level data on cultural attitudes, pain beliefs, or coping styles; therefore, we could not directly test for cultural moderation. Additionally, although PAIN OUT ASPIRE included patients from seven Asian regions, the de-identified dataset available for this secondary analysis did not include country, region, or hospital identifiers. As a result, we could not examine between-region heterogeneity, adjust for region- or site-level differences in postoperative care and patient education, or test whether associations differed by region. Therefore, our pooled estimates should be interpreted as average associations across participating sites, and residual confounding or effect modification by unmeasured cultural and healthcare system factors remains possible. Some predictor variables showed marginal multicollinearity (maximum pain VIFs: 4.963 and 4.770) and relatively high intercorrelations, which may have reduced the precision of coefficient estimates; however, VIFs remained below a problematic threshold.

Therefore, prospective studies should systematically collect surgical characteristics, multimodal analgesic data, baseline psychological assessments, and longitudinal outcomes to enable rigorous causal inference regarding the effects of perioperative opioid management on psychological distress.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that greater postoperative pain and more severe side effects are associated with higher levels of anxiety and helplessness across these seven East Asian regions. Only intraoperative administration of OMEs and OME use in the ward were associated with reduced postoperative psychological distress, while use in the preoperative process and recovery room did not show the same effect. This highlights the importance of understanding the specific timing and context of opioid use in postoperative care. Our findings underscore the need for a more holistic approach to postoperative care in East Asia. While managing severe complications remains crucial, healthcare providers should also address common, non-life-threatening side effects. Addressing these side effects can reduce patients’ postoperative psychological distress, improve overall recovery outcomes, and minimize the risk of complications. This may involve better communication with patients about potential side effects, proactive management strategies, and more comprehensive follow-up care.
