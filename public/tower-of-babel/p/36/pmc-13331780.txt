Pseudotumoral portal cavernoma mimicking hilar cholangiocarcinoma: A diagnostic pitfall
Authors: Asmae Kasimi, Mohammed Leknani, Imad Eddine Hamdi, Salah Eddine Rhbachi, Redouan El Hazzat, Hamid Ziani, Siham Nasri, Imane Skiker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Portal cavernoma cholangiopathy (PCC), also referred to as portal biliopathy, encompasses a heterogeneous spectrum of biliary tract abnormalities that develop as a consequence of extrahepatic portal vein obstruction (EHPVO) with subsequent cavernous transformation of the portal vein. Pseudotumoral portal cavernoma represents a distinct subtype of PCC and is characterized by the presence of numerous fine collateral venous channels that encase and simulate the course of the common bile duct (CBD). We report 2 cases of pseudotumoral portal cavernoma secondary to portal vein thrombosis in patients with underlying myeloproliferative disorders. These cases illustrate the diagnostic challenges associated with this rare entity and underscore the central role of cross-sectional imaging in establishing an accurate diagnosis and preventing misinterpretation as a biliary neoplasm.

Introduction

Portal cavernoma cholangiopathy (PCC) encompasses a spectrum of biliary tract abnormalities that arise as a consequence of extrahepatic portal vein obstruction (EHPVO). The condition most commonly manifests as segmental narrowing of the common bile duct (CBD), resulting from extrinsic compression by dilated peribiliary collateral venous channels. Pseudotumoral portal cavernoma represents a distinct morphological variant of PCC and is characterized by the presence of multiple small collateral veins that simulate a mass encasing the CBD [1]. Clinically, PCC is frequently associated with mild hyperbilirubinemia, although a substantial proportion of patients may remain asymptomatic.

Doppler color ultrasound, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) play a pivotal role in the diagnosis of PCC. Recent advances in cross-sectional imaging, particularly helical acquisition and multidetector computed tomography, enable rapid, high-resolution and artifact-free evaluation of the portal venous system and biliary tree, allowing comprehensive and noninvasive anatomical assessment [2].

We report 2 cases involving patients aged 60 and 53 years with underlying myeloproliferative disorders and imaging findings consistent with pseudotumoral portal cavernoma. Although pseudotumoral portal cavernoma is a recognized manifestation of portal cavernoma cholangiopathy, its imaging presentation may closely mimic hilar cholangiocarcinoma, particularly when peri-biliary collateral vessels produce a pseudomass appearance associated with biliary narrowing. Recognition of subtle imaging features, including portal vein thrombosis, portal venous phase enhancement, absence of diffusion restriction and serpiginous periportal collateral vessels, is essential to establish the correct diagnosis and avoid unnecessary invasive procedures associated with a high risk of hemorrhage. The present cases are noteworthy because both occurred in the setting of underlying myeloproliferative disorders and demonstrated imaging findings initially suggestive of biliary malignancy. These observations highlight the pivotal role of cross-sectional imaging in distinguishing this rare vascular entity from hilar cholangiocarcinoma.

Case report

Case 1

A 60-year-old man with a known history of essential thrombocythemia presented with intermittent right upper quadrant abdominal pain evolving over 2 months, without fever, jaundice, weight loss, or digestive bleeding. On admission, the patient reported intermittent abdominal discomfort without fever, jaundice, weight loss, or digestive bleeding. Physical examination revealed tender hepatosplenomegaly without palpable lymphadenopathy or signs of hepatic failure. There was no history of chronic liver disease, alcohol abuse, abdominal surgery, pancreatitis, or biliary disease. Family history was unremarkable. Laboratory investigations demonstrated mild cholestatic liver dysfunction characterized by hyperbilirubinemia and elevated alkaline phosphatase levels.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a circumferential soft-tissue lesion encasing the CBD, resulting in mild extrinsic compression. The lesion exhibited homogeneous enhancement confined to the portal venous phase. The main portal vein was completely thrombosed, with extension into the right and left portal branches. Based on these imaging findings, a diagnosis of pseudotumoral portal cavernoma, a subtype of portal cavernoma cholangiopathy, was established. Given the absence of significant biliary obstruction, cholangitis or progressive liver dysfunction, conservative management was favored. Clinical and radiological follow-up at 6 months demonstrated stable imaging findings without progression of biliary dilatation or development of suspicious malignant features.

Case 2

A 53-year-old woman with a known history of polycythemia vera presented to the emergency department with mild hematemesis. Her medical history was significant for portal hypertension secondary to chronic portal vein thrombosis. There was no history of cirrhosis, hepatobiliary surgery or pancreatic disease. Family history was noncontributory. Clinical examination revealed a hemodynamically stable patient with mild abdominal tenderness. There was no fever, jaundice, weight loss or altered bowel habits. Laboratory investigations showed preserved hemoglobin levels despite the episode of hematemesis and mild cholestatic liver abnormalities.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a mass encasing the CBD, resulting in upstream biliary ductal dilatation. The lesion exhibited enhancement during the portal venous phase. The main portal vein was not visualized. The diagnosis of pseudotumoral portal cavernoma was initially established on contrast-enhanced computed tomography, as magnetic resonance imaging could not be performed due to the patient’s claustrophobia. The diagnosis was subsequently supported by close radiological follow-up, which demonstrated stability of the hilar lesion with no interval change and no evidence of new lesions. Management consisted of nonselective beta-blocker therapy in combination with endoscopic variceal ligation for portal hypertension.

Discussion

Portal cavernoma cholangiopathy (PCC) encompasses a spectrum of biliary abnormalities occurring in the setting of extrahepatic portal vein obstruction (EHPVO) and portal cavernoma formation. EHPVO most commonly results from portal vein thrombosis, although its etiology is frequently multifactorial, involving both local and systemic predisposing conditions. In adults, reported causes include coagulation disorders, inflammatory or infectious abdominal conditions, chronic liver disease, hepato-bilio-pancreatic malignancies and myeloproliferative disorders, as observed in our patients [3]. The pathophysiology of PCC is mainly related to 2 mechanisms: extrinsic compression of the bile ducts by dilated peri-biliary collateral veins and ischemic injury of the biliary wall secondary to prolonged venous thrombosis [2].

Three morphological patterns of PCC have been described [3]. The fibrotic form is characterized by dominant biliary strictures with marked upstream biliary dilatation, while the varicoid form presents with multiple short-segment strictures and irregular biliary contours. The pseudotumoral form, observed in the present cases, is less common and results from numerous peri-biliary collateral veins producing a pseudomass appearance encasing the common bile duct. Although patients may remain asymptomatic despite biliary abnormalities, complications including jaundice, recurrent cholangitis and choledocholithiasis may occur.

Imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis of PCC and in distinguishing this benign vascular entity from biliary malignancy. Doppler ultrasound is usually the initial imaging modality and may demonstrate absence of the normal portal vein associated with cavernomatous transformation. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography allows high-resolution assessment of portal venous collaterals, portal vein thrombosis and associated biliary abnormalities [4]. Magnetic resonance imaging, including MRCP, has become the preferred noninvasive imaging technique because it enables simultaneous evaluation of the biliary tree and portal venous system. Recommended protocols include T1- and T2-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), MRCP sequences and dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging.

The principal differential diagnosis of the pseudotumoral form is hilar cholangiocarcinoma. In our cases, the main diagnostic challenge resulted from the presence of enhancing peri-biliary collateral tissue producing a hilar pseudomass associated with biliary narrowing, initially raising suspicion for malignant biliary obstruction. However, several imaging findings favored pseudotumoral portal cavernoma over cholangiocarcinoma, including complete portal vein thrombosis, enhancement predominantly during the portal venous phase, absence of diffusion restriction and visualization of serpiginous periportal collateral vessels surrounding the CBD. In the second case, lesion stability during imaging follow-up further supported the benign vascular nature of the lesion. Recognition of these features is essential because misdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary invasive procedures or inappropriate oncologic management.

Endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) may provide additional diagnostic information by demonstrating CBD varices and associated biliary calculi, while also helping differentiate benign vascular strictures from cholangiocarcinoma or chronic pancreatitis [5]. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) currently serves mainly therapeutic purposes, although it may also demonstrate characteristic biliary abnormalities such as strictures, caliber irregularities, ductal angulation and upstream dilatation [5]. Biopsy or surgical exploration of pseudotumoral portal cavernoma should generally be avoided because of the substantial risk of severe hemorrhagic complications in this highly vascular entity [6].

Management depends on symptom severity and the degree of biliary obstruction. Asymptomatic patients with preserved liver function may be managed conservatively with clinical and radiological follow-up. Endoscopic treatment, including biliary stenting or balloon dilatation, is reserved for symptomatic biliary obstruction or recurrent cholangitis. In refractory cases, portal decompression through portosystemic shunting may be required prior to any biliary surgical procedure in order to reduce intraoperative bleeding risk [6].

The present cases highlight the importance of integrating portal venous findings into the evaluation of hilar biliary lesions. Recognition of characteristic imaging features allowed accurate diagnosis of pseudotumoral portal cavernoma and prevented unnecessary invasive diagnostic procedures in both patients. These observations emphasize the pivotal role of multimodality imaging in differentiating this uncommon entity from hilar cholangiocarcinoma.

Conclusion

Pseudotumoral portal cavernoma represents an uncommon form of portal cavernoma cholangiopathy that may closely mimic hilar cholangiocarcinoma on cross-sectional imaging. The present cases highlight the importance of recognizing characteristic imaging findings, particularly portal vein thrombosis, periportal collateral vessels, portal venous phase enhancement and absence of diffusion restriction, in order to establish the correct diagnosis. Awareness of these features is essential to avoid unnecessary invasive procedures and potentially severe hemorrhagic complications in this highly vascular entity.
