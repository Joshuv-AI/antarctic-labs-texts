Evaluating solar-like behavior in turbulent alpha and Babcock–Leighton mechanisms using non-kinematic nonlinear flux-transport solar dynamos
Authors: Fadil Inceoglu, Rainer Arlt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we simulate 30,000 years of solar activity using turbulent-alpha (TA) and Babcock–Leighton (BL) mechanisms in a non-kinematic, nonlinear mean field flux-transport solar dynamo model. We evaluate their performances against observational data from proxies, like 14C, and direct solar observations. Both the TA and BL dynamos generate Schwabe-like variations, with the TA dynamo also generating periods that can be related to the QBOs and the Gleissberg cycle. The TA dynamo spends 13.3% (12.2%) of its time in a grand minimum (maximum) state, closely match the historical solar activity reconstructions from proxy records, while the BL dynamo underperforms. For the TA dynamo, the Schwabe cycle length variations during grand minima and the latitudinal and radial dependencies of the amplitude of variations both in Schwabe and QBO timescales align well with 14C data and solar observations, whereas the BL dynamo fails to reproduce these features.Keywords: Grand minimum, comparative analysis, solar dynamosSubject terms: Solar physics, Solar physics

Introduction

Direct solar observations and indirect solar activity proxies, such as magnetograms from ground- and space-based solar observatories, sunspot records, and cosmogenic radionuclide records, show that the Sun exhibits variations in its activity levels. The most known among these variations is the Schwabe cycle1, also known as the Sunspot cycle, which is directly connected to the 22-year Hale magnetic polarity cycle2. The duration of the Schwabe cycle ranges from 8 to 15 years with varying amplitudes. A longer-term trend observed in the modulation of the amplitude of the Schwabe cycle is known as the secular Gleissberg cycle3, the period of which ranges from 60 to 140 years4.

In addition to the results from the analyses of sunspot numbers, the results from the past production rates of the cosmogenic radionuclides, such as 14C and 10Be, which are inversely correlated with solar activity levels5, show that the Sun experiences periods when the solar activity levels were lower and higher than average6–10. These periods are known as the grand solar minimum and maximum periods, respectively. The most known grand solar minimum and maximum are the Maunder Minimum (1645–1715) and Medieval Maximum (1100–1250) periods4. Previous studies have shown that the Schwabe cycles persist during these grand solar minima8,10–19, and their lengths tend to be shorter than 11 years10,18,20. Recently, using a global annual compilation of past production rates of 14C, it was shown that there is a clear relationship between the rate of which the solar activity levels decrease (increase) and how long the onset (termination) of a grand minimum will last10. In addition, relationships were also observed between the durations of onsets and terminations and the rate of which the solar activity levels decrease and increase10.

In addition to these long-term behaviors, the Sun also shows shorter-term variations, the so-called Quasi-Biennial Oscillations (QBOs). The QBOs exhibit periods ranging from 0.6 to 4 years, with a clear separation at 1.5 years21. The QBOs are observed in the subsurface flows of the Sun, sunspot numbers, the photospheric magnetic fields, numbers of the high-latitude polar faculae, solar wind speeds and Earth’s magnetosphere22–30. Using data from the Michelson Doppler Imager (MDI) on the Solar and Heliospheric Observatory (SOHO) and the Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager (HMI) on the Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO), Ref.29 showed that the QBO signals are present with increasing amplitudes going deeper toward the solar tachocline around 0.71R⊙. Reference29 also showed that the source region of the QBOs is confined below around 0.78R⊙. Therefore, the QBOs are believed to be global phenomena extending from the subsurface layers of the Sun to Earth via the solar magnetic field throughout the Heliosphere. It was also shown that the latitudinal variation of the magnetic field in the Schwabe and QBO timescales is confined to latitudes below 45 degrees, where the variations in the QBO timescales are lower than those observed in the Schwabe timescales29.

The physical mechanism responsible for the amplification and spatio-temporal evolution of the solar magnetic field, which also explains its interaction with the flow fields is called the solar dynamo. In solar dynamos, the movement of electrically conducting fluid sustains a self-excited dynamo against ohmic dissipation and maintains the Sun’s global magnetic field within its convective envelope31–33.

Exciting an oscillatory, self-sustaining dynamo involves two fundamental processes: (i) the generation of a toroidal field from an existing poloidal field and (ii) the regeneration of the poloidal field from the newly created toroidal field. Solar differential rotation can shear any poloidal field to produce a toroidal magnetic field, a process known as the Ω-effect. For the regeneration of the poloidal field from the toroidal field, which is known as the α-effect, two promising mechanisms are: (i) the effect of rotating stratified turbulence, where the Coriolis force induces helical twisting of toroidal field lines to generate a poloidal field, also known as the turbulent alpha (TA) effect31,32, and (ii) the nonlocal Babcock–Leighton (BL) mechanism34–36. In the BL mechanism, the poloidal field arises from the uplift of toroidal flux in emerging active regions. Diffusion and advection then transport flux predominantly of one polarity to the poles, provided the active regions are tilted relative to the east-west direction. Inclusion of this pole-ward transportation of the magnetic flux via surface meridional flow, which then penetrates below the surface creating an equator-ward deep seated meridional flow led to the development of the flux-transport dynamo models37,38.

Previous studies concerning the mechanism that generates the observed grand solar minima-and maxima-like periods use kinematic models, where the model needs the flow field a priori to make the dynamo problem linear33,39. The most common ways to generate these periods use (i) random fluctuations in their alpha source terms40–45, and (ii) effects of the Lorentz force on the velocity field46–50. Our model is a non-kinematic model, where the flow field is generated based on the mean field differential rotation and meridional circulation framework51,52 that is coupled with the axisymmetric mean field induction equation53, and it generates the grand solar minima and maxima-like periods via the mean field Lorentz force feedback on the flow field. Unlike previous efforts, however, we calculate the full velocity field and our simulations are extremely long.

In this study, we use a non-kinematic, nonlinear flux-transport solar dynamo model, including a mean-field formulation for the full velocity field and thermodynamics with TA and BL mechanisms, separately, to study which of the two mechanisms is more likely to generate solar-like behavior in timescales related to QBOs, Schwabe, Gleissberg, and grand minima and maxima occurrences. To achieve this, we evaluated our solar dynamos using the results from previous studies using direct and indirect observations of the Sun on the QBO, Schwabe, Gleissberg and grand minimum/maximum timescales8–10,25,26,28,29. This approach leads to 5 fundamental tests; (i) fundamental frequencies found in the simulated magnetic field data, which can be associated with the QBO, Schwabe, and Gleissberg cycles, (ii) identification of grand solar minima and maxima and their duration percentages over the full duration of simulated data, and characteristics of the Waiting Time Distribution analyses for grand solar minima and maxima, (iii) relationship between the rate of which the solar activity decreases (increases) and the duration of the onset (termination) of a grand solar minimum, the relationship between the durations of onsets and terminations of grand solar minima, and the relationship between the rate of which the solar activity increases and decreases, (iv) variations in the length of the Schwabe-like cycles during solar minimum periods, and (v) differences in the latitudinal distribution of magnetic and flow fields between the Schwabe and QBO time-scales.

Results

To study which of the two mechanisms, which generates a poloidal field from an existing toroidal field, will produce results observed in various direct and indirect solar activity indicators, we used a non-kinematic, nonlinear flux transport dynamo model, including a mean-field formulation for the full velocity field and thermodynamics. This model generates meridional circulation and differential rotation based on the mean field differential rotation and meridional circulation framework51,52, which is coupled with the axisymmetric mean field induction equation53. Different from the other mean field models, this model captures the full nonlinear interaction between the generation of flow fields with the dynamo process and back-reactions26,51,53,54 (see “Methods”). Furthermore, in contrast to other models that generate the observed grand solar minima and maxima-like periods via random fluctuations in their poloidal field source or in angular momentum transport40–45,54, our model generates these periods as a result of the mean field Lorentz force feedback on the differential rotation and meridional circulation.

Throughout the full duration of our TA and BL dynamos, 30 millennia, the variations in the calculated toroidal magnetic field at the bottom of the convection zone at 0.71R⊙ show very different behaviors (Fig. 1). The TA dynamo exhibits greater variability in amplitudes both in shorter and longer timescales (blue and red lines in Fig. 1a,c,e,g,i), while the BL mechanism exhibits more monotonous variations (blue and red lines in Fig. 1b,d,f,h,j). The TA and BL dynamos show intermittent periods of low variability in their toroidal magnetic field strengths. This behavior is especially clear in the BL dynamo as these periods of weak activity extends much longer (Fig. 1). These periods can be related to the grand solar minima and maxima observed in the 14C and 10Be based solar modulation potential (SMP) reconstructions and past production rates in general7,8,10,11.

As the first test, we studied whether there are periods in the two dynamos that can be related to the QBO, Schwabe, and Gleissberg cycles as observed in the sunspots and magnetic fields on the Sun3,24–26,29,55. The FFT spectra of the TA dynamo show significant periods at 12.4 and 55 years with smaller peaks around 5.6 years (Fig. 2a–c). The BL dynamo, on the other hand, exhibits periods at 15, 33, and 6.2 years (Fig. 2d–f). The periods found in the TA can be related to the QBOs, Schwabe and Gleissberg cycles, while the periods found in the BL dynamo are not in line with the QBOs and Gleissberg cycles. The results of this test suggest that the TA dynamo has periods close to what is observed in the sunspot records.

As the second test, we identify the grand solar minima and maxima in the TA and BL dynamos, and compare them to those previously identified in the solar modulation potentials (SMPs) calculated based on the past production rates of the 14 C and 10 Be records8. Using our criteria (see “Methods”), we identified 78 grand minima and 82 grand maxima in the TA dynamo, 14 grand minima and 40 grand maxima in the BL dynamo. However, we must note that the TA and BL dynamos are around 3 times longer than the SMP series. Therefore, we calculated the percentage time of the solar activity spent in grand minimum and maximum state. The TA dynamo spends ∼13.3% its time in the grand minimum state, while it spends ∼12.2% of its time in the grand maxima state. The BL dynamos spend only ∼7.8% of its time in grand minimum state, while ∼7.6% of its time in the grand maximum state. The results from the SMP and sunpot reconstructions for the past 11,000 years show that the Sun spent 17% of its time in a grand minimum state and 12% of its time in a grand maximum state4.

Additionally, we applied the Waiting Time Distribution (WTD) analysis to grand minima and maxima periods found in the two dynamos and both SMP reconstructions (Fig. 3). For the grand solar minimum, the complementary cumulative distribution functions (ccdf) of the two dynamos and the two SMP reconstructions are best represented by an exponential fit meaning that the occurrences of grand solar minima in the TA and BL dynamos as well as in the SMPs are of random origin (Fig. 3a–d). The ccdfs of the grand solar minima identified in the TA dynamo are similar to those from the SMP reconstructions; most of the waiting times are blow 1000 years, while most of the waiting times for the grand solar minima identified in the BL dynamo are longer than 1000 years (Fig. 3a–d). A similar behavior can also be observed for the grand solar maximum periods (Fig. 3e–h).

As the third test, we investigate whether the relationships observed in the past production rates of 14C during annually resolved 7 grand solar minima can also be found in the TA and BL dynamos (Fig. 4). We observe an inverse correlation between the degree of which the solar activity levels decrease and the duration of onsets, and between the degree of which the solar activity levels increase and the duration of the termination periods in the both TA and BL dynamos (Fig. 4a,b,e,f). It must be noted that the results from the TA dynamo seem to be more in line with those observed in the past production rates of 14C than the results from the BL dynamo. Additionally, the TA and BL dynamos resulted in inverse relationship between the onset and termination durations, and the relevant slopes (Fig. 4c,d,g,h).

For the fourth test, we calculated the Schwabe cycle lengths during the identified solar grand minima in the TA and BL dynamos, as well as the annually resolved global compilation of 14C past productions rates (Fig. 5). During the 7 grand solar minima where we have annually resolved global 14C compilation data, the Schwabe cycle lengths show a bimodal distribution with local maxima around 7 and 13 years (purple bars in Fig. 5). The cycle lengths during the 78 grand minima identified in the TA dynamo tends to show a bimodal distribution where the local maxima cluster around 5 and 13 years, closely resembling the observations from the 14C compilation (red and purple bars in Fig. 5). In contrast, the cycle lengths calculated in the BL dynamo during the 14 identified grand minima show a unimodal Gaussian distribution, where the maximum clusters around 15 years (blue bars in Fig. 5).

Distribution of the cycle lengths found during the identified grand solar minima in the BL dynamo (blue), TA dynamo (red) and the annually resolved global compilation of 14C past production rates (purple).

As the fifth test, we calculated the average variations in the toroidal magnetic field as a function of latitude and in the rotation rate residuals as a function of radius and latitude (see “Methods”, Fig. 6). The results show that the TA dynamo generates magnetic fields that are more confined within the lower latitudes (60 S and 60 N) in both timescales (Schwabe and QBO) and the amplitude of variations is larger within this region (Fig. 6a). The BL dynamo, on the other hand, shows weaker amplitude variations, which are also distributed over a wider latitudinal range (Fig. 6b).

Amplitude of variations in the radial magnetic field as a function of latitude for the Schwabe timescale (blue) and the QBO timescale (orange) from the TA (a) and the BL (d) dynamos. Also shown are the amplitude of variations in the rotation rate residuals as a function of depth and latitude for the Schwabe and the QBO timescales from the TA dynamo (b,c) and the BL dynamo (e,f). We also show the latitudes in 30 degrees increments and 0.78R⊙.

The most important difference between the TA and BL dynamos comes from the average behavior of the rotation rate residuals in the Schwabe- and QBO-like timescales. In the Schwabe-like timescale, the main difference between the TA and BL dynamos is the behavior of the rotation rate residuals between ∼30 N and S latitudes. In the TA dynamo, the region between the 30 N and S latitudes exhibits the smallest variations with almost no dependence on radius (Fig. 6b), while the BL dynamo shows a higher amplitude of variations down to around 0.80R⊙, after which it becomes smaller (Fig. 6e).

In the QBO timescale, TA dynamos show latitudinal dependency in amplitude of variations below 0.78R⊙, which is more clearer close to 0.71R⊙ (Fig. 6c). The BL dynamo, on the other hand, does not produce the radial dependency of the amplitude of variations on the solar equator as well as the latitudinal dependency below 0.78R⊙, while showing a higher amplitude variation in a thin layer between 30 and 60 N latitudes and 30 and 60 S latitudes (Fig. 6f). These regions coincide with where the BL mechanism operates on top our dynamo domain. A similar structure can also be observed for the TA dynamo, however this time, the thing layer is above around 40 N and S latitudes and confined between 0.71R⊙ and 0.78R⊙ (Fig. 6c).

Discussion

In this study, we simulated for the first time the spatiotemporal variations of the magnetic and flow fields for two different alpha-mechanisms, turbulent-alpha and Babcock–Leighton, for 30,000 years. This allows us to evaluate the two different alpha mechanisms using observations of the variation in the solar activity levels as well as variations in the differential rotation rates in the QBO, Schwabe, Gleissberg, and longer timescales.

The dynamo model used in this study is a non-kinematic nonlinear flux transport dynamo model with TA and BL mechanisms which can be used separately. Different from the other dynamo models, our model generates the observed meridional and differential rotations based on the mean field differential rotation and meridional circulation framework51, which is updated based on Ref.52. This is also integrated with the axisymmetric mean field induction equation53. Unlike the other models, this dynamo model computes the full nonlinear interplay of the generation of differential rotation and meridional circulation with the dynamo process and back-reactions26,51,53,54 instead of continuing the existing efforts in kinematic dynamo models.

Both the TA and BL dynamos show fundamental periods at 12.4 and 15 years, respectively. These periods are in line with what we observe in the sunspot numbers, the Schwabe cycles the period of which ranges from 8 to 15 years. In addition to the Schwabe scale variations, the TA and BL dynamos show strong periods at 55 and 38 years, respectively. The 55-year period found in the TA dynamo resembles the Gleissberg cycle3, the period of which ranges from 60 to 140 years4. The Gleissberg scale variations were previously observed in different dynamo models that incorporate the back-reaction of the magnetic field on the angular momentum transport46,49. In our TA and BL dynamos, we also observe shorter than Schwabe scale periods. The TA dynamo shows a period of 5.6 years, while the BL dynamo exhibit 6.2 year variations. The presence of the QBO type signals in the mean field dynamos with TA and BL mechanisms were previously studied in detail26. Similar to those results, our dynamos were capable of generating QBO type signals resulting from the nonlinear interaction between the meridional circulation, differential rotation, and magnetic field26. However, a more detailed analysis is missing to pinpoint the exact physical mechanism, among which are the terms in the governing dynamo equations like the Lorentz force, shear, advection, and stretching26.

Both our dynamos were capable of generating periods of lower than average and higher than average activity periods, which are known as the grand solar minimum and maximum periods, respectively. However, the total percentage time the TA and BL dynamos spend in grand minima and maxima differ dramatically. The TA dynamo spends ∼13.3% its time in the grand minimum state, while it spends ∼12.2% of its time in the grand maximum state. The BL dynamos spend only ∼7.8% of its time in grand minimum state, while ∼7.6% of its time in the grand maximum state. The results from the quantitative solar activity level reconstructions, such as SMP or sunspot, for the past 11,000 years exhibit that the Sun spent 17% of its time in a grand minimum state, while it spent 12% of its time in a grand maximum state4. This shows that the BL dynamo fails to reproduce what is observed from the quantitative reconstructions of the past solar activity levels, while the TA dynamo is more successful.

Further, using a least-squares method, we fit exponential and power law fits to the ccdfs of the grand solar minima and maxima identified in the TA and BL dynamos and in the SMPs from Ref.8. Our results show that the occurrence characteristics of the grand minima and maxima reflects a random process, which is independent of the previous occurrence of a grand minimum or maximum. This means that there is no memory-bearing process. This contradicts with the previous results from SMP and sunspot reconstructions7,8. However, it must be noted that the previous studies7,8 used a method where the fitting process was performed based on maximizing the likehood where a “xmin” value must be found, which is randomly selected and after which a power-law is fitted56. These results clearly show the sensitivity of the results to the methods chosen to fit an exponential and power-law distribution.

We also calculated the observed relationship between the rate of which solar activity decreases (increases) on the onset (termination) of during grand solar minima10. The results from the TA dynamo are more similar to what was observed in the annually resolved past production rates of 14C than the BL dynamo. Even though both dynamos show a negative correlation, the slopes of the fits are very different in the BL dynamo. However, on the other hand, none of the dynamos was able to produce the observed positive correlation between the durations of onsets and terminations, and their slopes10. On the contrary, the TA and BL dynamos resulted in inverse relationship between these quantities.

We also calculated the cycle lengths during grand solar minima in the TA and BL dynamos and also using the annually resolved global compilation of 14C past productions rates from Ref.10. We found that in the past production rates of 14C, the distribution of the cycle lengths during the 7 grand minima shows a bimodal distribution with local maxima at 7 and 13 years. Similar to what is observed in the 14C data, our TA dynamo also shows a bimodal distribution for the cycle lengths during the 78 grand minima identified in the data, the local maxima of which are at 5 and 13 years. The shortening of the Schwabe cycles has also previously been observed in annually resolved 14C data during grand minima10,18,20,57. However, the cycle lengths calculated in the BL dynamo during the 14 identified grand minima show a unimodal Gaussian distribution where the maximum clusters around 15 years, failing to generate the observed features.

We also calculated the latitudinal dependency of the amplitude of variations on the Schwabe and QBO timescales for the TA and BL dynamos to compare them with the observational results29. The TA dynamo generates magnetic fields that are more confined within the lower latitudes (60 S and 60 N) in both timescales (Schwabe and QBO) and the amplitude of variations are larger within this region is more similar to what is observed on the Sun29, while the BL dynamo shows much smaller amplitude of variations that spread a much winder latitude band, failing to reproduce what is observed on the Sun. We also calculated the amplitude of variations of the differential rotation residuals for each latitude and radius. Both dynamos in the Schwabe-like time scales show increasing amplitude of variations with increasing latitude, which is also observed to be the case in a previous study29. However, for the QBO timescales, the results from the TA dynamo tends to be able to produce the observed latitudinal dependency below 0.78R⊙, which is also previously observed29, while the BL dynamos are not in line with those previously observed. Note that these behaviors are average behaviors calculated using randomly chosen a 1000 30 year-long windows.

In conclusion, we for the first time run 30,000 year-long extensive simulations of the variations in solar activity levels using a non-kinematic nonlinear flux transport dynamo model. This model, different from kinematic models, generates the flow fields based on the mean-field differential rotation and meridional circulation framework. This framework is then coupled with an axisymmetric mean field induction equation, using the Lorentz force as the saturation mechanism. This model allows us to use turbulent-alpha and Babcock–Leighton mechanisms separately or together to generate a poloidal field from a preexisting toroidal field. We evaluated the 30,000 year-long dynamos from the TA and BL mechanisms using the observational constraints from previous studies that use cosmogenic radionuclides, sunspot numbers, photospheric magnetic fields and differential rotation rate residuals. Our results suggest that the turbulent-alpha mechanism operating between 0.71R⊙ and 0.78R⊙ generates results that are more in line with those of observational studies. The BL dynamo, on the other hand, fails to generate important observational features such as the number of grand minima and maxima, how much time it spends in these periods, durations of grand minima and maxima, cycle length variations during solar grand minima, as well as the latitudinal and radial features of the magnetic field and rotation rate residuals.

Methods

The dynamo model

The dynamo model used in this study utilizes the mean field differential rotation and meridional circulation framework developed by Ref.51, which is integrated with the axisymmetric mean field induction equation53. The Λ-mechanism responsible for generating the mean field differential rotation and meridional circulation has been updated based on the work of Ref.52. While the large-scale flow field by itself does not function as a dynamo, it plays a role in the amplification and transport of magnetic fields. Additionally, it is influenced by the Lorentz forces produced by the magnetic fields. Our dynamo model’s computational domain spans from the southern to the northern pole in latitude and from 0.65R⊙ to 0.985R⊙ in radius, where we placed the bottom of the convection zone at 0.71R⊙. For further details on the dynamo model, we refer the reader to26,51,53,54 and dynamo equations can be found in the Supplementary Material. The reference differential rotation and meridional circulation are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The nonlocal BL mechanism operates on the domain surface, however the TA mechanism can be placed in a (thin) shell anywhere in the convection zone. For the TA mechanism, we use the formulation from Ref.58. As for the TA mechanism, we placed it between 0.70R⊙ and 0.77R⊙, 0.71R⊙ and 0.78R⊙, 0.72R⊙ and 0.79R⊙, 0.73R⊙ and 0.80R⊙, and 0.74R⊙ and 0.81R⊙ (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The turbulent magnetic diffusivity is assumed to vary with depth in the convection zone. In the top part, it is 108m2s-1 in accordance with measurements of the decay of magnetic structures on the solar surface59,60. The diffusivity decreases with depth to a value of 106m2s-1 in the lower part of the convection zone. The radiative interior is represented by an even lower diffusivity of 105m2s-1. The values are taken from a model of a BL dynamo53 and facilitate the transport of flux by meridional flows near the bottom of the convection zone. The cycle period of a purely turbulent dynamo is roughly consistent with the turbulent diffusion time, which, for a cycle period of 11 yr through the convection zone thickness, requires 108m2s-1 throughout the convection zone. The mixing length approach of a standard solar model also suggests diffusivities of 108m2s-1 or higher in the entire convection zone. Our values of the turbulent magnetic diffusivity are therefore lowered as compared to expectations, in order to specifically allow for BL solutions to be possible alongside of TA solutions. Note also that our average magnetic diffusivity of about 107m2s-1 is the same as in Ref.61. The code used here took part in that benchmark paper.

To obtain solar like oscillations for the BL dynamos, we increased the source term amplitude from 0.1 to 2.5 ms-1 with 0.1 ms-1 increments. The most stable solution was found for 0.2 ms-1 (Supplementary Figs. 3a and 4a, for the calculated toroidal and radial magnetic field strengths, respectively), and the simulations failed for values larger than 2.5 ms-1 due to numerical resolution limitations. The simulations with 1.5 ms-1 as the source term amplitude gave the most solar-like behavior where the dynamo shows high and low frequency oscillations with extended high and low activity periods. Larger source term amplitudes led to having much longer than Schwabe cycles as fundamental frequencies, therefore were not selected.

As for the TA mechanism, our parameter space was much larger as we tried 5 different locations for our TA dynamos to operate (Supplementary Fig. 2). For the TA dynamo located between 0.70R⊙ and 0.77R⊙, we increased the α-effect coefficient from 0.01 to 0.045 ms-1 with 0.001 ms-1 increments. However, for the other TA dynamos, the α-effect coefficient ranged from 0.01 to 0.3 with 0.01 ms-1 increments. The only configuration which led to an over 30,000 year long (100,001 iterations) simulation without any numerical limitations and that also shows solar-like oscillations was found for the TA dynamo operating between 0.71R⊙ and 0.78R⊙ (Supplementary Fig. 2c) with an α-effect coefficient of 0.23 ms-1. The most stable simulation for this configuration was observed at an α-effect of 0.02 ms-1 (Supplementary Figs. 3b and4b, for the calculated toroidal and radial magnetic field strengths, respectively).

Additionally, our dynamos were able to generate the observed grand solar minima- and maxima-like periods via the mean field Lorentz force feedback on the differential rotation and meridional circulation.

Determination of the upper and lower threshold values

Following Ref.8, to calculate the upper and lower threshold values, which will be used to identify grand solar minima and maxima periods in the dynamos, we used zero-crossing method. Prior to analysis, we first cutout the first 72 years of the toroidal magnetic field strengths averaged over the whole solar disk in the TA and BL dynamos. The reason for this is that the first 72 years in the simulations have not yet reached a saturation stage and could introduce numerical artifacts. Following this, we standardized (zscore) unsigned toroidal magnetic field magnitudes obtained from the TA and BL dynamos. We then low-pass filtered the standardized TA and BL data using a Butterworth filter of order 5 with a cutoff frequency of (1/23) year-1 to equalize their temporal resolution with the SMPs previously used in Ref.8. We then use the zero-crossing method to select periods where the amplitudes are above and below zero and register their amplitudes. The distributions of all these peaks and dips over the course of the full duration of the underlying data sets result in a bimodal Gaussian distribution (Supplementary Fig. 5). The local maxima of these bimodal distributions provided us with the required lower and upper threshold values to identify grand solar minima and maxima. For the TA dynamo, the lower and upper thresholds were − 1.28σ and 0.55σ, for the BL dynamo -1.08σ and 0.51σ (Supplementary Fig. 5)

Identification of grand solar minima and maxima

To identify grand solar minima and maxima periods, we applied a set of selection criteria to the low-passed and standardized data from the TA and BL dynamos, which are also used to calculate the threshold values. The criteria used to identify grand solar minima and maxima in the TA and BL dynamos are; (i) the duration of the period, which is determined by the zero-crossing method (see section “Determination of the upper and lower threshold values”), has to be longer than 35 years, (ii) the highest amplitude has to be over the higher threshold value for this period to be accepted as a grand maximum, and (iii) the lowest amplitude has to be below the lower threshold value for this period to be identified as a grand minimum (see section “Determination of the upper and lower threshold values” and Supplementary Fig. 5).

Waiting time distribution analysis

The waiting time is defined as the time span between two consecutive events. We chose the waiting times as the time between the lowest (highest) amplitudes between two consecutive grand solar minima (maxima). After calculating the waiting times between the consecutive minima and maxima, we constructed their complementary cumulative distribution functions (ccdf). The ccdfs represent the probability that a random variable “X” which follows a specific probability distribution, will have a value greater than or equal to “x”8. We then fitted power-law and exponential-law using the least-squares method.

Cycle lengths during grand minima

We detected the cycle lengths during each grand solar minimum period by using Fast Fourier Transformation. We first removed the long-term trend from the data to provide a flat background, then calculated their FFTs. We also calculated 1000 red noise spectra using each data set’s lag-1 auto-correlation coefficients and averaged them to obtain a background red noise level. If the amplitude of the highest peak in the data’s FFT is higher than the red noise spectrum, we registered period of the highest amplitude peak as the cycle length. This process is applied to all data from TA and BL dynamos as well as the annually resolved global compilation of the past production rates of 14C.

Amplitude of variations in magnetic and flow fields

To calculate the average amplitude of variations in the toroidal magnetic field and differential rotation rate residuals, we first identified a window of 30 years. This is chosen to reflect the length of magnetic and flow field data from SOHO and SDO in Ref.29. We first randomly choose 30 year long data from the 30,000 year long magnetic butterfly diagram. For the magnetic field data, we separated the timescales into Schwabe-like and QBO-like timescales using a Butterworth filter of order 5, where the cutoff frequency is (1/6.2) year-1 for the TA and (1/7.5) year-1 for the BL dynamo. These are chosen to exclude any effects of the second harmonics of the fundamental frequencies, 12.4 year and 15 year, respectively, found in these dynamos. We then calculated the standard deviation in each timescale per latitude. As for the differential rotation rates, after randomly selecting a 30 year period, we first removed the average differential rotation rate as a function of latitudes from the data to calculate the differential rotation rate residuals. Following this step, we separated the data into the two timescales and calculated the amplitude of variations in each latitude. This process is done for each radial depth ranging from the top of the domain 0.985⊙ to the bottom of the convection zone at 0.71⊙. These steps were repeated a 1000 times to calculate the average amplitude variations and their standard deviations.
