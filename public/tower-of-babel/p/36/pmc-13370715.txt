FURIN induces vascular endothelial cell death and efferocytosis through enzymatic cleavage of thrombospondin‐1
Authors: Runji Chen, Daifei Shen, Amandine Mahieux, Dedrick Song, Shiwan Wu, Xiaoxin Huang, Xu Yang, Kunwei Chen, David G. McVey, Elisa A. Liehn, Roshni R. Singaraja, Shu Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe vascular endothelium regulates molecular and cellular exchanges between the blood and tissues. Endothelial cell dysfunction and death, triggered by cardiovascular risk factors, can result in endothelial leakage, leading to atherosclerosis. Evidence indicates that the proprotein convertase FURIN can modulate endothelial cell behaviour and promote atherosclerosis. In this study, we investigated the underlying mechanisms: small interfering RNA (siRNA)‐induced knockdown of FURIN reduced vascular endothelial cell (VEC) apoptosis and efferocytosis by monocyte‐derived macrophages, whereas lentivirus‐mediated overexpression of FURIN had the opposite effect. A co‐immunoprecipitation assay and western blot analyses indicated that FURIN interacted with thrombospondin‐1 (THBS1). Furthermore quantitative RT‐PCR assays and western blot analyses showed that FURIN knockdown resulted in a decrease in THBS1 mRNA and protein levels, whereas THBS1 knockdown attenuated FURIN expression, and these reciprocal regulatory effects were augmented in the presence of LY2109761, an inhibitor of transforming growth factor‐β receptors. THBS1 knockdown largely abolished the influence of FURIN on VEC apoptosis and efferocytosis. Further assays indicated that FURIN knockdown enhanced the integrity and reduced the permeability of a monolayer of cultured VECs, which was counteracted by overexpression of THBS1. In a mouse model of atherosclerosis treatment with a FURIN inhibitor decreased VEC apoptosis in atherosclerotic lesions. Overall these results indicate that by cleaving THBS1 and suppressing THBS1 expression FURIN promotes VEC apoptosis and efferocytosis, leading to increased endothelial permeability. These findings provide a new insight into endothelial dysfunction, with atherogenic relevance and potential therapeutic implications. Key points Endothelial dysfunction and cell death may lead to endothelial leakage, triggering atherosclerosis.The proprotein convertase FURIN regulates endothelial cell behaviour and promotes atherosclerosis, but its specific mechanisms remain unclear.This study identifies thrombospondin‐1 (THBS1) as a novel substrate of FURIN in vascular endothelial cells. FURIN binds to and cleaves THBS1, thereby promoting endothelial apoptosis and efferocytosis, increasing endothelial permeability and impairing vascular homeostasis.These findings provide new insights into endothelial dysfunction with atherogenic and therapeutic relevance.

Introduction

The vascular endothelium serves as the primary barrier that regulates the exchange of molecules, cells and pathogens between the bloodstream and surrounding tissues (Komarova et al., 2017). Biomechanical or chemical insults can cause vascular endothelial cell (VEC) dysfunction, VEC apoptosis and endothelium leakage (Kockx & Herman, 2000; Rossig et al., 2001). This allows lipid infiltration and leukocyte penetration into the vascular wall, thereby promoting atherosclerosis that underlies coronary artery disease (CAD) and other atherosclerosis‐related disorders (Chistiakov et al., 2015; Mundi et al., 2018).

FURIN possesses proteolytic activity and is capable of processing and activating a variety of proteins (Benjannet et al., 2006; Somerville et al., 2004; Yana & Weiss, 2000). Recent studies have revealed that FURIN increases in atherogenesis and CAD. FURIN is located at the chromosome 15q26.1 locus that shows a strong association with CAD susceptibility in genome‐wide association studies (Nelson et al., 2017). We recently found that the CAD‐associated genetic variant rs17514846 at this locus influences FURIN expression in vascular endothelial cells (Yang et al., 2020). We showed that FURIN promotes monocyte adhesion to endothelial cells and trans‐endothelial migration (Yang et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2018) via an increase in the activity of the transcription factor nuclear factor‐κB (NF‐κB) and consequently the expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule‐1 (VCAM1) and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP1) (Yang et al., 2020).

Because VEC apoptosis promotes atherogenesis (Chistiakov et al., 2015; Kockx & Herman, 2000; Mundi et al., 2018; Rossig et al., 2001), in the present study we investigated if FURIN could affect VEC survival. Our study reveals that FURIN promotes VEC apoptosis and efferocytosis by monocyte‐derived macrophages, resulting in increased vascular permeability. Mechanistically, our study indicates that the effect of FURIN on VEC apoptosis and efferocytosis is related to thrombospondin‐1 (THBS1, also known as TSP‐1) cleavage by FURIN, as well as the regulation of THBS1 expression through the FURIN/transforming growth factor β1 (TGFβ1)/THBS1 pathway.

Methods

Ethical approval for animals

The study was approved by the Singapore A*STAR Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (number: 231805), and all procedures conformed to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; Cat# CRL‐2480, RRID:CVCL_3722) and human THP‐1 monocytic cells (Cat# TIB‐202, RRID:CVCL_A4CA) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, USA). Human artery endothelial cells (HAECs) were purchased from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology (ZQY001, Shanghai, China). THP‐1 monocytes were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)‐1640 medium. HUVECs were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium, whereas HAECs were grown in HAEC culture medium (Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology; ZMY001, Shanghai, China). All cell types were cultured in media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2.

siRNA‐mediated gene knockdown

HUVECs and HAECs were transfected with FURIN small interfering RNA (siRNA) (ThermoFisher Scientific; s9988, Waltham, USA), THBS1 siRNA (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) or negative control siRNA (ThermoFisher Scientific; 4390843, Waltham, USA). Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Invitrogen; 13778100, Waltham, USA) and OptiMEM I reduced serum‐free medium (ThermoFisher; 31985070, Waltham, USA) were used for siRNA transfection according to the manufacturer's protocol. The sequences of the FURIN siRNA and THBS1 siRNA are listed in Table 1. The negative control siRNA was gifted by GenePharma Company (Shanghai, China). FURIN and THBS1 knockdown efficiencies were verified using western blot.

Lentivirus overexpression

The lentivirus‐FURIN (LV‐FURIN), lentivirus‐THBS1 (LV‐THBS1) and a control vector (LV‐Vector) were constructed and packaged by iGeneBio Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). FURIN and THBS1 overexpression efficiencies were verified using western blot.

Cell proliferation assay

HUVECs transfected with FURIN siRNA or control for 48 h were seeded on a 96‐well plate (1 × 104 cells per well). For cell proliferation assay, cells were measured using Cell Counting Kit‐8 assay (MedChemExpress; HY‐K0301, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cell apoptosis analysis

HUVECs were transfected with siRNA for 48 h or transduced with lentivirus as described above. The cells were stained using the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Pharmingen; 556 547, San Diego, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were incubated with a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)‐labelled annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) for 20 min followed by flow cytometry analysis. Cells considered viable were FITC Annexin V and PI negative; cells in early apoptosis were FITC Annexin V positive and PI negative; and cells in late apoptosis or dead were both FITC Annexin V and PI positive. Total apoptosis was defined as the sum of cells in early and late apoptosis. Data were acquired on a BD Accuri C6 Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Diego, USA) and analysed using BD Accuri C6 software.

In vitro HUVEC tube‐formation assay

96‐well plates were precoated with 50 µL (per well) of Matrigel (Corning Inc.; 354234, NY, USA) and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. After transfection with FURIN siRNA or control siRNA for 24 h, HUVECs were serum‐starved overnight and then seeded onto Matrigel‐coated 96‐well plates (4.5×104 cells/well). Eight hours later tube formation was visualized using an inverted photomicroscope and quantified using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin for ImageJ software.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins of HUVECs and HAECs were separately prepared using radioimmuneprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology; P0013B, Shanghai, China). Equal quantities of protein were separated by 8% or 10% SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes, which were then blocked with a commercial blocking solution (Beyotime Biotechnology; P0240, Shanghai, China) and probed at 4°C overnight with primary antibodies: rabbit anti‐FURIN antibody (Abcam; ab3467; 1:1000 dilution, Cambridge, UK), mouse anti‐THBS1 antibody (ThermoFisher; MA513398; 1:1000 dilution, Waltham, USA) and mouse anti‐GAPDH antibody (Cell Signaling Technology; 5174; 1:3000 dilution, Danvers, USA). The membranes were washed and then incubated with HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse/rabbit secondary antibodies (Boster Biotechnology; BA1050/BA1054; 1:20,000 dilution, Shanghai, China). Detailed parameters of the antibodies are summarized in Table 2. The protein bands were visualized using an eBlot Touch Imager (eBlot Photoelectrics technology, China) or Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc XRS+ (Bio‐Rad, USA).

Co‐immunoprecipitation

Total cell proteins of HUVECs and HAECs were separately prepared using NP‐40 lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology; P0013F, Shanghai, China). After protein concentration was determined, 500 µg of protein samples were incubated with 3 µg of primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress; HY‐K0202, Shanghai, China) were then used to precipitate antibody‐bound proteins. After 8 h precipitation at 4°C the beads were separated using a magnetic stand (Beyotime Biotechnology; FMS008) and washed with ice‐cold 0.1% Tween‐20 in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) thrice. After 1×SDS loading buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology; P0015L, Shanghai, China) was added, samples were boiled for 5 min and analysed using western blot.

RT‐PCR analysis was performed using BrightCycle Universal SYBR Green qPCR Mix (ABclonal Technology; RK21219, Wuhan, China) on a Biometra TProfessional thermocycler (Biometra, Analytik Jena, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The primers used were listed in Table 3. β‐actin and GAPDH were used as reference genes. Relative target mRNA expression was evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

THBS1 in vitro cleavage assay

LV‐FURIN‐ or LV‐Vector‐transfected HUVECs were incubated with or without a FURIN neutralizing antibody (Proteintech; 18413‐1‐AP) for 24 h. The supernatant was collected and concentrated using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal ultrafiltration tube (30 kDa), centrifuged by 45 min and then analysed by western blot.

Inhibition of TGF‐β signalling pathway by LY2109761

LY2109761 (MedChemExpress; HY‐12 075, Shanghai, China) is a selective TGF‐β receptor type I/II inhibitor. We used LY2109761 at a concentration of 20 µM for TGF‐β signalling pathway inhibition. After transfection with FURIN siRNA and THBS1 siRNA for 48 h, HUVECs were incubated with LY2109761 for another 24 h and harvested to measure the mRNA levels of FURIN and THBS1.

Efferocytosis assay

To prepare THP‐1‐derived macrophages, cells were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA, Merck; P1585, Darmstadt, Germany) for 48 h. In vitro endothelial permeability assay

An endothelial permeability assay was conducted following established methods (Fang et al., 2018). HUVECs (2 × 104) were seeded onto 24‐well Transwell filters with 8 µm pores (Corning) and incubated to achieve 100% confluence. Then, the filters were gently rinsed with PBS. The upper chamber was induced with 30 ng/mL TNF‐α (MedChemExpress; HY‐P7058, Shanghai, China) for 48 h and then treated with 20 mg/mL FITC‐dextran (molecular weight approximately 150,000; Beyotime Biotechnology; ST2955, Shanghai, China) and incubated for 1 h. The fluorescence intensity of FITC‐dextran in the lower chamber was measured as an indicator of permeability using a microplate spectrofluorometer (GloMax Navigator; Promega; Madison, USA), with wavelength settings of 492 nm excitation and 520 nm emission.

Animal Study

Animals, housing and experimental design

Thirty‐two male Ldlr−/− mice (C57BL/6J background; Jackson Laboratory, USA) were used in this study. Mice were housed under controlled environmental conditions on a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Animals were maintained on a standard chow diet (1324_modified, Altromin GmbH & Co. Lage, Germany) until 12 weeks of age, after which they were switched to a Western‐type diet (WTD; D12079B, Research Diets, NJ, USA) for 8 weeks. Bedding was changed weekly, and the WTD was replaced twice weekly.

At the start of WTD feeding, mice were randomly assigned to receive intraperitoneal injections of either 1×PBS or the FURIN inhibitor α−1‐PDX (100 µg/kg; ThermoFisher, RP‐070, Waltham, USA) twice weekly for 8 weeks.

Animals were monitored daily for general health and welfare, including assessment for weight loss, dehydration, hunched posture, piloerection, lethargy, trauma and skin lesions.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All 32 mice purchased for the study were included in the analysis. Prespecified exclusion criteria were failure to receive the scheduled intraperitoneal injections or killing before the planned experimental end‐point due to predefined humane end‐point criteria. No animals met these exclusion criteria, and no animals were excluded from the study.

Anaesthesia, monitoring, supportive care and killing

At 20 weeks of age, mice were anaesthetized with ketamine hydrochloride (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) administered by intraperitoneal injection. Anaesthetic depth was monitored by assessing the absence of the pedal withdrawal reflex (toe pinch) and corneal reflex (blink reflex) before terminal procedures were initiated.

Animals were observed throughout the procedure for signs of inadequate anaesthesia or distress. Mice were also monitored daily throughout the study for predefined humane end‐points, including severe ulcerative dermatitis or large open wounds, inability to access food or water, body condition score <2, >10% body weight loss within 24 h or >20% body weight loss over 24 h. Animals meeting any of these criteria were to be killed immediately. No mice met humane end‐point criteria during the study.

At the terminal end‐point mice were killed under deep anaesthesia by exsanguination followed by transcardial perfusion with 1×PBS. Death was confirmed by cessation of circulation and respiration.

Tissue collection and histology

After perfusion, hearts were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) and embedded in paraffin. Serial cross‐sections (5 µm thick) of the entire aortic root were prepared for histological analysis, as described previously (Stary et al., 1995).

Immunofluorescence staining

Sections were double stained to determine proliferation or apoptosis of VECs. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated followed by antigen retrieval. The slides were then incubated for 30 min at room temperature (RT) in a blocking solution containing 1×PBS, 2% bovine serum albumin and 0.5% normal horse serum. Thereafter, sections were incubated with the primary rat anti‐CD31 antibody at 1:50 dilution (Abcam; AB56299, Cambridge, UK) at 4°C overnight. After being washed thrice for 5 min in 1×PBS the sections were incubated with an Alexa 647‐conjugated donkey anti‐rat IgG antibody (1:1000, ThermoFisher; A78947, Waltham, USA) at RT for 1 h. After being washed and reblocked in blocking solution, sections were incubated with a primary rabbit anti‐Ki67 antibody at 1:100 dilution (Abcam; AB15580, Cambridge, UK) at RT for 1 h. Thereafter sections were washed, incubated with an Alexa 488‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG polyclonal antibody (1:500, Abcam; Ab150077, Cambridge, UK) at RT for 1 h. After being washed thrice, the slides were mounted with Vectashield Plus Antifade Mounting Medium containing 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI; Vector Laboratories; H‐2000, Newark, USA).

For VEC apoptosis analyses, after deparaffinization sections were incubated for 30 min at 37°C in a blocking solution followed by incubation with TUNEL reagent (Roche; 11684795910, Basel, Switzerland) for 60 min at 37°C in the dark, according to the manufacturer's protocol. After washing with PBS the sections were blocked with the same blocking solution for 30 min at RT. Sections were then incubated with rat anti‐CD31 antibody at 1:50 dilution (Abcam; AB56299, Cambridge, UK) at 4°C overnight. After washing the sections were incubated with an Alexa 647‐conjugated donkey anti‐rat IgG antibody (1:1000, ThermoFisher; A78947, Waltham, USA) at RT for 1 h, and then washed. The slides were mounted with Vectashield Plus Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories; H‐2000, Newark, USA). Detailed information on the antibodies is provided in Table 5.

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using Mann–Whitney test (GraphPad Prism version 9; RRID:SCR_002798) where appropriate. Exact P‐values are reported for all comparisons, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

FURIN influences VEC growth, apoptosis and tube formation ability

To investigate whether FURIN affects VEC behaviour, we transfected cultured human VECs with either FURIN siRNA or control siRNA and then performed assays of proliferation, apoptosis and tube formation. SiRNA‐induced FURIN knockdown attenuated VEC apoptosis, whereas lentivirus‐mediated FURIN overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 1A,B,C,D ). FURIN knockdown promoted VEC proliferation and reduced tube‐formation capability (Fig. 1E,F ).

FURIN complexes with and processes THBS1

A bioinformatics analysis predicted multiple FURIN‐cleaving motifs in THBS1, an adhesive glycoprotein that mediates cell‐to‐cell and cell‐to‐matrix interactions (Bogyo et al., 2013). To test if FURIN could interact with and cleave THBS1, we performed co‐immunoprecipitation by incubating HUVEC and HAEC lysates with either an anti‐FURIN antibody or an anti‐THBS1 antibody. Subsequently a western blot analysis with the anti‐THBS1 antibody and the immunoprecipitant prepared with the anti‐FURIN antibody detected the presence of THBS1 (Fig. 2A ), indicating an interaction between FURIN and THBS1. In support, a western blot analysis with the anti‐FURIN antibody and the immunoprecipitants prepared with the anti‐THBS1 antibody detected the presence of FURIN (Fig. 2B ). Importantly, these findings were validated in HAECs, where identical co‐immunoprecipitation experiments yielded consistent results, further supporting the interaction between FURIN and THBS1 (Fig. 2C,D ).

Furthermore, western blot analysis showed that intracellular THBS1 levels were increased in FURIN‐overexpressing VECs compared to the LV‐Vector control (Fig. 2E ). However, no significant differences were observed among the multiplicities of infection (MOI) 1, MOI 3 and MOI 5 groups. Consistently, the corresponding supernatants showed increased levels of cleaved THBS1 in the FURIN‐overexpressing groups (Fig. 2F ), whilst the intensity of this band weakened in the presence of a FURIN‐neutralizing antibody (Fig. 2G ), suggesting that FURIN can cleave THBS1.

FURIN influences THBS1 expression

Quantitative RT‐PCR and western blot analyses showed that FURIN knockdown resulted in a decrease in THBS1 mRNA and protein levels in VECs, whereas FURIN overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 3A,B,C ), suggesting that FURIN influences THBS1 expression. Likewise, THBS1 knockdown led to a reduction in FURIN mRNA and protein levels, whilst THBS1 overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 3D,E,F ), suggesting that THBS1 exerts a reciprocal effect on FURIN expression.

Because both FURIN and THBS1 have been reported to be capable of activating TGFβ1 signalling (Wang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024), we tested if TGFβ1 is implicated in the reciprocal influence between FURIN and THBS1 expression observed in our analyses described above. Our experiment showed that in the presence of an inhibitor (LY2109761) of TGFβ1 receptors the effect of FURIN knockdown on THBS1 expression increased (Fig. 3G ), and similarly, the influence of THBS1 knockdown on FURIN expression became more pronounced (Fig. 3H ).

THBS1 mediates the effect of FURIN on VEC apoptosis

Following the above experiments, we investigated a possible role of THBS1 in FURIN‐induced VEC apoptosis. SiRNA‐induced THBS1 knockdown reduced VEC apoptosis (Fig. 4A,B ), whereas lentivirus‐mediated augmentation of THBS1 expression had the opposite effect (Fig. 4C,D ). Importantly, THBS1 knockdown attenuated the effect of FURIN overexpression on VEC apoptosis (Fig. 4E ), suggesting that FURIN promotes VEC apoptosis via THBS1. Similarly, FURIN knockdown reduced the effect of THBS1 on VEC apoptosis (Fig. 4F ), which is in agreement with FURIN being capable of processing THBS1, as described in Section 3.2.

FURIN induces apoptotic VEC efferocytosis by monocyte‐derived macrophages

Having found that FURIN promotes VEC apoptosis, we investigated whether FURIN influences the clearance of apoptotic VECs through efferocytosis by macrophages. We incubated HUVECs and HAECs sequentially with the apoptosis inducer TNF‐α, the pHrodo red fluorescence dye, and monocyte‐derived macrophages, and subsequently performed a cytometry analysis of the pHrodo fluorescence signal in the macrophages. The experiment showed that FURIN knockdown in HUVECs reduced, whilst FURIN overexpression in HUVECs enhanced, the pHrodo fluorescence signal in the macrophages (Fig. 5A,B ), indicating that FURIN promotes HUVEC efferocytosis by macrophages. Furthermore, we found that THBS1 knockdown in HUVECs attenuated the influence of FURIN on efferocytosis (Fig. 5B ), suggesting that THBS1 mediates the efferocytotic effect of FURIN. Importantly, these findings were independently validated in HAECs, in which consistent results were obtained (Fig. 5C ). In support blocking the THBS1 receptor CD36 using a CD36 neutralizing antibody markedly attenuated HUVEC efferocytosis by macrophages (Fig. 5D ).

FURIN affects VEC monolayer permeability

Having found that FURIN promotes VEC apoptosis and efferocytosis, we tested if this could lead to compromised structural integrity of the endothelial layer. We transfected VECs with either a FURIN siRNA to induce FURIN knockdown or a control siRNA, cultured the cells to form a confluent monolayer, stimulated them with TNF‐α to induce apoptosis, added monocyte‐derived macrophages to the endothelial monolayer, and subsequently performed a permeability assay. The assay showed a reduction in dextran infiltration across the monolayer of FURIN siRNA‐transfected VECs (Fig. 6A,B ), suggesting that FURIN promotes VEC monolayer permeability.

FURIN inhibition reduces VEC apoptosis in atherosclerotic lesions in a mouse model

Further to the above in vitro experiments, we investigated if Furin had an in vivo effect on VEC proliferation and apoptosis in atherosclerotic lesions in a mouse model. To this end, we fed Ldlr−/− mice a WTD to induce atherosclerosis and injected the mice with either a FURIN inhibitor (α−1‐PDX) or the solvent (PBS) as a control. Double immunostaining of the endothelial marker CD31 together with either the proliferation marker Ki67 or an apoptosis indicator (TUNEL staining) in atherosclerotic lesions showed that mice treated with the FURIN inhibitor had a higher percentage of CD31/Ki67 double‐positive cells and a lower percentage of CD31/TUNEL double‐positive cells compared to mice injected with the solvent (Fig. 7A,B ), suggesting that FURIN promotes VEC apoptosis in this mouse model.

Discussion

VEC apoptosis triggers the development of atherosclerosis that underlies several major diseases including CAD, stroke and aneurysms (Littlewood & Bennett, 2003). Our study reveals that FURIN promotes VEC apoptosis and indicates that FURIN exerts this effect via modulating THBS1. These findings provide a new insight into the molecular basis of VEC dysfunction, with relevance to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and potential therapeutic implications.

Being a proprotein convertase, FURIN has been reported to be capable of processing a number of proteins, including matrix metalloproteinase‐14 (MMP14) (Yana & Weiss, 2000), A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 7 (ADAMTS7) (Somerville et al., 2004) and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) (Benjannet et al., 2006). One of the novel findings from our study is that FURIN can cleave THBS1, thus identifying a previously unknown substrate of FURIN. This finding is noteworthy because THBS1 has been shown to be an important player in many biological and pathological processes, including atherogenesis (Adams, 1997; Febbraio et al., 2001; Krishna & Golledge, 2013).

Additionally, the results of our study indicate that FURIN enhances the expression of THBS1 in VECs, as we found that FURIN knockdown resulted in a decrease, whereas FURIN overexpression led to an increase, in THBS1 mRNA and protein levels. In line with our results, a previous study showed that baicalin (7‐d‐glucuronic acid‐5,6‐dihydroxyflavone), a molecule directly acting on FURIN, inhibited THBS1 expression, and this inhibitory effect was enhanced by knockdown of FURIN (Wang et al., 2023). Together, these observations suggest that TGFβ1 signalling may be involved in FURIN‐associated regulation of THBS1 expression. However, given that the present study examined this pathway using limited pharmacological evidence our data do not establish a definitive FURIN–TGFβ1–SMAD–THBS1 feedback mechanism, and the precise signalling events involved require further investigation.

Taken together, the results of our study indicate that FURIN can upregulate THBS1 expression and process the THBS1 protein. Notably, it has been reported that THBS1 is an important regulator of VEC apoptosis (Guo et al., 1997; Jiménez et al., 2000) and that THBS1 modulates VEC apoptosis by activating the caspase cell death pathway (Nor et al., 2000). In support, our present study also demonstrates that THBS1 promotes VEC apoptosis. Furthermore, our study showed that THBS1 knockdown attenuated FURIN‐induced VEC apoptosis, suggesting that FURIN promotes VEC apoptosis via THBS1.

A previous study found that apoptotic VECs could induce healthy VECs to secrete THBS1 and that THBS1 acted as an attractant to recruit macrophages and promote apoptotic cell clearance through efferocytosis by macrophages (Kirsch et al., 2010). Another study showed that THBS1 interacted with the externalized phosphatidylserine on apoptotic neutrophils and CD36 on the surface of macrophages, thereby facilitating macrophages to recognize and bind to apoptotic neutrophils for efferocytosis (Savill et al., 1992). Adding to these previous findings, our study indicates that FURIN can increase and process THBS1, and by doing so, promote CD36‐mediated efferocytosis of apoptotic VECs executed by monocyte‐derived macrophages. This new finding expands the current understanding of the molecular repertoire participating in efferocytosis and their roles in the efferocytosis process.

Our previous work has demonstrated that FURIN plays an important role in the development of atherosclerotic lesions (Yakala et al., 2019). Using animal models, we previously showed that the administration of an irreversible inhibitor of FURIN, α–1‐PDX (α1‐antitrypsin Portland), resulted in a reduction in atherosclerotic lesion area, whereas overexpression of FURIN caused a significant increase in lesion size (Yakala et al., 2019). Additionally, our in vitro studies showed that FURIN induced monocyte adhesion to VECs and monocyte trans‐endothelial migration (Yakala et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2018), suggesting that FURIN promotes atherogenesis, in part, by facilitating monocyte recruitment into the atherosclerotic lesion. Further to these previous findings our present study revealed that FURIN can induce VEC apoptosis in both in vitro and in vivo mouse models and increase endothelial permeability. This new finding provides an additional mechanistic explanation for the contribution of FURIN in the atherogenic process, as endothelial leakage promotes lipid infiltration into the vascular wall and is a hallmark in atherogenesis (Mundi et al., 2018).

Atherosclerotic plaques can contain many neovessels, which undermine plaque stability and facilitate plaque enlargement, as they provide additional passages for lipid infiltration and monocyte recruitment (Moreno et al., 2006; O'Brien et al., 1996). It has been suggested that VEC apoptosis facilitates the development of the angiogenic network (Watson et al., 2017). In line with this our study shows that FURIN, which induces VEC apoptosis, plays a role in angiogenesis. This represents another potential mechanism through which FURIN promotes atherogenesis.

Our study reveals novel functions of FURIN, particularly in THBS1 processing, VEC apoptosis, efferocytosis and endothelial permeability. These findings are useful for a better understanding of how FURIN participates in the development of atherosclerotic plaques and provide further evidence to suggest that FURIN is a potential therapeutic target.
