The Effect of Semaglutide on Antipsychotic-Induced Weight Gain and Other Metabolic Parameters, among a Cohort of Inpatients
Authors: Riddhita De, Yasser Amin Alfatwa, Pruntha Kanagasundaram, Julia Eve Saragosa, Joyce Chan, Gary Remington, Ravi Retnakaran, Marcos Sanches, Sanjeev Sockalingam, Sri Mahavir Agarwal, Margaret K Hahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisAntipsychotic use in severe mental illnesses (SMI) is associated with metabolic dysregulation, including antipsychotic-induced weight gain (AIWG), type 2 diabetes (T2D), and dyslipidemia. In the case of non-response to metformin which is currently recommended for AIWG mitigation, no clear alternatives exist. Semaglutide, a weekly injectable glucagon like peptide-1 receptor agonist, represents a promising option. However, effectiveness and safety data in SMI are lacking. With initiation of semaglutide, we hypothesized weight loss, improvements in metabolic indices, and good tolerability.Study DesignA retrospective chart review was conducted for inpatients receiving antipsychotics, initiated on once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide between January 4, 2018 and March 31, 2024 at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Toronto, Canada, that analyzed weight loss, metabolic parameters, and side effects.Study Results47 individuals with SMI were included: 59.6% males, mean age of 43 ± 13.2 years, 83% had dysglycemia, and 66% had T2D at baseline. The maximum dose of semaglutide in this cohort was 2 mg/week. There was a mean weight loss of 3.15 ± 0.77 kg (n = 47), 7.27 ± 0.97 kg (n = 24), 9.33 ± 1.16 kg (n = 15), and 12.25 ± 1.32 kg (n = 11) at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, respectively (P < .001). Individuals without T2D experienced greater weight loss versus those with T2D (P = .022). Additionally, significant decreases were noted in body mass index and HbA1c (P < .001), alongside improvements in lipid parameters; most common side effects were gastrointestinal in nature but did not result in semaglutide discontinuation.ConclusionThis retrospective chart review of inpatients with SMI supports efficacy and safety of semaglutide up to a year of follow-up, warranting future adequately powered randomized controlled trials.Keywords: GLP1-RA, schizophrenia-spectrum illness, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity

Introduction

Individuals with severe mental illness (SMI) have concerningly high rates of metabolic comorbidities, with over 50% facing added consequence of obesity.1 When compared to the general population, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) has also been found to be 3- to 5-fold higher.2,3 Moreover, patients with SMI die on average 15 to 20 years earlier from cardiovascular disease4 and the standardized mortality ratios of people with SMI including schizophrenia spectrum disorders are 2.5 times higher than the general population.5,6 As mortality rates related to cardiovascular disease have been decreasing in most nations,5 the increased mortality rates in SMI suggest that these individuals are not benefiting from improvements in cardiometabolic care. A growing body of literature shows that modifiable cardiometabolic risk factors remain vastly undertreated in this vulnerable population.7–9

Obesity remains an important cardiometabolic risk factor in SMI not only due to lifestyle factors ranging from an unhealthy diet and behaviors to a lack of physical activity,10 but also owing to metabolic dysregulation associated with antipsychotic treatments. While antipsychotic medications are widely prescribed for many psychiatric conditions, antipsychotic-induced weight gain (AIWG) remains a highly prevalent and distressing adverse effect, often leading to treatment discontinuation.11 Similarly, antipsychotics contribute to high rates of metabolic comorbidities including T2D, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.12,13 At present, metformin, which is considered the first-line treatment for T2D globally, has shown evidence in the prevention and management of AIWG among patients, and is now recommended across treatment guidelines for this indication.14–17 However, there are no clear, alternative pharmacological options available for the significant subset of patients who do not respond to metformin. Newer treatments for both diabetes and obesity include glucagon-like peptide-1-receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) which have shown early promise in people with SMI.18 GLP-1RAs mimic glucagon-like peptide-1, an incretin hormone that acts in a glucose dependent manner, slowing gastric emptying and displaying beneficial effects on glycemic control, alongside weight loss properties and improvements in dyslipidemia.19–21 Some of the earlier GLP-1RAs to have been explored in SMI (ie, exenatide and liraglutide) have shown promising, modest weight loss effects, with improvements in metabolic parameters and no major concerns for adverse events, other than increased rates of gastrointestinal effects including nausea and vomiting.18,22–26

Among this GLP-1RA drug class, semaglutide has emerged as the next-generation, weekly subcutaneous medication, with evidence of good tolerability and superior efficacy when compared to first generation GLP-1RAs.27 The Semaglutide Unabated Sustainability in Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes (SUSTAIN) trials 1 to 7 showed a mean body weight reduction ranging from 3.5 to 4.6 kg with semaglutide doses ranging from 0.5 to 1 mg weekly.28 Additionally, a range of trials called The Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity (STEP) trials explored a higher dose formulation of semaglutide (2.4 mg weekly) among overweight or obese individuals with and without T2D. These trials demonstrated weight loss ranging from 14.9% to 17.4%, with greater efficacy shown at higher doses and longer durations.29 In addition to weight outcomes, improvements were also demonstrated across metabolic parameters including HbA1c levels and lipid parameters, alongside a reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events.30 However, these studies excluded individuals with mental health diagnoses. Therefore, while semaglutide holds promise in those with SMI and dysmetabolism, there is a paucity of data in this population, which may have special considerations in relation to efficacy and safety.

Aim of Study

This study preliminarily evaluated the effects of semaglutide initiation on antipsychotic-induced weight gain and other metabolic variables, through the assessment of retrospective patient data within a controlled, inpatient hospital setting.

Methods

Overview

The specific objective of this retrospective study was to analyze the demographic, anthropometric, and metabolic data collected from inpatients with all psychiatric diagnoses at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH) in Toronto, Canada, who were taking antipsychotic medication(s) and initiated on subcutaneous semaglutide. The primary outcome was to assess changes in weight (in kg) during a period of up to 12 months post-semaglutide initiation. Secondary outcomes included changes in body mass index (BMI), HbA1c, and lipid parameters. We also reported on any side effects documented in the charts as an exploratory outcome, in addition to correlations between weight and other metabolic measures, to assess whether weight loss might predict changes in other metabolic variables.

Ethics Approval and Data Collection

This retrospective study was approved by the Research Ethics Board at CAMH (REB # 075/2024), where the requirement for informed consent was waived. Only data as outlined below were collected, with all relevant data records having been encrypted using passwords and anonymized where applicable.

Data were collected up to a period of 12 months as follows: baseline, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months following semaglutide initiation at CAMH. The study timeframe spanned from January 4, 2018 (when semaglutide was approved by Health Canada) to March 31, 2024. Data pertaining to primary and secondary outcomes were only collected for individuals who had a minimum of three months of follow-up and ascribed to predefined timepoints if they were within +6 weeks of the specified timepoint. To better assess our exploratory outcome of side effects and early discontinuations post-semaglutide initiation, individuals with <3 months of follow-up data were additionally analyzed.

We included any individuals with a severe mental illness (SMI), treated with antipsychotic(s) encompassing schizophrenia spectrum disorders (schizophrenia, schizoaffective, schizophreniform disorder, delusional disorder, psychotic disorder not otherwise specified), bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder, in addition to autism spectrum disorder and substance-induced psychotic disorder, provided these individuals were also receiving treatment with at least one antipsychotic medication, at a minimum of a “standard dose” as defined by the World Health Organization and/or dosage ranges recommended by the Schizophrenia Patient Outcomes Research Team Treatment Recommendations.31,32 We did not exclude any psychiatric comorbidities and our inpatient units ranged from chronic treatment units (ie, the psychosis recovery ward, and forensics units) to acute care units focused on intensive patient treatment, from where a subset of individuals may have been transferred to the aforementioned chronic care wards.

The endpoint for follow-up was defined as either (i) a discharge from the inpatient ward, (ii) if further follow-up could not be collected as part of the approved protocol timeframe, or (iii) semaglutide discontinuation.

Data Analysis

All data were analyzed using the IBM statistical package of SPSS, version 30. For our primary outcome, a linear mixed model analysis was run to estimate the effect of time (modeled as a categorical variable: baseline, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months) on weight post initiation of semaglutide. Age, sex, and baseline weight were included in the model as covariates. We also ran three subanalyses exploring weight trajectories of individuals (1) with versus without T2D, (2) semaglutide alone versus co-treated with metformin, and (3) those treated with versus without clozapine, by adding an interaction between these variables and time. A linear mixed model accounted for the longitudinal dependence in the data and full information maximum likelihood method was used for estimation, which handles missing values in the dependent variables under the missing at random assumption (MAR). The Akaike information criteria value was used to assess the need for residual covariance structure in addition to the random subject intercepts included in the model.

For our secondary outcomes of BMI, HbA1c, and lipid parameters, the same model was used by replacing the dependent variable. Our exploratory outcomes included any medication side effects which were tracked over follow-up, and the mixed model was utilized to determine whether weight loss predicted changes in other metabolic parameters as an additional exploratory outcome. Baseline descriptive analyses were reported including means with standard deviations (SD), medians presented with Q1–Q3, and frequencies as appropriate. All model-specific data have been presented with their marginal means and standard errors, and significance determined at P < .05.

Results

Sample Characteristics

The total sample size included 47 individuals; baseline characteristics of the sample are shown in Table 1. Mean age was 43 + 13.2 years. 29.8% of the individuals identified as Caucasian and 36.2% as Black, 59.6% were male, and 85.1% of the sample was diagnosed with either schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder. At baseline, 66% of the sample was noted to have T2D, 83% had dysglycemia, and 74.5% were already on metformin prior to being initiated on semaglutide. The mean weight was 113.2 ± 30.7 kg with a BMI of 40.2 ± 10.3 kg/m2 (63.8% of study individuals had a BMI of at least 35 kg/m2 or above) and a mean HbA1c of 7.3 ± 2.1% at baseline. While 40.4% of the cohort was prescribed one antipsychotic medication, the remainder were co-prescribed two or more antipsychotics. Furthermore, 66% of the cohort was taking at least one concomitant psychotropic medication, example—mood stabilizers.

The Effect of Dose on Weight over 12 Months of Follow-Up

While an interaction effect of semaglutide dose and time was not significant in our sample (F(4, 88.37 = 1.12; P = .35)), the main effect of semaglutide dose was found to be significant with absolute weight (in kg) as the dependent variable, independent of time. When adjusted for baseline weight, age, and sex, the beta coefficient showed that with each unit increase in dose in mg, there was a reduction of 2.46 ± 1.16 kg in weight (95% CI = −4.76, −0.17; P = .036).

Secondary Outcomes

The individual effects of time on various other metabolic parameters are presented in their respective sections below.

BMI: The effect of time on BMI was significant with a P value of <.001, when controlled for age, sex, and baseline BMI (Table 2). There was a mean reduction in the BMI as follows: 1.18 ± 0.29 kg/m2 at 3 months, 2.54 ± 0.37 kg/m2 at 6 months, 3.35 ± 0.44 kg/m2 at 9 months, and 4.33 ± 0.50 kg/m2 at 12 months, with P values being <.001 at all respective timepoints.

HbA1c: As seen in Table 2, the effect of time on HbA1c levels post semaglutide initiation was significant at all timepoints (P < .001). Following adjustment for age, sex, and weight, there was a mean reduction in HbA1c levels by 1.1 ± 0.3% (P < .001), 1.1 ± 0.3% (P < .001), 1.2 ± 0.3% (P < .001), and 1.2 ± 0.3% (P = .001) at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, respectively. We opted to focus on HbA1c instead of fasting glucose levels, due to difficulty in the ascertainment of fasting status.

Cholesterol and other parameters: Table 2 showcases the comparisons from baseline for total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL, and HDL levels. When adjusted for age, sex, and baseline values, semaglutide was associated with a significant reduction in total cholesterol (P = .004), LDL (P = .008), and triglycerides (P < .001) levels, while change in HDL was not significant (P = .28).

Over time, there was also an overall statistical difference in systolic blood pressure (P = .014) but not in diastolic blood pressure (P = .11) over 12 months. Waist circumference significantly decreased (P = .019) over 6 months of available data (Table S5).

Exploratory Outcomes

Does weight predict changes in HbA1c and triglycerides? Adjusting for baseline parameters, age, and sex in the mixed model analysis, weight predicted changes in triglycerides (P = .014) but not HbA1c changes (P = .66).

Side effects reported during semaglutide treatment: The most common side effects were gastrointestinal. Given that most individuals were already taking laxatives for constipation at baseline (42 of 47), side effects that were noted as ongoing or which had worsened were reported. At 3 months out of the 47 individuals on semaglutide, 21.3% reported ongoing constipation (n = 10), while 27.7% had newly reported nausea, diarrhea, and/or vomiting (n = 13). At 6 months, out of 24 individuals, 37.5% of the sample reported ongoing constipation (n = 9) and 8.3% reported a combination of abdominal discomfort, nausea, and diarrhea (n = 2). At 9 months out of 15 individuals, 33.3% reported adverse effects of constipation (n = 5) and 20% reported a combination of constipation, diarrhea, vomiting, dizziness, and abdominal pain (n = 3). At 12 months, out of 11 individuals on semaglutide, 45.5% reported ongoing constipation (n = 5). No discontinuations of semaglutide due to adverse effects were reported in the cohort.

Our clozapine subset analysis also revealed that of the 18 individuals on clozapine, at 3 months, 8 individuals reported side effects, 5 of whom experienced ongoing constipation while the remainder reported loose stools, vomiting and/or bloating. At 6 months, 2 of 9 reported ongoing constipation. At 9 months (n = 7) and 12 months (n = 6), only 1 person reported ongoing constipation.

Furthermore, among individuals who had less than 3 months ±6 weeks of follow-up (n = 67), it was found that there were 64 patients who were discharged to the community on semaglutide treatment (average time on semaglutide at the time of discharge being 11.6 days). From this sample, only 3 individuals had stopped their treatment at discharge due to the following: n = 2 due to possible diagnosis of Type 1 Diabetes and initiation of insulin by care team, and n = 1 due to the patient’s dislike of needles.

Discussion

The present chart review explored the association between semaglutide initiation on weight and other metabolic parameters, over 12 months in a naturalistic cohort of inpatients on antipsychotics at CAMH. In keeping with prior randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving people with obesity and/or T2D which have however systematically excluded individuals with SMI, our findings provide preliminary evidence that semaglutide may be an effective treatment in a population prescribed antipsychotics, including clozapine. Our results are also in-line with findings from a case-series from our center in 12 outpatients with SMI who failed to respond to metformin, with a reported mean weight loss of 8.67 kg over the course of 12 months of semaglutide treatment.33 Interestingly, our findings show greater weight loss in the inpatient population. This is likely attributable to a larger overall sample size, higher doses of semaglutide during the study period, alongside an inpatient setting, which may have enhanced treatment adherence to semaglutide. Additionally, a recently published RCT, Efficacy and Safety of Semaglutide Versus Placebo for People with Schizophrenia on Clozapine with Obesity (the COaST trial), was conducted among obese, clozapine patients over 36 weeks, which found a comparable weight reduction with semaglutide 2 mg.34 Another recently published RCT explored the efficacy of semaglutide 1 mg among those with schizophrenia, prediabetes, and obesity, finding a weight reduction of 9.21 kg over 30 weeks.35 It should also be highlighted that in keeping with findings from the STEP trials, semaglutide also appears to show greater efficacy as compared to the earlier GLP-1RAs in individuals with SMI. For example, an individual patient data meta-analysis of three RCTs conducted in individuals treated with antipsychotics found a greater weight reduction of 3.71 kg in the exenatide/liraglutide group when compared to controls after 16.2 ± 4.0 weeks of treatment.18 A subsequently published RCT also explored the effectiveness of liraglutide in schizophrenia spectrum disorders, reporting a mean weight loss of 5.7 ± 7.9 kg over 6 months of treatment.23

Our results exploring the efficacy of semaglutide up to a dose of 2 mg, alongside those by Siskind et al.,34 are also in keeping with data from the STEP 1 study, where over 68 weeks, participants without T2D prescribed semaglutide 2.4 mg experienced a 15.3 kg weight loss or a 14.9% change in weight from baseline.36 Similarly, the STEP 2 study which included individuals with T2D reported a mean weight decrease of 9.6% from baseline.37 These results are comparable to our findings which showed a 12.25 kg mean change in weight, and 10.7% body weight reduction over 12 months. In our cohort, 66% had a diagnosis of T2D at baseline. Interestingly, our subgroup analysis showed that individuals with T2D lost less weight with semaglutide over 12 months (9.75 kg) compared to those without T2D (18.22 kg). This is consistent with existing literature in the general population showing that semaglutide treatment in those with T2D was associated with lower weight loss versus those without T2D.29,38 It should be noted however that there are some key differences between the STEP studies and this investigation. These differences include a shorter study duration and lower semaglutide dose (mean dose of 1.36 mg and the highest dose reaching 2 mg), as compared to the STEP trials which used doses up to 2.4 mg, in line with the dose approved for chronic obesity management. Moreover, the T2D group in our inpatient cohort was found to be on a higher overall mean dose of semaglutide when compared to those without T2D over 12 months. This indicates that dose alone does not explain the reduced weight loss seen in the T2D group in comparison to those without T2D.

Our study also found that among individuals co-treated with semaglutide and metformin (74.5% of the sample) versus semaglutide alone, there were no overall differences in weight loss trajectories. However, sample size in the semaglutide group decreased by 9 and 12 months (Figure S2), which precluded statistical analyses warranting future examination.

While our study did not find a significant interaction effect of semaglutide dose and time on weight, this may have been due to the small sample size. Additionally, this may have been due to data collinearity between dose and time. Despite this, the relationship between dose and weight showed an inverse association, which is in keeping with findings from the STEP trials.29,39

Semaglutide treatment was also associated with changes in BMI, HbA1c, and triglyceride parameters. The improvement in BMI and HbA1c levels were similar to findings from the STEP trials, where a reduction of 5.54 kg/m2 for BMI was reported over the 68-week timeframe.36 This is comparable to our 12 month findings, albeit limited by a small sample size, where the BMI reduction was 4.33 kg/m2.36 Moreover, the mean HbA1c reduction of 1.2% is also comparable to the STEP 2 study, whereby a decrease of 1.6% at the 2.4 mg dose and 1.5% at the 1 mg dose were reported over 68 weeks.29,37 It should be highlighted that greater HbA1c improvements have been observed with semaglutide, compared to earlier generation GLP-1RAs.24 Triglyceride levels, as observed in our study, have also been shown to decrease in the STEP trials.36 While we saw consistent changes at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months in measures of BMI, HbA1c, and triglyceride levels, LDL and total cholesterol levels only became significant after the 3-month timepoint. HDL levels, despite showing overall absolute improvements, did not reach statistical significance, suggesting this variable might improve with a longer duration of treatment.40 Of note, our findings were limited by a loss to follow-up due to discharge from hospital, or missing data for lipid parameters in comparison to the primary outcome of weight.

Similarly, due to missing data in patient charts, the results of several important parameters including waist circumference could not be analyzed. Additionally, blood pressure measures were often recorded without denoting if they were measured sitting or lying down, introducing variability in the data. It is noteworthy to highlight however, that previous literature has shown GLP-1RAs like semaglutide to enhance arterial elasticity which can impact systolic blood pressure, therefore aligning with our observation of reductions in this parameter.41 In addition, our data suggest that weight loss may drive decreases in triglycerides but not HbA1c. This observation is in keeping with the physiological understanding of GLP-1RAs, which can directly impact pathways of glucose metabolism, independently of weight loss.

Additionally, the noted side effects in this study are consistent with literature reporting gastrointestinal side effects ranging from nausea to diarrhea. For example, the SUSTAIN trials 1-5 reported common adverse events of nausea or vomiting, even with lower doses of semaglutide (0.5 and 1.0 mg).42 Another systematic review and the STEP studies37 reported mild to moderate gastrointestinal adverse events including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation.43 Importantly, in our cohort, no semaglutide discontinuations were reported due to adverse effects.

In summary, the strengths of this chart review included a longitudinal follow-up of inpatients, controlling for variables such as medication adherence alongside a standard hospital diet. Conversely, it could be argued that more structured routines may improve weight and other metabolic outcomes, and could account for some of the improvements seen. This may however be countered by reduced physical activity, particularly if activity passes to the community are limited, as well as some literature suggesting that hospital diets may be high in processed ingredients, saturated fat, sodium, and sugar.44,45

Some additional limitations must also be acknowledged. Firstly, not all anthropometric and metabolic variables were collected in a standardized manner resulting in missing data which impacted analyses. Additionally, not all obesity-related comorbidities could be captured through our chart review which might be expected to improve with semaglutide such as fatty liver disease, obstructive sleep apnea among others. Furthermore, characteristics of patients who may have been offered semaglutide but declined are unknown, limiting generalizability of findings to those who agree to receive the intervention in an inpatient setting. There was a significant loss to follow-up after the 3-month timepoint especially at 12 months due to discharge from the hospital into the community, which undoubtedly impacted the statistical power of our findings. Another limitation was related to the tracking of side effect severity like constipation, given that 42 of the 47 individuals were already taking laxatives at baseline. That said, in keeping with recent data from the COaST trial,34 there were no reports of discontinuations due to severe constipation, which is particularly relevant for patients receiving clozapine as it can cause gastrointestinal hypomotility and in some cases also lead to bowel obstruction.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

Taken together, our chart review suggests that semaglutide is an effective, generally well-tolerated, and safe intervention in a cohort of inpatients, with comorbid class 2 or 3 obesity and frequent dysglycemia. With the pragmatism of once-weekly injections, semaglutide appears to be a promising medication among individuals with SMI, treated with various antipsychotics, including clozapine. Moving forward, well-powered RCTs are needed in individuals with SMI (with some currently underway and close to completion)46 to validate these findings and further explore effects on additional health outcomes related to metabolic health, such as cognition, personal recovery, and quality of life. Another important consideration for the future will be to track the proportion of individuals continuing semaglutide treatment post discharge to the community, as many individuals were lost to follow-up in this chart review.
