Postharvest life of cut narcissus in depth: differences of flower tissues longevity and stem structure anatomy
Authors: Azra Haghshenas, Abolfazl Jowkar, Esmaeil Nezami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite extensive research on extending the vase life of narcissus, the internal mechanisms—particularly the physiological, biochemical, and anatomical traits—that govern its postharvest longevity remain insufficiently understood. This study examined ten Iranian narcissus populations grown under uniform greenhouse conditions to identify floral and stem traits associated with variation in vase life. Longevity was measured separately for inflorescences, petals, and coronas, while biochemical and physiological attributes were compared between petal and corona tissues and structural changes were assessed in stems during postharvest storage.ResultsCorona tissues exhibited more than twice the longevity of petals and maintained higher relative water content, protein levels, and antioxidant enzyme activity, alongside lower lipid peroxidation, indicating greater resilience to oxidative and hydration-related stress. Anatomical analysis of stem cross-sections revealed progressive tissue shrinkage and parenchyma collapse during vase life, with no evidence of vascular occlusion in any population. Principal component analysis further identified carotenoid concentration, tissue thickness, antioxidant activity, hydration status, and vascular-bundle geometry as the major multivariate dimensions associated with extended vase life.ConclusionThese observations suggest that carotenoid accumulation represent an important biochemical feature associated with extended vase life. The anatomical changes show that postharvest hydraulic decline in narcissus stems is related with cell collapse and vascular tissue shrinkage. These observations are triggered by wounding at the cut surface and potential stress from continuous stem base submersion, and they are comparable with vascular occlusion. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that postharvest performance in narcissus is shaped by coordinated biochemical, hydraulic, and structural traits, providing a physiological and anatomical basis for improving postharvest handling and guiding future studies on flower longevity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09077-6.

Background

The longevity of cut flowers is one of the primary determinants of their postharvest quality and market value, directly influencing consumer satisfaction and profitability in the floriculture industry [1]. Vase life is shaped by a complex interplay of genetic background, preharvest environmental conditions, and both harvesting and postharvest handling practices [2–4]. Prolonging floral longevity remains a persistent challenge for both local and international markets, as it enhances market appeal, reduces waste, and maximizes economic returns [1, 5].

Among specialty cut flowers, Narcissus holds notable cultural and commercial importance worldwide, particularly in Iran, due to its distinctive corona–petal morphology, diverse colors, and pleasant fragrance [6]. In Iran, Narcissus tazetta and N. papyraceus are cultivated and harvested primarily in autumn and winter, often in mixed cropping systems under orchard canopies, on farms, or in their natural habitats. Despite its popularity, the postharvest behavior of narcissus flowers has not been studied in sufficient detail, particularly with respect to tissue-specific senescence dynamics and underlying anatomical or biochemical determinants.

Floral senescence is a genetically regulated process involving programmed cell death (PCD) that is triggered by developmental cues, pollination, or abiotic stress. At the cellular level, it is associated with altered membrane permeability, protein and nucleic acid degradation, sugar depletion, and shifts in antioxidant enzyme activities [7]. In cut flowers, sugar depletion following detachment from the mother plant often accelerates ethylene biosynthesis, hastening senescence [8]. While petal color, shape, and scent play key roles in pollinator attraction and reproductive success, these traits are no longer maintained once pollination is completed, leading to rapid deterioration of floral tissues [7, 9]. Under postharvest conditions, additional factors—such as water stress, low relative humidity, suboptimal temperatures, ethylene accumulation, nutrient depletion, vascular occlusion, and microbial proliferation—can further shorten vase life [3, 4, 7, 9].

Hydraulic dysfunction at the cut stem base is a critical factor in postharvest decline. Vascular occlusion can result from tylosis formation, deposition of gums or mucilage, microbial growth, or physical blockages such as air embolisms [10]. When water loss via transpiration exceeds uptake, stems experience water imbalance and turgor loss, triggering premature wilting and reduced vase life [10, 11]. Although vascular occlusion has been extensively investigated in various cut flowers, there is a lack of quantitative information on postharvest stem structural changes—particularly in cut narcissus—where potential contributions of tissue shrinkage or other anatomical alterations to water uptake decline remain unexplored. According to the current literature, no studies have specifically investigated changes in stem tissue and vascular bundles at the cut end of narcissus, particularly the Iranian varieties.

Previous research on narcissus has largely focused on the evaluation of preservative solutions and methods to delay senescence [12–17]. However, comprehensive studies integrating tissue-specific biochemical profiles, structural characteristics of stem vascular tissues, and morphological traits with postharvest performance are lacking. In particular, the corona—morphologically distinct from petals and developmentally analogous to stamen tissue [18]—may differ in its physiological resilience and senescence trajectory, but such differences remain underexplored in Iranian cultivars and populations.

The objectives of the present study were to:Evaluate the variation in vase life among ten Iranian narcissus populations under standardized growth and postharvest conditions;Compare the biochemical and physiological changes in corona and petal tissues during postharvest storage;Assess structural changes in stem and vascular tissues over vase life.

Understanding how these traits interact to determine postharvest performance can inform targeted breeding strategies, optimize handling protocols, and ultimately extend the marketability of Narcissus flowers while reducing postharvest losses.

Methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Taxonomic identification of ten Iranian Narcissus populations (i.e., N. tazetta and N. papyraceus) was carried out by the authors according to their morphological characteristics, outlined in the botanical descriptions. All of the plant materials were obtained with the assistance of local growers or site authorities and in accordance with institutional and ethical guidelines for plant research.

Narcissus populations were gathered from long-established farms and gardens, where bulbs have been cultivated continuously for more than 20 years. In addition, two accessions from the Behbahan region were collected directly from a natural habitat. Representative flowers of narcissus populations collected across various regions of Iran. Each panel includes the collection site (coordinates and location name), species and population identification, and a key diagnostic trait

Bulbs were planted in mid-autumn in the greenhouse in a 4:1 (v/v) mixture of silty-clay soil and leaf mold, arranged in rows spaced 20 cm apart, with 18 cm between bulbs within rows. No fertilizer was applied during the entire experimental period. Irrigation was carried out on average once per week, when the soil had visibly dried. The greenhouse was maintained at an average temperature of 25 ± 5 °C during the day and 15 ± 5 °C at night. Plants were grown under natural light, with an average photoperiod of 10.5 h light and 13.5 h darkness during the study period.

Harvesting and sample preparation

Flowers were harvested at the same developmental stage, defined as the point when the first flower on the inflorescence had just begun to open, and were immediately transferred to the laboratory. The stems, which varied in their initial length, were trimmed under water with a sharp blade to a uniform length of 10 cm. Each stem was placed in a 300 mL plastic cup containing 250 mL of distilled water; the cups were covered with plastic wrap to minimize evaporation. To assess postharvest longevity, the flower vases were maintained in a controlled-environment growth chamber (Noor Sanat Company, Iran) set at 23 °C, 60% relative humidity, 10 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1 light intensity, and a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod until the end of their vase life (i.e., complete senescence characterized by full discoloration and tissue desiccation), which varied among populations depending on their postharvest longevity. The same type of plastic cups, filled with distilled water and covered in the same way, served as water controls and were placed alongside the flowers in the growth chamber.

Vase life assessment

Vase life was evaluated based on standardized visual quality ratings adapted from previous narcissus studies [15, 19], with modifications to account for the specific senescence pattern of Narcissus. Nine stems per population were assessed individually. The day of harvest was recorded as day 0, and flowers were observed daily until the end of postharvest life.

Inflorescence vase life was defined as the number of days from harvest until 50% of the flowers within the inflorescence exhibited visible senescence symptoms—characterized by tissue discoloration (browning) and/or drying—at which point the inflorescence was deemed to have lost commercial quality.

Visual evaluation of senescence symptoms was performed under uniform lighting conditions, with particular attention to progressive color changes (e.g., browning at petal margins) and tissue desiccation, which are typical postharvest symptoms of narcissus flowers [17]. Individual floral tissue evaluation (Petals vs. Coronas)

Separate longevity measurements were taken for petals and coronas:

• Petal longevity was recorded as the number of days from harvest until the first visible browning at the petal margins (Score 2).

• Corona longevity was recorded as the number of days from harvest until the corona lost turgidity and showed clear browning, wilting or drying (Score 4).

• Score 1: Completely healthy and fresh.

• Score 2: Browning at the tips or margins of at least one or two petals (End of petal longevity).

• Score 3: Petals dried while corona remains intact.

• Score 4: Brown/dried petals with corona beginning to brown (End of corona longevity).

• Score 5: Both petals and corona completely brown/dried.

Because the progression of visible senescence symptoms differs among floral tissues, tissue-specific endpoint scores were defined to represent comparable stages of ornamental deterioration in petals, coronas, and the inflorescence. Vase life (longevity) was subsequently calculated as the number of days required for each tissue to reach its predefined senescence endpoint.

By using a structured visual scoring system and separating petal and corona assessments, potential subjective bias in vase life determination was minimized and tissue-specific differences could be more accurately quantified.

Measurement of morphophysiological characteristics

At the beginning of the experiment, the number of buds on each inflorescence was counted, and the cut flowers were weighed. To calculate water uptake, relative water uptake, and evaporation and transpiration rates, the weights of the vase containing water and flowers, as well as the weights of the water and control vases, were measured daily via a digital scale with a precision of two decimal places. Additionally, the dry weight, moisture content, and dry matter or biomass of the corona and petal tissues were separately calculated. The stem diameter was measured with a digital caliper, and on the day of harvest (day 0), the thickness of the corona and petal tissues was measured using a micrometer.

Water Uptake (WU) and Relative Water Uptake (RWU)

The amount of evapotranspiration was calculated on the basis of the difference in the weights of the vases at the start and end of the experiment. Relative water uptake (RWU) was determined via a slight modification of the method of Spricigo et al. (2021) by dividing the amount of solution absorbed by the initial weight of the flowers [20]. The amount of water uptake (WU) was calculated by the difference in the weight of the vase with flowers on the initial day and the final day minus the evaporation rate. Water content

The relative water content (RWC) of the tissues in the corona and petals was measured in nine replicates on the day of harvest and on the third day. The petals and corona were carefully and precisely separated and weighed using a digital scale (wet weight). The flower tissues were subsequently placed in Petri dishes and covered with distilled water for 24 h. After this, the tissues were placed on paper towels for 5 min to remove excess water and weighed again (turgid weight). The samples were then placed in an oven at 70 °C for 48 h and weighed once more (dry weight). Ion leakage and electrical conductivity

To measure cellular ion leakage, ten 5 mm diameter discs of the corona and petals were separated on the day of harvest and on the third day. The discs were placed in 10 mL of double-distilled water and kept on a shaker at room temperature for 24 h. Electrical conductivity was measured via a Metrohm 644 conductometer (Switzerland), and pH was measured via a Jenway pH meter (UK). Electrical conductivity was reported in μS/cm [2].

Stem anatomy and morphometric analysis

To study the structural characteristics of stems and their postharvest changes, Cross-sections were prepared from stems at harvest (day 0) and after three days (day 3) under the same controlled postharvest conditions and distilled-water vase solution used for vase-life assessment.

For each population, three stems were used at each sampling time. From each stem, a 0.5 cm segment was cut from the stem base and fixed in FAA solution (formalin 5 mL, acetic acid 5 mL, and 90 mL of 50% ethanol [21]). Three thin transverse sections were prepared manually from each segment using a sharp razor blade. Each section was placed on a microscope slide and observed immediately under a polarized light microscope at 25 × (Olympus BH2, Japan) and 100 × (Olympus BH41, Japan) magnifications. Images were captured using a high-resolution mobile phone camera (Sony Xperia Z5, Japan) mounted on a stable microscope adapter, ensuring precise alignment, high sharpness, and resolution suitable for quantitative analysis in ImageJ. A stage micrometer (millimeter–micrometer scale) was used for calibration prior to image acquisition to guarantee accurate measurement of anatomical features across all samples. For each section, one image was taken, resulting in three images per stem.

Quantitative traits extracted from stem and vascular bundle cross-sections included size-related parameters (perimeter, area, thickness), shape descriptors (circularity, solidity, Feret diameters, and major/minor axes; for definitions, refer to the ImageJ User Guide), and anatomical proportions (hollow cavity area, ratio of vascular tissue to stem area). Feret diameters represent the maximum and minimum distances between two parallel lines tangential to the object, corresponding to its largest and smallest dimensions. Solidity is defined as the ratio of the object area to the area of its convex hull and reflects the degree of compactness versus internal irregularity or concavity. Major and minor axes represent the lengths of the major and minor ellipses fitted to the object, providing a standardized measure of elongation and asymmetry. Together, these indices provide detailed morphological information relevant to water transport capacity and mechanical support. Measurement of biochemical indices and antioxidant enzyme activity

Mucilage sugars in stems

Immediately after harvest, the stems of cut flowers were separated and weighed with a digital scale accurate to two decimal places. The stems were longitudinally cut with a clean, sharp blade, and the soft tissue was scraped to remove all mucilage, which was collected in a clean container and weighed with the same scale. Mucilage extraction was performed for twelve stems from each genotype. The percentage of mucilage in the stem was calculated by dividing the mucilage weight by the stem weight. Then, 0.5 g of mucilage was diluted with 1.5 mL of ultrapure water (Millipore) and mixed for two minutes. The measurement of mucilage sugars was performed in triplicate. The sugar content was measured via a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system with a refractive index (RI) detector. The diluted samples were filtered through a 0.45-μm filter and injected into the HPLC system with a 20-µl volume. Sugar separation was performed at 65 °C via a Eurokat pb-300 mm × 8 mm Knauer column with deionized water as the mobile phase and a flow rate of 1.8 mL per minute through a Smartline 1000 KNAUER pump. The concentrations of sucrose, glucose, and fructose were calculated via calibration curves of pure standards.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

For the measurement of H2O2 and malondialdehyde (MDA) contents, the corona and petal tissues were carefully separated on Day 0 and Day 3, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. The extraction of H2O2 and MDA was performed with slight modifications to the method described by Zanganeh et al. [22]. A total of 0.1 g of tissue was powdered in liquid nitrogen, and 2 mL of trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added and homogenized. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was used to measure the MDA and H2O2 levels. MDA was measured via the method of Hodges et al. [23], and H2O2 was measured according to Alexieva et al. [24].

Carotenoids, soluble proteins, and antioxidant enzyme activity

To measure total protein and antioxidant enzyme activities, fresh corona and petal tissues were quickly and carefully separated on days 0 and 3. The samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until enzyme assays were performed. Enzyme extraction was carried out with slight modifications to the method described by Sun et al. [25]. In this method, 0.5 g of corona or petal tissue was powdered in liquid nitrogen, and 2 mL of cold 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH = 7) containing 1 mM EDTA and 1% (W/V) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was added and homogenized. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used to determine the enzyme activity and total protein content.

The total protein content and antioxidant enzyme activities of peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) were measured spectrophotometrically. POD activity was measured via guaiacol, SOD activity was assessed via nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), and CAT activity was determined via H2O2 [26]. The total protein content was measured via the Bradford method [27] with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. Corona and petal tissues were carefully separated on the day of flower harvest, frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until carotenoids were measured. The total carotenoids content was measured via DMSO and spectrophotometry [28].

Bacterial culture and enumeration

To assess bacterial colony growth in the vase water, bacterial cultures were prepared following the method of Rabiza-Świder et al. [29] with minor modifications. One mL of the vase solution was spread evenly on the surface of nutrient agar plates and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. After incubation, visible colonies were enumerated manually using the plate count method, expressed as colony-forming units per mL of vase solution (CFU mL⁻1).

Data analysis

The experiment was conducted in a completely randomized design (CRD) with ten Iranian narcissus populations (two species: Narcissus tazetta and N. papyraceus) as treatments. For vase life and anatomical traits, nine stems per population were evaluated (n = 9), while biochemical assays (e.g., enzyme activities) were performed with three biological replicates (n = 3). Data were analyzed using factorial ANOVA, considering population, tissue type (Corona vs. Petal), and time point (Day 0 vs. Day 3) as fixed factors, including their interaction effects. Mean separation was performed with Duncan’s multiple range test at both 1% (p < 0.01) and 5% (p < 0.05) significance levels, as indicated in the results. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 21, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

To complement the univariate analyses and to identify which traits were most strongly associated with vase life, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in XLSTAT. PCA was conducted on the full multivariate dataset, incorporating all observations from the ten narcissus populations. Three independent PCA models were generated to reflect tissue-specific trait sets and to capture their distinct contributions to longevity: (i) petal longevity and associated biochemical variables, (ii) inflorescence longevity and stem anatomical and physiological traits, and (iii) corona longevity and corresponding biochemical variables.

Results

Longevity variation among populations

Significant variation was observed in the longevity of cut flowers among the ten Iranian narcissus populations, evaluated under uniform greenhouse growth and postharvest conditions (Fig. 3). As illustrated in Figs. 4, the Shahla population from Khusf and the Porpar population from Khafr exhibited the longest floral longevity (5.6 and 5.3 days, respectively), whereas the white narcissus (N. papyraceus) from Shiraz had the shortest (2.7 days). Statistical analysis using Duncan’s multiple range test showed that the differences among populations were significant for inflorescence, petal, and corona longevity (p < 0.01). In all populations, the corona consistently exhibited longer longevity than the petals (Fig. 4, A and C). On average, corona longevity was 6.6 days, while petal longevity was 3.2 days. Biochemical and physiological comparison between corona and petal tissues

Significant differences were found between corona and petal tissues in several physiological and biochemical traits during vase life (Fig. 5). On day 0, the total protein content was higher in corona tissue compared to petals. Over the three-day storage period, protein levels decreased significantly in both tissues; however, the decline was more substantial in the corona (about 23.94% compared to 7.48% in petals). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity increased significantly in corona tissue over time (Fig. 5; p < 0.01), whereas in petals, a slight but statistically non-significant decrease was observed, indicating that SOD-mediated antioxidant activity was more strongly sustained in the corona. Peroxidase (POD) activity was consistently higher in petals compared to the corona; however, significant increases were only observed in the corona on day 3. These findings suggest that antioxidant defense mechanisms are more actively regulated in the corona at later stages of vase life.

Lipid peroxidation, as indicated by malondialdehyde (MDA) content, and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) levels were both significantly higher in petals than in corona (Fig. 5), corresponding to higher oxidative stress in petal tissue and indicating more oxidative damage. Relative water content (RWC) declined significantly in both tissues after three days (Fig. 5), but remained higher in the corona. Electrolyte leakage also increased significantly over time in both tissues (Fig. 5). However, no significant differences were observed between corona and petal tissues overall. In corona tissue, the increase from day 0 to day 3 was statistically significant (p < 0.05), reflecting progressive membrane degradation. While fresh weight was higher in petals compared to the corona on day 0, by day 3, the corona showed a greater fresh weight (Fig. 5). Additionally, dry weight was consistently higher in corona tissue than in petals at both time points (Fig. 5), suggesting a denser tissue structure that could reduce water loss and delay senescence.

Anatomical changes in stem and vascular tissues

Microscopic analysis of stem cross-sections revealed notable structural changes during vase life (Figs. 6 and 7). After three days of storage, significant reductions were observed in stem cross-sectional area (average decrease of about 25.52%), solidity, and vascular bundle area (p < 0.01; Fig. 6), indicating dehydration and shrinkage of stem tissues. Other morphometric traits such as stem perimeter, major and minor diameters, and internal thickness also decreased significantly over time (p < 0.05; Fig. 6), suggesting overall tissue collapse and loss of mechanical strength. In line with these structural changes, no evidence of vascular occlusion was detected (Fig. 7, shown for a representative population as all populations exhibited comparable anatomical patterns). Instead, clear signs of cellular collapse were observed in parenchyma tissues surrounding the central stem cavity by day 3 (Fig. 7). These observations suggest that postharvest stem dysfunction in narcissus is associated with progressive tissue degradation and loss of cellular turgor. Although no visible anatomical evidence of vascular occlusion was observed in the examined stem sections, the presence of micro-scale embolisms or submicroscopic blockages may not be excluded and merits further investigation. Multivariate associations between traits and vase life (PCA)

Principal component analysis revealed clear multivariate patterns linking longevity with biochemical, physiological, and anatomical traits.

The corona PCA (Fig. 8C) showed that corona longevity was linked to carotenoid concentration, corona thickness, fresh and dry weight, relative water content on day 3, and catalase activity as well as late-stage SOD activity. Oxidative stress indicators (MDA and H₂O₂) showed contributions in the opposite direction, consistent with their negative relationship with longevity.

Collectively, the PCA results indicate that structural attributes, hydration-related traits, and antioxidant status represent the principal multivariate dimensions underlying variation in the vase life of narcissus flowers, complementing the single-trait analyses presented earlier.

Discussion

The present study provides new insights into the physiological, biochemical, and anatomical determinants of postharvest longevity in cut Iranian narcissus flowers, with emphasis on inter-population variation and tissue-specific senescence dynamics. The marked differences in vase life observed among the ten Iranian populations, despite cultivation under identical greenhouse conditions, indicate substantial phenotypic variation in postharvest performance. Similar variation has been reported in other ornamental species such as cut rose [30], chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium; [31]), and peony (Paeonia suffruticosa; [32]), where vase life disparities are linked to differences in water balance, oxidative status, and structural traits. Such differences in the present study may reflect ecotypic adaptations to diverse native climates of Iranian narcissus populations, which range from the arid desert of Khusf and the warm semi-arid zones of Behbahan, Khafr, and Kazerun to the Mediterranean climates of Shiraz and Abdanan and the humid Caspian environment of Juybar. These contrasting conditions impose different pressures on water-use efficiency and xylem development, potentially shaping postharvest performance. Supporting this, previous studies reported that accessions from Khafr show high drought tolerance, whereas double- and semi-double flowered types are drought-sensitive [33]. Together with evidence from other species that water stress modifies xylem architecture and postharvest water uptake [3], these findings suggest that the climatic diversity of native habitats has contributed to the observed variation in vase life.

Corona–petal functional divergence and delayed senescence

The consistent observation that corona longevity was more than double that of petals underscores a marked tissue-specific senescence pattern. The corona’s higher protein content at harvest, superior water status, and elevated antioxidant enzyme activity (notably SOD) collectively suggest a greater capacity to counteract oxidative damage and sustain metabolic function. Lower MDA and H₂O₂ levels further indicate that lipid membrane integrity is better preserved in this tissue. In addition to enzymatic antioxidants, the corona—particularly in populations with yellow coronas—contained substantially higher carotenoid levels than petals. Carotenoids are known to quench singlet oxygen and neutralize other reactive oxygen species, acting synergistically with antioxidant enzymes to delay senescence [5, 34]. The markedly lower carotenoid concentration observed in the white-flowered genotype coincided with its shorter vase life, suggesting that pigment-based antioxidant capacity may contribute to the extended longevity of the corona. Taken together, these observations — also supported by the PCA results showing that carotenoid content is related with petal and corona longevity — suggest that carotenoid accumulation represent an important biochemical feature associated with extended vase life; however, the variation observed among populations indicates that floral longevity in narcissus is likely governed by a broader network of interacting physiological and genetic factors rather than by only pigment-based antioxidant capacity. Anatomically, the corona in narcissus is structurally distinct from petals: it develops later during floral differentiation and shares similarities with stamens in both tissue organization and ABC gene expression patterns [18, 35]. These developmental and structural features—combined with the greater tissue thickness of the corona compared to petals, as reported in previous observations in Narcissus [28], which results in a lower surface-to-volume ratio—may contribute to enhanced water retention and delayed senescence. Similar relationships between floral tissue thickness and extended longevity have also been noted in other cut flowers [32]. Collectively, these traits highlight the corona as a model for breeding programs aiming to transfer its antioxidant-rich, water-conserving characteristics to more short-lived floral tissues. Future studies incorporating additional sampling points beyond the early vase-life stage, would help to clarify the full temporal kinetics of physiological and biochemical changes associated with senescence, providing a more detailed understanding of how antioxidant activity, water status, and metabolic stability evolve throughout the entire lifespan of narcissus floral coronas.

Stem integrity, hydraulic capacity, and postharvest performance

The anatomical changes show that postharvest hydraulic decline in narcissus stems is related with cell collapse and vascular tissue shrinkage. These observations are triggered by wounding at the cut surface and potential stress from continuous stem base submersion, and they are comparable with vascular occlusion. In addition to these physiological factors, microbial proliferation in the vase solution may also influence stem tissue integrity, although the extent and underlying mechanisms of this interaction should to be clarified.

Although wounding is known to induce the generation of free radicals that can initiate physiological responses leading to vascular blockage [36], no published study has yet clarified the primary cause of the structural stem alterations observed after harvest. This study reveals the first documented evidence of such stem tissue collapse in narcissus. Many studies have attributed postharvest decline in cut flowers to vascular blockage caused by tyloses, gum deposition, or microbial proliferation [2, 3, 29, 36]. However, the microbiological and biochemical analyses of the current research showed that despite a positive relationship between soluble sugar concentrations—particularly sucrose—in stem mucilage and bacterial population density in the vase solution, microscopic examinations provided no visible anatomical evidence of vascular occlusion of any kind, either microbial or structural. The observed positive correlation between sucrose concentration and bacterial population density further supports the interpretation that sugar-rich stem exudates may facilitate microbial growth, which in turn could interact with tissue-level physiological processes contributing to stem degradation. Although these observations were based on light microscopy, the presence of micro-scale processes such as embolism, micro-occlusions, or submicroscopic microbial biofilms cannot be excluded. In line with efforts to mitigate xylem blockage, some studies have reported that specific postharvest treatments—such as gibberellic acid—can reduce vascular occlusion and help maintain xylem integrity [37]. At the same time, several reports—including those on peony and narcissus—have documented the absence of such occlusions [17, 38]. Collectively, these findings suggest that structural degradation and stress-induced cell collapse can be as critical as vascular blockage in limiting postharvest water uptake and longevity. Nevertheless, a reduction in stem vascular area does not necessarily indicate a complete loss of hydraulic function, as a proportion of xylem elements may remain capable of maintaining water transport to the inflorescence and the corona. Even with the observed shrinkage and wrinkling of the stem base tissues, water transport may therefore not be entirely interrupted, allowing continued movement of water toward the coronas, open flowers, and remaining buds within the inflorescence. Future studies should investigate this possibility in greater detail, ideally combining high-resolution imaging techniques with analyses of potential micro-scale vascular obstructions, including bacterial biofilm formation within the xylem.

Multivariate trait patterns underlying vase life variation (PCA)

The PCA results highlighted consistent multivariate patterns showing that longevity in narcissus is shaped collectively by antioxidant capacity, hydration-related traits, and structural integrity. In both petals and coronas, longer longevity clustered with higher carotenoid content, greater tissue thickness, higher fresh and dry biomass, and elevated catalase or late-stage SOD activity—while oxidative stress markers (MDA, H₂O₂) aligned in the opposite direction. These patterns indicate that antioxidant buffering and sufficient structural density help delay senescence, consistent with earlier findings that floral longevity is tightly governed by ROS balance and tissue-level water retention capacity [31, 32].

The inflorescence-level PCA emphasized the central role of stem hydraulic architecture. Longevity aligned strongly with the number of vascular bundles and with geometric descriptors of these bundles—including area, perimeter, Feret and MinFeret diameters, and solidity—which collectively characterize the total conductive capacity of the stem. Stems characterized by a greater number and overall cross-sectional area of vascular bundles exhibited an expanded conductive region, enabling more consistent hydraulic delivery to floral tissues and thereby supporting extended vase life. Also, relative water uptake, flower fresh weight, and stem mucilage sugars showed moderate associations with inflorescence longevity. Flower fresh weight likely reflects higher tissue hydration at harvest as well as greater initial biomass and carbohydrate reserves [3]. In parallel, mucilage sugars in the stem may contribute to osmotic driving force for water absorption [17], potentially supporting water flow through the conductive tissues. These relationships suggest that, beyond vascular-bundle architecture, hydration-related physiological traits also play a complementary role in determining postharvest longevity by influencing the stem’s ability to acquire and retain water. Although previous studies in cut flowers have addressed xylem blockage, bacterial proliferation, and general anatomical features affecting water transport [3, 32], detailed quantification of vascular-bundle geometry—and its changes during early postharvest stages—has rarely been documented.

Together, these multivariate patterns demonstrate that vase life in narcissus is governed not by isolated traits but by coordinated biochemical and structural mechanisms that jointly reduce oxidative stress, maintain hydration, and reinforce tissue stability.

Conclusions

This study provides the first integrative assessment of biochemical, physiological, and anatomical factors underlying postharvest longevity in Iranian narcissus populations, revealing substantial inter-population variation and pronounced tissue-level differences in senescence dynamics. The corona exhibited markedly greater resilience than petals, supported by stronger antioxidant activity, higher carotenoid content, and structurally denser tissue. Stem analyses indicate that postharvest hydraulic decline is closely associated with cell collapse and vascular tissue shrinkage, suggesting that cellular structural failure may contribute to reduced water uptake at a level comparable to that reported for vascular occlusion in cut flowers. Principal component analysis further showed that longevity is associated with a coordinated suite of traits—including antioxidant capacity, hydration status, and vascular-bundle architecture—highlighting the multivariate nature of postharvest performance. Collectively, these findings provide a clearer understanding of the physiological and structural determinants of vase life, offering practical opportunities for improving postharvest handling and informing breeding programs aimed at enhancing longevity in narcissus and other cut flowers.

Implications and future directions

The findings of this study highlight the value of an integrative approach for understanding the determinants of postharvest longevity in narcissus, demonstrating that vase life is shaped by the coordinated action of biochemical, physiological, and anatomical traits rather than by any single factor. The marked divergence in senescence behavior between the corona and petals suggests clear opportunities for leveraging tissue-specific characteristics—such as the corona’s higher antioxidant capacity, greater structural density, and superior water-retention ability—in breeding or management strategies aimed at prolonging floral longevity. At the stem level, the discovery that postharvest hydraulic decline is driven primarily by cellular collapse and vascular-tissue shrinkage, rather than classical vascular occlusion, reframes current assumptions and emphasizes the need for approaches that preserve structural integrity and prevent tissue dehydration at the cut surface. The PCA results further underscore the utility of multivariate frameworks for capturing how multiple traits interact to influence vase life, reinforcing the complexity and interconnectedness of the underlying mechanisms. Future research should focus on elucidating the genetic basis of these multivariate trait networks, generating time-resolved anatomical datasets to monitor postharvest changes in vascular-bundle geometry more precisely, and evaluating targeted postharvest treatments—such as osmotic agents, structural stabilizers, or antioxidant formulations—to mitigate stem collapse and extend longevity in narcissus. Additionally, future studies should evaluate stem structural responses under sterile or antimicrobial conditions to determine whether tissue collapse is primarily driven by intrinsic physiological processes or is influenced by microbial activity at the cut stem base.
