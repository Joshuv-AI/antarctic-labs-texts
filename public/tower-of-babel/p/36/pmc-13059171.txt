Plasma NGAL-detected kidney injury following paediatric spine surgery: role of intraoperative hypotension
Authors: Seymanur Altintas, Gulsen Bosna, Osman Ekinci, Kubra Bozali, Eray Metin Guler, Hakan Serhat Yanik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-operative acute kidney injury (AKI) remains a major complication in paediatric surgery. Serum creatinine is an insensitive marker, that increases only after significant impairment. Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) has emerged as an early biomarker capable of detecting tubular injury before functional decline. This study aimed to determine the incidence of clinical AKI and subclinical AKI in paediatric spine surgery using plasma NGAL levels and to evaluate the associations of intraoperative hypotension with kidney injury.MethodsIn this prospective single-centre study, 66 children (mean age 15.2 ± 1.7 years) who underwent elective posterior spinal instrumentation were enrolled. Plasma NGAL was measured after induction (T1, baseline), and subsequently at 6 and 24 h after induction (T2 and T3, respectively). Clinical AKI was defined by the KDIGO criteria. ROC analysis was used to determine NGAL cut-off values; patients with NGAL above the the cut-off but without KDIGO-AKI were classified as having subclinical AKI. Potential risk factors, particularly intraoperative hypotension, were analysed.ResultsClinical AKI occurred in 12% of patients. The NGAL-T2 cut-off for AKI was 86 ng/mL (AUC = 0.817; sensitivity = 100%; specificity = 63.8%). Twenty-nine patients exceeded this cut off; 8 developed clinical AKI and 21 (31.8%) had subclinical AKI. In multivariable analyses, intraoperative hypotension exposure remained independently associated with kidney injury. Durations of MAP < 70 and < 65 mmHg were significantly longer in the clinical AKI and subclinical AKI groups than in the non-AKI group, whereas time spent below MAP < 60 mmHg was significantly prolonged only in the clinical AKI group compared with both the subclinical AKI and non-AKI groups.ConclusionsAlthough 12% of the children developed clinical AKI, approximately one-third had subclinical AKI detectable only by NGAL. Plasma NGAL at the 6th hour demonstrated excellent sensitivity for predicting AKI, with a threshold of 86 ng/mL. Intraoperative hypotension was a major predictor, with a MAP < 70 mmHg linked to tubular damage whereas deeper hypotension (MAP < 60 mmHg) was linked to clinical AKI. This study is the first to define NGAL cut-off values and intraoperative MAP thresholds for postoperative AKI in paediatric non-cardiac surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04861-w.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, NGAL, Paediatric, Spine surgery, Intraoperative hypotension

Introduction

Postoperative acute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent and clinically significant complication following major surgical procedures, and it is strongly associated with increased morbidity and mortality [1]. In surgical patients, AKI may develop due to several factors, including the effects of anaesthetic agents, exposure to nephrotoxic drugs, haemodynamic alterations such as hypotension leading to reduced cardiac output, hormonal and immune responses triggered by surgical stress, embolic events, blood loss, hemodilution, and the administration of intraoperative vasoactive medications [2]. In spine surgery, additional risks arise from prone positioning, which increases intra-abdominal pressure (IAP), thereby exacerbating perioperative blood loss, impairing visceral organ perfusion, and reducing both renal microvascular and macrovascular blood flow, ultimately resulting in acute renal dysfunction [3]. Extensive studies in adults undergoing both cardiac and non-cardiac surgery have documented the incidence, risk factors, and adverse outcomes of postoperative AKI [3]. In contrast, although numerous investigations have addressed AKI following paediatric cardiac surgery, the epidemiology of AKI after non-cardiac procedures in children remains insufficiently explored [4].

According to current guidelines, AKI is diagnosed by either an elevation in the serum creatinine concentration or a decline in urine output [5, 6]. However, serum creatinine levels typically rise only after a reduction of at least 50% in the glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and this increase generally becomes evident 24–48 h after kidney injury has occurred [5, 6]. Moreover, serum creatinine is influenced by several factors, including age, muscle mass, nutritional status, and hydration [7]. Even in otherwise healthy children, low creatinine values are common, and creatinine levels may be further reduced in conditions such as malnutrition, fluid overload, or neuromuscular disorders [5]. Consequently, serum creatinine is not considered a reliable biomarker for AKI diagnosis, particularly in the paediatric population [8]. To facilitate the earlier detection of kidney injury and the timely initiation of appropriate interventions, including during the perioperative period, novel and more specific biomarkers have been proposed for the assessment of AKI [5, 8]. Among these, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) increases within approximately two hours of kidney injury and peaks at approximately six hours [7]. NGAL facilitates the recognition of tubular injury prior to the occurrence of a decline in renal function, thereby enabling early therapeutic interventions. The condition in which tubular damage is present, despite normal creatinine and urine output, is referred to as subclinical AKI. Importantly, subclinical AKI has been reported to be associated with adverse outcomes similar to those observed in clinical AKI [9–11].

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the ability of plasma NGAL levels to detect clinical AKI and subclinical AKI in paediatric patients undergoing spine surgery. As a secondary objective, this study sought to identify preoperative and intraoperative risk factors, particularly intraoperative hypotension, that may contribute to the development of both clinical and subclinical AKI.

Methods

Study design

This prospective single-centre observational study was conducted at the University of Health Sciences, Istanbul Haydarpasa Numune Training and Research Hospital between March 2023 and March 2024. Sixty-six paediatric patients (aged 10–17 years) scheduled for elective posterior spinal instrumentation under general anaesthesia were included.

Patients with immunosuppression, pre-existing renal, hepatic, or cardiac failure; severe respiratory insufficiency or bleeding diathesis; or an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status score higher than II were excluded. Patients who refused participation or for whom parental/legal guardian consent was not obtained were also excluded.

Definitions of AKI

Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 criteria using both serum creatinine (SCr) and urine output. Baseline SCr was defined as the most recent preoperative measurement obtained while the patient was clinically stable, within 24–48 h prior to surgery and SCr was monitored for 72 h after surgery. Urine output was quantitatively recorded during the first 24 h (0–6, 0–12, and 0–24 h cumulative volumes converted to mL/kg/h), Clinical AKI was diagnosed if KDIGO criteria were met at any time within these intervals. As per institutional policy aimed at early mobilization and reducing catheter-associated urinary tract infections, urinary catheters were routinely removed after 24 h; therefore, urine output monitoring was prespecified and intentionally limited to the first 24 postoperative period, which aligns with the study’s focus on early perioperative renal effects.

Intraoperative hypotension

Controlled hypotension was applied during paraspinal muscle dissection, screw and rod placement, and osteotomy stages to reduce blood loss and improve surgical field visibility. Systolic blood pressure was maintained below 90 mmHg, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) below 70 mmHg. was maintained using total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) with propofol (80–120 µg/kg/min) and remifentanil (0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min). When blood pressure exceeded the target range, TIVA infusion rates were titrated as the initial intervention. If the target range could not be achieved, intravenous nitroglycerin was administered (5–50 µg/min). In selected cases, when blood pressure remained above target despite nitroglycerin, esmolol was administered as a 500 µg/kg loading dose over 1 min followed by a continuous infusion of 50–150 µg/kg/min. The protocol was terminated after instrumentation was completed and final neuromonitoring signals were confirmed. Blood pressure was continuously monitored via invasive intra-arterial cannulation. Intraoperative hypotension was defined as a MAP below thresholds of 70, 65, and 60 mmHg for ≥ 5 min. The cumulative durations during which the MAP remained below 70, 65, and 60 mmHg were recorded separately.

Data collection

Preoperative demographic/clinical characteristics and baseline laboratory values were collected. Intraoperative data included duration of surgery, estimated blood loss, volumes of fluids and blood product transfusions, urine output, and cumulative time spent with MAP < 70 mmHg. Plasma NGAL levels were obtained at predefined time points (T1–T3). Postoperative laboratory assessment included renal function tests and routine blood parameters. According to the institutional postoperative care pathway, patients were routinely followed in the orthopaedic ward after surgery, with close clinical assessment, haemodynamic follow-up, and urine output monitoring as per protocol. Exposure to potentially nefrotoxic agents and the need for postoperative respiratory or haemodynamic support (mechanical ventilation and vasopressor/inotrope use) were captured. Postoperative outcomes included postoperative complications, hospital length of stay, 30-day readmission, and 3-month morbidity and mortality were also documented.

NGAL measurement

In addition to standard perioperative assessments, venous blood samples (4 mL) were collected at three predefined time points: immediately after anaesthesia induction (T1, baseline), at 6 h after induction (T2), and at 24 h after induction (T3). Samples were centrifuged, and plasma aliquots were transferred into Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Plasma NGAL concentrations were measured using a commercially available Human NGAL ELISA kit (Atlas Biotechnology Lab Materials; supplier: Oto Kir Med, Ankara, Türkiye). All assays were performed at the Medical Biochemistry R&D Laboratory, Hamidiye Faculty of Medicine, University of Health Sciences.

Sample size calculation

An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power, based on an expected AKI incidence of 17% (35/208) reported by Jobsis et al. At a 95% confidence level and α = 0.05, and considering the finite annual number of paediatric spine surgeries at our institution (approximately N = 70), the minimum required sample size was calculated as 54 patients.

Statistical analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to identify the threshold value of NGAL for predicting AKI according to the KDIGO criteria. Patients below this threshold were classified as NGAL−, and those above it were classified as NGAL+. Patients meeting KDIGO criteria were classified as clinical AKI (c-AKI), whereas patients with NGAL elevation above the cut-off without KDIGO criteria were defined as having subclinical AKI (sub-AKI). Accordingly, patients were divided into three groups: c-AKI, sub-AKI, and non-AKI (NGAL−). A series of comparisons were conducted among the three groups via one-way ANOVA for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-normally distributed variables. If overall significance was detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. Serial changes in plasma NGAL levels across time points (T1–T3) were analysed using repeated-measures ANOVA, and violations of sphericity were addressed using the Greenhouse–Geisser correction. To account for potential confounding, logistic regression analyses were performed. The primary outcome was clinical AKI. Given the limited number of events, multivariable analyses were conducted using Firth’s penalised logistic regression with parsimonious models to reduce small-sample bias. As a sensitivity analysis, predictors of any AKI phenotype (clinical AKI plus subclinical AKI) were additionally evaluated and compared with patients without AKI. All the statistical analyses were performed via SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

A total of 66 patients were included in the study, 57.6% of whom were female and of whom 42.4% were male. The mean age of the cohort was 15.2 ± 1.7 years. According to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, 53% of patients were ASA I and 47% were ASA II. The mean duration of surgery was 263 min, with an average intraoperative blood loss of 536.82 ± 32 mL. Clinical AKI was detected in 8 patients (12.1%); all cases were classified as KDIGO stage 1 and were diagnosed exclusively based on urine output criteria, as no patient met the serum creatinine-based KDIGO AKI criteria during the 72-hour follow-up.

A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the threshold NGAL values predictive of AKI. The optimal NGAL cutoff at T2 (6th hour) was 86 ng/mL (p = 0.004; AUC = 0.817, 95% CI 0.694–0.940; sensitivity = 100%, specificity = 63.8%), as shown in Fig. 1. Using this cutoff, the positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were 27.6% and 100%, respectively (true positive = 8, false positive = 21, true negative = 37, false negative = 0). At T3 (24th hour), the optimal cutoff was 81.73 ng/mL (p = 0.031; AUC = 0.737, 95% CI 0.540–0.934; sensitivity = 75%, specificity = 65.5%), as shown in Fig. 2.

Using this cutoff, the PPV and NPV were approximately 23.1% and 95.0%, respectively (TP = 6, FP = 20, TN = 38, FN = 2).

The NGAL threshold value predictive of AKI with the highest sensitivity and specificity was 86 ng/mL. Among the 29 patients with NGAL-T2 levels above this threshold, 8 developed c-AKI and 21 (31.8%) were classified as having subclinical AKI. The remaining 37 patients with NGAL-T2 levels below than 86 ng/mL were classified as non-AKI, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Patient classification based on NGAL-T2 levels and AKI status

Plasma NGAL levels increased postoperatively and peaked at 6 h (T2) in both the clinical AKI and subclinical AKI groups (c-AKI: 148.4→203.4→179.9 ng/mL; sub-AKI: 123.7→174.7→144.5 ng/mL), whereas the non-AKI group showed consistently low and stable values (69.8→70.4→71.8 ng/mL). NGAL levels varied significantly over time (p = 0.010) and differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001), with both AKI-related groups exhibiting higher values than the non-AKI group at all time points (T1–T3) (p < 0.05). Despite these significant time and group effects, the non-significant time × group interaction indicates that patients with clinical AKI and subclinical AKI exhibited consistently elevated NGAL levels across all time points. However, the trajectories of increase and subsequent decline over time did not differ statistically between groups, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

A range of variables—including age, sex, body weight, comorbidities, perioperative medication exposure, ASA class, preoperative anaemia, operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion requirement, and perioperative fluid administration—were evaluated for potential associations with kidney injury. Discussion

In the present study, the incidence of clinical AKI following paediatric posterior spinal instrumentation surgery was 12% (8/66). The incidence of postoperative AKI varies depending on the patient population and the type of surgery performed. AKI has been reported in 5–43% of patients after paediatric cardiac surgery [12], a considerably higher rate that is largely attributed to the immaturity of the renal parenchyma in patients younger than two years, the presence of congenital anomalies with low cardiac output, major procedures associated with severe blood loss, and the frequent use of cardiopulmonary bypass.

Two recent large cohort studies investigating AKI after non-cardiac paediatric surgery have reported different incidences. Schacham et al. reported AKI in 11% of patients under 18 years of age who underwent undergoing surgery [13], whereas Wingert et al. reported an incidence of 3.2% in their cohort [14], which included both surgical and procedural cases. In the latter study, only 70% of patients underwent general anaesthesia, and the inclusion of short, low-risk procedures and predominantly ASA I–II patients may have contributed to the lower incidence of AKI [13, 14]. Furthermore, both studies defined AKI solely on the basis of serum creatinine, without including urine output criteria, which may have resulted in an underestimation of the true incidence. In children, creatinine-based AKI accounts for only approximately two-thirds of all cases, and the addition of oliguria criteria is generally considered to be more reliable [15].

In terms of surgical procedures and patient populations, the study most similar to this one is a retrospective analysis of 208 children who underwent spinal instrumentation, which reported an AKI incidence of 17% on the basis of urine output criteria and increased serum creatinine was observed in only two of these patients. The absence of creatinine increases, and even decreases in some cases postoperatively, which is attributed to hemodilution resulting from a cumulative positive fluid balance during the intraoperative and early postoperative periods [4]. Similarly, in our study, none of the patients exhibited creatinine elevations sufficient to meet the AKI criteria, and AKI classification was driven by urine output criteria. In addition to haemodilution, relatively preserved renal function reserve in paediatric patients may cause transient decreases in renal perfusion, which may initially present as oliguria without an immediately measurable decrease in GFR or a detectable increase in SCr. Furthermore, while BMI values in our study were not low, BMI does not directly reflect muscle mass. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that reduced creatinine production due to low muscle mass limits the diagnostic utility of serum creatinine, particularly in children with spinal deformities and associated comorbidities. Given the limitations of serum creatinine in this population, the use of biomarkers such as NGAL has been recommended for evaluating AKI following orthopedic surgery [4]. NGAL has demonstrated superior predictive performance for AKI in paediatric patients compared with adults, particularly in the context of cardiac surgery [16]. However, normal ranges and definitive cut-off values for NGAL in children have not yet been established [17]. For instance, in children aged 2–6.5 years undergoing cardiac surgery, a plasma NGAL cut-off of 100 ng/mL has been identified as a predictor of AKI [18]. In our study, ROC analysis of NGAL levels indicated that a cut-off value of 86 ng/mL at T2 provided the best predictive accuracy for AKI (AUC = 0.817; sensitivity = 100%; specificity = 63.8%). Twenty-one patients with NGAL levels above 86 ng/mL (NGAL+) and tubular damage despite no functional kidney damage according to the KDIGO criteria were categorized as having subclinical AKI.

In univariable analyses, higher ASA physical status and greater intraoperative blood loss were associated with clinical AKI. However, in multivariable parsimonious Firth penalised logistic regression models constructed to account for the limited number of events, cumulative intraoperative hypotension exposure remained the main independent predictor of clinical AKI, whereas blood loss was not independently associated after adjustment. This pattern suggests that the observed unadjusted relationship between blood loss and AKI may reflect haemodynamic instability and a higher hypotension burden rather than a direct effect of bleeding itself. The strength of the hypotension–AKI association increased at lower MAP thresholds, with the strongest effect observed for cumulative exposure below MAP < 60 mmHg. This finding highlights the clinical relevance of more profound intraoperative hypotension in this surgical population.

Consistent with these multivariable findings, the mean duration below MAP 60 mmHg in patients with clinical AKI was nearly three times longer than that in patients with subclinical AKI and approximately four times longer than that in non-AKI patients (26.25 min, 9.05 min and 7.03 min, respectively). While the definition and clinical consequences of intraoperative hypotension in paediatric patients remain unclear [19], our findings suggest that prolonged exposure to a MAP below 70 mmHg contributes to tubular injury, increasing the risk of both clinical AKI and subclinical AKI, whereas cumulative exposure to a MAP below 60 mmHg serves as an independent risk factor specifically for the development of clinical AKI.

Controlled hypotension is frequently employed during spinal surgery to reduce blood loss and improve surgical visualization. However, our findings highlight the need to balance these benefits against potential renal risks. Renal autoregulation maintains relatively stable renal blood flow across a mean arterial pressure (MAP) range of approximately 80–180 mmHg; reductions below this threshold may compromise renal perfusion [20]. While moderate reductions in MAP may facilitate operative conditions, prolonged exposure to MAP below 70 mmHg was associated with tubular injury, and deeper hypotension (MAP < 60 mmHg) independently predicted clinical AKI. These findings suggest that hypotensive techniques should be individualized and limited in duration, with careful haemodynamic monitoring to avoid excessive or prolonged reductions in renal perfusion. Maintaining MAP near the lower limit of autoregulation and promptly correcting sustained hypotension may help optimize both surgical conditions and renal safety.

Persistent and severe intraoperative hypotension plays a key role in the pathophysiology of postoperative AKI by impairing renal autoregulation and inducing ischemia–reperfusion injury [21, 22]. In a study by Kheterpal et al., patients who developed AKI after non-cardiac surgery experienced significantly longer durations of MAP below 60 mmHg during the perioperative period [22]. Similarly, the 2019 PeriOperative Quality Initiative (POQI) consensus statement reported that even brief durations of MAP below 60–70 mmHg during non-cardiac surgery are associated with myocardial injury, AKI, and mortality [23]. However, paediatric data remain scarce. Schacham et al. reported no association between IOH and AKI in children undergoing non-cardiac procedures [13], and Wingert et al. likewise did not demonstrate such a relationship [14]. Both studies were retrospective and relied primarily on non-invasive blood pressure measurements.

Consequently, retrospective designs are inadequate to reliably establish IOH as a causal factor for postoperative AKI in children [14]. Furthermore, the absence of a standardized intraoperative hypotension threshold in paediatric patients contributes to heterogeneity across studies.

Clinical implications

Routine creatinine monitoring may underestimate perioperative kidney injury in children. The NGAL threshold identified in this adolescent cohort represents the first biomarker-based cutoff reported for paediatric patients undergoing spinal surgery.This study is also the first in paediatric non-cardiac surgery to establish intraoperative MAP values associated with AKI, including subclinical forms.The incorporation of NGAL measurements has the potential to enhance the early detection of AKI and to guide intraoperative blood pressure management.

Limitations

This was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. As there is no universally accepted definition of intraoperative hypotension in children, multiple MAP thresholds were analyzed and cumulative durations were used. This approach limits the evaluation of the potential impact of short hypotensive episodes.

Conclusion

Paediatric posterior spinal instrumentation is associated with a measurable risk of kidney injury. Although only 12% developed clinical AKI, approximately one-third of patients experienced subclinical tubular injury detectable only by NGAL. Plasma NGAL measured 6 h after induction, with a threshold of 86 ng/mL, provided excellent sensitivity for early detection. Avoiding prolonged intraoperative hypotension—particularly MAP values below 70 mmHg and especially below 60 mmHg —may mitigate the risk of AKI. These results emphasize the critical role of intraoperative hemodynamics in the development of AKI and support the integration of age-specific NGAL thresholds for early detection. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.
