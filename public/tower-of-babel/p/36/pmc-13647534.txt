Prediction of the secretome of Microbotryum intermedium, as well as candidate fungal effectors
Authors: Roxanne K Hayes, Michael H Perlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSecreted proteins are known to be an important virulence factor in the successful invasion and colonization of the host by fungi, especially for biotrophic parasites/pathogens of plants. To predict protein secretion, a genome must be evaluated by software that interrogates the genome for conserved sequences characteristic of known secreted proteins.ObjectivesThis work sought to identify putative secreted proteins of Microbotryum intermedium, a smut fungus found on plants of the Scabiosa family. To accomplish this, we used a pipeline of computational tools to serve as an efficient means of identifying potential targets as fungal effectors that can later be evaluated experimentally. So, this initial identification is ideally the first step in a larger investigation of the role of protein secretion in the development and progression of disease.DescriptionA pipeline of computational tools was used to stringently predict secreted proteins for Microbotryum intermedium, The pipeline was used to predict the canonical secretome of this fungus. The annotated M. intermedium genome (mycocosm.jgi.doe.gov/Micin1/Micin1.home.html) has 8,148 predicted genes, of which, only 296 were classified as putative secreted proteins using the stringent pipeline for canonical secretion prediction. These data inform future functional analyses that test candidates for their roles in pathogenicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12863-026-01469-9.Keywords: Smut fungi, Fungal effectors, Secretome, Plant pathogenesis

Objectives

The goal of this study was to predict the secretome for Microbotryum intermedium smut fungi. At the time this work began only one Microbotryum species had a published secretome [1]. Subsequently, interspecies and intraspecies comparisons were made with two additional species [2]. Here we focused on M. intermedium, whose annotated genome (mycocosm.jgi.doe.gov/Micin1/Micin1.home.html) could serve as proof-of-principle for additional Microbotryum species infecting Dianthus hosts. In these analyses, we updated and modified an established pipeline [1] to provide a conservative estimate of canonically-secreted proteins, i.e., those with clear secretion signal peptides located at their N-terminal region.

Data description

The collection of secreted proteins that is unique to a species of fungus is termed its fungal secretome [3, 4]. Such secreted proteins encoded within the fungal genome are comprised of both enzymatic proteins and small secreted proteins (SSPs), both of which have specialized and often unique functions [4]. Signal peptides harbor a secretion domain but lack a transmembrane domain that would typically anchor the protein to the cell wall, an important characteristic that allows them to be secreted during protein synthesis [4]. The pipeline used here for prediction of proteins with signal peptide sequences was chosen for its stringent assessment of secretion likelihood. Its combination of successive computational programs provided a conservative estimate of all such secreted proteins, as well as an opportunity to further interrogate their likelihood as potential fungal effector proteins from the final list of small-secreted, hypothetical proteins [5], through the addition here of Effector P. The pipeline involves an iterative passing of the predicted proteome through several programs [1] that detect conserved motifs to assess likely secretion or not based on established cutoff criteria. The most updated versions of programs from the original pipeline were used, as well as that for EffectorP. Thus, for this updated pipeline, the following programs were used in this order (Supplementary Fig. 1). Numbers of predicted proteins retained at each stage for each program is indicated in the schematic. TMHMM (version 2.0) is a program that was developed to detect transmembrane helices within a protein sequence. Transmembrane helices anchor a protein into the plasma membrane and they can serve a function across the membrane, on the intercellular portion of the membrane or the extracellular portion of the membrane [6]. For this project, we used a score of < 2 transmembrane helix predictions to retain protein sequences in the pool of potential secreted proteins. Anything more than 2 TMs indicated the protein was likely to be retained in the membrane without any external potentially functional portion. Next, Phobius v1.01 (web portal) was used. Phobius was developed to address some of the shortcomings of the existing programs that identify secreted proteins by looking at singular features such as transmembrane segments or signal peptides. Instead, Phobius forces the choice between the two sequence features so that more potential secreted proteins are captured [7]. For this program, we used the designation of secreted protein (SP) to retain protein sequences in the potential secreted proteins pool for this analysis; a sequence was retained if it had less than 2 transmembrane helices. PredGPI v 1.0 (web portal) is a program that detects the presence of GPI anchoring positions within a given sequence that would indicate that the protein is bound in the plasma membrane and secretion from the cell following post-translational modification in the endoplasmic reticulum [8]. For this program, a sequence was retained if the specificity score was less than 99.5%, where 99.0% − 99.5% indicates low probability for GPI linkage. NucPred (web portal) is a tool that predicts the likelihood of a protein, based on sequence and residue information, spending time in the nucleus [9]. The cutoff score of 0.8 was used in this research to determine if the protein sequence in question was likely to be released into the nucleus. TargetP v1.1 probes protein sequences for N-terminal signals that signal the following localizations: signal peptide (SP), mitochondrial transit peptide (mTP), chloroplast transit peptide (cTP), or thylakoid luminal transit peptide (ITP) [10]. For this work, only protein sequences that were assigned to contain a signal peptide were kept in the potential pool of secreted proteins. SignalP was originally developed to distinguish signal peptides that translocate proteins across the endoplasmic reticulum in eukaryotes so that they may be later secreted from the cell. We used the version SignalP 5.0; despite several updates, even the earlier SignalP 3.0 was independently evaluated as one of the most reliable signal peptide prediction tools in use [11, 12]. Sequences were retained if the predicted localization designation was “SP”. WoLF PSORT (web portal, accessed January 21, 2021)) was developed to identify features of a protein that can be exploited to determine where that protein will be localized and will identify the subcellular location specifically [13]. In this work, the parameter for organism type was “fungi” and proteins that were identified under the “EXTR” localization were used as potential secreted proteins as they were flagged for extracellular localization.

Sequences were iteratively removed from the pool of potentially secreted proteins as they failed to pass successive cutoff criteria until the end of the pipeline. Within biotrophic fungal species, SSPs that play a role in host colonization (i.e., effectors) fulfill four important functions: they enable the fungus to evade the host immune response, they modify host characteristics to suit the infection process/lifecycle, they fine-tune host signaling pathways to facilitate successful infection, and they change host enzymatic processes [4, 14]. Fungal effectors tend to range in size from 200 to 300 amino acids [4, 15, 16], though this is not an absolute size cutoff. Of the 8,148 predicted proteins in M. intermedium, only 296 were classified as putative secreted proteins after WoLFPSORT (see Table 1, Data Set 1 [17]. Of the 296 putative secreted proteins, 94 were predicted by Phobius (Table 1, Data set 2 [18] to be SSPs based on a size cutoff of 250 amino acids, a cutoff we have used previously [1]. It is standard in the fungal research community to consider SSPs as being between 200 and 300 amino acids [4, 15, 16] and we have previously used the 250 amino acid cutoff for another Microbotryum species, M. lychnidis dioicae in [1], as well as [2], which examined multiple Microbotryum spp. Out of 94 SSPs, 69 proteins [19] had no ascribed function, were labeled “hypothetical proteins”, and were, thus, candidate genes for further study to determine their potential role in the infection process. This set of proteins was again analyzed by TMHMM to remove any proteins containing 1 or 2 predicted transmembrane domains (Data set 4) [20]. As the final pass, EffectorP 3.0 was used to assess the likelihood each protein fits the criteria of a fungal effector. The authors of this program, one of the latest in determining fungal effectors specifically, use a machine learning approach to probing known effector sets for conserved sequence motifs that aid in identifying effector proteins. In training their model, the authors found that the most discriminative features of fungal effectors are protein size, protein net charge, and the percentage of cysteines, serines, and tryptophans in the protein sequence [5]. Several of these effector features, small protein size and the presence of cysteines, have been previously described but the remaining features described above are new criteria discovered by the machine learning models developed in the paper by Sperschneider et al. [5]. When EffectorP is run (on the FASTA input file), it will score each sequence as either a cytoplasmic effector, an apoplastic effector, both, or a non-effector. We included those that were labeled as an apoplastic effector or both (cytoplasmic and apoplastic). After passing the 53 putative hypothetical secreted proteins lacking any transmbembrane domains through EffectorP, 33 proteins remained (Table 1, Data sets 5 and 6 [19, 20]). No strong evidence for clustering of effector genes was observed in the genome of Microbotryum intermedium, in contrast to the trend seen in the distribution of effector genes within some smut fungal genomes [21–23]. From a total of 33 putative effector proteins retained after EffectorP analysis, some were within close proximity to one another (e.g., BQ2448_5553, BQ2448_5554 and BQ2448_5555 were each within approximately 2 kb of each other in the genome). The proteins in this group are similar in size and match similar hits in blastp searches; thus, they may be paralogues. However, further investigation of genomic locations for all predicted SSPs will be necessary to assess whether any additional paralogues of effectors in close proximity can be identified for this genome.

Limitations

We began this study repeating the pipeline used in [1], with the most updated versions of programs available. However, we realized after Phobius analysis, that the original pipeline used parameters for TMHMM that did not filter out proteins with 1 or 2 predicted transmembrane domains. Presumably, this was to allow inclusion of proteins that might be embedded in the membrane but contain an external portion, However, as the current study was meant to be as conservative as possible in the final set of predicted effectors for further study, here we decided to run TMHMM on the hypothetical proteins retained after the last Phobius step. In so doing, 16 proteins were indeed found to contain predicted transmembrane domains. With all of these removed from further consideration, the addition of the EffectorP 3.0 analysis left 33 putative effectors for further functional analyses. Thus, for studies where more conservative prediction of SSPs as possible effectors, we recommend using a cutoff of 0 for the number of predicted transmembrane domains when performing the initial TMHMM analysis in the pipeline. This would eliminate the need for another TMHMM round prior to use of EffectorP. A further caveat is that additional support for a role of predicted SSPs as effectors must include comparative transcriptome data for in vitro forms of M. intermedium versus expression during host plant infection stages, as well as functional analysis of suspected effectors. Moreover, the present analysis ignores possible additional effectors secreted by non-canonical pathways [24]. While it is important to recognize the role of non-canonically secreted proteins in the infection process, SecretomeP may not be the ideal tool for this project. Other authors have called the reliability of this tool into question [25]. Moreover, agreement between different programs for effector prediction can vary [26]. The paper would gain from the use of additional programs predicting effectors, e.g., Fungtion [27], Predector [28], EffHunter [29], Effector-GAN [30]. However, not all of these are readily available or require large computer memory and EffHunter actually incorporates all of the steps used in the current study, so would be unlikely to add to the findings here.
