Targeting HSP90 in cancer: advances in the development of inhibitors, mechanisms of action, and therapeutic applications
Authors: Xueyi Wang, Ruiqing Ni, Xiaohui Wang, Xun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) serves as a central orchestrator of oncogenic protein homeostasis, empowering tumor progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance. However, the clinical translation of HSP90 inhibition has been hampered by challenges such as intrinsic and acquired resistance, a lack of reliable biomarkers, and the toxicity associated with pan-inhibitors. This review comprehensively dissects these challenges and presents the evolving landscape of strategies to overcome them. We systematically catalog the development of over 20 representative HSP90 inhibitors, from classical N-terminal ATP-competitive agents to novel C-terminal and middle-domain binders, highlighting their distinct mechanisms and the rise of subtype-selective compounds designed to enhance therapeutic windows. Beyond monotherapy, we emphasize the paradigm shift towards combination regimens, detailing synergistic effects with chemotherapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapies that resensitize tumors to treatment. Critically, we explore the diagnostic frontier, reviewing how radiolabeled HSP90 inhibitors (e.g., [¹²⁴I]PU-H71 and [¹⁸F]San A derivatives) enable non-invasive tumor detection and patient stratification through positron emission tomography (PET). Finally, we chart the future course of the field, underscoring how precision medicine approaches—guided by biomarker identification and liquid biopsy—coupled with nanotechnology-driven delivery systems, are poised to unlock the full potential of HSP90-targeted cancer theranostics. This synthesis provides not only a mechanistic overview but also a strategic roadmap for the next generation of HSP90-directed oncology research.Keywords: Heat shock protein 90, HSP90 inhibitors, Cancer, Targeted therapy, Selective inhibitors, Combination therapy, Tumor resistance

Introduction

HSP90 is a highly conserved molecular chaperone protein that plays a crucial role in maintaining protein homeostasis [1]. However, in tumor cells, the expression level of HSP90 is significantly upregulated, which can be several times higher than that in normal cells, and its activity is also markedly enhanced. This phenomenon is closely related to the occurrence, development, metastasis, and drug resistance of tumors [2].

HSP90 acts as a central component of chaperone, a network of chaperone proteins, co-chaperones, and regulatory factors that collectively support the proteostasis and survival of malignant cells. By stabilizing numerous oncogenic client proteins, such as B-cell receptor (BCR)-Abelson murine leukemia viral oncogene homolog (ABL), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), as well as key signaling molecules such as kinases and transcription factors, HSP90 functions as an “oncoprotein enabler” in cancer biology [3].

The development of precision medicine has brought new opportunities to the clinical application of HSP90 inhibitors. Precision-medicine-based individualized treatment will become an important direction in future research and the application of HSP90 inhibitors [4].

Importantly, HSP90 interacts with multiple oncogenic signaling pathways. These kinases include receptor tyrosine kinases (e.g., epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), HER2, mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET)), serine/threonine kinases (e.g., protein kinase B (AKT), rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF), etc.), and transcription factors (e.g., hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α)), thereby facilitating tumor progression through enhanced proliferation, survival, migration, and angiogenesis [3]. HSP90 also contributes significantly to the multistep process of tumor metastasis by promoting epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), basement membrane invasion, and colonization at distant sites [5].

Another crucial function of HSP90 is its role in mediating resistance to chemotherapy and targeted agents. By stabilizing drug-resistance-associated proteins such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and multidrug resistance-associated protein (MRP), HSP90 supports drug efflux and reduces intracellular drug concentrations, ultimately contributing to treatment failure [6].

Given these multifaceted roles, HSP90 has emerged as a compelling target for cancer therapy. By inhibiting the activity of HSP90, its interaction with numerous oncoproteins can be disrupted, leading to the degradation of oncoproteins and inactivation of related signaling pathways, thus achieving the goals of suppressing tumor growth, metastasis, and overcoming drug resistance [7]. Recent advances in drug development have led to the discovery of several HSP90 inhibitors that have entered clinical trials and shown encouraging therapeutic potential [8].

Moreover, in recent years, the diagnostic potential of HSP90 has received much attention. For example, radiolabeled derivatives based on sansalvamide A (San A) are used for positron emission tomography (PET) imaging of HSP90 expression in pancreatic cancer. An earlier study showed that[18F]NOTA-Dimer-San A exhibited significantly higher tumor uptake than inflammatory tissues in nude mice, enabling the differentiation between pancreatic cancer and inflammation [9]. [124I] PU-H71 imaging is used for non-invasive detection of the epichaperone centered around HSP90 in various tumors. In 30 cancer patients, multiple types of tumors, such as breast cancer, prostate cancer, and pancreatic cancer, were successfully detected through PET using [124I]PU-H71. The tracer is rapidly cleared from healthy tissues while remaining in tumors for several days. It is safe with a favorable radiation dose, providing a new tool for HSP90-targeted therapy [8].

HSP90 and Tumor-Related Signaling Pathways

The structure and basic functions of HSP90

HSP90 is a protein that is highly conserved across nearly all organisms, from bacteria to humans [10]. In human cells, there are four main subtypes: HSP90α and HSP90β located in the cytoplasm, glucose-regulated protein 94 in the endoplasmic reticulum, and tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1) in the mitochondria [11]. Its structure is a homodimer [12], and each subunit contains three key functional domains: the N-terminal domain (NTD) is responsible for binding and hydrolyzing ATP, providing energy for chaperone activity [13]; the middle domain is responsible for specifically recognizing and binding to the “client proteins” that require assistance [14]; and the C-terminal domain (CTD), through its conserved MEEVD sequence, mediates interactions with various co-chaperone proteins. Together, they regulate the activity of HSP90, substrate specificity, and the binding/dissociation process of client proteins [15]. These three domains work in concert, enabling the HSP90 dimer to form a dynamic molecular platform that aids in the proper folding, assembly, and stabilization of client proteins [16]. Under normal physiological conditions, HSP90 is crucial for maintaining protein homeostasis within cells. It utilizes ATP hydrolysis-driven conformational changes to guide newly synthesized, misfolding-prone polypeptide chains from ribosomes to form correct three-dimensional structures [17]. Moreover, when cells encounter stress, such as heat shock, they can recognize and bind misfolded proteins, and cooperate with co-chaperones to attempt to refold them. This helps prevent the formation of toxic protein aggregates and cell damage [18], thus ensuring the normal functioning of cells.

HSP90 in tumor-related signaling networks

HSP90 plays a pivotal role in maintaining the stability and activity of numerous oncogenic signaling proteins, making it a central hub in the regulation of tumor-related pathways [19]. Unlike normal cells, tumor cells are highly dependent on HSP90 to sustain the function of mutated, overexpressed, or aberrantly activated client proteins. These proteins are involved in key signaling cascades, such as the phosphoinositide 3-kinase(PI3K)/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin(mTOR)pathway, the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway, the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducers and activators of transcription(STAT)pathway, and those associated with hypoxia and EMT. By chaperoning multiple nodes within these pathways, HSP90 contributes to uncontrolled proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion, and therapeutic resistance [19].

Stabilization of oncogenic kinases and transcription factors

HSP90 facilitates the maturation and stabilization of a wide variety of oncogenic kinases, including HER2, EGFR, B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF), AKT, and cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) [19]. Notably, in the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling pathway, HSP90 directly interacts with EGFR, HER2, MET, and other factors. For example, HSP90 binds to EGFR, stabilizing its conformation to maintain activity and stability. Upon ligand activation, EGFR triggers downstream pathways (e.g., Rat Sarcoma Viral Oncogene Homolog(RAS)-RAF-MEK-ERK and PI3K-AKT), promoting tumor proliferation and migration. These kinases are often overexpressed or mutated in tumors and require HSP90 to maintain their active conformations. In the absence of functional HSP90, these client proteins are rapidly degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. This chaperoning effect not only preserves oncogenic signaling but also helps cancer cells evade apoptosis [19].

Additionally, HSP90 supports the activity of transcription factors such as HIF-1α and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells(NF-κB), which are critical for tumor adaptation to hypoxic environments and inflammation. In virus-driven malignancies (e.g., KSHV-induced primary effusion lymphoma), tumor-enriched HSP90 (teHSP90) forms complexes with viral oncoproteins such as viral FLICE-like inhibitory protein༈vFLIP༉. This stabilizes key NF-κB pathway components (e.g., inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B kinase subunit gamma (IKKγ)), sustaining constitutive NF-κB activation to promote tumor survival and drug resistance. Inhibition of teHSP90 triggers degradation of vFLIP/IKKγ, blocking NF-κB signaling and inducing apoptosis/autophagy in lymphoma cells [20]. For instance, in hypoxic tumors, HSP90 stabilizes HIF-1α, promoting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression and tumor angiogenesis [19]. The mitochondrial HSP90 family chaperone TRAP1 further amplifies HIF-1a stabilization by inhibiting succinate dehydrogenase. This induces succinate accumulation, which blocks HIF-1a degradation, thereby enhancing tumor growth. TRAP1 also links Ras/ERK signaling to metabolic reprogramming: As a phosphorylation target of mitochondrial ERK1/2, TRAP1-mediated succinate dehydrogenase suppression directly connects MAPK pathway activation to the Warburg effect (mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming), providing a novel therapeutic target for HSP90 inhibitors [21]. HSP90 binds to HIF-1a, stabilizing its structure and facilitating its nuclear translocation. Nuclear HIF-1a then binds hypoxia-response elements, activating the transcription of angiogenesis genes (e.g., VEGF). HSP90 inhibition induces HIF-1a degradation, suppresses VEGF-driven angiogenesis and restricts tumor growth/metastasis [22].

Regulation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK/ERK pathways

Two of the most frequently activated signaling cascades in human cancers—PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK/ERK—are extensively modulated by HSP90 client proteins. AKT, a key regulator of cell survival and metabolism, is a well-established HSP90 client. HSP90 binds tightly to AKT, protecting it from degradation and maintaining its phosphorylated (activated) state [23]. Activated AKT phosphorylates downstream targets (e.g., glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3β), mTOR), driving cell-cycle progression, protein synthesis, and survival [24]. HSP90 binding is essential for the proper folding and stabilization of AKT, enabling downstream activation of mTOR and inhibition of pro-apoptotic factors. Similarly, the RAF/MEK/ERK cascade, which governs cell proliferation, is stabilized by HSP90 through its interaction with BRAF and other kinases [19]. HSP90 directly binds to RAF proteins, maintaining their structural integrity and activity to drive MEK-ERK activation, thereby promoting tumor proliferation and migration. This dependency is particularly critical in BRAF-mutant cancers (e.g., melanoma), where HSP90 stabilizes constitutively activated mutant BRAF proteins that are essential for sustained pathway signaling [25]. Targeting HSP90 simultaneously disrupts both pathways, resulting in synthetic lethality in tumor cells with dual activation.

Involvement in EMT, invasion, and metastasis

HSP90 also plays a non-negligible role in tumor metastasis. It promotes EMT, a key step in cancer invasion and dissemination, by stabilizing transcription factors such as Snail, Twist, and ZEB1 [19]. These factors repress epithelial markers (e.g., E-cadherin) and enhance mesenchymal traits, facilitating detachment, migration, and invasion. Furthermore, extracellular HSP90 (eHSP90) has been reported to remodel the extracellular matrix and activate matrix metalloproteinases, thereby enhancing tumor cell invasiveness and distant colonization.

Facilitation of therapeutic resistance

HSP90 also contributes to therapeutic resistance by stabilizing resistance-associated oncoproteins such as mutant EGFR, ALK fusions, and MET amplification. HSP90 enables tumor cells to bypass the inhibitory effects of targeted therapies [19]. Inhibiting HSP90 thus offers a unique advantage by concurrently disrupting multiple resistance pathways and restoring drug sensitivity (Fig. 2).

HSP90 plays a critical role in modulating tumor-related signaling pathways

The rationale for HSP90 as a target in cancer treatment

High expression of HSP90 in tumor tissues

Numerous studies have shown that HSP90 is highly overexpressed in a variety of tumor tissues, and that its expression level is significantly higher than that in normal tissues. In breast cancer, estrogen receptor-positive, HER2+, or triple-negative type, the expression of HSP90α and HSP90β in tumor tissues is upregulated [26]. Immunohistochemical analysis in HER2+ breast cancer tissues showed that the expression level of HSP90α is closely related to the overexpression of HER2, and that patients with high expression of HSP90α have a poor prognosis [27]. The expression of HSP90 in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tissues is significantly higher than that in adjacent normal lung tissues [28]. In colorectal cancer, the expression level of HSP90 in tumor tissues gradually increases with the progression, especially in patients with advanced colorectal cancer [29]. This phenomenon of HSP90 overexpression is not limited to solid tumors but has also been reported in hematological malignancies such as leukemia and lymphoma [30].

The high expression of HSP90 in tumor tissues may be related to various factors. Tumor cells are in a state of rapid proliferation, with increased intracellular protein synthesis, generating a large number of nascent proteins that need to be folded and stabilized. This may prompt the upregulation of HSP90 expression to meet the demand of the cell for protein homeostasis. Moreover, various stress factors in the tumor microenvironment, such as hypoxia, nutrient deficiency, and oxidative stress, can also induce tumor cells to upregulate the expression of HSP90 [31]. Moreover, several oncogenic signaling pathways, such as the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK and PI3K-AKT pathways, can upregulate HSP90 expression through transcriptional regulatory mechanisms once activated. For example, activated AKT can directly phosphorylate the transcription factor FOXO1, thereby suppressing its inhibitory effect on HSP90 gene transcription and ultimately promoting HSP90 overexpression [32].

The role of HSP90 in maintaining the stability and function of oncoproteins

Through its chaperone function, HSP90 interacts with numerous oncoproteins, maintaining their structural stability and biological function, which is crucial for the survival and development of tumor cells [33]. In various tumors, the aberrant activation of RTKs is an important driving factor in tumor development [34]. HSP90 binds to RTKs such as EGFR, HER2, and MET, assisting these receptor proteins in proper folding, assembly, and transportation to the cell membrane surface, and maintaining their stability on the cell membrane [35]. For instance, HSP90 binds to the intracellular domain of HER2, forming a stable complex and preventing HER2 from being ubiquitinated and degraded. When HER2 is overexpressed, the role of HSP90 in maintaining its stability becomes even more significant, enabling HER2 to continuously activate downstream signaling pathways, such as PI3K-AKT and RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK pathways, thus promoting the proliferation, survival, and migration of tumor cells. An earlier study showed that treating HER2 + breast cancer cells with HSP90 inhibitors led to reduction in the level of HER2 protein, growth of tumor cells, and blockage of downstream signaling pathways [36].

Serine/threonine kinases also play crucial roles in tumor signal transduction. Among them, AKT is a core protein of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. HSP90 binds to AKT to form a complex, protecting AKT from proteasomal degradation and maintaining its active state [37]. Activated AKT can phosphorylate a variety of downstream target proteins, regulating processes such as cell metabolism, survival, proliferation, and migration. The stabilizing effect of HSP90 on AKT is of great significance for the survival and growth of tumor cells, especially those with an activated PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Inhibiting the activity of HSP90 can lead to a decrease in the level of the AKT protein, inactivation of its downstream signaling pathway, and apoptosis of tumor cells [38].

Transcription factors play crucial roles in regulating the expression of tumor-related genes, and HSP90 is also involved in maintaining the stability and function of important transcription factors. HIF-1α is a key transcription factor that allows tumor cells to adapt to the hypoxic microenvironment. Under normoxic conditions, HIF-1α is hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylase and then degraded by the ubiquitin-proteasome system. However, under hypoxic conditions, HSP90 binds to HIF-1α, preventing its hydroxylation and degradation and stabilizing the HIF-1α protein [21]. The stabilized HIF-1α enters the nucleus, forms a complex with HIF-1β, binds to the hypoxia-response elements of target genes, and initiates the transcription of a series of genes related to tumor angiogenesis, glucose metabolism, and cell survival. Inhibiting the activity of HSP90 can cause rapid degradation of HIF-1α, block hypoxia-induced gene expression, and suppress the survival of tumor cells in a hypoxic environment and tumor angiogenesis [22].

Biological functions of teHSP90 complexes

Recent studies have revealed that teHSP90 exhibits distinct substrate selectivity compared to constitutive HSP90 in normal cells. TeHSP90 preferentially binds to mutated or wild-type oncoproteins critical for tumor survival, forming high-molecular-weight stress complexes to stabilize their function. For example, in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, teHSP90 complexes interact with key components of the BCR signaling pathway (e.g., CD79, SYK, and BTK), sustaining constitutive activation of BCR signaling to promote tumor proliferation and survival [39]. Pharmacoproteomic analyses indicate that teHSP90 client proteins are highly tumor-specific, providing a rationale for developing selective teHSP90 inhibitors.

Classification and Mechanism of Action of HSP90 Inhibitors

Structurally, HSP90 functions as a homodimer, with each monomer comprising three major domains: an NTD responsible for ATP binding and hydrolysis, a middle domain that binds client proteins, and a CTD involved in dimerization and co-chaperone interaction. These domain-specific characteristics provide distinct pharmacological binding sites and form the structural basis for classifying HSP90 inhibitors into NTDs, CTDs, and allosteric binders (Fig. 3).

Structure and different domains of HSP90

NTD binding inhibitors

Based on the crucial role of HSP90 in tumors, developing HSP90 inhibitors has become an important strategy for cancer treatment. NTD-binding inhibitors are the most widely studied class of HSP90 inhibitors. They exert inhibitory effects by tightly binding to the N-terminal ATP-binding site of HSP90. A variety of HSP90 inhibitors have been proven to have significant anti-tumor activity in research setting. Geldanamycin (GA) is one of the earliest discovered HSP90 inhibitors. It is a benzoquinone ansamycin compound isolated from Streptomyces. It binds tightly to the N-terminal ATP-binding site of HSP90, inhibits the ATPase activity of HSP90, thus interfering with the interaction between HSP90 and client proteins and leading to the degradation of client proteins [40]. In various tumor cell lines, GA exhibits potent anti-tumor activity. In breast cancer cell lines, GA can significantly reduce the levels of oncoproteins such as HER2 and AKT, inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells, and induce apoptosis [41]. In lung cancer cell lines, after treatment with GA, related proteins such as EGFR and HIF-1α are degraded, and the growth and migration abilities of tumor cells are significantly inhibited [42]. However, the adverse effects of GA, such as hepatotoxicity, limit its clinical application [10].

A recent study successfully developed a PET molecular probe, [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-BDA-GM, which targets HSP90 with high specificity and sensitivity and enables effective imaging of breast cancer and its metastases [43]. This probe has potential for early diagnosis and disease monitoring of breast cancer, and lays a solid foundation for the development of HSP90-targeted PET molecular imaging probes important in future clinical diagnosis and treatment monitoring.

17-AAG is a derivative of GA-17-AAG that retains the high affinity of GA for the N-terminal ATP-binding site of HSP90, while improving pharmacokinetic properties and water solubility, and reducing hepatotoxicity. In preclinical studies, 17-AAG has demonstrated good anti-tumor activity in a variety of tumor models. In a melanoma model, 17-AAG inhibits the growth and metastasis of tumor cells by degrading proteins such as BRAF and MEK and blocking the MAPK signaling pathway [12]. In a lung cancer model, after treatment with 17-AAG, the levels of related proteins (such as EGFR and HIF-1α)and the growth and invasion abilities of tumor cells are significantly inhibited [44]. In a colorectal cancer model, after treatment with 17-AAG, the levels of proteins such as AKT and β-catenin decrease, and the proliferation and invasion abilities of tumor cells are inhibited [45]. Unconventional protein secretion (UPS) is an important mechanism by which cells secrete proteins lacking signal peptides (such as IL-1β, Tau, etc.). The UPS pathway may be involved in the formation of the tumor microenvironment by regulating the secretion of inflammatory factors. HSP90 inhibitors (such as 17-AAG, and Ganetespib) have also been shown to inhibit the secretion of pro-cancer factors by blocking the UPS pathway [46].

In addition to geldanamycin-type inhibitors, other types of HSP90 inhibitors have also demonstrated anti-tumor activity in preclinical studies. For example, the purine analogue inhibitor PU-H71 can specifically bind to key amino acid residues in the NTD of HSP90, interfering with the interaction between HSP90 and ATP, thus blocking the chaperone function of HSP90 and inhibiting its activity [47]. In leukemia cell lines, PU-H71 can induce the degradation of various oncoproteins, including BCR-ABL and FLT3, effectively inhibiting the proliferation and survival of leukemia cells [38]. For solid tumors, PU-H71 has also demonstrated anti-tumor activity in cell lines and animal models of breast cancer [48], lung cancer [49], etc. PU-H71 can inhibit the growth of tumor cells, and induce apoptosis and autophagy.

[124I]PU-H71 is a radioactive probe that targets the tumor-specific epichaperome network. Unlike traditional HSP90 inhibitors, PU-H71 selectively binds to the HSP90 ATPase domain in the epichaperome, with almost no binding in healthy tissues, thus enabling tumor-specific imaging. [124I] PU-H71 PET detected tumors of different cancer types (breast, lymphoma, neuroblastoma, genitourinary, gynecologic, sarcoma, and pancreas). [124I] PU-H71 was retained by tumors for several days while it was cleared rapidly from bones, healthy soft tissues, and blood. Radiation dosimetry of [124I]PU-H71 was favorable, and patients experienced no adverse effects after treatment [8].

In addition, several new NTD-binding inhibitors, such as STA-9090 (ganetespib), have shown good anti-tumor potential in preclinical and clinical trials. STA-9090 has a high affinity for the N-terminal ATP-binding site of HSP90, can efficiently inhibit the activity of HSP90, leading to the degradation of various oncoproteins, and has favorable pharmacokinetic properties in vivo [22]. Moreover, in the treatment of liver fibrosis, research has indicated that the combined use of the HSP90 inhibitor STA-9090 and statins can synergistically inhibit the Hedgehog (Hh) pathway through complementary mechanisms (direct inhibition of HSP90 + indirect reduction in cholesterol), thus enhancing the anti-fibrotic and anti-tumor effects [50].

CTD-binding inhibitors

In existing research, HSP90 CTD inhibitors (KU135 [51]/KU758 [52]) have only been developed as therapeutic drugs, focusing on inducing tumor cell death, overcoming drug resistance, and synergistic effects of combination therapies. However, their direct application in the diagnostic field has not been explored. An earlier study developed mitochondrially-targeted Hsp90 CTD therapeutic drugs with stronger anti-tumor activity to overcome the drawbacks of traditional NTD inhibitors [53]. In 2017, stilbene analogs were designed and synthesized stilbene analogues as HSP-90 CTD inhibitors, and the core skeleton and side-chain structures were optimized to enhance anti-tumor activity and HSP-90 inhibitory potency [54].

CTD-binding inhibitors act on the CTD of HSP90. This domain contains a conserved MEEVD pentapeptide sequence, which plays a crucial role in the interaction between HSP90 and various co-chaperone proteins. CTD binding inhibitors bind to a specific region at the CTD of HSP90, interfering with the interaction between HSP90 and co-chaperone proteins, thus affecting the assembly and function of the HSP90 chaperone complex. Unlike NTD-binding inhibitors, CTD-binding inhibitors do not directly affect the binding and hydrolysis of ATP by HSP90. Instead, they indirectly influence the effect of HSP90 on client proteins by regulating the binding between HSP90 and co-chaperone proteins [55].

At present, research on CTD-binding inhibitors is relatively scarce. A few small-molecule compounds have been found to specifically bind to the CTD of HSP90. Nocodazole, which is originally developed as a microtubule-depolymerizing agent has been shown to bind to the CTD of HSP90 at certain concentrations [56]. Nocodazole may affect the interaction between HSP90 and the co-chaperone protein p23, which is important in the HSP90 chaperone complex. After binding to HSP90, it can stabilize the closed conformation of HSP90, promoting the binding and proper folding of HSP90 with client proteins [57].

Studies have also found that the abnormal expression of HSP90, a key chaperone for the folding of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), may lead to glucocorticoid resistance by interfering with GR maturation. CTD HSP90 inhibitors can restore the transcriptional activity of GR by releasing mature GR, and inhibit the growth of pituitary tumors. HSP90 forms a complex with GR, inhibiting its ability to bind DNA and form a transcription complex, resulting in glucocorticoid resistance. Therefore, targeting the CTD of HSP90 is a potential strategy for reversing glucocorticoid resistance, treating Cushing’s disease, and potentially extending to other cancers [58].

In order to develop more effective CTD-binding HSP90 inhibitors, researchers have used methods such as computer-aided drug design and high-throughput screening to search for compounds with higher affinity and specificity [59]. Lead compounds of new CTD binding HSP90 inhibitors have been discovered. In 2021, covalently modifying specific residues at the C-terminus of Hsp90 disrupted the interaction between Hsp90 and its co-chaperones (such as Cdc37), leading to the degradation of Hsp90-dependent kinase client proteins, and thus inhibiting the growth and survival of tumor cells. They discovered a new covalent inhibitor of Hsp90, DDO-6600. It disrupts the interaction between Hsp90 and Cdc37 by covalently modifying Cys598 at the C-terminus of Hsp90, resulting in the degradation of Hsp90-dependent kinase client proteins and inhibiting the growth and survival of tumor cells. These findings indicate that DDO-6600 provides a new strategy for the development of Hsp90 inhibitors and offers a potential candidate drug for cancer treatment [60]. Further in-depth research on the structure-activity relationship of CTD binding inhibitors and optimization of their chemical structures could lead to the development of more effective drugs for cancer treatment.

Inhibitors acting on other sites

In addition to NTD- and CTD-binding inhibitors, efforts have been made to find inhibitors that act on other sites of HSP90 with additional mechanisms of action, such as the middle domain or other specific regions of HSP90. The middle domain is a key region for HSP90 to recognize and bind client proteins, and its interaction with client proteins involves multiple amino acid residues and structural motifs. Theoretically, interfering with the binding between the middle domain and client proteins can directly block the chaperone function of HSP90 on client proteins, but there has been no further research yet. In addition, A previous research has revealed that co-chaperone proteins of HSP90 (FNIP1/2, Tsc1) could regulate the ligand response of steroid hormone receptors (such as GR and ERα) by forming dynamic complexes, providing a new mechanism for treatment resistance in cancers, especially hormone-dependent tumors [55]. This study suggests that inhibitors targeting specific co-chaperone complexes may overcome the drug resistance of existing HSP90 inhibitors.

In addition, the bacterial effector protein HopBF1 has been shown to block the immune signaling pathway by mimicking the client proteins of HSP90, specifically phosphorylating the ATP-binding domain of HSP90 and inhibiting its ATPase activity. HopBF1 mimics the αC-β4 loop structure of host kinases (such as v-src) and is recognized as a “pseudo-client protein” by HSP90 without the need for the co-chaperone CDC37, suggesting a mechanism for the specific recognition of kinase clients by HSP90. Therefore, phosphorylation site (Ser108) of HSP90 may serve as a target for new inhibitors. By disrupting the ATPase activity, oncoprotein degradation can be induced, which is a potential target for more specific and low-toxicity small-molecule inhibitors [61].

In addition, some inhibitors function by affecting the dimerization process of HSP90. HSP90 functions as a chaperone in the form of a homodimer, and its dimerization state is crucial for maintaining normal chaperone activity. Small-molecule compounds can bind to specific regions of HSP90, interfering with the interaction between HSP90 subunits and inhibiting the formation of dimers [62]. By designing inhibitors targeting different structural and functional sites of HSP90, the chaperone function of HSP90 can be more comprehensively disrupted, improving the efficacy of cancer treatment. Further research on the ability of HSP90 inhibitors to act on other sites and explore their potential in cancer treatment are needed.

Interface/co-chaperone disruptors

Beyond direct inhibition of HSP90’s functional domains, an emerging strategy focuses on disrupting critical protein-protein interactions (PPIs) within the chaperone machinery, particularly the interface between HSP90 and its co-chaperones [63]. This approach offers a more targeted means to interfere with the maturation of specific client proteins, potentially bypassing the heat shock response and associated toxicities triggered by pan-inhibitors. The HSP90-Cdc37 interaction is a prime target, as Cdc37 specifically recruits and facilitates the loading of numerous oncogenic kinases (e.g., CDK4, AKT, BRAF [64–66]) onto the HSP90 complex [67, 68]. Small molecules such as DDO-5936 [63] and DDO-6079 [69] have been shown to allosterically disrupt the HSP90-Cdc37 PPI, leading to the selective degradation of kinase clients without directly inhibiting HSP90’s ATPase activity. Natural products like Celastrol and its derivatives also function through this mechanism, binding to Cdc37 and inducing kinase client degradation [68, 70]. Similarly, the compound OCS targets the Glu-47 site on HSP90, destabilizing its complex with Cdc37 [66]. While targeting large PPI surfaces remains challenging, these co-chaperone disruptors represent a promising avenue for achieving greater therapeutic specificity by selectively dismantling tumor-driving kinase pathways [71, 72].

Isoform-selective inhibitors

The clinical limitations of pan-HSP90 inhibitors, notably their on-target toxicity and induction of the heat shock response [73], have spurred the development of inhibitors selective for specific HSP90 isoforms (HSP90α, HSP90β, GRP94, TRAP1) [74, 75]. Leveraging subtle structural differences in their ATP-binding pockets or unique allosteric sites, researchers are designing compounds that selectively inhibit one isoform over others. For instance, the HSP90β-selective inhibitor KUNB106, based on an isoquinolin-1(2 H)-one scaffold, exhibits > 50-fold selectivity over HSP90α and does not induce compensatory HSP90 upregulation [75–77]. For the endoplasmic reticulum-resident isoform GRP94, inhibitors like BnIm exploit a distinct hydrophobic pocket in its N-terminal domain, showing high specificity and potential in modulating immune responses and treating multiple myeloma [78]. Meanwhile, mitochondrial TRAP1-selective inhibitors are emerging as tools to probe their role in tumor metabolism and apoptosis [79]. The development of C-terminal inhibitors, such as novobiocin derivatives, which do not induce Hsp70 expression, provides another strategy for isoform-selective modulation [80, 81]. Although achieving high selectivity is challenging due to significant sequence homology among isoforms, these efforts aim to decouple therapeutic efficacy from mechanism-based toxicity, paving the way for a new generation of precision-targeted HSP90 therapeutics [82, 83].

San A and HSP90 middle domain Inhibition

San A is a natural product isolated from the marine fungus Fusarium spp [84]. Various synthetic methods are available, such as the zinc-catalyzed method, copper-catalyzed method, benzotriazole ester-mediated cyclization reaction, and microwave-assisted synthesis, etc. [5]. San A derivatives, through structural optimization and the integrated design for diagnosis and treatment, have great value as anti-tumor lead compounds [85] and are expected to become important candidates for the next-generation anti-tumor drugs [5].

Research has shown that San A exhibits significant cytotoxicity against drug-resistant pancreatic cancers (such as PL45 and BxPC-3), with activity comparable to existing anti-cancer drugs [84]. Its cyclic peptide structure has a well-defined 3D conformation and high target affinity, making it a suitable carrier for targeted probes. It has strong cell-penetrating ability and high metabolic stability (the cyclic peptide resists protease degradation), which is suitable for in vivo imaging or delivery systems [84]. Through structural optimization (single D-amino acid + N-methylation) and conformational locking, San A derivatives have become potent lead compounds for treating drug-resistant colon cancer, providing an important basis for the development of a new generation of targeted anti-cancer drugs [86]. San A shows significant cytotoxicity against pancreatic cancer, colon cancer (including the drug-resistant strain HCT116), breast cancer, prostate cancer, melanoma, etc., and has been proven to overcome 5-fluorouracil resistance, offering a new treatment for microsatellite-unstable colon cancer [87].

Radiolabeled derivatives based on San A have been employed for PET imaging of HSP90 expression in pancreatic cancer, offering a promising non-invasive approach for diagnosis and intraoperative guidance. In particular, the [18F]PEGylated decapeptide probe ([18F] PEGylated San A) demonstrated the most favorable imaging performance. In pancreatic cancer xenograft models, this probe exhibited strong tumor uptake, with a tumor-to-muscle ratio of 6.09 ± 1.92, and enabled high-contrast PET detection, providing a robust and sensitive strategy for HSP90-targeted diagnosis [9, 88]. Li et al. further designed a dual-modality imaging probe, [⁶⁴Cu]Cu-NOTA-SanA-Cy7, by incorporating both PET and near-infrared fluorescence capabilities into San A scaffold. This probe achieved high binding affinity to HSP90α-positive tumor cells (Kd = 1.08 ± 0.19 µM, e.g., EC109), and showed markedly higher tumor uptake relative to normal tissues in HSP90α-positive EC109 tumor-bearing nude mice via both PET and fluorescence imaging. The dual-modality nature of this probe supports both preoperative diagnostic imaging and intraoperative fluorescence navigation, offering added versatility for managing HSP90α-overexpressing tumors [89].

HSP90 inhibitors that have entered clinical trials

With a deep understanding of the crucial role of HSP90 in tumorigenesis and development, as well as the promising anti-tumor activity demonstrated by HSP90 inhibitors in preclinical studies, significant progress has been made for its clinical translation. As a result, a variety of HSP90 inhibitors have entered the clinical trial stage for safety and efficacy evaluation in cancer patients. 17-AAG is one of the first HSP90 inhibitors to enter clinical trials. In early phase I clinical trials, 17-AAG was used as a single agent to treat patients with various advanced cancers, including breast cancer, lung cancer and melanoma, etc., and showed anti-tumor activity (stabilization or shrinkage of tumor) in patients [90]. However, 17-AAG also exhibited several adverse effects, such as gastrointestinal reactions, fatigue, anemia, and elevated levels of transaminases, etc., which limited its dosage and clinical application. In order to improve the efficacy and tolerability of 17-AAG, a number of clinical trials of combination therapies have been subsequently carried out [91]. For example, 17-AAG combined with traditional chemotherapy drugs could enhance the anti-tumor effect, and increase the tumor remission rate of patients, maintaining adverse reactions within a tolerable range [92].

STA-9090 is also a much-watched HSP90 inhibitor that has been tested in clinical trials for multiple cancer types. In clinical trials for NSCLC, STA-9090, when used as a single agent to treat patients with advanced NSCLC, demonstrated certain anti-tumor activity, and the disease of patients was controlled. In a phase II clinical trial for small cell lung cancer, the trial was suspended due to toxicity [6]. In addition, clinical trials of STA-9090 for other tumor types, such as melanoma and ovarian cancer, are actively being carried out to further evaluate its efficacy and safety.

Pimitespib (TAS-116) is a novel small-molecule inhibitor of HSP90 that can inhibit both HSP90α and HSP90β [93]. In phase I clinical trials, Pimitespib, when used as a single agent to treat patients with various advanced solid tumors, demonstrated certain anti-tumor activity, and the adverse reactions were relatively mild. These symptoms mainly include diarrhea, nausea, night blindness, increased serum creatinine, taste disorders, decreased appetite, and eye abnormalities [94]. Currently, there are relevant reports indicating that Pimitespib is undergoing phase II clinical trials in combination with other anti-cancer drugs, with the promise of bringing better treatment outcomes for patients.

Pimitespib represents a landmark achievement in the clinical translation of HSP90 inhibitors, being the first and only HSP90 inhibitor approved for clinical use to date. It has received regulatory approval in Japan for the treatment of advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumors in patients who have failed prior standard therapies including imatinib, sunitinib, and regorafenib, positioning it as a fourth-line option [95, 96]. This approval is underpinned by its high selectivity for HSP90α/β [97, 98], oral bioavailability [94, 99], and an improved safety profile that mitigates the hepatotoxicity and ocular toxicity associated with earlier pan-HSP90 inhibitors [99, 100]. In phase II trials, Pimitespib (160 mg/day) demonstrated confirmed partial responses in a subset of GIST patients, highlighting its single-agent efficacy [99, 101]. Preclinically, it effectively degraded mutant KIT proteins and overcame imatinib resistance in GIST models (IC₅₀ < 0.5 µmol/L) [93, 102]. The success of Pimitespib not only addresses an unmet need in late-line GIST therapy but also establishes a critical precedent for the development of next-generation HSP90 inhibitors with enhanced selectivity and tolerability [96, 103, 104].

Analysis of clinical trial results

Based on the existing clinical trial results, HSP90 inhibitors show potential in cancer treatment, but also face challenges. In terms of efficacy, patients respond to monotherapy with HSP90 inhibitors, which manifests as tumor stabilization or shrinkage. However, the overall response rate of monotherapy is relatively low, and most patients experience disease progression during treatment. This may be related to the heterogeneity of tumor cells, the complexity of the HSP90 signaling pathway, and the existence of other compensatory mechanisms in tumor cells. There are differences in the expression levels of HSP90, the types of client proteins, and the degree of dependence in the tumor cells of different cancer patients, resulting in different sensitivities to HSP90 inhibitors. In addition, after being exposed to HSP90 inhibitors, tumor cells may maintain protein homeostasis by activating other signaling pathways or upregulating the expression of other chaperones, thus developing drug resistance [105].

The combination therapy strategy shows advantages in improving the efficacy of HSP90 inhibitors. Combining HSP90 inhibitors with traditional chemotherapy drugs can enhance the killing effect on tumor cells. Chemotherapy drugs can directly damage the DNA of tumor cells or interfere with the cell division process, while HSP90 inhibitors increase the sensitivity of tumor cells to chemotherapy drugs by degrading oncoproteins and blocking related signaling pathways. In addition, combining HSP90 inhibitors with targeted therapy drugs is also an effective strategy. For example, in NSCLC with EGFR mutations, the combination of HSP90 inhibitors and EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) can simultaneously block the activation of the EGFR and HSP90 signaling pathways. which maintains the stability of the EGFR protein, resulting in a synergistic anti-tumor effect [106]. In addition, combination therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors, which has emerged in recent years, and HSP90 inhibitors also shows promising prospects. HSP90 inhibitors may enhance the immunogenicity of tumor cells by regulating the tumor microenvironment, promoting the recognition and killing of tumor cells by immune cells. They work synergistically with immune checkpoint inhibitors to improve the immunotherapy efficacy for patients [46].

The adverse reactions of HSP90 inhibitors demonstrated in clinical trials have common characteristics, including gastrointestinal reactions, fatigue, hematological toxicity (such as anemia, leukopenia, etc.), and abnormal liver and kidney function. The mechanisms underlying these adverse reactions may be related to the inhibition of the physiological functions of HSP90 in normal cells [107]. The incidence and severity of adverse reactions vary among different HSP90 inhibitors. This may be related to the chemical structure and pharmacokinetic properties of the inhibitors, as well as their selectivity for HSP90 subtypes [108]. For example, due to its structural characteristics, 17-AAG is prone to cause relatively severe hepatotoxicity and gastrointestinal reactions. While new HSP90 inhibitors, such as Pimitespib, have relatively mild adverse reactions, the safety of their long-term use still needs to be closely monitored. Resistance Mechanisms and Countermeasures of HSP90 Inhibitors

The discovered resistance mechanisms

Although HSP90 inhibitors have shown potential in cancer treatment, the development of drug resistance to HSP90 inhibitors by tumor cells is an important issue in clinical applications. Currently, multiple mechanisms that lead to drug resistance in tumor cells to HSP90 inhibitors have been discovered [55]. Tumor cells can compensate for the loss of HSP90 function by upregulating the expression of other chaperone proteins. HSP70 is a chaperone protein closely related to HSP90 and plays an important role in maintaining cellular protein homeostasis. When tumor cells are exposed to HSP90 inhibitors, the expression of HSP70 is often upregulated [109]. Quantitative evidence from a breast cancer model (MCF-7 cells) demonstrated that the efficacy of a novel HSP90 inhibitor was associated with a 2.4-fold reduction in HSP90 expression itself, highlighting a potential compensatory feedback mechanism [110]. Studies have shown that HSP70 can partially substitute for the functions of HSP90, maintaining the stability and activity of oncoproteins [111]. For example, in breast cancer cells, when HSP90 is inhibited, HSP70 binds to HER2 and continues to protect HER2 from degradation, enabling the pro-cancer signaling pathway downstream of HER2 to remain activated. This ultimately leads to the development of resistance in tumor cells to HSP90 inhibitors [112].

Tumor cells may also develop drug resistance by activating other signaling pathways to bypass the signaling pathways blocked by HSP90 inhibitors [113]. Transcriptomic analyses support this finding, revealing that HSP90 client proteins (e.g., mutant KRAS) are enriched in resistant phenotypes, suggesting that HSP90 inhibition could overcome resistance by degrading these specific clients [114]. The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is an important survival and proliferation signaling pathway in tumor cells. HSP90 plays a key role in maintaining the stability and activity of AKT [115]. However, when HSP90 is inhibited, tumor cells can indirectly activate the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway by activating other upstream signaling molecules, such as RAS and HER3 [116]. Research has found that in tumor cells resistant to HSP90 inhibitors, the expression of HER3 is upregulated. HER3 forms heterodimers with HER2 and activates PI3K, leading to the phosphorylation and activation of AKT, which promotes the survival and proliferation of tumor cells [117]. In addition, tumor cells may also evade the effects of HSP90 inhibitors by activating other pro-cancer signaling pathways, such as MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways [118].

Increased drug efflux is also one of the mechanisms by which tumor cells develop resistance to HSP90 inhibitors [119]. Drug-resistance-related proteins, such as ATP-binding cassette transporters (ABC transporters), P-gp, and MRP, etc., are highly expressed in tumor cells [120]. These proteins can pump out drugs that have entered tumor cells, reducing the intracellular drug concentration and thus leading to drug resistance [121]. Like chemotherapeutic drugs, HSP90 inhibitors can also be affected by these drug efflux proteins. For example, in ALK-rearranged lung cancer, after treatment with 17-dimethylaminoethylamino-17-demethoxygeldanamycin (17-DMAG), a derivative of 17-AAG, the expression of P-gp was upregulated in drug-resistant cells, leading to a decrease in the intracellular drug concentration and thus causing the tumor cells to develop resistance to 17-DMAG [122].

Metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells is also related to resistance to HSP90 inhibitors. During the growth and proliferation of tumor cells, metabolic changes occur to meet their energy demands and biosynthetic requirements [21]. Metabolic pathways such as glucose metabolism and fatty acid metabolism often change in tumor cells resistant to HSP90 inhibitors. For example, drug-resistant tumor cells may increase glucose uptake and glycolytic activity to generate more energy and metabolic intermediates to maintain cell survival and proliferation [123]. Moreover, fatty acid synthesis and oxidative metabolism may also change, providing tumor cells with energy and raw materials for bio-membrane synthesis [124]. Emerging evidence quantifies these shifts; for instance, studies in microbial models have identified amino acid synthesis pathways (e.g., MET-SAM-PWY, METSYN-PWY) with log2 fold changes of 0.42–0.53 in resistant states, providing a quantitative glimpse into the metabolic rewiring associated with drug tolerance [125]. Furthermore, in melanoma models resistant to MEK/BRAF inhibitors, the C-terminal HSP90 inhibitor KU757 was found to effectively downregulate oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) and cell cycle pathways, specifically countering the altered metabolism of the resistant cells [126]. These metabolic reprogramming processes may be closely related to the drug resistance of tumor cells to HSP90 inhibitors. Regulating the metabolic state of tumor cells, enables them to survive under the pressure of HSP90 inhibitors [127].

Most existing studies describe drug-resistance mechanisms through pathway enrichment analysis or gene expression changes (such as log2 fold change). However, there is a lack of data directly comparing the survival rates of drug - resistant/sensitive models or clearly defining fold-change. In the future, more preclinical studies are needed to quantify these differences to guide the optimization of treatment strategies [128–130].

Strategies to overcome drug resistance

In response to the various mechanisms by which tumor cells develop resistance to HSP90 inhibitors, researchers are exploring a series of strategies to overcome drug resistance, including new types of HSP90 inhibitors. On the one hand, designing inhibitors that can target multiple domains of HSP90 simultaneously or synergize with other key molecules can enhance tumor cell killing. For instance, developing bifunctional inhibitors to block both the N-terminal ATP-binding site and the C-terminal co-chaperone interaction site of HSP90 may more effectively disrupt its chaperone function, reducing tumor cell resistance via upregulation of other chaperones or altering HSP90-co-chaperone interactions [131, 132]. On the other hand, the chemical structure of HSP90 inhibitors can be optimized to enhance their inhibitory activity and selectivity towards HSP90 [131]. Lead compounds of HSP90 inhibitors with higher affinity and specificity can be identified through computer-aided drug design and high-throughput screening. Then, their structures can be optimized to effectively inhibit the activity of HSP90 while reducing its toxicity to normal cells [132]. In addition, selective inhibitors that target specific HSP90 subtypes or HSP90 with specific modifications are being developed to improve the precision of treatment and reduce the occurrence of drug resistance [107].

Combining drugs with distinct mechanisms is an effective strategy to overcome HSP90 inhibitor resistance. HSP90 inhibitors can be combined with chemotherapeutics, targeted therapies, immunotherapies, etc. In resistance reversal, combination therapy blocks tumor cell growth/survival signals at multiple levels and inhibits resistance mechanisms. For example, co-administering HSP90 and HSP70 inhibitors simultaneously suppresses both chaperones, preventing tumor cells from compensating for HSP90 loss by upregulating HSP70 [6]. In preclinical studies, the combined application of some HSP70 inhibitors and HSP90 inhibitors has been shown to have a synergistic inhibitory effect on tumor cells [133]. In addition, combining HSP90 inhibitors with PI3K inhibitors, MEK inhibitors, etc., can simultaneously block the signaling pathways that are blocked by HSP90 inhibitors, which is expected to overcome drug resistance [134]. For example, the HSP90 inhibitor NVP-AUY922 combined with PI3K/mTOR dual inhibitor NVP-BEZ235 synergistically killed cholangiocarcinoma cell lines in vitro and significantly inhibited tumor growth/induced regression in animal models. This combination blocks the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway, and induces reactive oxygen species, exacerbates endoplasmic reticulum stress and forms vicious cycles to enhance tumor cell death [135]. These findings indicate that the combined use of HSP90 inhibitors and other targeted drugs can interfere with the growth and survival signals of tumor cells at multiple levels, effectively overcoming the problem of drug resistance.

During the clinical treatment process, rationally formulating a dosing regimen can also help overcome the drug resistance of HSP90 inhibitors. For example, by using an intermittent dosing approach, leaving tumor cells in a relatively “drug-free” state during drug-free intervals may reduce the adaptive drug-resistance response of tumor cells caused by long-term exposure to the drug [121]. Moreover, adjusting drug dosage in a timely manner based on patient-specific conditions and tumor dynamics can maintain treatment efficacy while preventing tumor cell resistance from suboptimal dosing or severe adverse reactions from excessive dosing. Additionally, dynamic monitoring of HSP90 and related pathway molecules—including their expression and activity—as well as resistance markers is crucial. This allows for the prompt detection of resistance signs and adjustment of treatment plans, thereby enhancing the efficacy of HSP90 inhibitors [136].

A deep understanding of tumor cell biology and resistance mechanisms is key to developing more effective strategies against HSP90 inhibitor resistance. With advances in precision and personalized medicine, future approaches may tailor individualised treatment plans via comprehensive molecular profiling of patient tumor samples. These plans could include selecting optimal HSP90 inhibitors, combination regimens, and administration methods to maximize efficacy, overcome resistance, and improve cancer patient outcomes. Combination Application Strategies Involving HSP90 Inhibitors and other Treatment Methods

Combination with targeted therapeutic drugs

Both chemotherapy and targeted therapy are important means of cancer treatment. Chemotherapy directly kills or inhibits tumor cells through cytotoxic drugs, but it often damages normal cells and causes serious adverse reactions. Moreover, tumor cells are prone to developing drug resistance. Although targeted drugs can act specifically on abnormally activated signaling pathways or molecular targets of tumor cells, and have the advantages of high efficiency and relatively few adverse reactions, their long-term efficacy is also limited by the development of drug resistance. HSP90 plays a crucial role in tumor cells. By stabilizing various chemotherapy-resistance-related proteins (such as P-gp [137] and MRP [138]) and oncoproteins (such as AKT and cRaf) [139], it is involved in maintaining the activity of multiple key signaling pathways and protein stability, thus mediating resistance to chemotherapy drugs and targeted drugs. Inhibiting the activity of HSP90 can degrade these resistance proteins and oncoproteins, increase the concentration of chemotherapy drugs within tumor cells, enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to DNA damage and apoptosis induced by chemotherapy drugs [139], and cooperate with targeted drugs to block the growth and survival signals of tumor cells at multiple levels, overcome the problem of drug resistance, and improve the treatment effect [38].

In terms of chemotherapy, HSP90 inhibitors have shown synergistic effects when combined with various drugs, such as paclitaxel, doxorubicin, and cisplatin. For example, a 2019 study found that the HSP90B subtype directly binds to microtubule-associated serine/threonine kinase 1 (MAST1), inhibits its ubiquitination-mediated degradation, and is a key driver of cisplatin resistance. Inhibiting HSP90B (such as using 17-AAG) can significantly reduce the MAST1 protein level and restore cisplatin sensitivity (IC50 decreased by 50–70%). The combination of 17-AAG and the MAST1 inhibitor lestaurtinib showed a synergistic effect both in vitro and in patient-derived xenograft models (combination index CI = 0.44–0.76), significantly inhibiting tumor growth (tumor volume decreased by 60–80%) [140]. In the study of liver cancer, geldanamycin (GA) combined with cisplatin can synergistically enhance the killing effect on liver cancer cells and overcome drug resistance. By inhibiting HSP90, GA induces the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of the apoptosis-inhibiting protein Survivin, relieves the inhibition of caspase-9, activates caspase-3/7, and thus activates the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [105].

In terms of targeted therapy, the combination of HSP90 inhibitors and various targeted drugs is also effective. For targeted therapies related to the RTK signaling pathway, studies have confirmed the synergistic effect of HSP90 inhibitors and RTK inhibitors in thyroid cancer [141]. In EGFR-mutated NSCLC, EGFR-TKIs are prone to drug resistance [142]. Research has found that HSP90 binds to and maintains the stability and activity of the EGFR protein, and its expression and activity are often upregulated in EGFR-TKI-resistant cells [10]. The combination of an HSP90 inhibitor and an EGFR-TKI can simultaneously block the activation of the EGFR signaling pathway and the function of HSP90 that maintains EGFR stability, degrades the EGFR protein, and increases the sensitivity of tumor cells to EGFR-TKIs [106]. Preclinical studies have also found that HSP90AA1 stabilizes Interferon alpha inducible protein 6 (IFI6) and transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGFB1) through the activator of Hsp90 ATPase homolog 1 (AHSA1) complex, activates the AKT signaling pathway and induces EMT, thus driving osimertinib resistance. This expands the molecular network of HSP90 in tumor resistance and suggests that targeting downstream molecules (rather than HSP90 itself) may reduce toxicity, providing a direction for the development of a new generation of inhibitors [143]. Although trastuzumab has improved the prognosis of patients with HER2-positive breast cancer, drug resistance, such as expression of p95HER2, activation of PI3K/AKT pathway, and the presence of cancer stem cells, remains a challenge. HSP90 binds tightly to HER2 to maintain its stability. Traditional inhibitors that target the N-terminal ATP-binding domain of HSP90 have more adverse reactions. In 2024, researchers developed a novel CTD-targeted inhibitor, HVH-2930. Experiments have shown that HVH-2930, by targeting the HSP90-HER2 axis and inhibiting the characteristics of cancer stem cells, exhibits a highly efficient and low-toxicity tumor-suppressing effect on trastuzumab-resistant HER2-positive breast cancer, indicating the feasibility of exploring combination immunotherapy [144].

In addition, HSP90 inhibitors can also be combined with BRAF inhibitors (such as vemurafenib and dabrafenib) for BRAF-mutated tumors. HSP90 is involved in maintaining the stability of the BRAF protein. Inhibiting its activity can degrade the BRAF protein and enhance the efficacy of BRAF inhibitors [145]. Preclinical studies have shown that the HSP90 inhibitor XL888 and BRAF inhibitors have a synergistic anti-tumor effect on BRAF-mutated melanoma cell lines and animal models [146]. In tumors with abnormally activated PI3K-AKT signaling pathways, HSP90 maintains the activity of this pathway by stabilizing proteins such as AKT. The combination of HSP90 inhibitors and PI3K inhibitors can block the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway at multiple steps, thus improving the treatment effect [134].

These findings suggest that the combined use of HSP90 inhibitors with chemotherapy drugs or targeted therapy drugs is a feasible and effective strategy. The ability to overcome drug-resistance mechanisms provides new options for cancer treatment.

Combination with immunotherapy drugs

Immunotherapy activates the immune system of the body to recognize and kill tumor cells, bringing a revolutionary change to cancer treatment. However, some patients have a poor response to immunotherapy drugs, and there are problems such as immune escape. Recent studies have found that HSP90 plays an important role in tumor immune regulation. The combined application of HSP90 inhibitors and immunotherapy drugs is expected to enhance the effectiveness of tumor immunotherapy [147].

The high expression of HSP90 in tumor cells can affect the immune state of the tumor microenvironment. On the one hand, HSP90 is involved in maintaining the stability and function of some immunosuppressive molecules in tumor cells, such as programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). PD-L1 binds to programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) receptor on the surface of T cells, inhibiting their activity and causing tumor cells to escape immune surveillance [21]. Studies have shown that HSP90 interacts with PD-L1, stabilizes the PD-L1 protein, and promotes its expression on the surface of tumor cells [148]. Inhibiting the activity of HSP90 can degrade PD-L1, reduce its expression on the surface of tumor cells, relieve the inhibition of T cells, and enhance the killing effect of T cells on tumor cells [149]. On the other hand, HSP90 also plays a role in the antigen-presentation process. Antigens within tumor cells need to be processed and then presented to T cells via the major histocompatibility complex (MHC). HSP90 is involved in the processing and transportation of antigens, affecting the efficiency of antigen presentation [150]. Inhibiting the activity of HSP90 may alter the antigen-presentation pathway, enhance the immunogenicity of tumor cells, and promote the recognition and killing of tumor cells by T cells.

Based on the aforementioned mechanisms, the combination of HSP90 inhibitors and immunotherapy drugs has shown promising synergistic effects in preclinical studies. HSP90 inhibitors can enhance the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors by promoting the degradation of PD-L1 [151]. For example, in mouse tumor models, after treatment with the HSP90 inhibitor AUY-922, the expression level of PD-L1 in tumor cells decreased. When combined with a PD-L1 inhibitor, it significantly enhanced the inhibitory effect on tumor growth and promoted the infiltration and killing of tumor cells by T cells [152]. In addition, HSP90 inhibitors can also be combined with other immunotherapy drugs, such as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy. The combination of HSP90 inhibitors and CTLA-4 inhibitors may enhance the activation and proliferation of T cells by regulating the activity of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, and synergistically inhibit tumor growth [153].

In clinical practice, the future lies in formulating personalized combination therapy regimens according to patients’ individual characteristics and the molecular typing of tumors. Given that there are differences in gene expression, signaling pathway activation, and sensitivity to therapeutic drugs among the tumor cells of different patients, a one-size-fits-all combination therapy regimen may not benefit all patients [154]. By conducting comprehensive gene sequencing, proteomic analysis, etc., on patients’ tumor tissues, molecular characteristics of patients’ tumors can be obtained. This can be used to predict patients’ responses to different combination therapy regimens, and tailor the most suitable combination therapy regimen for each patient [155]. While combination therapies and HSP90 inhibitors can enhance treatment efficacy, they may increase the risk and severity of adverse events e.g. immune-related reactions. Therefore, rigorous management of adverse reactions and timely optimization of the combination treatment regimen are essential to ensure patients’ tolerability.

Challenges and solutions

Despite progress in the development of HSP90 inhibitors, several challenges remain, and further solutions are needed. A key limiting factor is the tumor heterogeneity, i.e. differences in gene expression, signaling pathway activation, and the degree of dependence on HSP90 among the tumor cells of different patients. This affects the efficacy of HSP90 inhibitors, and may result in insensitivity to HSP90 inhibitors in certain patients [3]. To overcome this issue, further studies on the molecular mechanisms of tumor heterogeneity and precision medicine approaches to screen patient groups sensitive to HSP90 inhibitors are needed. For example, gene sequencing and proteomics can be used to analyze molecular markers of HSP90-related signaling pathways in tumor cells, and models for predicting patients’ responses to HSP90 inhibitors can be employed [156]. For patients who are primarily resistant to HSP90 inhibitors, identification of new combination-therapy targets and development of more effective combination-therapy regimens are critical.

Drug resistance is another major challenge in the clinical application of HSP90 inhibitors. After long-term treatment with HSP90 inhibitors, tumor cells may develop drug resistance through various mechanisms. Tumor cells may upregulate the expression of other chaperone proteins, such as HSP70 and HSP40, to compensate for the functions of HSP90 and maintain protein homeostasis [109]. However, tumor cells may activate other compensatory signaling pathways, bypass the signaling pathways blocked by HSP90 inhibitors, and continue to promote the growth and survival of tumor cells [157]. To overcome drug resistance, researchers are developing new HSP90 inhibitors with enhanced inhibitory activity, selectivity, and reduced drug resistance. Inhibitors that can simultaneously target multiple domains of HSP90 or act synergistically with other key molecules may enhance tumor cell killing. A 2021 study employed a multi-target ligand design strategy, in which molecular fragments important for binding HSP90, B-Raf, and PDHK1 targets were integrated. The resulting compounds were verified through in vitro experiments [158]. Another effective strategy is the combined use of multiple drugs with different mechanisms of action, such as simultaneously inhibiting HSP90 and other drug-resistance-related molecules or signaling pathways. A 2016 study found that the HSP90-Cdc37 system regulates the G1-phase progression by modulating the chaperone state of CDK4/6, affecting its dynamic binding to cyclins and inhibitors. ATP-competitive CDK4/6 inhibitors promote the degradation of CDK4/6 by disrupting its interaction with the HSP90 system, providing a new mechanism for combination-targeted therapy. Therefore, the synergistic application of CDK4/6 inhibitors (such as palbociclib) and HSP90 inhibitors can overcome drug resistance and enhance the therapeutic effects [159]. In clinical practice, rationally formulating treatment plans and adopting methods such as intermittent dosing and dosage adjustment may also help delay the development of drug resistance.

The adverse reactions of HSP90 inhibitors are also issues that require attention. Since HSP90 also has important functions in normal cells, inhibiting its activity may have an adverse effect on normal cells, leading to adverse reactions. To alleviate adverse reactions, further optimization of the structure of HSP90 inhibitors is needed, to improve their selectivity for HSP90 in tumor cells and reduce interference with normal cells: e.g. inhibitors that can specifically target HSP90 subtypes with high expression or specific modifications in tumor cells, or inhibitors with tumor-targeted delivery functions using the differences in the microenvironment and metabolism between tumor cells and normal cells. In a 2024 study, researchers proposed a customized deep-tumor treatment strategy based on multi-channel lanthanide nanocomposites. For the first time, they combined fluorescence-depth assessment with HSP90 inhibition, which significantly enhanced the efficacy of photothermal therapy (PTT) in deep-seated tumors. The hydrogen-bond network design of Ln-ZIF⊂rPMo provided an example for the efficient loading of hydrophilic HSP90 inhibitors [160]. Reasonable dosage adjustment and optimization of the dosing regimen are also crucial. The optimal dosage and dosing interval of HSP90 inhibitors in the human body can be determined by pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies. This ensures anti-tumor efficacy while minimizing the incidence and severity of adverse reactions as much as possible [161]. In addition, the combined use of adjuvant drugs can alleviate adverse reactions, such as anti-emetic and anti-diarrheal drugs for gastrointestinal reactions, and drugs that promote hematopoiesis for hematological toxicity, may be effective ways to improve patients’ tolerance.

In terms of clinical trial design, continuous improvement and refinement are needed. Most current clinical trials are grouped according to traditional tumor types and stages. However, due to tumor heterogeneity, patients with the same tumor type may have very different responses to HSP90 inhibitors. Future clinical trials should focus on patients’ molecular characteristics [12], adopt precision medicine concept, conduct hierarchical analysis based on the molecular markers of HSP90-related signaling pathways in patients’ tumor cells. Screening the patient groups most likely to benefit from HSP90 inhibitor treatment can improve the efficiency and success rate of clinical trials [150]. Moreover, translational research using samples, such as tumor tissues and blood from patients in clinical trials, to study the mechanism of action, drug resistance mechanism of HSP90 inhibitors, as well as predictive biomarkers for efficacy and adverse reactions, will provide powerful guidance for clinical treatment [88].

Although HSP90 inhibitors have shown potential in cancer treatment, the use of pan-HSP90 inhibitors may lead to various adverse reactions, thus limiting their clinical application. These toxicity issues mainly include the heat-shock response, which causes an increase in HSP90 levels, weakens the efficacy of inhibitors and potentially exacerbates tumor cell drug resistance; inhibits the maturation of the human ether-à-go-related gene(hERG)channel, resulting in cardiac and ocular toxicity; and owing to nonselective effects, affects the functions of various normal proteins within cells, leading to a lack of clinical efficacy and triggering other unexpected side effects. To overcome these problems, developing HSP90 subtype-selective inhibitors has emerged as an alternative approach. The HSP90β-selective inhibitors 4 d and 6a developed by Sanket J. Mishra et al. achieve selective inhibition of HSP90β by taking advantage of the subtle structural differences between HSP90α and HSP90β [74]. These inhibitors do not induce a heat-shock response or affect the hERG channel, resulting in a lower toxicity. Future research can further optimize the structures of these compounds to improve their affinity and selectivity. Additionally, by combining computer-aided drug design, high-throughput screening techniques, multi-omics analysis, and precision medicine approaches, patient populations sensitive to HSP90 inhibitors can be screened, to achieve personalized treatment, thereby exploring their potential in clinical applications.

Research prospects of HSP90 inhibitors

Research directions for new HSP90 inhibitors

To overcome the current limitations and improve treatment efficacy, new HSP90 inhibitor development will focus on several key directions: Firstly, the structure of inhibitors can be continuously optimized to enhance their selectivity and affinity for HSP90 (e.g. to specific domains or key amino acid residues). This can be achieved by computer-aided drug design and structure-based drug design based on the three-dimensional structure of HSP90 and the interaction patterns with inhibitors. Additionally, multi-target inhibitors that can simultaneously target multiple functional sites of HSP90 can be developed. Multi-target inhibitors can simultaneously interfere with several key processes, such as binding of HSP90 to ATP, interaction between HSP90 and client proteins, and assembly of HSP90 with co-chaperone proteins. This multi-target approach can more comprehensively inhibit the activity of HSP90 and reduce the likelihood of drug resistance.

In addition, developing selective inhibitors targeting specific HSP90 subtypes or HSP90 with specific modifications in tumor cells is of great significance. There are differences in the expression and modification status of HSP90 subtypes in different types of tumor cells. These differences may affect the sensitivity of tumor cells to HSP90 inhibitors [162]. Designing inhibitors that can specifically target these tumor-related HSP90s can improve the precision of treatment, reduce interference with the functions of HSP90 in normal cells and decrease the occurrence of adverse reactions. Designing a selective inhibitor for phosphorylated HSP90α is expected to achieve precise targeting of tumor cells with significant upregulation of HSP90α with specific phosphorylation [163]. Meanwhile, exploring new mechanisms of action of HSP90 inhibitors and identifying compounds with different modes of action compared to traditional inhibitors may open up new avenues. For instance, developing inhibitors that can influence the chaperone function of HSP90 by regulating its post-translational modifications, such as acetylation and methylation, or small molecules that can interfere with the transcription process of the HSP90 gene to regulate the expression level of HSP90 have been developed [164].

Precision medicine-based individualized treatment

Precision medicine advancements have opened new clinical opportunities for HSP90 inhibitors. Individualized therapy rooted in precision medicine will emerge as a pivotal focus for future research and application of these inhibitors [4]. By conducting multi-omics analyses of patient tumor tissues and blood samples, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, researchers can comprehensively profile the molecular features of tumors. This approach aims to identify biomarkers associated with the HSP90 signaling pathway, thereby predicting patients’ sensitivity and resistance to HSP90 inhibitors. For instance, gene sequencing can be used to detect the mutation status and copy number variations of the HSP90 gene in tumor cells, as well as the expression levels of genes that interact with HSP90. Proteomic techniques, on the other hand, can be applied to analyze the expression and modification status of HSP90, its client proteins, and co-chaperone proteins. These findings can ultimately be used to construct a prediction model that accurately assesses a patient’s suitability for HSP90 inhibitor treatment, as well as the potential treatment response and drug-resistance risk [165, 166]. Based on these biomarkers, patients can be managed in a stratified manner to achieve precise use of HSP90 inhibitors. For patients with high HSP90 expression and specific biomarkers, the use of HSP90 inhibitors for treatment should be prioritized, and the most suitable type of HSP90 inhibitor and combination therapy regimen should be selected according to the characteristics of the biomarkers [143]. For example, for tumor patients with specific HSP90 modification characteristics who rely on HSP90 to maintain the stability of key oncoproteins, inhibitors that can specifically target this modified HSP90, can be combined with targeted drugs against the signaling pathways of related oncoproteins for treatment [138].

During the treatment process, technologies such as liquid biopsy are utilized to dynamically monitor the molecular changes in tumor cells and the levels of HSP90-related biomarkers in patients, and adjust the treatment plan in a timely manner. Liquid biopsy is a non-invasive technology that monitors the dynamic changes in tumors by detecting biomarkers such as tumor cells, tumor DNA, RNA, and proteins in body fluids, such as patients’ blood and urine [167]. Through regular liquid biopsies, real-time monitoring of tumor cell molecular responses to HSP90 inhibitors becomes feasible for tracking changes in HSP90/client protein expression and activation of resistance-related pathways. Upon detecting resistance signs or suboptimal efficacy, prompt treatment adjustments, such as switching HSP90 inhibitor types or modifying combination therapy regimens/dosages, are critical. Clinical research has validated precision- medicine-based individualized strategies to enhance the efficacy of HSP90 inhibitors and patient outcomes. Large-scale trials integrate clinical, molecular, and response data to optimize biomarker screening, refine prediction models, and improve personalized plans, providing robust evidence for broader clinical application of HSP90 inhibitors. Guided by precision medicine, such tailored approaches hold promise for fully unleashing the therapeutic potential of HSP90 inhibitors, boosting the efficacy of cancer treatment, and extending survival of the patients.

Application of nanotechnology in the delivery of HSP90 inhibitors

Nanotechnology has demonstrated great potential in the field of drug delivery, providing a new approach to improve the therapeutic efficacy of HSP90 inhibitors [168]. By encapsulating HSP90 inhibitors in nanocarriers such as nanoparticles, nanomicelles, and liposomes, etc., their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties can be significantly altered [169]. Nanocarriers can enhance the stability of HSP90 inhibitors, reduce the degradation and clearance of the drug in the bloodstream, and prolong the duration of action of the drug [170]. Liposome is a commonly used nanocarrier that is composed of a phospholipid bilayer and possesses excellent biocompatibility and modifiability [171]. Encapsulating HSP90 inhibitors inside liposomes can protect the drugs from degradation by enzymes in the body and recognition by the immune system, enabling the drugs to exist more stably in the bloodstream and thus improving their bioavailability [170]. Moreover, modifying the surface of liposomes by linking targeting molecules (such as antibodies, aptamers, peptides, etc.), can achieve tumor-targeted delivery of HSP90 inhibitors. These targeting molecules can specifically bind to antigens or receptors that are overexpressed on the surface of tumor cells, guiding the nanocarriers to accumulate the drugs precisely at the tumor tissue site, increasing the drug concentration within tumor cells, enhancing the therapeutic effect, and reducing the toxicity and side effects on normal tissues [172]. In breast cancer research, liposomes modified with anti-HER2 antibodies on the surface were constructed to deliver HSP90 inhibitors. This targeted liposome can specifically recognize HER2-positive breast cancer cells and efficiently deliver HSP90 inhibitors into tumor cells, significantly enhancing the killing effect on HER2-positive breast cancer cells [173].

Nanotechnology has enabled the co-delivery of HSP90 inhibitors with other therapeutic drugs, further enhancing the efficacy of combination therapies [174]. Both HSP90 inhibitors and chemotherapeutic drugs can be encapsulated in nanoparticles simultaneously. Through the synergistic effect of the nanocarriers, the two drugs are released simultaneously within tumor cells, exerting their respective therapeutic effects and generating a synergistic effect [175]. A novel multifunctional cerium oxide nanoparticle platform for the diagnosis and effective treatment of NSCLC has been reported. This platform was loaded with doxorubicin and the HSP90 inhibitor ganetespib and led to cell death in 80% of NSCLC cells within 48 h [176]. In addition, nanotechnology can also be used to develop responsive nanocarriers, enabling HSP90 inhibitors to release drugs in response to specific stimuli, such as low pH value, high-concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and temperature changes in the tumor microenvironment [175]. This responsive release property can further improve the precision of drug delivery, enabling drugs to be released efficiently at the tumor tissue site while maintaining a low release level in normal tissues, thus enhancing the therapeutic effect and reducing toxicity and side effects. Recent advances in targeted therapy involve pH-responsive nanoparticles engineered for tumor-selective HSP90 inhibitor delivery. These nanoparticle remain stable in the neutral pH environment of the blood but undergo structural changes under acidic conditions in the tumor microenvironment. This leads to the rapid release of the HSP90 inhibitor, achieving precise targeting of tumor cells [168].

In addition, the combination of HSP90 inhibitors (such as GA) with photothermal nanomaterials has been shown to induce apoptosis of tumor cells at a low temperature (43 °C) and prevent the side effects of high temperatures. Traditional PTT requires heating the tumor locally to > 50 °C to induce necrosis, which also easily damages normal tissues and makes it difficult to heat deep-seated tumors uniformly. Inhibiting HSP90 can reduce the thermotolerance of tumor cells, enable low-temperature PTT (such as at 43 °C), thus improving treatment safety. Nanomaterials can be highly enriched in tumors through the enhanced permeability and retention effects and pH-responsiveness. The pH-responsive 1D-NCPs have shown excellent targeting ability, drug-loading capacity, and biodegradability, providing a new strategy for the clinical application of HSP90 inhibitors [161].

Apart from serving as drug carriers, nanomaterials themselves can exert therapeutic effects by directly interfering with the function of HSP90. For example, AgNPs-G developed by Pedro Félix-Piña et al. was synthesized via a chemical reduction method with a surface enriched with silver (Ag⁰) and hydroxyl groups [177]. AgNPs-G can reduce the stability of HSP90 within breast cancer cells and promote its release into the extracellular environment, thereby inducing immunogenic cell death. This provides a new strategy for nanomaterials to target HSP90 with low toxicity (CC50: 36 µM, normal MCF-10 A cells) and indicates a potential selective advantage.

San A and its analogs exert anti-tumor effects through multiple mechanisms, including the inhibition of HSP90, the inhibition of topoisomerase, and the induction of tumor cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase. These effects have been verified in various preclinical cancer models. Although San A shows promising anti-tumor potential, its pharmacokinetic properties (metabolic stability, water solubility, etc.) are currently the main obstacles to its clinical translation. Research indicates that improving its pharmacokinetic properties is one of the current research priorities. More toxicological studies and preclinical data are still needed to support its entry into clinical trials. In particular, it is necessary to verify its efficacy and safety in the complex tumor microenvironment. As an HSP90 inhibitor, San A may be combined with other targeted drugs or traditional chemotherapy to enhance efficacy and overcome drug resistance. Future research could explore combination therapy regimens involving San A. San A is a lead compound with multi-mechanism of anti-tumor activity, but its clinical application still faces challenges in pharmacokinetics and toxicology. Future research should focus on structural optimization, preclinical verification, and breakthroughs in delivery technology to fully unleash its therapeutic potential.
