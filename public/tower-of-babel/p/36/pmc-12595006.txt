Added value of serum proteins to clinical and ultrasound information in predicting the risk of malignancy in ovarian tumors
Authors: An Coosemans, Jolien Ceusters, Chiara Landolfo, Thaïs Baert, Kainat Jamil, Wouter Froyman, Ruben Heremans, Gitte Thirion, Sandra Claes, Julie Oosterlynck, Roxanne Wouters, Ann Vankerckhoven, Francesca Moro, Floriana Mascilini, Adam Neumann, Anne-Sophie Van Rompuy, Dominique Schols, Jaak Billen, Toon Van Gorp, Ignace Vergote, Tom Bourne, Caroline Van Holsbeke, Valentina Chiappa, Giovanni Scambia, Antonia Testa, Daniela Fischerova, Dirk Timmerman, Ben Van Calster
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAssessment of Different Neoplasias in the Adnexa (ADNEX) is the best-performing model to estimate the risk of malignancy in ovarian tumors. We assessed the incremental value of serum proteins over ADNEX predictors in an exploratory multicenter study. In a cohort of 932 patients with an adnexal mass scheduled for surgery, 33 serum proteins were preoperatively quantified using multiplex high-throughput and electrochemiluminescence immunoassays. Using multivariable logistic regression, ADNEX predictors alone had an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.91 (95% confidence interval, 0.89–0.93) to discriminate benign from malignant tumors. This AUC increased most after adding HE4 (+0.03) or CA125 (+0.02). CA72.4 increased AUCs to discriminate secondary metastatic tumors from benign (+0.03), borderline (+0.03), and stage I tumors (+0.03). CA15.3 increased AUCs to discriminate borderline tumors from stage I (+0.01) and stage II–IV tumors (+0.03). While CA125 and HE4 had the highest added value over ADNEX predictors, CA72.4 and CA15.3 may improve discrimination between malignant subtypes.Subject areas: Oncology

Introduction

It has been reported that approximately 1.1% of women will be diagnosed with ovarian cancer during their lifetime.1 The accuracy of diagnostic methods is crucial, as it directly influences clinical decision making and patient management strategies. In the majority of cases, a benign ovarian cyst does not require intervention. In contrast, stage I ovarian cancer necessitates a different management approach compared to advanced-stage disease or borderline tumors. Additionally, the treatment strategy for ovarian metastases differs from that of primary ovarian cancer.2,3,4

The International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) group has optimized the diagnostic potential of transvaginal ultrasound by developing the Assessment of Different Neoplasias in the Adnexa (ADNEX) risk prediction model.5 ADNEX estimates the risk of five tumor types: benign, borderline, stage I primary invasive, stage II–IV primary invasive, and secondary invasive (metastasis to the ovary) tumors. ADNEX has an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.93 to discriminate between benign and any type of malignant tumors in patients scheduled for surgery or expectant management.6,7 The protein biomarker cancer antigen 125 (CA125) is an optional predictor in ADNEX. Using the version with CA125 mainly improves the discrimination of stage II–IV invasive tumors from stage I invasive and secondary metastatic tumors.5,7 Nevertheless, the AUC between some malignant subtypes is around 0.7–0.8: a meta-analysis reported AUCs of 0.72 for borderline vs. stage I primary invasive tumors and 0.78–0.82 for discrimination between invasive subtypes.7 Next to CA125, human epididymis protein 4 (HE4) is also often used. Based on a recent systematic review, however, HE4 does not outperform CA125 to discriminate benign from malignant tumors.8

Biomarker research, however, does not need to focus solely on the tumor level. Recently, Holder et al. nicely illustrated the different methods and different foci of biomarker research in cancer.9 Focusing on host factors or the immune system are two promising avenues. Over the past two decades, it has become clear that the immune system is an important factor in the onset and progression of cancer.10 Unlike traditional biomarkers that focus solely on tumor-derived proteins, immune-based biomarkers capture the interactions between tumor cells, the tumor microenvironment, and the host immune response.11 These interactions are vital in ovarian cancer progression. Until now, the focus of ovarian cancer immune biology has been on the adaptive immune system. The cluster of differentiation-8 (CD8+) T cells at diagnosis are positively associated with prognosis, while regulatory T cells are negatively associated.12,13 However, more and more reports have now indicated that the immune system is much more complex in ovarian cancer and that the importance of the innate immune system cannot be underestimated.14,15 Our previous work suggested that the immune system, both at the cellular and the protein levels, may be used as a biomarker to discriminate benign from malignant ovarian tumors.16,17

The goal of this study was to evaluate whether a combination of ultrasound and protein measurements (related either to the cancer or to the tumor immune system) could result in a further improvement of the performance of the ADNEX model.

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 1,090 patients were enrolled in the study. After applying the exclusion criteria, the final cohort consisted of 932 patients with an ovarian mass, collected at six centers (Figure S1). Among these patients, 474 (51%) had a benign and 458 (49%) had a malignant adnexal tumor. Among the malignant tumors, there were 135 (14%) borderline tumors, 84 (9%) stage I primary invasive tumors, 208 (22%) stage II–IV primary invasive tumors, and 31 (3%) secondary metastatic tumors. The patients had a median age of 54 years (interquartile range [IQR], 42–64; range, 18–88), and 58% of the patients were postmenopausal (Table 1).

Protein characteristics

For the analysis, we excluded eight of the 33 investigated proteins. Four proteins were excluded because a large majority of the samples were at or below the lower limit of detection (IL-12p70, IL17F, MCP-1, and galectin-3) (Figure S2). The other four proteins were omitted because these proteins were not measured from the start (the Procartaplex composition was changed by Life Technologies during the analysis of the study), resulting in a large number of missing values (MMP1, MMP8, MMP12, and MMP13). CA125, HE4, CA15.3, and CA72.4 were the best to discriminate stage II–IV primary invasive from benign tumors, with AUCs between 0.85 (CA72.4) and 0.96 (HE4) (Table 2; Figure S3). CA125 (0.78), HE4 (0.78), and CA72.4 (0.72) also had AUCs above 0.7 to discriminate stage I primary invasive from benign tumors. Adding proteins to clinical and ultrasound information to predict malignancy

The AUC of the seven ADNEX variables was 0.91 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.89–0.93) to discriminate between benign and malignant tumors (Table 4; Figure S6). When proteins were individually added to this model, the highest improvement in AUC was observed for HE4 (AUC, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.92–0.95), CA125 (0.93; 95% CI, 0.91–0.94), CA72.4 (0.92; 95% CI, 0.91–0.94), and CA15.3 (0.92; 95% CI, 0.90–0.93) (Table 4). The results for the likelihood ratio test are shown in Table S7. Based on lasso, seven proteins (HE4, CA72.4, CA125, IL6, Eotaxin2, Arginase, and sFLT1) were selected on top of the ADNEX variables, resulting in an AUC of 0.93 (95% CI, 0.93–0.96). The selection of these proteins was not consistent for the different bootstrap samples, except for HE4, CA125, and CA72.4 (Figure S7). The prespecified combination of HE4 and CA125 resulted in an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.93–0.95) when added to the ADNEX variables.

Adding proteins to clinical and ultrasound information to predict subtypes of malignancy

For the multiclass discrimination, the ADNEX variables had a polytomous discrimination index (PDI) of 0.53 (95% CI, 0.51–0.59) (Table 4; Figure S6). The highest improvement in PDI was observed when adding CA125 (0.58; 95% CI, 0.56–0.65), HE4 (0.57; 95% CI, 0.55–0.64), CA15.3 (0.56; 95% CI, 0.54–0.62), CA72.4 (0.55; 95% CI, 0.53–0.61), and CEA (0.55; 95% CI 0.52–0.60) (Table 4; Figure S6). The results for the likelihood ratio test are shown in Table S7. Based on lasso, four proteins (HE4, CA72.4, CA125, and CA15.3) were selected on top of the ADNEX variables, with a PDI of 0.56 (95% CI, 0.54–0.69). These proteins were selected with high certainty (Figure S8). The prespecified combination of HE4 and CA125 resulted in a PDI of 0.59 (95% CI, 0.56–0.65) when added to ADNEX variables.

The analysis of pairwise AUCs indicated that the discrimination between stage II–IV primary invasive and secondary metastatic tumors benefited most from adding proteins to ADNEX (AUC increases up to 0.17). The discrimination between benign and borderline tumors (increases at most 0.01) and between borderline and stage I primary invasive tumors (increases at most 0.02) benefited least (Table 5). CA125 was particularly interesting to discriminate stage II–IV primary invasive from other tumor types (AUC increases ranged between 0.01 and 0.16). HE4 was of most interest to discriminate benign from other tumor types (increases between 0.01 and 0.03) and stage II–IV primary invasive from other malignant tumor types (increases 0.03–0.13). CA72.4 had among the highest increases to discriminate secondary metastatic from other tumor types except stage II–IV primary invasive (decreases 0.03). CA15.3 had good results to discriminate borderline from stage I and stage II–IV primary invasive tumors (increases 0.02–0.03). Macrophage-derived chemokine (MDC) resulted in the highest increase (0.02) to discriminate borderline from stage I primary invasive tumors, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), in the highest increase (0.04) for stage I primary invasive vs. secondary metastatic tumors.

Discussion

Our study supports the status of CA125 as the best serum protein to discriminate benign from malignant ovarian tumors, with an AUC of 0.84. However, when adding biomarkers to the ADNEX version without CA125 (i.e., only based on clinical and ultrasound parameters), HE4 increased the AUC more than CA125. For discrimination between the five major tumor types, adding CA125 had the highest increase in PDI, followed by HE4. These two markers appeared particularly useful to identify stage II–IV primary invasive tumors. CA72.4 was promising as a biomarker to improve discrimination between secondary metastatic and non-invasive or early-stage tumors, and CA15.3 and MDC, as a biomarker to improve discrimination between borderline and primary invasive tumors.

Our results indicate that the preoperative discrimination of a borderline tumor vs. a stage I ovarian cancer remains difficult, with univariable AUCs of at most 0.67 for all proteins. Nevertheless, subclassification of the masses could have a major clinical impact, since the outcome differs for patients in each group. It is important to note that the final pathology ultimately determines the definitive diagnosis. Given that our results for borderline tumors and stage I ovarian cancer are based on a smaller cohort, further studies involving larger cohorts and potentially more specific proteins may enhance preoperative discrimination for these more challenging tumor types. Moreover, patients with these tumors are often young women with a desire to retain their fertility, such that the choice between a more extensive (stage I invasive) and a more conservative (borderline tumor) surgical intervention is vital. However, when considering the specific histopathology of these tumors, some proteins emerged as potential biomarkers to differentiate serous borderline tumors from serous epithelial stage I primary invasive tumors. These findings could be due to chance, but perhaps also to the different biological and immunological behavior of these tumors. The ADNEX variables alone resulted in an AUC of 0.70 to discriminate borderline from stage I primary invasive tumors. Addition of MDC or CA15.3 increased the AUC to 0.72, which is still suboptimal. Nevertheless, there is a biological rationale for MDC as a possible candidate biomarker for these two malignancy subtypes. MDC is being secreted by pro-tumoral macrophages, leading to the recruitment of regulatory T cells and T helper 2 cells, increasing the immune suppressive microenvironment.18,19 It can be expected that its expression is different between an invasive and a non-invasive neoplasm.

Several studies have explored blood-based biomarkers to improve the diagnosis of ovarian cancer. Palmer et al.20 identified CA125 (MUC16), HE4 (WFDC2), mesothelin (MSLN), and metalloproteinase-7 (MMP7) as promising candidates for early detection.20 Similar to our study design, they included symptomatic women for serum collection and reported variations in biomarker performance across different histological subtypes. However, our study further assessed the sensitivity and specificity of two well-established serum biomarkers, CA125 and HE4, stratified according to menopausal status, thereby offering deeper insight into their diagnostic accuracy within both pre- and postmenopausal populations. Likewise, Mor et al.21 developed a four-analyte test, including leptin, prolactin, osteopontin, and insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF-2), achieving 95% sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing ovarian cancer from healthy controls, though further validation in larger and diverse populations is needed.21 Mari et al.22 reviewed emerging liquid biopsy technologies, such as circulating tumor cells, nucleic acids, and extracellular vesicles, for their potential use in screening and treatment monitoring, highlighting their potential to enhance early detection compared to traditional serum proteins, while emphasizing the need for prospective cohort studies to validate these biomarkers.22 Furthermore, Husseinzadeh23 noted that combining novel markers with CA125 generally improves diagnostic accuracy, yet the desired sensitivity and specificity for effective diagnosis and monitoring have not been fully achieved, suggesting that further improvements are necessary.23 This could be interpreted as a need for additional strategies, possibly integrating other clinical features.

Pils et al.24 demonstrated the added value of integrating gene expression profiles with serum protein panels, highlighting biomarkers such as CA125, leptin, prolactin, osteopontin, IGF2, and macrophage migration inhibitory factor as contributors to enhanced diagnostic performance.24 Their retrospective analysis was based on a smaller cohort of malignant cases across Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics stages I–IV, whereas our prospective study includes a larger, more diverse cohort, encompassing malignant cases, borderline tumors, and ovarian metastases, better reflecting clinical heterogeneity. Both studies emphasize combining multiple biomarkers to improve diagnostic accuracy; however, we further integrated clinical and ultrasound features to add diagnostic value. Furthermore, Mukama et al.25 identified nine serum protein biomarkers, including CA125 and HE4, with good discrimination for detecting ovarian cancer within nine months of diagnosis, though diagnostic effectiveness declined significantly for samples collected earlier and none of the additional markers significantly improved detection beyond CA125.25 More recently, a comprehensive review by Ghose et al. (2024) not only highlighted research efforts beyond CA125 and HE4, noting potential benefits of combining these markers with others such as folate receptor alpha (FOLR1), kallikrein-related peptidase 11 (KLK11), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) to improve accuracy, but also underscored significant barriers to clinical implementation, including the need for extensive validation, integration into diagnostic frameworks, cost-effectiveness, accessibility, and regulatory approval.26 Despite considerable research on blood-based biomarkers, significant improvements in ovarian cancer diagnosis remain limited.

Unlike previous studies focusing solely on biomarker performance, our approach integrates protein markers with established clinical and imaging predictors to determine whether they improve diagnostic accuracy beyond current methods. In this context, we evaluated 33 serum proteins but highlighted four biomarkers, CA125, HE4, CA72.4, and CA15.3, that showed potential for improving discrimination among various ovarian tumor subtypes. Notably, while earlier research has largely emphasized binary classification (e.g., benign vs. malignant or healthy vs. malignant),20,21,24,25 our study is, to our knowledge, the first to evaluate a panel of serum biomarkers on top of the ADNEX model across a spectrum of malignant subtypes, including borderline ovarian tumors, stage I, stage III–IV, as well as metastatic tumors to the ovary. This multidimensional approach offers a more comprehensive assessment and may better support individualized risk stratification in the diagnostic pathway.

While the search for biomarkers to differentiate between types of ovarian tumors is important, we realize that the ADNEX variables (without CA125) already have a high ability to discriminate between benign and malignant tumors. This means that the room for improvement beyond these variables is limited. Nevertheless, this is partly an ethical and economical debate that goes beyond the scope of this study. All biomarkers come with a cost, are not accessible in all hospitals and certainly not in low-resource countries.

In conclusion, CA125 and HE4 are the most important biomarkers to diagnose ovarian tumors, either univariably or as addition to established clinical and ultrasound variables. Biomarkers that merit further study are CA72.4 and CA15.3, as these may be of use to improve the discrimination between malignant subtypes. The discrimination between borderline and stage I primary invasive tumors is not strongly improved by adding proteins and remains a difficult yet important clinical problem.

Limitations of the study

This is by far the largest study on (immune-related) biomarkers in combination with ultrasound for ovarian tumors. Our study also has some limitations. First, measuring proteins are snapshots and do not provide any functional information. Second, to avoid interference, we excluded patients with a prior history of cancer and immune-related diseases. While this refers to a small fraction of patients, they also need an appropriate diagnostic workup in daily clinical practice. Third, most of the patients have been recruited in centers with a high prevalence of malignancy. This increases the number of malignancies, which reduces uncertainty in the analysis for rarer subtypes (borderline, stage I primary invasive, secondary metastatic). Nevertheless, this tends to result in an over-representation of high-risk patients. Despite this, the number of secondary metastatic tumors (n = 31) remained modest. Given the number of proteins evaluated and the low or modest amount of patients with borderline, stage I primary invasive, or secondary metastatic tumors, we labeled our study as exploratory. Fourth, the clinical and ultrasound variables of ADNEX already have an AUC above 0.9 to discriminate between benign and malignant tumors. This makes it difficult to improve the performance further by adding new variables.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and participants

In this exploratory prospective cohort study, we recruited 932 female patients who presented with an adnexal (i.e. ovarian/paraovarian, tubal/paratubal) tumor. They were consecutively enrolled at six hospitals between June 2015 and December 2019: the University Hospitals Leuven (Leuven, Belgium), Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore (Rome, Italy), General Faculty Hospital of the Charles University (Prague, Czech Republic), Queen Charlotte’s and Chelsea Hospital (London, UK), Ziekenhuis Oost-Limburg (Genk, Belgium), and Istituto Nazionale dei Tumore (Milan, Italy). The Research Ethics Committee of the University Hospitals Leuven acted as central ethics committee and approved the study (S51375, S59207). Ethics approval was also obtained at all other local ethics committees. Written informed consent was required before recruitment. All patients underwent a preoperative standardized transvaginal ultrasound scan according to the IOTA methodology27 in the framework of IOTA5 (NCT01698632) and IOTA7 (NCT02847832) studies. All patients were selected for surgery by the managing clinician. Exclusion criteria were the following: (1) age less than 18 years old, (2) pregnancy, (3) refusal of preoperative transvaginal ultrasonography and/or blood sample, (4) known simultaneous and/or previous malignancies within five years, (5) simultaneous autoimmune disease and/or treatment with immune-modulating drugs, (6) infectious serology (i.e. human immunodeficiency virus [HIV], Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C), (7) denial or withdrawal of the written informed consent, and (8) surgery more than 120 days after the ultrasound examination. Data cleaning was performed by a team of biostatisticians and ultrasound examiners. Data cleaning included sending queries to participating hospitals to retrieve missing information or to correct inconsistencies. We report the study according to the Reporting Recommendations for Tumor Marker Prognostic Studies (REMARK) guideline.28 Of the patient samples collected in Leuven, 254 of them were used in a biomarker paper, published earlier by our group in 2020.17

Method details

Serum samples

Serum from a blood sample (BD Vacutainer SST II advance (ref 367953)), taken before surgery from each patient, was isolated following a standardized protocol. Within 3 days of collection, the sample was centrifuged at 800 rcf (acceleration 9 and deceleration 9) for 10 minutes at 20°C. The supernatant (= serum) was collected in non-sterile cryovials (VWR 720-1003) and stored in aliquots at -80°C. Samples were shipped to the Laboratory for Tumor Immunology and Immunotherapy at KU Leuven, where the assays were performed. After thawing, aliquots were used for analysis (i.e. defrosted samples were not refrozen for additional analyses).

Protein analysis

Circulating levels of 33 proteins were evaluated (see Table 6). Proteins were measured with Luminex® assay and electrochemiluminescence immuno-assay (ECLIA), according to the manufacturers’ instructions, as previously described by our group.17 For Luminex technology, assays were performed using aliquots of 150 μL of serum. Customized Procartaplex™ Immunoassay kits were purchased (Life Technologies, Merelbeke, Belgium) to determine 23 proteins. 96 well plates were loaded with antigen-specific capture antibody-coated magnetic beads. Next, samples, standards and blanks were added and incubated for two hours together with the beads. The High Sensitivity IL-10 plex required an overnight incubation. Biotinylated detection antibodies were added as a second step. The antibody/antigen complex was then visualized by incubation with streptavidin-conjugated R-phycoerythrin. In between all steps of the protocol, a washing procedure was performed by means of a magnetic plate washer. Read-out was performed with Bio-plex 200 system of Bio-Rad (Hercules, California, USA). Concentrations of the proteins were determined using five parameter log curves generated by the Bio-Plex Manager 4.1.1 software.

For ECLIA (Roche Diagnostics), 500 μL of serum was required. Kits were obtained from Roche Diagnostics to determine ten proteins. Biotinylated antibodies and monoclonal specific antibodies labeled with a ruthenium complex formed a sandwich complex. Next, streptavidin-coated paramagnetic microparticles were added. Chemiluminescence was then induced by applying a voltage and measured with a photomultiplier (Cobas E601 analyzer, Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). Results were determined via a calibration curve which is instrument-specifically generated by 2-point calibration and a master curve provided via the reagent barcode. Protein quantification was blinded to histology and clinical/ultrasound measurements.

Reference standard

The reference standard was the histopathological diagnosis by the local pathologist after surgical removal of the adnexal mass. Tumors were classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors and malignant lesions were staged according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) criteria.29 The pathologist worked according to local protocols, so it is possible that they were aware of the subjective assessment by the ultrasound examiner. Pathologists were blinded to protein quantifications. In this study, borderline tumors were considered malignant due to their biological behavior and clinical risks (such as recurrence and progression to invasive carcinoma).30,31 Although borderline tumors are not invasive cancers, certain types (e.g., serous borderline tumors) exhibit atypical cellular features, the potential for metastasis, and the risk of progression to invasive carcinoma.

Quantification and statistical analysis

ADNEX model

The ADNEX model is a multinomial logistic regression model based on three clinical predictors (age, serum CA125, type of center) and six ultrasound predictors (maximal diameter of the lesion, proportion of solid tissue, more than ten cyst locules, number of papillary projections, acoustic shadowing, and presence of ascites).5 Type of center refers to oncology referral units vs other units. In this study, only one center (Genk, Belgium) was not an oncology center. ADNEX estimates the risk of five tumor types: benign, borderline, stage I and stage II-IV primary invasive ovarian cancer, and secondary metastasis. One minus the probability of a benign tumor equals the estimated risk of malignancy. By design, clinical and ultrasound measurements were blinded to histology and protein quantifications.

Statistical analysis

The 33 proteins considered as parameters were log-transformed (log with base 2) for all statistical analyses. Missing values (due to technical issues and/or insufficient serum sample) and values out of range were addressed using single stochastic imputation based on ‘multivariate imputation by chained equations’ (mice).32 The protein levels were imputed using predictive mean matching regression with the mice package in R. Imputation was based on the predictors in the ADNEX model (except type of center), all protein measurements, and outcome.

The unadjusted discriminative ability of each protein was calculated using univariable AUCs, with 95% confidence interval (CI) based on the logit transform method.33 AUCs were calculated to discriminate between benign masses and all malignant tumors, as well as to discriminate between the five tumor types: benign, borderline, stage I invasive, stage II-IV invasive, and secondary metastasis. For the calculation of the AUC, the function auc.nonpara.mw from the R-package auRoc was used.

To investigate the added diagnostic value of the proteins for predicting the risk of malignancy, we first fitted a binary logistic regression model of the outcome (benign vs any type of malignancy) on seven clinical and ultrasound predictors from the ADNEX model, using logistf in R: age, maximum diameter of lesion, proportion of solid tissues, presence of more than 10 cyst locules, number of papillary projections, presence of acoustic shadows, and presence of ascites. Type of center was not used because all but one centers were oncology referral centers and contributed 92% of patients. Then, we fitted similar models with the seven ADNEX predictors plus (1) each protein separately, (2) CA125 and HE4, and (3) a set of proteins based on lasso (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) selection. The lasso selection was based on a model with the seven ADNEX predictors and all proteins in which a lasso-penalty was applied to the proteins but not to the ADNEX predictors.34,35 This analyses was performed using the function cv.glmnet from the R-package glmnet. For all these models, the AUC was calculated and the added value of proteins on top of the ADNEX variables was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test. We report p-values to measure the strength of the evidence against the null hypothesis of no added value. However, we do not specify a significance level and hence do not determine statistical significance. To investigate the added value for predicting the risk of malignant subtypes, similar analyses were performed using multinomial logistic regression for the five tumor types. This model is fitted using the brmultinom function from the R-package brglm2. For the multinomial models, the Polytomous Discrimination Index (PDI) was calculated as a multiclass AUC extension, as well as pairwise AUCs using the conditional risk method.36,37 Likelihood ratio tests were performed to evaluate the added value of proteins. For multivariable analyses, we used bootstrapping with 200 resamples to correct AUCs and PDIs for optimism. All regression models included Firth’s correction.38

The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) for CA125 and HE4 at common cut-offs (35 U/mL for CA125 and 70 pmol/L for HE4 in premenopausal patients and 140 pmol/L in postmenopausal patients8) were calculated for each center. Overall values were based on a random-effects bivariate logit-normal model, using the reitsma function of the mada-package. Also, an overall estimate while ignoring the multicenter nature of the data was calculated, using 95% CI based on Wilson’s score method.39

A post-hoc subgroup analyses based on menopausal status was performed for the univariable AUCs. These calculations focus on the comparison of benign masses vs all malignant tumors.

Two sensitivity analyses were performed for the univariable AUCs: (1) by using meta-analysis of center-specific AUCs, and (2) after using multiple imputations (100 imputations) for the missing values. For the meta-analyses, center-specific values of logit(AUC) and its standard error were calculated using the auc.nonpara.mw function from the package auRoc. The overall AUC was obtained by combining these center-specific results using a random effects meta-analysis with the function rma.uni from the package metafor. For the second sensitivity analyses, 100 imputed datasets were created using predictive mean matching regression with the mice package in R. Logit(AUC) and its standard error were calculated for each imputed dataset. The results were combined using Rubin’s rule, and then the point estimate and the confidence limits were back-transformed to the original scale.

We followed a prespecified statistical analysis plan for this study. Methods S1 presents the sample size determination. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.1.0, using packages mice, auRoc, logistf, glmnet, brglm2, mada and metafor.
