Effects of apelin on neonatal brain neurogenesis in L-NAME-induced maternal preeclampsia
Authors: Canberk Tomruk, Cansın Şirin Tomruk, Burcu Denizlioğlu, Murat Olukman, Gülinnaz Ercan, Soner Duman, Timur Köse, Emel Öykü Çetin Uyanıkgil, Yiğit Uyanıkgil, Ayşegül Uysal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to investigate the possible protective effects of apelin, which is known to have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, on changes in neurogenesis in newborns of pregnant rats with L-NAME-induced preeclampsia. Wistar albino female rats were divided into four experimental groups: Control, Apelin, Preeclampsia and Preeclampsia + Apelin. Blood pressure was measured on the 5th, 11th and 17th days of gestation, urine protein was analyzed from urine samples collected for 24 h on the 6th, 12th and 18th days and serum creatinine was analyzed from serum samples. Maternal kidney and placenta tissues were obtained to establish the preeclampsia model, and neonatal brain tissues including the cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum regions were obtained to investigate neurogenesis and examined by histological and immunohistochemical methods. The number of newborns, body weight and brain weight of the newborns were measured. eNOS, IL-10, nNOS and NO levels in the brain analyzed via ELISA. Mean arterial pressure, urine protein and serum creatinine increased in the preeclampsia. Newborn weight decreased in the Preeclampsia group, the values in the Preeclampsia + Apelin group were closer to the Control and Apelin groups. In the Preeclampsia group, edema and dilatation in the proximal and distal tubules of kidneys, perivillous fibrin deposition and increase in syncytial nodules of placenta were observed. VEGF immunoreactivity decreased and iNOS immunoreactivity increased in both kidney and placenta. In neonatal brain tissue examinations, cytotoxic edema accompanied by thinning of cortex, delayed migration and lower cell counts in the hippocampus, and increase in intercellular spaces and EGL thickening in the cerebellum were observed in the preeclampsia. Expression of NeuN, GFAP, MBP, IL-10, eNOS, nNOS and NO levels decreased, whereas expression of Iba-1 increased in the preeclampsia. In the Preeclampsia + Apelin group, these findings were similar to the Control and Apelin groups. Apelin administration was found to be beneficial for preventing the adverse consequences of preeclampsia, but further experimental and clinical studies are needed to better understand these effects.Keywords: Preeclampsia, Apelin, NeurogenesisSubject terms: Disease model, Intrauterine growth, Neurogenesis

Introduction

Preeclampsia, a pregnancy-specific pathology, is defined by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) as the presence of hypertension and proteinuria occurring after 20 weeks of gestation in a previously normotensive patient1. It is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality for pregnant women and fetuses and affects 2–8% of pregnancies worldwide. Major risk factors for the development of preeclampsia include a history of chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes mellitus, antiphospholipid syndrome and obesity2.

One of the leading hypotheses for the development of preeclampsia is placental dysfunction due to impaired remodeling of spiral arteries in early pregnancy3. In spiral arteries that cannot physiologically adapt to pregnancy, pathological changes such as foamy cells and fibrinoid necrosis in the vessel wall, called decidual vasculopathy; perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration, thrombosis and tunica media hypertrophy, which may accompany them, are observed4. Placental insufficiency leads to fetal growth restriction and neurogenesis is also affected5,6.

Nitric oxide (NO) is a potent vasodilator signaling molecule, and an increase in endothelium-derived NO during pregnancy contributes to increased uteroplacental flow and decreased total vascular resistance. Disruptions in NO-related pathways and polymorphisms in nitric oxide synthase (NOS) have been reported in women with preeclampsia7,8. In pregnant mice and rats, administration of the NOS inhibitor Nω-Nitro-L-arginine methyl ester hydrochloride (L-NAME) increases vascular resistance by peripheral vasoconstriction and causes an increase in blood pressure, resulting in preeclampsia-like symptoms9,10.

Apelin, a propeptide hormone first isolated from the bovine stomach, is converted by aminopeptidases, serine proteases and carboxypeptidases into shorter and more active forms such as Apelin-12, Apelin-13, Apelin-17 and Apelin-3611. Apelin-13 and its posttranslational modification pyroglutamate apelin-13 [(Pyr1)-apelin-13] are more stable and bioactive forms of apelin and are the most common forms of apelin in human plasma, heart and placenta11,12. Moreover, plasma and placental chorionic villi apelin levels are decreased in preeclampsia12,13.

Apelin is involved in the regulation of oxidative stress in various tissues. In myocardial cells, it reduces ischemia–reperfusion injury by reducing mitochondrial oxidative damage and inhibiting lipid peroxidation14. Apelin-13 administration improves renal function after ischemia–reperfusion injury by increasing antioxidant enzyme activity in the kidney in a dose-dependent manner15. The effects of apelin on oxidative stress and inflammation in brain ischemia–reperfusion have also been investigated16. The apelin receptor protein, apelin mRNA and protein are widely distributed in neuronal cell bodies and fibers and play important roles in signaling pathways in these cells17. It has been shown that apelin administration activates the AMPK/GSK-3β/Nrf2 pathway in brain tissue, has anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects and protects against ischemic stroke18,19. Yang et al.20 also showed that Apelin-13 has protective effects on brain ischemia–reperfusion by activating the PI3K/Akt and ERK1/2 signaling pathways in mice.

In this experimental study, the role of apelin in neurogenesis in newborns of preeclamptic pregnant rats was investigated for the first time.

Methods

Experimental design

32 Wistar Albino female rats weighing 200–250 g, 8–10 weeks old, and their newborns produced at the Ege University Laboratory Animals Application and Research Centre were used. Female rats were housed in single cages in a room with a temperature of 24 ± 1 °C and a 12-h light–dark cycle (lights on at 07.00 a.m.) and were fed a standard laboratory diet and water and food were provided ad libitum. Vaginal smears taken daily for the determination of estrous cycles were spread on a slide, fixed with methanol and stained with 5% Giemsa stain (Merck, New York, USA). The rats that were left to mate on the night of the proestrus/oestrus period and whose sperm cells were observed in vaginal plaque or vaginal smear the next morning were considered pregnant. Pregnant rats were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 8). Control pregnant group (Control): Saline (1 ml/day, i.p.) was administered starting on gestational day (GD) 9; Apelin-treated pregnant group (Apelin): Apelin (60 nmol/kg, i.p., b.i.d.) was administered starting on GD 13; Preeclamptic pregnant group (PE): L-NAME (250 mg/kg/day, i.p.) was administered starting on GD 9; Preeclamptic pregnant rats treated with apelin (PE + Apelin): L-NAME (250 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered starting on GD 9 and apelin (60 nmol/kg, i.p., b.i.d.) was administered starting on GD 13. Treatments were administered starting from the relevant day until the end of pregnancy (day 21). L-NAME (N5751, Sigma, Missouri, USA) was dissolved in saline at 50 mg/ml, and a stock solution was prepared. Apelin-13 ([Pyr1]-Apelin-13 trifluoroacetate salt) (SML2084, Sigma, Missouri, USA) was dissolved in saline at a concentration of 1 mg/ml, after which a stock solution was prepared. Since the half-life of apelin is short, which is 16 min when administered intraperitoneally in rats21, it was administered twice a day (b.i.d.) at 09.00 a.m. and 05.00 p.m. to observe optimum efficacy based on similar studies in the literature22–24. All injections were administered in equal volume (1 ml). In the day of the euthanasia, the animals received 80 mg/kg ketamine (VetaKetam, Vet-Agro, Lublin, Poland)-10 mg/kg xylazine (VetaXyl, Vet-Agro, Lublin, Poland) combined anaesthesia was administered i.p. to pregnant rats and their newborns. This study was carried out with the ethical approval of Ege University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Approval No.: 2020-018) and was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ARRIVE guideline.

Blood pressure measurement

Tail blood pressure was measured (NIBP200A Blood Pressure System, BIOPAC Systems, Goleta, USA) on GDs 5, 11 and 17 to confirm the preeclampsia model. The pregnant rat was placed in a stabilizer, and the tail was cleaned with distilled water. The cuff sensor was attached to the tail by placing it in the heater unit previously brought to 34 °C. The heater was covered with a green surgical drape and the subject was allowed to calm down in a quiet, warm and dark environment for approximately 30 min. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were recorded using Biopac Student Lab Pro 3.7.0 software (BIOPAC Systems, Goleta, USA). At each measurement, 3 consecutive measurements were made and averaged, and the mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated.

Urine collection

In order to demonstrate proteinuria in preeclampsia, urine samples were collected for 24 h on GDs 6, 12 and 18 using a metabolic cage, and after filtering with gauze to remove feed crumbs, the samples were collected in 1.5 ml tubes and stored at −20 °C for biochemical analyses.

Tissue samples

Pregnant rats were anesthetized, the abdomen was opened, and the kidneys were removed. While one of the kidneys is reserved to be used for biochemical analysis, the other kidney was placed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (1.04.005.1000, Merck, New York, USA) fixative for histological analyses. Since dam rats exhibit placentophagy after birth, to examine the effects of drug administration on the placenta, two of the pregnant rats in each group were subjected to cesarean section on the last day of pregnancy (GD 21), and placenta tissues were obtained; the other pregnant rats were left to undergo normal delivery. Half of the placentas were stored at −80 °C for biochemical analyses in the same way as the kidneys were, while the other half were placed in 4% PFA fixative. In addition, intracardiac blood was collected from the dam rats, and plasma and hemolysates were prepared on the same day and stored at −80 °C for biochemical analyses.

Neonates were weighed before anesthesia, and after anesthesia, the brain tissues of half of the neonates at each birth were washed with saline, weighed and stored at −80 °C for biochemical examination. In order to use the brain tissues of the other neonates for histological examinations, intracardiac perfusion was performed, and the brain tissues were removed and placed in 4% PFA. Sampling was done by using 1 newborn from each mother for histological and biochemical analyses.

Histological tissue processing

The tissues were placed in 4% PFA fixative, and a routine histological tissue processing protocol was applied. After fixation, the tissues were removed from the fixative by incubation in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 24 h, passed through an ascending alcohol series, cleared with xylol (28,973.328, VWr Chemicals, Pennsylvania, USA) and embedded in paraffin (39,601,006, Leica, Illionis, USA). Using a microtome (RM 2145, Leica, Illinois, USA), 5 μm sections were taken on polylysine slides (165014, Patolab, Istanbul, Turkey), and Haematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) (CS70930-2, Dako, California, USA) staining was performed after deparaffinisation with xylol.

Immunohistochemical analyses

It was aimed to confirm the development of the experimental model by detecting the changes in inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which are involved in the mechanisms of histopathology developing in the kidney and placenta in preeclampsia, and these primary antibodies were selected to examine the ameliorative effects of apelin application on preeclampsia. Neuronal nuclei (NeuN) as a marker of mature neurons, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) as a marker of radial glial cells and astrocytes, ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule-1 (Iba-1) as a marker of activated microglia and myelin basic protein (MBP) as a marker of maturing oligodendrocytes are the primary antibodies selected to study neurogenesis.

The sections were washed in distilled water after passed through graded alcohol and incubated in sodium citrate for 30 min in microwave for antigen retrieval. After washing in distilled water, the sections were incubated in 1% Triton X solution for 5 min, and 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min and washed 3 times with PBS. After 30 min of serum blocking (sc-2018, Santa Cruz, Texas, USA), kidney and placenta sections were treated with Anti-iNOS (BS-2072R, Bioss, Massachusetts, USA) and Anti-VEGF (sc-152, Santa Cruz, Texas, USA) and brain sections were incubated with Anti-MBP (BS-0380R, Bioss, Massachusetts, USA), Anti-Iba-1 (BS-1363R, Bioss, Massachusetts, USA), Anti-NeuN (BS-1613R, Bioss, Massachusetts, USA), Anti-GFAP (sc-33673, Santa Cruz, Texas, USA) primary antibodies (all antibodies at 1:100 dilution) overnight at +4 °C. Then, the sections were washed 3 times with PBS, incubated with HRP (sc-2018, Santa Cruz, Texas, USA) for 30 min, and washed again with PBS, after which the chromogen reaction was performed with DAB (ab64238, abcam, Cambridge, UK). After washing with distilled water, the sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (05–06,002/L BioOptica, Milano, Italy) and covered with entellan after soaking in alcohol and xylol, respectively.

The stained slides were digitized using a binocular light microscope (BX-51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with an integrated color digital camera (DP72, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and imaging software (cellSens Entry 1.7, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Cerebral cortex thickness, cerebellar fissure depth and external granule layer (EGL) thickness were measured with the same software. Cell counts in the hippocampus region were performed manually. Immunohistochemical staining was analyzed by measuring immunoreactivity intensity with ImageJ 1.54f. (U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

Biochemical analyses

Serum creatinine and urine proteinuria levels were measured to confirm renal damage in preeclampsia. During the sacrifice of the dam rats, intracardiac blood was collected, and the serum was isolated and stored in aliquots at −80 °C until further analysis. Serum creatinine (7D64-20, Abbott, Illinois, USA) was analyzed with an automatic analyzer (Architecht C16000, Abbott, Illinois, USA). 24-h urine samples collected on GDs 6, 12 and 18 were centrifuged and analyzed in the same automated analyzer using a commercial urine protein kit (7D79-21, Abbott, Illinois, USA).

Since interleukin 10 (IL-10), NO, endothelia nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) have effects on neurogenesis in addition to their roles in preeclampsia and neuroinflammatory mechanisms, their changes in brain tissue were analysed. After washing with saline, the brain tissues were divided into portions of 100 mg per 1 microcentrifuge tube and stored at −80 °C. The supernatant was obtained after tissue homogenization with a homogenizer (Potter S 8,533,024, B.Braun, Melsungen, Germany) in cold buffer solution under appropriate conditions was analyzed for protein content, and the amount of protein per gram was determined. In addition, homogenates were prepared according to the kit procedure and analyzed with NO (E-BC-K035-M, Elabscience, Texas, USA), eNOS (E-EL-R0367, Elabscience), IL-10 (E-EL-R0016, Elabscience, Texas, USA) and nNOS (CSB-E14034r, Cusabio, Texas, USA) ELISA kits.

Statistical analyses

The normality of the quantitative data was checked by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to examine the changes in time-dependent variables, which was used to assess the normality of the data. When the Time*Group interaction was found to be significant, repeated-measures ANOVA was used for intragroup comparisons, and one-way ANOVA was used for intergroup comparisons. For variables that did not meet the normality assumption, the Friedman test (Wilcoxon signed rank test for pairwise comparisons) was used for intragroup comparisons, and the Kruskal–Wallis test (Dunn test for pairwise comparisons) was used for intergroup comparisons. One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons of ELISA results and other clinical variables between groups. The Bonferroni correction was used for pairwise group comparisons. The Kruskal–Wallis method was used for comparisons of birth data between groups. Normality of histomorphometric and immunohistochemical staining data was analyzed by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and analysis of variance was performed by Levene’s test. Since the data did not conform to a normal distribution, comparisons of CA3 cell counts were performed with Dunn’s test after Kruskal–Wallis test, and since the other parameters conformed to a normal distribution, one-way ANOVA was performed with the Bonferroni correction if the variances were equal and the Dunnett T3 test if the variances were not equal. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0., IBM Corp., New York, USA) software. The significance level was set as 0.05 in all analyses.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ege University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committe. Approval number: 2020-018.

Serum creatinine level

A statistically significant difference was found only in the Apelin and PE groups (p = 0.015). Serum creatinine values of the PE + Apelin group were found to be close to the Control group (Table 2).

Analysis of the birth data of newborns

The number of pups born in one litter was lower in the PE groups than in the Apelin group (Table 2).

The neonatal weight, which was significantly lower in the PE group than in the Control. PE + Apelin group was not different to the Control group, but to the PE group (p = 0.010) (Table 2).

In the analysis of neonatal brain weights, although the Kruskal–Wallis test showed statistical significance between the groups (p = 0.035), the differences disappeared after Bonferroni correction when the groups were compared pairwise (Table 2).

Histological findings

Maternal kidney

While normal renal histology was observed in the Control and Apelin groups, dilatation in Bowman’s space of glomeruli, narrowing in glomerular capillaries and an increase in mononuclear cells were observed in the PE group. Dilatation and epithelial damage were observed in the proximal and distal tubules. Similar to the Control group, no dilatation in the Bowman space and narrowing of the glomerular capillaries were observed in the PE + Apelin group; and there were fewer mononuclear cells in the mesengial interstitium than in the PE group. In the proximal and distal tubules, epithelial damage was not detected (Fig. 1). According to the anti-VEGF staining results, diffuse and intense immunoreactivity was observed in tubules, vessels and glomerular mesangial cells in the Control and Apelin groups, while immunoreactivity was decreased in these structures and cells in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs Control). In the PE + Apelin group, immunoreactivity was observed between the Control and PE groups (Fig. 1, Table 3). Anti-iNOS staining revealed a small number of iNOS-positive cells around tubules and vessels in the Control and Apelin groups. In the PE, intense immunoreactivity was observed, especially in glomerular mesangial cells (p < 0.001 vs. Control). In the PE + Apelin group, mesangial immunoreactivity was decreased compared to the PE group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1, Table 3).

Placenta

In the Control and Apelin groups, the chorionic villi and fetal vessels, stroma, decidual spiral arterioles and syncytial nodes had normal histology. Syncytial nodes increased in number and were larger in the PE group. In the PE + Apelin group, syncytial nodes were observed to be more similar in number and size to those in the Control and Apelin groups (Fig. 1). According to the anti-VEGF staining results, positive immunoreactivity was found in trophoblasts, villus connective tissue cells and capillary endothelium in the Control and Apelin groups. A much lower level of immunoreactivity was observed in the same cells in the PE group than in the other groups (p < 0.001 vs. Control and Apelin); in the PE + Apelin group, staining was detected between the Control and PE groups (p < 0.001 vs. Control and Preeclampsia) (Fig. 1, Table 3). According to the anti-iNOS staining results, medium-intensity immunoreactivity was observed in cytotrophoblasts, syncytiotrophoblasts and vascular endothelial cells in the Control and Apelin groups, while high immunoreactivity was observed in these cell types in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control and Apelin). In the PE + Apelin group, immunoreactivity was lower than that in the PE group (p < 0.001) and was similar to that in the Control and Apelin groups (Fig. 1, Table 3).

Results of neonatal brains

Cerebral cortex

In the Control and Apelin groups, cells that migrated from the ventricular zone to the upper parts of the cortex and were accompanied by radial glial cells (RGCs) and a neocortex layer compatible with the neonatal period, including the transient layer called the cortical plate (CP) between the intermediate and marginal zones, were observed. In the PE group, especially in the CP region, the number of cells was lower, intercellular spaces were greater, and cellular organization was impaired. In the PE + Apelin group, cellular organization of CP was similar to the Control group, and cytotoxic edema was lower in the PE + Apelin group than in the PE group. Intracellular edema was found in few neurons and glia (Fig. 2). In the measurement of cortex thickness (total thickness of marginal zone and CP), the thickness was highest in the Control, followed by Apelin and PE + Apelin and the lowest cortex thickness was observed in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control) (Table 4).

Hippocampus

In the Control group, hippocampus and dentate gyrus (DG) were compatible with the developmental period, pyramidal cells were arranged in 3–4 rows, and migration was close to completion. Apelin group had a histological structure similar to that of the Control group. In the PE, migration was retarded compared to the Control, and it was remarkable that the pyramidal cell layer was 8–10 rows. It was found that this group had the highest number of cells in the areas that would be shaped as polymorphic layer in the future, and delayed migration cells were abundant in CA3 and CA4. There was a greater amount of intercellular space between pyramidal cells, which may be related to edema and retarded migration. DG formation was also found to be impaired. In the PE + Apelin group, the pyramidal cell layer consisted of 5–6 layers, and there were fewer cells in the areas that would be formed as polymorphic layer, similar to the Control and Apelin groups. While CA3 was similar to the Control, the delay in migration continued in the CA4 layer, but the number of cells that experienced migration failure was less than that in the PE group. DG formation was similar to that in the Control group, and the intercellular spaces were found to be at a level between Control and PE (Fig. 2). When examined the cell counts in CA1, CA2, CA3 and CA4, significantly fewer cells were detected in the PE group than in the other groups (p < 0.001 vs. Control in all 4 regions) and number of cells was greater in the PE + Apelin group than in the PE group (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, p = 0.572 and p < 0.001 vs. PE for CA1, CA2, CA3 and CA4, respectively) (Table 4).

Cerebellum

In the Control group, EGL, molecular layer (ML) and inner granule layer (IGL) were significantly formed. The decrease in the number of cells in the EGL layer and the radial arrangement of the cells were interpreted as the continuation of radial migration. A histological architecture similar to that of the Control group was observed in the Apelin group. EGL was significantly thicker in the PE group than in the Control and Apelin groups (p < 0.001 vs. Control and Apelin) (Table 4). ML was found to be thicker compared to Control and contained prominent intercellular spaces. Cerebellar fissure depth, which can be considered an indicator of developmental delay, was significantly lower in the PE group than in the other groups (p < 0.001). It was observed that the histological changes in the PE + Apelin group were milder than the PE group and were closer to the Control group. EGL thickness was found to decrease compared to PE (p < 0.001) (Table 4). In the ML, the cells were distributed in a larger area than in the Control group. In the IGL, the number of cells was lower than that in the Control and Apelin groups, while the intercellular spaces were decreased compared to the PE. The cerebellar fissure depth, which was shorter in the PE group, partially improved in the PE + Apelin group (p < 0.001) (Table 4, Fig. 2).

Anti-NeuN immunohistochemistry

In the cerebral cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum, high levels of NeuN immunoreactivity were observed in the Control and Apelin. In the PE group, the NeuN + cell number and immunoreactivity decreased in accordance with the retardation of neurogenesis (p < 0.001 vs. Control in all three regions), and patchy staining patterns were detected in some parts of the CP in the cortex. NeuN immunoreactivity in the PE + Apelin group was higher than the PE group (p = 0.019, p < 0.001 and p < 0.001 vs. PE, in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum, respectively) (Table 4, Fig. 3).

Anti-GFAP immunohistochemistry

GFAP, which is expressed in RGH during neurogenesis, was found to be intensely and regularly expressed in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus in the Control and Apelin groups. In the PE group, patchy staining was observed in the cortex. Although no statistically significant difference was observed in the cerebral cortex, immunoreactivity was significantly lower in the hippocampus (p < 0.001 vs. Control). In this region, immunoreactivity in the Control group was more intense than the PE (p < 0.001). GFAP immunoreactivity increased in the PE + Apelin group compared to PE (Table 4, Fig. 4). In the cerebellum, intense GFAP immunoreactivity was observed in the EGL in the Control and Apelin groups, while lower immunoreactivity was observed in the IGL than in the other layers. In the PE groups, GFAP immunoreactivity was low, especially in the EGL in PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control) (Table 4, Fig. 4).

Anti-Iba-1 immunohistochemistry

In the cerebral cortex, Iba-1 was detected most intensely in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control) and immunoreactivity was decreased in the PE + Apelin group (p < 0.001 vs. PE) (Table 4, Fig. 5). In the hippocampus, Iba-1 staining was found to be localized at low levels in the Control and Apelin groups and in the peripheral regions of neurons that had completed development. In the PE group, more intense Iba-1 immunoreactivity was found in the periphery of cells with delayed migration than in the Control and Apelin groups (p < 0.001). In the PE + Apelin group, the immunoreactivity was similar to that in the Control group (Table 4, Fig. 5).

MBP, which is found in myelin structures in the central nervous system, was expressed at the lowest level in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control). In the PE + Apelin group, the immunoreactivity was higher than in the PE group and lower than in the Control and Apelin groups (p < 0.001 vs. PE) (Table 4, Fig. 6).

Discussion

Many studies in which L-NAME was administered to create a preeclampsia model have been conducted, and Li et al. reported that when subcutaneous L-NAME was administered starting on the 9th day of pregnancy, the MAP on the 10th, 15th and 20th days increased continuously compared to that in the control group until the end of pregnancy25. In our study, it was determined that the MAP, which was similar to that of the Control on GD 5 before the start of L-NAME administration, increased significantly on GD 11 in the L-NAME-treated groups compared to that in the Control group (p < 0.05).

Studies have shown that the apelin/APJ system has a protective effect on ischemia–reperfusion injury, chronic renal failure, and podocyte damage seen in diabetes15,26–28. Hamza et al. created a preeclampsia model with L-NAME and administered Apelin-13 between GD 6–20. It was shown that apelin administration significantly decreased MAP on day 18 compared to that in the preeclampsia group23. In a study using the preeclampsia model induced by reduced uterine perfusion pressure (RUPP), it was shown that Apelin-13 application reduced MAP in rats with preeclampsia, but no significant difference was observed between the Control and Apelin groups, similar to our results22. In our study, although the MAP decreased on GD 17 in the PE + Apelin (mean 129 mmHg) compared to that in the PE (mean 150 mmHg), the difference was not statistically significant. The reason for this may be that apelin administration started on a later day of pregnancy than in other studies. We aimed to examine the efficacy of apelin in reversing the preeclampsia-inducing effects of L-NAME administration after the development of these effects, rather than timing it to mimic prophylactic administration. Apelin administration starting on earlier days may yield more effective results.

Renal damage in preeclampsia leads to creatinine elevation and proteinuria29. Increased serum urea, BUN, creatinine and urine protein levels were also observed in L-NAME-induced preeclampsia models23,30,31. Hamza et al. showed that apelin administration decreased these values23. In our study, L-NAME administration caused an increase in urine protein compared to that in the Control group at GD 12. At GD 18, although proteinuria was exacerbated in both groups compared to that in the Control group, the decrease in the PE + Apelin group compared to the PE group was not statistically significant. Similarly, serum creatinine levels were increased in the PE, however, apelin application did not produce a significant effect.

Impaired uteroplacental blood flow in preeclampsia can lead to IUGR and delay fetal brain development, leading to ischemic brain injury in the newborn32,33. Javadian et al. reported that offspring and placenta weights decreased in a RUPP-induced preeclampsia model34. In another study, the newborn weight decreased from 5.44 g in the control group to 4.26 g in the L-NAME group10. In our study, the mean newborn weight decreased from 5.60 g in the Control group to 4.52 g in the PE group and was 5.12 g in the PE + Apelin (p = 0.010 vs. PE). When the effects of Apelin-13 on preeclamptic transgenic rats were examined, it was found that there was no significant difference in the number or weight of newborns35. In our study, there was no statistically significant difference in the number of newborns between the PE-PE + Apelin groups. In the study of Liu et al., L-NAME administration decreased the number of offspring and brain weight36. In our study, newborn brain weights were similar in all groups.

Preeclampsia leads to glomerular endotheliosis in the kidney, characterized mainly by endothelial edema and narrowing of glomerular capillaries37. Hamza et al. showed that Apelin-13 application alleviated these changes seen in preeclampsia23. In our study, similar findings were found for PE, and apelin administration decreased glomerular endotheliosis and epithelial damage.

VEGF inhibition by sFlt-1 in preeclampsia leads to impaired integrity and function of endothelial cells and glomeruli, resulting in increased blood pressure38,39. Apelin-13 has been shown to increase VEGF in different experimental models24,40. In our study, VEGF immunoreactivity was lower in the PE group than in the Control and Apelin groups, and was greater in the PE + Apelin group than in the PE group.

Hipni et al. demonstrated increased iNOS immunoreactivity in the kidneys of mice with preeclampsia41. In vitro and in vivo studies have shown that different apelin subtypes reduce iNOS expression42,43. In our study, a small number of iNOS positive cells were observed around the tubules and vessels in the Control and Apelin groups, whereas very intense immunoreactivity was observed in glomerular mesangial cells in the PE group. In the PE + Apelin group, the immunoreactivity was lower than PE.

Trophoblast dysfunction caused by placental inflammation and oxidative stress plays an important role in the development of preeclampsia44. Wang et al. found numerous trophoblastic nodules and infiltration vacuolization in the placenta in the L-NAME-induced preeclampsia group45. In another study, apelin administration decreased placental changes in preeclampsia, and degenerative changes were limited to a small number of giant trophoblastic cells and spongiotrophoblasts24. In our study, syncytial nodes were found to be increased and large in the PE group. In the PE + Apelin group, the morphology was similar to that of the Control group and there were few syncytial nodes.

It is known that the decrease in VEGF receptor production involved in angiogenesis in preeclampsia causes cytotrophoblasts to disrupt the invasion of uterine arterioles and cause them to remain high resistance vessels; therefore, it plays an important role in the pathophysiology of preeclampsia46. Xu et al. showed that L-NAME administration decreased VEGF, eNOS and NO levels in the placenta47. Apelin is thought to mediate angiogenesis through the upregulation of VEGF or VEGFR248. In our study, anti-VEGF immunoreactivity was found to be low in the PE group. Increased immunoreactivity was detected in the PE + Apelin group compared to the PE group.

NO is thought to play a role in spiral artery remodelling, and NO production is impaired in preeclampsia. iNOS is released in the placenta by the endothelium of fetal blood vessels, syncytiotrophoblasts, extravillous trophoblasts and Hofbauer cells in the villous stroma49. NO and iNOS levels were shown to increase in the placenta in a preeclampsia model induced by L-NAME administration50. Studies have shown that apelin administration decreases iNOS levels by exerting an anti-inflammatory effect43,51. In our study, intense immunoreactivity were observed in cytotrophoblast, syncytiotrophoblast and vascular endothelial cells in the PE group, in accordance with the literature. The effects of apelin on iNOS levels in the placenta were analyzed for the first time, and iNOS immunoreactivity was found to be lower in the PE + Apelin group compared to the PE.

Shafik et al. examined the brain development of newborns in the preeclampsia model created with L-NAME, and spongiosis consisting of irregular clear spaces in both gray and white matter, shrinkage of neurons and a decrease in cell density were found52. Liu et al. showed that cortex thickness decreased at P0 in the L-NAME group compared to the control. In this study, brain tissues from P56 were also examined, and the number of NeuN + cells decreased, while the number of GFAP + cells increased in the L-NAME group36. In our study, fewer neurons were found in the CP in the PE group than in the Control and Apelin groups, and the increase in the intercellular area was evaluated as hypoxia-induced cytotoxic edema. The lowest cortex thickness was observed in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control). These pathological changes were alleviated in the PE + Apelin group.

In the hippocampus, the SGZ region of the DG is known to provide a neurogenic niche and is one of the regions where neurogenesis is sustained throughout life53. Yin et al. reported that hippocampal neuron proliferation decreased and that apoptosis and autophagy were triggered in a mild intrauterine hypoperfusion model54. Importantly, in our study, pyramidal cells were disorganized and in 8–10 rows as a result of the delay in migration in the PE group. Among the cells in the hippocampal regions, the lowest number of cells was observed in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control). There was a partial recovery of the number of cells in PE + Apelin group; it was thought that this difference may be due to the positive effect of apelin on migration.

In pregnant guinea pigs, IUGR has been shown to cause a decrease in cerebellar volume, myelination and Purkinje cell counts6. McDougall et al. found that the EGL width was 30% greater in IUGR pups compared to control pups at postnatal day 7 in a rat model of intrauterine growth retardation generated by bilateral uterine vascular ligation at postnatal day 18. Although there were also findings in the direction of increased ML width, no difference was found in IGL width compared with that of the control. There were differences in the structure and organization of GFAP + Bergmann glial fibers, but their density was not different between control and IUGR offspring55. In our study, the thickness of the EGL and ML layers were greater in the PE group than in the Control and Apelin groups, and the increase in EGL thickness was significant compared to that in the Control group (p < 0.001). Foliation process also plays an important role in the development of the cerebellum; as fissures form and deepen, the cerebellum acquires the morphology of the adult56. The depth of the cerebellar fissure was lower in the PE group than in the Control and Apelin groups (p < 0.001), and its morphology was similar to the E17.5 cerebellum in the study of Sudarov et al.59, which was evaluated as cerebellar developmental retardation. In the PE + Apelin group, these findings were attenuated, and the levels were close to the Control group.

In a study in which NeuN immunoreactivity was used to determine the density of mature neurons in the cerebral parietal cortex of rat pups in which intrauterine growth retardation was induced by uterine artery ligation, it was found to decrease in the growth retardation group57. Ruff et al. divided offspring with developmental delay resulting from placental insufficiency into mild and moderate developmental delay groups according to birth weight. When hippocampal damage was examined in these rats, no significant decrease was observed in terms of the thickness of the CA1 region or neuronal cell loss, while neuronal cell loss and a decrease in the number of NeuN + neurons were found in the CA2 regions of rats with both mild and moderate developmental delay. In the CA3 region, the thickness decreased only in rats with moderate developmental delay58. In our study, NeuN immunoreactivity decreased in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control) and was higher in the PE + Apelin group than the PE group. However, despite this increase, the improvement is partial as it is still significantly different from the Control group in all three regions. AMPK/Nrf2 and BDNF/TrkB signals, which are effective mechanisms in neurogenesis59–62, have been shown to increase with apelin application19,63, may be the cellular pathways underlying the increase in NeuN.

During neurogenesis, neuroepithelial cells lose their epithelial characteristics, acquire glial characteristics and differentiate into RGCs. RGCs play an important role in the development of the nervous system by guiding the migration of neurons from their initial site of origin around the ventricle to their final location in the CP. GFAP has been identified in RGCs in humans and primates since the onset of neurogenesis, while in rodents, it was detected when gliogenesis progressed64. In the study of Fang et al. GFAP + astrocytes and Tuj-1 + neurons, which are precursor neural cells formed during the early differentiation period of neural stem cells, were examined in the IUGR model in rats; it was reported that the percentage of Tuj-1 + neurons was significantly lower than the control group, while GFAP + astrocytes were higher65. In our study, GFAP staining of the cerebral cortex revealed disruption of the scaffold structures of RGCs in the PE group, and intercellular spaces were thought to have developed due to this disruption. There was less intercellular space in the PE + Apelin group than in the PE group. Cumberland et al.66 reported that GFAP expression in the amygdala and CA1 region of the hippocampus did not significantly change in a guinea pig model of IUGR compared to the control, while in another study, GFAP decreased in male offspring with IUGR, while no significant difference was observed in female offspring67. In a study by Mohseni et al. investigating the effects of Apelin-13 in ethanol-induced neurotoxicity, it was shown that apelin administration increased antioxidant enzyme activity, prevented lipid peroxidation and decreased GFAP levels, which increased in neurotoxicity in the hippocampus of neonatal rats68. Analysis of the hippocampus in our study revealed that GFAP immunoreactivity decreased in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control). In the PE + Apelin group, GFAP was greater than the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. PE), and it was thought that apelin administration had a positive effect on the protection of the RGC scaffold. In the cerebellum, there were fewer RGCs in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control). The variable results observed in different studies may be due to changes in the maturation of GFAP-expressing cells due to the postnatal day on which the examinations were performed, different results when the analyses were performed in terms of cell number or immunoreactivity intensity, and different effects of glial morphological changes and neuroinflammation caused by preeclampsia during neurogenesis.

A study investigating the changes in the microglial marker Iba1 in rats with preeclampsia, revealed that Iba1 expression increased in the cerebral cortex of offspring on postnatal day 1 and increased in the cerebral cortex and cerebellum of both mothers and offspring rats on postnatal day 6069. Luo et al. showed that hippocampal Iba1 and GFAP levels increased in a streptozotocin-induced Alzheimer's disease model and Apelin-13 administration alleviated neuroinflammation by decreasing Iba1 and GFAP63. In our study, the highest Iba-1 immunoreactivity in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum was detected in the PE group (p < 0.001). The fact that Iba-1 immunoreactivity in the PE + Apelin group decreased compared to PE but was significantly higher than the Control group suggests that apelin is partially effective in suppressing microglial function.

Tachibana et al. reported that, MBP immunoreactivity in the cingulum region was significantly lower in an L-NAME-induced preeclampsia model compared to the control in the offspring at postnatal days 15 and 305. In our study, when the cerebral cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum structures were examined, the lowest level of staining was observed in the PE group (p < 0.001 vs. Control). Although MBP immunoreactivity in the PE + Apelin group increased compared to the PE group, it was still lower than the Control group.

L-NAME administration decreases anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels while triggering inflammation in the serum, placenta and kidney70,71. In a L-NAME-induced preeclampsia model, a decrease in the serum VEGF, PLGF, IL-10, SOD and catalase levels was positively correlated with the serum apelin concentration, and Apelin-13 administration increased these values24. In our study, IL-10 was lower in the brain tissue of the PE group compared to Apelin group, and increased in the PE + Apelin group and reached levels similar to those of the Control and Apelin groups (p = 0.008 vs. PE). It has been shown in other studies that apelin suppresses neuroinflammation by decreasing TNF-α and IL-1β and increasing IL-10 levels63,72. When these effects are evaluated together with the decrease in IBA-1 expression with apelin administration, which is increased in microglia in neuroinflammation, it is thought that apelin plays a role in suppressing neuroinflammation by these mechanisms.

nNOS-derived NO is involved in the regulation of many physiological mechanisms, such as synaptic plasticity, learning, memory, and neurogenesis73. The neuroprotective effects of NOS inhibitors such as L-NAME and 7-nitroindazole are time dependent. While they have a protective effect in the early stages of ischemic damage and at low doses, dose selection is important because high doses potentiate ischemia-induced neurodegeneration74. In our study, while L-NAME disrupted uteroplacental circulation in the PE and PE + Apelin groups and caused intrauterine growth retardation and ischemic brain damage, it may have assumed a limited neuroprotective role through the mechanisms mentioned above by decreasing the amount of NO, nNOS and eNOS in the PE group than in the Apelin group. Thus, similar findings were obtained in brain tissue, consistent with studies showing that nNOS levels decrease in preeclampsia in different tissues75,76. Studies have shown that Apelin-13 activates soluble guanylate cyclase and increases synaptic plasticity by increasing cGMP levels via endothelial NO77. Apelin-13 also induces antioxidant protein expression through the AMPK/P-GSK-3/Nrf2 pathway78. In our study, increased levels of nNOS (statistically significant; p < 0.001), NO and eNOS in the PE + Apelin group than in the PE group are consistent with the findings of these studies. There are studies showing that the increase in nNOS contributes to hippocampal neurogenesis79, however, Luo et al. state that it has a bidirectional effect on neurogenesis80. Although it has been shown in different tissues that apelin application causes an increase in nNOS81, it was examined in newborn brain tissue for the first time in our study. Because nNOS is involved in different neurophysiological mechanisms such as neurogenesis and neuroinflammation and has bidirectional effects, these changes need to be evaluated in further studies.

Conclusions

In our study, L-NAME administration caused a significant increase in MAP and urine protein levels and decreased newborn weight. Apelin administration had positive effects on newborn weight and histopathological changes caused by L-NAME in the placenta and maternal kidney. When different parts of the central nervous system of newborn rats born from preeclamptic pregnant rats were examined, neurogenesis was shown to be impaired as a result of the negative effects observed in both neurons and glia. Apelin administration was found to be beneficial for partially reducing these negative effects. In conclusion, it has been found that apelin administration may be beneficial for preventing the adverse effects of preeclampsia. However, further experimental and clinical studies are needed to better understand these effects and to evaluate their clinical use.
