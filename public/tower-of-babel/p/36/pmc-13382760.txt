Mechanical Robustness and Conductivity Retention in Ti 3 C 2 MXene-Enhanced Electrodes on Flexible Substrates
Authors: Shriswaroop Sathyanarayanan, Andrews Nirmala Grace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates Ti3C2T x MXene thin film coatings that were made onto five flexible substrates, namely PET, PEN, ITO-PET, ITO-PEN, and PI, over thicknesses ranging from 5 to 100 nm. The coatings were evaluated in terms of sheet resistance before and after 500 bending cycles, optical transmittance in the UV–visible range, and the thermal and rheological properties of the MXene ink. The substrates were exposed to UV-Ozone to modify their surface wettability in order to control the nucleation of MXene film formation. The data show that MXene significantly enhances and stabilizes the conductivity of brittle ITO electrodes under bending by forming continuous pathways that bridge cracks in the fractured ITO layer, whereas uncoated ITO-PET suffers a severe loss of conductivity after deformation. Among the tested systems, a 100 nm MXene layer on ITO-PEN offers the lowest sheet resistance while maintaining useful transparency, making it a strong candidate for flexible electrodes. A 30 nm MXene coating on PET, however, exhibits the best mechanical reliability, with only a minor change in resistance after repeated bending, indicating a favorable balance between network percolation and strain tolerance. Results on PEN and PI emphasize how substrate chemistry and UV–ozone–induced hydrophilicity influence MXene nucleation, film continuity, and long-term electro-mechanical stability. The low-viscosity MXene ink and its moderate thermal conductivity further support its use in scalable slot-die processing and applications that benefit from localized Joule heating. The impact of substrate selection, surface preparation, and film thickness on the performance of MXene-based flexible conductors was studied thoroughly and provides valuable insights for designing robust, optically adjustable, and manufacturable devices.

Introduction

Flexible electronics over the recent years have become the platform for next-generation applications, including wearable devices, flexible sensors, energy storage systems, and smart textiles. The need to develop high-performance flexible conductive electrodes (FCEs) with sustainable conductive stability under repeated mechanical strain remains a critical challenge. Among the conventional conductive electrode materials, indium tin oxide (ITO) has been dominating industrial applications for decades due to its exceptional electrical conductivity and relatively low sheet resistance , despite being a ceramic material. ITO exhibits fundamental limitations when employed on flexible polymer substrates such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyethylene naphthalate (PEN), and polyimide (PI) − by cracking when subjected to mechanical deformation. Mechanical brittleness has always been a limiting factor for ITO coated on flexible substrates. ITO conductive substrates lose their conductivity to a large degree upon bending because of the rise in microcracks. These cracks propagate under strain and are irreversible. Due to this, ITO-coated substrates, with their restricted bending radius, are unfavorable for flexible electronics. While conventional approaches have involved substrate engineering or interlayer modification, these strategies often introduce complexity, cost, and processing challenges without fully resolving the underlying brittleness problem.

Over the past decade, 2D nanomaterials with exceptional mechanical and electrical performance have gained attention as potential alternatives to conventional conductive materials for flexible applications. MXenes are one among such highly desirable materials, demonstrating high conductivity while being mechanically flexible as well. These are transition metal carbides or nitrides with the general formula of Ti3C2T x . MXene possesses metallic conductivity exceeding many conventional conductive materials, excellent sheet resistance in the range of 100–1000 Ω/sq, a hydrophilic surface enabling stable aqueous dispersions, and inherent mechanical flexibility imparted by its 2D layered structure. Many recent studies demonstrate the practicality of MXene films as a promising alternative conductive material for applications where conventional materials prove unsatisfactory.

Despite these advances, the synergistic integration of Ti3C2T x MXene coatings on ITO-coated flexible substrates to understand the effect of 2D flakes on conductivity upon flexing has not been thoroughly explored. In this work, Ti3C2T x MXene aqueous ink, synthesized via a mild method, is deposited as a top conductive layer on both ITO-coated and bare flexible polymer substrates to test the hypothesis that the 2D layered MXene flakes, possessing inherent flexibility, can physically and electrically bridge the fracture sites formed in underlying ITO layers upon mechanical deformation, as well as provide a continuous conductive network over bare substrates, positioning itself as a competitive candidate for alternative commercial conductive coating. The conductive pathways across cracked ITO regions will be restored by the inherent properties of the MXenes, maintaining electrical continuity and conductivity retention despite physical strain. Furthermore, on intrinsically nonconductive substrates (PET, PEN, and PI), the MXene coating would bestow conductivity through percolation networks of interconnected 2D flakes, enabling the polymer substrates to be used as conductive electrodes.

The scientific and technological significance of this work is multifold. First, it addresses the critical mechanical fragility of ITO on flexible substrates through a simple, scalable coating method rather than complex substrate engineering. Second, it leverages the inherent 2D flexibility and superior electronic properties of MXenes to enable durable, bendable conductors. Third, it demonstrates that composite ITO/MXene architectures can achieve superior electromechanical stability and conductivity retention compared with either constituent material alone. Fourth, it transforms nonconductive polymer substrates into functional conductive electrodes, expanding material design flexibility. Finally, the mild synthesis method for MXene aqueous ink offers environmental and cost advantages over more complex routes, facilitating potential industrial scaling and adoption.

This work is focused on the structural, morphological, optical, and electrical properties of the MXene coating deposited on conductive and nonconductive substrates. The five distinct substrates chosen for this study are ITO-coated PET, ITO-coated PEN, bare PET, bare PEN, and polyimide (PI). Comprehensive characterizations using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, atomic force microscopy (AFM), profilometry, optical microscopy, four-probe conductivity measurements, viscometry, and thermal conductivity analysis provide a complete understanding of the coating surface properties, electronic transport mechanisms, and thermal characteristics. Mechanical durability testing through 500 bending cycles over a fixed radius reveals exceptional retention of electrical conductivity, even on a single-pass coating of MXene-coated bare flexible substrates. The results obtained in this study clearly support Ti3C2T x MXene to be a highly suitable material for fabricating next-generation flexible conductive electrodes with necessary electro-mechanical performance and practical device realization of wearable and flexible device systems. This work is strategically designed to evaluate not just the individual electrical or mechanical merits of MXene coating of different thicknesses but also its combined performance with existing coating and substrate properties. This study tests the hypothesis that the 2D flaky architecture of MXene inherently provides stability against bending-induced failure while preserving conductive pathways, directly improving the commercially available ITO-coated substrates.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of Ti3C2Tx MXene Aqueous Ink

Ti3C2T x MXene was synthesized using a mild etching procedure based on in situ HF generation from a LiF/HCl mixture. ,, Briefly, 1 g of Ti3AlC2 MAX phase powder was slowly added to 20 mL of 9 M hydrochloric acid solution containing 2 g of lithium fluoride under continuous stirring in a polypropylene vessel. The reaction mixture was maintained at 35–40 °C and stirred for 24–48 h to selectively etch the Al layers from the MAX precursor. The resulting suspension was washed repeatedly with deionized water via centrifugation (3500 rpm, 10 min) until the supernatant reached pH ≈ 6. The sediment was redispersed in water and sonicated under an argon atmosphere for 1–2 h to delaminate multilayered Ti3C2T x into single- and few-layer flakes. The final dispersion was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 1 h to remove any unexfoliated particles. The supernatant, consisting of homogeneous Ti3C2T x aqueous ink, was collected for further use. The concentration was typically adjusted to 10–20 mg mL–1 by controlled dilution with deionized water.

Substrate Preparation

All five flexible substrates chosen for this study, including ITO-coated PET, ITO-coated PEN, pristine PET, pristine PEN, and PI (polyimide) films, were cut into uniform pieces of 10 × 10 cm2 to account for multiple coating thicknesses. Each substrate was cleaned using ultrasonication in acetone and deionized water for 10 min each to remove surface contaminants, followed by drying under nitrogen flow. The substrates were then tested for contact angle to assess their hydrophilicity, and subsequently exposed to UV ozone treatment for 10 min to enhance surface hydrophilicity and improve the wetting and adhesion properties for subsequent MXene ink coating.

Coating Deposition

On each 10 × 10 cm2 substrate, five different MXene coatings with increasing thicknesses were applied to investigate layer-dependent properties. Starting with a single-pass coating, the Ti3C2T x MXene aqueous ink was deposited via slot-die coating, uniformly covering the substrate surface. The slot-die parameters were kept constant at 18 mm/s die-head speed and 0.12 mL/min ink flow rate for all the coatings and all substrates. A consistent coating of width 10 mm and length 90 mm was made for all thicknesses and on all substrates for better comparison. The substrate bed was kept at a constant temperature of 60 °C. This led to the individual layers drying quickly, allowing us to coat subsequent layers to vary the thickness. Once all the coatings were made, the substrate was kept on a hot plate at 70 °C for 30 min for complete drying of the ink. This systematic multilayer approach enabled detailed studies of thickness-dependent performance on each substrate type.

Results and Discussion

The following methods were selected to comprehensively evaluate the mechanical robustness and conductivity retention of the MXene coatings. Structural and morphological characterizations (XRD, Raman, AFM, surface profilometry) establish the material foundation for durability, while electrical measurements, both pre- and postbending, quantify retention. Bending cycle tests directly probe electro-mechanical stability, and rheological/thermal analyses provide insights into processability and operational stability, all essential for assessing flexible electrode performance.

Structural Characterization of Ti3C2Tx MXene Ink

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of delaminated Ti3C2 MXene deposited on fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass confirms the successful synthesis of 2D MXene flakes, as seen in Figure . The XRD pattern, overlaid with the standard FTO reference (JCPDS 77-0452), reveals dominant sharp peaks from the SnO2 cassiterite phase at approximately 26° (110), 34° (101), 38° (200), 52° (211), and higher angles up to 70–80° 2θ, consistent with the polycrystalline FTO texture.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of delaminated Ti3C2Tx coated on FTO substrate.

A distinctive broad peak at a lower 2θ angle (∼6–7°) in the experimental spectrum signifies the (002) basal plane of delaminated Ti3C2T x , shifted from the ∼9.5° position typical of multilayered Ti3C2 derived from MAX phase etching. This shift corresponds to an expanded interlayer d-spacing of ∼1.3–1.5 nm, arising from successful delamination-induced few-layer restacking, water/ion intercalation, and characteristic surface terminations (T x = −OH, −O, −F).

Minor features, such as subtle overlaps marked in the pattern, may stem from residual MAX phase or trace TiO2, but the absence of prominent impurity peaks underscores high-purity deposition. Overall, these observations validate the structural integrity of the MXene-FTO heterostructure.

The Raman spectrum collected using a 532 nm laser for the delaminated Ti3C2T x MXene aqueous ink reveals several characteristic features that confirm successful synthesis and phase transformation of MXenes, as seen in Figure . The most prominent aspect is the presence of a strong, sharp peak at low wavenumbers, centered near 200 cm–1, which corresponds to the A1g vibrational mode involving Ti and C atoms arranged in the MXene lattice. This intense low-frequency signal is recognized as a fingerprint for Ti3C2T x MXene, distinctly setting it apart from the parent Ti3AlC2 MAX phase, whose Raman features generally appear at higher shifts, notably above 650 cm–1, due to Al-related lattice vibrations. In addition to the dominant A1g mode, secondary peaks between 380 and 400 cm–1 reflect the in-plane Eg vibrations of carbon atoms, supporting the existence of well-formed MXene sheets. Broader features observed in the 600–800 cm–1 region arise from the presence of surface terminations (such as −OH, −F, =O) and from disorder created by extensive exfoliation.

Raman spectroscopy of Ti3C2T x MXene aqueous ink.

The absence of any pronounced high-frequency resonance signals above 900 cm–1 further substantiates the complete removal of the Al layer and rules out contamination by unreacted MAX phase or other titanium carbide compounds. Raman spectroscopy, taken at multiple points on the substrate, has shown that no oxidation has taken place across the coating (Figure S1).

The successful synthesis of high-crystallinity, delaminated Ti3C2T x MXene confirmed by XRD and Raman spectroscopy is foundational to the observed performance. The expanded interlayer spacing and absence of Al-related phases not only facilitate efficient charge transport, directly influencing conductivity retention, but also contribute to the material’s intrinsic mechanical flexibility, which is critical for robustness under strain.

UV–vis spectroscopy is used for understanding the optical transparency of all the coatings of MXene on all the flexible substrates with the results comprehensively detailed in Figure . Across all five substrate typesITO-PET, ITO-PEN, PET, PEN, and PIa consistent and monotonic reduction in transmittance is observed with an increasing MXene layer count, directly attributable to the cumulative absorption and interfacial scattering losses introduced by the progressively thicker Ti3C2T x films. A linear trend showing layer-dependent reduction of transmittance across all the substrates confirms the uniformity of coating achieved with the slot-die coating technique. For ITO-coated substrates (Figure a,b), the bare films exhibit high baseline transparency at approximately 78% at 550 nm; upon adding a single-pass coating of MXene, this value shows a 5%, resulting in around 70% transmittance.

However, with three or more MXene layers, transmittance declines more sharply, reaching approximately 25% and 42% for ITO-PET and ITO-PEN, respectively, at ∼100 nm thickness, as seen in Figure . This result underscores a critical trade-off: while MXene enhances electrical conductivity and mechanical robustness, its metallic nature and high carrier concentration come at the cost of optical transmission, particularly in the near-infrared region, where plasmonic absorption by MXene becomes significant.

When it comes to the intrinsically transparent bare polymer substrates (PET, PEN, and PI; Figure c–e), a similar trend is observed with the increase in MXene layers. With a single-pass coating of MXene, the transmittance is reduced from approximately 70% to 50%. As the number of coatings increases, there is a proportional drop in transmittance owing to the spread and stacking of 2D flakes on the substrate. The consistent linear reduction curve across all the substrates also suggests better interfacial adhesion and fewer optical defects, which is consistent with the improved mechanical compliance observed in bending tests. Among all the substrates, ITO-PEN demonstrates exceptional transmittance even at ∼100 nm thickness of MXene with approximately 42%, which is twice the transmittance of other substrates. This could be because the destructive interference due to the MXene coated on top of ITO-coated PEN is much less and that MXene flakes are packed more uniformly on ITO-PEN than on ITO-PET, which explains the reduction in scattering.

From an application perspective, these results provide a clear substrate- and thickness-dependent roadmap for designing MXene-based flexible electrodes. For high-transparency applications like touchscreens or organic photovoltaics, 1–2 MXene layers on ITO-PEN offer an optimal compromise. For wearable sensors or foldable circuits, where mechanical durability outweighs optical demands, 3–5 MXene layers on PET or PI deliver robust conductivity with sufficient visibility for user interaction or indicator integration.

Morphological Analysis of MXene Coatings

UV-Ozone and Contact Angle Measurements

Substrate pretreatment optimization is critical for achieving uniform MXene thin films, as aqueous MXene inks require hydrophilic surfaces for wetting and nucleation control. The coating sides of all the substrates were precisely exposed to a 253 nm UV-ozone light source for 10 min to facilitate surface activation and then kept in ambient conditions for 50 min as a resting period to monitor the difference in hydrophilicity. Contact angle measurements quantified hydrophilicity, revealing substrate-specific optimal exposure durations of 10 min, beyond which monolayer oxygen coverage reaches saturation. AFM Topography and Surface Roughness Analysis

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis confirms the presence of MXene flakes through surface topography, along with the roughness of the Ti3C2T x MXene coatings with different thicknesses. In Figure a, representing the least thickness MXene layer (single-pass coating), the topography image reveals a relatively smooth surface with some micrometer-scale flakes and minor aggregates. The area roughness value (Sa) is calculated to be 3.42 nm, and the height profile shows fluctuations consistent with the arrangement of flakes across the area, confirming the successful deposition of a thin, nearly continuous MXene film. Individual features visible on the surface are indicative of individual or aggregated Ti3C2T x nanosheets lying atop the substrate, with only limited vertical disparity and low peak-to-valley difference. The deflection graph (Figure ) further supports the uniformity and homogeneity of this thin coating, showing minimal disruption and noise. Figure b depicts a closer network of flakey surface structures; this was the analysis done on the substrate coated with multilayered MXene coating. The topography image depicts a more nonuniform vertical displacement, indicating a varying number of MXene flake stacks on the surface.

The area roughness value remains comparable at 3.62 nm, consistent with good layering and high-quality coating of MXene stacking, even when the thickness is increased. Visible 2D MXene flakes in different height gradients in the image show how the multilayer stacking has taken place with an increasing number of coatings. The thickness evolution of the Ti3C2T x MXene films coated using the Research Laboratory Coater (RLC, manufactured by Infinity PV, Denmark) shows a systematic and nearly monotonic increase from 5.48 nm (T1) to 99.1 nm (T5), as measured by surface profilometry (Figure ). This gradual thickness buildup with successive coating cycles indicates that each pass deposits a relatively uniform, ultrathin layer, consistent with well-dispersed, fully delaminated MXene flakes rather than aggregated, thick particulates. The lowest coating (T1 ≈ 5.48 nm) falls within the regime expected for few-layer MXene films, suggesting that the ink predominantly contains single- and few-layer Ti3C2T x sheets that form a continuous conductive network even at minimal thickness. As the number of coatings increases, the cumulative thickness (up to ≈99.1 nm at T5) supports the formation of a dense, stacked lamellar architecture that enhances interflake contact and promotes efficient in-plane charge transport, thereby providing good electrical conductivity suitable for flexible conductive electrode applications.

Thickness evolution of MXene films with successive coating cycles.

The uniform, low-roughness morphology and controlled thickness progression are not merely indicators of coating quality but are directly correlated with mechanical robustness. Smooth, continuous films minimize stress concentration points during bending, while the reproducible layer-by-layer buildup ensures consistent conductivity retention by maintaining percolation pathways even under deformation.

Optical Microscopy Images and Defect Analysis

Figure a reveals extensive microcracking in the bare ITO-PET substrate post-500 bending cycles, manifesting as orthogonal fracture networks with crack widths of 1–5 μm and characteristic ITO debris islands (∼10–50 μm lateral dimensions), consistent with brittle failure under ∼1–2% substrate strain. This morphology correlates with the observed 278% increase in sheet resistance, as fracture propagation severs ITO percolation pathways.

Optical images of (a) cracked ITO on PET, (b) MXene flakes on PEN substrate and (c) MXene bridging the cracks between ITO, maintaining electrical continuity.

Figure b depicts pristine MXene morphology on undeformed ITO-PET, exhibiting a highly uniform 2D flake distribution with lateral flake overlap forming a continuous conductive network, responsible for the initial low sheet resistance. Critical evidence appears in Figure c, where postbending MXene/ITO-PET reveals MXene flakes dynamically spanning ITO fracture gaps. It can be clearly seen how individual MXene nanosheets bridge microcracks, maintaining electrical continuity through flake-to-flake contact points, highlighted by the bright spots. At higher magnification (Figure c), single-pass coating of MXene efficacy can be confirmed, with isolated flakes confirming conformal contact across fracture widths via intrinsic 2D deformability and van der Waals adhesion to opposing crack faces. Higher magnification (×2000) shows clear fracture lines across ITO and the bridging effect by MXene flakes (Figures S2 and S3).

The percolation-preserving mechanism explains the 20–95% resistance mitigation versus bare ITO: MXene’s high intrinsic conductivity (∼104 S/cm), combined with fracture-conforming morphology, provides redundant conductive shunts across the brittle ITO network, reducing normalized ΔR from 278 to 4.2–12.6. The observed uniform, bright MXene domain spanning the entire fracture landscape confirms statistically robust bridging density (>1 flake/10 μm2), enabling subpercolation MXene thicknesses to sustain functional conductivity post-ITO failure.

Electro-Mechanical Analysis

To further understand how well MXene coatings maintain conductivity under mechanical stress, conductivity retention through pre- and postbending resistance measurements were done to assess mechanical robustness via bending cycle stability and failure mode analysis across substrates and thicknesses. MXene coatings systematically reduce sheet resistance across all substrates, with performance strongly dependent on thickness and substrate type. As shown in Figure a–e, the choice of substrate significantly influences the baseline conductivity. ITO-PEN substrates achieve the lowest values, progressing from 46.92 Ω/sq (5 nm) to 12.37 Ω/sq (100 nm), surpassing the bare ITO-PEN baseline (14.31 Ω/sq). ITO-PET shows similar initial performance, ranging from 36.11 to 18.08 Ω/sq, while PI reaches 17.98 Ω/sq at 100 nm thickness. Polymer substrates without ITO exhibit higher resistance: PET improves dramatically from 2910.54 Ω/sq (5 nm, incomplete coverage) to 34.11 Ω/sq (100 nm), whereas PEN remains electrically poor, ranging from 6692.7 to 90.03 Ω/sq, indicating nucleation limitations on bare polymer surfaces.

The data reveal a clear substrate-dependent response to mechanical stress, directly illustrating the protective and conductive bridging role of the MXene layer. The PET substrate with MXene coating demonstrates the most impressive mechanical compliance, and the increase in sheet resistance after bending is minimal. This superior performance stems from the absence of a brittle ITO interlayer. The compliant PET substrate accommodates strain through viscoelastic relaxation, and the directly adhered MXene coating undergoes distributed microcracking. The overlapping flake morphology ensures that lateral conductivity is preserved across these microfissures, leading to outstanding conductivity retention. In contrast, ITO-PET with MXene coating shows a significant, though not catastrophic, increase in resistance. This intermediate performance underscores a competition between two mechanisms: the detrimental effect of the brittle ITO layer fracturing and the beneficial bridging effect of the MXene topcoat. The MXene layer mitigates the extreme failure seen in the control sample but cannot fully compensate for the severe mechanical mismatch and poor adhesion at the ITO-PET interface, which leads to delamination and wider crack formation. The control experiment, bare ITO-PET, provides the most compelling evidence for the necessity of MXene integration. Here, the sheet resistance increases by over 3 orders of magnitude after bending, representing a complete electrical failure. This catastrophic rise is the hallmark of ITO’s intrinsic brittleness, where cyclic strain causes pervasive crack propagation that severs all conductive pathways. The dramatic difference between the coated and uncoated ITOPET/PEN quantifies the “crack-bridging” efficacy of the MXene coating, transforming a mechanically failed electrode into a functional one.

These results visually validate the proposed failure hierarchy. Systems with high intrinsic compliance (MXene/PET) show minimal degradation. Systems with a brittle interlayer but a robust bridge (MXene/ITO-PEN) show managed degradation. Systems with poor interfacial adhesion and high modulus mismatch (MXene/ITO-PET) show significant but mitigated degradation. Finally, systems with no protective mechanism (Bare ITO-PET) fail completely. This hierarchy provides a critical framework for selecting substrate and coating architectures based on the required durability for specific flexible electronic applications, from robust wearable sensors (prioritizing PET) to high-conductivity flexible displays (prioritizing ITO-PEN with MXene protection). After 500 bending cycles, ITO-PEN maintains excellent conductivity (28.50–103.55 Ω/sq), representing only ∼100–130% increase. PI shows comparable retention (40.93–535.57 Ω/sq), while PET coatings remain functional (52.46–3941 Ω/sq). ITO-PET suffers catastrophic degradation (94.36–490.22 Ω/sq for MXene, 12,629 Ω/sq for bare ITO-PET), and PEN coatings deteriorate significantly (226.84–17,544 Ω/sq). ITO/MXene: Electrical Enhancement and Bridging Effect

MXene coatings on ITO substrates demonstrate dual behavior: conductivity enhancement at optimal thicknesses and crack-bridging postmechanical failure. On ITO-PEN, 100 nm MXene (12.37 Ω/sq) outperforms bare ITO-PEN (14.31 Ω/sq) by 15%, while 60 nm MXene/ITO-PET (19.61 Ω/sq) improves over bare ITO-PET (45.39 Ω/sq) by 57%. Improved performance can be attributed to MXene’s superior intrinsic conductivity (∼10,000 S/cm), which compensates for ITO’s limitations. The critical discovery is MXene’s crack-bridging capability after ITO fracture. Bare ITO-PET resistance explodes 278x (45.39 → 12,629 Ω/sq), but MXene-coated ITO-PET shows only 4–13x increases. Even at 5 nm (thinnest coating), MXene/ITO-PET resistance rises 13.6x versus ITO-PET’s 278x, indicating MXene sheets maintain percolation across ITO cracks. This bridging effect is thickness-dependent: thicker coatings (60–100 nm) exhibit lower ΔR normalized (4.2–6.2) versus thinner films (8.5–12.6), as more MXene provides redundant conductive pathways.

ITO-PEN leads electrically at 12.37 Ω/sq (100 nm MXene, prebending), rising to 28.50 Ω/sq post-500 cycles (∼2.3× increase) and outperforming bare ITO-PEN (14.31 Ω/sq) by 15%. PI ranks second (17.98 Ω/sq prebending to 40.93 Ω/sq post), PET excels in retention at 30 nm (272.83–300.32 Ω/sq, 1.1x), but PEN lags due to nucleation issues (90.03–6692.7 Ω/sq prebending). ITO-PET fails catastrophically despite 18.08 Ω/sq initially. Thus, from the overall comparison, ITO-PEN-100 nm (best conductivity) > PI-100 nm > PET-100 nm > PET-30 nm (top retention) can be taken as the order of performance for guiding flexible electronics optimization.

Mechanical Stability Index (MSI)

After 500 bending cycles, conductivity retention varies dramatically with substrate. PET exhibits superior stability: 30 nm coating retains 91% conductivity (272.83 → 300.32 Ω/sq, ΔR_norm = 0.10), while 100 nm retains 66% (34.11 → 52.46 Ω/sq). PI shows moderate retention (∼50–75% across thicknesses), and ITO-PEN maintains ∼45–80% conductivity despite low starting resistance. PEN coatings degrade severely (>150% resistance increase), while ITO-PET suffers catastrophic failure (12–13x resistance increase for MXene, 278x for bare ITO). Substrates like ITO-PEN offer excellent initial conductivity but moderate retention, whereas PET exhibits superior robustness with near-perfect conductivity retention after 500 cycles, albeit with lower initial conductivity. This substrate-dependent behavior underscores both properties that must be optimized in tandem for application-specific designs.

Failure Analysis and Mechanism Discussion

Three distinct failure mechanisms dictate MXene coating performance under bending stress because the interfacial properties between the substrate and coating and their mechanical properties create two different failure methods. ITO-PET substrates experience complete brittle delamination because the rigid ITO interlayer develops microcracks that cause it to detach from the flexible PET underlayer, which creates open circuit pathways that interrupt percolation. The ITO fracture remains intact because MXene provides partial mitigation through crack-bridging. The direct polymer substrates PET and PI show distributed microcracking, which occurs within the MXene layer because the polymer backbone permits MXene flakes to maintain their connection through minor fissures by using overlapping conductive networks. The intrinsic nucleation failure of PEN substrates leads to islanded MXene film formation because surface wetting becomes difficult, generating gaps that prevent proper percolation during mechanical strain and before bending occurs. The critical thickness dependence shows 30 nm as an ideal thickness because MXene achieves electrical continuity while maintaining flexibility, which enables it to handle bending stresses without producing critical crack propagation with considerable transmittance. The dominant factors that control electro-mechanical durability in flexible conductor design stem from the combination of film microstructure, substrate compliance, and interfacial adhesion.

Interplay between Mechanical Robustness and Conductivity Retention

The performance of flexible conductive electrodes ultimately depends on the synergy between mechanical robustness and conductivity retention. Our data demonstrate that these are not independent metrics but are intrinsically linked through material structure, substrate interaction, and coating architecture. The crack-bridging mechanism of MXene is the quintessential example of this linkage: it mechanically bridges fractured ITO (enhancing robustness) while simultaneously restoring electrical pathways (preserving conductivity). Similarly, the 30 nm thickness optimum represents the point where the MXene network is continuous enough for low resistance yet compliant enough to withstand cyclic strain without catastrophic failure. This interdependence can be seen in Figure , which maps the Mechanical Stability Index (MSI) against initial sheet resistance for all substrate-thickness combinations. The optimal zones of high MSI with low resistance are occupied by PET and PI with 30–60 nm MXene, demonstrating that durability and conductivity can be concurrently maximized through informed material and design choices.

Rheological and Thermal Properties

Viscosity Analysis of MXene Aqueous Ink

The coating behavior of the Ti3C2T x MXene films can be directly correlated with the rheological characteristics of the aqueous ink and the resulting thickness evolution across successive coating cycles. The MXene ink exhibits a low and narrowly distributed viscosity in the range of 2.43–2.64 mPa·s for the three independent trials (P1 = 2.64, P2 = 2.55, P3 = 2.43), indicating a well-dispersed, weakly interacting colloidal system that can flow readily under shear while still containing a sufficient concentration of few-layer flakes to form continuous films. This rheological window is optimal for slot-die blade coating, as it enables uniform spreading without excessive edge beading or coffee-ring formation and minimizes the risk of flake restacking during solvent evaporation. Consistent with this, the film thickness measured by surface profilometry increases in a quasi-linear manner from 5.48 nm (T1) to 13.81 nm (T2), 31.65 nm (T3), 62.02 nm (T4), and 99.1 nm (T5), confirming that each additional pass deposits a well-defined incremental MXene layer rather than producing highly irregular or discontinuous coatings.

The combination of low viscosity and stable thickness scaling implies that the MXene flakes remain well-delaminated and homogeneously distributed in the ink, promoting dense in-plane percolation networks with strong interflake contact in the dried films. Consequently, the stability of the ink’s rheological behavior during coating is a decisive factor in how the electrode develops: it regulates the gradual buildup of film thickness, promotes the formation of a uniform and defect-free surface, and ensures that successive layers align in a way that maximizes electrical conductivity in multilayer MXene electrodes. ,

Thermal Conductivity Measurements

The study assessed the thermal properties of Ti3C2T x MXene ink throughout its working temperature range, and its respective thermal conductivity measurements along with thermal resistivity measurements are obtained, as seen in Figure . At 25 °C, the ink exhibits k ≈ 0.58–0.59 Wm1–K–1 with very low experimental error (∼0.005 Wm1–K–1), which demonstrates excellent measurement consistency between different tests and shows that the material maintains its structural integrity during room temperature measurements.

The thermal conductivity value decreases to 0.426 Wm1–K–1 at 50 °C, while thermal resistivity increases from ∼170 to ∼235 °C·cmW–1 because the partially hydrated MXene dispersion experiences greater phonon-defect and phonon–fluid interactions, which create obstacles that block effective heat transfer.

At 80 °C, the thermal conductivity rises significantly to 1.158 Wm1–K–1, while the thermal resistivity drops to 86.38 °C·cmW–1, suggesting a transition to a more thermally conductive state at elevated temperature. The interflake contact improvement, together with the interfacial thermal resistance reduction, creates this enhancement as thermal energy enables better alignment and packing of Ti3C2T x flakes, while decreased solvent viscosity enables phonon and electron heat transport through better material arrangement. This property proves useful for flexible conductive coatings that require heat dissipation during electrical operation.

Conclusion

This comprehensive investigation of MXene (Ti3C2T x ) coatings on five flexible substratesITO-PET, ITO-PEN, PET, PEN, and PIacross five thicknesses (∼5–100 nm) establishes definitive guidelines for next-generation flexible electronics. MXene fundamentally outperforms brittle ITO conductors under mechanical stress, with substrate choice and coating thickness serving as the primary performance levers. ITO-PEN with 100 nm MXene delivers superior electrical performance with the lowest sheet resistance (12.37 Ω/sq), ideal for transparent electrodes and heaters, despite moderate mechanical degradation. PET with 30 nm MXene, albeit with higher initial sheet resistance, emerges as the mechanical durability champion, retaining 91% conductivity after 500 bending cycles through optimal strain distribution and MXene flake bridging across microcracks.

Three failure mechanisms clarify the hierarchy: ITO-PET suffers catastrophic delamination from elastic modulus mismatch, PET/PI enables distributed microcracking with MXene connectivity preservation, and PEN exhibits nucleation-limited percolation failure. The 30 nm thickness balances electrical continuity with mechanical compliance across all substrates.

A 30 nm coating in PET is suited for wearables and sensors requiring >5-year durability because of its high MSI, but the conductivity needs to be improved. ITO-PEN-100 nm is best suited for high-conductivity displays and heaters, and PI-30 nm can be applied for thermal-demanding applications. MXene’s crack-bridging capability eliminates ITO’s mechanical Achilles heel, enabling reliable flexible electronics with unparalleled bending tolerance. This work provides substrate-thickness-performance mapping for scalable device manufacturing.
