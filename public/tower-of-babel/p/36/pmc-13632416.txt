Neuromuscular characteristics of individuals with chronic ankle instability during unilateral landing tasks: a meta-analysis of electromyographic studies
Authors: Mahsa Hakimi Poor, Reza Rajabi, Mohammad Hossein Alizadeh, Shahrzad Zandi, Ebrahim Ebrahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic ankle instability (CAI) is a prevalent consequence of lateral ankle sprain. Electromyographic (EMG) investigations examining neuromuscular control during unilateral landing tasks have produced inconsistent findings, particularly during the critical post-initialcontact (post-IC) phase. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize current evidence on post-IC muscle activation patterns in individuals with CAI during unilateral landing tasks.MethodsThis PRISMA-compliant review (PROSPERO: CRD420261415793) searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science from inception to June 2026. Cross-sectional studies comparing EMG activity between individuals with CAI and healthy controls during unilateral landing tasks were included. Study quality was assessed using Joanna Briggs Institute checklists; random-effects meta-analysis was performed with Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software, evaluating heterogeneity (I2, Q-test) and publication bias (Egger’s test).ResultsSeventeen studies involving 757 participants met the inclusion criteria. Compared with healthy controls, individuals with CAI demonstrated significantly lower post-IC activation of the peroneus longus (p = 0.005), gluteus medius (p = 0.027), and biceps femoris (p = 0.010), alongside significantly greater activation of the soleus (p = 0.036) and rectus femoris (p = 0.013). No significant differences were observed for the tibialis anterior (p = 0.453), medial gastrocnemius (p = 0.552), or lateral gastrocnemius (p = 0.845). Substantial heterogeneity was identified for several muscles, particularly the peroneus longus, tibialis anterior, soleus, and rectus femoris (I2 = 76–90%). Subgroup analyses suggested potential sex-related differences in peroneus longus activation patterns.ConclusionsIndividuals with CAI exhibit distinct neuromuscular alterations during the post-IC phase of unilateral landing tasks, characterized by reduced activation of key lateral ankle and proximal stabilizing muscles and increased activation of muscles associated with joint stiffening and impact attenuation. Rehabilitation strategies should extend beyond the ankle to target proximal muscle function and landing mechanics to optimize dynamic stability and reduce recurrent injury risk.Keywords: Ankle instability, Electromyography, Landing, Meta-analysis

Introduction

The ankle joint is a fundamental component of the human locomotor system [1], serving as the primary interface between the body and the ground during daily and athletic activities [2, 3]. Because of its crucial biomechanical role and exposure to substantial external loads, the ankle is among the most frequently injured joints [4, 5]. In particular, lateral ankle sprains represent one of the most common musculoskeletal injuries worldwide [6], accounting for 85% of all ankle sprains [7]. A considerable proportion of individuals who sustain a lateral ankle sprain fail to achieve complete recovery and subsequently develop chronic ankle instability (CAI) [8]. CAI is characterized by recurrent ankle sprains, persistent symptoms, reduced functional capacity, and deficits in postural control [9, 10]. Studies have shown that up to 40% of individuals experience residual symptoms after an initial ankle sprain [11, 12], highlighting the substantial burden of this condition. Moreover, new papers emphasize the interaction between mechanical insufficiencies and sensorimotor dysfunction. Damage to ligamentous mechanoreceptors following ankle sprain may disrupt afferent sensory feedback, leading to alterations in central nervous system processing and motor output [13, 14]. These neurophysiological adaptations can influence muscle activation patterns, movement coordination, joint stabilization strategies, and dynamic balance performance [15]. Consequently, individuals with CAI may exhibit altered neuromuscular control during tasks that challenge ankle stability [16, 17], particularly those involving rapid transitions between eccentric and concentric muscle actions [18].

Among the various functional tasks used to investigate dynamic stability, unilateral landing tasks have received considerable attention [19]. These tasks resemble situations in which ankle sprains commonly occur and therefore provide an ecologically valid framework for examining the neuromuscular mechanisms underlying CAI [20]. In addition, successful landing performance depends on precise feedforward and feedback control processes, including anticipatory muscle activation before ground contact and rapid reactive responses immediately after landing [21]. Impairments in these mechanisms may compromise dynamic joint stability and increase susceptibility to recurrent injury [22]. Additionally, electromyography (EMG) has emerged as one of the most valuable tools for investigating neuromuscular function in individuals with CAI [23]. Studies have shown differences in EMG activity in various muscles, including the tibialis anterior, biceps femoris, soleus, and medial gastrocnemius, in individuals with CAI compared to healthy controls [24, 25]. In contrast, some papers have demonstrated no significant differences regarding EMG activity of muscles between individuals with CAI and healthy peers in landing tasks [26, 27].

Despite a growing body of literature, the neuromuscular characteristics of individuals with CAI during unilateral landing tasks remain poorly understood. Existing EMG studies have produced conflicting findings regarding both the direction and magnitude of muscle activation alterations [28]. Importantly, much of the controversy appears to stem from methodological variability related to the timing of EMG assessment and various unilateral landing tasks. To date, evidence synthesis in this area has been limited. No synthesis has specifically examined electromyographic characteristics during unilateral landing tasks with particular attention to the post-IC phase, despite its central role in impact attenuation, dynamic joint stabilization, and injury prevention [29]. Therefore, the purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to examine the neuromuscular characteristics of individuals with CAI during unilateral landing tasks using electromyographic outcomes of muscles in post-IC phase.

Methods

This meta-analysis was carried out following the guidelines set forth by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [25]. The study was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database under registration number CRD420261415793.

Search strategy and keywords

We conducted a systematic search across Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed from the databases’ inception to 15 June 2026 to identify relevant studies. The references of pertinent papers were also carefully screened by two independent reviewers (E.E., M.H.) to see possibly more relevant literature and consulted with an expert (R.R) in the research area.

The following keyword combinations were used with the help of AND and OR operators as follows: (“ankle” OR “talofibular” OR “tibiotalar” OR “talocalcaneal” OR “talocrural” OR “unstable” OR “instability” OR “sprain” OR “chronic ankle instability” OR “ankle instability” OR “functional ankle instability” OR “talofibular ligament” OR “calcaneofibular ligament” OR “laxity” AND “neuromuscular control” OR “motor control” OR electromyograph* OR “EMG” OR “electromyogram” OR “muscle activity” OR “root mean square” OR “root-mean-square” OR “RMS” OR pattern* OR recruitment OR activ* OR coactiv* OR co-activ* OR cocontract* OR co-contract* OR timing OR onset OR offset OR delay OR integrat* OR “feedforward” AND “landing” OR “jump landing” OR “jump” OR “jumping” OR “touchdown” OR “land” OR “take-off” OR “jump-landing” OR “single leg landing” OR “one leg landing” OR “unilateral landing” OR “drop” OR “drop landing”).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study was designed based on the PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparison, and Outcomes) framework [30], including:

Population (P): Individuals diagnosed with CAI according to established clinical criteria (e.g., International Ankle Consortium guidelines or equivalent diagnostic definitions).

Population (P): Individuals diagnosed with CAI according to established clinical criteria (e.g., International Ankle Consortium guidelines or equivalent diagnostic definitions).

Exposure (E): Performance of unilateral landing tasks.

Exposure (E): Performance of unilateral landing tasks.

Comparison (C): Healthy individuals without ankle instability.

Comparison (C): Healthy individuals without ankle instability.

Outcome (O): EMG measures of neuromuscular activity (e.g., muscle activation).

In addition, studies were excluded if they included participants with acute ankle sprains, previous lower-extremity surgery, neurological disorders, vestibular impairments, or other musculoskeletal conditions that could influence neuromuscular control or landing performance. Furthermore, studies were excluded if they did not report EMG outcomes or provided insufficient quantitative data for extraction and meta-analysis. Review articles, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, editorials, letters, case reports, dissertations, study protocols, animal studies, cadaveric investigations, and computational simulation studies were not considered.

Study selection

In this study, two authors (M.H., E.E.) independently examined and selected the articles’ titles and abstracts according to the inclusion criteria and PRISMA standard methodology, utilizing a standardized Excel data extraction sheet [31]. All eligible human studies and trials published up to the search date (15 June 2026) were considered for inclusion. The supervising author addressed and assessed discrepancies between the researchers (R.R.). Their searched records were imported into EndNote 20. The software was also used to remove duplicate articles.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent researchers (M.H. and E.E.) conducted the methodological quality and risk-of-bias assessment using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools [32]. The specific appraisal checklist was selected according to the research design of each included study, including a cross-sectional design. Any disagreements between the two reviewers were first discussed to reach consensus, and unresolved discrepancies were adjudicated by the supervising author (R.R.). Data extraction was independently performed by the same two reviewers using a standardized Microsoft Excel sheet developed for this review. The extracted information included the first author’s name and year of publication, study design, participant characteristics (including demographic data and chronic ankle instability status), EMG measurement outcomes, assessment methods, data collection instruments, and main findings. Following independent extraction, both reviewers compared their datasets to ensure consistency and accuracy. Any inconsistencies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with the supervising author. The extracted data were subsequently organized and summarized in Table 1 for further analysis and synthesis.

Data analysis

We performed all statistical analyses using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) software, version 4.0 (Biostat Inc., Englewood, NJ, USA) [46]. For each eligible study, we extracted the mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size for both the CAI and healthy control groups. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated using Hedges’ g with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [47]. Hedges’ g was selected because it provides an unbiased estimate of the standardized effect size by applying a correction for small-sample bias, which was relevant given the sample sizes of several included studies [48]. Effect sizes were interpreted according to Cohen’s criteria as trivial (< 0.20), small (0.20–0.49), moderate (0.50–0.79), and large (≥ 0.80) [49]. Data heterogeneity was assessed using the Q-test and the I2 statistic, with I2 values representing the percentage of total variation due to heterogeneity. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered to represent low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [50]. Publication bias was evaluated with Egger’s regression test, where P-values < 0.05 were considered significant [51]. We focused on the participants, interventions, and outcome measures when assessing the clinical heterogeneity of the included reports.

Results

A preliminary search across the selected databases retrieved 921 records (Fig. 1). All references were imported into EndNote, where duplicates were detected and eliminated, resulting in 387 unique articles. Following a review of titles and abstracts, 66 articles were deemed eligible for further evaluation, while the remaining records were excluded. Full-text screening was then carried out on 24 articles, of which 18 satisfied the pre-established inclusion criteria.

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process

Study characteristics

A total of 17 cross-sectional studies published between 2004 and 2025 were included in this meta-analysis, encompassing a combined sample of 757 participants. All studies compared individuals with CAI to healthy controls during unilateral landing tasks. Sample sizes ranged from 20 to 200 participants per study, with CAI and control groups generally matched in numbers. The gender distribution varied across studies: some included exclusively male participants [24, 26, 39], others recruited exclusively female participants [25, 45], while the remaining studies included mixed-gender samples. Regarding the landing tasks employed, the majority of studies utilized single-leg jump landing or drop landing protocols, while others incorporated more sport-specific variations such as diagonal single-leg rebound jumps [24, 39], unilateral side jump landing [34], single-leg forward jump followed by landing [35], single-leg hop tests [40, 42], and single-leg drop-vertical jumps [37]. Participant populations included recreational athletes, collegiate soccer players, physically active adults, and general young individuals. The primary outcome measure across all included studies was surface EMG activity, assessed during the post-IC phase of landing. The muscles examined were the peroneus longus (PL), tibialis anterior (TA), medial gastrocnemius (MG), lateral gastrocnemius (LG), and soleus, at the ankle level, as well as proximal muscles including the gluteus medius (Gmed), rectus femoris (RF), and biceps femoris (BF). Initial contact was identified using force plate thresholds, foot-switch systems, or motion capture events, whereas the analyzed post-IC intervals ranged from the first 50 ms [25] after landing to 100–200 ms [26, 37, 42, 43] or the entire landing phase.

Meta-analysis

The EMG activity of the selected muscles (mentioned above) was extracted and analyzed between the CAI and healthy control groups at the post-IC during unilateral-landing tasks across the selected studies, with the results presented as follows.

Peroneus longus

Ten studies [24, 25, 27, 33, 35, 39, 42–45] encompassing a total of 307 participants were included for Peroneus Longus. The results of the analysis regarding the EMG activity of the Peroneus Longus muscle revealed a significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.005, 95% CI = 0.100–0.577), indicating greater EMG activity of the Peroneus Longus in the healthy control group (Fig. 2). The Q-test and I2 test results showed significant heterogeneity across Peroneus Longus (p < 0.001, I2= 90%). According to the high heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was done and the results showed no significant differences in studies that included both genders (p = 0.374, 95% CI= − 0.154 to 0.410) or exclusively female participants (p = 0.647, 95% CI= − 0.324 to 0.098), whereas it reached significance in studies involving exclusively male participants (p = 0.000, 95% CI = 3.616–5.895), suggesting a gender-related influence on the difference in Peroneus Longus EMG activity between the two groups. Egger’s test showed that publication bias was significant in Peroneus Longus (p = 0.001). The trim and fill method was used to assess the possible effect of future studies on the results. The results showed that adding one randomly hypothetical study to the right side of the graph could not change the meta-analysis results (Fig. 3).

Tibialis anterior

Nine studies [24–26, 35, 39, 42–45] encompassing a total of 278 participants were included for Tibialis Anterior. The results of the analysis regarding the EMG activity of the Tibialis Anterior muscle did not reveal a significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.453, 95% CI= − 0.568 to 0.254) (Fig. 4). The Q-test and I2 test results showed significant heterogeneity across Tibialis Anterior (p < 0.001, I2= 84%). Based on the observed heterogeneity, subgroup analysis showed no significant differences in studies that included both genders (p = 0.684, 95% CI= − 1.030 to 0.675), exclusively female participants (p = 0.312, 95% CI= − 0.739 to 0.236), or exclusively male participants (p = 0.211, 95% CI= − 0.621 to 2.816), indicating that gender does not appear to influence the difference in Tibialis Anterior EMG activity between the two groups. Moreover, Egger’s test showed that publication bias was not significant in Tibialis Anterior (p = 0.230).

Quality assessment

The JBI Checklist provides a systematic and valid approach for assessing the methodological quality of cross-sectional studies (Table 2). The overall quality of a study is determined by the extent to which it satisfies the checklist criteria, with scores used to classify studies into high, moderate, or low-quality categories. Studies in which 7 or 8 of the eight items are answered affirmatively (87.5–100%) are considered high quality. Those meeting 5 or 6 of the eight criteria with a “Yes” response (62.5–75%) are classified as moderate quality, while studies achieving affirmative responses on four or fewer items (50% or below) are rated as low quality.

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized available EMG evidence on post-IC phase neuromuscular activity during unilateral landing tasks in individuals with CAI. The principal findings indicate that individuals with CAI exhibit reduced activation of the peroneus longus, gluteus medius, and biceps femoris, accompanied by increased activation of the soleus and rectus femoris during the landing phase. In contrast, no significant differences were observed for the tibialis anterior, medial gastrocnemius, or lateral gastrocnemius. By focusing specifically on the post-IC phase, our findings reveal a complex, muscle-specific pattern of neuromuscular reorganization that extends well beyond the ankle joint, a finding with significant implications for understanding the pathomechanics of CAI and guiding targeted rehabilitation strategies.

One of the most clinically relevant findings of this meta-analysis was the reduced post-landing activation of the peroneus longus in individuals with CAI. The peroneus longus is the primary dynamic stabilizer of the lateral ankle, and its timely activation is essential for resisting the inversion moment generated at initial contact [52]. Reduced activation of this muscle may compromise the ability to rapidly control frontal-plane ankle motion immediately following ground contact [53], thereby increasing mechanical stress on already compromised lateral ligament structures [54]. This observation aligns with contemporary sensorimotor models of CAI, which propose that damage to ligamentous mechanoreceptors following ankle sprain impairs afferent feedback and subsequently alters motor output [55]. The diminished peroneal activity identified in the present study may therefore represent a manifestation of disrupted reflexive stabilization mechanisms and impaired sensorimotor integration [56]. Moreover, subgroup analyses suggested that sex may influence peroneus longus activation patterns, with significant differences observed in male cohorts. Although this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of studies, it raises the possibility that sex-specific neuromuscular strategies may contribute to variability in CAI presentations. Differences in lower-limb alignment, muscle strength characteristics, hormonal influences, and movement strategies between males and females may partially explain these findings and warrant further investigation [57, 58].

In contrast to the reduction in peroneal activity, individuals with CAI demonstrated greater soleus activation during landing. This finding may reflect a compensatory neuromuscular strategy aimed at enhancing ankle stiffness and increasing sagittal-plane control when frontal-plane stabilization is compromised [59, 60]. The soleus contributes to postural regulation and shock absorption during weight acceptance [61]. Increased activation of this muscle may therefore represent an attempt to compensate for impaired lateral ankle control by increasing overall joint rigidity [33]. While such a strategy may provide short-term stability, excessive reliance on muscular stiffening could reduce movement adaptability and increase loading on adjacent structures [62]. In addition, elevated soleus activity may be interpreted as an adaptive but potentially inefficient compensation for persistent sensorimotor deficits [34]. Additionally, the increased activation of the rectus femoris provides further evidence that neuromuscular adaptations in CAI extend beyond the ankle joint. The rectus femoris contributes to knee stabilization and deceleration during landing through eccentric control of knee flexion [63]. Enhanced activation of this muscle may indicate that individuals with CAI shift the demand for impact attenuation proximally to compensate for reduced ankle stability [64]. These findings are aligned with the concept of regional interdependence [65], whereby impairments at one joint influence movement strategies throughout the kinetic chain. Increased quadriceps activation may therefore represent an attempt to maintain functional performance despite impaired distal control [66]. However, this strategy may alter normal force distribution during landing and potentially increase mechanical demands at the knee joint.

The proximal adaptations observed in the gluteus medius and biceps femoris provide additional support for the existence of widespread neuromuscular alterations in CAI. The gluteus medius plays a critical role in frontal-plane pelvic stability and lower-extremity alignment during single-leg tasks [67]. Reduced activation of this muscle may impair control of hip adduction and internal rotation [68], potentially contributing to altered lower-limb kinematics observed previously in individuals with CAI [69]. Likewise, decreased biceps femoris activity may diminish the capacity to control hip and knee motion during landing, further compromising dynamic stability [70]. These findings support growing evidence that CAI should not be viewed solely as an ankle pathology but rather as a condition involving alterations across the entire lower-extremity neuromuscular system.

Furthermore, no significant differences were identified for the tibialis anterior, medial gastrocnemius, or lateral gastrocnemius. The absence of significant findings for these muscles may indicate that not all components of the neuromuscular system are equally affected by CAI [40]. Alternatively, the substantial heterogeneity observed for some muscles suggests that methodological differences among studies may have obscured true effects. Variations in landing protocols, participant characteristics, athletic background, chronicity of symptoms, EMG normalization procedures, and definitions of the post-IC phase likely contributed to inconsistent findings. The particularly high heterogeneity observed for the peroneus longus, tibialis anterior, soleus, and rectus femoris emphasizes the need for greater methodological standardization in future EMG investigations. An important source of heterogeneity among the included studies was the variability in EMG assessment methodology. Although all studies employed surface EMG to evaluate muscle activity during unilateral landing tasks, considerable differences were identified in signal acquisition and processing procedures. Specifically, studies differed in EMG normalization methods, with some expressing muscle activity as a percentage of maximal voluntary isometric contraction (%MVIC), whereas others normalized the signal to peak or mean activity recorded during the landing task or reported root mean square (RMS) amplitudes using study-specific reference values. In addition, the definition of the post-IC analysis window varied considerably across studies, ranging from the first 50 ms [25] after ground contact to longer post-landing intervals or phase-based analyses [42, 43]. Variability was also evident in electrode placement protocols, EMG filtering parameters, sampling frequencies, and the unilateral landing tasks used (e.g., drop landing, forward jump landing, rebound landing, or side-jump landing), all of which may influence the magnitude and timing of recorded muscle activity. Such methodological inconsistencies likely contributed to the substantial statistical heterogeneity observed for several muscles, particularly the peroneus longus, tibialis anterior, soleus, and rectus femoris, and may partly explain the conflicting findings reported in the literature. Consequently, the pooled effect estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and future investigations would benefit from greater standardization of EMG acquisition, normalization procedures, and reporting according to internationally accepted recommendations. The present findings can be interpreted within the framework of contemporary neurophysiological theories of CAI. Recent evidence suggests that recurrent ankle sprains produce not only peripheral mechanical insufficiencies but also central nervous system adaptations affecting sensory processing, motor planning, and movement execution [33, 71]. Altered activation patterns observed across both distal and proximal muscles may therefore reflect centrally mediated changes in motor control rather than localized muscular dysfunction alone [72]. Such adaptations may develop as protective responses following injury but persist over time, ultimately contributing to recurrent instability episodes and reduced movement efficiency.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of adopting comprehensive rehabilitation approaches that address both local ankle impairments and proximal neuromuscular deficits. Traditional rehabilitation programs often emphasize strengthening of the peroneal musculature and balance training. While these interventions remain important, the current evidence suggests that rehabilitation should also target hip stabilizers and posterior-chain musculature to restore coordinated lower-extremity control during dynamic tasks. Furthermore, landing retraining interventions designed to optimize neuromuscular activation patterns may represent a valuable component of rehabilitation programs for individuals with CAI.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Most included studies employed cross-sectional designs, preventing causal inference regarding whether altered muscle activation contributes to CAI development or emerges as a consequence of the condition. Sample sizes were generally modest, and substantial heterogeneity was observed across several analyses. Additionally, the limited number of studies investigating proximal muscles reduced the strength of evidence for some outcomes. Furthermore, substantial methodological variability in EMG acquisition and processing, including differences in normalization procedures, signal processing parameters, and landing protocols, limited direct comparability across studies and likely contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Although the use of standardized mean differences partially accounted for differences in outcome scales, it cannot fully eliminate the influence of methodological inconsistencies on pooled estimates. Additionally, the absence of a uniform definition of initial contact and standardized post-IC analysis windows across studies limited direct comparability of EMG outcomes and may have contributed to the observed between-study heterogeneity. Despite these limitations, the present review provides the most comprehensive synthesis to date of post-IC neuromuscular activity during unilateral landing tasks in individuals with CAI and offers important insights into the sensorimotor mechanisms underlying persistent ankle instability. Future studies should adopt standardized definitions of IC and consistent post-landing analysis windows to improve comparability across investigations.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis review demonstrates that individuals with CAI exhibit distinct neuromuscular alterations during the post-IC phase of unilateral landing tasks. Specifically, reduced activation of the peroneus longus, gluteus medius, and biceps femoris, together with increased activation of the soleus and rectus femoris, suggests the presence of both local ankle dysfunction and proximal compensatory adaptations throughout the lower-extremity kinetic chain. Future research should also explore longitudinal designs to determine whether these neuromuscular alterations precede recurrent sprains or emerge as adaptive consequences, as this distinction has profound implications for injury prevention programs.
