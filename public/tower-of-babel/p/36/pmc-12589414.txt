Effect of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell on optical and anti-icing properties on polydimethylsiloxane-modified SiO₂ composite thin films
Authors: Faezeh Saeedi, Akbar Eshaghi, Mazaher Ramazani, Hossien Zabolian, Alireza Nasr Esfahani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, the effect of PAA@SiO₂ core-shell particles on polydimethylsiloxane-SiO₂ nanoparticles modified by TMMS and HDMS thin films was investigated. Initially, SiO₂ nanoparticles were modified with HDMS and TMMS. After that, the optimal amount of the PAA@SiO₂ core-shell particles was added to the modified silica solution. The PDMS-M/SiO₂-PAA@SiO₂ CSPs solution was prepared and applied on the glass substrates by the immersion method. The water contact angle, transparency, chemical composition, surface morphology, and environmental stability of the thin film were investigated using AFM and UV/vis spectroscopy, FTIR, XPS, FE-SEM, anti-icing, and UV resistance evaluation tests. The results showed that the hydrophobic coating was produced with a contact angle of 110°, a sliding angle of 10°, and a transparency of 92.87%. In addition, the anti-icing test showed an increase in the icing time for a water droplet from 270 s to 960 s and a decrease in the shear stress from 257 kPa to 87 kPa in the PAA@SiO₂ core-shell coated glass with respect to the bare glass.

Introduction

Anti-icing and superhydrophobic thin films have attracted considerable attention for mitigating ice nucleation and adhesion in cold environments1. These coatings possess applications across diverse sectors, including automotive glazing, civil infrastructure, wind turbine systems, aerospace components, and optical surveillance devices. Strategies such as plasmonic metamaterials, nanocomposite architectures (e.g., PFA, SiO₂, ZnO/PDMS), and bioinspired designs (superhydrophobic layers, SLIPS) effectively reduce ice accumulation, delay crystallization, and maintain structural or aerodynamic performance. Furthermore, transparent PDMS/SiO₂ thin films with high optical transmittance (> 90%) are particularly promising for preserving clarity in imaging optics such as security cameras under cold and humid conditions2–8. For example, Gong et al9. applied a stable superhydrophobic thin film using PDMS and hydrophobic SiO₂ nanoparticles on a glass surface by spraying method. In recent years, inorganic silica as a shell and organic polymer as a core in the core-shell structure have attracted significant attention and have been the subject of extensive scientific research. The SiO₂ shell can impart important properties to the polymer core. Wang et al12. synthesized PAA@SiO₂ core-shells with controllable size and thickness using a modified Stöber method. The interaction between the polymer chain and the hydroxyl group of the SiO₂ nanoparticles caused the deposition of SiO₂ nanoparticles on the shell. Yuan et al13. created hollow silica spheres using an alkaline-catalyzed sol and polyacrylic acid (PAA) and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) as precursors. Hollow silica spheres are applied using the dip-coating technique on alkali aluminosilicate glass, producing anti-reflective nanostructured silica coatings with varying thicknesses and porosities. These hollow silica spheres have an inner diameter of 20 nm and an outer thickness of 6–20 nm. In the wavelength range of 300 to 900 nm, the average light transmission of the coating at normal incidence is between 94% and 96.9%, which is a significant increase compared to the 91.8% of the bare glass surface.

In this study, we present a multifunctional nanostructured thin film that combines hydrophobicity, optical transparency, and anti-icing properties, specifically designed for optical devices operating in cold and humid environments. The innovation lies in the synergistic integration of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), SiO₂ nanoparticles modified with TMMS and HDMS, and PAA@SiO₂ Core–Shell Particles (CSPs) into a single coating system. Unlike previous works that primarily addressed either transparency or anti-icing performance, this research adopts an integrated strategy by simultaneously optimizing surface chemistry, microstructure, and nanoparticle dispersion to achieve high transparency, reduced ice adhesion, and delayed freeze–thaw cycles. The incorporation of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles play a crucial role in tailoring surface roughness and interfacial energy, enhancing droplet mobility and minimizing ice accumulation without compromising light transmission. For the first time, the combined effect of dual-silane-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles and PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles structures within a PDMS matrix is investigated, leading to the fabrication of a durable, transparent, and anti-icing coating on glass substrates. Moreover, the simple, scalable, and dip-coating-compatible fabrication process offers practical potential for applications in security camera lenses and other outdoor optical systems.

Experimental method

Materials

Thin film was fabricated using high-purity ingredients. such as ethanol (99%, Merck), polyacrylic acid ((PAA, average M.W. 5000, 50 wt%), 98%, Exir), butanol (98%, Scharlau), hexamethyldisilane (HMDS, 98%, Sigma Aldrich), tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS, 98%, Sigma Aldrich), trimethoxymethylsilane (TMMS, 98%, Sigma Aldrich), polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, 98%, Merck), butyltin dilaurate (DBTDL, 99%, Merck), ammonium hydroxide (32%, NH4OH, 99%, Merck), and toluene (99%, Sigma Aldrich).

Preparation of the glass substrates

The soda–lime glass substrates were cleaned prior to deposition. Surface contaminants were first removed using soap and water, followed by washing with ethanol and deionized water for 5 min. The cleaned substrates were then dried at 100 °C for 30 min.

Preparation of the PAA@SiO2 Core–Shell particles

0.16 g of PAA was dissolved in 6 mL of ammonium hydroxide and dispersed for 30 min using an ultrasonic device. The resulting solution was slowly added dropwise to 100 mL of ethanol and stirred. 1.5 mL of TEOS was divided into five equal portions and added to the reaction solution, which was vigorously stirred five times at 1 h intervals. Then, it was stirred continuously for 3 h, and the solution was aged for 36 h. The resulting solution was placed in a microtube (1 mL) and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of ethanol was added to the microtube. The mixture was placed in an ultrasonic device for 30 min, yielding PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles dispersed in ethanol. Figure 1a illustrates the preparation of the PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles.

Preparation of the PDMS solution with SiO2 nanoparticles co-modification by TMMS and HDMS and PAA@SiO₂ core-shell particles

To prepare HDMS-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles, 2 mL of TEOS was first dissolved in 30 mL of butanol under magnetic stirring. After 5 min, 2 mL of HDMS was added dropwise to the solution. Following 30 min of stirring, 3 mL of deionized water was introduced in three steps over 20 min. The solution was then stirred for 2 h and aged for 24 h. For the synthesis of TMMS-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles, 2 mL of TEOS was dissolved in 11 mL of butanol under magnetic stirring. After 5 min, 2 mL of TMMS was slowly added and stirred for 30 min. Separately, 1.1 mL of ammonium hydroxide was dissolved in 22 mL of butanol and stirred for 30 min. The previously prepared TMMS–TEOS solution was then slowly added and stirred for 3 h, followed by aging for 48 h.To prepare the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) solution with a PDMS/DBTDL ratio of 10:1, PDMS was dissolved in 30 mL of toluene. Subsequently, 13.5 mL of HDMS-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles and 15 mL of TMMS-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles were added to the PDMS solution. Finally, 0.2 mL of PAA\@SiO₂ core–shell particles was added in ten successive steps. The preparation process of the solution is illustrated in Fig. 1b.

Thin film characterization

The morphology of the core–shell particles was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi HF3300), while field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Hitachi S4160, cold field emission, accelerating voltage 15 kV) was employed to study the surface morphology and film thickness. Prior to SEM imaging, the samples were coated with a \~10 nm gold layer using a sputtering process under an argon atmosphere at 0.1 mbar, with a current of 20 mA for 60 s, to ensure adequate conductivity. Atomic force microscopy (AFM, Dimension Icon, Bruker, Germany) in tapping mode was used to measure surface roughness, using a silicon cantilever (TESPA-V2) with a nominal tip radius of 8 nm, resonance frequency of \~320 kHz, and applied contact force of 1–10 nN. Roughness parameters (Ra, Rq) were obtained from at least three different 10 × 10 μm² regions per sample.

Surface chemistry was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Perkin Elmer Spectrum 400) with a spectral resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ and averaging over 32 scans per sample. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Kratos Axis Ultra DLD) was used to investigate the chemical composition of the thin films, focusing specifically on the C 1 s peak. Survey spectra were collected from 0 to 1200 eV, and high-resolution spectra were recorded for the C 1 s region. Pass energies were set to 160 eV for survey scans and 20 eV for high-resolution scans, and a low-energy electron flood gun was applied for charge neutralization. Peak fitting and quantitative analysis were performed using Casa XPS software following standard protocols14. Optical properties were measured using a UV–VIS–NIR spectrophotometer (Shimadzu MPC-3100) in the 400–800 nm wavelength range. Transmittance and reflectance were recorded at five different positions on each sample. Water contact angle (WCA) and slip angle (SA) measurements were conducted using a goniometer system (Krüss DSA100, Germany). Sessile drops of 5 µL deionized water were deposited on the film surface, and the droplet profile was fitted using the Young–Laplace method. Slip angles were measured by gradually tilting the sample stage until the droplet began to move. At least five measurements per sample were performed for both WCA and SA, and the mean ± 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:^\circ\:\:$$\end{document}were reported.

Anti-icing performance was evaluated by placing coated and uncoated glass substrates in a refrigerator maintained at − 12 °C. Water droplets (~ 5 µL) at an initial temperature of 20 °C were deposited using a precision needle injector tube. Freezing behavior, droplet mobility, and shape evolution were recorded using a high-speed digital camera (Dino-Lite, AM-4515T8-Edge) at 500 fps. Ice adhesion strength was measured using a Jikan IAT-40 device at a surface temperature of − 17 °C and ambient temperature of 30 °C. Each test was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility. Film hardness was determined using the pencil hardness test according to ASTM D3363, with three measurements per sample to obtain. The UV stability of the optimized films was assessed by exposing the samples in a UV testing chamber equipped with fluorescent UV lamps. After 604 h of exposure, WCA and optical transmittance were measured at designated intervals, with each measurement repeated three times to monitor any changes.

Results and discussion

The water contact angle (WCA) and slip angle (SA) of the PDMS-M/SiO₂ thin films containing PAA\@SiO₂ core–shell particles are presented in Fig. 2. As shown, the WCA initially increases from 107° to 110° for the sample with 1.6 mL of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles, while the SA decreases from 25° to 10°. When the PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles content is further increased from 1.6 mL to 2.0 mL, the WCA decreases from 110° to 95°, and the SA increases from 10° to 30°. These trends are consistent with previous studies in the field, indicating that excessive addition of core–shell particles can reduce hydrophobicity and increase droplet pinning on the surface11,15–17, the hydrolysis reaction of the TEOS and polycondensation with HDMS leads to the formation of the HDMS-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles, while the hydrolysis reaction of the TEOS and polycondensation with TMMS leads to the formation of the TMMS-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles. Modified SiO₂ nanoparticles added to the polydimethylsiloxane solution have methyl groups on their surface, which increases their hydrophobicity18. also, according to the research done on the dissolution of PAA in ammonia12,19–21, which leads to the formation of the NH₄+ on the surface, and after reaction with TEOS, it hydrolyzes on the surface and forms hydroxyl groups. By making PAA@SiO₂ core-shell particles with hydroxyl groups on the surface, when PAA@SiO₂ core-shell particles are added to the solution, some of these hydroxyl groups are replaced by methyl groups, which causes a slight increase in WCA. However, the WCA increases beyond this value, leading to the dominance of hydroxyl groups. As a result, the surface groups reduce the WCA. The transmittance spectra of PDMS-M/SiO₂ thin films containing different amounts of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles are shown in Fig. 4. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the transmittance of the PDMS-M/SiO₂ thin film with 1.6 mL of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles is approximately 92.78% at 550 nm. In comparison, the PDMS-M/SiO₂ film without PAA@SiO₂ particles exhibits a transmittance of 93.24%, while bare glass shows a transmittance of 89.6%. According to Fig. 4b, the reflectance values for PDMS-M/SiO₂ with 1.6 mL PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles, PDMS-M/SiO₂, and bare glass are 4.32%, 3.98%, and 8.13%, respectively. The slightly lower transmittance of the PDMS-M/SiO₂ thin film containing 1.6 mL PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles compared to the particle-free PDMS-M/SiO₂ film is attributed to increased light scattering caused by the core–shell particles. Nevertheless, an optimal amount of core–shell particles can minimize light scattering and enhance optical transmittance.

An essential factor contributing to the high optical transmittance of PAA@SiO₂-based thin films is the presence of an effective refractive index gradient at the interface. As demonstrated by Yu et al.22 in their investigation of SiO₂@TiO₂ core–shell particles, when the refractive index of the shell closely approximates that of the surrounding medium (e.g., air or glass), the abrupt mismatch at the material interface is significantly reduced, leading to lower surface reflection and diminished scattering. In the case of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles, the outer SiO₂ shell (with a refractive index of ~ 1.45) facilitates a gradual optical transition between the particle surface and the environment, thereby minimizing light deflection and enhancing transmittance.

Additionally, the porous architecture of the coatings plays a critical role in modulating light–matter interactions. In highly porous films, air occupies the voids between particles, effectively lowering the average refractive index (neff) compared to dense or compact layers. When neff is closely matched with that of the substrate or ambient medium, Fresnel reflections are significantly suppressed, resulting in enhanced light transmission. However, a reduction in interparticle spacing—caused, for instance, by increased shell thickness or particle aggregation—leads to a decrease in effective porosity, promoting stronger light scattering. Such morphological evolution may transition the optical appearance of the thin film from transparent to semi-transparent or even opaque, depending on the degree of structural densification23.

The FTIR spectrum of the PAA@SiO₂ core-shell nanoparticles (Fig. 5b) provides clear evidence of the chemical composition and structural features of both the core and the shell components. Firstly, the broad absorption peak observed around 3400 cm⁻¹ is primarily attributed to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl (–OH) groups. These hydroxyl groups are predominantly present on the surface of the SiO₂ shell as silanol groups (Si–OH), indicated by the weaker peak near 3200 cm⁻¹. It is important to note that this broad peak can also incorporate contributions from residual solvent molecules, particularly ethanol, which is used during the dispersion of the PAA@SiO₂ core-shell particles. This solvent presence can contribute to the intensity and breadth of the absorption band, but the dominant source remains the silanol groups intrinsic to the silica surface. The core of the nanoparticle, composed of PAA, is confirmed by the presence of characteristic peaks associated with its functional groups. The strong peak at 1655 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the stretching vibrations of the carbonyl group (C = O), a signature functional group of PAA. Additionally, the region around 1300 cm⁻¹ reveals peaks associated with the C–O stretching vibrations, indicative of ester or carboxylate groups within the PAA structure, further validating the presence of the polymer core. Turning to the SiO₂ shell, the spectrum shows a pronounced peak in the range of 1000–1100 cm⁻¹, which corresponds to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of the Si–O–Si bonds forming the silica network. This is a hallmark of the silica shell structure. Complementarily, a peak observed near 800 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the bending vibrations of Si–O bonds, providing additional confirmation of the successful formation of the SiO₂ shell20,27.

Figure 6 presents the FTIR spectra of the PDMS-M/SiO₂ and PDMS-M/SiO₂-PAA@SiO₂ CSPs solutions. The sharp absorption peak observed at 1062 cm⁻¹ in the PDMS-M/SiO₂ sample is attributed to the stretching vibration of the Si–O–Si bond, confirming the formation of a silica network. The absorption bands at 1780 cm⁻¹ and 806 cm⁻¹ correspond to the stretching vibrations of Si–OH groups, and their increased intensity after the incorporation of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles indicates a higher density of surface hydroxyl groups in the thin film. The peak located at 1655 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the stretching vibration of the carbonyl group (C = O). Moreover, the region around 1300 cm⁻¹ exhibits absorption peaks related to C–O stretching vibrations, which are characteristic of ester or carboxylate groups within the PAA structure, further confirming the presence of the polymer core. The bands near 1365 cm⁻¹ and 2986 cm⁻¹ are associated with the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of CH₃ groups, respectively, while the peak at 1275 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the Si–C stretching and –CH₃ bending vibrations.

The broad absorption band near 3400 cm⁻¹ is mainly attributed to surface hydroxyl groups on the nanoparticles. Although residual solvents such as butanol (used during SiO₂ modification), ethanol (used for dispersing PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles), and toluene (from the PDMS solution) may contribute slightly, the dominant contribution arises from the hydroxyl groups at the particle interfaces. The incorporation of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles increase the availability of surface hydroxyls, enabling hydrogen bonding with the PDMS matrix and stabilizing a cohesive, cross-linked silica–polymer network28,29. The observed increase in the intensity of the –CH₃ absorption peaks explains the enhanced hydrophobicity of the modified SiO₂ nanoparticles, as the methyl groups reduce the surface energy and limit water adsorption. Furthermore, the increased intensity of the Si–O–Si peak indicates the formation of additional bonds between TMMS- and HDMS-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles, PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles, and the PDMS matrix. These interactions reinforce the silica network, improve the mechanical and thermal stability of the films, and contribute to superior hydrophobicity and resistance to thermal or ice-induced stress26,27,30–33.

The deconvoluted C 1 s spectrum of PDMS-M/SiO₂–PAA@SiO₂ CSPs (Fig. 7; Table 1) shows significant surface chemical modifications compared to the thin PDMS-M/SiO₂ layer without core–shell particles, confirming the successful incorporation of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles into the polymer matrix. In the PDMS-M/SiO₂ layer, the C–Si component accounts for only 14.2%, and the surface is primarily composed of PDMS chains and methyl groups (C–H/C–C). The C–Ox component, originating from residual butanol, contributes approximately 26.2%. Upon addition of the core–shell particles, the C–Si fraction nearly doubles to 29.2%, indicating surface enrichment with silicon–carbon species (Si–C/Si–O–C), likely due to PDMS chain reorganization and surface localization of the CSPs. The C–Ox fraction decreases to 14.4%, reflecting partial coverage of the surface by the SiO₂ shell of the CSPs and the PDMS matrix; in other words, surface oxygenated groups previously present on PDMS-M/SiO₂ are now replaced or encapsulated by the core–shell particles. Additionally, a new peak at ~ 288.9 eV corresponding to O–C = O appears exclusively in PDMS-M/SiO₂–PAA@SiO₂ CSPs, providing direct evidence of carboxyl functionalities derived from the PAA core. These changes indicate that the surface of PDMS-M/SiO₂–PAA@SiO₂ CSPs is simultaneously silicon-enriched and functionalized with active carboxyl groups, which likely enhances CSP adhesion to the PDMS matrix and improves the hydrophobic and anti-icing performance of the thin layer. Overall, XPS results provide strong and direct evidence for the successful incorporation of PAA@SiO₂ particles and the chemical reorganization of the surface, establishing a solid foundation for understanding the enhanced surface properties of PDMS-M/SiO₂–PAA@SiO₂ CSPs18,20.

According to the AFM images in the Fig. 10, the surface roughness of the PDMS-M/SiO₂ thin film is 2.211 nm, which is increased compared to the surface roughness of the PDMS thin film, which is 1.848 nm. The surface roughness of the PDMS-M/SiO₂-PAA@SiO₂ CSPs thin film is 4.46 nm.

On a hydrophobic surface, water molecules tend to escape from contact with the surface and remain in the form of droplets. The increase in roughness reduces the contact of the liquid with on hydrophobic surface, and the contact angle increases. As a result, the water droplet becomes more spherical and slides on the surface, indicating the improvement of the hydrophobicity. Therefore, one of the reasons for the increase in the hydrophobicity of the PDMS-M/SiO₂-PAA@SiO₂ CSPs thin film is the increase in surface roughness9,34.

Figure 11; Table 2 shows the ice formation on the bare and coated glass. a water droplet on the bare glass surface forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules due to the abundance of the hydroxyl groups on the glass surface. These bonds attract water molecules and reduce the water contact angle. A droplet on the bare glass surface begins to ice in approximately 200 s and completely within 270 s. The icing for the coated glass started after 300 s, and the droplet was fully iced after 450 s. In the PDMS-M/SiO₂ thin film, icing begins at 680 s and completes at 710 s. However, in the PDMS-M/SiO₂-PAA@SiO₂ CSPs thin film, icing starts at 760 s and finishes at 910 s. According to the Tabel 2, the shear stress between ice and a water droplet decreases from 257 kPa for the PDMS thin film to 87 kPa for the PDMS-M/SiO₂-PAA@SiO₂ CSPs thin film18,35. There are two main reasons for the delay in the icing of a water droplet on this thin film. The first reason is the presence of the hydrophobic methyl groups on the surface of the modified SiO₂ nanoparticles, which reduces surface energy, causing the water droplet to ice more slowly than on the PDMS thin film. The second reason is the creation of nanoscale roughness on the thin film due to the presence of SiO₂ nanoparticles modified with HDMS and TMMS, as well as PAA@SiO₂ core-shell particles. For a droplet on the PDMS thin film, it is assumed that latent heat is released during the formation of the ice crystals and transferred to the substrate, leading to a decrease in the droplet’s temperature.

In contrast, on smooth surfaces such as bare glass or thin PDMS films, heat transfer occurs at a faster rate, leading to a higher icing rate of the water droplets. With increased surface roughness, the wetting state progresses according to the Wenzel model, increasing the contact angle and reducing ice adhesion strength. Therefore, hydrophobic thin films combined with nanoparticles naturally slow the icing process of the water droplets through a combination of increased contact angle, reduced thermal conduction, and an elevated energy barrier for ice nucleation. These properties make such surfaces beneficial for preventing ice formation and anti-icing applications36–39.

For the PDMS-M/SiO₂-PAA@SiO₂ CSPs, an environmental stability test was performed for resistance to the UV light. As shown in Fig. 12, the thin film remained hydrophobic under UV light for up to 604 h, and on the other hand, no significant change in the light transmittance was observed after UV exposure. The observed ~ 10° decrease in water contact angle and slight reduction in transmittance are likely due to partial degradation of hydrophobic organic groups on the silica nanoparticles, leading to the formation of polar functional groups (–OH and –C = O) that alter interactions with the PDMS network. Similar effects have been reported for silica-based hydrophobic coatings under UV exposure40,41. Although XPS analysis was not performed on the present sample, these studies support the plausibility of the proposed mechanism.

According to the research, free radicals caused by PDMS chain degradation and photochemical reactions can lead to cross-linking between PDMS and silica nanoparticles, which increases the refractive index and slightly reduces the light transmittance42. Conclusion

In summary, this study investigates the effect of incorporating PAA@SiO₂ core–shell nanoparticles into an anti-icing thin film composed of PDMS and SiO₂ nanoparticles, which were co-modified using TMMS and HDMS. The films were fabricated on glass substrates via a dip-coating process. A hydrophobic thin film of PDMS-M/SiO₂–PAA@SiO₂ CSPs was successfully produced, exhibiting a water contact angle of 110°, a slip angle of 15°, a high optical transmittance of 92.78%, and a low reflectance of approximately 4.5%. The integration of PAA@SiO₂ core–shell particles led to a significant increase in surface roughness, from 1.484 nm to 4.46 nm, contributing to enhanced anti-icing performance. In the anti-icing tests, the delay in freezing time of water droplets improved markedly, increasing from 270 s (for bare glass) to 960 s for the coated surface. Moreover, the average ice shear stress was reduced from 257 kPa to 87 kPa, indicating a substantial decrease in ice adhesion due to the surface modification. These improvements are attributed to the synergistic effects of increased surface roughness, low surface energy, and the porous structure introduced by the PAA@SiO₂ particles. The resulting film also demonstrated ultraviolet (UV) radiation, highlighting its potential application as a durable anti-icing coating for optical devices, particularly surveillance camera lenses in cold and humid environments.
