5-aminolevulinic acid improves drake post-thaw sperm quality via enhancing mitochondrial function
Authors: Hongyan Zhang, Bin Wu, Yongjin Liu, Qingzhe Meng, Lingjiang Min, Shuer Zhang, Zhansheng Liu, Adedeji O Adetunji, Zhendong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryopreservation of sperm is an essential approach for the preservation and utilization of poultry genetic resources. However, during the freezing process, drake sperm often suffers structural damage caused by low-temperature stress and physical injury, which impairs mitochondrial function and markedly reduces post-thaw sperm quality and fertilizing ability. In this present study, different concentrations of 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA; 0, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 μM) were supplemented into the freezing extender, and several key parameters of post-thaw drake sperm were evaluated to investigate the potential role of 5-ALA in semen cryopreservation. The results demonstrated that supplementation with 500 μM 5-ALA significantly improved post-thaw sperm motility, acrosome integrity, and viability compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Further analyses revealed that treatment with 500 μM 5-ALA significantly increased the heme levels in sperm (P < 0.05), enhanced the activity of mitochondrial complex IV, and elevated both mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and adenosine triphosphate ATP levels (P < 0.05), indicating that 5-ALA facilitated mitochondrial energy metabolism. In addition, 500 μM 5-ALA treatment significantly reduced sperm reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, lipid peroxidation (LPO) content, apoptosis levels and DNA fragmentation (P < 0.05). This indicates that 5-ALA can reduce the damage caused by oxidative stress during freezing, inhibit LPO reactions, indirectly protect the integrity of sperm membrane and nucleus, and thus maintain the survival ability of sperm. Taken together, these findings indicate that supplementation with an appropriate concentration of 5-ALA effectively supports the maintenance and recovery of mitochondrial function. Thereby improving the efficiency of the electron transport chain and enhancing ATP production to meet the energy demands of sperm. Moreover, by alleviating the damage caused by oxidative stress to sperm, the integrity of sperm structure has been stabilized, and ultimately contributes to the enhancement of post-thaw sperm motility.Keywords: Drake, Sperm, Cryopreservation, Mitochondrial function

Introduction

Cryopreservation of avian semen is a crucial technique for the conservation of genetic resources, selective breeding, and the application of artificial insemination in poultry production (Woelders, 2021; Yeste, 2016). It plays a significant role in enhancing the utilization efficiency of breeding stock and ensuring the sustainable use of valuable genetic resources (Partyka and Nizanski, 2022). By extending the reproductive potential of male poultry across both temporal and spatial scales, cryopreservation allows superior genetic traits to be disseminated effectively across regions and seasons, providing a reliable technological foundation for modern poultry production. However, the fertilizing ability of post-thaw avian semen, particularly from waterfowl species such as drake, remains suboptimal (Donoghuea and Wishart, 2000). To date, the fertility of post-thaw drake semen remains substantially lower than that of fresh semen, directly limiting the efficiency of artificial insemination and the utilization of genetic resources (Bezerra, et al., 2024; Perez-Rivero, 2025).

The freezing and thawing processes induce multiple forms of damage to sperm. Cold shock disrupts the fluidity of the sperm membrane, ice crystal formation causes direct mechanical damage to cellular structures, and osmotic stress during dehydration and rehydration cycles leads to cellular swelling and shrinkage, compromising organelles and membrane integrity (Drobnis, et al., 1993). Collectively, these stressors result in significant reductions in sperm motility, disruption of membrane structures, and ultimately a decline in fertilizing ability (Contreras, et al., 2023; Hai, et al., 2024). In addition, reactive oxygen species (ROS) gradually accumulate within sperm during cryopreservation. Excessive ROS can induce LPO, protein oxidation, and DNA damage, further impairing sperm viability and motility (Fraser, et al., 2011; Grotter, et al., 2019). These findings underscore that maintaining sperm function in cryopreserved semen fundamentally depends on minimizing both physical and oxidative damage while preserving sperm energy metabolism systems and structural integrity.

Sperm are highly specialized cells whose primary functions rely on sustained and efficient energy supply (Miki, 2007). Sperm motility, fertilization capacity, and acrosome reaction all require ATP as an energy source (Cao, et al., 2024). The two major pathways for ATP production in sperm are glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Rodriguez-Gil, 2006). The relative contributions of these pathways vary among species. In avian sperm, particularly drake sperm, energy production is predominantly glycolytic, but OXPHOS also plays an indispensable role in maintaining motility and regulating sperm functional patterns (Kamali Sangani, et al., 2017). During cryopreservation, lipid phase transitions of the membrane, ROS accumulation, and intracellular osmotic imbalance render organelles-especially mitochondria-highly susceptible to damage (Castro, et al., 2025). Mitochondria are essential for sustaining sperm function, and their dysfunction directly affects the electron transport chain (ETC), resulting in impaired ATP synthesis, reduced motility, and decreased sperm viability, ultimately compromising sperm survival (Sharafi, et al., 2022).

Within mitochondrial energy metabolism, heme is an essential cofactor (Swenson, et al., 2020). As a central component of cytochromes, heme is widely incorporated into ETC complexes, particularly complexes III and IV, and directly determines the activity and efficiency of electron transfer (Mobius, et al., 2010). Heme biosynthesis relies on 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA), the universal precursor of all porphyrins and heme (Sansaloni-Pastor, et al., 2022). Studies have demonstrated that exogenous supplementation with 5-ALA increases sperm heme levels, thereby enhancing mitochondrial OXPHOS efficiency, elevating ATP production, and improving cellular resistance to oxidative stress (Kalainayakan, et al., 2018; Shimura, et al., 2019). Besides, 5-ALA has also been shown to reduce ROS accumulation, protect membrane and proteins from oxidative damage, and maintain cellular structural and functional stability (Niu, et al., 2017).

In livestock reproduction research, a limited number of studies have explored the regulatory effects of 5-ALA on sperm function. Previous studies indicated that dietary supplementation of 5-ALA in boars enhances heme synthesis, increases mitochondrial ETC activity, raises ATP levels, and reduces oxidative stress in sperm (Wang, et al., 2025a). The positive effects of 5-ALA on improving boar sperm motility suggest that 5-ALA has considerable potential for regulating sperm energy metabolism and mitigating oxidative damage. However, the potential application of 5-ALA in sperm cryopreservation has not been systematically investigated, and research on its use in avian sperm remains particularly scarce. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the effects of 5-ALA supplementation on the functional quality of post-thaw drake sperm and to elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying its action.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai, China) unless specified.

Animals and semen collection

Semen samples were obtained from five healthy, sexually mature Wendeng Black drakes. Fresh drake semen was collected twice a week using the hand-held method and only semen samples with sperm motility exceeding 90 % and morphological abnormalities < 10 % were used for use. The average sperm concentration of fresh semen was approximately 3 × 10⁹ sperm/mL. To reduce individual differences, in each replicate experiment, the semen of five Wendeng Black drake was mixed and evenly distributed to five treatment groups. The experiment was repeated three times in total. (n = 3).

Media preparation

EK solution (a conventional avian semen diluent commonly referred to as “EK solution/extender” in poultry semen cryopreservation) was used to prepare the pre-treatment extender and served as the thawing solution. The EK solution was composed of 0.14 g/L sodium citrate, 1.4 g/L sodium glutamate, 0.98 g/L disodium hydrogen phosphate, 0.21 g/L potassium dihydrogen phosphate, 0.7 g/L glucose, 0.2 g/L fructose, 0.7 g/L inositol, 0.1 g/L polyvinylpyrrolidone, and 0.02 g/L protamine sulfate. Freezing extender was prepared by supplementing the base EK solution with 8 % (v/v) dimethyl formamide.

Semen processing and cryopreservation

Fresh drake semen was initially diluted 1:1 (v/v) with EK solution and transported to the laboratory within 15 min in a 37 °C incubator. Upon arrival, the semen was further diluted with freezing extender to a final sperm concentration of 4 × 10⁸ sperm/mL. At this stage, semen samples were divided into five treatment groups, each supplemented with different concentrations of 5-ALA (0, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 μM). Subsequently, the diluted semen was loaded into 0.25 mL straws. The straws were then gradually cooled to 4 °C over a 1.5 h period. Once the samples reached 4 °C, they were equilibrated for 15 min before being transferred to a programmable freezer (SIBO Livestock Equipment Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China). The freezing protocol commenced at a cooling rate of -3 °C/min, reducing the temperature from 4 °C to -1 °C. Thereafter, the cooling rate increased to -30 °C/min until the temperature reached -140 °C. After equilibrating at -140 °C for 360 s, the straws were removed and stored in liquid nitrogen for long-term preservation. During thawing, the straws were placed in a 37 °C water bath for 30 s (Najafi, et al., 2024). After that, the straw was cut open, and the semen was transferred into a prewarmed centrifuge tube, which was then incubated at 37 °C for 15 min.

Assessment of sperm motility

Sperm motility parameters were evaluated using CASA (HT, Beverly, MA, USA) system, as described by previous study (Zhu, et al., 2022). Sperm samples were loaded into a Makler chamber, and motility parameters were measured in five randomly selected microscopic fields, with a minimum of 300 sperm per field. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm viability and acrosome integrity

Viability and acrosome integrity of sperm were assessed using FITC-PNA/PI dual staining. In brief, 0.6 µL of FITC-PNA (1 mg/mL) and 0.54 µL of PI (2.4 mM) were added to 1 mL of diluted semen for staining, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 5 min in the dark. FITC-PNA fluorescence was excited with a 488 nm blue laser and collected at 530/30 nm, whereas PI fluorescence was excited at 488 nm and collected using a 585/42 nm bandpass filter. Sperm was analyzed by flow cytometer (BeamCyte-1026M, Changzhou, China), with 20,000 events recorded per sample. Using FlowJo (v10.8.1) to analysis raw data. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed using the fluorescent dye JC-1. Sperm samples incubated with 10 µM carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) for 30 min were used as the positive control. For staining, 200 µL of JC-1 working solution was added to 400 µL of sperm samples, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The samples were then washed twice with JC-1 buffer prior to analysis. When the MMP of sperm is low, JC-1 remains in its monomers form, does not accumulate in the mitochondrial matrix, and emits green fluorescence. In contrast, when MMP is high, JC-1 accumulates in the mitochondrial matrix and forms aggregates, emitting orange fluorescence. JC-1 monomers and JC-1 aggregates were excited using a 488 nm blue laser. The green fluorescence emitted by JC-1 monomers was recorded using a 530/30 bandpass filter (sperm with low mitochondrial membrane potential), while the orange fluorescence emitted by JC-1 aggregates was recorded using a 585/42 bandpass filter (sperm with high mitochondrial membrane potential). Sperm was analyzed by flow cytometer (BeamCyte-1026M, Changzhou, China), with 20,000 events recorded per sample. Using FlowJo (v10.8.1) to analysis raw data. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm ATP levels

Sperm ATP levels were quantified using a commercial ATP detection kit (ADS-W-A001-96, AIDISHENG, Yancheng, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. Sperm was disrupted using an ultrasonic processor on ice (200 W, 4 s pulse with 12 s intervals, repeated 15 times), followed by boiling for 10 min to ensure complete lysis. After cooling, a precipitation reagent was added, and the mixture was centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. The resulting supernatant, along with the colorimetric reagent and termination solution, was sequentially added to a 96-well plate. The absorbance of the sample at 636 nm was measured using a microplate reader. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm mitochondrial complex IV activity assays

Mitochondrial complex IV assay kits (ADS-W-X010, AIDISHENG, Jiangsu, China) were used to measure the activities mitochondrial complex IV. As described by Zhang et al. (Zhang, et al., 2022), mitochondria were isolated from sperm and disrupted by sonication (on ice, 200 W, 3 s pulses with 12 s intervals, repeated 40 times), and the samples were maintained on ice for subsequent analyses. For the assay, 20 μL aliquots of each sample were mixed with 180 μL of the working solution in a 96-well microplate. Absorbance readings at 550 nm were obtained at 0 and 15 min with a microplate reader. Enzymatic activity was calculated according to the corresponding formula. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm heme levels

Heme levels in sperm were measured using boar heme ELISA kit (Jingmei Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) following manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sperm samples were first lysed and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. The assay was then performed by sequentially adding the supernatant, horseradish peroxidase (HRP), and calibration standards into microplate wells pre-coated with anti-heme antibodies. The colorimetric reaction was quantified by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm apoptosis levels

Annexin V-FITC cell apoptosis detection kit (C1062, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were used to measure the levels of apoptosis in sperm. According to manufacturer’s instructions, the semen sample was centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Thereafter, the collected samples were counted. Furthermore, the sperm were resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min and the resulting precipitation was retained. 195 μL Annexin V-FITC binding solution, 5 μL Annexin V-FITC, and 10 μL PI in sequence was added to the retained precipitate and the mixture was incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 20 min. Annexin V-FITC fluorescence was excited with a 488 nm blue laser and collected at 530/30 nm, whereas PI fluorescence was excited at 488 nm and collected using a 585/42 nm bandpass filter. Sperm was analyzed by flow cytometer (BeamCyte-1026M, Changzhou, China), with 20,000 events recorded per sample. Using FlowJo (v10.8.1) to analysis raw data. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm DNA fragmentation levels

DNA fragmentation in sperm was assessed using the TUNEL (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling) assay. In brief, 100 µL of sperm sample was fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Following fixation, the samples were washed with PBS and permeabilized in PBS containing 0.3 % Triton X-100 for 5 min. Each sample was incubated with 50 µL of TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. After two additional washes with PBS. FITC-dUTP were excited using a 488 nm blue laser, the green fluorescence emitted by FITC-dUTP was recorded using a 530/30 bandpass filter. Sperm was analyzed by flow cytometer (BeamCyte-1026M, Changzhou, China), with 20,000 events recorded per sample. Using FlowJo (v10.8.1) to analysis raw data. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm reactive oxygen species levels

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in sperm were assessed using ROS assay kit (S0033S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. 100 µL of DCFH-DA was added to 200 µL of semen sample, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, the samples were washed three times with PBS. DCF were excited using a 488 nm blue laser, the green fluorescence emitted by DCF was recorded using a 530/30 bandpass filter. Subsequently, flow cytometer (BeamCyte-1026M, Changzhou, China) analysis was performed, with 20,000 events recorded for each sample. Using FlowJo (v10.8.1) to analysis raw data. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Assessment of sperm lipid peroxidation content

Lipid peroxidation (LPO) content in sperm was assessed using a lipid peroxidation assay kit (A106-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sperm sample was diluted with PBS and subjected to ultrasonic disruption in an ice-water bath (300 W, 5 s pulse with 20 s intervals, repeated 10 times). The sperm samples were mixed with the prepared buffer provided in the kit. Absorbance was measured at 586 nm using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. Data was first tested for normality distribution (Shapiro-Wilk’s test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test); if necessary, data transformation (arcsin square root) was applied. One-way ANOVA (General linear model) was used to compare basic sperm parameter; post hoc comparison was performed using the Tukey’s test. Data was expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0. For any analysis, P < 0.05 was considered to denote statistically significant differences.

Results

Assessment of motility, viability, and acrosome integrity on post-thaw drake sperm

Supplementation of the freezing extender with 5-ALA significantly enhanced the total motility and progressive motility of post-thaw drake sperm (P < 0.05; Table 1). Compared with the control group, sperm samples supplemented with 500 μM 5-ALA exhibited a 14.6 % increase in total motility (44.84 ± 0.55 % vs. 30.24 ± 1.20 %) and a 7.93 % increase in progressive motility (15.00 ± 1.15 % vs. 7.07 ± 0.29 %). In addition, supplementation with 5-ALA markedly improved several kinematic parameters, including curvilinear velocity (VCL), straight-line velocity (VSL), average path velocity (VAP), linearity (LIN), wobble (WOB), and amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH), relative to the control group. However, no significant differences were observed in beat cross frequency (BCF) and straightness (STR) between the 5-ALA-treated and control groups (P > 0.05). As shown in Fig. 1, supplementation with 500 μM 5-ALA significantly increased the proportion of sperm exhibiting both acrosome integrity and viability compared to the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1G), which was consistent with the observed trend in total sperm motility.

Assessment of ATP levels and mitochondrial membrane potential on post-thaw drake sperm

To evaluate whether the addition of 5-ALA influences mitochondrial function in sperm, the MMP and ATP levels of post-thaw drake sperm were assessed. Supplementation with 5-ALA significantly increased MMP compared to the control group (P < 0.05), with the highest levels observed in the 500 μM treatment group (Fig. 2F). However, no significant differences were detected between the 100 or 1000 μM groups and the control (P > 0.05). Moreover, ATP levels in sperm from the 500 μM 5-ALA treatment group were significantly higher than those in both the control and the other treatment groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 2H).

Discussion

Cryopreservation of sperm has long been regarded as an indispensable tool in animal reproduction, playing a pivotal role in germplasm conservation, genetic improvement, and the modernization of livestock and poultry production systems (Yanez-Ortiz, et al., 2022). However, during the cryopreservation process, sperm is often subjected to a series of irreversible injuries. These damages include, but are not limited to, membrane disruption caused by ice crystal formation, impaired mitochondrial function, and compromised DNA integrity, which may eventually trigger apoptosis (Ozimic, et al., 2023). In this present study, we demonstrated that supplementation with an appropriate concentration of 5-ALA effectively alleviated cryo-induced sperm damage. Specifically, 5-ALA supplementation significantly improved sperm motility, viability, and acrosome integrity, enhanced MMP and ATP production, increased heme levels and Complex IV activity, and reduced both apoptosis and DNA fragmentation levels in post-thaw drake sperm.

Sperm motility is widely recognized as one of the most critical determinants of fertilization capacity (Liu, et al., 1989). Our study showed that supplementation with 500 μM 5-ALA significantly increased both total and progressive motility of post-thaw drake sperm. In addition, velocity parameters such as VCL, VSL, and VAP, as well as kinematic indices including LIN, WOB, and ALH, were markedly improved. These improvements not only indicate an overall enhancement of sperm motility but also suggest that the motility pattern becomes more efficient and directional, which is of paramount importance for successful penetration of the oolemma and completion of fertilization. Furthermore, the acrosome, as an essential structure necessary for sperm-egg binding, is highly susceptible to damage during the freezing process, subsequently leading to a decrease in fertilization rate (Ichikawa, et al., 2016; Olexikova, et al., 2019). Our findings revealed that supplementation with 500 μM 5-ALA significantly increased the proportion of sperm with intact acrosomes and viability, consistent with previous studies in mammalian sperm (Wang, et al., 2025a). This improvement may be attributed to the ability of 5-ALA to restore mitochondrial function and ATP generation disrupted during semen freezing, while also enhancing antioxidant defenses and reducing LPO to protect the acrosome (Sun, et al., 2023; Zhang, et al., 2019). Furthermore, 5-ALA may further support the integrity of the acrosome by maintaining the stability of the acrosome protein.

Mitochondria are the central organelles of sperm energy metabolism and are intimately involved in ATP production, which directly determines sperm motility, capacitation, and fertilization capacity (Amaral, 2022). During cryopreservation, exposure to low temperatures and oxidative stress frequently causes structural damage to mitochondria, leading to a reduction in ATP synthesis efficiency and consequently impairing the stable supply of energy required for sustained sperm motility. Zhu et al. (Zhu, et al., 2022) reported that mitochondrial functionality in sperm is closely correlated with the linearity of sperm motility, further highlighting the pivotal role of mitochondrial integrity in maintaining sperm quality. In this present study, supplementation with 500 μM 5-ALA significantly improved the post-thaw sperm MMP and ATP levels. These findings suggest that 5-ALA not only alleviates cryo-induced mitochondrial damage but may also restore mitochondrial activity in post-thaw drake sperm, thereby stabilizing MMP and enhancing ATP generation. The increased ATP supply subsequently supports improved sperm motility.

As a key precursor in the biosynthesis of heme, 5-ALA likely exerts its effects through the reinforcement of mitochondrial energy metabolism. In this study, supplementation with 500 μM 5-ALA markedly increased heme levels and Complex IV activity in post-thaw drake sperm. Heme is an essential prosthetic group of cytochromes, particularly cytochrome c oxidase, where its abundance directly influences electron transfer efficiency (Panda, et al., 2023). Enhanced activity of Complex IV observed in this study suggests a more efficient electron transport chain, ultimately leading to improved ATP synthesis. Previous studies in mammalian models have reported that exogenous 5-ALA promotes heme synthesis, enhances complex IV activity, and elevates OXPHOS efficiency by improving ETC (Wang, et al., 2025a). This further indicates that 5-ALA facilitates the recovery of post-thaw drake sperm quality by enhancing the efficiency of mitochondrial energy metabolism.

Cryopreservation is known to compromise sperm membrane integrity and disrupt mitochondrial function, both of which are critical triggers for apoptotic pathways and DNA fragmentation (Baumber, et al., 2003; Macente, et al., 2019). In addition to membrane damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, recent studies have shown that excessive ROS generation is another critical factor contributing to sperm injury (Wang, et al., 2025b). The continuous accumulation of ROS in sperm induces LPO, disrupts the sperm membrane structure that is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids, and produces toxic by-products such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA) (Zhu, et al., 2025). These substances not only impair mitochondrial function but also attack nucleic acids, leading to DNA strand breaks and chromatin instability, ultimately promoting the activation of apoptotic signaling (Chen, et al., 2024). In the present study, we found that treatment with 500 μM 5-ALA significantly reduced DNA fragmentation and apoptosis levels in post-thaw drake sperm, while also significantly reduced ROS levels and LPO content, all of which were significantly lower than those in the control group. This protective effect may be associated with the ability of 5-ALA to enhance cellular antioxidant capacity, thereby suppressing the formation of LPO products such as 4-HNE and MDA. By decreasing the accumulation of those cytotoxic aldehydes, 5-ALA prevents oxidative modification of membrane lipids and proteins, helping to preserve mitochondrial function and nuclear integrity during cryopreservation.

By demonstrating that 5-ALA improves ETC efficiency and alleviates cryo-induced mitochondrial dysfunction to restore ATP production and sperm motility, our study provides a straightforward yet effective strategy to overcome the current limitations of drake semen cryopreservation. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the potential effects of 5-ALA on sperm performance in the fertilization environment have not yet been evaluated. Further studies are warranted to investigate whether the beneficial effects of 5-ALA supplementation observed in vitro can translate into improved reproductive outcomes in vivo.

Conclusion

In summary, supplementation with an optimal concentration of 5-ALA markedly improved mitochondrial function in drake sperm during cryopreservation, enhancing the efficiency of the electron transport chain and promoting ATP production. The improved energy supply not only supported sperm motility and progressive movement but also mitigated cryo-induced stress. Moreover, by mitigating the damage caused during cryopreservation and suppressing LPO, 5-ALA indirectly preserved the integrity of the sperm membrane and nucleus, thereby maintaining sperm viability. These findings indicate that 5-ALA not only enhances sperm motility through improved mitochondrial energy metabolism but may also reduce DNA damage and apoptosis by lowering ROS accumulation and LPO generation, providing a dual protective effect on sperm and offering a novel strategy for optimizing cryopreservation protocols for drake sperm.
