Fluorinated Polymers for Hydrovoltaic Energy Harvesting: Mechanisms and Multifunctional Integration
Authors: Seungjae Lee, Changwoo Nam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hydrovoltaic energy harvesting converts interactions at water–solid interfaces into electricity through capacitive discharge, streaming, and diffusion. Fluorinated polymers such as poly­(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE), fluorinated ethylene propylene (FEP), and poly­(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) are central to these processes owing to their high electronegativity, hydrophobicity, and chemical stability. These materials facilitate charge separation and storage while offering structural versatility for device fabrication. This review highlights recent progress in applying fluorinated polymers across the three hydrovoltaic mechanisms, with emphasis on structure–property–function relationships that govern interfacial charge dynamics. Approaches for integrating multiple mechanisms and coupling hydrovoltaic devices with complementary energy-harvesting systems are also summarized. Together, these advances underscore the key role of fluorinated polymers in enabling multifunctional hydrovoltaic platforms and point toward strategies for improving performance, durability, and system-level integration.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the continuous use of fossil fuels has led to persistent environmental issues. − Consequently, finding alternative methods for generating energy without relying on fossil fuels has become a global challenge. In response, sustainable and eco-friendly energy technologies such as solar photovoltaic, , solar-thermal, , wind, , hydro, , and geothermal energy , have been actively researched and commercialized. However, these technologies still face challenges in terms of energy production, including intermittency due to weather conditions, regional dependency due to resource distribution, economic feasibility associated with high initial investment, and additional energy consumption for operation. −

In recent years, hydrovoltaic technology, which generates electrical energy via direct interaction between water and materials, has been studied to address these challenges. − This technology can convert nearly all forms of water on Earth into electrical energy, including rainwater, groundwater, seawater, evaporated water, and atmospheric moisture. When an aqueous phase comes in contact with a solid surface, a hydrovoltaic electricity generator (HEG) operates through three distinct mechanisms. The first is the capacitive discharge, which results from the periodic release and recharge of charges accumulated on the solid surface. − The second is streaming, which is induced by charge transport driven by fluid flow. − The third mechanism is diffusion, which arises from charge separation caused by ionic concentration gradients and convective motion. − Based on these mechanisms, hydrovoltaic systems are capable of continuously generating electricity without the need for external mechanical inputs or complex devices, while exhibiting low dependence on geographical location or climate conditions.

The performance of the HEG is governed by the overall water–solid interfacial interaction. Consequently, a range of research initiatives have focused on optimizing this interfacial process. − Among the candidate materials designed to directly interact with water, fluorinated polymers such as poly­(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE), poly­(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), and fluorinated ethylene propylene (FEP) exhibit high charge storage and separation capabilities, distinctive wettabilities, and excellent thermochemical stabilities. Owing to their charge storage, separation, and wettability capabilities, they generate strong local electric fields, contributing to an improved output across capacitive discharge, streaming, and diffusion mechanisms. Their excellent thermochemical stability ensures their long-term durability in high-salinity and high-temperature environments. Furthermore, PVDF can be readily processed into films, fibers, and porous membranes, offering flexibility in device designing. − These properties collectively suggest that fluorinated polymers will play a central role in advancing current and future hydrovoltaic technologies.

Recent studies have actively pursued composite and hybrid designs that simultaneously harness two or more hydrovoltaic mechanisms or combine them with other energy conversion technologies to extend device output limits and operating conditions. − The integration of multiple mechanisms and cross-technology processes has been demonstrated to enhance the output stability and overall energy conversion efficiency. Furthermore, this integration facilitates the simultaneous harvesting of multiple commodities.

The majority of reviews published to date on hydrovoltaic technology have focused on operational mechanisms and applications and have primarily emphasized a broad range of materials, including carbon-based materials. ,− However, fluorinated polymers have not been extensively investigated. To fill this gap, this review introduces the characteristics of representative fluorinated polymers, such as PTFE, PVDF, and FEP, and briefly outlines the mechanisms of hydrovoltaic technology, followed by an overview of recent studies on how these polymers are applied within each mechanism. Based on this, we present a rationale for the use of fluorinated polymers in future hydrovoltaic systems. Subsequently, combinations of multiple mechanisms or hybrid approaches with other technologies are introduced to propose potential strategies for overcoming the inherent limitations of hydrovoltaic systems. This comprehensive review aims to provide insights into future directions for material design and system integration in hydrovoltaic energy harvesting.

Mechanism

In this section, we examine the three core mechanisms that enable hydrovoltaic energy harvesting. First, we focus on capacitive discharge, which is defined as a phenomenon in which periodic charge accumulation and release at a solid–liquid interface generates an electrical output. Subsequently, we discuss streaming, a phenomenon that occurs when a fluid flows across a charged surface. Finally, we consider diffusion, which is driven by ionic concentration gradients. In this section, a comprehensive review of each mechanism is presented, accompanied by several case studies that illustrate the principles discussed.

Capacitive Discharge

The capacitive discharge mechanism converts the mechanical motion of water, such as falling droplets, ocean waves, and flow through a conduit, into electrical energy. During this process, an electric double layer (EDL) periodically forms and collapses at the water–solid interface, altering the capacitance and producing transient high voltages and power; this phenomenon is known as capacitive discharge. ,, As illustrated in Figure a, when a switch is opened during the charging stage, the electrochemical effects induced by the mechanical motion of water draws negative charges to the electrode surface, while positive charges accumulate inside the electrode. When a droplet impacts and slides across an electrode, the water level rises under wave action or the water column advances along a pipe, and the actual contact area changes over time, increasing the capacitance. Because the circuit remains open, the newly induced charges cannot escape; instead, they accumulate at the electrode–water interface, thereby increasing the potential difference between the two electrodes. Once the water flow closes the circuit, the discharge stage begins. The stored positive charges are rapidly released through an external circuit, generating a brief yet intense potential difference. This is followed by renewed movement of water, which initiates the same cycle of charge accumulation and release. A single cycle can produce voltages reaching several hundred volts and, in some instances, even approaching a kilovolt range.

Li et al. reported a droplet-based electricity generator (DEG) that converted the mechanical energy of falling water droplets into electrical energy (Figure b). The device was designed with a double-electrode structure consisting of a thin gold film as the bottom electrode, deposited on one side of a PTFE film, and a copper tape as the top electrode attached to the opposite side. When a spreading droplet touches the top electrode and connects to the PTFE film bearing trapped surface charges, opposite charges are induced from the bottom electrode or ground to the top electrode owing to electrostatic induction. This process generates electrical output. As the droplet contracts and slides downward, the induced opposite charges flow back to the bottom electrode or ground, restoring the initial state for the next energy generation cycle. The bottom electrode is charged by electrostatic induction as the electric field of the charged droplet in air penetrates the dielectric film between the droplet and electrode. ,, This process is represented by an equivalent RC circuit model (Figure c). R w represents the ionic resistance through the spreading droplet; C 1 and C 2 correspond to the electric double-layer capacitors at the droplet–top electrode and droplet–PTFE interfaces, respectively; and C P is a parallel-plate capacitor formed between the water column and bottom electrode. Since the Debye length is only a few tens of nanometers, C 1 and C 2 are much larger than C P. Hence, the charge–discharge behavior of the series RC circuit is effectively governed by the electrical relaxation time τ = R w C P. Here, τ is the time constant of the circuit, defined as the product of R and C. The theoretical prediction that a larger C P results in a longer τ and increased transferred charge is consistent with the experimental results, showing a pulse width of approximately 18.5 μs and transferred charge of 15 nC for the double-electrode configuration, and 3.9 μs and 0.74 nC, respectively, for the top-electrode-only configuration. Based on this RC-controlled mechanism, the DEG exhibits an open-circuit voltage of approximately 250 V and a short-circuit peak current of 0.6 mA. The instantaneous power density can be enhanced up to 160 W m–2 upon surface charge injection.

Nan et al. proposed a submerged and completely open solid–liquid triboelectric nanogenerator (SOSL-TENG) that converts oscillating water flows induced by waves into electrical energy (Figure d). The device consists of an internal electrode formed by attaching a thin copper film to an acrylic tube support; over this, an FEP film with high electronegativity is wrapped to act as a friction layer that effectively stores negative charges. A 0.3 mm diameter copper wire is helically wound around the FEP film to serve as an external electrode. The energy conversion process during one cycle is represented by six stages (I–VI) (Figure e). In the initial state (I), the FEP surface is saturated with negative charges due to repeated contact and separation with water, while positive charges are induced on the internal copper film. When the water level rises but has yet to contact the external copper wire (II), the circuit remains nearly open owing to electrostatic shielding, and the current is minimal. Once the water touches the copper wire, the internal and external electrodes are short-circuited through the water column (III); negative ions migrate to the external electrode, and electrons flow through the external circuit, generating a brief cathodic current pulse. As the water continues to rise and pass above the FEP film (IV), charge transfer ceases. When the water level reduces and detaches from the external electrode (V and VI), the positive charges return to the internal electrode, reversing the current direction and preparing for the next cycle. Owing to the high charge affinity and hydrophobicity of FEP, the SOSL-TENG achieved a transferred charge of 2.58 μC per cycle, a short-circuit current of 85.9 μA, and an open-circuit voltage of 208 V under wave conditions of 1 Hz.

Cui et al. developed a tube-based triboelectric nanogenerator (T-TENG) that generates electricity using the reciprocating motion of a flowing water column (Figure f). The device features a simple coaxial structure in which copper electrode rings are wound at regular intervals on the outer surface of a PTFE tube with an inner diameter of several millimeters to form external electrodes. A copper foil of the same material is attached to the inner wall to serve as the internal electrode. As water flows through the tube, negative charges accumulate at the PTFE–water interface, and positive charges are electrostatically induced on the copper electrodes. The charge transfer and discharge sequences during the up-and-down motion of the water column are schematically represented by eight stages (stages i–viii) (Figure g). In the initial stages (stages i–ii), the interfacial potential difference is minimal before the water column reaches the electrode. As the water begins to cover the electrodes (stage (iii)), the cations in the water partially shield the electrons on the PTFE surface, causing them to flow through an external circuit to the ground, thereby generating a current. Once the water column fully passes the electrode (stage (iv)), the potential difference reaches its maximum and then gradually diminishes. During the return flow (stages v–viii), the direction of charge transfer is reversed, completing one energy generation cycle. Under a flow rate of 0.2 L min–1, the device achieved an open-circuit voltage of approximately 40 V, a short-circuit current of 0.4 μA, and a transferred charge of 25 nC per cycle.

Streaming

The streaming mechanism generates electrical energy by utilizing the continuous water flow induced by pressure gradients, capillary action, or evaporation-driven flow to transport charges. − When water flows along the walls of micro-, nanochannels, or porous channels, whose surfaces carry negative charges, an EDL is established. This EDL consists of a stern layer, in which counterbalancing cations are tightly adsorbed onto the surface, followed by a more diffuse layer (Figure a). − Figure b depicts a case in which the diffuse layers on both walls overlap within a narrow channel (typically less than a few hundred nanometers in width). When the inlet pressure P 1 is greater than the outlet pressure P 2, the flow velocity increases, causing the entire overlapping diffuse layer to migrate in one direction. This results in the buildup of a potential difference ranging from tens to hundreds of millivolts.

Streaming current and streaming potential are both directly proportional to the pressure difference ΔP, as demonstrated by the following expressions. When a pressure gradient is established along a channel, the cations within the EDL are dragged by the flow, generating a streaming current expressed as 1 Is=Aε0εrΔPζηl where A and l are the cross-sectional area and length of the channel, respectively; ε0 and εr are the vacuum permittivity and relative permittivity, respectively; η is the viscosity of the electrolyte; and ζ is the zeta potential of the surface. Under the same conditions, the streaming potential generated when the accumulated charges balance the conductive current is given by 2 Vs=ε0εrΔPζση where σ is the electrical conductivity of the electrolyte.

These equations show that the pressure difference (ΔP) acts as the driving force or “engine” in this energy conversion mechanism. This directly determines the magnitudes of the generated voltage and current. A higher-pressure difference results in a stronger flow rate, transporting more charges and consequently leading to a higher potential difference. ,

When an external load is connected, current flows and part of the potential difference is consumed. The provided pressure-driven flow persists, and the continuous supply of new ions ensures the stability and rectification of direct current generation. In this manner, the streaming mechanism, in contrast to the capacitive discharge mechanism that relies on repetitive transient discharges, provides a relatively low voltage but a continuous current through the ion drag induced by a steady flow.

Zhang et al. proposed a microfluidic generator (MFG) that converts a pressure-driven microfluidic flow into continuous direct current (Figure c). A quasiporous channel was formed by an array of micropillars fabricated via soft lithography at the center of a PDMS chip, and tubular electrodes inserted at both ends simultaneously served as inlet/outlet ports and charge-collecting electrodes. When a 1 μM KCl solution was injected, it contacted the negatively charged PDMS walls, inducing fixed negative charges on the surface and attracting counterions (K+) in the solution to form an EDL. When a pressure difference was maintained between the channel ends, cations within the EDL were transported toward the outlet along with the fluid, generating a streaming current I s and resulting in charge separation along the channel that accumulated as a streaming potential V s (Figure d). A voltage–time plot under repeated fluid injection (ON) and cessation (OFF) (Figure e) demonstrated that a sharp voltage of approximately 160 mV was generated exclusively during flow, and it reverted to 0 mV when the flow ceased, thereby substantiating that the output emanated purely from the pressure-driven flow. When an external circuit was connected, the streaming potential V s acted as an electromotive force and continuously drive a conductive current I con without requiring additional rectification. Under a flow rate of 0.4 mL min–1, the device stably output an open-circuit voltage of approximately 0.16 V and a short-circuit current of 1.75 nA. The current magnitude remained nearly constant regardless of the load resistance, confirming that the MFG can function as a steady current source.

Park et al. proposed a leaf-inspired energy-harvesting foam (LIEHF) that converts solar-induced liquid evaporation and capillary transport (transpiration) into a continuous direct current (Figure f). A porous PDMS sponge uniformly embedded with carbon nanotube (CNT)/carbon black exhibits strong black light absorption, inducing rapid evaporation at the upper surface and generating a pressure difference between the lower absorption and upper evaporation surfaces. Water rises through the microcapillaries owing to this pressure gradient, and cations within the EDL are dragged along with the upward flow, generating a streaming current and corresponding streaming potential between two copper mesh electrodes. The electrical output was highly dependent on the zeta potential of the carbon-composite surface. Increasing the CNT/carbon black­(CB) content from 1 wt % to 2 wt % raised the zeta potential from approximately −15 mV to −40 mV (Figure g). As the zeta potential increased, both the open-circuit voltage (V oc) and short-circuit current (I sc) improved simultaneously. This enhancement was attributed to a thicker EDL overlap at higher zeta potentials, which led to the exclusion of co-ions and strengthened the convection of cations along the upward flow, thereby amplifying the potential difference (Figure h). − A single LIEHF device operating under 1 sun conditions demonstrated a stable open-circuit voltage of approximately 254 mV, a short-circuit current of 42 μA, and an instantaneous power density of 1 μW cm–2.

Diffusion

The diffusion mechanism may be confused with the streaming mechanism, because both generate electrical energy based on ionic concentration gradients. However, they differ fundamentally in their initial driving forces. Unlike the streaming mechanism, in which ionic movement and the resulting concentration gradient are caused by fluid flow induced by capillary action, evaporation-driven flow, or external pressure, the diffusion mechanism generates electrical energy through the spontaneous diffusion of ions driven by a pre-existing ionic concentration gradient without any fluid flow (Figure a). ,

Liu et al. reported a humidity-driven power generation device that produced a direct current voltage solely through natural moisture absorption by placing a porous carbon film comprising a side-by-side structure of a high-functional-group region (HFGR) and low-functional-group region (LFGR) in a high-humidity environment (RH > 95%) (Figure b). The HFGR was created by treating one side of the carbon film with oxidative plasma, introducing a high density of proton-donating groups, such as carboxyl (−COOH), while the LFGR remained untreated. When water vapor is adsorbed into the nanopores of the carbon structure, a larger number of carboxyl groups dissociate in the HFGR, releasing free protons. The resulting proton concentration gradient between the two regions leads to the development of a potential difference across the metal electrodes. In practice, sealing the device in a beaker to increase the relative humidity immediately increases the open-circuit voltage, which remains stable for several hours. When the lid was removed and humidity was decreased, the voltage returned to 0 mV, and this response was reproducible (Figure c). This confirms that the output is directly governed by the degree of vapor adsorption and not by the pressure difference or fluid flow. The key to voltage generation lies in the fact that adsorbed water promotes proton dissociation from −COOH groups and facilitates proton transport along hydrogen-bonded water chains from HFGR to LFGR, thereby maintaining charge separation. Consequently, the output voltage increases with a larger functional group concentration difference and higher relative humidity, both of which enhance water adsorption. In contrast, when thick water layers are formed within the pores due to condensation, the proton concentration gradient is eliminated, leading to a drop in potential. This moisture-adsorption-based proton pump mechanism can provide a continuous voltage of several tens of millivolts, along with a current in the nanoampere range without any external driving components.

Xu et al. reported a graphene oxide film (GOF) generator that produced electricity using only water vapor without any pretreatment or additives (Figure d). When a thin GOF is sealed with gold electrodes and exposed to moisture from the top, the carboxyl (−COOH) groups within the porous structure rapidly dissociate and release free H+ ions. This process occurs in three stages (Figure e). In the first stage, known as the ionization stage, the adsorbed water detaches protons from the −COOH groups. In the second stage, which involves charge separation and ion migration, the released H+ ions migrate along the GO layers via the Grotthuss mechanism, , leading to the formation of a potential difference. In the final stage (recombination stage), when the moisture supply is halted, the proton concentration gradient gradually disappears, and the potential decreases. The voltage–time trace shows a rapid increase followed by a gradual decline during each adsorption (first stage) and desorption (second stage) cycle (Figure f). Under this mechanism, electrons flow in the opposite direction through an external circuit, resulting in the immediate generation of a direct current voltage of 0.4–0.7 V and current density of 2–25 μA cm–2.

Key Components and Requirements

In this section, we summarize the essential requirements that the key components must fulfill for each mechanism based on the mechanistic principles discussed above.

The key component of the capacitive-discharge mechanism is the dielectric material. The dielectric must possess high charge-trapping capability and long-term storage stability. Furthermore, it should also have a highly electronegative surface to facilitate the induction of opposite charges (positive charges) on the electrode upon contact with water. ,, Hydrophobicity is required to ensure distinct switch-on and switch-off states. To maintain long-term performance without degradation under operational conditions, the dielectric must exhibit mechanical, chemical, and thermal stability.

In the streaming mechanism, the essential component is the channel through which the charges are transported. These include nano- and microscale channels and porous matrices. The channel walls should exhibit a high zeta potential and uniform surface charge, and the channel diameter must be maintained at the scale of the Debye length to ensure the overlapping of diffuse layers. ,, In addition, suitable wettability and chemical resistance are required to sustain continuous fluid flow and prevent fouling, and the system must have fundamental mechanical robustness to withstand repeated pressure variations without deformation.

The key component of the diffusion mechanism was the presence of two surfaces with different functional group densities. One surface must be rich in proton-donating groups such as −COOH and −OH, which readily release H+ ions upon moisture adsorption, while the other surface must contain fewer functional groups to maintain the ionic concentration gradient. , The porous structure must provide effective water pathways and a high surface area. Furthermore, the material must exhibit mechanical and chemical stability to prevent functional group loss and structural collapse during repeated wetting–drying cycles.

Fluorinated Polymer

Fluorinated Polymers: PTFE, FEP, and PVDF

Fluorinated polymers are polymers in which some or all of the carbon–hydrogen bonds in the hydrocarbon backbone are replaced by carbon–fluorine bonds. Owing to the high bond energy of the C–F bonds and high electronegativity of fluorine atoms, these polymers exhibit outstanding chemical and thermal stabilities, exceptionally low surface energies and friction coefficients, excellent weather resistance, and unique electrical properties comparable to those of conventional plastics. ,, Fluorinated polymers are categorized into perfluorinated polymers, in which all hydrogen atoms are replaced by fluorine, and partially fluorinated polymers, in which both fluorine and hydrogen atoms coexist. The perfluorinated category includes PTFE, in which all the C–H bonds are eliminated, and FEP (Figure a). The partially fluorinated category includes PVDF. PTFE is synthesized by the free-radical polymerization of tetrafluoroethylene, which yields a highly crystalline helical structure with a crystallinity of up to 98%, along with a melting point, density, and dielectric constant of ∼320 °C, 2.1 g·cm–3, and ∼2.0, respectively. However, their extremely high melting points and viscosities result in their poor processability. FEP is produced by random copolymerization of tetrafluoroethylene with approximately 5 mol % of hexafluoropropylene, which reduces its crystallinity to around 70% and adjusts its melting point to the range of 260–280 °C. As a result, FEP maintains chemical, thermal, and weather resistances comparable to those of PTFE, while offering improved processability. PVDF is synthesized by polymerizing vinylidene fluoride, and it can form various crystalline phases, including α, β, and γ phases, depending on processing conditions. With a melting point in the range of 155–192 °C and maximum service temperature of 150 °C, PVDF is readily processable into membranes, fibers, and films; furthermore, its mechanical strength, chemical resistance, and dielectric constant (ranging from 8 to 9) can be tuned by controlling its crystallinity (40–65%) and phase transformation. ,

In addition to the aforementioned properties, wettability is a critical characteristic. Tang et al. investigated contact electrification-induced wetting (CEW) across various dielectric films and analyzed how the physicochemical properties at the liquid–solid interface correlated with changes in the contact angle and surface charge accumulation. In their study, 1000 water droplets were sequentially dropped onto each film to saturate the surface with as much charge as possible, followed by measurement of the contact angle changes (Figure b). For PTFE, the contact angle decreased significantly from an initial value of approximately 114° to 99° after CE, and for FEP it decreased from 110° to 92°. In contrast, PVDF exhibited a relatively small change, decreasing from 58° to 54°. This was attributed to the higher electronegativity and lower dielectric constants of PTFE and FEP, which allowed for greater surface charge accumulation compared with PVDF. Furthermore, when analyzing the relationship between the surface charge density (measured via KPFM) and contact angle variation for the same samples (Figure c), PTFE and FEP exhibited values exceeding the prediction line because of their excessively high surface charge densities, whereas PVDF showed results closer to the predicted range. This indicates that PTFE and FEP accumulated more charge on their surfaces because of their higher electronegativity, whereas PVDF exhibited a relatively lower charge storage capability. Owing to these properties, PTFE and FEP are commonly employed as dielectrics in capacitive-discharge mechanisms.

As previously mentioned, PVDF can be more easily processed into membrane-type forms with nano- and microscale channels owing to its lower melting point compared to those of PTFE and FEP. This characteristic makes it commonly used in streaming mechanisms, where its higher wettability relative to those of PTFE and FEP facilitates fluid transport within channels. Guo et al. reported that introducing an electret-like surface charge injection and polarization treatment using a corona discharge on a PVDF nanofiltration membrane simultaneously enhanced the surface potential and separation performance (Figure d). In their experimental setup, a membrane sample was placed between a grounded electrode and brass plate within a cell. Multiple needle electrodes were mounted on the upper brass bar generate a high-voltage corona discharge, allowing the injection of ionized air electrons into the membrane surface and pore interiors. During this process, the polar C–F bonds in the membrane responded to the external electric field by reorienting their dipole alignment, and the injected space charges were trapped in the pore walls, forming a quasi-permanent negative-charge layer. The Zeta potential–pH curves obtained by varying the applied voltage with the corona treatment time fixed at 7.5 min are presented in Figure e. The untreated membrane lost most of its surface potential above pH 6, whereas the electret-treated membranes showed increasingly negative surface potentials as the applied voltage increased and maintained values below 15 mV even in the neutral pH range (6–8). Notably, the membrane treated at −30 kV exhibited the most negative surface potential, reaching approximately −30 mV at pH 7, indicating full alignment of the internal dipoles. However, further increasing the voltage to −35 kV resulted in a decline in potential due to excessive space charge accumulation and localized degradation. Simultaneously, partial transformation of α/γ phases into the more polar β phase within the PVDF structure further contributed to the enhancement of the surface potential by increasing the permanent dipole moment. As a result, at pH values above 6, the membrane surface exhibits a strong negative potential of up to approximately −20 mV, which is a key factor in improving rejection and antifouling performance through electrostatic repulsion against negatively charged organic dyes and foulants such as bovine serum albumin (BSA).

In summary, this study demonstrated that corona-based electret treatments can introduce stable negative charges on membrane surface and within pores, significantly enhancing the zeta potential across a broad pH range. Furthermore, it shows the potential for simultaneous optimization of the surface potential, crystalline phase, and subsequent separation performance, offering a practical guideline for the effective utilization of PVDF in the streaming mechanism of hydrovoltaic technologies.

However, challenges arise when fluorinated polymers are used. A comprehensive review of the existing evidence reveals substantial environmental burdens and widespread contamination across the production, use, and disposal stages. This finding contradicts the hypothesis that these materials are of uniformly low importance. A primary factor contributing to this issue is the reliance on per and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) processing aids, such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), and the ether PFAS HFPO–DA, in GenX processed. These substances demonstrate high persistence and mobility and have been detected in surface water, groundwater, and soil near production facilities. During the synthesis and downstream processing of fluorinated compounds, residual monomers, oligomers, and byproducts may be released into the atmosphere (during heating) and water bodies (via wastewater), thereby contributing to the overall quantity of perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). In addition to legacy surfactants, short-chain perfluoroether acids such as HFPO, DADONA, EEA, and NH4 have become prevalent processing aids for PTFE and PVDF. However, public data on their properties, environmental occurrences, toxicology, and production volumes remain limited. Mounting evidence indicates environmental contamination near manufacturing sites. In the use phase, PTFE-coated cookware shed Teflon microplastics and nanoplastics directly into food. Based on estimates derived from Raman and SEM analyses, the quantity of particles released has been estimated to be approximately two million three hundred thousand particles from a broken coating and approximately nine thousand one hundred particles from a single crack during a brief simulated cooking cycle. The findings, when considered collectively, lend support to the implementation of a life-cycle-based oversight framework, as well as targeted monitoring at manufacturing and incineration sites. Furthermore, these findings provide a compelling rationale for the restriction of applications to essential uses unless the emissions in question can be eliminated. −

PTFE

As previously discussed, PTFE is the most widely employed dielectric in hydrovoltaic devices due to its high electronegativity, stable electret properties, and excellent chemical and environmental durability. These features strongly facilitate charge accumulation and release at water–solid interfaces, establishing PTFE as a prototypical material for capacitive discharge systems. Across different device configurations, the performance of PTFE-based systems has been consistently shown to scale with the minimization of external capacitance, the control of contact line–electrode edge interactions, the enhancement of interfacial slip through superhydrophobic texturing, and the use of asymmetric electrode arrangements.

In droplet-based devices, Li et al. reported that droplets spreading and retracting on inclined PTFE electrets produce sharp high-voltage pulses when momentarily bridging the upper and lower electrodes (Figure a,b). By designing narrow top electrodes and reducing the effective capacitance of the measurement circuit, transient spikes were converted into stable kilovolt-class pulses (Figure c). These results indicate that the optimization of electrode geometry and circuit capacitance plays a more decisive role in performance scaling than droplet size or falling conditions.

In flow-based systems, Cheedarala et al. demonstrated that lining a channel with PTFE and introducing superhydrophobic surface textures lowered interfacial adhesion and extended the slip length, thereby accelerating repetitive contact–separation events and stabilizing average output (Figure d,e). Their results also revealed consistent qualitative trends, namely that tap water outperformed deionized water due to faster electric double-layer relaxation facilitated by dissolved ions, and superhydrophobic PTFE outperformed pristine PTFE due to enhanced droplet mobility and interfacial polarization (Figure f,g). These findings highlight the importance of combining fluid composition, surface wettability, and flow waveform design in amplifying output.

PTFE is only rarely applied in streaming-type mechanisms, but several studies have shown its potential in this context. Liu et al. employed porous PTFE membranes with asymmetric electrode configurations, using a carbon electrode on the reservoir side and a copper electrode on the evaporating surface, to couple streaming potentials and interfacial potentials in phase and thereby produce stable low-voltage direct current output (Figure h). Reversing the electrode polarity caused cancellation of these contributions and diminished performance, confirming that the structural alignment of potential components is essential for device operation (Figure i).

Overall, PTFE-based devices benefit from four broadly applicable design strategies: consistent minimization of external capacitance, acceleration of contact line dynamics through electrode edge and surface structuring, maximization of interfacial slip with superhydrophobic surfaces, and asymmetric electrode configurations that enable in-phase coupling of streaming and interfacial potentials. These principles apply across droplet, flow, and evaporation-driven modes, with PTFE’s superior charge storage stability serving as the foundation that amplifies performance across all operating environments.

FEP

As noted earlier, FEP combines high electronegativity and strong chemical durability with lower crystallinity than PTFE, resulting in better processability. These properties make FEP one of the most versatile dielectrics for capacitive discharge-type hydrovoltaic devices, where stable electret states can be repeatedly exploited under liquid impact or motion.

Li et al. demonstrated the Kelvin droplet electricity generator (K-DEG), in which corona charging continuously replenished surface charges on FEP. A falling droplet served as a transient conductor that triggered the rapid release of stored charges. In their results, individual droplets produced sharp voltage spikes (Figure a), while sequential droplet trains generated repeatable pulse outputs (Figure b). Further optimization of the electrode width and circuit capacitance converted these transient responses into stable high-voltage pulses (Figure c). This work underscored how FEP’s strong charge retention can be amplified by coupling electret stability with circuit-level design.

Deng et al. extended this approach to confined geometries by introducing multiple water plugs in FEP-lined tubes. Each plug created localized charge transfer when moving along the FEP surface, and sequential plug motion led to cumulative amplification of output. Their study showed how the formation of successive contact–separation zones produced strong voltage accumulation (Figure d), while plug spacing and geometry further modulated the overall waveform (Figure e). At larger scales, this effect enabled kilovolt-class cumulative pulses (Figure f). This highlighted that FEP surfaces maintain robust negative charge states under repeated cycling, and careful control of plug dynamics can provide an effective strategy for scaling output.

Luo et al. developed a different strategy with a rotary solid–liquid TENG, where an FEP liner and copper electrodes were mounted inside a rotating chamber. Continuous contact and separation during motor-driven rotation produced alternating-current signals with stable amplitudes (Figure g), while long-term testing confirmed charge density retention across hundreds of thousands of cycles (Figure h,i). This demonstrated the durability of FEP in dynamic liquid environments and its potential for practical energy harvesting under continuous operation.

Taken together, these studies show that FEP-based hydrovoltaic devices benefit from combining material stability with system-level innovation. Whether through droplet-induced switching, sequential plug dynamics, or rotary cycling, FEP enables reliable charge storage and release under diverse fluid conditions. The common lesson is that while FEP provides a stable electret platform, meaningful performance improvements arise when it is paired with geometrical control, circuit design, and continuous-motion engineering, making it a durable and processable material for capacitive discharge architectures.

PVDF

As noted earlier, PVDF stands out among fluorinated polymers for its superior processability and versatile phase behavior, enabling fabrication into porous membranes, nanofibers, and thin films. These attributes, combined with high dielectric constants and strong interfacial polarization, make PVDF particularly suitable for diffusion-driven mechanisms, where sustained ion migration across charged channels is required, as well as for streaming-type hydrovoltaic systems, which rely on fluid motion in confined pathways. Its relatively higher wettability compared with PTFE and FEP further facilitates liquid penetration and charge separation, positioning PVDF as a prototypical material for ion-selective membranes and multifunctional interfaces.

A representative example of the diffusion mechanism was demonstrated by Duan et al., who developed a multilayer moisture-enabled generator composed of a hygroscopic gel, a self-supporting P-CN/PVDF-HFP film, and asymmetric electrodes (Figure a). SEM observations revealed a directional porous skeleton in the gel and a PVDF-HFP matrix that immobilized P-CN within the pores, creating vertical pathways for vapor uptake and proton diffusion (Figure b,c). In this system, PVDF-HFP acted as a mechanically stable scaffold, while the incorporated P-CN provided negatively charged channels that enabled selective cation transport. Coupling hygroscopic and photocatalytic components further allowed light-assisted regeneration of proton gradients, thereby sustaining continuous direct-current output even when moisture-driven processes diminished. This study highlights the role of PVDF-based porous membranes as active diffusion layers that simultaneously support ionic conduction, mechanical integrity, and environmental responsiveness.

Song et al. provided a notable example of the streaming mechanism by introducing an ultrathin ferroelectric P­(VDF-TrFE) interlayer between a Si nanowire substrate and a CNT electrode (Figure d,e). The ferroelectric polarization of P­(VDF-TrFE) effectively tuned band bending at the collection interface, thereby controlling selective charge transport induced by streaming flows within the channels. Depending on the polarization direction, electron injections were suppressed while hole extraction was promoted, which substantially enhanced ion selectivity and device output. The nanometer-thick interlayer served as a tunneling barrier that preserved fluid transport while simultaneously modulating the internal electric field. This approach demonstrated that ferroelectric field control at the nanoscale can amplify streaming-induced charge migration, enabling PVDF-based systems to achieve some of the highest reported power densities along with stable long-term operation.

Collectively, these studies provide two general design lessons. First, diffusion-driven PVDF membranes, especially when combined with hygroscopic or photocatalytic components, can stabilize direct current generation by sustaining proton transport and regenerating ionic gradients. Second, streaming-type PVDF systems benefit from the incorporation of ferroelectric copolymers such as P­(VDF-TrFE), which allow precise tuning of band alignment and internal fields without obstructing liquid flow. By leveraging both diffusion and streaming mechanisms, PVDF emerges as a versatile platform whose superior processability and tunability enable its evolution from a passive ion-conduction medium into an active foundation for hybrid and multifunctional hydrovoltaic energy-harvesting architectures.

Integration of Multiple Mechanisms and Cross-Disciplinary Technologies

Single-mechanism, single-technology, and single-device approaches have limitations in achieving commercialization-level performance in the field of resource harvesting (such as insufficient power output of hydrovoltaic systems and limited water yield of desalination technologies). To overcome these limitations, many studies have explored the integration of two or more mechanisms to enhance productivity and combination of different technologies to achieve multifunctionality. This chapter reviews research that combines different hydrovoltaic mechanisms or integrates hydrovoltaic technologies with other technologies to realize multifunctional systems (e.g., capacitive discharge-streaming coupling, hydrovoltaic-desalination integration).

Among these, capacitive discharge-streaming integration represents a typical mechanism-mechanism coupling in hydrovoltaic systems. Chen et al. devised a monolithic hybrid generator in which a nanostructured hydrophobic FEP triboelectric layer was laminated onto a silicon nanowire-array (SiNW) hydrovoltaic module using a shared Ag electrode (Figure a). The Al stripe deposited on the FEP served as the top electrode of the triboelectric nanogenerator, while the Ag layer simultaneously acted as the bottom electrode of the triboelectric nanogenerator and cathode of the hydrovoltaic device. The vertically aligned SiNWs were in contact with a porous carbon fabric anode that drew residual water into nanochannels, enabling the same raindrop to trigger two consecutive charge-harvesting pathways within a single stack. The momentary droplet impact on the FEP corresponded to a capacitive discharge mechanism, whereas the remaining water absorbed by the SiNW nanochannels resulted in an evaporation-driven streaming mechanism. When a 50 μL tap water droplet fell from a height of 12.5 cm, it spread across the FEP surface and briefly connected the Al stripe to a negatively charged electret. This transient liquid capacitor drove electrons from the Ag layer to the Al electrode and produced a sharp pulse that quickly reached 200 V before returning to the baseline (Figure b). The peak amplitude increased during the first several impacts as the surface charge accumulated on the FEP and then stabilized once the electret was saturated, confirming the self-limiting property of the capacitive discharge process typical of droplet-based triboelectric generators. After the impact, the droplet was drawn into the SiNWs by the carbon fabric. Continuous evaporation drove capillary flow, which in turn transported mobile positive ions through the nanochannels. The streaming effect under investigation generated a steady open-circuit voltage of approximately 550 mV and short-circuit current density of approximately 30 μA cm–2, which was maintained with high stability (Figure c). By integrating the high-voltage millisecond-scale pulses of the capacitive discharge stage with the low-voltage direct-current output of the streaming stage, the device captured both the instantaneous mechanical energy and latent hydrodynamic energy of each droplet.

Moving beyond mechanism-mechanism coupling, Ming et al. demonstrated a representative case of hydrovoltaic-desalination technology integration by introducing a photothermal aerogel–hydrogel binary system that emulates xylem conduits to couple rapid interfacial evaporation with ion-selective charge transport (Figure d). A graphite-doped PVDF aerogel trapped heat near the evaporating interface, while a sulfonic PNIPAM hydrogel threaded through the aligned pores became less wettable under solar heating. This cooperation created vertical gradients in temperature, wettability, and cation concentration, such that water was pumped upward; vapor escaped from the hot hydrophobic top, and cations migrated to establish an internal electric field. The dual gradient was sustained because heat remained near the surface and the contact angles increased from 60° at the base to 91° at the top surface, maintaining a continuous flow through the pore array. The aerogel–hydrogel synergy enabled a round-the-clock hydrovoltaic output. Over a 24 h light–dark cycle, the open-circuit voltage grew from about 200 mV to beyond 300 mV, peak short-circuit current density reached 200 μA cm–2, and power density approached 60 μW cm–2, confirming persistent high-energy generation without external bias (Figure e). Peak daytime performance under 1 sun reached 312 mV and 200 μA cm–2, surpassing a graphite-only aerogel reference by more than an order of magnitude. Evaporation kinetics mirrored these advantages. Under 1 sun, an SHA-HVG evaporated seawater at 2.75 kg m–2 h–1three times faster than an aerogel without a hydrogel filler and 11 times faster than the hydrogel alone (Figure f)owing to intensified interfacial heating, capillary pumping, and a reduced enthalpy of vaporization in ion-rich channels. This study showed that a carefully engineered aerogel–hydrogel couple can coharvest solar heat and ambient moisture to deliver self-sustained desalination and stable direct-current electricity for off-grid sensors, greenhouse irrigation, and other distributed applications.

Similarly, Li et al. realized hydrovoltaic-desalination integration through a membrane-thermoelectric architecture, enabling continuous day–night water–power cogeneration by stacking a CNF@CTAB MXene PTFE Janus membrane on a floating thermoelectric (TE) module such that solar heating of the photothermal top creates a vertical temperature gradient across TE legs, while seawater drawn by capillary action evaporates at the interface (Figure g). The TE module was seated in skeletonized PS foam, with its bottom wetted and its top in contact with the Janus membrane using thermal grease. Continuous wicking from all four sides sustained evaporation, and the membrane heat was funneled into the TE module for power generation. Under dark and windy conditions, convective cooling of the top surface increase the temperature difference and maintained the electrical output, enabling 24 h operation. The robustness of the photothermal layer was evidenced by repeatable heating–cooling cycles under 1 sun. The composite and Janus membranes showed higher steady temperatures than CNF@MXene, and both tracked the same two-stage fast rise and plateau behavior (Figure h). Representative values after 1 h were 45.7 °C for CNF@CTAB MXene, 45.2 °C for CNF@CTAB MXene PTFE, and 42.3 °C for CNF@MXene, confirming that CTAB MXene drives the enhancement, while the PTFE overlayer does not penalize photothermal conversion. Coupling the Janus membrane with the TE module enabled stable cyclic solar thermal voltage generation and a scalable output (Figure i). With an increase in the incident light flux from 0.5 to 3 sun, both the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current increased substantially, reaching several-fold higher values across the tested range. Repeated light on/off cycling produced reproducible voltage and temperature transients, while integration of the module boosted the output power to 0.75 mW, representing a 17.44-fold improvement over bare TE modules. At night, wind-driven cooling lowered the top-surface temperature from 25.3 °C to 9.7 °C as the wind speed increased from 0 to 4 m s–1, thereby increasing the open-circuit voltage from 110.3 mV to 452.3 mV. This study presents a membrane TE architecture that coharvests solar heat for desalination and electricity during the day and leverages wind-assisted thermoelectric power at night, while maintaining persistent output without sacrificing evaporation performance.

While the preceding studies highlight the potential of multifunctional hydrovoltaic systems, it is imperative to address specific challenges such as device durability under long-term operation, fouling in real-world aqueous environments, and the development of reliable large-area fabrication methods. In addition, ensuring long-term stability and cost-effective manufacturing remains essential for commercialization prospects if these systems are to be successfully scaled up to practical applications.

Conclusion

Hydrovoltaic energy harvesting represents a promising yet nascent field that offers sustainable electricity generation from the ubiquitous presence of water in various forms. Despite their eco-friendly and decentralized nature, current hydrovoltaic systems still face challenges in terms of achieving the performance levels necessary for practical and commercial deployment. The primary barriers include limited output power, environmental dependence, and limited durability under real-world conditions.

This review highlights the unique advantages of fluorinated polymers such as PTFE, FEP, and PVDF in addressing these challenges. Their high electronegativity, intrinsic hydrophobicity, chemical resistance, and compatibility with electret-based designs render them suitable for hydrovoltaic applications involving capacitive discharge, streaming, and diffusion mechanisms. By examining recent studies, we have shown how these materials not only improve charge storage and separation but also enable innovative device architectures and hybrid systems.

However, challenges persist in fully actualizing the potential of hydrovoltaic systems. Key research gaps include ensuring long-term charge stability under realistic operational environments, developing scalable and reproducible fabrication methods for large-area devices, and implementing eco-design strategies to mitigate environmental concerns associated with fluorinated materials. To overcome these barriers, future research should also prioritize improving output power density, strengthening device durability against fouling, reducing manufacturing costs, and engineering multifunctional architectures that integrate hydrovoltaic mechanisms with complementary technologies.

Addressing these gaps through interdisciplinary innovation that combines materials science, fluid dynamics, surface physics, and device engineering will be essential for establishing hydrovoltaic energy harvesting as a practical and sustainable solution for decentralized clean energy generation.
