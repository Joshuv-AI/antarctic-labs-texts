Identification of KASP markers and putative genes for herbicide (metribuzin) tolerance in common wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)
Authors: Guannan Liu, Daniel Mullan, Dongcheng Liu, Aimin Zhang, Ling Xu, Hui Liu, Guijun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWeeds significantly threaten wheat production by competing for essential resources and facilitating the spread of pests and diseases. The use of herbicides is the most common approach for controlling weeds. There is an urgent need of mining molecular markers and key genes for herbicide tolerance to accelerate marker assisted/molecular design breeding of new wheat cultivars with improved tolerance to herbicides. However, it is very difficult and costly to mine molecular markers and key genes for complex quantitative trait like herbicide tolerance, especially in wheat, a typical allopolyploid with large complex genome.ResultsTwo practical KASP markers were developed with 77.6%–87.9% conformity between genotype and phenotype of the tolerance to metribuzin (a commonly used herbicide) in wheat with targeted optimization of a conventional BSA-based approach named SRV. The two SNPs corresponding to these two KASP markers are closely linked with two independent genes on chromosome 2AS and both encoding for IgA (Immunoglobulin A) FC receptor protein. More interestingly, another 9 genes having the same annotation of IgA FC receptor were detected within 1 Mbp distance of the two SNPs, forming a gene cluster.ConclusionsThis study has provided valuable insights into the development of KASP markers for metribuzin tolerance in wheat and has identified putative genes that may be involved in herbicide resistance metabolic regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09525-3.Keywords: Metribuzin, Herbicide tolerance, KASP marker, Functional genes, SNP, Recombinant inbred lines, Triticum asetivum L

Background

Despite the modernization of agriculture in cereal crops, considerable crop quality and quantity losses due to weed infestations occur each year [1–4]. Weeds compete vigorously with cultivated crops for essential resources, including sunlight, water, nutrients, and space [5]. Furthermore, their detrimental effects on agriculture extend to inhibiting crop growth through the release of allelochemicals, serving as hosts for various pests and diseases, and clogging or damaging machinery that impedes harvesting efficiency and increases management costs [6–9]. These adverse impacts can directly or indirectly damage crop production and economic returns. Globally, weeds are estimated to cause severe agricultural production losses, amounting to approximately 200 million metric tons each year [1]. In Australia alone, weed-related losses in agriculture are estimated to exceed $5 billion annually (https://www.csiro.au/en/research/plants/pathogens-pests-weeds/biological-control-of-weeds). Wheat, a staple crop consumed by around 35% of the world’s population, is susceptible to weed infestations [10]. Grassy and broadleaf weeds alike can reduce wheat yields by 15–50% [11, 12]. As a result, weed control in wheat cultivation ranks among the highest priorities in agricultural production.

Controlling weeds in commercial crops primarily involves five major approaches: preventive, cultural, chemical, biological, and manual/mechanical methods [13–15]. Among these methods, herbicide application is widely used as an effective and time-efficient means of weed management [16, 17]. In wheat, metribuzin (C8H14N4OS) is one of the most potent, time-saving, and reliable herbicides for weed control. Metribuzin is a photosynthetic-inhibiting herbicide and a triazine herbicide (group C). It is a broad-spectrum selective herbicide commonly used in crops such as wheat, tomato, and soybean [18–20] and typically applied as a preemergent to control various broad leaf weeds and grasses [21]. Early weed control can improve photosynthetic efficiency and prevent early senescence, improving wheat yield [21].

However, the narrow safety margin in metribuzin application poses a risk of crop injury [22]. Such injury typically manifests as stunted plants with chlorotic and necrotic older leaves [23]. Developing and cultivating herbicide-tolerant wheat varieties is imperative to safeguard crops against herbicide damage and optimize crop yields.

Wheat tolerance to metribuzin is a complex quantitative trait that is mainly controlled by nuclear genes with additive genetic effects [24]. Previous studies using quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have detected stable major QTLs on chromosomes 4 A, 5 A, and 6 A, with the QTL on chromosome 4 A explaining 20%–45% of the phenotypic variation for metribuzin tolerance [25]. At the molecular and physiological levels, enhanced herbicide detoxification mediated by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, glycosyltransferases, and reactive oxygen species scavenging systems, as well as protection of photosynthetic machinery, are considered key mechanisms underlying metribuzin tolerance [1]. Candidate genes associated with xenobiotic transport and metabolic detoxification have been proposed, and their functional validation will facilitate marker-assisted breeding for herbicide-resistant wheat cultivars [26].Identifying more reliable and effective markers and genes for molecular breeding of new cultivars with enhanced resistance is important. However, common wheat, being allohexaploid with an extensive and complex genome [27], presents formidable challenges in marker development.

With access to complete wheat genome sequences and various omics tools, there is a pressing need for novel and efficient methods to identify molecular markers and causal genes in wheat. Previous studies have predominantly relied on large populations for investigating candidate markers through QTL mapping or GWAS [28–30]. However, these methods are time-consuming and costly. Moreover, the markers identified through these approaches often exhibit a substantial gap between marker location and actual causal genes, rendering them less suitable for marker-assisted selection (MAS) [31]. In contrast, Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR (KASP) markers, previously used in marker-assisted plant breeding for traits such as pre-harvest sprouting, stripe rust and powdery mildew resistance, and drought tolerance, offer advantages in terms of cost-effectiveness and high-throughput capability [32–35]. However, no KASP markers have been developed or validated for metribuzin tolerance in commercial wheat cultivars. Therefore, developing efficient and reliable KASP markers for metribuzin tolerance in wheat using information from genotyping platforms like DArT sequences is vital.

Previous research used an SQV (S = SNP, Q = QTL, and V=Validation) approach to study pre-harvest sprouting and drought tolerance in wheat [34, 36]. This approach can quickly screen and validate molecular markers and putative genes in contrasting near-isogenic line populations (tolerant and susceptible phenotype). In a prior study, we developed a population of RILs in wheat by crossing metribuzin-tolerant and metribuzin-susceptible cultivars [30]. The present study targets extreme lines within metribuzin-tolerant and metribuzin-susceptible genotypes in the RIL population to develop several KASP markers and validate them in commercial wheat cultivars with known responses to metribuzin. We also aim to identify some putative genes for metribuzin tolerance in wheat, capitalizing on DArT and wheat genome sequence data. The overall objective of the study is to provide valuable insights into the molecular regulation of metribuzin tolerance and its application in MAS for wheat improvement.

Screening candidate SNPs for metribuzin response

Among the RILs with contrasting metribuzin tolerance, 7,865 SNPs were identified. Only 0.83% of the SNPs exhibited more than 85% polymorphic in the two group populations. Specifically, eight SNPs showed 100% consistency with the phenotype of the two extreme groups with contrasting metribuzin response, 18 SNPs showed 95% consistency, 16 SNPs showed 90% consistency, and 23 SNPs showed 85% consistency (Table S1). The eight SNPs with 100% consistency were selected for conversion into KASP markers.

Conversion of the selected SNPs into KASP markers and validation

The eight identified SNPs (DArT2262912, DArT3952140, DArT1088479, DArT1137743, DArT978526, DArT1862392, DArT1074803, DArT1228505) were converted into KASP markers, designated as H912, H140, H479, H743, H526, H392, H803, H505, respectively (Table 2). Two of the eight developed KASP markers, H803 and H912, exhibited perfect genotype and phenotype matches across the 20 selected RILs and their parents (Fig. 2; Table 3). Conversely, the other KASP markers did not exhibit good separation or precise genotype and phenotype matches and were thus excluded from further analysis. The two KASP markers (H803 and H912) were validated in an additional 58 genotypes, including 36 commercial wheat cultivars with known metribuzin responses, 20 RILs with contrasting metribuzin responses, and their two parents (Table 3; Fig. 2). The genotypes clustered into two groups: allele group 1 (blue dots indicating the susceptible allele – FAM allele) and allele group 2 (red dots indicating the resistant allele – HEX allele) (Fig. 2). The accuracy of the two KASP markers (H803 and H912) between genotype and phenotype in the 58 genotypes was 77.59 and 87.93%, respectively, (Table 4). Therefore, KASP markers H803 and H912 were selected for the final assessment of metribuzin response.

Candidate genes for metribuzin tolerance

The DArT SNPs, 1,074,803|F|0–30:C > G-30:C > G (corresponding to KASP marker H803) and 2,262,912|F|0–45:C > T-45:C > T (corresponding to KASP marker H912), which were instrumental in developing high-efficiency KASP markers for metribuzin tolerance evaluation, were used to search for candidate genes associated with metribuzin tolerance on chromosome arm 2AS of the Chinese Spring reference wheat genome (version 2.1, accessed 5 May, 2024). Within a 0.2 Mbp distance from these DArT SNPs, 23 high-confidence genes were identified. Five of these were upstream of the DArT SNP 1,074,803|F|0–30:C > G-30:C > G, and eight were downstream within 0.2 Mbp. Similarly, five were upstream of the DArT SNP 2,262,912|F|0–45:C > T-45:C > T, and six were downstream within 0.1 Mbp (Fig. 3). Table 5 presents detailed information on the 23 genes. Notably, TraesCS2A03G0371200 is the nearest high-confidence gene to the DArT SNP 1,074,803|F|0–30:C > G-30:C > G (H803), with a distance of 2,092 bp, and TraesCS2A03G0372800 is close to the DArT SNP 2,262,912|F|0–45:C > T-45:C > T (H912), with a distance of only 404 bp.

Discussion

Biotic stresses such as weeds significantly threaten crop production, particularly wheat, in many agricultural regions worldwide. As a result, herbicide application is a necessary and effective strategy for farmers. Metribuzin is a selective herbicide used to control annual broadleaf and grassy weeds. However, the increased resistance of weeds to herbicides has necessitated higher herbicide doses [37, 38], underscoring the need to develop wheat cultivars with improved metribuzin tolerance. Molecular-assisted selection offers an efficient approach to expedite breeding efforts and enhance breeding efficiency.

This study selected and used 20 RILs of wheat that exhibited extreme metribuzin-tolerant or metribuzin-susceptible phenotypes for KASP marker development. RILs with alleles from the metribuzin-tolerant parent (Chuan Mai 25) demonstrated superior performance in physiological traits, such as chlorophyll content and visual leaf color, compared to their counterparts.

The validation results for the two KASP markers for metribuzin demonstrated the feasibility of the RSV. The two KASP markers were located on the same wheat chromosome 2 A, with a close physical distance of 0.85 Mbp on the short arm, consistent with the previous study [30, 39]. The previous study reported seven QTL associated with metribuzin tolerance, with the QTL on 2AS (Qrcc.uwa.2AS) one of three major QTL explaining 11.39% of the phenotypic variation [30], a finding strongly supported by the reliability of the marker identified in this study.

Based on the BSA approach, RSV integrates SNPs, extreme RIL lines, SNPs, and KASP validation, focusing on the extreme phenotype of one trait, not the whole population. It reduces the need for genotyping the entire RIL population, saving time and funding. Moreover, it can quickly identifying and validating molecular markers and putative candidate genes, which reduces the workload associated with screening genetic markers during the initial data filtering stage in genome-wide association studies, genome re-sequencing, and/or other related methods such as DNA arrays or SNP chips. Compared with BSA-QTL mapping to select the putative candidate gene, RSV is similarities with BSA-based methods in using extreme phenotypic bulks to enrich trait-associated variants. However, it needed to emphasize that RSV is not designed for de novo QTL mapping; instead, it uses previously reported or preliminary QTL intervals as a reference framework to prioritize and validate candidate markers, rather than defining new QTL positions. The conventional BSA-QTL approaches usually identify large confidence intervals typically but often lack mandatory, population-wide validation of individual markers, the modified RSV workflow used in this study introduces a rigorous, mandatory validation step as its core innovation. This step systematically evaluates and filters candidate markers across the entire segregating population, which substantially reduces false positives and background noise inherent to bulk-based variant calling. In this regard, RSV shifts the focus from interval mapping to high-confidence marker development, making it more tailored for downstream marker-assisted selection than standard BSA-QTL pipelines.

This study selected eight candidate DArT SNPs from the initial screening of the two RIL groups with contrasting metribuzin tolerance/susceptibility across the wheat genome. Subsequent validation with commercial wheat cultivars checked the broad-spectrum effectiveness of the KASP markers, narrowing them to two for MAS. This approach should help researchers select wheat cultivars with extreme tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses by quickly identifying and validating molecular markers and putative genes for quantitative traits.

While the two KASP markers (H803 and H912) identified in this study did not achieve 100% accuracy in predicting metribuzin tolerance in the 58 wheat cultivars/lines with known phenotypes, they still demonstrated a high degree of accuracy (77.59–87.93%). Possible explanations for this result include (1) metribuzin is a complex trait controlled by multiple genes and QTLs [25]; the QTL we investigated may not control the metribuzin tolerant or susceptible phenotype in some cultivars in this study; (2) metribuzin tolerance requires many metabolic routes, with not all metribuzin-tolerant wheat governed by the same pathway [40]; (3) other major QTL on other chromosomes may affect the accuracy of this single QTL validation; (4) the physical distance between the marker and the genes controlled by metribuzin tolerance/susceptibility, and not all cultivars necessarily have the linkage. Based on above mentioned Large size of validation varieties is also needed. In this study phenotypic variability led us to strictly select germplasm accessions with relatively consistent metribuzin tolerance phenotypes to ensure the reliability of marker validation results. Additionally, the limited availability of some target wheat cultivars during this study period further constrained the sample size for validation, resulting in a smaller number of accessions than initially planned. More varieties could be added in the future study. The KASP markers associated with major metribuzin tolerance loci identified in this study could be useful for high-throughput evaluation and MAS of the metribuzin tolerance trait.

The two putative candidate genes identified in this research, TraesCS2A03G0371200, the nearest upstream gene to DArT SNP 1,074,803|F|0–30:C > G-30:C > G, and TraesCS2A03G0372800, the nearest upstream gene to DArT 2,262,912|F|0–45:C > T-45:C > T used to generate the highly efficient KASP marker (H912)- had the same functional annotation (IgA FC receptor), which is often reported in human and mammal immune systems [41, 42]. Immunoglobulin A (IgA) belongs to a predominant antibody (Ab) class, usually in mucosal areas, shielding mucosal surfaces from pathogen invasion [41]. IgA is also involved in anti- or non-inflammatory Ab [43, 44]. However, while few studies have investigated the IgA FC receptor in plants. In a transgene experiment in Arabidopsis, the IgA antigen-binding activity was maintained when reacted with Shiga toxin 1 (Stx1)-specific [45]. The functions of IgA FC receptor in plants remain to be further explored in future studies.

Furthermore, we identified 23 genes within 0.5 Mbp physical distance of the two DArT SNPs using IWGSC v2.1_HC_gene as the reference genome. As the two DArT SNPs are very close to each other on chromosome 2AS (only 0.85 Mbp), the genes searched around these two SNPs partially overlap. The distance between the most upstream and downstream genes is only 1.15 Mbp. Interestingly, 11 of the 23 genes have the same gene functional annotation: IgA FC receptor (TraesCS2A03G0370600, TraesCS2A03G0370700, TraesCS2A03G0371000, TraesCS2A03G0371100, TraesCS2A03G0371200, TraesCS2A03G0371700, TraesCS2A03G0372500, TraesCS2A03G0372600, TraesCS2A03G0372700, TraesCS2A03G0372800 and TraesCS2A03G0373100), accounting for 47.83% of the gene count. The rest of the genes expressed different functions.

TraesCS2A03G0371300, within 8,212 bp of the DArT SNP (1074803|F|0–30:C > G-30:C > G), encodes hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins (HRGPs), which planys use to defend against infection by fortifying their cell walls with HRGPs [46]. Some researchers studied the effects of glyphosate herbicides on plant defense mechanisms related to HRGPs, speculating that glyphosate may impact these mechanisms [47]. TraesCS2A03G0372000 encodes glycosyltransferase, with glycosylation one of the most common posttranslational modifications in proteins [48]. Studies have reported that glycosyltransferase could be a potential gene conferring herbicide detoxification through glucosylation [49, 50].

TraesCS2A03G0373500, within 0.08 Mbp of the DArT SNP (2262912|F|0–45:C > T-45:C > T), encodes the pentatricopeptide repeat-containing family protein (PPR protein). The mitochondrial PPR protein LOI1 reportedly regulates isoprenoid biosynthesis in Arabidopsis [51]. Another study reported that the LOI1 mutant decreased the sensitivity to herbicide clomazone [52].

Overall, this study has provided valuable insights into the development of KASP markers for metribuzin tolerance in wheat and has identified candidate genes that may be involved in herbicide resistance metabolic regulation. Further research is needed to elucidate the specific functions of these candidate genes and their roles in metribuzin tolerance.

Conclusion

Two novel SNPs located on chromosome 2AS, 1,074,803|F|0–30:C > G-30:C > G and 2,262,912|F|0–45:C > T-45:C > T with 100% consistency of genotype-phenotype associations among twenty RILs and their parents showing extreme metribuzin tolerant/susceptible phenotypes, were responsible for metribuzin tolerance. Two highly efficient KASP markers generated from these two SNPs had 87.93% and 77.59% conformity of genotype and phenotype when tested in 58 wheat lines with known metribuzin responses. The developed KASP markers for metribuzin tolerance can be employed for high-throughput germplasm evaluation and marker-assisted breeding in wheat. The two candidate genes closely linked to the SNPs, TraesCS2A03G0371200 and TraesCS2A03G0372800 both encoding for IgA FC receptor protein, might be responsible for metribuzin tolerance in wheat.

Materials and methods

Plant material for genotyping and KASP validation

We used a RIL population derived from a Chuan Mai 25 ⋅ Ritchie cross developed using a fast generation-cycling system [53–55]. Chuan Mai 25, a wheat variety from P.R. China, is known for its high metribuzin tolerance, while Ritchie is susceptible to metribuzin [25]. After obtaining the F₁ generation via hybridization, successive selfing was performed up to the F₈ generation to construct a mapping population consisting of 137 RILs, which ensured the genetic background of the population was nearly homozygous [30]. The study used the extremes from the 137 F8 RILs exhibiting contrasting phenotypic traits: visual senescence score (VS) and mean chlorophyll content (MCC) for the metribuzin-treated RILs, measured previously [30].

For KASP marker validation, we used 36 commercial wheat cultivars, as listed in Table S2. Seven of these cultivars with consistent metribuzin phenotypes were selected from annual crop sowing guide handbooks (2013–2019) published by the Grains Research and Development Corporation (GRDC) (https://grdc.com.au/resources-and-publications/all-publications/nvt-crop-sowing-guides). Most of the information presented is sourced from the Australia National Variety Trials program, covering five states (Western Australia, Queensland, South Australia, New South Wales, and Victoria). The remaining 29 cultivars were chosen based on published studies [21, 39]. Among the 36 cultivars, nine were categorized as metribuzin tolerant, and 27 were metribuzin susceptible.

Based on the BSA approach, we expanded this concept to a RIL population commonly used in this study named it RSV (R = RIL, S = SNP, and V=Validation) (Fig. 4).

General workflow for the RSV-based approach for KASP marker development. Note: The integrated approach uses two groups of RILs with extreme phenotype (R), SNP array (S), and validation using commercial varieties with differences in the specific trait (V)

RIL phenotyping

F₈ RILs were sown one seed per cell in 5 × 6 cell sand-filled trays (50 × 50 × 60 mm) in the University of Western Australia greenhouse (31°57′S, 115°47′E), watered every two days, with three biological replicates (six plants per row) for control and treatment. Each replicate placed in a separate tray to avoid systematic errors caused by block effects. Trays were watered to 100% field capacity pre-herbicide, followed by pre-emergent metribuzin [25] sprayed in two passes (106.19 L ha⁻¹, 50 cm height, 3.6 km h⁻¹, 200 kPa) via flat-fan nozzles (TeeJet XR11001 flat-fan, Spraying Systems Co, Wheaton, IL, USA). Seedlings were transferred to a phytotron (25/15°C, 14-h photoperiod, 800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and watered bi-daily; validation populations used identical settings. This experiment was adopted a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) to minimize the impact of environmental heterogeneity in the greenhouse and phytotron. And the seedling trays (each tray as a block) were randomly arranged in the greenhouse before sowing, and the positions of trays were re-randomized after metribuzin application and transfer to the phytotron. Blocking was implemented based on the spatial distribution of the greenhouse/phytotron to account for potential variations in light intensity, temperature, and humidity across different positions.

The F8 RILs were included two main treatments: group for CK (no metribuzin application) and group for metribuzin treatment ( pre-emergent application of 400 g a.i. ha − 1). The treatment was applied uniformly to the seedling trays using a calibrated cabinet sprayer, following the protocol described by Bhoite et al. [25].

After 14–15 days of treatment, plants were scored from 0 to 10 based on leaf senescence, denoted as VS [25]. A score of 0.00 represents 100% senescence, indicating extreme sensitivity to metribuzin phytotoxicity, 5.00 represents moderate, while a score of 10.00 indicates no damage. All of the control plants remained healthy with the VS score of 10. In addition, chlorophyll content, represented by MCC, was measured using a portable Minolta SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter (Spectrum Technologies, Inc., Plainfield, IL, U.S.). Three replications were conducted for each test, following the protocol established by Bhoite et al. [24].

Ten RILs with high VS (≥ 6.00) and high MCC (≥ 15.90) values and ten RILs with low VS (< 4.00) and low MCC (< 9.00) values were selected from the RIL population to represent the tolerant and susceptible groups. The above data’s classification is based on previous same RILs population’s related phenotype data [30]. These 20 RILs were subjected to detailed genotyping using DArT sequencing analysis.

DNA extraction and DArT sequencing

DNA was extracted from two-week-old young leaves using the cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method [56]. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop™ 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, WA, Australia). Genotyping of the RILs and their parents (Chuan Mai 25 and Ritchie) was carried out using DArT sequencing performed by Diversity Arrays Technology Pty Ltd. (Canberra, Australia). The library preparation strictly followed the standard DArTseq protocol for hexaploid wheat (available at https://www.diversityarrays.com/): genomic DNA was digested with a combination of PstI (restriction endonuclease with a rare-cutting site) and MseI (frequent-cutting site) to generate genome-specific fragments, which were then ligated to DArT barcode adapters, PCR-amplified, and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (100-bp single-end reads). For marker generation, DArT’s proprietary analytical pipeline was used to call SNP markers from the sequencing reads, with no genotype imputation performed for missing data (all missing genotypes were retained as uncalled for subsequent analysis) [30, 57]. The DArTseq sequencing generated a total of 28,668 polymorphic SNP markers (marker density: 6.39 cM per marker) across the 21 chromosomes of hexaploid wheat, with an average missingness rate of < 3% across all markers in the F₈ RIL mapping population.Stringent QC and filtering criteria were applied to raw SNP data to ensure high genotyping quality: SNP calling criteria: Only reads with Phred quality score (Q) ≥ 30 and mapping depth ≥ 5× were used for SNP calling; biallelic SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 were retained to exclude rare variants. Filtering parameters: Markers with > 10% missing data, significant segregation distortion (χ² test, P < 0.01), or ambiguous genotyping signals (no clear differentiation between parental alleles) were removed from the dataset. The raw data of SNP clustering and genotype calling were analyzed using Excel 2013, with the marker alleles for each RIL compared to those of their parental lines, Chuan Mai 25 (A) and Ritchie (B), following the protocol in Xu et al. [30].

Screening SNP markers for metribuzin response

The SNP data were cross-referenced with the WheatOmics database in the DArT_URGI Sect [58]. The filtered SNPs were assigned to tolerant or susceptible RILs and their parents. The SNPs with 100% genotype–phenotype consistency among the 20 RILs and their parents were selected for KASP development and analysis.

KASP marker development and validation

The selected SNPs with 100% genotype–phenotype associations were converted into KASP markers for genotyping within the validation population. KASP primers were designed by LGC Science Shanghai Ltd. (LGC). A junction sequence of the fluorescent moieties FAM (6-carboxy-fluorescein) and HEX (hexachloro-fluorescein) was added to both 5’ ends of the forward KASP primers for genotyping. KASP marker preparation protocols and PCR conditions adhered to the KASP manual (https://www.biosearchtech.com/support/education/kasp-genotypingreagents/running-kasp-genotyping-reactions) using SNPline™ instrumentation. The PCR was performed in a 3 µL total volume using a 384-well plate format. KASP genotyping assays were screened and analyzed using Kraken™ software (v16.3.16.16288). The KASP-related experiments were conducted on the SNPline™ platform by LGC. KASP markers were validated by testing the 20 RILs and their two parents. Confirmed KASP markers were validated in the 36 commercial cultivars with metribuzin response phenotypic data sourced from published papers and GRDC [21, 24, 25, 39]. T-tests were used to detect significant differences between tolerant and susceptible groups.

Identification and annotation of candidate genes

Tightly linked SNPs revealed by the KASP assay with > 75% accuracy rate were selected to identify candidate genes associated with metribuzin tolerance. Genes containing or flanking these SNPs underwent a BLASTN (Basic search) against the IWGSC Chinese Spring genome database (https://urgi.versailles.inra.fr/jbrowseiwgsc/gmod_jbrowse) and WheatOmics 1.0 (http://202.194.139.32/jbrowse-1.12.3-release/?data=Chinese_Spring1.0) using IWGSC v2.1_HC_gene. IWGSC Annotation v1.0 was used to obtain additional annotation information, including domains, gene families, structural details, and biological functions.
