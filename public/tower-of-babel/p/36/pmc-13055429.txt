Sulfonated Hyaluronic Acid‐Based Polymers and Hydrogels Using Thiol‐Ene and Thiol‐Michael Reactions
Authors: Ivo Anton Octave Beeren, Pieter Jelle Dijkstra, Ane Albillos Sanchez, Jopeth Ramis, Francesca Perin, Carlos Mota, Sandra Camarero‐Espinosa, Lorenzo Moroni, Matthew Brandon Baker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNon‐sulfated polysaccharides like hyaluronic acid (HA) have been widely studied as scaffold material for tissue engineering applications. To mimic the function of sulfated glycosaminoglycan in the matrix, sulfate groups can be grafted. However, here, harsh reaction conditions are required which induce significant backbone degradation. As an alternative, sulfonates (R‐SO3 −) have been shown to resemble the function of sulfates yet have not been introduced on polysaccharides. Using a two‐step strategy, we introduced a tunable amount of sulfonate groups on HA, without requiring harsh reaction conditions and organic solvents. By varying the degree of carboxylic acid activation using 4‐(4,6‐dimethoxy‐1,3,5‐triazin‐2‐yl)‐4‐methylmorpholinium chloride (DMTMM), norbornene (NB, 3–18%) or maleimide (MAL, 2–14%) groups were grafted. Subsequently, 3‐mercapto‐1‐propanesulfonate was coupled in high efficiency on the addressable groups via orthogonal thiol‐ene and thiol‐Michael addition. Additionally, we demonstrated the formation of hydrogels using poly(ethylene glycol)‐di‐SH as a crosslinker. However, because of the low crosslinking kinetics, HA‐MAL appeared not useful for application. Simultaneous addition of the crosslinker and MPS to norbornyl‐conjugated HA's in various ratios enabled the formation of hydrogels with tunable stiffness and degree of sulfonate groups. The simple strategy is likely applicable to other commonly used polysaccharides and therefore interesting to the broader tissue engineering community.Keywords: carboxylic acid activation, hyaluronic acid, sulfonated hydrogels, sulfonation

Introduction

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) play a pivotal role in physiological functions such as anti‐coagulation [1], (stem) cell signaling [2], and immune responses [3]. The functioning of GAGs is determined by their molecular weight, degree of sulfation, and position of the sulfate groups on the polysaccharide chain. The types of GAGs range from non‐sulfated, such as hyaluronic acid (HA), to almost fully sulfated, such as heparan sulfate [4, 5]. Except for HA, all mammalian GAGs are linked to a core protein to form a proteoglycan. The proteoglycans are organized in predominantly the peri‐cellular environment by binding to high molecular weight HA via a linker protein [6].

GAGs play a critical role in articular cartilage (AC), where they contribute to its unique properties [7]. These polysaccharides carry negatively charged sulfate groups and generate a positive osmotic pressure by water retention and repulsive interactions. This contributes to a high resistance to compression during motion and provides a low friction surface [8, 9, 10, 11]. In addition, the charged matrix absorbs nutrients from the synovial fluid enabling tissue homeostasis [12]. To emphasize the importance, an imbalance in GAG metabolism or change in degree of sulfation has been associated with the development of osteoarthritis [13, 14, 15]. Thus, the mimicry of this negatively charged microenvironment is considered crucial for AC tissue engineering applications.

Indeed, hydrogels formed from non‐sulfated polysaccharides precursors, such as HA and alginate, are unable to retain efficiently positively charged proteins [16, 17]. The introduction of sulfate groups (R‐O‐SO3 −) in star‐poly(ethyleneglycol)‐heparin(‐derivative) hydrogel systems enabled the sequestering and release of positively charged proteins such as chemokines [18] and growth factors [19]. Moreover, the sulfates prolonged the exposure of chondrogenesis‐associated proteins (TGF‐β1/3), which represses the deposition of undesired hypertrophic cartilage matrix by encapsulated cells [17, 20, 21, 22, 23].

These studies highlight the potential strength of introducing similar groups as the sulfate on commonly used polysaccharides such as HA or alginate, yet this has been considered challenging to perform in a reliable manner. Strong acids such as chlorosulfonic acid are used to introduce sulfate groups via an esterification reaction of the hydroxyl groups of polysaccharides [24, 25]. Nowadays, the most widely employed method uses sulfur trioxide dimethylformamide complexes (SO3/DMF) [26, 27, 28]. However, this method also leads to a significant decrease in molecular weight of HA from 1000 to 50 kg mol−1 [27, 29].

Alternatively, several studies highlighted that sulfonate groups (R‐SO3 −) may provide a more synthetically amenable alternative, while retaining the beneficial properties of sulfated polymers [30, 31]. For example, sulfonate groups on polymers were able to sequester (chondrogenic‐associated) growth factors with similar affinity as sulfate groups [32, 33, 34]. Moreover, Xie et al. developed a bottlebrush like polymer by coupling sulfonated polymers onto adipic dihydrazide grafted HA. They demonstrated that the sulfonated polymer strongly interacted with collagen II, facilitated a lubrication function, and improved tissue regeneration in an osteoarthritic rat model [35].

As proteoglycans in AC are organized along HA chains, we build on this polysaccharide in this study as a proof‐of‐concept polysaccharide [6]. We aimed to develop a straightforward route to modify HA with high control over the degree of sulfonation, without requiring harsh reaction conditions or organic solvents. We performed a two‐step synthesis: (i) conjugation of norbornene or maleimide groups on the HA backbone by an activated ester approach, and (ii) subsequent coupling of sodium 3‐mercapto‐1‐propanesulfonate (MPS) via thiol‐ene or a thiol‐Michael addition reaction, respectively (Figure 1). Finally, we showcased the successful formation of (sulfonated) hydrogels using poly(ethylene glycol)‐di‐thiol (PEG‐di‐SH) in combination with MPS.

A novel synthetic two‐step method is presented to introduce sulfonate groups to hyaluronic acid (HA). After conjugating norbornene‐amine (NB‐NH2) or maleimide‐amine (MAL‐NH2) groups, 3‐mercapto‐1‐propanesulfonate (MPS) was coupled using orthogonal thiol‐ene or thiol‐Michael addition reactions, respectively. The degree of sulfonation is controlled by the degree of substitution of NB or MAL groups in the first step.

Results and Discussion

Tuning the Degree of NB and MAL Conjugation on HA Using Carboxylic Acid Activation Chemistry

Because a high molecular weight HA provides highly viscous solutions, and a very low molecular weight HA (< 20 kg mol−1) is associated with immunostimulatory properties [36, 37], a moderate molecular weight HA was chosen for this study (≈200 kg mol−1).

A well‐known method to activate carboxylic acid groups is by using 1‐ethyl‐3‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) in combination with N‐hydroxysulfosuccinimide (sulfoNHS). Addition of a primary amine like 5‐norbornene‐2‐methylamine (NB‐NH2, endo‐ and exo‐ isomeric mixture) then affords the conjugate via the formation of an amide bond. The pH (4.5–6.5) and stoichiometry of EDC to sulfoNHS was varied to determine the effect of the reaction conditions on the degree of NB substitution (Table S1). After reaction, the products were evaluated by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The integral ratio of the NB vinylic protons (5.90–6.30 ppm) and the ─CH3 protons (2.0 ppm) of the N‐acetyl‐D‐glucosamine was used to determine the DoSNB. Changing the stoichiometric excess of the sulfoNHS or NB‐NH2 to the carboxylic acid groups (pH 4.5–5.5) only slightly increases the DoSNB from 5.5 to 6.5%. Increasing the pH to 6.5 as well as lowering the EDC stoichiometric excess to 1.5 equivalents compared to the carboxylic acid groups (pH 5.5) yielded a DoSNB of only 1%. Activation of EDC of carboxylic acid groups is reported optimally at pH 4.5 [38], and may explain the large decrease in substitution at pH 6.5.

To obtain higher degrees of substitution of NB to HA, we attempted the use of 4‐(4,6‐dimethoxy‐1,3,5‐triazin‐2‐yl)‐4‐methylmorpholinium chloride (DMTMM). D'Este and coworkers demonstrated that DMTMM activation afforded a better control and higher degree of substitution compared to EDC/NHS activation of HA [39]. In a series of experiments, the stoichiometric excess of DMTMM to carboxylic acid groups of HA and subsequent addition of an equal molar amount of NB‐NH2 or 1‐(2‐aminoethyl)maleimide hydrochloride (MAL‐NH2) was performed (Scheme 1). The ratio between the integral value of the NB (5.90–6.30 ppm, 2H) or MAL vinylic protons (6.85–6.90 ppm, 2H) to the HA backbone protons (3.25–4.15 ppm, 10H) was used to calculate the degree of NB substitution and degree of MAL substitution (DoSMAL) (Figures S1 and S2). The DoSNB ranged from 3% to 18%, and the DoSMAL from 2 to 14%, aligned with the stepwise increase of DMTMM present in the reaction (Table 1). Throughout the rest of the paper, the HA conjugates are represented as HA‐NBx, or HA‐MALx, with x being the degree of substitution of NB or MAL, respectively.

Sulfonation of HA‐NB and HA‐MAL via Orthogonal Chemistry

To begin, we synthesized HA with sulfated hydroxyl groups, by addition of a five times molar excess of SO3/DMF complex to the tetrabutylammonium salt of HA (HA‐TBA) (Scheme S1). After reaction, we evaluated the molecular weight using GPC. The M n of the product was 19 kg mol−1 revealing significant degradation of the HA backbone (198 kg mol−1 pristine HA) (Figure S3) [26, 27, 28]. Then, we coupled 1.0 equivalent (respective to NB or MAL) of sodium 3 mercapto‐1‐propanesulfonate (MPS) to the HA‐NB and HA‐MAL polymers for comparison. GPC analysis of the HA‐NB‐MPS and HA‐MAL‐MPS showed that the presented methods did not induce degradation of the HA backbone (M n = 230 kg mol−1) (Figure S3).

The structure of the products after reaction with MPS were determined by NMR spectroscopy. In the 1H NMR spectra, the signal of the vinylic NB protons disappeared and 3 new multiplets at 3.04–2.98, 2.80–2.73, and 2.72–2.67 ppm appeared (Figure 2), suggesting ligation of the MPS. Digging further, a diffusion ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) measurement was performed to demonstrate that the diffusion coefficients of these new peaks were the same as those of HA, indicating the attachment of the MPS to the polymer backbone (Figure S4). Decoupling in the presence of a scalar interactions (DIPSI) spectrum further indicated that these three new multiplets were in proximity. During the DIPSI experiments, a fourth signal at ≈2.03 ppm coinciding with the acetyl (─CH3) proton signal of the HA (Figure S5) was found. A 2D heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC, 1H–13C) spectrum showed coupling of these four new multiplets to the carbon signals at 53, 33, 33, and 27 ppm, respectively (Figure S6). The signal at ≈2.03 ppm was assigned to the central ─CH2─ protons of the coupled MPS, since it coupled to the protons adjacent to both the sulfur and sulfonate group. Given the stronger electron withdrawing nature of the sulfonate group, the multiplet at ≈ 3.00 ppm was assigned to the protons adjacent to the SO3 − group. According to the 2D spectrum and integral ratio, the two multiplets at ≈2.74 and ≈2.70 ppm represented a split signal and were assigned to the ─S─CH2 protons. The peak splitting was attributed to the endo‐ and exo‐ mixture of the NB and the regio‐isomers resulting from the thiol‐ene reaction. With this analysis, we were confident that we created the HA‐NB‐SO3 − as intended.

In the 1H NMR spectrum of HA‐MAL‐SO3 −, we observed the disappearance of the signal of the maleimide vinylic protons at 6.88 ppm, and appearance of three multiplets at 3.08–3.00, 2.97–2.90, and 2.88–2.80 ppm (Figure 3). The DOSY spectrum confirmed attachment of MPS groups (Figure S7) to the larger HA polymer. HSQC showed that these signals were correlated to carbon signals with shifts of 52, 32, and 32 ppm, respectively. Like the MPS coupling to NB, a split signal of the methylene protons (2.97–2.90 and 2.88–2.80 ppm) adjacent to the sulfur group was observed. In addition, DIPSI and HSQC spectra revealed that the signal of the central methylene protons (2.05 ppm) of the MPS was coupled to a carbon signal at 27 ppm (Figures S8 and S9). Here, we also concluded that we created the HA‐MAL‐SO3 − as intended.

The efficiency of the MPS grafting on HA‐NBx and HA‐MALx was evaluated by adding 1.0 equivalents of MPS. The degree of sulfonation was calculated from the integral values of the HA backbone protons (3.25–4.25 ppm, 10H) and the multiplet at ≈3.0 ppm belonging to the methylene protons next to the sulfonate group. The thiol‐ene reaction afforded a degree of sulfonation of 3 ± 1, 11 ± 1, 18 ± 1% on HA‐NB3, HA‐NB11, and HA‐NB18, respectively, similar to the DoS of NB groups (Figure 2). The thiol‐Michael addition yielded a degree of sulfonation of 2 ± 0 and 8 ± 0% on HA‐MAL2 and HA‐MAL8, respectively (Figure 3). These results implied that both orthogonal reactions conjugated MPS with an (almost) one‐to‐one conversion efficiency (>95%). In the following paragraphs, these HA‐based polymers with sulfonate groups are represented as HA‐(NB‐SO3 −)x and HA‐(MAL‐SO3 −) x with x being the DoS of sulfonate groups.

The presence of grafted NB‐SO3 − and MAL‐SO3 − groups was confirmed by a dimethyl methylene blue (DMMB) assay, an assay that is widely used to quantify GAG content in tissue engineering samples [40]. When DMMB binds to a polyanionic substrate, a negative shift in the β band (λ max = ≈595 nm) occurs, while a new absorption arises at λ max = 525 nm. Subtraction of the peak maxima (I 525 nm‐I 595 nm) reflects a relative change in the amount of bound DMMB [41]. Here, we prepared solutions (1% w/v) of the starting materials (HA, HA‐NB18, and HA‐MAL8) and sulfonated HA's (HA‐(NB‐SO3 −)18 and HA‐(MAL‐SO3 −)8). Subtraction of the peak maxima showed that the HA‐(NB‐SO3 −)18 as well as HA‐(MAL‐SO3 −)8 react in the DMMB assay in a similar manner to sulfated GAGs (Figure 4A and Figure S10), notably the curve shape upon binding in this study matches nicely with previous studies on GAGs (like chondroitin sulfate) [41]. The intensity values of the HA‐(NB‐SO3−)18 and HA‐(MAL‐SO3−)8 are 2–3 times higher than their non‐sulfonated counterparts. When we compare these intensity values to a historically recorded standard curve of a DMMB assay, we would estimate that the polymer values of HA‐(NB‐SO3−)18 and HA‐(MAL‐SO3−)8 reflect GAG concentrations of 0.78 and 2.24 µg mL−1, respectively.

DLS measurements on dilute solutions (0.1 mg mL−1) of HA conjugates in PBS were performed to determine their particle size distributions and zeta potentials. We observed that the HA conjugates formed small particles with a major size distribution at approximately 30 nm (Figure S11). Zeta potential measurements were used to determine changes in the charge at the particle‐solution interface by the incorporation of sulfonate groups. The zeta potentials of HA, HA‐NB18, and HA‐MAL8 displayed a zeta potential of ‐9.1 ± 1.1, ‐7.1 ± 1.1, and ‐10.1 ± 1.7 mV, respectively (Figure 4B). Further, we observed a slightly lower zeta potential of the HA‐(NB‐SO3 −)18 and HA‐(MAL‐SO3 −)8 of ‐10.6 ± 3.7 and ‐12.9 ± 2.2 mV, respectively (Figure 4B). Though the extent of ionization is large for both near neutral pH, the sulfonate groups (pKa = ≈1.0) have a lower pKa than the carboxylic acid groups (pKa = ≈3.5), which could lead to this slight decrease.

Hydrogel Formation

The successful coupling of MPS onto norbornene or maleimide grafted HA, without exhaustion of the reactive functional groups, further opened the possibility to prepare hydrogels by crosslinking with dithiols. More simply, the remaining unreacted double bonds provided the opportunity to crosslink the network via thiol‐ene/Michael‐type reactions to form hydrogels. We started a screening experiment to assess hydrogel formation of HA‐NBx (DoS of 11 and 19%) at concentrations ranging from 0.25 to 2.0% (w/v) with PEG‐di‐SH (5 kg mol−1). We added PEG‐di‐SH containing 1.0 equivalent of thiol groups (0.5 equivalents of PEG‐di‐SH) relative to the NB groups and exposed the mixture to UV light (365 nm) for 60 s. Except for the lowest concentration of 0.25% (w/v), stable hydrogels were formed that passed a vial inversion test (Table S2). All experiments described hereafter were performed at a HA concentration of 1.0% (w/v) to ensure gelation across all formulations.

Subsequently, we also qualitatively explored the preparation of sulfonate group containing hydrogels by the combined addition of PEG‐di‐SH and MPS in a single step. Due to the specificity of the reactions, one could produce hydrogels in a simpler process where both sulfonate functionalization and crosslinking occurred in a single step. Towards this goal, HA‐NBx solutions containing 0.5 equivalent of thiol groups from the PEG‐di‐SH crosslinker and 0.5 equivalent of MPS, relative to NB were subjected to UV for 60 s. All HA‐NBx formulations (1% w/v) formed stable hydrogels (Figure S12). When performing the same experiment and equivalents with HA‐MALx, we observed that even after solutions containing HA‐MALx were left for 24 h the gels appeared very soft (i.e., the HA‐MAL3 flowed upon vial inversion) (Figure S12) and crosslinking appeared slow. The underperformance of the maleimide‐based crosslinking may be explained by the use of a non‐optimized buffer for a thiol‐Michael addition in combination with a low functional group density. This led us to conclude that the current HA‐MALx system is less useful for the preparation of gels and excluded them from further rheological evaluation.

Subsequently, the rheological properties of the HA‐NBx hydrogels were assessed by performing time sweeps during in situ UV crosslinking and subsequent strain sweeps. In our first experiment, we wished to keep the amount of crosslinker constant across the series. Relative to the NB groups in HA‐NB3, 1.0 equivalent of thiol groups from PEG‐di‐SH was added. The same amount of crosslinker was also added to the HA‐NB11 and HA‐NB18 (targeting 0.36 and 0.24 equivalents of NB groups, respectively) to maintain the same total polymer content. All formulations gelled within a few seconds upon applying UV light (Figure 5A and Figure S13A). A step increase in the storage modulus was observed, from 17 ± 8 Pa (i.e. HA‐NB3) to 208 ± 18 Pa (i.e., HA‐NB18) (Figure 5C). The relatively low value of the HA‐NB3 suggested that incomplete crosslinking had taken place. In addition, the strain at break of HA‐NB3 hydrogels was shifted towards higher strains (≈900%) compared to other formulations (≈200%). The formation of a lower crosslinked network likely enabled further stretching of the network before disintegration (Figure S13B). The hydrogels with higher DoS of NB formed elastic hydrogels with similar storage moduli, indicating the formation of continuous network with a similar crosslink density. While keeping the crosslink density and polymer content consistent allowed us to understand the effect of the polymer architecture, we were also interested in the mechanical properties at higher crosslink densities. To this end, we explored the crosslinking of HA‐NB18 with PEG‐di‐SH at a 1:1 ratio of SH and vinylic groups and observed a storage modulus of 319 ± 30 Pa (Figure 5C).

In parallel, the effect of simultaneous addition of MPS and crosslinker on hydrogel properties was investigated. Initially, 0.24 and 0.76 equivalents of SH groups of PEG‐di‐SH, with respect to the NB groups, were reacted with HA‐NB18. Fast gelation was maintained, but the stiffness decreased significantly from 208 ± 18 (crosslinker only, see above) to 20 ± 8 Pa (Figure 5B,D and Figure S13C). The addition of MPS likely interfered with the formation of the hydrogel network, reducing the number of crosslinks formed. The shift in strain‐at‐break towards higher strains further indicated the formation of an incomplete network (Figure S13D). Therefore, we increased the relative amount of crosslinker from 0.24 to 0.46 equivalents and reduced the amount of MPS to 0.54 equivalents. This new formulation resulted in a stiffness increase from 20 ± 8 to 104 ± 8.0 Pa (Figure 5D).

Taken together, we here presented a method to form sulfonated HA‐based hydrogels. We do observe that the MPS inclusion in the formulation does lower the modulus and crosslink density when compared to the HA‐NB alone. The exact alteration to the network structure is not immediately evident; however, these results do suggest that when moving to applications requiring high moduli (cartilage, skin, etc.) one would need to have higher polymer content or higher crosslink density in this process for this class of materials. From a cartilage regeneration point of view, future work will be directed to the effect of sulfonate concentration on the retention of positively charged proteins such as transforming growth factor‐β. As higher degrees of carboxylic acid activation (≈50%) via the DMTMM activation may be achieved [39, 42] and concentration of the HA can be increased, we envision the preparation of HA hydrogels with increased stiffness and degree of sulfonation in future studies.

Exploring the Grafting of a Sulfonated Polymer on HA‐MAL

In the reported strategy, we theoretically graft one sulfonate group per disaccharide unit. However, in human tissue, proteoglycans with sulfated GAGs are organized in a bottle brush like architecture on HA [43]. Therefore, we explored the grafting of a sulfonated polymer on the synthesized HA‐MAL or HA‐NB in a first proof‐of‐concept experiment. Reversible addition‐fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) was selected as polymerization mechanism, as the end group of the resulting polymer can be cleaved via an aminolysis reaction to liberate thiol groups [44]. 3‐Sulfopropyl (meth)acrylate (SO3(M)A) potassium salts were polymerized, and butylamine was added to hydrolyze the chain transfer agent (Scheme S2). We were able to successfully polymerize the monomers as shown by the broadening of the monomeric peaks at 4.42, 3.27, and 2.39 ppm (Figure S14, top). After performing an aminolysis via addition of butylamine, we observed the disappearance of peaks corresponding to the chain transfer agent (Figure S14, bottom). We finally obtained aminolyzed poly(SO3MA) and poly(SO3A) of 40 kg mol−1 (Ð = 1.15) and 30 kg mol−1 (Ð = 1.12), respectively.

To assess whether thiol‐Michael addition or thiol‐ene chemistry were viable grafting strategies, we first performed a model reaction using aminolyzed poly(SO3((M)A) and monofunctional PEGs (Figure S15A). The thiol‐Michael addition between PEG5k‐MAL and poly(SO3MA) was performed at pH 7 and 9. At pH 7, the PEG‐MAL trace disappeared after 24 h, and simultaneously a small shift towards higher molecular weight (from 35.7 to 37.3 kg mol−1) of the poly(SO3(M)A) was observed (Figure S15B,D). When we attempted the coupling of PEG5k‐NB to the two aminolyzed polymers via thiol‐ene chemistry, no apparent shift nor disappearance of the PEG peak was observed, even after applying 10 min of UV light (Figure S15D). Compared to a typically used primary thiol, the liberated secondary and tertiary thiols may have a decrease reactivity due to steric constraints or stabilize the formed radical due to hyperconjugation effects [45, 46]. These results suggested that the thiol‐Michael addition at pH 7 could provide a path forward to conjugation of a sulfonated polymer on HA. In the Supporting Information, a small trial experiment of the grafting of poly(SO3MA) on HA‐MAL is described (Scheme S3 and Figure S16). At pH 7, the appearance of a shoulder in the GPC trace implied the formation of a higher molecular weight species. However, due to difficulties in purification, characterization, and apparent low conjugation efficiencies, this approach requires further optimization and likely necessitates a new conjugation strategy to form polysulfonated bottlebrush architectures.

Conclusions

We have presented a method to graft sulfonate groups via intermediate conjugated norbornene or maleimide groups on HA under aqueous conditions. The degree of NB or MAL substitution was varied by changing the ratio of DMTMM to carboxylic acid groups of the HA in a controllable fashion. Subsequently, thiol‐ene and thiol‐Michael addition chemistry were used to conjugate MPS nearly quantitatively to the HA backbone. To unlock a higher degree of sulfonation via the reported method, a systematic study could be performed to tailor the amount of carboxylic acid activation with DMTMM. The NB groups conjugated to HA further allowed the preparation of sulfonated hydrogels using simultaneous addition of MPS and PEG‐di‐SH. Rheological evaluation demonstrated the formation of soft elastic hydrogels and indicated a convenient way forward to the production of one‐pot hydrogels with higher functionality. Since sulfated/sulfonated HA is interesting for biomedical applications, further research could test the cytocompatibility of this method of sulfonation (as cytocompatibility for sulfated/sulfonated HA is well reported) [30], the effect of the hydrogels on cell culture, or effect of concentration of sulfonate groups on the retention of chondrogenic‐inducing proteins such as TGF‐β. Finally, the presented sulfonation strategy is well applicable to other commonly applied polysaccharides in tissue engineering applications and opens opportunities to conjugate a variety of thiolated molecules. In this context, we could also introduce both handles simultaneously to decouple bioconjugation from crosslinking.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, unless stated otherwise, and used without further purification. Sodium hyaluronate (≈ 200 kg mol−1) was bought from Lifecore and used as received.

Synthesis

HA‐NBx

In a typical reaction, sodium HA (350 mg, 0.88 mmol, 1.0 equiv of carboxylic acid groups) was dissolved in 0.1 m 2‐(N‐morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer (0.3 m NaCl, pH 6.5) at 1% w/v. According to the stoichiometry shown in Table 1, DMTMM and 5‐methylamine‐2‐norbornene (TCI chemicals) were added in either 1:1, 2:2, or 4:4 molar ratio to vary the degree of substitution. For example, 2.0 equiv of DMTMM (484 mg, 1.75 mmol) and 2.0 equiv of NB‐NH2 (224 µL, 1.75 mmol) were added. The products were precipitated in cold EtOH and washed with cold EtOH (3x). The product was re‐dissolved in distilled water (dH2O), transferred to a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) Snakeskin dialysis membrane, and dialyzed against 50 mm NaCl (2x), 25 mm NaCl (2x), 12.5 mm NaCl, 5 mM NaCl, and dH2O. The product was obtained as a white fluffy solid after freeze‐drying. Final yield = 300 mg, 86%. 1H NMR (700 MHz, D2O, δ in ppm): δ 0.53 (t, J = 9.0 Hz), 1.16 (t, J = 11.9 Hz), 1.26 (d, J = 7.0 Hz), 1.39 (b), 2.01 (s), 2.61 (s), 2.64 (s), 2.72 (t, J = 10.9 Hz), 2.84 (m), 3.07 (t, J = 11.9 Hz), 3.18 (m, J = 11.9 Hz), 3.70 (b, HA backbone), 4.45 (b, HA backbone), 5.97 (d, J = 5.1 Hz), 6.02 (d, J = 5.1 Hz), 6.14 (m), 6.25 (t, J = 5.2 Hz).

HA‐MALx

For the synthesis of HA‐MALx, the procedure of HA‐NBx described above was used with minor adaptations. HA (300 mg, 0.75 mmol, 1.0 equiv of carboxylic acid groups) was dissolved in degassed 0.1 m MES buffer. Subsequently, a stoichiometric amount of DMTMM to 2‐maleimidoethylamine trifluoroacetate salt of 1:0.5, 2:2, and 4:4, relative to the carboxylic acid groups were added. The products were dialyzed against a series of MQ containing 50 mm NaCl (2x), 25 mm NaCl (2x), 12.5 mm NaCl, 5 mm NaCl and finally with MQ. The product was obtained as a white fluffy solid after freeze‐drying. Final yield (HA‐MAL8) = 259 mg, 87%. 1H NMR (700 MHz, D2O, δ in ppm): δ 2.01 (s), 2.03 (b, HA backbone), 3.70 (b, HA backbone), 4.45 (b, HA backbone), 5.99 (d, J = 11.8 Hz), 6.33 (d, J = 11.8 Hz), 6.89 (s).

HA‐(NB‐SO3)x / HA‐(MAL‐SO3)x

In a typical reaction, HA‐NBx or HA‐MALx (with various degree of substitution of NB or MAL) were dissolved in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) overnight. Then, relative to the amount of NB or MAL groups, 1.0 equiv MPS was added from a stock solution (10 mg mL−1), according to the volumes depicted in Table 2. All used solutions for the HA‐(NB‐SO3) synthesis contained 2 mM lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP). Thiol‐ene reaction was initiated by applying UV light (365 nm) for 1 min. The solutions containing HA‐MAL were left at RT for 24 h. All solutions were transferred to a Snakeskin dialysis membrane (10 kDa MWCO) and dialyzed against 12.5 mm NaCl and twice against dH2O. The products were collected as white fluffy solids after freeze‐drying.

Equipment

NMR Spectroscopy

Samples were dissolved in buffered D2O (pH 7.42, [phosphate] = 56.9 mm and [3‐(trimethylsilyl)‐1‐propanesulfonic acid‐d 6 sodium salt] = 0.11 mm). All NMR spectra were recorded at 299.7 K using a Bruker Avance III HD 700 MHz spectrometer equipped with a cryogenically cooled three‐channel TCI probe, and analyzed with TopSpin 4.0 software (Bruker, Germany).

Gel Permeation Chromatography

Molecular weight and dispersity of the HA were determined by GPC on a Prominence‐I LC‐2030C3D LC (Shimadzu) system comprising an autosampler, a Shodex SB‐G 6B guard (6.0 mm x 50 mm) column, followed by a Shodex SB‐803/SB804 HQ (8.0 mm x 300 mm) column, a refractive index detector, and a photodiode array detector. 0.1 m NaNO3 in distilled water was used as mobile phase at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 at 25°C. Calibration was performed using PEG standards up to 545 000 M W (PEG calibration kit, Agilent Technologies). Purified samples were always dissolved at a concentration of 1.0 mg·mL−1 in 0.1 M NaNO3. All samples were filtered through filters with pore size 0.2 µm prior to injecting a volume of 50 µL.

Dynamic Light Scattering

Dynamic light scattering was conducted with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK), equipped with a red laser (683 nm, He–Ne), and was used to determine the hydrodynamic radius and zeta potential of diluted HA conjugates. All measurements were performed at a temperature of 25°C. HA, HA‐NB18, HA‐MAL8, HA‐(NB‐SO3 −)18, and HA‐(MAL‐SO3 −)8 were dissolved at a concentration of 0.1 mg mL−1 in PBS (1.02 mPa⋅s, refractive index = 1.335, Dielectric constant = 79.0). A refractive index of 1.563 and absorption value of 0.001 [47] was used for HA solutions. The hydrodynamic radius of HA conjugates was determined using polystyrene disposable cuvettes, and zeta potentials using disposable folded capillary cells (DTS1070).

Rheology

Rheological measurements were performed using a DHR‐2 from TA instruments equipped with UV crosslinker platform and solvent trap using a 20 mm cone‐plate with an angle of 2.002° at 20°C. A time sweep was performed for 360 s at 1 rad s−1 and 1% strain. UV (365 nm, 10 mW cm−2) was applied after 120 s for 60 s. Finally, we performed a strain sweep from 1–1000% strain at 1 rad s−1.

DMMB Assay

HA, HA‐NB18, HA‐MAL8, HA‐(MAL‐SO3 −)8, HA‐(NB‐SO3 −)11, and HA‐(NB‐SO3 −)18 samples were dissolved in PBS at a concentration of 1% w/v. Then, 5 µL of 2.3 m NaCl, 150 µL of 1,9‐dimethyl‐methylene blue zinc chloride double salt solution (38.5 µm, pH 3.0), and 25 µL of samples were combined in a 96 black imaging wells plate. The absorbance was measured between 500 and 700 nm, with increments of 5 nm, on a Clariostar spectrophotometer. We corrected the absorbance curves with the curves yielded by a PBS + DMMB solution [39]. The intensity values at λ 595 nm from λ 525 nm were subtracted to assess the relative differences in sulfonate content.

Grafting of Aminolyzed, Sulfonated RAFT Polymers on PEG or HA

The synthesis of aminolyzed poly(SO3MA), poly(SO3A), and subsequent grafting on monofunctional PEG‐NB/MAL as well as grafting on HA‐MALx are described in the Supporting Information.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data were presented as means ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. After confirming the normal distribution of the data, we determined statistical significance by a one‐way analysis of variance with Tukey's honest significant difference tests. Otherwise, a non‐parametric equivalent test was performed. (****) p < 0.0001, (***) p < 0.001, (**) p < 0.01, (*) p < 0.05.
