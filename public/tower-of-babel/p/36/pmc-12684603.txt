Procedural Volume and Outcomes in Lower‐Extremity Peripheral Vascular Interventions: Insights From the NCDR Peripheral Vascular Intervention Registry
Authors: Zainali Chunawala, Arman Qamar, Kevin Kennedy, Neil Keshvani, Ambarish Pandey, Dharam J Kumbhani, Cheong Jun Lee, Gregory Mishkel, Eric Secemsky, Deepak L Bhatt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLower‐extremity peripheral vascular interventions (PVIs) are increasingly used for treatment of peripheral artery disease. However, the relationship between hospital and operator PVI volume with in‐hospital major adverse limb events (MALEs) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) is not well established.MethodsWe used the NCDR PVI (National Cardiovascular Data Registry) registry, including procedural data from April 1, 2014, to December 31, 2019, assessing in‐hospital MALEs and MACEs during hospitalization for PVI. Annualized PVI volumes were categorized on the basis of operator or site volume. Generalized linear mixed models assessed volume–outcome relationships after adjustment of covariates.ResultsBetween 2014 and 2019, 55 785 PVI procedures were performed at 97 hospitals by 555 operators. Adjusted analysis showed no statistically significant association between hospital volume and MALEs (highest versus lowest volume: odds ratio, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.74–1.11]; P=0.35) or hospital volume and MACEs (highest versus lowest volume: odds ratio, 1.23 [95% CI, 0.95–1.16]; P=0.12). Notably, undergoing PVI with high‐volume operators was associated with lower odds of in‐hospital MALEs (odds ratio, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.55–0.97]; P=0.03) and MACEs (odds ratio, 0.64 [95% CI, 0.44–0.95]; P=0.03). Female sex and comorbidities including dyslipidemia, prior percutaneous coronary intervention, heart failure, and acute or chronic limb ischemia were associated with higher MALE risk. Older age and comorbidities including hypertension, dyslipidemia, end‐stage renal disease requiring dialysis, severe lung disease, prior myocardial infarction, heart failure, and acute limb ischemia were associated with greater MACE risk.ConclusionsIn contemporary practice, a significant relationship was observed between operator volume and adjusted PVI outcomes. Highest‐volume operators had lower rates of in‐hospital MALEs and MACEs compared with lowest‐volume operators.Keywords: major adverse cardiovascular events, major adverse limb events, peripheral artery disease, peripheral vascular interventionsSubject Categories: Peripheral Vascular Disease

Lower‐extremity peripheral artery disease (PAD) has emerged as a critical health concern, causing substantial morbidity and death, with an estimated 12.5 million Americans affected. 1 , 2 , 3 The cost associated with hospitalization and revascularization procedures for patients with PAD continues to rise, intensifying the economic burden. 4 , 5 , 6 Lower‐extremity peripheral vascular interventions (PVIs) have expanded significantly and gained prominence as one of the primary approaches to revascularization in patients with PAD. 7 , 8 , 9 However, given the increasing prevalence of PAD, the expanding use of PVI as a treatment modality, and its significant cost burden, ensuring optimal quality of care of patients with PAD undergoing PVI is of principal importance. Moreover, ensuring equitable access to high‐quality PVI care is critical, as access inequities disproportionately affect racial and ethnic minorities and women, contributing to disparities in PAD outcomes. 10 , 11 , 12

Previously, the role of procedural or surgical volume, both at the individual operator and site levels, has been investigated as a surrogate to define quality of care in many cardiovascular settings. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Despite the apparent intuitiveness and ease of collecting volume as a metric, there exists a significant degree of heterogeneity in PVI procedural use nationwide arising from differences in operator specialty, training requirements, and procedural location. Furthermore, the relationship between procedural volume and patient outcomes in the context of PVI remains largely unknown. Accordingly, the objective of our study was to investigate the association between the volume of PVI procedures conducted by hospitals and individual operators and the occurrence of in‐hospital major adverse limb events (MALEs) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). We hypothesized that higher operator and hospital procedural volumes would be associated with lower rates of in‐hospital MALEs and MACEs in patients undergoing PVI procedures.

Methods

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its Supplemental Material.

Study Population and Cohort Derivation

The NCDR (National Cardiovascular Data Registry) PVI registry is a large, nationwide, voluntary, continuous quality improvement registry of all patients undergoing PVIs. The NCDR PVI registry contains detailed information on patient demographics, clinical characteristics and comorbidities, procedural information, and outcomes. Additionally, the registry captures procedure operator and facility characteristics. Details regarding data collection and standardization and registry structure and governance have been published previously. 18 Data managers at each center supervise data collection, and data are submitted securely through a web‐based data collection tool. Clinical end points were determined on the basis of coding within the registry rather than adjudicated by a commission. Waiver for informed consent and authorization for this study was granted by the Advarra Institutional Review Board. All PVI procedures (N=55 785) from April 2014 to December 2019 were included in the analysis at 97 sites by 555 operators.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were MALEs and MACEs during the hospitalization. MALE was defined as a loss of patency of the revascularization, repeat intervention, or a major amputation of the revascularized limb. MACE was defined as a cerebrovascular event, myocardial infarction (MI), or death. Other secondary outcomes included individual components of the composite end points, length of stay, access site complications involving bleeding at the access site, unexpected intubation, and generalized bleeding events within 72 hours indicated by a hemoglobin drop ≥3 g/dL or requiring blood transfusion.

Clinical Characteristics

Baseline patient characteristics included age at the time of PVI, sex, race, and other sociodemographic factors. In this study, sex refers to biological attributes (male/female) as recorded in the NCDR registry, while gender refers to sociocultural roles (men/women). Analyses of sex‐based outcomes reflect biological differences, whereas gender‐related disparities are discussed in the context of systemic factors. Race and ethnicity were reported by site personnel and used as sociopolitical categories to account for disparities in access to care and systemic inequities rather than innate biological differences. Their inclusion serves as a proxy for structural inequities that may affect access to high‐quality care, including referral to high‐volume operators, insurance coverage, and differences in community resources. Clinical comorbidities and characteristics included hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, end‐stage renal disease on dialysis, coronary artery disease (CAD), severe or very severe lung disease, prior MI, cardiomyopathy or left ventricular systolic dysfunction, prior heart failure (HF), prior percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), prior coronary artery bypass grafting, prior valve surgery, tobacco use, and family history of premature CAD. CAD was defined as a current or previous history of coronary artery stenosis ≥50% (by cardiac catheterization or other modality or of direct imaging of the coronary arteries), prior MI, prior PCI, or prior coronary artery bypass grafting. Severe and very severe lung disease were determined on the basis of home oxygen therapy and Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease criteria: forced expiratory volume in 1 second <40%. 19 Clinical information on stage of PAD at presentation and maximum claudication distance were included. The maximum claudication distance was collected as a part of the baseline clinical assessment and was reported by the patients during their clinical evaluation. It indicated the patient‐reported maximum distance at which the leg pain became so severe that it forced the patient to stop walking. Procedural status (elective, urgent, or emergent), procedure indication, procedure location, and procedural information such as procedural vital signs, sedation, medication administration, contrast volume, and fluoroscopy time were included.

The annualized counts of hospital and operator procedure volumes were determined by dividing the cumulative number of PVI procedures performed at the hospital or by an individual operator throughout the study duration by the number of months between the first and last procedures, then multiplying by 12 months. In instances in which multiple operators participated in a procedure, each operator received credit for their contribution to the procedure.

Statistical Analysis

Annualized hospital volume of PVI procedures was analyzed as a categorical variable sorted into volume groups of either operator or site volume. Baseline and clinical characteristics were presented as frequencies (percentages) and continuous variables presented as mean±SD or median (interquartile range). Procedural characteristics and procedural outcomes were described across these volume groups. Categorical variables were compared using Mantel–Haenszel trend test and continuous variables compared using the linear trend test.

The primary analysis examined the association between volume groups of hospital procedural volume and postprocedural MALEs and MACEs. The secondary analysis examined the association between volume groups of operator procedural volume and postprocedural MALEs and MACEs. Multivariable logistic regression models were developed to assess volume–outcome relationships, with marginal estimates reported. Relationships were plotted as curves for annualized hospital procedural volume and outcomes and for annualized operator procedural volume and outcomes. Analyses were repeated following adjustment of relevant covariates including age; gender; race; history of hypertension; dyslipidemia; diabetes; CAD; heart failure; lung disease; current dialysis; tobacco use; severity and presentation of PAD; and family history of CAD, chronic limb ischemia, and acute limb ischemia. Further adjustment for operator volume was included in the assessment of hospital procedural volume and clinical outcome relationship. Additionally, to account for clustering of patients within physicians/hospitals we included a random intercept for hospital and operator. All analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Hospital and Operator Procedural Volumes

Between quarter 2, 2014, and quarter 4, 2019, a total of 55 785 PVI procedures were performed at 97 hospitals by 555 operators. Annualized PVI hospital procedural volume ranged from 4 to 907 (median, 261) procedures, while annualized operator procedural volume ranged from 4 to 256 (median, 66) procedures. Patient and Procedural Characteristics

Mean age of all patients who underwent PVI was 69.0±11.2 (80.7% White individuals, 40.6% women). There was an overall high prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension (90%), diabetes (53.9%), CAD (50.7%), and current or former tobacco use (77%). Of total procedures, 76% were classified as elective, 21.8% were classified as urgent, and 2.2% were emergent. Most procedures were for typical claudication (44.1%) and critical limb ischemia (44.5%), with less frequent treatment of acute limb ischemia (6.0%). Overall, 81.7% of procedures took place in a catheterization laboratory, 6.3% in an operating room, and 12.0% in an interventional radiology suite.

Across volume groups of hospital volume of PVI, there was no significant difference in age (Table 1). Highest‐volume centers cared for a greater percentage of Black patients (22.8% versus 12.5%, P<0.001) and a lower percentage of White patients (74.5% versus 85.0%, P<0.001). There was a significantly greater prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, prior MI, and prior HF among the highest‐volume centers. Finally, compared with the lowest‐volume centers, the highest‐volume centers had a greater proportion of procedures performed in an operating room (7.6% versus 3.2%, P<0.001) and fewer procedures conducted in the catheterization laboratory (84.1% versus 93.6%, P<0.001).

Procedural Outcomes and Association With Volume

Highest‐volume centers had higher rates of arterial thrombosis (1.1% versus 0.6%, P<0.001), embolism (0.8% versus 0.3%, P<0.001), and major dissection (2.5% versus 0.8%, P<0.001) and lower rates of bleeding events (2.1% versus 2.6%, P<0.001) or blood transfusion (3.4% versus 4.5%, P<0.001) as compared with lowest‐volume centers. Overall, the rates of in‐hospital outcomes were 1.8% for MALEs, 1.0% for MACEs, and 0.8% for all‐cause death. The incidence of MALEs was greater in highest‐volume centers compared with lowest‐volume centers (2.0% versus 1.8%, P=0.02). However, there were no significant differences observed in in‐hospital MACEs or in‐hospital death across groups of hospital volume (Table 3). In the adjusted analysis, there was no association observed between groups of hospital volume and MALEs (highest‐ versus lowest‐volume: odds ratio [OR], 0.91 [95% CI, 0.74–1.11]; P=0.35) and groups of hospital volume and MACEs (highest‐ versus lowest‐volume: OR, 1.23 [95% CI, 0.95–1.59]; P=0.12; Figure 3).

Highest‐volume operators had higher rates of arterial thrombosis (0.9% versus 0.5%, P≤0.001), embolism (0.6% versus 0.3%, P<0.001), and major dissection (1.8% versus 0.5%, P<0.001) and lower rates of generalized bleeding events (2.3% versus 3.1%, P=0.02) or access‐site complications involving bleeding at access sites (0.9% versus 1.0%, P=0.01) compared with lowest‐volume operators. Across groups of operator volume, the rates of in‐hospital MALEs were comparable among highest‐ and lowest‐volume operators. Incidence of in‐hospital MACEs (0.9% versus 1.2%, P=0.01) and in‐hospital death (0.7 versus 1.0, P=0.01) were lowest among highest‐volume operators as compared with lowest‐volume operators (Table 3). In the adjusted analysis, undergoing PVI with highest‐volume operators (versus lowest‐volume operators) was associated with lower odds of in‐hospital MALEs (highest‐ versus lowest‐volume: OR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.55–0.97]; P=0.03) and MACEs (highest‐ versus lowest‐volume: OR, 0.64 [95% CI, 0.44–0.95]; P=0.03; Figure 3). Additionally, the intermediate‐operator‐volume group was also associated with lower risk of MALEs as compared with the lowest‐volume group (OR, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.49–0.89]; P=0.01). These associations remained consistent after further adjusting for the location of the procedures (eg, catheterization laboratory, operating room, interventional radiology suite; Table S1). On further stratification based on PAD categories, this association remained consistent without any statistically significant heterogeneity (P‐interaction>0.20). The association also remained consistent when stratified on the basis of procedural indication. However, no significant association was observed when stratified on the basis of procedural status (elective versus nonelective).

Female sex, comorbidities such as dyslipidemia, prior PCI, prior HF, and acute or chronic limb ischemia were all associated with higher risk of MALEs (Table S2). Hispanic ethnicity was also linked to a higher risk of MALEs compared with non‐Hispanics, although no differences were observed when stratified by race. Additionally, older age, comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, dialysis, severe lung disease, prior MI, prior HF, and acute or critical limb ischemia were all associated with greater risk of MACEs (Table S3). No differences in MACE risk were observed across racial or ethnic groups.

Discussion

From this analysis of >55 000 PVI procedures nationwide, there was substantial variability in operator and hospital volume for PVIs. In the multivariable adjusted models, incidence of in‐hospital MALEs and MACEs was lowest among the highest‐volume operators. Conversely, there was no clear relationship between hospital volume and MALE or MACE events after PVI in the adjusted analysis.

The volume–outcome relationship has been investigated across a wide variety of procedural and surgical domains. In cardiovascular operations such as coronary artery bypass grafting 16 , 20 and valve surgery, 15 , 21 , 22 higher hospital and surgeon volume has been shown to be associated with a lower mortality rate. Similar findings have been seen among vascular surgery procedures such as abdominal aortic aneurysm repair, carotid endarterectomy, and arterial lower‐limb bypass procedures. 23 This relationship was also described for some analyses of PCI, 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 transcatheter aortic valve repair, 28 and carotid stenting 29 where an inverse relationship has been seen with volume and outcomes. However, most studies focus on carotid and aortic procedures, with only limited evidence for lower‐extremity PAD interventions. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 To our knowledge, the present study is 1 of few investigations assessing the volume–outcome relationship in PVI.

Our study aimed to address the knowledge gap by conducting a contemporary assessment of the relationship between hospital and operator volume of PVI and in‐hospital outcomes. In this study, highest‐volume operators were associated with a lower incidence of in‐hospital MALEs and MACEs when adjusted for clinical comorbidities and patient demographics. However, hospital volume was not associated with in‐hospital MALEs or MACEs. These findings seem to be concordant with the “practice makes perfect” hypothesis, which reasons that higher volumes per operator lead to improved decision making and operator technique and decreased postoperative complications, resulting in improved clinical outcomes. In the past several years, volume has been an appealing quality assessment, as it is relatively easy to determine and does not require detailed clinical data. Previous studies have reported significantly better outcomes for vascular procedures with high‐volume providers, both in the elective and emergent setting. 23 Our study adds value by expanding on the volume–outcome relationship in the specific context of PVI, particularly in a real‐world setting where patient populations and treatment practices are highly variable. Based on our findings, it is conceivable that individual operator volumes may be more significant than institutional volume for optimizing PVI procedural outcomes.

On the contrary, higher hospital volumes were not associated with a clear reduction in incidence of MALE and MACE events. There are many possible reasons for this lack of relationship. As compared with transcatheter aortic valve repair, which is a more novel technology, PVI procedures use similar technology as coronary angioplasty and coronary stenting. Importantly, the relationship between PCI volume and clinical outcomes in contemporary cohorts is much more modest than when the procedure initially was developed, 34 , 35 and perhaps the relationship between any percutaneous procedural volume and outcomes is attenuated with greater temporal procedural familiarity. Consequently, there may be greater familiarity with PVI procedures despite low hospital volumes given crossover of skills from coronary interventions. This may be especially important given that >80% of procedures in the present study occurred in a catheterization laboratory as opposed to interventional radiology and in an operating room. Moreover, it is not uncommon to have multiple physicians from different specialties with endovascular privileges. Conceivably, these differences in cross‐specialty practices could lead to variation in care quality and outcomes. Finally, there are many different PVI procedures, including angioplasty, atherectomy, and stent placement across various lower‐extremity arteries. Given this heterogeneity in procedures, assessment of general PVI volume may not have enough specificity to properly elucidate the relationship between hospital volume and outcomes. Given these findings, it seems plausible that depending primarily on institutional procedural volume may not adequately discern between low‐performing or high‐performing institutions in PVI within the contemporary landscape.

In our study, female sex and comorbidities such as dyslipidemia, prior PCI, and HF were associated with higher risk of a MALE. Several studies have explored sex‐related differences of outcomes in patients with PAD. 36 , 37 For example, the K‐VIS ELLA (Korean Vascular Intervention Society Endovascular Therapy in Lower Limb Artery Disease) registry demonstrated that female patients undergoing endovascular treatment had higher rates of major amputation, complex lesions, procedural complications, and limb‐specific adverse events compared with male patients. 38 Similarly, results from the Vascular Quality Initiative registry reported that female patients undergoing lower‐extremity PVI for symptomatic PAD faced a greater risk than male patients of developing periprocedural complications, including access‐site bleeding and above‐knee amputation. 39 These disparities may stem from factors such as older age, smaller vessel size, or more severe anatomic disease burden in female sex. Additional contributors include less physical activity, smaller calf muscle mass, higher proportion of asymptomatic disease, and more comorbidities including arthritis or osteoporosis, leading to delayed detection of PAD in female patients. 40 , 41 , 42 Physicians should be aware of the increased risk of procedural complications and worse prognosis in female patients undergoing endovascular therapy for PAD to improve risk stratification and preventing adverse events in this high‐risk population. Furthermore, disparities in outcomes among female patients and racial and ethnic minorities may not solely reflect individual biological or clinical risk but may stem from systemic inequities, including differences in access to care, underdiagnosis, or referral bias. For instance, racial and ethnic minorities are less likely to access high‐volume operators due to geographic and economic constraints. 43 Similarly, women may face gender‐specific barriers, such as underdiagnosis of PAD, leading to more severe disease at presentation. 44 These findings underscore the need for interventions to address systemic inequities in access to high‐quality PVI care.

Additionally, older age and comorbidities including hypertension, dyslipidemia, end‐stage renal disease, prior MI, or HF were associated with greater risk of MACEs. This is plausible considering the greater extent of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in this patient population. 45 Moreover, renal disease, in particular, impairs platelet function, increasing bleeding risks with antithrombotic therapy, while paradoxically raising the risk of thrombotic events like early graft failure or stent thrombosis. 46 Many of these risk factors explored previously are not modifiable at the time of PVI, including end‐stage renal disease on hemodialysis, age, congestive heart failure, prior coronary artery bypass grafting, and diabetes. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 However, these factors could serve as valuable tools for clinicians to use in pre‐PVI patient counseling and in making informed decisions regarding necessary post‐PVI follow‐up.

These findings have important implications. There is significant variability in performance of PVI by different specialties, with interventional cardiology, interventional radiology, and vascular surgery all performing PVI procedures nationwide. 51 However, training pathways are heterogeneous. The recent American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions guidelines recommended 100 peripheral angiograms and 50 peripheral interventions before independent practice, as well as other time‐based training competencies across all 3 specialties. 52 The present findings are concordant with the above recommendations and highlight the significance of individual operator volumes, potentially outweighing institutional volume in the context of these procedures. Our study findings indicate that operator volume may be a valuable surrogate to assess PVI quality across these centers.

There are notable limitations to this study. There were limited follow‐up data available, and it is not known if longer‐term out‐of‐hospital assessment of both MACEs and MALEs will alter our findings. Future studies should explore MALEs at different time points to provide a more comprehensive assessment of procedural quality. Additionally, clinically relevant outcomes such as access‐site complications, urinary retention, and acute kidney injury were not consistently available in the data set used for this study. Moreover, interpreting MALE rates in patients with chronic limb‐threatening ischemia may be challenging since a failed limb salvage procedure requiring amputation would also be classified as a MALE event on the basis of the coding system. Since the clinical end points were determined on the basis of coding within the registry rather than adjudicated by a commission, this could be a source of potential bias. Furthermore, the absolute differences in event rates across volume groups are small in our study. Although the data source used was the NCDR PVI registry, which consists of a heterogeneous group of centers across the United States, there was a predominance of White patients in our study population, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to more diverse populations. Furthermore, the voluntary nature of NCDR participation may contribute to selection bias, particularly in relation to centers serving more racially and socioeconomically diverse populations. There may also be differences between operator specialties and types of centers that participate in the registry and those that do not participate, which may have led to residual confounding and may limit generalizability. Additionally, the lack of detailed data regarding medication use and procedure types (stent, angioplasty, or hybrid) may contribute to residual confounding. Future analyses incorporating these variables are warranted to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their roles in the volume–outcome relationship. Finally, appropriateness of procedures per se was not evaluated, and therefore we could not determine what proportion of better outcomes seen with high‐volume operators could have been due to low risk but inappropriate cases. However, registries are a valuable contributor to real‐world evidence, and this article adds to the growing literature regarding the role of volume and its association with clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this analysis of >55 000 PVI procedures from the NCDR PVI registry, there was substantial variability in operator and hospital volume of PVI. After multivariable adjustment, the highest volume operators were associated with a lower incidence of in‐hospital MALEs and MACEs. However, there was no clear association between hospital volume of PVI and either MACEs or MALEs. Future studies are warranted, including those with long‐term outcome assessment, to evaluate operator and hospital volume as a potential surrogate to assess PVI appropriateness and quality to improve outcomes.
