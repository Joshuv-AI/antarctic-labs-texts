Characteristics of brain structural damage related to cerebral small vessel disease in general population with intracranial artery stenosis
Authors: Zi-Ang Pan, Zi-Yue Liu, Xing-Qi Pan, Fei-Fei Zhai, Ding-Ding Zhang, Ming Yao, Li-Xin Zhou, Jun Ni, Zheng-Yu Jin, Shu-Yang Zhang, Li-Ying Cui, Fei Han, Yi-Cheng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsCovert MRI markers of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) can coexist with large artery atherosclerosis. We aimed to explore whether the spatial distributions of these markers were diverse in people with or without intracranial artery stenosis (ICAS).MethodsThis cross-sectional analysis included 1206 stroke-free participants (aged 55.69±9.27, 62.94% female) with brain MRI and MR angiography from community-based Shunyi cohort. We analysed the relationships between ICAS and CSVD markers. We also compared the probability maps of lacunes, cerebral microbleeds (CMB), white matter hyperintensities (WMH) and cortex morphology at a voxel/vertex-wise level in groups with and without ICAS.ResultsICAS increased the risk of lacunes by 2.99-fold (95% CI 1.99 to 4.50, p<0.001), lacunes ≥3 by 5.32 times (95% CI 2.76 to 10.28, p<0.001), correlated with WMH volume (β=0.332, SE=0.059, p<0.001), WMH Fazekas scores ≥5 (OR 4.50, 95% CI 2.44 to 8.29, p<0.001) and brain parenchymal fraction (β=−0.012, SE=0.002, p<0.001), but not with CMB. ICAS is associated with lacunes in the corresponding blood supply area. Lacunes that coexist with ICAS were prone in basal ganglia, while the lacunes without ICAS appeared in centrum semiovale more often. WMH with ICAS was prone to present in deep white matter involving the bilateral pyramidal tracts and superior thalamic radiation. People with ICAS were susceptible to worse cortical atrophy of right superior frontal and left rostral anterior cingulate. No obvious distributional differences were found for CMB between the two groups.ConclusionsSince ICAS may be involved in the upstream pathogenesis of lacunes, white matter lesions and cortical atrophy, the impact of ICAS should not be ignored when evaluating MRI markers of CSVD.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Stroke, Magnetic Resonance Angiography, Arteries

Introduction

Covert cerebrovascular brain lesions, including lacunes, white matter hyperintensities (WMH), cerebral microbleeds (CMB) and brain atrophy, are highly prevalent in the ageing population. These structural brain changes, detected by MRI, are associated with cognitive decline, gait disturbances, loss of functional independence and an increased risk of future stroke and dementia.1 2

Although commonly regarded as MRI markers of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), these covert cerebrovascular brain lesions are also associated with large artery atherosclerosis. Previous population-based and hospital-based cross-sectional studies have reported the associations between intracranial large artery stenosis and the presence of CSVD markers.3,5 Large and small vessel pathologies frequently coexist, likely due to shared vascular risk factors. In addition, large vessel disease can cause ischaemic or haemorrhagic damage to the downstream brain parenchyma.6 7 We, therefore, hypothesise that the spatial distribution of covert cerebral lesions, including lacunes, WMH, CMB and brain atrophy, differs depending on the presence or absence of intracranial artery stenosis (ICAS).

To test this hypothesis, we conducted MRI and MR angiography (MRA) analyses in the Shunyi community-based cohort study. Our objective was to explore whether individuals with coexisting ICAS exhibit distinct distributional patterns of covert brain lesions, thereby improving our understanding of chronic brain injury related to pathology in both large and small cerebral vessels.

Patients and methods

Population

This cross-sectional investigation used data from the ongoing Shunyi community cohort study, which examines determinants of cardiovascular disorders and age-related pathologies.8 The research enrolled 1586 residents aged 35+ from five villages in Beijing’s suburban Shunyi district between June 2013 and April 2016. Participants underwent comprehensive initial evaluations including MRI screenings, with 1257 completing baseline neuroimaging. After excluding 4 subjects lacking complete MRI/MRA data and 47 individuals with prior stroke history, the final cohort comprised 1206 participants.

Brain MRI and ICAS evaluation

MRI was conducted on a single 3-T Siemens Skyra scanner (Siemens, Germany). Arterial stenosis was quantified through MRA, focusing on the most stenotic vascular segments.9 ICAS was defined as ≥1 stenosis with any degree affecting internal carotid artery (ICA), middle cerebral artery (MCA), anterior cerebral artery (ACA), intracranial segment of the vertebral artery (VA), basilar artery (BA) or posterior cerebral artery (PCA). This study did not include the extracranial carotid stenosis. In order to describe ICAS more specifically, we divided ICAS as: (1) anterior circulation ICAS, only had stenosis in intracranial segment of ICA, MCA or ACA; (2) posterior circulation ICAS, only had stenosis in intracranial segment of the VA, BA or PCA and (3) mixed ICAS, coexisting stenosis in anterior and posterior circulation large arteries mentioned above.

Evaluations of lacunes and CMB

The neuroimaging markers for CSVD imaging markers were characterised following established Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes in Neuroimaging.10 Specifically, lacunes were identified as focal fluid-filled cavities measuring 3–15 mm in the subcortical white matter, basal ganglia, brainstem or cerebellum. CMB manifested as small, round or ovoid hypointense lesions detectable on susceptibility-weighted imaging. In order to reflect lesion burden, we conduct quantitative stratified assessments by dividing lacunes and CMB as <3 lesions or ≥3 lesions. Certified neurologists, unaware of clinical information, independently evaluated these imaging markers. Intrarater consistency was verified through duplicate evaluations of 50 randomly selected cases with a minimum 30-day interval between assessments, yielding the kappa value of 0.95 for lacunes and 0.90 for CMB.

We manually labelled lacunes on T1WI and CMB on SWI using MRIcron (nitrc.org/projects/mricron). All identified lesions underwent normalisation to standard Montreal Neurologic Institute (MNI) 152 template through Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). Subsequently, summation of normalised lesions was performed to construct a lesion probability map (LPM) to reflect the spatial distribution of these lesions on account of the presence of ICAS.

Assessment of WMH

WMHs were initially evaluated by Fazekas grading scores and dichotomised WMH Fazekas scores of <5 or ≥5, then automated segmentation of WMH was performed using a voxel classification approach called brain intensity abnormality classification algorithm (BIANCA) that analyses relative intensity and spatial characteristics.11 12 Manual annotations were performed on fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences from 25 subjects to create the training dataset. The segmentation model incorporated both FLAIR and T1-weighted images through BIANCA’s machine learning framework. Probability maps generated by BIANCA underwent thresholding at 0.95 probability to create binary lesion masks, from which individual WMH volumes were calculated. Postprocessing quality checks with manual corrections were implemented when automated results required refinement. Algorithm performance was validated through leave-one-out cross-validation methodology.12 Our analysis demonstrated a mean Dice coefficient of 0.87 and an interclass correlation coefficient of 0.94. The detailed measurements of WMH were described in our former study.13

Spatial patterns of WMH were visualised through group-level probability mapping. The methodological pipeline involved aligning fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences to MNI 152 template using FSL’s computational tools, specifically using FMRIB's linear image registration tool (FLIRT) and nonlinear alignment tools (FNIRT/applywarp) for spatial normalisation. These transformation parameters were subsequently applied to native-space WMH masks through tri-linear interpolation approach, followed by binarisation at 0.5 threshold to maintain lesion volume fidelity. For population-level analysis, normalised WMH distributions were calculated by voxel-wise summation of individual lesion maps divided by cohort size, generating spatial probability indices reflecting regional WMH prevalence rates across subjects.13

Assessment of brain atrophy

Our previous article displayed the procedures of bias correction, quality control and tissue segmentation during the atrophy measurements.13 The Freesurfer computational pipeline (V.6.0) operated within native anatomical space, enabling precise differentiation of cerebral grey and white matter compartments for comprehensive whole-brain assessments.14 Cortical volume map at individual vertex points was generated through this analytical workflow. A comprehensive description of cortical morphology for further vertex-wise analysis was obtained. Brain atrophy was quantified through the brain parenchymal fraction (BPF), representing the proportional relationship between brain tissue volume (aggregate of grey and white matter) and the total intracranial volume.

Assessments of covariates

Hypertension diagnosis incorporated any of these criteria: documented medical history of hypertension, current antihypertensive therapy, systolic ≥140 mmHg or diastolic ≥90 mmHg. Diabetes mellitus identification required self-reported diagnosis, glucose-lowering medication use, fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c levels≥6.5%. Hyperlipaemia determination involved self-reported history, lipid-lowering drug usage or triglyceride ≥1.70 mmol/L, total cholesterol ≥5.20 mmol/L or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥3.4 mmol/L. Cardiac diseases including atrial fibrillation and coronary heart disease were diagnosed by experienced cardiologists according to self-reported history, clinical manifestation, medication, previous auxiliary examinations and EEG at baseline.

Statistical data analysis

Quantitative variables were expressed using appropriate measures of central tendency and dispersion: mean values with SD, medians with IQRs (P25–P75) or frequency counts with proportional percentage. Demographic comparisons between groups with and without ICAS were conducted through independent-samples t test for continuous measures or χ2 test for categorical variables. WMH volume underwent natural logarithmic transformation to achieve normal distribution. The following variables were considered in the multivariable model: age; sex; and conventional vascular disease risk factors, including body mass index, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipaemia, cardiac disease, serum creatinine and current smoking. Multivariable logistic and linear regression models were employed to examine relationships between ICAS and MRI markers of CSVD. Analytical models underwent sequential adjustments—initially for demographic factors (age and sex), followed by comprehensive incorporation of the aforementioned vascular risk elements. Statistical significance thresholds maintained a two-tailed p<0.05. All computational procedures were executed using SPSS statistical software package V.19.0 (IBM).

Statistical image analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted through FSL’s ‘randomise’ implementation, employing permutation testing (n=5000 iterations) within a generalised linear modelling (GLM) framework for non-parametric inference.15 Subject-level WMH maps were aggregated into four-dimensional datasets for voxel-based group comparisons of lesion distribution patterns. The analytical pipeline incorporated threshold-free cluster enhancement correction with significance set at p<0.05,11 16 while controlling for age, sex and vascular disease risk factors. In order to reduce the impact of WMH burden on its distribution, we also adjusted the WMH volume during the comparison.

A vertex-level exploratory analysis of the entire cortex was performed using FreeSurfer’s mri_glmfit tool to examine cortical volume differences between ICAS and non-ICAS groups. Model 1 was univariate. Model 2 incorporated demographic adjustments for age and sex, while model 3 further included adjustments for vascular risk factors. Cluster-level significance was established through permutation testing (5000 iterations) with a cluster-defining vertex threshold of p<0.001 and family-wise error correction at p<0.05.13 We have uploaded all the code to GitHub. The website address is https://github.com/anniepan-pumc/TBSS-ICAS, which is convenient for viewing and verifying.

Individuals with ICAS displayed significantly older and less percentage of women, heightened vascular disease risk profiles and more pronounced CSVD marker manifestations compared with people without ICAS.

Relationship between ICAS and CSVD MRI markers

As presented in table 2 and online supplemental table 1, ICAS was associated with all the CSVD markers in the univariate regression. But after the adjustment of age and sex, ICAS was only significantly related to lacunes present (OR 3.64, 95% CI 2.47 to 5.35, p<0.001), lacunes ≥3 (OR 5.81, 95% CI 3.13 to 10.78, p<0.001), WMH volume (β=0.358, SE=0.057, p<0.001), WMH Fazekas scores ≥5 (OR 3.92, 95% CI 2.24 to 6.85, p<0.001) and BPF (β=−0.012, SE=0.002, p<0.001). The significance of the association was maintained after adjusting for vascular disease risk factors. ICAS could still increase the risk of lacunes present by 2.99 times (95% CI 1.99 to 4.50, p<0.001), lacunes ≥3 by 5.32 times (95% CI 2.76 to 10.28, p<0.001) and also correlated with WMH volume (β=0.332, SE=0.059, p<0.001), WMH Fazekas scores ≥5 (OR 4.50, 95% CI 2.44 to 8.29, p<0.001) and BPF (β=−0.012, SE=0.002, p<0.001). However, ICAS did not significantly correlate with CMB present or CMB ≥3 after adjusting for age, sex or other vascular disease risk factors. We also analysed the associations between ICAS ≥50% and CSVD-related imaging markers. The results are similar with any degree of stenosis (online supplemental table 2). In order to demonstrate the impact of ICAS on lacunes, we further analysed the associations between ICAS and lacunes in different locations (online supplemental table 3). It shows that ICAS is associated with lacunes in the corresponding blood supply area.

Discussion

In this community-based cross-sectional study, we reached ICAS burden significantly related to ischaemic markers such as lacunes and WMH, but not CMB. Our findings are consistent with a previous study based on 225 Chinese stroke-free participants.17 Similar results can also be observed in the studies of carotid stenosis in the general population. The 3C-Dijon Study found carotid stenosis was positively correlated with lacunes and severe WMH grade.6 Taizhou study also found carotid stenosis was significantly related to WMH but not with CMB.18 We noticed Lishui study showed ICAS was correlated to CMB; it may be because this study included participants older and did not exclude people with previous stroke, who shared more vascular risk factors with ICAS.19 Further longitudinal research is needed to provide additional evidence.

Atherosclerosis of the upstream large artery is prone to cause ischaemia or infarction of the downstream brain parenchyma due to embolism and hypoperfusion. Therefore, the lacunes and WMH are not entirely the imaging changes of small vessel disease. However, ICAS does not lead to more haemorrhagic brain structural damage downstream. Because CMB is trace bleeding caused by arteriolosclerosis, microaneurysms, fibrinoid necrosis or amyloid angiopathy of small vessels, it is more likely to represent pure small vessel injury.20

Although both the deep and supratentorial lacunes are widely regarded as imaging markers of CSVD, the distribution of lacunes may reveal different pathogenesis. Lacunes that coexist with ICAS often occur in basal ganglia, which is the area of primary perforators from MCA. The mechanism may be secondary to obstruction of perforating arteries. Atheromatous branch occlusion or artery-to-artery embolism is likely to be a common cause for ICAS-related small infarction. On the contrary, lacunes without ICAS may indicate pure arteriolosclerosis, generally located in the supratentorial area, such as centrum semiovale, where the junctional region between primary perforators and cortical branches is located and where peripheral small vessels are located.2 21 Our results emphasise the need for a detailed classification of lacunes. WMH distribution between people with and without ICAS was also obvious. Previous study showed ischaemic stroke patients with ICAS had significantly higher total WMH burden, especially the deep WMH.22 Similarly, in our voxel-wise statistics, WMHs of participants with ICAS were more extensive to the deep white matter. ICAS may impair cerebrovascular reactivity and cause cerebral haemodynamic changes,2 21 increased intracranial vascular pulsation due to stenosis may also be related to WMH in the asymptomatic general population.23 Because the junctional region between primary perforators and cortical branches is more sensitive to hypoperfusion compared with periventricular region, ICAS-related WMH is prone to appear in deep white matter. This area involves the bilateral primary motor and sensory tracts. Hence, WMH coexisting with ICAS is prone to result in movement deficit and cognitive impairment.24

We drew accordant results in brain atrophy with former studies focused on extracranial artery stenosis. The Framingham Offspring Study found carotid stenosis was associated with total brain atrophy.25 SMART-MR study found carotid stenosis was associated with total brain and cortical grey matter atrophy.26 They even discovered the relationship between carotid stenosis and ipsilateral brain atrophy was more profound than contralateral hemisphere.27 The detailed mechanism remains unclear. Large artery stenosis may lead to lessened cerebral blood flow that, if not adequately compensated for by collateral circulation, the perfusion pressure distal to the lesion begins to decline, which will trigger regional brain microcirculatory disturbances that can evolve into a neurodegenerative process, eventually leading to brain atrophy.28 The cortical grey matter is especially vulnerable to hypoperfusion, on account of the higher oxygen and glucose requirements for grey matter than white matter.29 The results we got may verify the former hypothesis. In addition, we found cortical atrophy in superior frontal and rostral anterior cingulate that was pronounced when coexisting with ICAS, close to projection region of ICAS susceptible white matter lesion area. It may suggest the cortical projection area damage secondary to the hypoperfusion of subcortical junctional region.30 31

This investigation boasts several methodological advantages, including an extensive participant cohort and an epidemiological cohort design incorporating rigorously collected clinical and neuroimaging datasets. The implementation of quantitative CSVD assessment techniques through automated segmentation complemented by expert manual verification enabled detailed spatial characterisation of cerebrovascular lesions while minimising potential information bias. Furthermore, our analytical approach incorporated spatial distribution analyses of CSVD markers in ICAS patients, using expert-annotated lacunes and CMB alongside voxel-wise WMH mapping and cortical vertex-based morphological assessments, which visually demonstrated the effect of ICAS on CSVD markers.

This study presents certain limitations. Primarily, incomplete neuroimaging data among participants might introduce selection bias, as they exhibited worse status of both large and small vessels. Second, the inability of MRA to directly visualise arterial wall morphology suggests that lumen-based assessments could potentially lead to underestimation of atherosclerotic pathology. But MRA is still practical and appropriate for asymptomatic ICAS screening in large community populations.

In conclusion, the pattern of small vessel-related brain structural damage was different when coexisting ICAS or not. ICAS is related closely to ischaemic covert lesions and may involve the upstream pathogenesis of lacunes, aggravate age-related white matter degeneration and influence certain cortical morphology. The impact of ICAS on CSVD-related structural damage should not be overlooked.
