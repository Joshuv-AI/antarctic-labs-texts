Role of sRAGE in clinical heterogeneity and inflammation in endometriosis patients undergoing IVF; case-control study
Authors: Neda Emami, Ashraf Moini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndometriosis is a multifactorial, chronic inflammatory disorder that affects a substantial proportion of women during their reproductive years and is frequently associated with pelvic pain and infertility. Accumulating evidence suggests that advanced glycation end products (AGEs), generated through dietary exposure and oxidative stress, may contribute to disease progression by activating the receptor for AGEs (RAGE), thereby promoting inflammatory and fibrotic signaling pathways. Rather than serving as an initiating factor, the AGE–RAGE axis is increasingly recognized as an amplifier of persistent inflammation and lesion persistence in endometriosis. The soluble isoform of RAGE (sRAGE) acts as a circulating decoy receptor that sequesters AGEs and attenuates downstream pro-inflammatory cascades. Although dysregulated sRAGE concentrations have been documented in various metabolic and reproductive conditions, its specific role in endometriosis—particularly within the follicular microenvironment and in relation to inflammatory status—remains incompletely characterized.MethodsIn this prospective case–control study, 71 women of reproductive age undergoing controlled ovarian stimulation and oocyte retrieval for in vitro fertilization (IVF) were enrolled. The case group included 40 women with confirmed endometriosis, while 31 women without endometriosis served as controls. Detailed clinical histories were obtained for both groups. Serum and follicular fluid samples were collected, and sRAGE concentrations were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).ResultsNo significant differences were observed between groups regarding age or body mass index. Both serum and follicular fluid sRAGE concentrations were significantly elevated in women with endometriosis compared with controls (both P < 0.001). Evaluation of clinical heterogeneity demonstrated significantly lower follicular fluid sRAGE levels among endometriosis women with concomitant allergic conditionsnull (P = 0.012). Within the endometriosis cohort, sRAGE concentrations did not vary significantly according to pain severity, including dysmenorrhea, pelvic pain, or dyspareunia. Likewise, no significant associations were identified with thyroid disorders, gastrointestinal disease, adenomyosis, migraine, asthma, diabetes, or multiple sclerosis. Furthermore, sRAGE levels were not correlated with the number of retrieved oocytes or embryos in either group.ConclusionWomen with endometriosis exhibited significantly elevated serum and follicular fluid sRAGE levels, suggesting altered AGE–RAGE axis activity. However, the absence of association with ovarian response indicates that sRAGE may reflect inflammatory status rather than reproductive outcomes.

Introduction

Endometriosis is a prevalent gynecological disorder associated with chronic pelvic pain and infertility, significantly impairing quality of life. Although its exact pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, evolving evidence has shifted the perception of endometriosis from a localized pelvic condition to a complex, systemic inflammatory disease. Emerging data highlight its interplay with immune dysregulation and its associations with autoimmune and neurological disorders, findings that are increasingly influencing clinical management strategies [1]. Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) preferentially accumulate under metabolic conditions frequently observed in endometriosis, including insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and chronic low-grade inflammation. These bioactive molecules contribute to pathophysiological processes that parallel the metabolic and inflammatory complications increasingly reported in affected women [2]. Accumulating evidence implies advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in ovarian dysfunction. AGE deposition within ovarian tissue may impair folliculogenesis and steroidogenesis, partly through oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways in granulosa cells. These alterations may ultimately compromise oocyte competence and reproductive potential [3]. Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) arise from a major post-translational modification process involving the non-enzymatic glycation of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. This biochemical reaction, driven by persistent exposure to reducing sugars, leads to the formation of structurally stable and biologically active compounds capable of altering cellular function [3]. Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) can either induce the formation of cross-links between critical molecules in the extracellular matrix (ECM) basement membrane or exert their effects via receptor-mediated pathways. Among these, the multi-ligand transmembrane receptor RAGE (receptor for advanced glycation end products) has been identified as a key mediator of inflammation. RAGE is expressed in diverse cell types, including endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and ovarian tissue. Its activation triggers pro-inflammatory signaling, cellular stress, and cytotoxicity primarily through the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway. In RAGE-expressing cells, AGE–RAGE interactions stimulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation via NADPH oxidase activation, highlighting the central role of oxidative stress in mediating the deleterious effects of RAGE activation [4]. Multiple studies have demonstrated RAGE expression in tissues and cell types relevant to endometriosis [4]. RAGE expression has been demonstrated in utopic endometrium and in ectopic endometriotic lesions, including ovarian implants, with additional localization in vascular endothelial cells and macrophages, highlighting its role in the inflammatory milieu. A soluble isoform, sRAGE, lacks transmembrane and intracellular domains and acts as a decoy receptor, binding AGEs and mitigating downstream pro-inflammatory signaling [5]. Chronic exposure to advanced glycation end products (AGEs) throughout reproductive life may induce cumulative oxidative injury within ovarian follicles by disrupting the follicular microenvironment. Such alterations can impair granulosa cell metabolism and antioxidant capacity, compromise follicular vascularization, and promote local hypoxia. Collectively, these mechanisms may hinder follicular maturation and contribute to age-related deterioration in oocyte quality [6]. Furthermore, follicular fluid sRAGE concentrations have been reported to be higher in younger women compared with those of advanced reproductive age undergoing IVF, suggesting a potential age-related decline in its protective capacity within the follicular microenvironment [7]. Given the potential protective properties of sRAGE against AGE-mediated damage, its association with biomarkers across various pathological conditions has been increasingly explored to elucidate its relevance in reproductive function and inflammatory regulation. However, limited evidence exists regarding the relationship between sRAGE concentrations and the clinical heterogeneity and inflammatory profile of endometriosis-associated infertility. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess serum and follicular fluid sRAGE levels as markers of oxidative stress in women with endometriosis undergoing assisted reproductive treatment.

Method and material

A total of 71 women of reproductive age were enrolled in this case-control study, conducted at Erfan and Arash Hospitals in Tehran, Iran, between September 2024 and November 2025, to investigate the association of follicular fluid (FF) and serum soluble receptor for advanced glycation end products (sRAGE) concentrations with endometriosis.

The case group comprised 40 women diagnosed with endometriosis, whereas the control group included 31 women. All women in the control group had normal gynecological findings, no clinical or imaging evidence of endometriosis, no history of diabetes, thyroid disorders, chronic inflammatory or immune diseases, dysmenorrhea, or chronic pelvic pain. All controls underwent IVF because of male-factor infertility.

whose partners had infertility. Endometriosis diagnosis was established based on clinical symptoms—including worsening premenstrual pain, pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, and pain during ovulation—alongside MRI findings, elevated CA-125 levels, and/or laparoscopic confirmation. All participants in the case group fulfilled the World Endometriosis Society Consensus criteria [8]. Prior to sample collection, comprehensive demographic and clinical data were obtained from all participants, including age, body weight, history of allergies, immune system disorders, cancer, thyroid disease, gastrointestinal and dermatological conditions, as well as detailed reproductive and gynecological symptom history (e.g., premenstrual pain, pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, and ovulation-associated pain).

Experimental sample collection and determination

Both hospitals followed standardized protocols for pituitary desensitization in both the case and control groups, using gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonists. The protocols were flexible, allowing for subcutaneous administration of gonadotropins (GONAL-F 150 IU/day, Merck, Germany) from the third to fifth day of the menstrual cycle to stimulate follicular development. Follicular growth was monitored via transvaginal ultrasonography after 5–7 days. When at least two follicles reached a mean diameter of 15–17 mm, two injections of recombinant human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) (OVITRELLE 6500 IU/day, Merck, Germany) were administered. Oocyte retrieval was performed 34–36 h later under transvaginal ultrasound guidance. Follicular fluid (FF) was collected from the first large follicle aspirated, ensuring it was free of blood, and centrifuged at 15,000 g for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was immediately stored at -80 °C until further analysis. For the quantification of FF and serum sRAGE concentrations, commercially available ELISA kits (ZELLBIO ZB-10027 C-H9648, ZELLBIO GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) were employed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD or frequency (%). Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. For normally distributed continuous variables, differences between two groups were analyzed using the independent sample t-test, and differences among more than two groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For non-normally distributed variables, differences between two groups were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test, and differences among more than two groups were examined using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In the current study, women with endometriosis undergoing assisted reproductive treatment had significantly higher serum and follicular fluid sRAGE concentrations compared with control subjects. Additionally, women with endometriosis and concomitant allergic conditions exhibited significantly lower follicular fluid sRAGE concentrations (P = 0.012).

This elevation implicates altered activity of the AGE–RAGE signaling axis and may reflect increased oxidative stress and chronic inflammation in women with endometriosis. While previous investigations have linked oxidative stress to endometriosis (suggesting increased reactive oxygen species and decreased antioxidant capacity) [9], our results specifically identify sRAGE as a measurable marker within systemic circulation and follicular microenvironments, further implicating AGE–RAGE dysregulation in reproductive pathology.

In endometriosis, localized AGE accumulation—driven by iron overload from hemorrhagic lesions and exacerbated oxidative stress—may intensify inflammation in the peritoneal and follicular environments, promoting a chronic inflammatory loop that is partially counterbalanced by increased soluble decoy receptor (sRAGE) expression [10].

Despite the elevated sRAGE levels observed, we did not detect significant associations between sRAGE concentrations and ovarian response parameters such as the number of retrieved oocytes or embryos. This suggests that while sRAGE may reflect inflammatory activity, it does not directly influence the functional aspects of ovarian responsiveness in assisted reproductive treatment (ART). Although some studies in non-endometriosis populations have reported correlations between follicular fluid sRAGE and markers of ovarian reserve or gonadotropin requirements, implying a potential protective or modulatory role of sRAGE in follicular health, these associations appear condition-specific and were not recapitulated in our cohort [11].

Further supporting a compartment-specific mechanism, recent mechanistic mapping demonstrates a pattern of AGE–RAGE activation that is particularly pronounced in ectopic endometriotic tissues and peritoneal fluid, with elevated RAGE ligands such as HMGB1 and S100 family proteins contributing to fibrosis and inflammatory amplification. In contrast, systemic elevations in sRAGE may represent a downstream or compensatory response to localized inflammatory signaling rather than a direct driver of reproductive dysfunction [9].

Interestingly, we observed lower follicular fluid sRAGE in women with concomitant allergic conditions, which may point to complex immunomodulatory interactions between systemic atopic inflammation and local ovarian microenvironments. While the literature on sRAGE modulation in allergic disease is limited, dysregulated immune responses in allergic conditions could feasibly alter decoy receptor production or ligand binding dynamics, influencing sRAGE availability in follicular fluid. Additional research is needed to elucidate these interactions and their implications for reproductive outcomes [12]. In contrast to specific inflammation biomarkers in other contexts—such as VEGF, TNF-α, IL-6, and CRP, which have shown robust correlations with follicular dysfunction in (Polycystic Ovary Syndrome) PCOS and other reproductive disorders—sRAGE displayed no significant association with clinical parameters such as pain severity or systemic comorbidities in our endometriosis cohort. This reinforces the notion that sRAGE primarily reflects an underlying chronic inflammatory milieu rather than acute symptom severity or comorbidity profiles [13]. The disconnect between biomarker levels and clinical phenotypes has been reported in other chronic inflammatory conditions, emphasizing the complexity of translating molecular markers into straightforward clinical correlations [14].

The lack of association between sRAGE levels and pain severity is consistent with evidence indicating that symptom intensity in endometriosis does not reliably correlate with inflammatory burden. Pain generation in endometriosis involves neuroangiogenesis, peripheral nerve fiber infiltration, and central sensitization rather than purely inflammatory activity [15]. Although endometriosis is frequently associated with autoimmune, endocrine, and systemic disorders, molecular biomarkers do not consistently mirror comorbidity profiles. Clinical heterogeneity in endometriosis reflects complex immune and neuroendocrine interactions that may not be captured by single circulating mediators [16]. The divergence in sRAGE behavior across reproductive conditions underscores the complex role of the AGE–RAGE axis in reproductive biology. For example, follicular fluid sRAGE has been reported to be lower in women with PCOS compared to controls, where it was inversely correlated with inflammatory markers including TNF-α and IL-6, suggesting a protective anti-inflammatory role of sRAGE in that context [17]. In contrast, elevated sRAGE in endometriosis may represent a reactive compensation to high AGE burden and persistent inflammation rather than a protective mechanism associated with favorable ovarian response. Oxidative stress itself is a well-established feature of endometriosis pathophysiology, with studies demonstrating increased reactive oxygen species, impaired antioxidant defenses, and granulosa cell stress contributing to impaired oocyte development and follicular dysfunction [18]. These stressors likely interact with AGE–RAGE dynamics, amplifying local inflammation and possibly promoting cellular senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction within granulosa cells, further complicating the reproductive environment in endometriosis.). We acknowledge that circulating AGEs, inflammatory cytokines including CRP, IL-6 and TNF-α, VEGF, and oxidative stress biomarkers were not measured in the present study. Therefore, although elevated sRAGE concentrations suggest alterations in one component of the AGE–RAGE axis, the underlying mechanisms cannot be fully elucidated. Future studies incorporating AGEs, inflammatory mediators, and oxidative stress markers are required to better characterize the biological significance of sRAGE in endometriosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, women with endometriosis exhibited significantly higher serum and follicular fluid sRAGE concentrations than controls, suggesting altered AGE–RAGE axis activity within the inflammatory and oxidative stress milieu characteristic of the disease. However, because circulating AGEs, inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress biomarkers were not measured, and the study design was observational, no causal or mechanistic conclusions can be established. Furthermore, sRAGE concentrations were not associated with ovarian response parameters, indicating that sRAGE may primarily reflect the inflammatory microenvironment rather than ovarian performance. Future prospective studies incorporating comprehensive AGE–RAGE pathway markers, inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress biomarkers, and reproductive outcomes are warranted to further clarify the biological and clinical significance of sRAGE in endometriosis-associated infertility.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the moderate sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle subgroup differences. In addition, specific AGE subtypes (e.g., pentosidine and carboxymethyl-lysine), inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress biomarkers were not quantified, restricting mechanistic interpretation of the observed associations. Finally, more comprehensive reproductive outcomes, including pregnancy and live birth rates, were not evaluated. Future longitudinal studies assessing dynamic changes in AGE–RAGE pathway components throughout ART cycles and their relationship with reproductive outcomes are needed to establish their temporal and predictive significance.
