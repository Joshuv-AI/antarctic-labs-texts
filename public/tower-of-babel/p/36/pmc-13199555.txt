Clinicopathological and molecular characteristics of small cell carcinoma of the ovary, hypercalcemic type: three case reports
Authors: Weiyang Jiang, Shijie Li, Yi Peng, Jue Xiao, Ding Wang, Chunyan Chen, Yan Zhang, Chong Zhang, Chunling Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall cell carcinoma of the ovary, hypercalcemic type (SCCOHT), is a rare and aggressive ovarian cancer that affects populations ranging from children to young adults. It is usually associated with germline and somatic variants of SMARCA4, which encodes the SMARCA4 protein (BRG1), as well as SMARCA2 (BRM) deficiency, both subunits of the switch/sucrose-nonfermenting (SWI/SNF) chromatin remodeling complex. Here, we report the clinical, morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular characteristics of three cases. All three patients showed abnormally elevated preoperative CA-125 levels and hypercalcemia during disease progression. Two patients were initially misdiagnosed with adult granulosa cell tumor (AGCT) in local hospitals. These tumors typically exhibit a diffuse sheet growth pattern characterized by small cells with follicle-like spaces and contain variable numbers of larger cells with a rhabdoid appearance. Immunohistochemistry reveals an absence of SMARCA4 (BRG1) and SMARCA2 (BRM) in these tumors, along with diffuse expression of WT1-N terminus, vimentin, and variable expression of keratins and EMA. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified SMARCA4 mutations. Integrated clinical, morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analyses enhance diagnostic accuracy, despite the condition often being misdiagnosed.Keywords: Small cell carcinoma of the ovary, Hypercalcemic type; SMARCA4; SMARCA2; SWI/SNF complex; Genomic analysis

Introduction

Small cell carcinoma of the ovary, hypercalcemic type (SCCOHT), is a rare and aggressive ovarian cancer that predominantly affects patients from childhood to young adulthood [1–2]. It accounts for < 0.01% of all ovarian cancers, with fewer than 500 cases documented to date [1]. Patients usually present with non-specific symptoms such as abdominal pain and distension related to a pelvic mass [1–2]. Hypercalcemia and elevated CA-125 are common laboratory findings [1–2].

In almost all cases, SCCOHT is associated with somatic and germline pathogenic variants in SMARCA4, which encodes the SMARCA4 protein (BRG1), a catalytic subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex [3–7]. Loss of SMARCA4 function leads to dysregulation of chromatin remodeling and transcriptional control, thereby contributing to tumorigenesis .

Due to its rarity, the lack of awareness among pathologists about the disease, insufficient availability of immunohistochemistry and molecular pathology diagnosis methods in primary hospitals, and overlapping histological features with other small round cell tumors, the misdiagnosis rate remains high. In this report, we describe three cases of SCCOHT, including clinical, morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analyses, as well as treatment. This report aims to enhance diagnostic accuracy and raise awareness of SCCOHT, which may contribute to the early diagnosis of this rare and aggressive disease.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Chongqing University Cancer Hospital. Three patients were identified from surgical pathology files between January 2019 and December 2024. We searched for cases of ovarian cancers with “SCCOHT”, “granulosa cell tumor (GCT)”, “small cell carcinoma of the ovary, pulmonary type (SCCOPT)” and “undifferentiated ovarian carcinoma (UDOC)”. One case suspected of SCCOHT was excluded due to the inability to proceed with immunohistochemistry and genetic testing for confirmation. Two senior pathologists independently reviewed these patient cases to ensure diagnostic consensus. Clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records.

All hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) slides were reviewed. Four-micrometer formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were subjected to IHC using the following antibodies: ARID1A (BAF250A) (clone: EPR13501), Calretinin (clone: MX027), CD10 (clone: MX002), CD34 (clone: MX123, ), CD45 (clone: MX145), CD56 (clone: MX039), CD99 (clone: MX111), CD117 (clone: YR145), CEA (clone: MX068), CgA (clone: MX018), CK5/6 (clone: MX040), CK7 (clone: MX053), CK8/18 (clone: MX004 + MX035), CK19 (clone: MX054), CK20 (clone: MX059), CK (CAM5.2) (clone: CAM5.2), CK (LMW) (clone: 34βH11), CK (Pan) (clone: MX005), D2-40 (clone: D2-40), Desmin (clone: MX046), DOG1 (clone: MX067), EMA (clone: MX132), ER (clone: SP1), FLI-1 (clone: G146-22), HER-2 (clone: MXR001), HMB45 (clone: HMB45), HNF1β (clone: MX092), Inhibin-α (clone: MX098), Ki-67 (clone: MX006), MelanA (clone: MX118), MelanA (clone: MX118), Ep-CAM (clone: MX066), MyoD1 (clone: MX049), Myogenin (clone: MX078), NapsinA (clone: MX015), Nestin (clone: 10C2), Oct3/4 (clone: MX084), p16 (clone: MX007), p53 (clone: MX008), Pax-8 (clone: MXR013), PLAP (clone: MX112), PR (clone: SP2), PTH (clone: MRQ-31),S100 (clone: MXR034), SALL4 (clone: MX128), SF-1 (clone: EP434), SMA (clone: /), SMARCA2 (BRM) (clone: EPR23103-44), SMARCA4 (BRG1)clone: (JH56), SMARCB1 (INI1) (clone: MRQ27), SOX-10 (clone: MXR026), Syn (clone: MX038), TLE1 (clone: IF15), TTF1 (clone: MX011), Vimemtin (clone: MX034), WT1-N terminus (clone: MX012).Antibodies were visualized using the EnVision Plus detection system (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). Clinical findings

Case 1

A 19-year-old female was admitted to a local hospital with abdominal pain for 20 days in 2018. MRI revealed a large solid‒cystic mass in the abdomino-pelvic region. A laboratory test revealed an elevated CA-125 level of 205 U/mL. She underwent exploratory laparotomy, which included right adnexectomy, left ovarian biopsy, appendectomy, omentectomy, and pelvic lymph node dissection. Intraoperatively, a 20 × 20 cm right ovarian solid-cystic mass with spontaneous rupture was identified. The patient was diagnosed with AGCT and classified as FIGO stage IC. The patient subsequently received six cycles of TC (paclitaxel /carboplatin). Follow-up CA125 and MRI indicated complete remission. Subsequently, CA125 and color Doppler ultrasound were checked every three months without abnormalities. Twelve months post-operation, the patient was transferred to our hospital due to disease recurrence. MRI (Fig. 1A) and PET-CT (Fig. 1B) revealed multiple solid‒cystic masses in the retroperitoneal and abdominal‒pelvic cavities. Laboratory tests revealed hypercalcemia (2.99 mmol/l, normal range: 2.11–2.52 mmol/L), and her CA125 level was elevated to 107.4 U/mL (normal range: 0–30.20 U/mL). The patient underwent cytoreductive surgery, which included hysterectomy, left adnexectomy, resection of a right-sided peritoneal mass, and a colorectal surface mass. Surgical tumor reduction was incomplete. The final diagnosis was SCCOHT. Unfortunately, the patient died 13 months after the initial diagnosis.

Case 2

A 38-year-old female who presented with a palpable abdominal mass for fifteen days in 2021 was admitted to a local hospital. Physical examination revealed an abdominal mass six years prior. Her father died of lung cancer. She underwent exploratory laparoscopy, which included right adnexectomy, left ovarian endometrioma cystectomy, myomectomy, and biopsies of the right iliac lymph nodes. Intraoperative findings revealed a 16 × 12 cm mass replacing the right ovary. The tumor was solid-cystic, and the solid areas had a lobulated appearance. The patient was diagnosed with AGCT and classified as FIGO stage IIA. She underwent hysterectomy, left adnexectomy, and omentectomy one week later, followed by three cycles of TC (paclitaxel 175 mg/m2 day1, carboplatin AUC5 day1). No follow-up was performed after chemotherapy.The tumor recurred fourteen months after the operation. The patient subsequently underwent cytoreductive surgery, which involved resection of an abdominopelvic mass and an abdominal wall mass. Following this, she was transferred to our hospital. A pathology consultation confirmed the diagnosis of SCCOHT. MRI (Fig. 3A) and PET-CT (Fig. 3B) revealed multiple pelvic-abdominal and retroperitoneal masses. Laboratory tests revealed hypercalcemia (3.48 mmol/mL), and her CA125 level was elevated to 72.80 U/mL. The patient subsequently completed eight cycles of BEP + Bev (etoposide 100mg/m2 day1-5, cisplatin 20mg/m2 day1-5, bleomycin 15 mg day1, 3, and 5, and bevacizumab 7.5 mg/kg day1). After the sixth cycle of treatment, MRI (Fig. 3C) showed a partial response to the BEP + Bev. However, follow-up MRI in three months and six months later (Fig. 3D) show disease progression, and the patient was lost to follow-up twenty-eight months after the initial diagnosis.

Case3

A 16-year-old female was admitted to a local hospital with abdominal pain and a pelvic mass for one month in 2024. CT (Fig. 5A) revealed an abdomino-pelvic mass measuring 11.3 × 11.6 × 10.3 cm, which appeared to arise from the right adnexa. She underwent surgical resection of the right adnexa. Grossly, the right ovary was replaced by a solid nodular mass measuring 18 × 15 × 6 cm. The outer surface was bosselated with a few areas of hemorrhage. The cut surface was solid and gray to white, with a few areas of hemorrhage and necrosis. The patient was diagnosed with SCCOHT and classified as FIGO stage IIA. After one month, the patient was transferred to our hospital. Laboratory tests revealed mild hypercalcemia (2.53 mmol/l), and her CA125 level was elevated to 137.4 U/mL. A follow-up CT (Fig. 5B) revealed a 2.2 × 1.9 cm retroperitoneal soft tissue nodule adjacent to the right abdominal aorta. The patient then underwent radical surgery for ovarian cancer, which included hysterectomy, left adnexectomy, appendectomy, omentectomy, abdominectomy, pelvic and para-aortic lymph node dissection, and excision of an intestinal wall nodule. Final pathology revealed SCCOHT, which was classified as FIGO stage IIIA1 owing to the presence of metastatic tumors in one of seven right pelvic lymph nodes and both para-aortic lymph nodes. Postoperatively, the serum calcium and CA125 levels returned to normal. The patient completed three cycles of EP (etoposide 75 mg/m2 days 1–3, cisplatinmg 20 mg/m2 day1-3), and there was no evidence of tumor recurrence ; however, she was lost to follow-up three months after the operation.

Discussion

SCCOHT is an uncommon and aggressive ovarian cancer with an unfavorable prognosis despite the application of aggressive multimodal therapy. Robert E. Scully first reported the pathological and clinical aspects [8–9]. About two-thirds of patients present with elevated serum calcium [2, 8]. The exact mechanism behind hypercalcemia is not well understood but may be attributed to the secretion of parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP) by tumor cells [2, 10–11]. Occasionally, elevated CA-125 levels are also observed. Both hypercalcemia and CA-125 can return to normal after complete tumor removal, suggesting their potential utility as clinical indicators for SCCOHT [12]. The histogenesis of SCCOHT has been debated for the past three decades, with theories postulating epithelial [2, 9, 13–14], sex-cord stromal, or germ cell origins [3, 15–16], but none have been confirmed. In the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, SCCOHT is listed under the miscellaneous category of ovarian neoplasms [17].

In 2013, a study demonstrated absent SMARCA4 (BRG1) staining and the presence of SMARCA4 pathogenic variants (PVs) in two cases of SCCOHT [3]. The following year, several research groups published studies confirming that SCCOHT is associated with both somatic and germline loss-of-function PVs of SMARCA4, leading to absent SMARCA4 (BRG1) staining in patient tumor tissue [4–7]. Some SCCOHT cases do not harbor SMARCA4 alterations but exhibit absent staining and/or molecular abnormalities in SMARCB1 (INI1) or ARID1A and ARID1B, which are components of the SWI/SNF complex [5–6, 18–21].

Besides, in SCCOHT, SMARCA4 mutations are not ‘hot-spot’ and can occur anywhere along the whole gene (ENST00000344626, 5392 bp) with different mutation types including frameshift, stop/nonsense, splice-site, missense and in-frame deletions [12, 21].

SCCOHT is most frequently diagnosed in childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood [1–2, 22], with the youngest reported case being 12 months old [23]. Compared with non-carriers, carriers of germline SMARCA4 mutations tend to present at a younger age [24]. Patients often exhibit non-specific symptoms such as pelvic mass-related abdominal pain and distension [1–2, 17].

Unilateral tumors, especially in the right ovary, are quite common [1–2]. Familial cases are more often bilateral [25–26]. Spread beyond the ovary is also common. Tumors are generally large, ranging from 3.6 to 30 cm in maximum diameter. Macroscopically [2, 12], they are usually solid, cream-colored or white-gray neoplasms with cystic foci, while a minority are predominantly cystic. Necrosis and hemorrhage are frequently observed.

Histologically [2, 12], almost all tumors show a predominantly diffuse growth pattern, interrupted by follicle-like spaces, which usually contain eosinophilic fluid but may also be empty or filled with basophilic fluid. Tumor cells grow in clusters, cords, nests, or singly. Large cells are present in varying proportions in about 50% of tumors and are classified as “small cell carcinoma, large cell variant” when large cells predominate. These large cells have abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, prominent nucleoli, and eccentric nuclei, sometimes imparting a rhabdoid appearance. About 15% of tumors contain a minor mucinous component, which may consist of benign cysts or glands; in rare cases, malignant mucinous features with signet-ring-like cells are observed. About 12% of cases display small mucinous cysts or glandular foci lined with mature epithelium, atypical mucinous cells, or signet-ring cells. The stroma is generally scant, but edematous or myxoid stromal areas are more commonly seen in large regions.

Tumor cells exhibited diffuse expression of WT1-N terminus, and negative expression in individual cases [20], and variable expression of p53 (wild-type pattern or mutation type), p16, EMA, and keratin markers (particularly in the large cell component), as well as neuroendocrine markers (Syn, CD56), CD10, calretinin, and in some cases, parathyroid Hormone (PTH). Staining was consistently negative for CgA, desmin, S100, inhibin, and TTF-1 [12, 27–29]. Loss of Pax-8, ER, and PR expression may also be a feature of SCCOHT. Although ER and PR expression have not been widely investigated in SCCOHT, these neoplasms invariably do not stain using antibodies directed against hormone receptors [22].

All three cases in this study were WT1-N terminus positive, p53 showed a wild-type pattern, and they exhibited varying degrees of epithelial marker and neuroendocrine (Syn, CD56) positivity. PTH, CgA and markers indicating ovarian cancer tissue (Pax − 8, ER, and PR) were consistently negative.

Immunohistochemical loss of SMARCA4 (BRG1) protein expression secondary to SMARCA4 mutations has been shown to be highly sensitive and specific for the diagnosis of SCCOHT [24, 30–32]. Loss of SMARCA4 (BRG1) is defined as the absence of nuclear staining in tumor cells, with surrounding stromal cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and lymphocytes serving as positive internal controls. However, SMARCA4 staining is not always “all or nothing”; weak or partial BRG1 nuclear staining can also be considered abnormal [33–35].

SMARCA4 mutational loss in SCCOHT is often associated with concurrent loss of SMARCA2 (BRM), the residual ATPase of the SWI/SNF complex. Combined SMARCA4 (BRG1) and SMARCA2 (BRM) deficiency is considered a hallmark of SCCOHT [21–22, 36–38]. SMARCA2 deficiency occurs through epigenetic inactivation, as deletions and mutations are rare in human tumors and have not been reported in SCCOHT [22, 36].

To minimize diagnostic ambiguity, molecular analysis of the SMARCA4 gene should be performed to identify pathogenic variants, in combination with SMARCA4 (BRG1) and SMARCA2 (BRM) IHC analysis. Some patients with retained SMARCA4 (BRG1) protein expression show loss of SMARCB1 (INI1), ARID1A (BAF250A), or ARID1B (BAF250B) expression [5–6, 20–22, 31].

It is necessary to differentiate SCCOHT from primary or metastatic ovarian neoplasms such as AGCT, juvenile granulosa cell tumor (JGCT), small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) (metastatic or pulmonary type), undifferentiated carcinoma (UDC), germ cell tumor (GCT) (including Dysgermino), undifferentiated ovarian carcinoma (UDOC), dedifferentiated ovarian carcinoma (DDOC), and synovial sarcoma (SS). Differentiating SCCOHT from JGCT remains challenging due to overlapping immunohistochemical and morphological features, especially when hypercalcemia is present in JGCT. Sex cord markers such as Inhibin-α, FOXL2, and SF1 are not reliably expressed in SCCOHT [39–40]. Nuclear SMARCA4 (BRG1) immunoreactivity is absent in SCCOHT but present in AGCT and JGCT [6, 30]. FOXL2 and DICER1 mutations are frequently observed in AGCT [40], whereas JGCTs have demonstrated mutations in FOXL2, AKT1, GNAS, and DICER1 [41].

SCCOPT represents a true neuroendocrine neoplasm. Histopathologically, its tumor cells display predominantly diffuse sheet-like growth without follicle-like spaces. Immunohistochemically, they consistently express neuroendocrine markers such as CgA, frequently show p53 overexpression, are negative for Vimentin, and retain nuclear expression of SMARCA2 (BRM), SMARCA4 (BRG1), and/or SMARCB1 (INI1) [28].

Because SCCOHT occurs in young patients and is highly malignant, germ cell tumors—specifically dysgerminoma, embryonal carcinoma, or yolk sac tumor—are often misdiagnosed by pathologists unfamiliar with its distinctive features. Negative staining for β-hCG, AFP, Oct 3/4, PLAP, and CD117 helps exclude germ cell tumors [17].

UDOC and the undifferentiated carcinoma component of DDOC are characterized by diffuse sheet-like proliferation of discohesive and monomorphic epithelioid cells, often showing partial or total loss of keratin and EMA expression, with consistently negative staining for Pax8, ER, and PR, and frequent inactivation of core SWI/SNF complex proteins [42–43]. UDOC and SCCOHT WT1-N terminus may serve as a useful diagnostic marker, as the majority of SCCOHTs express WT1-N terminus, although WT1-N terminus expression in DDOC/UDOC is not well characterized [42–44]. NGS panel testing may also aid diagnosis if pathogenic variants frequently seen in endometrioid carcinomas (e.g., PTEN, KRAS, CTNNB1) are detected [43]. While SCCOHT tumors exhibit remarkable inter-tumor homogeneity and genomic stability; they generally have fewer somatic copy number alterations and low TMB [12, 22, 45].

SCCOHT commonly exhibits immunohistochemical expression of the SS marker TLE1, which represents a potential diagnostic pitfall, as synovial sarcoma may be included in the differential diagnosis, especially in patients with retained SMARCA4 immunoreactivity [33]. More than 90% of the cases have a characteristic chromosomal ectopict translocation t (x; 18) (p11.2; q11.2), leading to the SS18-SSX fusion gene.

SCCOHT is typically treated with surgery, autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT), and platinum-based chemotherapy combined with high-dose chemotherapy (HDCT), with radiotherapy applied in some cases [1]. All chemotherapeutic regimens were platinum-based (either cisplatin or carboplatin). The commonly used regimen were VPCBAE (vinblastine, cisplatin, cyclophosphamide, bleomycin, doxorubicin, and etoposide), BEP (bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin ), and PAVEP (cisplatin, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and etoposide) [1, 22]. Germline SMARCA4 PV genetic testing should be performed in SCCOHT patients, and at-risk family members should be monitored [22].

Data from the literature indicate that the prognosis of SCCOHT is generally poor [1]. The five-year overall survival (OS) rates were 51% (95% CI 35–75) for patients with FIGO stage I disease and 24% (95%: 14–40) for patients with FIGO stages II, III, and IV combined. Recurrence occurred in 36.4% (32/88) of patients, primarily involving the pelvis and abdomen. The metastatic pattern of SCCOHT involves the lungs, liver, pancreas, bladder, mediastinum, bones, spleen, cerebellum, vagina, and breast.

The FIGO stage remains the primary determinant in assessing prognosis, with FIGO stage IA disease having the most favorable prognosis [1, 2]. Other potentially favorable prognostic features include the following: age > 30 years, normal preoperative calcium level, tumor size of up to 10 cm, and absence of large cells [1, 2].

Conclusions

SCCOHT is a rare and aggressive ovarian cancer with a poor prognosis and a high rate of misdiagnosis, and information on adequate treatment for this cancer is lacking.

Integration of clinical findings, immunohistochemical features, and SMARCA4 gene mutation testing is crucial for accurate diagnosis and may lead to new treatment options. However, the current data are based on small case series and case reports, and due to the absence of long-term survival data, SCCOHT remains a formidable clinical challenge. Continued research efforts and a multidisciplinary approach hold promise for developing better therapies and achieving improved outcomes for patients and their families affected by this condition in the future.
