Single-cell stemness analysis highlights Midkine-LRP1 pathway and identifies new bladder cancer subtypes
Authors: Cheng Qian, Jin Qin, Zixian Lin, Shenggan Wang, Anmei Deng, Tengda Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBladder cancer (BC) is among the ten most common malignant tumors worldwide. Tumor stem cells contribute significantly to postoperative recurrence and disease progression. Understanding stem cell interactions with other tissue cells and developing a prognostic model may improve BC management. Through single-cell RNA sequencing, we identified tumor stem cells in BC tissue. We identified 91 genes specifically upregulated in the stem cell cluster versus other clusters. Of these, 67 whose high expression correlated with poorer patient survival were defined as high-risk stemness genes (HRSGs). Further analysis showed that the MDK-LRP1 axis is the primary communication pathway between stem cells and other cell types, involving 31 of these HRSGs. Based on these 31 HRSGs, patients were stratified into two stemness clusters (ST cluster A and B), with ST cluster B associated with poorer prognosis. We further selected prognosis-related genes from differentially expressed genes between the clusters to construct a risk model. Patients in ST cluster B exhibited higher risk scores, aligning with clinical outcomes. Among the HRSGs, ACTN1 emerged as a key gene, showing elevated expression in patients with poor survival and advanced disease stages. Immunohistochemistry confirmed significantly increased ACTN1 protein levels in BC tissues. Additionally, protein interface analysis indicated that the Cys104 residue of Midkine potentially interacts with both LRP1 and ACTN1 within a 5 Å distance, suggesting a critical interaction site. These findings provide novel insights into stem cell–mediated BC progression and offer potential prognostic and therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04009-0.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Stemness, Single-cell sequencing, Cell chat, Protein interface analysis, ACTN1, Midkine, LRP1

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) poses a substantial global health burden, owing to its consistently high incidence and associated mortality [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there were 610,000 new cases of BC and 220,000 deaths globally in 2022 [3]. The traditional classification of BC based on histopathology revealed that urothelial carcinoma is the most common subtype [4]. Among all patients with BC, 25% have muscle-invasive BC and 75% have non-muscle-invasive BC [5, 6]. They were also classified by the Tumor Node Metastasis (TNM) system [7]. BC is staged using the TNM system: the T category ranges from Tis (carcinoma in situ) and Ta (non-invasive papillary carcinoma) through T1 (invasion of the lamina propria), T2 (invasion of the muscularis propria), T3 (extension into perivesical tissue), to T4 (invasion of adjacent organs or pelvic/abdominal wall); the N category classifies regional lymph node involvement as N0 (no metastasis), and N1–N3 denote progressively increasing degrees of regional lymph node involvement and the M category distinguishes M0 (no distant metastasis) from M1 (distant metastasis present) [8–10]. Although the clinical features and classification of BC have been previously described, patients are often treated with uniform therapeutic approaches, disregarding tumor heterogeneity [4–7]. This lack of individualized treatment frequently contributes to poor clinical outcomes. Moreover, the key molecular drivers underlying BC development and progression remain insufficiently characterized [11]. Therefore, establishing a more refined classification system for BC is essential to identify potential therapeutic targets and to enable clinicians to design more precise and effective treatment strategies.

BC stem cells are the main causes of tumor complex biological behavior and are involved in tumor occurrence, recurrence, and metastasis [12]. Reportedly, BC stem cells maintain the tumor cells’ self-renewal and proliferation and can migrate to distant organs to promote metastasis [12]. Normal stem cells with fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 mutations can transform into BC stem cells and promote the formation of non-muscle invasive BC, while mutations in p53 or PTEN may promote the development of muscle-invasive BC[12]. The signatures in the crosstalk between mesenchym al stem cells and BC stem cells can predict the patients’ prognosis or immune response [13].

Single-cell sequencing (scSeq) is a powerful technique for amplifying and sequencing transcriptome or genome at a single-cell level [14]. It enables the identification of novel tumor cell subsets and the construction of comprehensive single-cell gene expression profiles [15–17]. Prior to the advent of scSeq, conventional high-throughput sequencing approaches often overlooked genetic material derived from cancer stem cells and other low-abundance yet functionally critical cell populations [14, 18]. The development of single-cell sequencing has effectively addressed this limitation [14, 18]. For instance, in breast cancer, scSeq has been employed to elucidate the dynamic metabolic evolution of early disseminated lesions [19]. More recently, scSeq has also been utilized to investigate specific cellular markers in both epithelial and non-epithelial compartments of muscle-invasive bladder cancer [20].

Although advances have been made in BC research, systematic analyses of high-risk stemness genes (HRSGs) and their roles in intercellular communication at the single-cell level remain lacking. Here, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from BC to identify stem cell-specific genes and key communication pathways. We defined HRSGs within the dominant MDK-LRP1 axis and constructed a risk assessment model based on their expression. A key HRSG was further identified, and protein interface analysis suggested potential interactions between Midkine, LRP1, and this protein. These findings provide novel insights into BC progression and potential targets for prognostic and therapeutic strategies. The study workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

Data preparation

The scRNA-seq of seven BC patients (GSE135337) was obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) on the platform 10X Genomics. Patients’ information is summarized in Table S1. Among them, three patients were in Ta stage, two were in T1, one was in T2, and one was in T3. The age of the cohort was 65.29 ± 14.53 years. A total of 430 transcript files and 415 copy number variation (CNV) files of BC patients were obtained from the cancer genome atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/, 2023–04–10). The transcript files were downloaded from the Illumina platform, and the CNV files were from Affymetrix SNP 6.0 platform. Subsequently, 430 patients, average age 68.14 years, were included in this study (Table S2). Among 430 patients, 117 were females and 313 were males, and their information is summarized in Table S2. The Perl programming language was used to merge all the separated files into one summary document and further used to transform the gene identification numbers into gene symbols. Next, we used the limma package to arrange the data matrix and used fpkm function to acquire the transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) files. The validation dataset included 14 normal bladder tissue samples and 14 BC tissue samples, the normal bladder samples were obtained from the GTEx Portal (https://gtexportal.org), and the BC samples were obtained from the GSE223971 dataset.

In this study, all immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining results for each protein were retrieved from the publicly available Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). In the HPA workflow, all cases undergo initial selection based on microscopic evaluation of representative hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections. Subsequently, 1 mm diameter tissue cores are extracted from the corresponding formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded blocks to construct tissue microarrays (TMAs). For protein expression analysis, TMA sections are subjected to standardized IHC staining protocols, and the stained slides are digitized using high-resolution slide scanners. The resulting images are systematically reviewed and annotated by expert pathologists with respect to staining intensity and the proportion of positive tumor cells for each validated antibody.

Seurat analysis for BC scRNA-seq

scSeq data from seven BC tissues were merged into a matrix comprising > 10,000 single-cell profiles. The matrix was converted into a Seurat object, and cells with > 50 detected genes and < 5% mitochondrial gene content were retained. Batch correction was performed during scRNA-seq data integration. Specifically, we applied the Harmony algorithm to integrate multiple samples and correct for batch effects. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to identify key features of each principal component, with p-values visualized accordingly. The top 20 PCs were used to compute cell proximity metrics. Subsequently, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) was applied for dimensionality reduction and cell-type annotation. Differentially expressed genes for each cell cluster were identified relative to other clusters, and genes specifically enriched in the stem cell population were selected for further analysis.

CellChat analysis and determination of HRSGs

For CellChat analysis, we first created the CellChat object, then input the ligand-receptor database, and calculated the probability for cell–cell communication. The intercellular communication correlation was the output, and then their interaction network was extrapolated at the signaling pathway level. Based on the results, the overall communication status of the cells was displayed as a map. Next, we determined the main CellChat pathway and selected the involved stemness (ST) genes. Then, we chose the genes whose high expression indicated patients’ low survival probability, termed HRSGs. The Edge Percolated Component (EPC) function in Cytoscape software was used to evaluate the importance of each HRSG.

Identification of the BC ST subclusters

We divided 430 transcript files from BC patients into ST subclusters based on HRSGs using ConsensusClusterPlus package [21] in R. Every mRNA file was clustered into k groups based on the average k values. The number of times this process was repeated could be used to calculate the consensus values and estimate the stability of the classification sets. The survival probability of patients in different BC ST subclusters was calculated using the Survival package [22–24], and the results are presented by Kaplan–Meier plots. PCA was performed to assess the efficiency of patient subgrouping. The RCircos and maftools packages were used to visualize the chromosomal distribution and generate waterfall plots of CNVs for HRSGs. An integrated heatmap was constructed to display the clinical characteristics of the ST subclusters, including patient stage, status, T category, and N category. The GSVA, GSEABase, and pheatmap packages were employed to conduct Single-Sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA).

Establishment of the prognostic risk model

Limma package was used to identify the differentially expressed genes (fold change > 1.5, P-value < 0.05) between the two ST clusters. Then, we used univariate Cox proportional risk regression method to find the prognostic-related genes from the above differential gene cluster. We divided the patients into different prognosis (PR) clusters based on these genes and plotted their survival curves and clinical features as a heatmap, as described above. The reshape2 and ggpubr packages were used to draw the boxplots for the expression of HRSGs. Then, the BC patients were randomly divided into training (202 samples) and testing groups (201 samples). The prognostic risk model was built in the training group and validated in the testing group. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied to select genes corresponding to the minimal cross-validation error for subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis. Each gene was assigned a weighted coefficient, and the overall risk score was calculated as the weighted sum of gene expression levels. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the model’s predictive performance. The timeROC package was used to plot time-dependent ROC curves at 1, 3, and 5 years, and to calculate the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values.

Nomogram and alluvial diagram

Survival/regplot/rms packages were employed to draw the nomogram. Firstly, we used the intersect function in R to merge patients’ risk scores and their clinical information. Secondly, we used coxph function and regplot package to draw the nomogram, and the nomoRisk score was obtained by the predict function in R. This nomogram could predict patients’ survival probability of 1, 3, or 5 years. The calibration curve was a tool to evaluate the predicted ability of the nomo model. The ggalluvial/ggplot/dplyr packages were used to draw an alluvial diagram and to observe the correlations between different subpopulations and the directed movement of the same patient between different subpopulations.

Interface analysis of targeted proteins

All protein docking procedures were conducted using the HDOCK server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) with default settings, which employs a hybrid docking strategy combining template-based modeling and free docking. The docking scores provided by HDOCK (in kcal/mol) were used as surrogate measures of binding energy. No site-specific constraints were applied unless otherwise noted. HDOCK can analyze different conformations of protein–protein docking, binding activity under different conformations, and amino acid residues with interaction distances within 5 Å. The protein data bank was applied to identify each molecule obtained from the RCSB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). PyMOL (version 4.3.0) software (https://pymol.org/) was used to isolate the primitive ligand and protein structures and dehydrate. It was also used to map the amino acid residues between two proteins. Ligplus software was used to analyze the acting forces between two proteins from a two-dimensional perspective. We obtained the FASTA files of the key functional regions of Midkine, LRP1, and ACTN1 from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/), corresponding to the PDB IDs-1MKC, 1CR8, and 2EYI, respectively. Subsequent protein–protein docking analyses were performed based on the protein sequences from these structure-derived files, with amino acid positions referenced according to these sequences. For ease of interpretation, the protein names used in the protein interaction analyses represent the functional regions corresponding to these downloaded sequences.

Statistical analysis

We used t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare two or more two continuous datasets of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, otherwise Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. The significance level was P < 0.05. All the statistical calculations were performed using IBM SPSS 21.0 or the R software.

Results

Determined the HRSGs in BC

We analyzed the database of BC scRNA-seq, and patients’ information is shown in Table S1. The results showed that RNA counts were positively correlated with their features (Figure S1a). The RNA counts in the seven samples are shown in Figure S1b, and about 1500 variable genes were identified in the cells (Figure S1c). PCA found PC1 and PC2 that could distinguish the cells in the seven samples (Figure S1d).

The sequencing results of 20,204 cells were extracted to form a matrix that was analyzed using the Seurat method. All cells were divided into 15 subtypes (Fig. 2a, Figure S1e). The genes in each subtype were distinct, and the cells were annotated using SingleR package (Fig. 2a-b). Figure 2b shows the tissue stem cell subgroup marked in green. The genes expressed in different cell types are listed in Table S3. We generated a Venn diagram based on the featured genes of monocytes, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, T cells, and tissue stem cells. We noted that, although the tissue stem cell and endothelial cell clusters did not overlap at the cellular level in Fig. 2b, a subset of genes was commonly upregulated in both populations (Fig. 2c). This reflects the fact that certain regulatory pathways are shared across different cell types. However, the genes most likely to determine distinct cellular functions or fates are those uniquely and highly expressed within each cluster. Accordingly, as highlighted by the green region in Fig. 2c, we identified 91 genes specifically expressed in tissue stem cells for subsequent analyses (Table S4). Here, "specifically expressed" refers to genes that were significantly upregulated in the stem cell cluster compared to all other cell populations identified in the single-cell transcriptomic analysis. Then, we conducted a survival analysis and identified 67 HRSGs from 91 stemness genes; the high level of HRSGs indicated the low survival probability of patients with BC (Fig. 2d, Table S5).

Identified HRSGs involved in the main stem cell communicational axis in BC

We conducted a CellChat analysis by creating CellChat object and calculating the probability of intercellular communication. The number of cell interactions is shown in Fig. 2e. MDK-low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) axis was the main communicational pathway between stem cells and other cells (Fig. 2f). Among all the cells, tissue stem cells mainly interacted with epithelial cells through the MDK-LRP1 signaling pathway (Fig. 2g). The epithelial cells have tumor features by highly expressing keratin family proteins (Table S3). Gene-rich analysis revealed 31 HRSGs involved in the MDK -LRP1 pathway (Fig. 2h). The survival curves of the 31 HRSGs are shown in Figure S2, and high levels of these genes indicated a low survival probability in patients with BC. The expression of 31 HRSGs combined with the clinical features is shown in Table S6. The score of 31 HRSGs was calculated by EPC; the nodes’ colors changing from yellow to red indicated increasing scores (Fig. 2h). Based on the network, we found that genes, such as actinin alpha 1 (ACTN1), collagen type VI alpha 1 chain (COL6A1), were the key nodes (Fig. 2h). We further validated the expression of the 31 HRSGs in different cell types in BC tissue and observed that they were higher in tissue stem cells than in other cell types, especially ACTN1 (Fig. 2i). The CNV of 31 HRSGs is shown in Fig. 3a; the CNV of HRSGs, such as ACTN1 was gained, while that of tropomyosin 2 (TPM2) was a loss (Table S7). We analyzed the somatic mutation of 31 HRSGs, and identified that collagen type VI alpha 3 chain (COL6A3), ACTN1, and matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP2) were the top 10 genes with high mutation frequency (Figure S3). ACTN1 mainly had missense and nonsense mutations in BC (Figure S3).

Classified BC patients into two ST subclusters based on HRSGs

We stratified patients with BC into distinct ST clusters based on the transcript levels of 31 HRSGs. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the consensus matrix; when k = 2, the matrix demonstrated high stability (Fig. 3b). Accordingly, BC patients were classified into ST cluster A and ST cluster B (Fig. 3c, Table S8). PCA further confirmed the separation between ST clusters A and B along PC1 and PC2 (Fig. 3d), with the top 20 PCs detailed in Table S9. All 31 HRSGs exhibited higher expression levels in ST cluster B compared to cluster A (Fig. 3c). Clinically, ST cluster B was enriched for patients with Stage III–IV disease, higher proportions of deceased patients, advanced T stage (T3–4), N stage (N1–3), and high-grade tumors (Fig. 3c). In addition, the majority of patients in ST cluster B were aged > 65 years and exhibited poorer survival probabilities relative to those in ST cluster A (Fig. 3c and Fig. 3e).

Patients were divided into high- or low-risk subgroups

We compared the gene levels between the two ST subclusters to identify the differently changed genes. Then, we conducted univariate Cox regression analysis for those differential genes and identified 2998 prognosis-related genes (Table S10). Based on these genes, the patients were further divided into two PR clusters (Fig. 4a, Table S11). We found that patients in PR cluster A were mostly in stage III-IV (vs. stage I-II), dead state (vs. alive), T3-4 (vs. T1-2), high grade (vs. low grade), ST cluster B (vs. ST cluster A), as shown in Fig. 4b. PR cluster B had a higher survival probability than A (Fig. 4c). The levels of 31 HRSGs were lower in PR cluster B than in A (Fig. 4d). Then, we performed LASSO regression analysis and found 59 genes that could be used to establish the prognostic model (Fig. 4e).

The features of BC prognosis (PR) subclusters. a Consensus matrix (k = 2) for determining PR clusters based on prognostic genes. b Results of survival analysis of the two PR clusters. c Clinical features of different PR or ST subgroup. d The expression of 31 HRSGs between the two PR clusters. e LASSO regression analysis of the prognosis-related genes in BC

A multivariate Cox regression analysis was used to construct the risk model (Table S12), generating a risk score for each patient. Patients were subsequently stratified into high- and low-risk groups (Table S13). The high-risk group exhibited significantly lower survival probability (Fig. 5a), with higher risk scores correlating with increased mortality (Fig. 5b). These findings were consistently validated in both the training and test cohorts (Fig. 5c-f). The model showed robust predictive performance, with AUC > 0.8 at the 1-, 3-, and 5-year time points in the overall cohort (Fig. 5g), and AUC > 0.9 in the training cohort (Fig. 5h). Consistently, the model also exhibited high predictive efficiency in the testing cohort, with AUCs of 0.978, 0.783, and 0.814 at the 1-, 3-, and 5-year time points, respectively (Figure S7).

We downloaded 195 bladder cancer sequencing samples treated with the anti–PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor atezolizumab (Tecentriq) from the IMvigor210CoreBiologies_1.0.0 dataset. Based on the expression levels of the 31 HRSGs (Fig. 1), we stratified these samples into low_stem and high_stem groups (Figure S9A). Using the risk model developed in this study, we likewise calculated a risk score for each sample and divided them into low_risk and high_risk cohorts (Figure S9B). Integrating clinical data, we found that both the high_stem and high_risk groups exhibited a significantly lower proportion of patients achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), and a significantly higher proportion with progressive disease (PD) (Figure S9A-B). In addition, the fraction of early‐stage tumors (Stage I-II) was reduced, whereas that of advanced tumors (Stage III-IV) was increased in these groups (Figure S9A–B). When mapped to the LundTax system, the high_stem/high_risk cohorts showed a marked loss of MS1a/MS1b (“Urobasal A”) and MS2a1/MS2a2 (“Genomically Unstable”) subtypes-both of which are generally associated with lower grade, non-muscle-invasive disease or intermediate risk-but a pronounced enrichment of MS2b2.2 (“Squamous-Cell-Like”), a subtype linked to high-stage, aggressive tumors with poor prognosis (Figure S9A–B). This shift toward the squamous-cell-like phenotype underscores the more aggressive biology and worse clinical outcomes observed in high_stem and high_risk patients.

Establishment of nomo model and analysis of the variable risk levels between different BC subtypes

We used patients’ ages, T categories, N categories, genders, states, stages, and risk scores to establish the nomo model, which could calculate the probability of patients’ survival to > 1, 3, or 5 years (Fig. 6a). The nomo score for every patient is shown in Table S13. In the nomogram, we observed the points axis and the different values in terms of gender and N categories that corresponded to an accurate value in the point axis; all the values were added to obtain the total points. The nomogram also displayed that the total points were 453, the 1-year survival probability for patients was about 0.65, the 3-year survival probability was about 0.2, and the 5-year survival probability was about 0.1 (Fig. 6a). We used the calibration curve to evaluate the efficiency of the nomo model (Figure S4). The model was considered effective in predicting patient survival if the survival rate of patients was evaluated using the model and the evaluation results were distributed diagonally on the nomo plot (Figure S4). In this study, we used the nomo model to evaluate patients’ survival probability at 1-, 3-, and 5-years, and the results were almost the diagonal on the plot (Figure S4).

Pathway enrichment and immune cell infiltration rate analysis and determination of critical genes among 31 HRSGs

We performed GSVA to identify differential pathway activity between ST clusters A and B. The top 20 enriched pathways are presented in Fig. 7a and Table S14. ST cluster B was characterized by upregulated pathways, including leukocyte transendothelial migration, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 7a). We further conducted ssGSEA and observed significant differences in immune cell infiltration between the clusters, particularly in activated B cells, activated CD4+ and CD8+T cells, and regulatory T cells (Fig. 7b).

ssGSEA, GSVA, and the key gene among 31 HRSGs. a-b The results of GSVA (a) and ssGSEA (b). (c) Correlation of 31 HRSGs. (d) Comparison of 31 HRSG levels between different clinical subgroups. (e) IHC results of 31 HRSGs. ST, stemness; No sig, no significance

The correlation analysis showed that all 31 HRSGs were positively correlated with each other (Fig. 7c). Then, we compared the levels of 31 HRSGs between patients with different clinical features and found that the mRNA levels of 17 genes, including COL6A1, COL6A3, and ACTN1, were higher in BC patients with tumor (vs. without), in state of disease control (vs. progression), in dead (vs. alive), in N1–3 category (vs. N0), in high grade (vs. low grade), in stage III-IV (vs. stage I-II), in T3-4 (vs. T1-2), as shown in Fig. 7d. The expression of other genes except the above was also changed in at least one type of clinical subgroup (Fig. 7d). The mRNA level of CD63 was lower in BC patients with tumors, in dead patients, and in patients with high grades, but its level increased with the advancement of T categories, stages, or N categories (Figure S5). Interestingly, we found that the key genes shown in Fig. 2i were highly expressed in bladder cancer tissues compared to normal tissues, as illustrated in Figure S6. Therefore, the mRNA level of those 31 HRSGs is considered a critical factor in identifying high-risk patients.

To further explore the function of HRSGs, we analyzed the IHC results of each gene and found that the ACTN1 protein was highly expressed in the tumor tissue of BC patients (Fig. 7e). This could be the key protein in stem cells promoting tumor progression.

Interface analysis of Midkine, LRP1, and ACTN1

We conducted an interface analysis of Midkine, LRP1, and ACTN1. The results showed that after docking LRP1 with Midkine protein, the binding energy between the two was − 186.02 kcal/mol. The complex between LRP1 and Midkine protein was docked with ACTN1 protein, and the binding energy between the two was − 217.98 kcal/mol. Typically, the lower the binding energy of the two proteins’ docking, the better the binding stability.

The results of force analysis between LRP1 and Midkine proteins showed that the aspartic acid (Asp)29 amino acid residue of LRP1 protein was bound to the lysine (Lys)87 amino acid residue of Midkine protein by one 3.8 Å hydrogen bond (Fig. 8). The histidine (His)7 amino acid residue and Asp27 amino acid residue of LRP1 protein were bound to the cystine (Cys)104 amino acid residue and arginine (Arg)89 amino acid residue of Midkine protein by two hydrogen bonds of 3.4 Å and 3.7 Å, respectively (Fig. 8). The Cys19 amino acid residue of LRP1 protein was bound to the glycine (Gly)70 amino acid residue of Midkine protein by one 2.9 Å hydrogen bond (Fig. 8). The Cys19 amino acid residue and the glutamine (Gln)12 amino acid residue of LRP1 protein was bound to the tyrosine (Tyr)90 amino acid residue and Gly70 amino acid residue of Midkine protein by two hydrogen bonds of 2.6 Å and 2.9 Å, respectively (Fig. 8).

The Cys104 amino acid residue of the Midkine protein could bind to His7 amino acid residue in LRP1 protein and Lys157 amino acid residue in ACTN1 protein, which might be the key point to connect and promote the function of these proteins.

Discussion

BC is one of the world’s top ten malignant tumors, which seriously threaten human health. Since the mechanism is not clear, there is a lack of effective therapeutic targets [25]. Clinically, most of the treatment plans are formulated according to the pathological and TNM stages of patients, rendering it difficult to achieve precise treatment [4–7]. Stem cells play a vital role in maintaining the number and proliferation of BC cells and are the main causes of BC progression and tumor recurrence after surgery [12, 26–29]. In BC tissue, stem cells maintain the self-renewal of tumor cells and promote tumor proliferation and invasion by communicating with other cells [12, 26–29].

LRP1 belongs to the low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor family and functions as a transmembrane protein involved in cell signaling [30, 31]. It is highly expressed in the blood–brain tumor barrier, tumor neovasculature, and gliomas, where members of the vascular peptide family can bind to LRP1 to facilitate transcytosis [32, 33]. LRP1 participates in various biological processes, including lipid metabolism, cell migration, and the inflammatory response [30]. Recent studies have shown that endothelial-specific deletion of LRP1 promotes glucose metabolic homeostasis [34]. Dysregulation of LRP1 in fibroblasts within healing tissues contributes to the uncontrolled expansion of contractile myofibroblasts and fibrosis development [35]. In cancer, LRP1 suppression has been reported to inhibit Notch signaling and reduce tumorigenesis in leukemia models [36]. Additionally, binding of serine protease 2 to LRP1 can remodel the tumor microenvironment and promote tumor progression [37].

Midkine is a heparin-binding growth factor that is overexpressed in the epidermis, bronchial epithelium, or lymphocytes, maintaining normal homeostasis [38]. In many types of cancers, Midkine is highly expressed during the early tumor progression into advanced stages [38–40]. It promotes the proliferation of tumor cells and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, as well as drug resistance [38, 41]. The overexpression of Midkine can remodel the glioblastoma microenvironment by pushing the M2 polarization of microglia [42]. In primary cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC), Midkine is overexpressed in fibroblasts, promotes their growth, and blocks tumor cell migration [43]. In the recurrent cSCC, Midkine is highly expressed in tumor-specific keratinocytes and contributes to their proliferation and metastasis [43]. The exosomes released from the nasopharynx could inhibit endothelial tube formation and migration by targeting Midkine [44]. Reportedly, Midkine is highly expressed in the stem-like state cell population [45]. The inhibition of Midkine decreases the self-renewal and tumorigenic capacity of glioblastoma with stem-like features [46]. However, Midkine-LRP1 pathway in BC is yet to be elucidated.

In this study, MDK-LRP1 is the main pathway for BC stem cells to communicate with other tissue cells (Fig. 2e-f). The 31 HRSGs involved in the MDK-LRP1 pathway can be used to identify patients with poor prognosis and high-risk scores in the ST subtype (Fig. 2h, Fig. 3–4, Fig. 6). These results were supported by the previous studies described above. We observed that the pathways upregulated in the ST cluster B include the immune-related pathways, such as leukocyte transendothelial migration, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and the upregulated levels of immune cells, such as activated CD4+T cells or CD8+T cells (Fig. 7a-b). These findings indicated that HRSGs involved in the MDK-LRP1 might promote tumor progression by the above pathway. Next, we developed a risk assessment model and nomo model (Fig. 5, Fig. 6a) that allows an accurate assessment of BC patients’ prognoses.

The regulation of the actin cytoskeleton pathway was significantly upregulated in patients within ST cluster B, who exhibited poor prognosis (Fig. 7a). Across different cancer types, ACTN1 exhibits tissue-specific functional mechanisms and significant clinical relevance. In triple-negative breast cancer, ACTN1 contributes to chemotherapy resistance by enhancing tumor cell survival, and serves as a key marker gene for the basal-like epithelial cell subpopulation, showing strong associations with poor prognosis and treatment response, thus representing a promising prognostic and therapeutic target [47]. Moreover, in the breast cancer microenvironment, ACTN1 facilitates tumor progression by promoting the activation of cancer-associated fibroblasts, thereby remodeling the tumor stroma and enhancing cancer cell proliferation and metastasis [48]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, ACTN1 competitively binds to MOB1, thereby suppressing the phosphorylation of LATS1 and YAP, resulting in inhibition of the Hippo signaling pathway and promotion of tumor cell growth and proliferation [49]. In cholangiocarcinoma, ACTN1 is enriched in serum extracellular vesicles, primarily derived from malignant cholangiocytes, and its expression level is significantly associated with poor patient survival outcomes [50]. ACTN1 also localizes to the membranes of non-muscle cells, where it contributes to cell–cell adhesion [51]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, ACTN1 is involved in the critical pathways driving cancer progression [49]. Based on tissue-level transcriptomic profiles from the Human Protein Atlas, ACTN1 shows relatively high expression in normal bladder tissue (Figure S8), suggesting a strong basal expression preference in the urinary system. Notably, microarray analysis using the GeneChip Human Exon 1.0 ST platform, followed by PCR validation, identified tumor-specific splice variants of ACTN1 in BC [52], indicating a potential functional alteration mediated by alternative splicing. Additionally, ACTN1 has been implicated in BC progression via the focal adhesion pathway [53], and its silencing significantly suppresses BC cell proliferation [54], further supporting its potential as a therapeutic target in this malignancy. In our study, ACTN1 was identified as a key gene among the 31 HRSGs involved in the MDK-LRP1 signaling axis, exhibiting high expression in advanced BC patients with poor prognosis (Fig. 7d-e). Taken together, these findings suggest that ACTN1 participates in diverse oncogenic mechanisms across multiple cancer types. In BC, it may display more pronounced tissue-specific expression and regulatory activity, highlighting its promise as a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target.

To further investigate the interaction between Midkine-LRP1 and ACTN1, we conducted protein-to-protein interface analysis (Fig. 8–9). The various biological functions are achieved through protein-to-protein interactions [55]. The interface between proteins is large and flat, the forces on this interface include hydrophobic, hydrogen bonding, and salt bridge, and the key amino acid residues interact by these forces, playing critical in the binding between proteins and their functions [56–60]. For the first time, we analyzed the interaction forces between LRP1, Midkine, and ACTN1 at a microscopic level and the amino acid interactions at the protein interface. The structural interface analysis revealed that Cys104 of Midkine forms critical hydrogen bonds with both LRP1 (His7) and ACTN1 (Lys157), positioning it at the core of the ternary protein complex (Fig. 8–9). This convergence suggests that Cys104 may serve as a functional “hot spot” crucial for stabilizing the Midkine-LRP1-ACTN1 assembly. Given its central role and chemical reactivity, Cys104 represents a potentially druggable pocket, particularly amenable to covalent or reversible covalent inhibitors targeting thiol groups. If structurally accessible and not involved in disulfide bonding, Cys104 could be selectively engaged by electrophilic fragments or small molecules, thereby disrupting critical protein–protein interactions. Such targeted intervention may destabilize the ternary complex and modulate downstream signaling, highlighting the therapeutic relevance of this interface. These findings provide a rational basis for further structure-based drug design efforts aiming to develop small molecules or peptides that selectively interfere with the Midkine-centered interface.

We acknowledge that this study has some limitations, such as the need for further validation experiments to clarify the roles of key molecules and specific amino acid residues. In the future, we will perform knockdown and site-directed mutagenesis experiments to investigate the functions of Midkine, LRP1, and ACTN1 in BC and to elucidate their interaction mechanisms.

Conclusion

BC stem cells are thought to contribute to tumor recurrence and metastasis. In this study, we suggest that BC stem cells may interact with other cells through the Midkine/LRP1/ACTN1 pathway, with the Cys104 amino acid residue in the Midkine protein potentially serving as a key site for this interaction. Moreover, we propose a new classification of BC ST-subtypes based on high-risk stemness genes involved in the Midkine/LRP1 pathway, which may provide a useful reference for clinicians in developing more precise treatment plans or diagnostic strategies.
