No detectable early subclinical cardiotoxicity within 12 weeks of thoracic radiotherapy under predominantly low cardiac dose exposure: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Iván Valdés – Orrego, Francisco Acevedo, Tomas Merino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly radiation-induced cardiac injury in patients receiving thoracic radiotherapy (RT) remains insufficiently characterized. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) and circulating biomarkers such as high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) may detect subclinical myocardialdamage.MethodsThis prospective single-center cohort study enrolled 47 adults with thoracic malignancies treated with curative-intent RT. Cardiac assessments were performed at baseline, 1 week, and 12 weeks post-RT. GLS was measured by two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography, and hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP were quantified using high-sensitivity immunoassays. Mean heart dose (MHD) was categorized as < 10 Gy or ≥ 10 Gy to explore dose-stratified differences within this predominantly low-dose exposure cohort. Temporal changes and dose-stratified differences were analyzed using non-parametric tests.ResultsGLS remained stable across timepoints (median − 18.06% at baseline, − 18.72% at week 1, and − 18.59% at week 12; p = 0.650). Similarly, hs-cTnT showed no significant variation (7.30, 7.50, and 6.60 ng/L, respectively; p = 0.842), and NT-proBNP demonstrated modest, non-significant increases (43.0, 48.5, and 55.5 pg/mL; p = 0.797). Stratification by MHD revealed no significant differences in GLS or biomarker levels at any timepoint. No dose-dependent associations were observed These findings were consistent across subgroups and in sensitivity analyses restricted to completers, in a cohort with a median MHD of 5.1 Gy.ConclusionsContemporary thoracic RT was not associated with detectable early subclinical cardiotoxicity within the first 12 weeks under predominantly low cardiac dose exposure. Early cardiac alterations may depend on higher radiation exposure or longer follow-up, supporting longitudinal surveillance strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-026-00539-2.

Introduction

Despite major advances in modern radiotherapy techniques, long-term treatment-derived cardiotoxicity continues to be a problem for patients [1]. Indeed, late effects can emerge months or even decades post-irradiation. These effects can include coronary artery disease, valvular dysfunction, cardiomyopathy, pericarditis, and heart failure [2]. Unlike late radiation-related cardiac effects, early cardiac toxicity in patients with thoracic malignancies remains poorly investigated and clinically challenging to detect. In this context, early recognition of myocardial injury is crucial to improve long-term outcomes. Serum biomarkers of cardiac damage and myocardial strain imaging may facilitate the identification of subclinical cardiac changes, allowing the identification of high-risk patients for a more personalized risk-adjusted follow-up [1].

Previous studies demonstrate that circulating biomarkers such as high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) are reliable indicators of myocardial injury and cardiac stress, respectively [3]. Hs-cTnT is a well-established marker of radiation-induced cardiotoxicity, capable of detecting subclinical cardiomyocyte injury across diverse oncologic therapies [4]. Elevated hs-cTnT levels following thoracic radiotherapy have been reported even in the absence of overt clinical symptoms, suggesting subclinical myocardial damage [5]. High-sensitivity assays permit detection of subclinical troponin elevations below the traditional clinical threshold, making hs-cTnT particularly well suited to identifying early and subtle myocardial injury in patients undergoing thoracic radiotherapy [1, 3]. Similarly, NT-proBNP, a marker of cardiac wall stress and hemodynamic overload, has similarly shown utility in detecting early radiation-associated cardiac changes [3].However, while circulating biomarkers capture biochemical evidence of myocardial damage, they do not provide direct functional assessment of myocardial performance. In this regard, global longitudinal strain (GLS), assessed by speckle-tracking echocardiography, is a sensitive measure of myocardial deformation that allows the detection of subclinical left ventricular dysfunction prior to the detection of changes by conventional parameters such as left ventricular ejection fraction. In patients undergoing thoracic radiotherapy, early radiation-induced myocardial injury may preferentially affect longitudinal myocardial fibers, rendering GLS particularly valuable for the early identification of cardiac toxicity [6]. When combined with circulating cardiac biomarkers, GLS may further enhance early cardiotoxicity detection and improve risk stratification in this vulnerable population. Because radiation-related cardiac injury is influenced by the extent of cardiac exposure, mean heart dose (MHD) is commonly used as a surrogate of overall cardiac radiation burden [7].

Our prospective study evaluated the effects of thoracic radiotherapy on GLS and circulating cardiac biomarkers within 12 weeks after therapy. We also tested whether these changes differed according to MHD in patients with thoracic malignancies.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a prospective single-center cohort study including 47 adult patients with histologically confirmed thoracic cancers (breast, lung, esophageal, thymic, mediastinal tumors or plasmacytoma) who were scheduled to receive curative-intent thoracic radiotherapy (RT). Patients were recruited between January 2020 and June 2022 at the UC Christus Cancer Center. Eligibility criteria required age ≥ 18 years, no prior thoracic RT, no pre-existing severe left ventricular dysfunction or symptomatic heart failure, and no planned palliative RT. Patients with significant comorbidities limiting life expectancy, pregnancy, or incomplete baseline clinical information were excluded. All participants had preserved baseline cardiac function and were able to undergo strain echocardiography and serum biomarker testing. Baseline characteristics of the study population, including cardiovascular risk factors (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking status), were retrospectively collected from available clinical records and are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Additional cohort characterization including detailed cancer diagnoses, breast cancer laterality, and radiotherapy dose and fractionation schedules is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Information regarding systemic anticancer therapies administered before or during radiotherapy was retrospectively collected when available and summarized descriptively (Supplementary Table S3).

Radiotherapy and dosimetry

RT was delivered following international heart-sparing recommendations using CT-based planning with three-dimensional conformal or intensity-modulated techniques. Dose–volume histograms were reviewed to extract dosimetric parameters. The MHD, available for 44 patients, was used as the primary exposure metric and categorized as low (< 10 Gy; n = 36) or high (≥ 10 Gy; n = 8), in accordance with established cardio-oncology thresholds. The median tumor prescribed RT dose was 41.1 ± 8.7 Gy, and the cohort-wide median MHD was 5.1 Gy (IQR 2.9–7.2).

Cardiac assessments

Cardiac evaluations were performed at three predefined timepoints: before RT (baseline), 1 week after completion of RT, and 12 weeks post-RT. GLS was measured using standardized two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography, integrating apical two-, three-, and four-chamber views in accordance with ASE/EACVI guidelines. GLS was expressed as a negative percentage, reflecting myocardial longitudinal shortening during systole. Serum biomarkers, including high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), were measured using validated high-sensitivity immunoassays and processed in the institution’s clinical laboratory.

Patient availability decreased over follow-up, with complete assessments available in approximately 75–81% of patients at week 1 and 55–59% at week 12, depending on the cardiac parameter assessed (Supplementary Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. Because data were non-normally distributed, non-parametric tests were applied. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare MHD groups at each timepoint and to evaluate baseline-to-follow-up deltas for GLS, hs-cTnT, and NT-proBNP. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to explore temporal changes across the three evaluation timepoints. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Stata version 14.2. As an exploratory analysis requested during peer review, post hoc statistical power was estimated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.6) based on paired effect sizes derived from observed within-subject changes between baseline and week 12. Because the primary analyses were non-parametric, these calculations were considered complementary and intended to quantify the ability of the study to detect observed effects. The number of available observations for each cardiac parameter at each study timepoint and within each MHD stratum, which varied due to missing assessments and loss to follow-up, is reported in Supplementary Table S4.

Results

The cohort comprised 47 patients with a mean age of 54.5 ± 12.3 years, predominantly female (85.1%) and with breast cancer (70.2%). Among the 42 participants with available cardiovascular risk factor data, diabetes mellitus was present in 23.8%, hypertension in 42.9%, and dyslipidemia in 19.0%. Most participants had never smoked (69.0%), whereas 16.7% were current smokers and 14.3% were former smokers (Supplementary Table S1). Systemic anticancer therapy data were available for 37 of 47 participants; the most frequently documented regimens included taxane-containing chemotherapy (62.2%), anthracycline-containing chemotherapy (40.5%), endocrine therapy (35.1%), platinum-based chemotherapy (21.6%), and HER2-targeted therapy (18.9%). Detailed systemic therapy information is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Across the cohort, serial assessments revealed no evidence of early cardiac injury after thoracic radiotherapy. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) remained stable throughout follow-up, with median values of − 18.06% at baseline, − 18.72% at week 1, and − 18.59% at week 12. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) showed similarly minimal fluctuation over time (7.30 ng/L at baseline, 7.50 ng/L at week 1, and 6.60 ng/L at week 12), and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) increased modestly but without statistical significance (43.0 pg/mL at baseline, 48.5 pg/mL at week 1, and 55.5 pg/mL at week 12). No significant temporal differences were observed for any parameter (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Stratification by MHD also demonstrated no dose-dependent differences. GLS values were comparable between the low-dose (< 10 Gy) and high-dose (≥ 10 Gy) groups at baseline (–18.60% versus − 16.40%, p = 0.17), week 1 (− 19.00% versus − 16.90%, p = 0.14), and week 12 (− 18.90% versus − 15.90%, p = 0.10). The same pattern was observed for soluble biomarkers: hs-cTnT medians were 6.5 ng/L versus 8.55 ng/L at baseline (p = 0.11), 7.3 ng/L versus 8.55 ng/L at week 1 (p = 0.17), and 6.1 ng/L versus 9.75 ng/L at week 12 (p = 0.15), for low-dose and high-dose groups respectively. NT-proBNP likewise showed no significant differences between dose groups at baseline (43.0 pg/mL versus 48.0 pg/mL, p = 0.54), week 1 (42.0 pg/mL versus 69.0 pg/mL, p = 0.61), or week 12 (56.0 pg/mL versus 42.0 pg/mL, p = 0.92) (Fig. 1). Overall, neither longitudinal trends nor dose-stratified comparisons identified evidence of early subclinical cardiotoxicity.

Post hoc power analyses demonstrated limited statistical power for detecting the observed changes in GLS, hs-cTnT, and NT-proBNP. Estimated power values were 13.9% for GLS (dz = 0.26; n = 13), 5.1% for hs-cTnT (dz = 0.02; n = 27), and 36.6% for NT-proBNP (dz = 0.33; n = 26), indicating that the study was underpowered to detect small treatment-related effects. Sample sizes varied across study visits due to missing assessments and loss to follow-up; detailed numbers of evaluable patients for each outcome according to study timepoint and MHD category are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

Our study used complementary measures of cardiac function (GLS, hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP) and did not find evidence of early cardiotoxicity in cancer patients that underwent thoracic radiotherapy. In recent decades, GLS has emerged as a reliable parameter to assess myocardial deformation and is widely recognized as a sensitive marker of early myocardial dysfunction [8]. Its growing use reflects the ability of strain analysis to detect subtle functional changes that may not be captured by conventional echocardiographic indices [9]. In this cohort, the maintenance of GLS values suggests that thoracic radiotherapy was not associated with changes in myocardial contractility. While GLS is well established as a surrogate of late cardiotoxic effects, evidence regarding its behavior in the early post-radiotherapy period, particularly within the first year, remains scarce [10]. Nonetheless, prior studies have described early GLS declines as early as six weeks following left-sided breast cancer radiotherapy, with persistence up to 12 months and no analogous changes in right-sided disease [11]. Indeed, Trivedi et al. demonstrated that GLS changes were observed at assessments beyond the 12-week window, suggesting that the evaluation window may be critical for detecting radiation-associated cardiac dysfunction [11]. Similarly, Chen et al. reported both immediate post-treatment and six-month reductions in GLS in patients with locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer, underscoring that functional and biochemical abnormalities may emerge several months after treatment rather than in the immediate post-radiotherapy period, consistent with our null findings at 12 weeks [12]. In this context, the present study contributes evidence that the first 12 weeks after thoracic radiotherapy may represent a pre-injury window under low cardiac dose exposure, during which subclinical cardiac changes are not yet detectable by the assessed parameters.Along with imaging-based assessments, circulating biomarkers have gained attention as markers or predictors of early radiation-associated cardiotoxicity. Unfortunately, to date the available evidence is somehow limited with only a small number of modestly sized studies evaluating short-term changes in cardiac troponins and natriuretic peptides. Demissei et al. reported that higher MHD was associated with greater early increases in NT-proBNP measured during or immediately after a median radiotherapy course of approximately 20 days, whereas cardiac troponin levels remained unchanged and showed no association with heart dose over the same period [13]. Notably, that study included a higher proportion of lung cancer and lymphoma patients with greater MHD, factors that may partially explain the discordance with our predominantly breast cancer cohort with a low median MHD of 5.1 Gy. Similarly, Tao et al. demonstrated that higher MHD was associated with post-treatment increases in both NT-proBNP and hs-cTnT, with biomarker elevations detected approximately two months after treatment, supporting a dose-dependent relationship between cardiac radiation exposure and early subclinical myocardial injury [14]. In that study, patients with thoracic malignancies received higher cardiac doses than those observed in our series, which may account for the discordant findings. Consistently, a meta-analysis of cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction showed that elevated hs-cTnT had early diagnostic value, particularly three and six months after treatment, suggesting that troponins may serve as early markers of cardiotoxicity across diverse oncologic therapies, including radiotherapy [4].

Our study has certain limitations that must be acknowledged. First, our sample size was relatively small and derived from a single center, limiting statistical power, adjustment for potential confounders, and generalizability. Formal post hoc power analyses confirmed that the study had limited power to detect small short-term changes in GLS and circulating biomarkers, largely reflecting the modest sample size and losses to follow-up. Losses to follow-up were attributable to patient withdrawals and to temporary suspension of study activities during the COVID-19 pandemic, which led to the closure of the CICUC and interrupted scheduled cardiac assessments. Nevertheless, sensitivity analyses restricted to complete paired observations yielded consistent negative findings, and the corresponding non-parametric effect sizes were trivial to small. These observations suggest that, although subtle effects cannot be excluded, the magnitude of any early treatment-related cardiac changes within 12 weeks was likely limited.

Although the study population was heterogeneous with respect to cancer diagnosis and radiotherapy fractionation schedules, the limited sample size precluded meaningful subgroup analyses according to tumour type, laterality, or treatment schedule. These factors should be explored in larger prospective cohorts.

Beyond tumour heterogeneity, a substantial proportion of patients had received potentially cardiotoxic systemic therapies prior to or concurrently with radiotherapy (Supplementary Table S3). These exposures represent potential confounders of the observed cardiac biomarker and strain trajectories; however, the limited sample size precluded meaningful adjustment or subgroup analyses according to systemic treatment history.

Another limitation is the incomplete availability of certain cardiovascular and echocardiographic covariates. Although baseline cardiovascular risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking status, and concomitant systemic anticancer therapies were available and have now been reported, data regarding pre-existing coronary artery disease and additional echocardiographic parameters were not systematically collected. GLS was the only echocardiographic parameter consistently available across study assessments. Consequently, residual confounding related to underlying cardiovascular disease burden or baseline cardiac structure and function cannot be completely excluded.

Beyond clinical covariates, the dosimetric approach also has limitations. The 10 Gy mean heart dose threshold was selected as a clinically motivated exploratory cutoff based on previously published dose stratifications [15] and institutional clinical practice. However, only a limited number of patients were included in the ≥ 10 Gy subgroup, restricting the statistical robustness of subgroup analyses.

Although MHD remains one of the most widely reported cardiac dosimetric parameters, emerging evidence suggests that radiation exposure to specific cardiac substructures, particularly the left anterior descending coronary artery and left ventricular regions, may be more strongly associated with early cardiac imaging abnormalities and biomarker changes than whole-heart dose metrics alone [16–18]. In this regard, McWilliam et al. demonstrated that dose to the heart base specifically was linked with poorer survival in lung cancer patients, further underscoring the heterogeneous radiosensitivity of cardiac substructures [19]. Detailed cardiac substructure dosimetry was only available in a small minority of patients (n = 4) and therefore could not be reliably evaluated, representing an important limitation of the present study. Comprehensive cardiac substructure contouring and dosimetric analysis should be incorporated in future prospective cardio-oncology studies to better characterize dose-response relationship.

Despite these limitations,, this study has notable strengths, including its prospective design, the integration of complementary cardiac assessments (GLS, hs-cTnT, and NT-proBNP) at three predefined timepoints, and the systematic collection of cardiac radiation dose within a well-characterized clinical cohort. The use of high-sensitivity assays for troponin and NT-proBNP enhanced the ability to detect subclinical biomarker changes, and the inclusion of patients with diverse thoracic malignancies broadens the generalizability of the findings beyond breast cancer populations.

Overall, these findings support the concept that early cardiac effects of thoracic radiotherapy are highly dependent on both radiation dose and timing of assessment. The absence of detectable changes in GLS and cardiac biomarkers within the first 12 weeks after treatment in our cohort likely reflects relatively low cardiac exposure and an early evaluation window that may precede the development of measurable subclinical injury. While prior studies have reported early alterations in myocardial strain or circulating biomarkers following thoracic radiotherapy, these findings have been heterogeneous and appear to depend on cancer type, radiation dose, and follow-up duration. In this context, our results are aligned with evidence suggesting a temporal progression of radiation-associated cardiotoxicity, whereby functional or biochemical abnormalities may emerge several months after treatment rather than in the immediate post-radiotherapy period. From a clinical perspective, these data suggest that routine early cardiac monitoring within the first 12 weeks may not be warranted for all patients receiving contemporary thoracic radiotherapy under low cardiac dose conditions. Rather, risk-adapted longitudinal surveillance strategies, prioritizing patients with higher cardiac dose exposure, pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors, or prior cardiotoxic systemic therapies, may represent a more targeted approach to capturing delayed cardiotoxic effects and optimizing long-term cardio-oncology care.
