NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy modulates colitis-associated ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells and mucosal repair
Authors: Qiqi Huang, Li Liu, Yue Zhao, Jing Xiao, Yanting Yang, Yue Hong, Haiyang Ji, Huangan Wu, Xiaopeng Ma, Dan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFerroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) acts as a crucial mechanism driving intestinal mucosal injuries and inflammatory reactions in colitis. Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy, a primary pathway for intracellular iron storage, takes part in regulating ferroptosis. Nevertheless, it is yet unknown whether ferritinophagy plays a role in ferroptosis of IECs in colitis. Hence, this experiment used dextran sulfate sodium salt (DSS) to establish an inflammation model with NCM460 cells and an animal model of colitis to discover the role of ferritinophagy in ferroptosis in IECs and colonic mucosal inflammatory damage.ResultsDSS induced ferroptosis in IECs, presenting as significantly increased contents of Fe2+, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and malondialdehyde (MDA), decreased protein expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and increased expression of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX2) (all P<0.05). Moreover, administration of ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1 and autophagy inhibitor methyladenine (3-MA) ameliorated colonic tissue damage in the animal model of colitis, reducing the disease activity index (DAI), colon macroscopic damage index (CMDI), and pathological score, as well as the serum contents of mucosal permeability markers diamine oxidase (DAO) and D-lactic acid (D-LA) (all P<0.05). Further, knockdown of NCOA4 downregulated the expression of microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3) and autophagy-related protein 5 (ATG5) proteins, upregulated the expression of ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), inhibited ferritinophagy, and decreased ferroptosis in IECs induced by DSS, thus ameliorating colonic inflammatory damage and mucosal permeability (all P<0.05). On the contrary, overexpression of NCOA4 encouraged ferritinophagy and aggravated ferroptosis in IECs and intestinal damage.ConclusionsThis study provided evidence supporting that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy participates in modulating colitis-related ferroptosis in IECs, and suppressing ferritinophagy protects IECs and promotes colonic mucosal repair.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00747-x.Keywords: Colitis, Intestinal epithelial cell, Ferroptosis, Inflammation, Ferritinophagy, Nuclear receptor coactivator 4

Background

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a prevalent, complex intestinal disorder mainly manifested as diarrhea, mucous stools, bloody stools, rectal tenesmus, and crampy abdominal pain [1]. As a 2023 systemic review reported, IBD had a prevalence exceeding 0.3%, which exhibited a progressively increasing trend in some newly industrialized countries, including China [2, 3]. IBD is known to be complex, repeatedly relapsed, and protracted, with a high risk of comorbid anxiety and depression, significantly hindering the patient’s quality of life and imposing personal and social financial burden [4–6]. Therefore, it is of great clinical significance to conduct in-depth research on the potential pathogenesis of IBD and seek effective treatment targets and methods.

A typical pathological feature of IBD is recurrent nonspecific inflammation, which may involve the rectal and colonic mucosa and submucosa [7]. Damage to intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) and subsequent inflammatory responses play a crucial role in the onset and progression of colitis [8–10]. Ferroptosis has emerged as a hotspot in cell death research. Research has demonstrated that multiple ferroptosis-associated genes are significantly upregulated or downregulated in colonic mucosal tissues of colitis patients (versus healthy controls), accompanied by pathological changes such as colonic epithelial loss, crypt damage, and inflammatory infiltration [11]. Ferroptosis in IECs has also been confirmed in dextran sulfate sodium salt (DSS)-induced colitis models [12]. These findings all support that ferroptosis is a key mechanism in IEC damage in colitis, and inhibiting IEC ferroptosis can slow down the progression of colitis [13–16].

Ferroptosis is an autophagy-dependent pattern of cell death [17]. Ferritinophagy, mediated by nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) to degrade ferritin to regulate intracellular free iron storage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, has been confirmed as an upstream mechanism in inducing ferroptosis [18]. Inhibition or knockout of NCOA4 can block ferroptosis or ferroptosis-related lipid ROS accumulation; vice versa, overexpression (OE) of NCOA4 boosts ferroptosis [19, 20]. However, the role of NCOA4-mediated ferritonophagy in colitis-associated IEC ferroptosis remains vague. Therefore, by establishing an inflammation cell model and colitis animal model using DSS, this study aims to investigate if NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy participates in IEC ferroptosis in DSS-induced colitis to unveil the pathological mechanism of colitis and provide novel insights into potential therapeutic targets.

Methods

In vitro

NCM460 cells (human IECs) were purchased from Lonza. The cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) (Thermofisher, MA, USA), which was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell, Wuhan, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin under standard conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Silencing NCOA4 and overexpressing NCOA4 in NCM460 were achieved using the lentivirus (LV) transfection system (1×108 TU/mL, MOI = 40) (Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The success of transfection was verified by detecting the expression levels of NCOA4 mRNA and protein in the cells. When the transfection was confirmed a success, the inflammation cell model was prepared by treating the cells with 20 mg/mL DSS (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA) solution for 12 h at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 according to the references [21, 22].

In vivo

Clean-grade healthy male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats weighing (160 ± 20) g were provided by Shanghai SLACK Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., China (License No.: SCXK(Hu) 2022-0004). The colitis rat model was established by ad libitum consumption of 3.5% DSS for 7 d [23, 24]. When the modeling process ended, each group randomly contributed a rat for model verification using macroscopic damage scoring and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining (Nanjing Jianchen, Nanjing, China). Upon successful modeling, rats in the ferroptosis inhibitor group (ING) received intraperitoneal injection of liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1, Selleck, TX, USA) (10 mg/kg) once daily [25]; rats in the vehicle group (VG) received intraperitoneal injection of 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Byeotime, Shanghai, China) (1. 67 mL/kg) once daily; rats in the autophagy inhibitor group (IG) received intraperitoneal injection of 3-metryladenine (3-MA, MCE, NJ, USA) (7.08 mg/kg) once daily [26]; rats in the ferritinophagy activator group (AG) received intraperitoneal injection of 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI, MCE, NJ, USA) (35.4 mg/kg) once every other day [27]. The intervention period lasted 8 d for all groups.

Further, Specific Pathogen Free (SFP) male C57BL/6 mice weighing (20 ± 2) g were purchased from Shanghai SLACK Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., China (License No.: SYXK(Hu)2018-0040). NCOA4 knockdown (KD) and OE were achieved using the adeno-associated virus (AAV) transfection system (1×1011v.g./200uL) (Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) via tail vein injection. One month after AAV injections into the mouse tail vein, the colonic green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescence intensity was observed under a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan), and IECs were isolated to determine the expression levels of NCOA4 mRNA and protein. When NCOA4 was confirmed to be overexpressed or knocked down in IECs, the colitis mouse model was established via 7 d of free consumption of 3% DSS solution [28].

The whole experiment protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No.: YYLAC-2021-144; PZSHUTCM2502280011; PZSHUTCM2503030004). Before the experiments, all animals were acclimated to the housing environment and maintained on a standard diet to minimize stress and ensure their overall health. By the end of the experiment, the animals were euthanized by sodium pentobarbital anesthesia via intraperitoneal injections and abdominal aorta blood collection. All experimental procedures complied with ethical regulations for experimental animals.

Isolation of IECs

IECs were isolated following the references [22, 29]. The colons were cut into small pieces and incubated in a proper volume of digestion buffer (containing 1% type I collagenase and 1% dispase II) (BioFroxx, Einhausen, Germany) at 37 °C for 45 min. FBS was then added to terminate digestion. The digestion solution was filtered using a 70 μm cell strainer to collect the precipitate, which was resuspended in MEM medium supplemented with FBS. Then, 1×106 cells were seeded onto culture dishes and cultured in MEM medium containing 10% FBS for 24 h to remove non-adherent cells. The cells were identified using immunofluorescence staining.

Cell viability

Cell viability was assessed using cell counting kit 8 (CCK8) (Keygentec, Nanjing, China). Each well was seeded with 1×104 IECs and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK8 was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 ℃. The optical density (OD) of each well was determined using a microplate reader at 450 nm.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) detection

The intracellular MDA content was measured using MDA detection kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). 1×106 cells were treated with an appropriate volume of cell lysis buffer and centrifuged at 4 ℃ and 1600 g to collect the supernatant. In a 96-well plate, 100 µL of the sample was added to each well, followed by the addition of 200 µL working solution. The OD of each well was measured using the microplate reader (BioTek, VT, USA) at 532 nm. The content of MDA of each sample was calculated based on the standard curve.

ROS detection

The ROS content in the cells was detected using the 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) staining. 1×104 cells were seeded and cultured for 24 h. DCFH-DA was added for incubation following the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The OD of each well was measured using the microplate reader (BioTek, VT, USA) at 525 nm. The ROS content of each sample was calculated based on the standard curve. The colonic ROS expression level was observed using GENMED’s fluorescent assay. Fresh colon tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT), frozen, and sectioned at 10 μm. Experimental procedures were performed sequentially following the kit instructions (GENMED, Shanghai, China). The tissue sections were excited for fluorescence imaging under the fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan). Three fields of view were randomly selected from each sample and photographed. The results were semi-quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ, and the average optical density (AOD) of positive targets was calculated.

Fe2+ detection

The FerroOrange fluorescent probe was adopted to observe the level of Fe2+ in the cells. Each well was seeded with 1×104 cells, which were cultured for 24 h. The fluorescent probe was added for incubation following the Fe2+ detection kit instructions (Dojindo, Kyushu, Japan). The fluorescence intensity (FI) of each well was measured using the microplate reader (BioTek, VT, USA) at 570 nm. The colonic Fe2+ content was detected using the colorimetric assay. Experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions of the colorimetric kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). The OD of each well was measured using the microplate reader (BioTek, VT, USA) at 593 nm. The Fe²⁺ content of each sample was calculated based on the standard curve.

Disease activity index (DAI)

The animals were observed for their changes in body mass, length of colon, stool consistency, blood in stool, etc. The body mass, stool consistency, and severity of bloody stool were taken to calculate DAI to assess the symptoms [30, 31].

Colon macroscopic damage index (CMDI)

By referencing previous reports [32, 33], the macroscopic damage of the colon was evaluated to observe the disease activity and inflammatory injury in the animals.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The cellular ultrastructure was observed using a transmission electron microscope. The cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 30 min at room temperature away from light. Colon tissues were fixed for 2 h. The cells and tissues sequentially underwent dehydration, epoxy resin embedding, sectioning, and uranyl acetate-lead citrate staining. The samples were observed and photographed under the transmission electron microscope (FEI, OR, USA).

HE staining

Fresh colon tissues were fixed in neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at 4 μm. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were sequentially stained with hematoxylin and eosin and mounted with neutral balsam. The pathological features of colon tissues were observed under an optical microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan).

Detection of serum diamine oxidase (DAO) and D-lactate (D-LA) contents

The serum DAO content was detected using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and the serum D-LA content was measured using the microplate method. Colonic mucosal permeability was also assessed. Experimental procedures strictly followed the kit instructions (Mlbio, Shanghai, China). The OD value was measured using the microplate reader at 450 nm, and the content of each sample was calculated according to the standard curve.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Adherent cells were fixed with 4% ‌paraformaldehyde. The cells were incubated at room temperature overnight with diluted antibodies, including cytokeratin 19 (CK19) (1:100, Proteintech, IL, USA), NCOA4 (1:100, Santa Cruz, Texas, USA), glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and cyclooxygenase 2 (COX2) (1:100, Abcam, Cambs, UK). The next day, the fluorescent secondary antibody was added for incubation at room temperature in the dark. Finally, dihydrochloride (DAPI) was used to stain cell nuclei. The cells were observed and photographed under the fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan). The semi-quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ, and the AOD of the positive targets was calculated.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Colon tissues were fixed, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm. The sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The sections were then incubated at 4 °C with diluted GPX4 and COX2 antibodies (1:100, Abcam, Cambs, UK). The next day, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibodies (Dako, CPH, DK) were added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. An appropriate amount of diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen was applied for color development, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, differentiation, and bluing. Finally, the sections were mounted with neutral balsam. The samples were observed and imaged under the optical microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan). The semi-quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ, and the AOD of the positive targets was calculated. ‌.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The ribonucleic acid (RNA) extraction kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) was adopted to extract the total RNA of the cells. Then, complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA) synthesis kits (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) were used for cDNA synthesis. Finally, the PCR test machine (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was used for DNA amplification. Amplification condition: 95℃ for 30s; (95℃ for 5s and 60℃ for 20s) × 40 cycles. Melting curve: 95℃ for 5 s; 60℃ for 1 min; 95℃ (Ramp rate: 0.11℃/s; Acquisition Mode: Continuous; Acquisitions: 5 per℃). Data collection and analysis were performed using LightCycler 480 software. All data were converted using 2−ΔΔCt to obtain the relative expression of the target gene mRNA. Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting (WB)

The cells or colon tissues were treated with an appropriate amount of radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to isolate the total protein. The proteins were transferred onto the polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, MA, USA) and blocked using 5% BSA. The diluted primary antibodies (NCOA4, 1:1500, Santa Cruz, TX, USA; ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3), autophagy-related protein 5 (ATG5), COX2, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambs, UK) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the diluted HRP-labeled secondary antibody (1:1000, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the samples were treated with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and incubated for 2 min. The gray values of the target proteins and reference bands were quantified using the gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). The ratio between the target and the reference was defined as the relative expression level of the target protein.

Statistical analyses

The experimental data were analyzed using statistical product and service solutions (SPSS) version 25.0. The measurement data were checked for normality, and those in a normal distribution were expressed using mean ± standard deviation. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for between-group comparisons when the homogeneity of variance was confirmed using the least significant difference method. The Games-Howell test was used to analyze between-group differences when the data had heterogeneity of variance. The data in a skewed distribution were expressed using the median (quartiles), and the rank-sum test was used for between-group comparisons. All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Inhibiting ferroptosis improves colonic inflammatory injuries and mucosal permeability

Ferroptosis is recognized as a pathological mechanism in colitis. Using a colitis rat model established by free consumption of DSS solution, this study aimed to observe the role of ferroptosis in mucosal injuries and inflammation in colitis (Fig. 1A). The findings demonstrated that DSS-induced colitis rats had loose and bloody stool and a higher DAI compared to the normal group (NG) (P < 0.05; Fig. 1B). Optical microscopy revealed significant colonic injuries, manifesting as successive ulcers and inflammatory reactions in the model rats, together with increased CMDI and pathological scores and elevated serum DAO and D-LA contents (P < 0.05; Fig. 1C-G), suggesting increased mucosal permeability. The detection of colonic key ferroptosis markers showed that the contents of Fe2+ and ROS rose significantly, the expression of GPX4 protein was downregulated, and the expression of COX2 was upregulated in the colon of model group (MG) rats (P < 0.05; Fig. 1H-N). After the intervention with ferroptosis inhibitor Lip-1, hematochezia turned negative, the DAI and CMDI dropped, along with alleviated colonic injuries, presented as healed ulceration and mild inflammation revealed by optical microscopy, and the pathological score and serum contents of DAO and D-LA also decreased. Meanwhile, this group also showed decreased Fe2+ and ROS contents, upregulated GPX4 protein expression, and downregulated COX2 protein expression in the colon tissues (P < 0.05; Fig. 1H-N). There were no significant differences between the VG and MG (P > 0.05). The findings suggest that inhibiting ferroptosis can improve colonic inflammatory injuries and mucosal permeability.

Autophagy participates in regulating the onset of ferroptosis in IECs in colitis

Previous research demonstrates that autophagy is involved in ferroptosis in multiple ways. In this experiment, compared to the NG, the expression of colonic LC3 and ATG5 protein was upregulated in MG rats, suggesting enhanced autophagy, and the expression of LC3 and ATG5 protein was downregulated in IG rats compared to MG (P < 0.05; Fig. 2P, Q). Compared to the MG, rats in the IG showed improvements in the general symptoms and colonic injuries, together with reduced DAI, CMDI, and pathological scores, decreased serum contents of DAO and D-LA, and downregulated colonic COX2 protein expression (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A-I, M). The model rats also had elevated ferroptosis in IECs, together with pathological features such as plasma membrane rupture, mitochondrial cristae reduction or loss, and increased mitochondrial membrane density under the optical microscope (Fig. 2E, F). Rats in the IG received intervention with the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA to verify if autophagy is involved in the modulation of IEC ferroptosis and colonic inflammatory damage. The results demonstrated that the intervention notably downregulated the expression of autophagy-related proteins in the colon of IG rats, together with decreased ROS and Fe2+ contents and increased GPX4 expression in the colon tissue, as well as improved plasma membrane and mitochondrial manifestations and reduced ferroptosis in IECs under the electron microscope (P < 0.05; Fig. 2J-L, N, O). There were no significant differences between the AG and MG (P > 0.05). The findings here suggest that inhibiting autophagy alleviates ferroptosis in IECs and promotes mucosal repair.

NCOA4 influences DSS-induced ferroptosis in IECs

We performed in vitro studies with inflammatory cell models established using NCM460 cells, treated with LV and DSS, to observe the impact of NCOA4 on ferroptosis in IECs by detecting key ferroptosis-associated markers (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. 1). DSS solution (20 mg/mL) was then added to establish inflammatory cell models. Compared to the blank control (BC), cells in the MG and LV negative control (NC) group presented a significant reduction of cell viability, increased contents of Fe2+, ROS, and MDA, downregulated GPX4 protein expression, upregulated COX2, and elevated ferroptosis (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B-O). Compared to the MG, cells in the LV NCOA4 OE group showed a further reduction of cell viability, increased contents of Fe2+, ROS, and MDA, downregulated GPX4 expression, and aggravated ferroptosis; cells in the LV NCOA4 KD group demonstrated increased cell viability, reduced contents of Fe2+, ROS, and MDA, upregulated GPX4 protein expression, and reduced ferroptosis (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B-O). The findings suggest that knocking down NCOA4 alleviates DSS-induced ferroptosis in NCM460 cells; in contrast, overexpressing NCOA4 results in an opposite outcome.

We conducted further studies to see if NCOA4 plays the same role in vivo by injecting AAV into the mouse’s tail vein to achieve the OE and KD of NCOA4 and establishing colitis animal models via free consumption of DSS (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared to the NG, mice in the MG and AAV NC group demonstrated elevated contents of Fe2+, ROS, and MDA, downregulated GPX4 protein expression, and increased ferroptosis in IECs, together with notable pathological features in the plasma membrane and mitochondria under the electron microscope (P < 0.05; Fig. 4B-I). These findings indicate that suppressing the expression of NCOA4 can mitigate ferroptosis in IECs in colitis mice; in contrast, NCOA4 OE promotes ferroptosis in IECs.

NCOA4 mediates ferritinophagy in IECs

Ferritinophagy influences intracellular Fe2+ storage by degrading ferritin, thus playing a role in ferroptosis. NCOA4, a crucial factor in mediating ferritinophagy, has been proven to be associated with the onset of colitis. In this study, we conducted both in vivo and in vitro experiments to observe the effect of NCOA4 on ferritinophagy in IECs after DSS stimulation. After modeling, compared to the BC, the expression levels of NCOA4, LC3, and ATG5 were upregulated, and the expression of FTH1 was downregulated in the MG and LV NC (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A-H). There were no significant differences between the MG and LV NC (P > 0.05). Compared to the MG, cells in the LV NCOA4 OE group demonstrated elevated expression of NCOA4, LC3, and ATG5 and reduced expression of FTH1; cells in the LV NCOA4 KD group showed decreased expression of NCOA4, LC3, and ATG5 proteins and increased FTH1 protein expression (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A-H). The in vitro experiment findings suggest that NCOA4 modulates the activity of ferritinophagy and ferrous ions.

NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy is a potential target in repairing the mucosa and treating colitis

The above results imply that inhibiting autophagy is a crucial mechanism in improving ferroptosis in IECs and inflammatory injuries in colitis. Given the premise that NCOA4 regulates ferritinophagy, we further observed the effects of NCOA4 on colonic mucosal injuries in DSS-induced colitis models. The experiment revealed that compared to the NG, colitis mouse models had diarrhea and hematochezia symptoms, increased DAI and CMDI, shortened colons, significantly damaged colonic tissues manifesting as ulcerations and inflammatory responses, increased pathological scores, and elevated serum DAO and D-LA contents (P < 0.05; Fig. 7A-H). Compared to the MG, mice in the AAV NCOA4 OE group showed worsened general symptoms, elevated DAI and CMDI, more damaged mucosa, and worse inflammation under the optical microscope, increased pathological scores, and higher serum contents of DAO and D-LA (P < 0.05; Fig. 7A-H); mice in the AAV NCOA4 KD group demonstrated improved colonic injuries and inflammatory responses, reduced DAI, CMDI, and pathological scores, and decreased serum DAO and D-LA contents (P < 0.05; Fig. 7A-H). The findings indicate that inhibiting the expression of NCOA4 mitigates colonic inflammatory injuries and mucosal permeability in colitis mice, and overexpressing NCOA4 aggravates colonic lesions.

Discussion

With the industrialization worldwide, IBD has gradually become a common digestive disorder. A joinpoint regression and age-period-cohort analysis based on The Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2019 reported that the age-standardized incidence of IBD over the past 30 years in China rose from 1.47 to 3.01 per 100,000 people [4]. It’s well recognized that the onset of IBD results from the interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental influences on intestinal microbiota, and the pathogenesis includes intestinal barrier dysfunction mediating abnormal activation of intestinal immunity. It involves multiple causal factors and thus is considered a refractory condition [34]. The recurrent flare of IBD significantly hinders the patient’s quality of life and also raises the risk of developing colitis-associated cancer [35, 36]. Although China has a relatively lower incidence compared to European countries and the USA, the financial cost and medical burden for the families and society will be substantial if its rise is out of control. Therefore, it’s always been the focus of research on IBD to discover its clinical risk factors and seek effective intervention targets. This study demonstrates that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy participates in the onset of colitis by inducing ferroptosis in IECs, and regulating ferritinophagy can improve colonic inflammatory injuries and mucosal permeability. The findings provide a potential target for the treatment of colitis.

IECs mainly consist of intestinal epithelial cells, goblet cells, enteroendocrine cells, and Paneth cells, serving as the primary cellular components of the intestinal mucosal barrier [34]. Multiple factors can cause abnormal deaths of IECs and damage to the mucosal barrier, leading to the abnormal activation of intestinal immunity, which is the fundamental pathological mechanism of the onset and progression of colitis [37, 38]. Current diagnosis and treatment consensus proposes that the target of treating colitis should focus on inducing and maintaining clinical remission and mucosal healing, preventing complications, and improving the quality of life [39, 40]. Previous research showed that drugs that correct internal immune system dysfunction and replenish nutrients to improve the external environment contributed to alleviating colitis and clinical symptoms, indicating that intestinal barrier integrity and epithelial damage are two crucial targets [41]. Hence, inhibiting the death of IECs is a key premise to alleviate mucosal injuries and promote healing. Research has discovered that IEC ferroptosis is prevalent in colitis patients and models [42, 43]. Ferroptosis in IECs can activate immune-inflammatory responses in the mucosa via various pathways, thus playing a role in the onset and persistence of colitis [13, 14]. Ferroptosis is driven by the lethal accumulation of lipid peroxides catalyzed by cellular free irons. Many lipid peroxidation-derived electrophiles, such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), MDA, and acrolein, increase inflammatory responses, induce DNA damage, and inhibit DNA repair, which contributes to the damage of the intestinal barrier and epithelial cells [44, 45]. Therefore, suppressing ferroptosis can mitigate colonic inflammatory reactions and mucosal injuries [46]. The current study demonstrated elevated ferroptosis in IECs in DSS-induced inflammatory cell models and colitis models, presenting as increased Fe2+, ROS, and MDA contents, downregulated GPX4 protein expression, and upregulated expression of inflammatory factor COX2. After the intervention with ferroptosis inhibitor Lip-1, cellular ferroptosis was notably suppressed, together with alleviated colonic injuries, inflammatory responses, and mucosal permeability, indicating that inhibiting ferroptosis can help mitigate colonic inflammatory injuries and improve mucosal permeability in colitis models, consistent with previous reports [47].

Ferroptosis is an autophagy-dependent cell death. Autophagy modulation exists in multiple metabolic pathways associated with ferroptosis. This inspires us to discover if autophagy is linked to colitis-associated ferroptosis in IECs. Therefore, we treated DSS-induced colitis models with the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA in this study, finding that this group of rats showed reduced colonic ferroptosis, as well as decreased colonic contents of Fe2+, ROS, and MDA, upregulated GPX4 protein expression, downregulated COX2 expression, and repaired pathological features of plasma membranes and mitochondria in IECs, similar to the effects of ferroptosis inhibitor. The results indicate that inhibiting autophagy can alleviate colonic ferroptosis in colitis models. However, as an inhibitor of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and a broad-spectrum autophagy inhibitor, 3-MA influences the formation of autophagosomes via class III PI3K, but further evidence is required to reveal which autophagosomes play a role in the process of ferroptosis.

The mechanism of ferroptosis is highly complex, primarily involving amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, and iron ion metabolism. Among these pathways, aberrant iron metabolism-mediated iron overload can trigger ferroptosis in IECs and subsequent inflammation, which is one of the pathological mechanisms of colitis and may be closely associated with the development of colitis-related cancer (CRC) in later stages [48]. In-depth research on the modulation of ferroptosis in IECs may uncover potential therapeutic strategies for colitis and associated tumors from the perspective of iron metabolism. NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy serves as an important pathway for regulating intracellular Fe2+ storage. NCOA4 degrades FTH1 via the autophagy pathway, releasing Fe2+ and subsequently boosting the Fenton reaction and the production of ROS, which damages plasma membranes and mitochondria, finally leading to ferroptosis. Novel studies have found that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy plays a role in ferroptosis in IECs induced by radiation and ischemia [49, 50]. However, whether this pathway is involved in the regulation of colitis-associated IEC ferroptosis remains to be investigated.

As a selective autophagy receptor, NCOA4 is a critical protein mediating ferritinophagy. High-throughput microarray revealed differential expression of NCOA4 and FTH1 mRNAs in the mucosal tissues of ulcerative colitis patients, possibly correlating with the disease [11]. Previous research has confirmed that ionizing radiation-induced ferroptosis in IECs depends on NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy [49]. To clarify if NCOA4 exerts regulatory effects on IEC ferritinophagy and subsequent ferroptosis in DSS-induced colitis, we first established inflammatory cell and colitis models using DSS to observe the change in the activation of ferritinophagy. The results showed that compared to the normal group, the model group demonstrated a notable increase in the expression of IEC ferritinophagy-associated proteins NCOA4, ATG5, and LC3 and a decrease in the expression of FTH1, consistent with previous reports [11, 51]. We then adopted lentiviral and adenoviral transfection techniques to overexpress and knock down NCOA4 in NCM460 cells and primary IECs. Under NCOA4 OE, DSS-induced models showed markedly elevated LC3 and ATG5 expression in both cell types, accompanied by increased levels of ferroptosis markers Fe2+, MDA, and ROS and decreased GPX4 expression. Conversely, NCOA4 KD produced opposing effects. Previous studies imply that 4-OI could induce tumor ferroptosis and exert anti-inflammatory effects [27, 52]. Hence, we adopted 4-OI as a ferritinophagy agonist to intervene in DSS-induced colitis models, aiming to observe the impact of ferritinophagy on colonic ferroptosis. However, the results indicated insignificant influence of 4-OI on key ferritinophagy proteins or ferroptosis markers in colitis models, possibly due to differences in disease models. This study, from both in vivo and in vitro perspectives, demonstrates that modulating NCOA4 and its mediated ferritinophagy represents one of the pathways to alleviate colitis-associated ferroptosis in IECs, which is in line with the previous report [53]. In contrast, our research used human intestinal epithelial cell NCM460 to clarify the regulatory mechanism of ferritinophagy and then adopted knockdown or overexpression of NCOA4 in vivo to further verify it.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that aberrant activation of ferritinophagy mediated by NCOA4 serves as a crucial mechanism driving ferroptosis in IECs and subsequent onset of colitis, and regulating NCOA4 can alleviate colonic inflammatory injuries and mucosal permeability (Fig. 8). However, the study has certain limitations. The mechanisms underlying colitis-associated IEC ferroptosis are rather complex, plausibly involving multiple pathways. A key question remains whether ferritinophagy-involved iron metabolism interplays with other metabolic pathways, such as amino acid and lipid metabolism. Moreover, this study has provided evidence on the regulatory effects of ferritinophagy on colitis-related ferroptosis, but further studies are needed to seek effective interventions or drugs. Additionally, investigating the role of iron overload and ferritinophagy regulation in colitis-associated tumors may provide novel therapeutic insights from the perspective of colonic iron metabolism.
