COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and intentions among parents of children with mental and behavioral disorders
Authors: Mohamed Adil Shah Khoodoruth, Sami Ouanes, Kristian Somintac, Shaima Gulistan, Asieh Dehwari, Widaad Nuzhah Chut-Kai Khoodoruth, Mohammed Nasser Alamri, Majid Alabdulla, Yasser Saeed Khan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParent vaccine hesitancy (PVH) remains a significant public health concern, particularly emphasized during the COVID-19 pandemic. This study investigates PVH among parents of children with various psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions, such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety disorders, and behavioral disturbances, extending previous research focused on autism spectrum disorder (ASD). We examined PVH in children aged 5–11 years with psychiatric and behavioral disorders excluding ASD. A cross-sectional questionnaire-based analysis was conducted in Qatar, involving 244 parents of neurodiverse children and 245 parents of neurotypical children as controls. The Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV) survey assessed PVH. Results indicated a significantly higher prevalence of vaccine hesitancy among parents of children with psychiatric disorders (54.1%, 95% CI [47.8%, 60.4%], n = 132) compared to controls (11.7%, 95% CI [7.5%, 16.0%], n = 26), p < .001. The PACV total score was significantly higher in parents of children with a mental disorder. Higher education among parents and lower PACV scores significantly influenced the intent to vaccinate against COVID-19. Tailored interventions by child and adolescent mental healthcare professionals, such as harnessing artificial intelligence to fight disinformation, are crucial to addressing vaccine hesitancy, enhancing acceptance, and improving outcomes for children with psychiatric disorders.KEYWORDS: Parent vaccine hesitancy, COVID-19 vaccination, child and adolescent psychiatry, psychiatric disorders, neurodiversity

Introduction

Despite a well-established safety profile, rigorous approval processes, and ongoing surveillance of adverse events, parental skepticism toward vaccines remains a prevalent issue, especially highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic.1 Vaccine hesitancy is important to address not only because it increases the risk of infection but also because it erodes public trust in health systems, weakens herd immunity, and is often fueled by misinformation, which can lead to larger societal consequences such as outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases and a strain on healthcare resources.2 Our prior research has indicated that such vaccine hesitancy among parents (PVH) affects vaccination rates similarly among children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and their neurotypical counterparts, underscoring the broad impact of PVH across diverse child populations.3 The urgency of addressing PVH has gained additional weight, considering the heightened COVID-19 mortality rates among individuals with mental illnesses, which can be attributed to both their increased comorbidities and the profound social disruptions caused by the pandemic.4 These findings underscore the critical vulnerability of this demographic, further complicated by hesitancy toward vaccinations.

As of May 2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) officially declared that COVID-19 no longer constitutes a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC), transitioning to a post-pandemic phase where the virus is expected to remain a persistent, yet manageable global health challenge.5 However, while the emergency phase has ended, the pandemic continues as a global health threat, with the same WHO report emphasizing the need for ongoing public health measures, vaccination efforts, and surveillance to control transmission and prevent severe disease.5 Furthermore, the emergence of other public health emergencies, such as the global mpox outbreak, highlights the ongoing need for research into vaccine hesitancy and its implications, as new infectious threats continue to arise even as one pandemic concludes.6 This situation underscores the importance of developing targeted strategies to address vaccine hesitancy in a rapidly evolving global health landscape.

This study aims to extend these findings by examining PVH in a broader range of mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions beyond ASD, such as anxiety disorders, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and behavioral disturbances, which have not been as extensively studied in the context of vaccine hesitancy. This area is important since the factors influencing PVH may differ substantially based on the psychiatric or behavioral condition, cultural perceptions of the condition, and the associated social stigma. Addressing potential gaps in this area will enable the development of targeted interventions to address the specific concerns and barriers faced by our neurodiverse groups.

Methods

This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board at Hamad Medical Corporation (MRC-01-22-126). Informed consent was obtained from participating parents/legal guardians. The methodology employed in this study follows the detailed protocols and procedures described in our earlier work.3 Briefly, this study, a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based analysis conducted in Qatar from March 2023 to January 2024, focused on the vaccination attitudes among parents of neurodiverse children with psychiatric disorders other than ASD aged 5–11 years. The survey used was the Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV), which identifies vaccine hesitancy and predicts under-immunization.7 The details of the scoring system used for the PACV questionnaire were previously detailed.3 The sample included 244 parents from an initial pool of 306, with a response rate of 79.7%. Participants were recruited from the Child and Adolescent Mental Health Service (CAMHS) of Hamad Medical Corporation (HMC). The multidisciplinary CAMHS receives referrals from multiple sources, including primary care, schools, and general hospitals.8 Parents or guardians of neurotypical children (controls) aged 5–11 years were recruited at Qatar’s Pediatric Emergency Center, which serves patients under 14 and acts as a central hub for other such centers nationwide.

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS v26 for Windows. We followed the same statistical analysis methods as in our previous study.3 We compared percentages using Pearson’s Chi-square and Fisher’s Exact Test in case of non-validity. Comparisons of two means were made using the t-test for independent variables. Comparisons of more than two means were made using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). To compare PACV total scores between parents of children with mental illness other than ASD and parents of controls (children with no psychiatric disorder), we conducted an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with the PACV total score as a dependent variable, the diagnostic group (mental disorder vs control) as a fixed factor, and child’s sex, child’s age, being a first child, responder’s relationship to the child, parent’s citizenship status, parent’s educational level, and parent’s marital status as covariates. Homogeneity of variances was verified using Leven’s test. Effect size was estimated using partial Eta squares (η2). To assess the relationship between the intent to vaccinate one’s child against COVID-19 and PACV score, we built a binary logistic regression model with the intent to vaccinate (item 20 dichotomized as “1” for “Yes” and “0” for “No” or “Not sure”) as the dependent variable, and the following variables as independent variables: PACV total score, group (mental disorder vs controls), child’s sex, child’s age, being a first child, responder’s relationship to the child, parent’s citizenship status, parent’s educational level, and parent’s marital status. We calculated the odds ratio and its 95% confidence interval. For both the ANCOVA analysis and the binary logistic regression, independent variables were selected based on the PACV questionnaire and on empirical evidence. Multicollinearity was ruled out since the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was less than 10. For all analyses, we used an alpha value of 0.05.

Results

We included 244 parents of children with a psychiatric disorder other than ASD and 245 parents of controls (children without any psychiatric disorder) in the study. The most common psychiatric disorders were ADHD (52.5%, n = 128), behavioral issues (15.6%, n = 38), and anxiety disorders (13.1%, n = 32). Children with a psychiatric disorder were significantly older than controls (9.1 ± 1.5 years vs. 8 ± 1.8, p < .001) and were more likely to be male (69.4%, n = 159 vs 55%, n = 133, p < .001). There was also a significantly higher proportion of Qatari nationals among parents of children with a psychiatric disorder (9.4%, n = 23 vs. 2.9%, n = 7, p < .001), as well as a higher proportion of divorce (7%, n = 17 vs. 2.9%, n = 7, p = .032). Other sociodemographic characteristics did not differ between parents of patients with a psychiatric disorder and parents of controls (Table 1).

Around one-quarter of children had been vaccinated against COVID-19 at the time of the study, with no difference between groups. In both groups, around one-third of the parents stated that the main reason for this vaccination was that it was mandatory for certain concessions (travel or entrance to leisure places).

Answers to the PACV questionnaire are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, parents of children with mental disorders, compared to parents of neurotypical children, exhibit higher vaccine hesitancy and greater concern about vaccine safety and side effects but similar levels of trust in their child’s doctor.

A significantly higher prevalence of vaccine hesitancy was observed among parents of children with psychiatric disorders compared to controls. Specifically, the prevalence of vaccine hesitancy (defined by a total PACV score > 50) was 54.1% (n = 132) [47.8%; 60.4%] in the psychiatric disorder group versus 11.7% (n = 26) [7.5%; 16.0%] in the control group, p < .001.

Parents of children with mental disorders (excluding ASD) had significantly higher PACV scores (51.7 ± 9.9) compared to parents of controls (40.8 ± 9.6), with p < .001 (Table 2). Even after controlling for covariates (child’s sex, child’s age, being a first child, responder’s relationship to the child, parent’s citizenship status, parent’s educational level, and parent’s marital status), ANCOVA analysis showed that PACV score was still higher in parents of children with a psychiatric disorder, with a high effect size (p < .001; partial eta squared = 0.201). In addition, mothers, Qatari parents, and parents with higher education also had higher PACV scores (Table 3).

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that while a quarter of children in both groups were vaccinated against COVID-19, vaccine hesitancy remains significantly higher among parents of children with mental and behavioral disorders other than ASD (54.1%) compared to parents of neurotypical children (11.7%). Additionally, parental educational level and lower PACV scores were significant predictors of a higher intent to vaccinate against COVID-19, indicating that more educated parents and those with lower hesitancy scores are more likely to vaccinate their children.

The differences in PVH between parents of children with ASD reported at 18% in our previous study and those of children with other psychiatric disorders, at 54%, can be attributed to several factors. The level of social support and community engagement among parents of children with ASD is often more robust due to more established advocacy and support networks. For example, organizations like Autism Speaks provide extensive resources and support through community groups and schools, empowering parents with information and enhancing their engagement with the health needs of their children, including vaccination.9,10 This can lead to better dissemination of vaccine-related information and encouragement to vaccinate, unlike parents of children with less common or more stigmatized disorders. Moreover, parents of children with psychiatric disorders other than ASD may perceive a higher risk associated with vaccinations due to potential interactions with medications or the perceived fragility of their child’s health. These parents might also feel their children are more vulnerable to adverse events of vaccines. Concerns about adverse effects are a prominent reason for vaccine hesitancy among parents,11 which could be exacerbated in the context of psychiatric conditions.

Our study, which found that 42% of parents of neurotypical children and a significantly lower 14.1% of parents of children with psychiatric issues intend to vaccinate their children against COVID-19, highlights an important dimension of vaccine hesitancy. These results align with the broader trends seen in the cross-sectional analysis from five Arab countries, where 56% of parents expressed intentions to vaccinate their children against COVID-19, driven by concerns about side effects, efficacy, safety, and logistical barriers.12 However, the disparity in vaccination intent between parents of neurotypical children and those of children with psychiatric conditions highlights an additional layer of complexity in vaccine hesitancy among parents of vulnerable groups. This reduced vaccination intent could exacerbate health inequities, leaving children with psychiatric conditions further at risk during the pandemic. From the Health Belief Model (HBM) perspective,13 parents of children with psychiatric conditions likely perceive greater risks and barriers associated with vaccinating their children. This includes concerns about potential side effects and interactions with psychiatric treatments, which may overshadow perceived benefits. Additionally, these parents may receive weaker cues to action, such as recommendations from healthcare providers, and perceive lower susceptibility or severity of COVID-19 for their children.

Our analysis identified two significant predictors of the intent to vaccinate children against COVID-19: the parent’s educational level and the PACV total score. While parents with higher education may exhibit higher levels of hesitancy (as reflected by higher PACV scores), mainly because they might be more likely to be exposed to anti-vaccine material, they might also feel a stronger sense of urgency or perceived need to protect their child through vaccination due to their better health literacy, better access to information and a higher understanding of the benefits and risks of vaccination.14 However, this trend is not universal; for instance, in Malaysia, highly educated parents showed higher vaccine hesitancy for their children aged 5–11, perceiving the COVID-19 vaccine as unsafe and ineffective.15 In contrast, higher PACV scores, reflecting greater vaccine hesitancy, significantly reduced the intent to vaccinate, highlighting the impact of vaccine attitudes on vaccination decisions.16

The largest proportion of parents who changed their minds about delaying or not getting a vaccination for their child cited “information or assurances from a health care provider” as the main reason, highlighting the critical role of pediatricians as credible sources of vaccine recommendations.17 We also believe that child and adolescent mental health professionals have an essential role in maintaining a trusting, non-confrontational dialogue with concerned parents. Mental health professionals can address specific concerns related to the impact of vaccines on child mental health, such as fears about vaccine-related neurological effects or the exacerbation of existing psychiatric conditions. They can provide evidence-based information and psychoeducation to dispel myths and alleviate fears, which is crucial in overcoming vaccine hesitancy.18 Additionally, they can guide parents through their vaccination decision-making by addressing emotional and cognitive concerns, fostering informed choices, and collaborating with pediatricians to ensure consistent and credible messaging.16,17 Child and adolescent mental health professionals can curb misinformation by actively engaging in public forums and social media, correcting false information promptly, and utilizing innovative approaches such as artificial intelligence (AI) tools and educational games to combat disinformation.19 By engaging in these roles, child and adolescent mental health professionals can significantly impact vaccination decisions, ultimately contributing to better health outcomes for children and the wider community.

This study on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among parents of children with mental and behavioral disorders offers valuable insights by focusing on a population often overlooked in vaccine research, including conditions like ADHD and anxiety disorders. Strengths include the use of a validated survey (PACV) and a comparison with neurotypical controls. However, the cross-sectional design limits causality and the study’s focus on Qatar may hinder generalizability to other regions. Additionally, potential response bias and reliance on self-reported data pose limitations.20 Despite these limitations, the study emphasizes the need for targeted interventions to reduce PVH in vulnerable populations.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant challenge of vaccine hesitancy among parents of children with mental and behavioral disorders, where over half express hesitancy compared to a much lower rate in neurotypical children. Education and parental attitudes strongly influence the decision to vaccinate, underscoring the need for targeted, informed interventions. Child and adolescent mental health professionals play a crucial role in addressing these concerns. By fostering trust, providing clear, evidence-based guidance, and utilizing innovative tools like AI to combat misinformation, they can help reduce hesitancy and ensure vulnerable children receive necessary protection. Ultimately, this research calls for focused efforts to develop strategies that support parents in making informed vaccine decisions, ensuring better health outcomes for all children.
