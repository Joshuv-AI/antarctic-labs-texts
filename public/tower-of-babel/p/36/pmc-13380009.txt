Organoid-loaded core-shell cryomicroneedles induce biomimetic follicular units and hair regeneration
Authors: Ling Xiao, Peng Chen, Yunfan Tang, Ying Chen, Ze Zhang, Peng Zheng, Xiaoqiang Liu, Luojia Liu, Xu Chen, Lufan Xia, Ning Ma, Shi-Cang Yu, Jiaping Zhang, Feifei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTissue-engineered hair follicle regeneration offers a promising method for increasing hair follicle count, yet it faces challenges such as difficulty with hair emergence through the skin and the potential for clustered and disordered growth. Achieving biomimetic regeneration of single or double hair follicular units is therefore essential. Microneedle arrays (MNs) can mimic natural hair density, enabling controlled hair regeneration in direction and density. However, most MNs for hair regeneration are designed for drug delivery, which can only modulate the hair cycle of existing follicles and cannot generate de novo hair follicles in bald areas. MNs designed for tissue-engineered hair follicle delivery are rare and face challenges in sustaining cellular vitality and achieving epidermal breakthrough. Thus, a novel approach based on core-shell MN technology and hair follicle organoids (HFOs) culture was developed for hair follicle unit regeneration. In this study, degradable core-shell cryomicroneedles (CryoMNs) with uniform walls and multiple small channels were loaded with HFOs. When applied to nude mice, the core-shell CryoMNs supported biomimetic follicular unit growth, mimicking natural hair regeneration. Controllably degradable core-shell CryoMNs offer nutrient channels for HFOs and cutaneous channels for hair growth, controlling hair growth direction and spacing, and enabling orderly follicular unit regeneration in patients with baldness. This study introduces novel ideas and strategies for the subcutaneous delivery of organoids and the systematic regeneration of tissue-engineered hair follicles.Keywords: MNs, Hair regeneration, HFOs, HFPCs, PLGA

Introduction

Hair loss has emerged as a significant global health concern, affecting approximately 50% of adults worldwide, a prevalence that continues to rise steadily [1]. Current standard interventions primarily comprise pharmaceutical therapies [2,3] and surgical hair transplantation [4]. However, these approaches neither increase the total number of hair follicles nor are they suitable for patients with extensive alopecia. In contrast, tissue-engineered hair follicle regeneration [[5], [6], [7], [8]] offers the unique potential to expand the total follicular pool. Among emerging strategies, medical hair regeneration leveraging hair follicle progenitor cells (HFPCs) to culture organoids represents a particularly promising therapeutic avenue [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. The principal clinical methods for delivering in vitro-cultured HFPCs include subcutaneous injection [13] and the chamber-based implantation technique [14]. However, subcutaneous injection frequently results in intradermal hair clustering without epidermal emergence; conversely, the chamber method inevitably induces additional surgical trauma and scarring. Moreover, both approaches commonly lead to localised hair accumulation and uncontrollable hair growth orientation—failing to satisfy aesthetic requirements for natural-looking hair restoration and thereby posing substantial challenges to clinical translation.

To achieve effective skin penetration and appropriate hair follicle density, microneedle arrays (MNs) represent a promising approach [15]. MNs enable painless skin piercing while offering adjustable direction and spacing to match endogenous hair density—features essential for replicating natural follicular unit patterns [16,17]. Although MNs have been extensively utilised in drug delivery [18,19], devices specifically engineered for living cell delivery remain scarce. Recently, Chang [20] developed CryoMNs, which currently represents the first MN suitable for delivering living cells. In contrast, other cell-loaded MNs primarily rely on the release of small molecular proteins from cells, which have minimal requirements for cell viability [21]. Fortunately, MNs have recently been extended to deliver organoids for tissue regeneration [22]. Zheng [23] employed this technology to encapsulate HFPCs within a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel, successfully loaded CryoMNs with HFPCs and observed black hair growth within 14 days. However, the hair tends to grow in clusters beneath the skin, rather than emerging as single or double roots. This is due to the absence of formed hair follicle organoids (HFOs), which necessitate a low-adhesion surface and ample growth space, while the GelMA hydrogel is currently unable to provide. Moreover, rapidly degradable hydrogels fail to prevent skin retraction and closure, thereby precluding hair from erupting through the skin surface. Therefore, developing slow-degrading MN shells is essential to assist cells in breaching the epidermal barrier and emerging at the surface. Lee [24] described a poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) shell with a 2-week degradation cycle, aligning closely with the natural hair growth cycle for effective epidermal breakthrough. Besides, among biodegradable polymers, PLGA exhibits high mechanical strength, providing excellent skin penetration performance as a MN shell. However, the fully enclosed PLGA shell cannot supply nutrients to the cells within, necessitating the incorporation of channels into the shell to facilitate nutrient absorption from surrounding tissues.

The design of the MN shell is pivotal, yet the significance of its contents cannot be overstated. MNs loaded with HFPCs not only face challenges in forming hair follicle tissue but also result in hair growth that is clustered rather than being uniformly distributed in the pattern of hair follicular units. However, when HFPCs are cultured three-dimensionally to form HFOs, the efficiency of hair induction is significantly enhanced, and the growth of follicular units can be induced [25,26]. Researchers have recently succeeded in culturing HFOs with 100% sprouting rates using Type I collagen or Matrigel [27,28]. However, culturing HFOs within PLGA MNs is highly challenging due to the significant reduction in PLGA's hardness upon contact with water. CryoMNs can transform water-containing soft components into high-hardness solid components while maintaining the viability of the encapsulated substances, ensuring efficient transdermal delivery of various active substances [[29], [30], [31]]. Our research employs CryoMNs technology to HFOs cultured within MN moulds, forming HFO-loaded cryomicroneedles (HFO-CryoMNs). These are subsequently integrated as a core into the hollow, multi-channel PLGA MN, thereby fabricating the core-shell structured PLGA@hair follicle organoids cryomicroneedles (PLGA@HFO CryoMNs). This method ensures the intact subcutaneous delivery of HFOs without compromising the MNs' penetration capabilities, facilitating in situ hair regeneration.

Results and discussion

Design of core-shell CryoMNs with uniform wall thickness and multi-channels for loading HFOs

Dermal papilla cells (DPCs), a specialised type of fibroblast, are situated at the base of hair follicles, playing an important role in the process of hair follicle regeneration [32]. During hair follicle development, DPCs gradually aggregate to form dermal papilla spheres (DPSs) [33]. To design MNs that fully accommodate DPSs for hair tissue development, DPSs diameters from adult C57BL/6 mice was measured. Whisker pad tissue was clipped, follicular units was isolated, and DPSs were extracted after collagenase I digestion (Fig. 1a). The isolated follicular units (Fig. 1b and c) and individual DPSs (Fig. 1d and e) were shown. DPCs progressively migrated from DPSs, with healthy morphology, high viability, and structural integrity (Fig. 1f). Furthermore, DPSs were extracted for diameter analysis, with five samples each containing about 100 cell masses, which shows a 52–280 μm range and an average of 109 ± 33 μm (Fig. 1g). Unlike the maximum diameter of mouse DPSs reported in other literature that is about 50 μm [34], the adult C57 mouse tentacles collected in this study better simulate the range of human DPSs. The research results are also consistent with the previously reported range of human DPSs (100-450 μm) [35]. To ensure DPCs viability and accommodate larger HFOs, the MN structure was engineered with an inner diameter of 500 μm.

Core-shell MNs offer a novel approach for skin penetration, protecting therapeutics during transdermal delivery and enabling controlled release at the site of application [36,37]. However, conventional core-shell MNs, typically fabricated by layer stacking, often have non-uniform shell thicknesses, which hinder the accurate estimation of encapsulated cell numbers. To address these limitations, two complementary MN moulds and caps were developed (Fig. 1h and i). Traditional core-shell MNs, though effective for cell encapsulation and delivery, often fail to maintain cell viability, necessitating integration of a porous shell design that allows nutrient permeability and cell survival, with pore sizes carefully calibrated for selective nutrient diffusion while retaining cells [24]. Considering the DPS's diameter, we tailored the MN pore sizes to a range of 50-70 μm to ensure selective nutrient passage (Fig. 1h). Furthermore, to enhance material exchange, inspired by the design of perforated MNs [38], eight channels were incorporated into the design of each MN. Adopting a combined cylindrical and conical MN structure, inspired by the base of a 96-well plate, we enhanced the encapsulation of HFPCs and improved culture conditions for HFOs, overcoming limitations of traditional conical MNs in cell encapsulation and sphere formation (Fig. 1i).

Design and fabrication of MNs for natural hair growth mimicry and ordered hair follicle unit regeneration

After establishing the size and structure of the MN moulds, the MN array design was then optimized. A pivotal aspect of our research involved designing MN arrays with density, depth, and orientation that closely mimicked the natural hair growth pattern. Literature reports indicate that the density of human scalp hair ranges from 100 to 300 hairs/cm2 [39], varying significantly by ethnicity, with East Asians and Africans exhibiting lower densities compared to Caucasians [4]. However, in hair transplantation procedures, the density of hair implantation is 65-85 FUs per square centimeter because the transplanted follicular units usually contain 1-4 hairs [4,40]. Therefore, we designed MNs in a 10×10 array with a spacing of 1150 μm (Fig. 2a and b), covering a total area of 1.44 cm2, corresponding to an implantation density of 100 FUs/1.44 cm2 = 69 FUs/cm2. Notably, the reported MN-based hair grafting approaches often exhibit either overly sparse (approximately 20 FUs/cm2) [41] or excessively dense (approximately 3000 FUs/cm2) seeding densities [42], both of which deviate substantially from the human physiological range. In contrast, our design offering a more clinically relevant and biomimetic strategy for hair regeneration. Furthermore, the general thickness of the epidermis ranges from 0.2 to 1.4 mm, while the dermis ranges from 0.4 to 2.4 mm [43]. The skin of nude mice is relatively thinner compared to human skin but lack of definitive experimental data to substantiate its specific thickness [44]. Considering that hair growth typically originates from the deep dermis and is arranged in parallel, and in conjunction with the design scheme of CryoMNs [20], a parallel array of MNs with a depth of 1300 μm was designed.

As shown in Fig. 2c, the MN moulds were fabricated with a 10×10 array of MN cavities, each exhibiting distinct and regular columnar structures. Each cavity wall, featuring eight columns, was designed to form MN channels. As shown in Fig. 2d, the MN caps were fabricated with a 10×10 array and exhibited a circular shape when viewed from above; these caps were smaller than the cavities in the MN moulds and matched them in arrangement. As depicted in Fig. 2e, the biodegradable material PLGA was cast within the MN moulds, and the MN arrays were formed by pressurisation with MN caps. Following demoulding from both moulds, MN shells (PLGA MNs) with a multi-channel structure were achieved. As shown in Fig. S1, the MN moulds were fabricated from PDMS, while the MN caps were constructed from copper. Additionally, to prevent chemical reactions that could hinder demoulding and to conserve materials, a PDMS cap was developed. Fig. S2a–f illustrates our designs for milky white PDMS moulds for MN shell fabrication and transparent PDMS moulds designed for cell culture applications. Notably, Fig. S2c, f demonstrates the precise alignment between the MN moulds and caps, ensuring that the MN shell preparation requirements were satisfied.

Characterization of multi-channel PLGA MNs for loading HFOs

PLGA MN were fabricated using a novel double-layer sandwich technique, yielding uniform, thin-walled, opaque white structures with cylindrical and conical profiles (Fig. 3a and b). Channels encircle the cylindrical portion and partially surround the conical portion. Crucially, the base is sealed to contain cells and prevent leakage. Microscopy and scanning electron microscope (SEM) images disclosed the symmetrical layout of eight interconnected channels around the MN shell, with widths increasing from the internal to the external surface (Fig. 3c and d). The diameters of 50 pores were measured randomly, yielding an average diameter of 57.38 ± 5.72 μm. Confocal microscopy vividly depicted the distinct morphologies of conical and cylindrical segments at various levels. From the top-down view in Fig. 3e, the MN shell's uniformity is apparent across both conical and cylindrical segments. The conical segment's shell is mostly enclosed, with an interconnecting channel structure near its start, featuring eight notches on the outer wall (Video S1). In the lateral view, the cylindrical segment displays a clear channel structure, in contrast to the conical segment, which has channels only at cylinder connections (Video S2). Unlike other core-shell MNs [45,46], our MN shells are carefully separated to ensure uniformity in thickness, which is essential for reserving ample space for the loading of HFOs. Additionally, the continuous channels that span shell interior to exterior, enabling nutrient acquisition from surrounding tissues and preparing HFOs for in situ growth and development.

The compressive performance of the prepared MNs was assessed using a universal material testing machine (Fig. 3f). As illustrated in Fig. 3g and h, the PLGA MN exhibited significant rigidity, with a fracture force of 1.3N for a single MN, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [24]. However, PLGA MNs experienced a significant drop after being loaded with culture medium for only 5 min, with a fracture force to 0.33N, with a further decrease observed over time, suggesting that the hardness of cells directly loaded with PLGA MN is significantly reduced. Interestingly, loading the PLGA MN with CryoMNs did not affect its rigidity. This can be attributed to the CryoMNs' resistance to immediate melting upon loading and their inherent rigidity before thawing. Subsequently, the permeability of small molecules through the PLGA MN was evaluated, with the base being submerged in a growth factor solution and the peptide chain concentration periodically measured using a spectrophotometer (Fig. 3i). As shown in Fig. 3j, the peptide chain concentration in the solution rose initially, then declined. PLGA, a copolymer of lactic acid, glycolic acid, and polyethylene glycol [47], initially increased peptide concentration due to partial dissolution. After day one, as PLGA dissolution stabilized, small molecules, including growth factors, permeated through the MN channels, leading to a decrease in solution peptide concentration.

Then, the in vitro penetration characteristics of the PLGA MN were evaluated. Through artificial membrane puncture experiments, the penetration attributes of pure PLGA MNs were compared to those of CryoMNs-loaded PLGA MNs. As shown in Fig. 3k, PLGA MNs loaded with CryoMNs demonstrated superior penetration depths, with a maximum reach of 766 ± 8 μm, exceeding both the unmodified shells (508 ± 15 μm) and most biodegradable MN systems (200-600 μm) [48]. In contrast to recently reported chaotrope-guided gelatin MNs that lacked quantitative depth data and likely remained in the upper dermis [49], our 766 μm penetration reaches the deep dermis, fully covering murine anagen follicles (500-700 μm) and approaching the human bulge region, thereby enabling broader applicability for dermal and systemic delivery. The degradation of the PLGA MN was analyzed by evaluating both weight loss and lactic acid concentration. Lactic acid was specifically monitored because it has been reported to activate hair follicle stem cells via Wnt/β-catenin and other signaling pathways [50], which may contribute to the regenerative effects of PLGA@HFO CryoMNs. The potential roles of other degradation products (glycolic acid and polyethylene glyco) will be explored in future studies. As shown in Fig. 3l, the MNs were immersed in culture medium supplemented with 1 mg/mL lipase and incubated at 37°C with shaking. Periodically, test tubes were filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, the solid material was weighed and the filtrate collected for lactic acid concentration analysis. As depicted in Fig. 3m, the MNs' weight progressively decreased over the initial 14-day period, reaching 24% of the initial weight by day 14. As shown in Fig. 3n, lactic acid concentration in the MNs rose steadily from day 0 to 14, reaching 4.44 mmol/L by day 14. However, due to the unknown ratios of lactic acid, glycolic acid, and polyethylene glycol in the terpolymer composition, we were unable to quantify the lactic acid concentration or determine the release percentage. Furthermore, literature suggests that hair growth after HFO transplantation in nude mice usually happens within 2 weeks [51], aligning with the 76.07 ± 2.27% PLGA degradation observed by day 14.

To better visualize the structure, we fabricated ethoxylated trimethylolpropane triacrylate (ETPTA) MN, leveraging the transparency of ETPTA to enhance visualisation [52]. Fig. S3 shows that the ETPTA MN also incorporated channels that span both the internal and external surfaces, similar to the PLGA MN. This design feature ensures continuity in functionality. Fig. S4 clearly demonstrates the transparency of the ETPTA MN, which facilitates the distinct visualisation of the MN pores and the internal nests intended for cell loading. The PLGA MN displayed an opaque, milky white appearance, hindering light transmission. Although the channels of the shell were discernible, the internal nests were not visible, remaining obscured from direct observation. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. S5, the 3D imaging revealed that the ETPTA MNs exhibited a uniform array, with each MN measuring approximately 1.3 mm in height. The dermal cells (DCs) loading efficacy for both MN types was assessed at room temperature. Cells were drawn into the MN's pores by vacuum extraction. As depicted in Fig. S6, the PLGA MNs exhibited continuous absorption of the medium, resulting in expansion and softening over the course of the experiment. While it was possible to deposit the cell suspension into the ETPTA MN holes, this procedure required multiple aspirations, resulting in significant cell loss. Moreover, since ETPTA MN cannot be degraded by tissue, it is not suitable for hair regeneration. Instead, hollow PLGA MNs was combined with CryoMNs protocol not only maintains the hardness of MNs, but also maintains live cell delivery.

Ultimately, the biocompatibility of the PLGA MN was evaluated using the CCK8 assay, which indicated that cell viability remained above 90% for DCs/ECs co-cultured with the PLGA MN over a 72-h period, suggesting no adverse effects on cell survival (Fig. S7a–b). To preliminarily assess the translational potential of our PLGA MN with human cells, we isolated human DPCs from scalp tissue and evaluated their viability after co-culture with PLGA MN. As shown in Fig. S7c, human DPCs could efficiently outgrow from DPSs and maintained typical fibroblast-like morphology after passaging. The CCK8 assay revealed that after 72 h of co-culture with PLGA MN, cell viability remained above 94% (Fig. S7d), indicating no significant cytotoxicity of the PLGA material or the fabrication process on human DPSs. These results suggest that the PLGA MN is biocompatible with both mouse and human HFPCs, supporting its potential for future clinical translation in hair regeneration therapy.

In vitro optimisation of three-dimensional (3D) HFOs culture conditions

Epithelial-mesenchymal interactions (EMIs) play a pivotal role in the development of hair follicles [5,53]. In vitro-extracted epithelial and dermal mesenchymal cells interact to facilitate the culture of HFPCs, enabling them to generate hair and form hair follicle germs (HFGs). Transplantation of cultured HFGs in vivo can effectively stimulate new hair growth [27]. Following established protocols, HFPCs were cultured to form HFOs and the culture conditions were optimized for enhanced outcomes. Based on previous literature [27,28], HFPCs were prepared at a dermal-to-epidermal cell ratio of 1:1. To determine the optimal initial cell concentration, HFOs were cultured at various cell densities (0–6400 cells/well) and monitored HFOs diameter as well as hair follicle germ and hair formation were monitored over a 14-day observation period. As shown in Fig. 4a, HFPCs aggregate to form HFOs by day 3, with HFGs forming when the cell quantity reaches ≥800 cells/well, and hair formation observable at ≥6400 cells/well. As shown in Fig. 4b, there is a direct correlation between the quantity of cells and the diameter of HFOs, culminating in the largest observed diameter of 282 μm. Consequently, to ensure stable HFG formation, we selected a quantity of 800 HFPCs per well for subsequent studies. Besides, two videos demonstrated the dynamic processes of HFPCs aggregating into spheroids and sprouting (Video S3 and Video S4).

Matrigel, by encapsulating stem cells in the form of a hydrogel, has been widely used for organoid culture across various tissue types. Recent advancements have enabled researchers to successfully culture epithelial and mesenchymal cells derived from newborn mice within 3D Matrigel scaffolds, resulting in HFOs that exhibit a perfect 100% hair bud formation rate [28]. Consequently, using the optimal cell concentration of 800 cells/well, HFOs culture with or without Matrigel were then compared to assess the benefit of Matrigel supplementation and HFOs diameter and hair formation were evaluated. As shown in Fig. 4c and d, HFOs derived from HFPCs and cultured in a Matrigel-supplemented medium demonstrate a significantly larger diameter compared to those cultured without Matrigel, with a maximum diameter reaching 345 μm. Notably, HFOs cultured in the presence of Matrigel exhibited longer hair growth by day 14.

Finally, to determine the appropriate Matrigel concentration for HFOs culture, different Matrigel concentrations were tested and their effects on HFOs formation were compared. As shown in Fig. 4e, Matrigel concentration significantly affected HFOs aggregation: at concentrations of 2% or less, HFPCs efficiently formed HFOs and HFGs. In contrast, at concentrations of 4% and 8%, HFPCs were entrapped within the Matrigel, hindering aggregation and preventing the formation of HFOs and hair follicles. Compared with 1% group, the 2% group had more buds and larger diameters after 14 days of incubation. This observation is consistent with previous literature reports [28]. Therefore, to optimise culture outcomes, 2% Matrigel was selected for all subsequent HFOs culture. The diameter range of HFO cultured with 800 cells/well and 2% Matrigel was ranged from 131 to 203 μm (Fig. 4f). The maximum HFOs diameter recorded was 345 μm (Fig. 4d), which was slightly smaller than the inner diameter of the MN moulds we designed. This indicates that the design of the MN moulds is reasonable. Due to the technical challenges of human hair follicle organoid culture [5], the current study primarily uses neonatal mouse cells. However, with the rapid advancement of stem cell biology, a fully human system is expected to become feasible in the near future [54,55]. Matrigel was used as a physiologically relevant matrix for organoid formation; nevertheless, future optimisation of its composition or exploration of defined synthetic substitutes will further improve clinical translatability.

3D cultivation of HFOs within MN moulds for CryoMN preparation

In prior sections, optimal HFOs cultivation parameters were established in round-bottom low-adhesion 96-well plates, with well diameters around 10.7 mm—substantially larger than our 0.5 mm pore MNs. Fig. 5a illustrates the complete process of HFPCs aggregation to form 3D HFOs and the subsequent induction of HFGs. Determining the ideal cell seeding density for MN moulds necessitates further research. Considering that not all cells descend fully into the wells during MN moulds loading, we increased the culture's cell density. The sprouting rate of HFOs in MN moulds was assessed over a 3-day period, with seeding densities ranging from 4000 to 30,000 cells per well. Cell membrane dyes DiO and DiI were utilised to label epidermal cells (ECs) and DCs, respectively, to assess the 3D morphology of HFOs. The results showed that the HFOs volume was positively correlated with the HFPCs quantity within the MN moulds, the hair follicle sprouting rate and subsequent hair growth was observable (Fig. S8a). This observation is consistent with that in round-bottom low-adhesion 96-well plates, thereby validating the utility of our prepared MN moulds for the large-scale cultivation of HFOs. Fig. S8b indicates that the maximum sprouting rate of HFOs, at 63.63 ± 3.05%, was achieved with a cell quantity of 10,000. While no statistically significant difference in hair follicle sprouting rates was observed between 10,000 and 20,000 cells, a significant difference was noted when the 10,000 cells group was compared to all other cell quantities. Therefore, a cell count of 10,000 was considered to be the optimal inoculum for HFOs cultivation within the MN moulds. Since the highest sprouting rate of HFO cultured in our research using MN moulds was only 63.63 ± 3.05%, the Wnt agonist RSPO1 was added to the culture medium. This increased the hair follicle sprouting rate to 90.00 ± 1.00%, and we thoroughly explored its underlying mechanism [56]. RSPO1, a known agonist of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [57], promotes the aggregation of HFPCs into 3D HFOs by activating the Wnt signaling pathway. Furthermore, RSPO1 activates the NF-κB, Rap 1 and PI3K-Akt pathways, which together with the Wnt pathway promote and accelerate hair follicle sprouting.

Then, the entire culture process of HFOs in round-bottom low-adhesion 96-well plates and within MN moulds was comparatively observed, with a cell concentration of 10,000 cells per well being utilised. As shown in Fig. 5b, on the first day, the distribution of HFPCs within the round-bottom low-adhesion 96-well plates was relatively sparse, with only a small portion of cells aggregating. By the third day, HFPCs had completely aggregated to form HFOs, and the HFG structures could be observed. During the 5-7 days culture period, the HFOs exhibited smaller cell spheroids, indicating a more compact cell arrangement, and the HFG structures were more pronounced. HFPCs cultured within the MN mould were also relatively dispersed on the first day. However, due to the smaller pore size of the MNs compared to cultivation in 96-well plates, the cell density was higher, and aggregation was better. By the third day, the cells had essentially completed aggregation, but there were still a few cells distributed around the aggregates, which may be related to some cells growing adherently on the walls of the MN mould. Despite hydrophobic treatment on the mould, the hydrophobic effect was not as effective as that of the round-bottom low-adhesion 96-well plates. On day 5, the cells aggregated more compactly, and distinct HFG structures were observable. By day 7, the cell aggregation was more complete, and the HFGs had developed into hair-like structures. Besides, the confocal image showed the 3D structure of HFOs when DCs were stained with DiI (Fig. 5c). However, the 3D reconstruction image revealed a hemispherical morphology of the HFO, indicative of the inability of the confocal microscope to penetrate the thick cell layers fully.

Furthermore, the cell viability of HFOs cultured within the MN moulds was evaluated at various time intervals. The study showed that the majority of HFPCs introduced into the MN moulds had aggregated within 24 h, with an overall cell viability of 84.52 ± 2.25% among the aggregated HFPCs (Fig. S8c–d). Following aggregation, the cell viability was recorded at 80.21 ± 6.69% at the 48-h mark and 80.13 ± 6.13% at 72 h. HFPCs maintained high viability after a 3-day cultivation period in the MN moulds, indicating that HFOs nurtured within these MN moulds were suitable for further experimental applications. After 2 weeks of cultivation, HFPCs in the MN moulds assembled to form spherical HFOs, and string-like structures extended from the spheres outwards, resembling hair (Fig. S8e). Additionally, Fig. S8f indicates that after dehydration and gold sputtering, SEM revealed a dispersed arrangement of cells within the MN moulds, likely due to the iterative cell washing and dehydration processes. Despite this, filamentous structures were observed extending from the cells, due to nascent hair. These results suggest that HFOs cultured in MN moulds exhibit high cell viability and possess the characteristics necessary for developing and forming new hair.

To provide objective, reproducible evaluation of HFO formation in different platforms, both the sprouting rate and the morphological parameters (HFO diameter and germ length) were quantified at day 7 of culture. As shown in Fig. S8g, the sprouting rate in round-bottom 96-well plates reached 98 ± 3%, which is fully consistent with the near-100% efficiency reported by Kageyama in the same platform [28]. In contrast, the MN mould achieved a sprouting rate of 90 ± 1%. The slight reduction is likely attributable to physical constraints within the mould that limit nutrient diffusion and 3D expansion. As shown in Fig. S8h, morphometric analysis revealed that HFO diameters were comparable between the two platforms (173 ± 5 μm vs. 180 ± 16 μm, p > 0.05). Notably, germ length was significantly longer in the MN mould (89 ± 8 μm) than in the round-bottom plate (42 ± 3 μm) (Fig. S8i, p < 0.01). This difference may reflect the directional constraints imposed by the narrow mould geometry, which may guide germ elongation along the needle axis and thereby promote the formation of elongated, aligned germ structures.

Preparation of PLGA@HFO CryoMNs and evaluation of internal HFOs morphology and viability

As shown in Fig. 6a, our design principle involves initially constructing a hollow MN shell with pores and a CryoMN loaded with HFOs, followed by stacking the two to achieve the final core-shell MN. This approach maintains the rigidity of the MN shell without compromising the cellular cultivation environment. HFPCs were initially seeded onto MN moulds and cultured in vitro for 3 days to obtain HFOs (Fig. 6b). Subsequently, drawing on previous experience in the fabrication of CryoMNs [23], 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 10% Matrigel were incorporated into the culture medium when replacing it in the MN mould. After gradient freezing, the MN array HFO-CryoMNs were successfully created. Fig. 6c, e demonstrated that HFO-CryoMNs and PLGA MNs have matching MN arrays and spacing, which facilitates precise alignment and allows them to be completely opposed to form the nested HFO-CryoMNs structure (Fig. 6d). As shown in the enlarged view of Fig. 6c, the HFO-CryoMNs were intact with distinct ice crystal structures before melting, and the HFO was encapsulated within the MN, remaining protected from exposure. Among the layers, the upper layer consists of CryoMNs containing HFO, while the lower layer is composed of PLGA MNs. At room temperature, the CryoMNs gradually integrate with the PLGA MNs. The enlarged view of Fig. 6d details the mating process of individual MNs, showing that as HFO-CryoMNs melt, they release HFO into the grooves of the PLGA MNs. Thus, the freezing solution facilitates the loading of HFOs onto all MN arrays.

In addition, the microscopic morphology of HFOs within the MN array was examined. Cell membrane dyes DiO and DiI were utilised to label ECs and DCs, respectively, and the distribution of these two cell types within HFOs after a 3-day culture period in the MN moulds was observed. Fig. 6f presents the image of HFOs within the MN moulds for 3 days under an upright fluorescent microscope following the thawing of HFO-CryoMNs. The image displays a predominance of red fluorescence, which overshadows the green. Subsequently, the sample was examined under a confocal microscope for more detailed observation. Fig. 6g–i confirm that, in agreement with previous reports [28], red-fluorescing DCs localised to the periphery of HFOs, whereas green-fluorescing ECs were restricted to the interior. Furthermore, the arrows indicate the prominent HFGs structure outside the sphere of HFOs, which possesses the potential for hair development. Simultaneously, section observations were also conducted on HFOs cultured in the mould for 3 days. As shown in Fig. 6j–m, the section results showed that the fluorescence within the HFOs was mixed, and the area with green fluorescence was significantly less than that with red fluorescence, which may be related to the short cell aggregation time.

To evaluate post-thaw viability, HFO-CryoMNs that had been cultured for 3 and 7 days were prepared respectively, and the thawed cells were stained using a live cell kit. Fig. 6n and o displays post-thaw viability of 65.39 ± 2.97% for the 3-day cultures and 66.02 ± 1.94% for the 7-day cultures, with no significant difference observed, indicating that cell viability is independent of the culture duration. Incorporating the findings from Fig. S8d, cells cultured in the MN moulds for 72 h demonstrated a viability of 80.39 ± 6.13%, indicating a 15% reduction attributed to the freezing process. Nevertheless, a report [20] details post-thaw activity of 30.8 ± 2.2% for mesenchymal stem cells and 53.1 ± 2.4% for melanocytes, noting that cells can proliferate after release from CryoMNs. Additionally, HFOs cultured in hydrogels before freezing exhibit an activity of 72.45 ± 3.14% [23]. this comparison indicates that the cultured HFOs have robust cell viability, rendering them promising for hair regeneration therapy. These data demonstrate that the HFO-CryoMN not only preserves cell viability but also retains function associated with hair follicle induction and outgrowth.

Based on previous plans for preparing CryoMNs [20,23], the effects of different concentrations (2.5%, 5%, 10%) of DMSO on the cellular activity of cryopreserved HFO were investigated (Fig. S9a). HFOs cryopreserved with 5% DMSO exhibited significantly higher post-thaw viability than the other group (p < 0.05), confirming that a 5% DMSO concentration is preferable for this platform, consistent with previous reports on CryoMN systems [20,23]. Interestingly, a recent study showed that 2% DMSO is more conducive to cryopreservation of HFOs [41], but this study did not add Matrigel, which is different from our literature. To determine the maximum storage duration under cryopreservation, we assessed HFO viability after 0, 7, 14 and 28 days of storage at −80°C in HFO-CryoMNs (the representative image of day 0 is from Fig. 6n, which was cultured for 3-days and storaged for 0-days). Live/dead staining (Fig. S9b) and quantitative analysis (Fig. S9c) showed that the cell viability shortened gradually with the extension of cell cryopreservation time at the early stage (0-14 days), and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05), but cell viability stabilized after 14 days. No further significant decline was observed from day 14 to day 28 (p > 0.05). These results indicate that prolonged cryopreservation time will hinder the activity and function of HFO, and the cryopreservation MNs need to be used promptly after cryopreservation. However, prolonged culture time (Fig. 6n and o) will not affect the activity of cryopreserved cells. Therefore, HFO should be prepared into HFO-CryoMN at the time of application, rather than cryopreservated them for a long time.

Furthermore, Fig. S10 shows that HFOs cultured inside HFO-CryoMNs exhibited localised Ki67-positive proliferation zones, indicating active proliferation in specific area. Furthermore, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) expression was strongly detected around the periphery of the HFOs, where hair follicle sprouting was observed, suggesting that the emerging hair bud-like structures were associated with high dermal papilla activity. These results are consistent with literature reports [58,59]. Despite the advantages of our HFO-CryoMNs, the current strategy relies on cryopreservation to maintain HFO viability. This requirement may limit its practical handling and clinical accessibility. To address this limitation, future efforts will explore the delivery of HFOs at room temperature using alternative matrices, such as collagen or synthetic hydrogels, which could provide a supportive microenvironment for organoids without the need for freezing.

Biomimetic hair follicular unit regeneration induced by PLGA@HFO CryoMNs

HFO-CryoMNs were incorporated into PLGA MNs on the bed of liquid nitrogen, thereby fabricating core-shell structured MNs, designated as PLGA@HFO CryoMNs. The capacity of PLGA@HFO CryoMNs to penetrate and persist within the skin is crucial for evaluating their suitability in hair regeneration therapies. Fig. S11a shows puncture formation in nude mouse skin after PLGA@HFO CryoMN penetration; skin damage is evident in the magnified image. As depicted in Fig. S11b, the haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining results revealed a distinct layered structure in MN-penetrated skin tissue, which was absent in non-penetrated skin. Fig. S11c further confirms the MN penetration depth. The uninjured skin of nude mice naturally presents a depression depth of 101 ± 16 μm. In contrast, the skin punctured by PLGA@HFO CryoMNs exhibited complete epidermal penetration, characterised by a subsidence depth of 587 ± 85 μm. These results are consistent with the outcomes of our in vitro puncture experiments, as illustrated in Fig. 3k. Notably, the observed skin subsidence depth represents the residual wound after MN removal. This measurement may not accurately reflect the depth of MN penetration, as the rapid closure of subcutaneous tissue post-extraction could obscure the true depth. In accordance with our experimental design, the cells encapsulated by the MNs are predominantly situated at the conical base, specifically beneath the 500 μm mark at the base of the MNs. Consequently, the observed puncture depth validates the MNs' capacity to effectively deliver cells into the skin tissue.

Additionally, the degradation of PLGA@HFO CryoMNs were assessed in nude mouse skin tissue. During the experiment, PLGA@HFO CryoMNs prepared by combining rhodamine B stained PLGA MN with HFO-CryoMNs were inserted completely into the dorsal skin of nude mice and secured them firmly (Fig. S11d). On the day of puncture, the MNs were completely inserted into the skin tissue, with their base surfaces in close apposition to the skin surface. Cryosection imaging revealed progressive degradation of PLGA MNs (Fig. S11e): at day 0, the MN shell was visible (irregular shape, possibly due to tissue contraction); by day 7, the base had diffused, internal fluorescence dissipated, but epidermal channels remained, with hair formation initiating around the MNs; at day 14, no MN remnants remained, and hair grew along the channels with trace residual fluorescence. Thus, PLGA MNs largely degraded within two weeks, matching the hair growth cycle.

PLGA@HFO CryoMNs were vertically implanted into the dorsal skin of nude mice within a cryogenic environment and compared with four control group treated with PLGA MNs alone, HFO-CryoMNs (without PLGA shell), HFO injection alone, and Non-porous PLGA@HFO CryoMN (without the multi-channel design). As shown in Fig. 7a, after a 14-day treatment period, black hair growth was observed on the dorsal skin of nude mice in the PLGA@HFO CryoMN treated area, but no hair grew in the PLGA MNs group, HFO-CryoMNs group and traditional core-shell MNs. While, the HFO injection alone produced disorganized dermal hairs without epidermal breakthrough (the other groups are shown in Fig. S12). The HFO-CryoMNs group (without PLGA shell) produced no surface hairs; histological analysis revealed hair follicles confined to the subcutaneous layer, failing to achieve epidermal breakthrough. The HFO injection alone group likewise showed no visible surface hairs, but upon lifting the skin, disorganized hair shafts were observed in the dermis, and they remained trapped beneath the epidermis. The traditional core-shell MNs group (without multi-channel design) exhibited no hair growth at any skin layer, likely because the enclosed space prevented adequate nutrient exchange and HFO survival. Only the complete PLGA@HFO CryoMN system yielded transdermal hair regeneration. Human hair is comprised of follicular units, which are skin tissue areas containing multiple closely adjacent hairs [4]. As illustrated in the schematic diagram Fig. 7b, a follicular unit may contain either a single hair or double hairs. By culturing HFPCs into HFOs before transplantation, the human follicular unit transplantation technique is mimicked, aligning with natural hair growth patterns and meeting the demands of hair regeneration. Interestingly, our induced hair growth predominantly consists of single hairs, with some double hairs also induced. Our MNs facilitated the formation of two types of follicular units, primarily single-haired, which may correlate with MN dimensions, penetration depth and the immaturity of HFOs cultivation techniques. In subsequent studies, we intend to refine the morphology of MNs and enhance the outgrowth rate of HFOs, thereby increasing the cultivation of double and triple hair follicular units. This strategy is designed to boost the density of new hair growth in the skin of nude mice, furthering the objectives of hair regeneration research. Subsequently, hair regeneration in adjacent areas were evaluated, revealing single hairs spaced by about 2 mm (Fig. 7c). This aligns with our MN array, indicating the feasibility of using MN-embedded HFOs for controlled hair regeneration spacing. To evaluate the spatial control efficiency, hair follicle unit density was quantified in successful regeneration regions. As shown in Fig. 7d, the measured density was 55 ± 10 FUs/cm2 (for each experiment, 6 regions were analyzed). This value was not statistically different from the theoretical implantation density of 69 FUs/cm2 (p > 0.05), indicating that PLGA@HFO CryoMN achieved approximately 80% of the designed density in the successful areas. The successful regeneration of hair follicles with a density approaching the theoretical design value validates the spatial controllability of PLGA@HFO CryoMN. Compared with the MN hair planting reported in previous literature [23,41], the hair density planted in our study is more consistent with the human hair regeneration density.

After examining the skin of nude mice 14 days post-treatment and conducting H&E staining, the presence of hair follicle tissue in multiple areas within the PLGA@HFO CryoMN treatment group was observed (Fig. 7e). Hair shaft diameter and depth were measured from 20 randomly selected follicles per independent experiment. The diameter of regenerated hairs (Fig. 7f) was comparable to that of native nude mouse hairs (p > 0.05), both hair shafts have a diameter of about 37 μm, suggesting normal shaft thickness. This finding indicates that MNs loaded with HFOs can induce the regeneration of hair follicles in mice, aligning with the potential of such MNs to stimulate hair growth. However, the depth of regenerated hair bulbs (Fig. 7g) was significantly shallower than that of native hairs (351 ± 70 μm vs. 628 ± 59 μm, p < 0.01). This difference is likely attributable to the limited penetration depth of PLGA@HFO CryoMN, which restricted organoid implantation depth. This observation is consistent with the physical constraint imposed by the MN length (approximately 587 ± 85 μm), which limited the depth at which organoids were deposited. We therefore anticipate that extending the MN length in future iterations will enable deeper follicle placement, potentially achieving full physiological depth. To verify whether the observed hair follicle tissue is the nude mouse's own white hair, we applied hair shaft markers gp100 [60,61] for further assessment of the newly grown hair. The results revealed high expression of gp100 in hair follicles of the PLGA@HFO CryoMN reconstructed black hair follicles (Fig. 7h), suggesting that the observed hair follicle units can produce melanin, confirming they are not the mouse own hairs. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 7i) revealed that the gp100 mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of reconstructed hair was significantly higher than that of the native hairs (p < 0.001). 6 randomly follicles were measured from per independent experiment.

To assess whether the regenerated follicles undergo periodic cycling, observation of the same transplantation sites was extended to 7 weeks (Fig. S13). At week 2, hairs were ∼50 μm (active anagen). By week 4, hairs shortened to ∼5 μm, indicating catagen/telogen transition. At week 5, no hairs remained on the skin surface (telogen). Importantly, at week 7, new hairs re-emerged (∼50 μm), demonstrating a second anagen phase. Lef1 was highly expressed at week 2, confirming active anagen. At week 4, Lef1 was downregulated while Fgf5 became elevated, indicating anagen-to-catagen transition. At week 5, Hopx was strongly expressed while Lef1 and Fgf5 were low, confirming a quiescent telogen phase. Importantly, at week 7, Lef1 expression re-emerged, demonstrating successful re-entry into the second anagen phase. Consistent with this marker timeline, H&E staining revealed characteristic morphological changes: deep pigmented anagen follicles at week 2, shortening follicles with apoptotic features at week 4, club-hair-bearing telogen follicles at week 5, and re-elongating follicles at week 7. These temporal expression dynamics are consistent with established literature reporting Lef1 attenuation in catagen/telogen [62], Fgf5 upregulation at the anagen-to-catagen transition [63], and Hopx as a specific telogen bulge marker [64]. These data confirm that the regenerated follicles complete a full hair cycle (anagen→catagen/telogen→anagen) and support sustained, periodic regeneration—not a transient response. The 14-day endpoint in the main study was chosen to capture initial sprouting and density control, while the extended observation proves long-term functionality. Overall, the current results provide a strong proof of concept that density adjustable, spatially organized, and cyclically reproducible hair regeneration can be achieved using this platform.

Characterization of PLGA@HFO CryoMNs-mediated regenerated hair through immunofluorescence staining with specific markers

The development of HFOs involves the following stages: CD34+ dermal cells [65] aggregate at the basal layer, forming a signaling center that initiates hair growth. Subsequently, HFOs develop into the characteristic double-layered epithelial structure of the hair follicle, comprising the outer root sheath (ORS) and inner root sheath (IRS) [66]. The proliferating hair matrix (Ki67+) propels the hair shaft upward [67], where it vertically penetrates the epidermis and stratum corneum to emerge as a hair shaft protruding through the skin.

As shown in Fig. 8a, Sox2, a hair follicle induction marker [68,69], was abundantly enriched in the dermal papilla region of regenerated follicles. The hair matrix region exhibits substantial Ki67 enrichment, indicating active cellular proliferation that facilitates the upward growth of the hair shaft (Fig. 8b). Subsequently, β-catenin staining images reveal U-shaped cellular bands observed in the dermal papilla region of IRS and within the hair matrix, suggesting these cells possess the potential to activate Wnt signaling [70] and drive hair shaft differentiation (Fig. 8c). Meanwhile, Krt14, a keratinocyte differentiation marker [71], exhibited a typical flower-like pattern in the middle outer root sheath, indicating well-organized epithelial differentiation (Fig. 8d). The stem cell marker CD34 was observed to accumulate in the hair follicle bulge region, indicating that the constructed hair follicles possess robust regenerative capacity (Fig. 8e). Additionally, numerous arrector pili muscles are attached to the ORS of the hair follicle indicated by the α-SMA-stained regions (Fig. 8f). These structures demonstrate that induced new hair follicle tissue possesses the potential for periodic hair regeneration, though the specific hair growth cycle remains to be further explored in subsequent experiments. Immunofluorescence staining showed that the HFOs expressed Sox2, Ki67, β-catenin, Krt14, CD34 and α-SMA at different levels (Fig. 8g). 6 randomly follicles were measured from per independent experiment. The significantly higher expression of Krt14, β-catenin and Sox2 compared with Ki67, CD34 and α-SMA suggests that the regenerated HFOs are in a stage where differentiation and inductive signaling predominate over active proliferation and stem cell quiescence. Specifically, the elevated Sox2 confirms a functional dermal papilla niche; the strong Krt14 indicates well-established epithelial differentiation; and the high β-catenin reflects active Wnt signaling driving hair morphogenesis. In contrast, the relatively lower Ki67, CD34 and α-SMA levels are consistent with the fact that at the day 14 time point, the hair follicles have already passed the peak proliferative phase, the bulge stem cells have entered a more quiescent state, and the arrector pili muscle is still maturing. These quantitative findings further support the mechanistic conclusion that our CryoMN platform effectively induces hair follicle differentiation and signaling rather than merely triggering a transient proliferative burst.

To assess the proliferative activity of HFOs at different stages after implantation, we performed Ki67 immunofluorescence staining on tissue sections at day 7 and day 14 post-transplantation. As shown in Fig. S14, Ki67-positive cells were readily detectable in regenerated hair matrix at both time points. Quantitative analysis of MFI revealed that Ki67 expression remained robust from day 7 to day 14, with no significant increase (p > 0.05), indicating that the proliferative capacity of HFOs is sustained during the initial integration and hair shaft emergence phases. The sustained Ki67 expression from day 7 to day 14 suggests that the PLGA@HFO CryoMN platform supports continuous cell proliferation throughout the early stages of hair follicle morphogenesis, which is essential for successful hair shaft emergence and regeneration cycling.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The current 7-week follow-up period and small sample size (n = 3) are insufficient to confirm sustained multi-cycle regeneration. Extended studies with larger cohorts are necessary before clinical translation can be considered. Additionally, while PLGA MNs show compatibility with human DPCs, human HFPC-derived organoid formation within the CryoMN platform has not yet been demonstrated. Given the biological differences between neonatal murine and adult human HFPCs, and the current requirement of >100 days for human HFO establishment, validation of human organoid compatibility remains a prerequisite for clinical translation and will be pursued in future studies.

Conclusions

Using a nested MN mould design and a double-layer sandwich preparation method, we developed multi-channel hollow PLGA MNs featuring mechanical stability, biodegradability, and uniform wall thickness. Subsequently, we successfully cultured 3D HFOs within the MN moulds, which led to hair follicle sprouting and de novo hair formation. The HFOs were then loaded into PLGA MNs to create biodegradable core-shell microneedles (PLGA@HFO MNs) with the MN shell channel utilised to ensure the long-term survival of HFOs in vivo and to induce hair regeneration. Our results demonstrated that these core-shell CryoMNs stimulated the growth of melanized hair in nude mice, mimicking the natural hair growth pattern through follicular unit formation. The engineered MN shell not only facilitated HFOs loading but also provided as a conduit for hair penetration through the skin, thereby enabling preliminary control over the direction and density of hair growth. This approach overcomes the major limitations of conventional tissue-engineered hair regeneration, including impaired hair emergence and disorganized, clustered growth.
