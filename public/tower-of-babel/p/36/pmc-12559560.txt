GNPNAT1 acts as a prognostic biomarker associated with immune infiltration in lung adenocarcinoma and promotes the proliferation and invasion of cancer cells
Authors: Qingfeng Li, Qingyin Lan, Hongmiao Wang, Jingyi Li, Yijia Tang, Xue Yi, Meigui You, Yujuan Tang, Yaping Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer persists as the foremost cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) representing the predominant histological subtype. Although GNPNAT1 has been implicated as a prognostic indicator in breast and prostate malignancies, its functional significance and mechanistic role in LUAD remain to be elucidated.MethodsWe performed comprehensive bioinformatic analyses to compare GNPNAT1 expression levels and prognostic impact in LUAD versus normal pulmonary tissues. Functional enrichment and single-cell transcriptomic analyses were employed to infer GNPNAT1-associated biological pathways. Immune cell infiltration was quantified using established deconvolution algorithms to assess correlations with GNPNAT1 expression. In vitro assays—including Transwell migration/invasion, colony formation, and flow cytometric evaluation of apoptosis and cell-cycle distribution—were conducted following GNPNAT1 knockdown. Western blotting was utilized to measure alterations in cyclins, CDKs, and MAPK/ERK pathway proteins.ResultsGNPNAT1 was significantly upregulated in LUAD specimens compared with non-neoplastic lung tissue (P < 0.001), and elevated expression portended reduced overall survival. Enrichment analyses highlighted GNPNAT1’s involvement in cell proliferation and cell-cycle regulation. Correlation studies revealed that GNPNAT1 expression positively associated with Th2 cells, total T helper cells, and γδ T cells, while inversely correlating with Th17 cells, B lymphocytes, and CD8⁺ T cells. Functional suppression of GNPNAT1 markedly impaired LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro (P < 0.01), induced apoptosis, and provoked G2/M cell-cycle arrest. Furthermore, GNPNAT1 silencing resulted in decreased levels of Cyclin B1, Cyclin D1, CDK1 (P < 0.01), and phosphorylated ERK.ConclusionGNPNAT1 is overexpressed in lung adenocarcinoma and serves as an adverse prognostic biomarker. Its modulation of immune cell infiltration and engagement of the ERK/MAPK signaling cascade underscores its potential as both a prognostic indicator and therapeutic target in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03818-z.Keywords: GNPNAT1, Lung adenocarcinoma, Immune infiltration, Cell proliferation, Invasion, Prognosis

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the foremost cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) and the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 2.4 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths were recorded in 2022 [1, 2]. In China, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) constitutes roughly 40% of all non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) diagnoses. The natural history of LUAD typically involves progression from precancerous lesions—such as atypical adenomatous hyperplasia—through adenocarcinoma in situ and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, culminating in fully invasive disease [3–5]. At the molecular level, LUAD is stratified into distinct subtypes defined by driver mutations in EGFR, ALK, and KRAS [6, 7]. Although targeted agents (e.g., osimertinib for EGFR-mutant LUAD) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies) have improved outcomes, the five-year survival rate for advanced LUAD remains a mere 18%, largely due to acquired therapeutic resistance and metastatic progression [8–11]. This dismal prognosis underscores the pressing need for novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Accumulating evidence highlights metabolic reprogramming as a hallmark of tumorigenesis. Glucosamine-6-phosphate N-acetyltransferase 1 (GNPNAT1, also known as GNA1) catalyzes the N-acetylation of glucosamine-6-phosphate—an essential step in the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP) that yields N-acetylglucosamine-6-phosphate from glucosamine-6-phosphate and acetyl-CoA [12–15]. The HBP produces UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), the donor substrate for protein O-GlcNAcylation, which has been implicated in promoting cancer cell proliferation and facilitating immune evasion [14]. As a rate-limiting enzyme, GNPNAT1 dysregulation perturbs the O-GlcNAcylation cycle and thereby modulates key oncogenic signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT and MAPK [16]. In breast carcinoma, GNPNAT1 overexpression drives epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and chemoresistance through HIF-1α stabilization, enhancing tumor cell proliferation and invasion; it has thus emerged as a potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in breast cancer [17–19]. Conversely, in prostate cancer, GNPNAT1 downregulation paradoxically activates the PI3K/AKT axis, fostering castration-resistant disease progression [20]. These tissue-specific effects underscore the multifaceted role of GNPNAT1 in oncogenesis.

Within LUAD, the tumor microenvironment (TME) exerts a critical influence on therapeutic response. Immune infiltration patterns—particularly CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion and regulatory T-cell (Treg) predominance—correlate with poorer clinical outcomes [21]. Notably, HBP metabolites regulate immune cell function via O-GlcNAcylation of key proteins; for example, PD-1 O-GlcNAcylation stabilizes the receptor on T cells, thereby exacerbating resistance to immune checkpoint blockade [22]. However, the relationship between GNPNAT1-mediated metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion in LUAD has yet to be elucidated. Preliminary bioinformatic analysis of the TCGA-LUAD cohort reveals an inverse correlation between GNPNAT1 expression and cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration, suggesting that GNPNAT1 may orchestrate both intrinsic tumor cell signaling and extrinsic immunosuppressive mechanisms [23].

In the present study, we integrate multi-dimensional datasets and functional assays to dissect GNPNAT1’s oncogenic roles in LUAD. We validate its prognostic significance using TCGA and CPTAC cohorts, characterize its association with immune cell dynamics via infiltration analysis, and elucidate its contribution to cell-cycle progression through the MAPK/ERK pathway. Unlike prior investigations, our results demonstrate that GNPNAT1 inhibits apoptosis and regulates G2/M transition in LUAD cells, participating in tumor proliferation via ERK/MAPK signaling. Collectively, these findings nominate GNPNAT1 as a promising therapeutic target in LUAD, bridging metabolic dysregulation and immune suppression in lung carcinogenesis.

Materials and methods

Cells and reagents

Cell lines

Human lung cancer cell lines A549, H460, H1299, 95D), HCC827, and H1975, together with the immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line HBE, were procured from the Shanghai Stem Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination prior to use.

Reagents

Key reagents—including culture media, sera, transfection reagents, antibodies, and assay kits—were sourced from commercial suppliers. Detailed supplier names, catalog numbers, and lot numbers are provided in Table S3. All antibodies were used at the manufacturer’s recommended dilutions, and all kits were employed according to the supplied protocols.

Experimental methods

Data processing and gene expression, survival, and correlation analyses

Raw counts from RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) experiments and corresponding clinical metadata for tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues across 33 cancer types were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the Genomic Data Commons portal, and from normal tissue samples in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database. All statistical and graphical analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.1). Differential gene expression analyses were conducted with the ggplot2 package, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using survminer and survival. Statistical significance of survival differences was assessed by the log-rank test, while univariate Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and associated p-values. Pairwise gene–gene correlations were visualized with ggstatsplot, employing Pearson’s correlation for normally distributed data or Spearman’s rank correlation for non-parametric datasets. All downloaded data are openly accessible at the following repositories:

RNA-seq data: https://pan.baidu.com/s/1ze4Ya4nv41TkSYIptFaeKQ (access code: n437).

Clinical metadata:https://pan.baidu.com/s/1PZEAe8p5uhSdmy2MORFJVQ (access code: usxa).

Human Protein Atlas (HPA) analysis

The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) is an open-access, integrative resource that maps the spatial proteome in human cells, tissues, and organs through antibody-based imaging, mass spectrometry–based proteomics, transcriptomics, and systems biology approaches [24, 25]. In our study, the HPA web portal was queried for “GNPNAT1.” Under the “Protein expression” module, we selected the “Cancer” view and specifically chose “Lung adenocarcinoma” to visualize immunohistochemical (IHC) staining intensities. Comparative analysis of IHC staining in LUAD specimens versus normal lung tissues allowed semi-quantitative assessment of GNPNAT1 protein abundance.

UALCAN database analysis

UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu) provides user-friendly access to TCGA transcriptomic and proteomic datasets for 31 distinct cancer types [26]. For proteomic profiling, we navigated to the “Proteomics” section, selected “Lung adenocarcinoma,” and entered “GNPNAT1” to retrieve relative protein expression data. Expression levels in primary LUAD samples were compared against adjacent normal lung tissues to corroborate transcriptomic findings at the protein level.

Kaplan–Meier plotting analysis

KM-plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) [27] is a state-of-the-art, web-based survival analysis platform that computes Kaplan–Meier curves in real time, relying on a manually curated repository of gene expression and clinical outcome data aggregated from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), the European Genome-phenome Archive (EGA), and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). To assess the prognostic relevance of GNPNAT1, patient samples are stratified into two cohorts based on expression quantiles of the biomarker of interest (e.g., upper versus lower half). Survival outcomes—specifically overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS)—are compared between these groups using Kaplan–Meier estimators. The hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI), along with the log-rank test P-value, are calculated automatically to quantify the risk differential. In the present study, we accessed the “mRNA (RNA-seq) KM-plotter for pan-cancer” module, input the gene symbol “GNPNAT1,” selected a median split to dichotomize patients into high- and low-expression groups, and retrieved the corresponding OS and RFS curves and statistics for GNPNAT1.

ROC and time-dependent ROC analyses

RNA-seq expression data for GNPNAT1 were downloaded from the TCGA portal and processed as follows: expression values in transcripts per million (TPM) were log₂-transformed using the formula log₂(TPM + 1), after which clinical annotations and any duplicate entries were removed to ensure data integrity. Diagnostic performance was evaluated by generating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with the pROC R package (version 1.18.0), computing area under the curve (AUC) values and 95% CIs. To investigate prognostic accuracy over time, time-dependent ROC analyses were performed using the timeROC package (version 0.4), which estimates AUCs at specified follow-up intervals. Visualization of both static and time-dependent ROC curves was conducted using ggplot2 (version 3.3.6). Underlying prognosis datasets supporting these analyses are accessible at https://pan.baidu.com/s/1KmzuCYgu9J8ItH9ZIjCTxA (extraction code: 2ctt).

Analysis of single-cell datasets

To explore gene-associated functional states at single-cell resolution, we employed CancerSEA (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CancerSEA) [28], an online resource that profiles the functional roles of individual genes across 14 distinct cellular phenotypes in LUAD. These states include angiogenesis, apoptosis, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), differentiation, proliferation, DNA damage response, metastasis, hypoxia, inflammation, cell-cycle regulation, DNA repair, stemness, and quiescence. Although originally exemplified for DLGAP5, we queried the database for GNPNAT1 to retrieve its putative involvement across these functional axes in lung adenocarcinoma.

Linked omics database analysis

LinkedOmics (http://www.linkedomics.org/) [29] is an open-access portal comprising multi-omics datasets from all 32 TCGA cancer types and 10 CPTAC cohorts. To identify genes co-expressed with GNPNAT1 in LUAD, we selected the “Lung adenocarcinoma” HiSeq RNA dataset, entered “GNPNAT1” as the query gene, and employed Pearson’s correlation test to compute correlation coefficients. Co-expressed genes were filtered by |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 and adjusted p < 0.05, yielding the top 50 candidates. Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses of these co-expression partners were performed using the clusterProfiler R package [30], with graphical representations—volcano plots, heatmaps, and enrichment bar charts—rendered via ggplot2. These analyses elucidate the signaling networks and biological processes in which GNPNAT1 participates within LUAD.

Cell culture and transfection

Six human lung adenocarcinoma cell lines (A549, H460, H1975, HCC827, 95D, and H1299) and one normal human bronchial epithelial cell line (HBE) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). All cell lines were verified to be free of mycoplasma contamination. Cells were maintained in either Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI-1640) medium or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, humidified atmosphere).

For gene silencing, short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting GNPNAT1 and corresponding negative-control siRNAs were procured from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Transfections were carried out using the riboFECT CP Cell Transfection Kit (RiboBio) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Clone formation experiment

First, transfect cells with either siRNA-GNPNAT1 or negative-control siRNA (si-NC) for 24 h. Then, seed transfected cells at 500 cells per well in a 12-well plate and incubate for 7 days, allowing discrete colonies to form. Next, fix colonies in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and subsequently stain with 1% crystal violet for 20 min. Finally, capture representative images and enumerate colonies containing ≥ 50 cells under a light microscope.

Transwell migration and invasion experiment

Perform Transwell assays in 24-well inserts with an 8 μm pore size to assess the migratory and invasive capacities of A549 and H1975 LUAD cells. For invasion assays, coat the upper chamber membranes with 100 µL of Matrigel diluted 1:10 in serum-free DMEM and incubate at 37 °C for 6 h to allow gelation. Seed 1 × 10⁵ transfected cells (si-NC or si-GNPNAT1) in serum-free medium into the upper chamber and add medium containing 20% FBS to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. For migration assays, omit FBS in the lower chamber. After 24 h at 37 °C, remove non-migrated/invaded cells from the upper surface, fix the cells that traversed the membrane, stain with crystal violet, and image under a microscope for quantification.

Western blot

Lyse cellular proteins in RIPA buffer and quantify protein concentration using a BCA assay kit. Load equal amounts of protein onto a 10% SDS–PAGE gel and resolve by electrophoresis. Transfer proteins onto a PVDF membrane via electroblotting. Block nonspecific binding by incubating the membrane in PBS containing 5% non-fat milk (or BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Incubate the membrane with primary antibody specific to the target protein overnight at 4 °C. After washing, incubate with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Detect bands using FG Super Sensitive ECL reagent and image on a chemiluminescence system.

For densitometric analysis in ImageJ: convert images to 8-bit (Image → Type → 8-bit) to reduce background, then apply background subtraction (Process → Subtract Background; rolling ball radius ≈ 50). Open Analyze → Set Measurements, and select Area, Mean Gray Value, and Integrated Density. Encircle each band with the rectangular selection tool. Use Analyze → Gels → Select First Lane, then Analyze → Gels → Plot Lanes to generate lane intensity plots. Close peaks with the straight-line tool, and click within each closed peak with the wand tool to record the integrated density (gray value) of each band. Calculate relative protein expression by normalizing the target protein band intensity to that of the internal reference protein.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol™ reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA synthesis was conducted with a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative PCR was performed on a real-time fluorescence cycler using SYBR™ Green qPCR Mix. The relative expression level of each target mRNA was determined by the 2-∆∆CT method, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous control for normalization.

Statistical analysis

Apart from analyses automatically generated by public databases, all statistical evaluations and graphical presentations were carried out using GraphPad Prism 8. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were employed. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests was used for multiple-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

GNPNAT1 is highly expressed in LUAD

First, we analyzed GNPNAT1 mRNA expression levels across various human cancers, including lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). Data from the TCGA and GTEx databases demonstrated that GNPNAT1 transcripts were significantly upregulated in multiple malignancies, most notably in LUAD (Fig. 1A, B). Comparable results were obtained when comparing LUAD tumor tissues with matched adjacent non-cancerous lung samples (Fig. 1C, D). To corroborate these findings at the protein level, we interrogated the CPTAC database, which revealed elevated GNPNAT1 protein expression in LUAD versus normal lung tissues. This overexpression was further validated by immunohistochemical staining data from the Human Protein Atlas, which showed stronger GNPNAT1 positivity in LUAD specimens compared to non-malignant controls (Fig. 1E, F).

B–D) GNPNAT1 mRNA expression in LUAD patients and paired adjacent non-tumor samples. E, F GNPNAT1 protein abundance in LUAD as determined by CPTAC proteomics and IHC from HPA

Correlation between GNPNAT1 expression and clinicopathological factors

Subsequently, we examined the association between GNPNAT1 expression and key clinicopathological parameters. As shown in Fig. 2A–D, GNPNAT1 transcript levels correlated significantly with clinical cancer stage (normal vs. Stage I–IV; P < 0.001), lymph node metastasis status (normal vs. N0–N3; P < 0.001), and patient age groups (normal vs. 21–40, 41–60, 61–80, 81–100 years; P < 0.001). GNPNAT1 expression was markedly higher at each tumor stage compared to normal lung tissue, consistent with the preceding bioinformatic analyses.

Clinical significance of GNPNAT1 in LUAD. Correlation of GNPNAT1 expression with: A Clinical cancer stage. B Lymph node metastasis status. C Patient age. D Patient gender

GNPNAT1 expression correlates with adverse prognosis in LUAD

To elucidate the prognostic significance of GNPNAT1 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), we evaluated the association between GNPNAT1 mRNA expression levels and clinical outcomes (overall survival [OS], progression-free survival [PFI], and relapse-free survival [RFS]) using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. Elevated GNPNAT1 expression was significantly correlated with inferior OS, PFI, and RFS (p < 0.05 for all endpoints; Fig. 3A-D). Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated that GNPNAT1 expression exhibited moderate predictive accuracy for 1-year (AUC = 0.673), 3-year (AUC = 0.623), and 5-year OS (AUC = 0.631) in LUAD patients (Fig. 3E). Similarly, robust predictive performance was observed for 1-year (AUC = 0.701), 3-year (AUC = 0.599), and 5-year disease-free survival (AUC = 0.631) (Fig. 3F). Notably, GNPNAT1 expression also demonstrated moderate discriminatory capacity for 1-year (AUC = 0.631), 3-year (AUC = 0.583), and 5-year progression-free survival (PFS; AUC = 0.569) (Fig. 3G).

E-G Time-dependent ROC curves evaluating the prognostic predictive performance of GNPNAT1 expression for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, disease-free survival, and progression-free survival (PFS)

Diagnostic potential of GNPNAT1 in LUAD

Building on prior evidence demonstrating elevated GNPNAT1 expression in breast cancer compared to normal tissues, coupled with bioinformatic analyses revealing differential expression between LUAD and non-malignant lung tissues, we investigated the diagnostic utility of GNPNAT1 in LUAD. ROC curve analysis confirmed high diagnostic accuracy, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.903 for distinguishing LUAD from normal tissues (Fig. 4A). Subgroup analysis across clinical stages (I-IV) revealed consistently strong discriminatory performance, with AUC values of 0.873 (Stage I), 0.937 (Stage II), 0.943 (Stage III), and 0.947 (Stage IV) (Fig. 4B-E). All AUC values exceeded 0.8, underscoring the robust diagnostic potential of GNPNAT1 across disease stages.

Diagnostic Value of GNPNAT1 in LUAD. A ROC curve evaluating the diagnostic performance of GNPNAT1 expression for LUAD detection. B-E Stage-specific ROC curves demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy (AUC > 0.8) for clinical Stages I-IV

Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis

To characterize the molecular mechanisms underlying GNPNAT1’s role in LUAD, we performed co-expression analysis using the LinkedOmics platform. Heatmaps visualized the top 50 gene sets showing significant positive or negative correlation with GNPNAT1 expression (Fig. 5A-C). Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses revealed enrichment of biological processes (BP) and molecular functions (MF) linked to cellular proliferation and mitosis, including organelle fission, nuclear division, chromosome segregation, mitotic sister chromatid segregation, and mitotic nuclear division (Fig. 5D). Notably, chromosomal regions, mitotic spindles, DNA replication origins, centromeres, and histone kinase activity emerged as key cellular components (CC) and molecular functions (MF) associated with GNPNAT1 co-expressed genes (Fig. 5E-F). KEGG pathway analysis further highlighted significant enrichment of cell cycle regulatory processes (Fig. 5G), suggesting a pivotal role for GNPNAT1 in modulating tumor proliferation through cell cycle dysregulation.

Single-cell resolution analysis of GNPNAT1 functional activity in LUAD

To delineate the cellular heterogeneity of GNPNAT1 expression and its association with malignant functional phenotypes at single-cell resolution, we leveraged the CancerSEA database for comprehensive functional annotation. As depicted in Fig. 6A-B, cross-dataset validation revealed a significant positive correlation between GNPNAT1 expression and key oncogenic processes, including cellular proliferation (p < 0.001), cell cycle progression (p < 0.01), DNA damage response (p < 0.05), and DNA repair capacity (p < 0.01) in LUAD. These findings collectively reinforce the hypothesis that GNPNAT1 orchestrates malignant transformation by modulating fundamental cellular mechanisms underlying tumor aggressiveness.

Immune infiltration landscape modulated by GNPNAT1 in LUAD

Given the pivotal role of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in shaping LUAD progression and therapeutic responsiveness, we systematically evaluated the correlation between GNPNAT1 expression and immune cell subset abundance. Spearman’s rank analysis revealed a significant positive association between GNPNAT1 mRNA levels and Th2 cells (p < 0.001), T helper cells (p < 0.01), and γδ T cells (Tgd; p < 0.05). Conversely, inverse correlations were observed with Th17 cells (p < 0.01), B cells (p < 0.05), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC; p < 0.01), follicular helper T cells (TFH; p < 0.05), eosinophils (p < 0.01), conventional dendritic cells (DC; p < 0.05), CD8 + T cells (p < 0.01), immature DCs (iDC; p < 0.05), and mast cells (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7A-D). These dichotomous relationships suggest that GNPNAT1 may reprogram the tumor microenvironment by favoring immunosuppressive over cytotoxic immune phenotypes.

Immune Infiltration Correlation Network of GNPNAT1 in LUAD. A-D Heatmap visualization of Spearman’s correlations between GNPNAT1 expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cell subsets, with red/blue gradients indicating positive/negative associations (p < 0.05 considered significant)

Functional validation of GNPNAT1 in LUAD cell line models

Building on the bioinformatic evidence implicating GNPNAT1 in LUAD pathogenesis, we performed mechanistic studies to validate its oncogenic role. Initial expression profiling across multiple LUAD cell lines demonstrated constitutive GNPNAT1 overexpression, particularly in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells (Fig. 8A). Subsequent siRNA-mediated knockdown in A549 and H1975 cells achieved > 70% mRNA suppression (p < 0.001), as confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 8B-C). Functional assays revealed that GNPNAT1 depletion significantly attenuated cellular proliferation (colony formation assay, p < 0.01), migration (Transwell assay, p < 0.05), and invasiveness (Matrigel-coated Transwell assay, p < 0.01) compared to scrambled controls (Fig. 8D-F). Collectively, these data establish GNPNAT1 as a critical driver of LUAD aggressiveness through its pro-proliferative and pro-metastatic activities.

B, C qRT-PCR validation of GNPNAT1 knockdown efficiency in A549 and H1975 cells following siRNA transfection. D Colony formation assay quantifying proliferative capacity post-knockdown. E, F Transwell migration and invasion assays demonstrating reduced metastatic potential in GNPNAT1-depleted cells

Knockdown of GNPNAT1 promotes apoptosis in LUAD cells

Apoptosis constitutes a genetically programmed cell death process, and its dysregulation represents a well-established oncogenic mechanism. Consistent with antecedent Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and LUAD single-cell sequencing analyses demonstrating GNPNAT1’s association with cellular proliferation, we subsequently performed GNPNAT1 knockdown in A549 and H1975 cell lines followed by comprehensive apoptosis assessment via flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 9A, B). The quantitative apoptosis cytometry results unequivocally demonstrated that GNPNAT1 suppression substantially augmented early apoptotic rates (5.88% versus NC: 0.85%) and late apoptotic fractions (14.5% versus NC: 0.58%) across both cellular models. These collective observations substantiate that GNPNAT1 depletion potentiates apoptotic mechanisms while concurrently inhibiting proliferative capacity in LUAD neoplasms.

Effect of GNPNAT1 on Apoptotic Pathways. A, B Flow cytometric quantification of apoptotic indices in scramble control versus GNPNAT1-knockdown cohorts of A549 and H1975 cellular populations

GNPNAT1 modulates cyclin regulatory networks

Pathological disruption of cell cycle regulatory machinery constitutes a fundamental etiological factor in malignant transformation. Having empirically validated GNPNAT1’s proliferative-promoting oncogenic functions—a biological process intrinsically coupled to cell cycle progression—and further corroborated by bioinformatic projections indicating GNPNAT1-cell cycle interconnectivity, we transfected A549 and H1975 cellular systems with siRNA targeting GNPNAT1. Subsequent cell cycle distribution profiling via flow cytometry revealed significant arrest phenotypes at G1 and S phase checkpoints (Fig. 10A, B). Complementary Western immunoblotting analyses confirmed concomitant downregulation of three cardinal cell cycle regulatory proteins: cyclin B1, cyclin D1, and cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) (Fig. 10C). These mechanistic insights collectively indicate that GNPNAT1 orchestrates cell cycle progression through transcriptional and/or post-translational regulation of cyclin B1, cyclin D1, and CDK1 molecular effectors.

C Western blot evaluation of cyclin B1, cyclin D1, and CDK1 protein expression dynamics in GNPNAT1-knockdown versus control cellular populations

MAPK/ERK signaling cascade mediates GNPNAT1-Driven LUAD proliferation

To delineate the molecular underpinnings of GNPNAT1-facilitated LUAD proliferation, we investigated alterations within mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signal transduction components. Immunoblotting assessment of phospho-activation states for P38 and nuclear factor κB subunit P65 following GNPNAT1 suppression revealed cell line-divergent modulation: phospho-P38 and phospho-P65 intensities were elevated in A549 yet attenuated in H1975 cellular contexts, suggesting context-dependent functional heterogeneity in GNPNAT1 proliferative signaling. Conversely, phospho-ERK signaling exhibited consistent and pronounced attenuation across both cellular systems upon GNPNAT1 depletion (Fig. 11A, B). The MAPK pathway—an evolutionarily conserved signal transduction apparatus—orchestrates cellular proliferation, differentiation, metabolic programming, and stress adaptation through sequential three-tiered kinase cascade mechanisms. Particularly, the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) subfamily (exemplified by ERK1/2) occupies a nodal position within growth factor-activated RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK signal transduction. Its pathological hyperactivation accelerates neoplastic cell cycle progression via transcriptional upregulation of cyclin D1 and c-Myc oncoproteins, constituting a principal therapeutic resistance mechanism in KRAS-mutated colorectal malignancies [31]. Given that MAPK/ERK signal transduction operates through hierarchical phosphorylation events, our experimental evidence confirms that GNPNAT1 ablation suppresses ERK phospho-activation, thereby implicating the MAPK/ERK signaling axis as a critical mechanistic conduit for GNPNAT1-mediated proliferative augmentation in LUAD pathogenesis.

Discussion

Glucosamine-6-phosphate N-acetyltransferase 1 (GNA1) catalyzes the transfer of an acetyl moiety from acetyl coenzyme A (AcCoA) to glucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcN6P), generating N-acetylglucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcNAc6P)—a pivotal intermediate in uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) biosynthesis [32, 33]. As a cardinal enzyme within the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP), GNPNAT1 facilitates UDP-GlcNAc production, the essential substrate for protein O-GlcNAcylation modifications. Accumulating evidence implicates aberrant O-GlcNAcylation in oncogenic progression through dysregulation of cancer-associated signaling cascades [34, 35]. Our investigation demonstrates that GNPNAT1 expression is significantly elevated in LUAD tissues and cellular models, correlating with advanced tumor staging and diminished patient survival. These observations align with prior documentation in breast and prostate malignancies [18, 20], yet contrast with findings in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) where GNPNAT1 downregulation potentiates metastatic dissemination via PI3K/AKT pathway hyperactivation [20]. This apparent paradox likely originates from tissue-specific regulatory frameworks, underscoring the multifaceted nature of GNPNAT1 in tumor pathobiology.

Previous investigations document substantially augmented GNPNAT1 transcription and expression in prostate adenocarcinoma, whereas lymph node metastases and CRPC specimens exhibit GNPNAT1 depletion relative to localized disease. Notably, GNPNAT1 ablation in CRPC-mimetic cellular systems enhances proliferative and invasive capacities through PI3K-AKT pathway stimulation [20]. GNPNAT1 overexpression may derive from genomic amplification events, hypomethylation of regulatory elements, or miRNA regulatory deficiencies [36]. Our supplementary analyses indicate that transcriptional upregulation by specific transcription factors likewise contributes to GNPNAT1 elevation. Co-expression network interrogation further suggests GNPNAT1 involvement in cell cycle transitions, oncogenic transformation, and immune microenvironment modulation within LUAD [37]. Independent studies corroborate GNPNAT1-immune infiltration relationships, implying immunomodulatory functions and therapeutic implications [38, 39].

Critically, GNPNAT1 expression demonstrates an inverse correlation with cytotoxic immune infiltrates (CD8 + T lymphocytes, Th17 cells) and antigen-presenting populations (dendritic cells, B lymphocytes), while exhibiting positive association with immunosuppressive Th2 subsets (Fig. 7). This immunoregulatory profile mirrors recent pancreatic cancer investigations [40], implying that GNPNAT1 may foster immune evasion through Th2-polarized T-cell differentiation. Given the established role of Th2-dominant microenvironments in conferring immunotherapy resistance [41], we posit GNPNAT1 as a candidate biomarker for predicting immune checkpoint inhibitor responsiveness in LUAD.

The MAPK/ERK pathway (Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK cascade) constitutes an evolutionarily conserved eukaryotic signaling module governing proliferation, differentiation, survival, and metabolic reprogramming via phosphorylation hierarchies [42]. Upon growth factor stimulation (e.g., EGF, PDGF), receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) activation recruits Ras GTPases to the plasma membrane, initiating sequential phosphorylation of Raf, MEK, and ERK kinases. Activated ERK undergoes nuclear translocation to modulate transcription factors (e.g., Elk-1, CREB) and cyclin regulators (e.g., Cyclin D1) [43]. Pathway dysregulation associates with diverse malignancies (e.g., colorectal carcinoma, melanoma), particularly through constitutive activation driven by BRAF or RAS mutations [44]. Considering the pleiotropic involvement of MAPK/ERK signaling in oncogenesis, we postulated its participation in GNPNAT1-mediated LUAD proliferation. Our experimental data reveal that GNPNAT1 depletion significantly attenuates ERK phosphorylation in both A549 and H1975 cellular systems. This finding aligns with prior demonstrations that MAPK/ERK governs cyclin D1 and CDK4 regulation in gastric carcinoma, thereby impeding cell cycle progression and tumorigenesis [45]. Similarly, MAPK/ERK signaling mediates cyclin D1 and B1 expression to potentiate proliferation in human erythroleukemia models [42].

At the mechanistic level, GNPNAT1 knockdown induced G1/S phase arrest with concomitant downregulation of cyclins B1/D1 and CDK1 (Fig. 10), paralleled by diminished ERK phosphorylation (Fig. 11). The ERK/MAPK cascade represents a well-characterized regulator of cyclin D1 transcription and G1/S transition. While P38 and P65 phosphorylation exhibited cell line-contextual variability, the consistent suppression of p-ERK across both models (Fig. 11A, B) strongly implicates ERK/MAPK signaling as a conserved mediator of GNPNAT1’s pro-proliferative functions. This conclusion harmonizes with observations in colorectal cancer where hyperactivated ERK sustains cyclin D1 expression in KRAS-mutant neoplasms [33].

Compared with previous studies, this research further elucidates that GNPNAT1 can regulate cyclin B1, cyclin D1, CDK1, and ERK proteins in lung adenocarcinoma and further explored the association between GNPNAT1 expression and immune-infiltrating cells. Despite these advancements, certain methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First, functional validation has been primarily limited to in vitro experiments, and future studies will include in vivo verification. Second, the immune infiltration analysis was conducted using bioinformatics algorithms, and the accuracy of these results requires further experimental validation, such as flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry. Third, although the phosphorylation level of ERK was observed to decrease following GNPNAT1 knockdown, a direct causal relationship has not been definitively established, in the future, adding rescue experiments would strengthen the mechanistic conclusion. Lastly, while the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC) for diagnostic accuracy is relatively high, the data were derived from a retrospective dataset; therefore, prospective studies are necessary to validate the clinical applicability of these findings.

Conclusion and limitations

In summary, our integrative multi-omics investigation establishes GNPNAT1 as a multifaceted oncogenic driver in LUAD pathogenesis through dual complementary mechanisms: (1) Direct potentiation of cell cycle progression via ERK/cyclin D1 signaling cascade activation; (2) Actively sculpting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment conducive to immune evasion. These mechanistic insights collectively nominate GNPNAT1 as both a robust prognostic stratification biomarker and a compelling therapeutic target, particularly for MAPK inhibitor-resistant LUAD subpopulations. Pharmacological inhibition targeting GNPNAT1 enzymatic activity, strategically combined with immune checkpoint blockade regimens, may represent a rational combinatorial therapeutic approach meriting comprehensive preclinical evaluation.

However, several methodological limitations warrant explicit acknowledgment: 1. While this study establishes GNPNAT1’s association with cell cycle regulation and MAPK/ERK signaling, the precise molecular mechanisms governing these regulatory relationships remain incompletely characterized; 2.Experimental validation of GNPNAT1-mediated proliferation and migration promotion was confined exclusively to in vitro cellular models without in vivo corroboration; 3.Tumor immune microenvironment analyses relied solely on bioinformatic inference without experimental validation, thereby constraining definitive immunological conclusions. To address these constraints, future research trajectories will include: 1.Systematic dissection of GNPNAT1’s regulatory mechanisms in LUAD through phosphoproteomic and transcriptional network analyses; 2.Implementation of orthotopic xenograft models and metastasis assays to substantiate in vivo functional significance; 3.Comprehensive immunological validation utilizing patient-derived co-culture systems and syngeneic models to definitively establish GNPNAT1’s role in tumor immune modulation. In the future, we will further verify the regulatory mechanism of GNPNAT1 in lung adenocarcinoma and supplement animal experiments to increase the reliability of the data. We will also further verify the role of GNPNAT1 in tumor immunity.
