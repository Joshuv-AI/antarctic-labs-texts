Estimating clinical and economic impacts and cost-effectiveness of introducing 20-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine in Taiwan’s pediatric National Immunization Program and impacts of a delayed implementation
Authors: Ya-Min Yang, Yi-Wei Wang, An Ta, Elizabeth Vinand, Megan Rutherford, Liping Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFollowing 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) introduction in Taiwan, pneumococcal disease incidence declined; however, incidences driven by non-PCV13 serotypes highlight the need for broader serotype coverage. In response to recent approval of 20-valent PCV (PCV20) for Taiwanese children, this study evaluated 1) health and economic impact of PCV20 in Taiwan’s pediatric National Immunization Program (NIP) vs. standard of care (PCV13) and 15-valent PCV (PCV15) from both payer and societal perspectives; and 2) short- and long-term consequences of delayed implementation. A population-based, multiple-cohort Markov model based on Taiwan-specific epidemiological inputs accounting for population changes was adapted to assess health and economic outcomes of PCV20 vs. PCV13 and PCV15 over 10 y. Both direct effects in vaccinated cohort and indirect effects in the broader population were incorporated to capture full impact of switching PCV13 to PCV20 in Taiwan’s pediatric NIP. Over the long-term, PCV20 2+1 would avert 162,435 pneumococcal disease cases and 4,340 deaths vs PCV13 2+1, while generating $2.3 billion in cost savings, indicating dominance. Similar results were observed vs. PCV15. Despite higher estimated costs than the 2+1 schedule, PCV20 (3+1) remained cost-effective vs. both comparators (2+1). Delaying PCV20 introduction resulted in substantial losses, with a 1-y delay causing over 3,200 additional cases and exceeding NT$40 million in forgone savings, doubling with a 2-y delay. Implementing PCV20 in Taiwan’s pediatric NIP is projected to improve health outcomes and reduce costs, while delays in implementation would lead to significantly avoidable losses compared with PCV13- or PCV15-based programs.

Introduction

Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) aim to provide immunologic protection against some of the most common Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae) serotypes known to cause pneumococcal disease.1 Vaccination (e.g., with PCVs) and surveillance efforts represent an important part of pneumococcal disease prevention worldwide and in Taiwan.1,2

In 2006, privately funded 7-valent PCV (PCV7) was the first PCV introduced in Taiwan, followed by publicly funded immunization efforts for high-risk children aged <5 y in 2009.3,4 Following the introduction of PCV7, although invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) rates decreased among both vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals in Taiwan between 2008 and 2012, there was a simultaneous rise in non-vaccine serotypes.5 Of those, serotype 19A became the most prevalent, increasing from 5.5% to 25.3% between 2008 and 2012.5 In response to this, a national 13-valent PCV (PCV13) catch-up program was implemented for children aged 1 to 5 y between 2013 and 2014.4 In 2015, PCV13 was included in the publicly funded national pediatric routine immunization program under a 2+1 schedule.6 Under this schedule, the primary doses are given to children aged 2 and 4 months, followed by a booster at age 12 to 15 months.6–8

Since the introduction of PCV13, the incidence of IPD among children in Taiwan has decreased annually, especially the disease caused by serotype 19A, which is covered by PCV13. However, an increase in incidence due to non-PCV13 serotypes was also observed in recent years.7 Higher-valent vaccines with increased serotype coverage are now available, including 20-valent PCV (PCV20), which received approval for pediatric use (aged 6 weeks to 17 y) from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2023, the European Commission in 2024, and Taiwan’s FDA in December 2024, under a 3+1 vaccination schedule.9–11

To assess the potential disease impact and economic benefits of higher-valent PCVs, this cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) estimated the clinical and economic value of implementing a PCV20 vaccination program in the Taiwanese pediatric population vs. the current standard of care (SoC; PCV13), as well as a hypothetical 15-valent PCV (PCV15) for contextual comparison. The analysis also evaluated the potential immediate consequences (i.e., health benefits and cost-savings forgone) of a 1- and 2-y delay in implementing PCV20 into the pediatric National Immunization Program (NIP).

Methods

Model overview

A previously published multiple-cohort population-based Markov model, which underwent both conceptual and technical validations during the development stage, was adapted to conduct a CEA from both payer and societal perspectives, with a 10-y time horizon and an annual discount rate of 3% applied to costs and benefits in line with Taiwan’s guidelines for health technology assessment.12–18

In Taiwan, the payer perspective represents government-funded healthcare expenditures, including National Health Insurance – reimbursed medical services and publicly funded vaccine procurement through the Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (TCDC). Accordingly, the payer perspective included only direct medical and vaccination costs borne by the healthcare system.

A societal perspective was evaluated to capture broader economic costs associated with pneumococcal disease and vaccination. In addition to direct medical and vaccination costs, the societal perspective included indirect costs of vaccine administration (e.g., travel costs) and productivity losses attributable to pneumococcal disease. While productivity losses due to work absenteeism were considered for adult patients, productivity loss associated with children reflected caregiver time losses from healthcare visits and disease episodes. Considering both perspectives provided a more comprehensive assessment of the value of PCV20 vaccination for decision-making in Taiwan. Similar to previous studies, the model also incorporated annual transitions between Markov states, as described in previous studies.12–16 During each 12-month cycle, individuals could transition into one of three distinct health states, regardless of vaccination status: (1) active disease, in which individuals could experience one or more pneumococcal disease (i.e., allowing for simultaneous multiple infections and reinfections); (2) pneumococcal disease-free; or (3) absorbing death. Age groups were used to stratify epidemiological characteristics such as disease incidence, disease fatality, vaccine effectiveness, disease-associated costs, and quality of life.

Following the common approach in vaccine modeling and inherited from previous economic evaluations of PCV20, the model captured the full impact of the vaccination program over 10 y accounting for the entire population and incorporating multiple birth cohorts over the modeled time horizon, thereby reflecting dynamic changes in population structure over time. At the beginning of each year, a new cohort consisting of infants aged <12 months entered the model and was assumed to be vaccinated at a constant vaccine uptake rate of 96.80% (based on 2023 data).19 These infants were followed over time, accounting for disease risk, hospitalizations, and mortality as they aged. Simultaneously, the existing population was also followed, with age-specific characteristics applied at each time step, including aspects such as disease incidence, case-fatality rates, and general mortality. Infants aged <2 y were the primary vaccination target, while older individuals were assumed to be unvaccinated, except for 12.4% of adults aged ≥65 y who were assumed to be covered by the adult immunization program and excluded from receiving indirect effects.20 By continuously simulating new birth cohorts and tracking all individuals over time, the model reflects the evolving population structure and captures both direct and indirect effects of vaccination across different age groups.

In the current analysis, the model was updated to the Taiwan context by incorporating country-specific data and reflecting the current local clinical practice. To align with the current recommended vaccination schedule for PCV13, a PCV20 2+1 dosing schedule was considered the base case analysis, with both primary doses administered during the first year of life and the booster dose administered at months 12 to 15, as per Taiwan’s guidelines.8 However, the cost-effectiveness based on a PCV20 3+1 dosing schedule was also assessed in a scenario analysis.

The estimated clinical outcomes included the numbers of cases of IPD, hospitalized pneumonia, non-hospitalized pneumonia, complex otitis media (OM), and simple OM, as well as pneumococcal disease-related deaths. These clinical outcomes were used to estimate economic outcomes associated with the disease, life years (LY), and quality-adjusted LYs (QALY), as well as incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICER) between vaccine strategies.

Inputs

The model was tailored for Taiwan with local inputs including epidemiological data (serotype coverage, disease incidence and case fatality rates), population and general mortality data, healthcare resource use and cost inputs, as well as vaccination program characteristics being prioritized. Where local data were not available, inputs from prior published PCV models and the latest non-Taiwanese published literature, such as vaccine effectiveness estimates, baseline utilities, and pneumococcal disease disutilities, were used as proxies, with assumptions clearly stated (Table 1). Epidemiology

The model utilized Taiwan population estimates from 2023 to determine the size of age-stratified population groups and annual birth cohort projections.23,35 Age-specific disease incidence rates per 100,000 individuals and the breakdown of IPD cases (meningitis vs. bacteremia) were informed by Taiwan-specific data, from a report by Taiwan’s Center for Disease Control and previous study by Lu et al. with age adjustments made using population size data.21–23 In this study, mortality was considered as a combination of both overall population mortality and disease-specific fatality rates applied to cases of IPD (assumed the same for meningitis and bacteremia) from Lu et al. and hospitalized pneumonia from Lu et al.22,24,36 No disease-specific mortality was assumed to be attributed to cases of non-hospitalized pneumonia, complex OM, or simple OM. Long-term complications of pneumococcal infections were excluded from the analysis due to insufficient data specific to Taiwan. Serotype coverage for each vaccine was stratified by age group based on averaged 2018 to 2021 Taiwan-specific data sourced from Taiwan Centers for Disease Control.21,37–39

Utility

To estimate the quality of life associated with each vaccination strategy, the model employed a baseline utility score for the general population adjusted by subtracting disutility values associated with specific pneumococcal disease events.25 Due to lack of Taiwan-specific tariff for EQ-5D data, a study from China by Li et al.25 was used as reference, which was adjusted using Taiwan 2023 population data.

Resource use and costs

All costs were expressed in New Taiwan dollars (NT$), and direct and indirect medical costs were inflated to 2025 using the consumer price index from the National Statistics Republic of China (Taiwan).32 For analyses performed from the payer perspective, only direct medical costs associated with pneumococcal disease events and direct vaccination costs were considered. Direct medical (pneumococcal disease management) cost inputs were sourced from a previously published study in Taiwan (Lu et al. 2022), with underlying unit costs based on 2018 price levels. These costs were inflated to 2025 price level to reflect the current disease management expenditure.24,32 For analyses performed from the societal perspective, direct costs, indirect costs of vaccine administration (i.e., travel), and indirect costs resulting from pneumococcal disease (i.e., productivity loss) from Wu et al. (2013) were considered.18 For pediatric population, productivity impacts were estimated based on caregiver time losses due to healthcare visits and disease episodes, while direct work loss among adult patients was accounted as indirect costs for adult population. Similarly, these costs were adjusted to 2025 pricing values using latest CPI data.32,33

Vaccine effectiveness

Due to the limited Taiwan-specific data, vaccine effectiveness parameters were mainly derived from previous PCV CEAs (Detailed inputs are reported in Supplementary Table 1).12–16 Adverse events were not included in the analysis, as the safety of PCV13, demonstrated in multiple international studies with rare treatment-related adverse events and no serious events or deaths, supported the approval of the higher-valent PCV15 and PCV20, which exhibited similar safety profiles.40–43

The assessment covered the entire population, capturing both direct and indirect vaccine benefits. Direct effects were immediate within the vaccinated group, while indirect effects gradually accrued across the population over the 10 y horizon.

In the absence of direct effectiveness data for higher-valent vaccines (PCV15/PCV20), vaccine effectiveness inputs were derived using an established extrapolation approach and were applied as an average vaccine effectiveness estimate across serotypes, consistent with previous studies.41,44–47 Direct effects for PCV15 and PCV20 were based on a real-world study across nine European countries for PCV13 (88.7%) and extrapolated to additional serotypes based on vaccine serotype coverage.48 For non-invasive disease, efficacy data from PCV7 trials were used, adjusting for the current serotype coverage of each vaccine vs. PCV7 coverage in 2000.49–51 These results demonstrated an efficacy of 25.5%, 6.0%, 12.3%, and 6.4% against radiographically confirmed non-invasive hospitalized pneumonia, non-hospitalized pneumonia, complex and simple OM, respectively. To align with the vaccine 2+1 schedule with the booster dose given after the age of 1 y, the direct effect among individuals aged <1 y was assumed to be at 67% of the total direct effect (i.e., reduced vaccine effectiveness due to incomplete schedule within the first year of life), corresponding to two-thirds of the full impact from a complete schedule. In the scenario under the 3+1 schedule, the direct effect adjustment was set at 75% of the total direct effect (i.e., equivalent to roughly three-fourths of the full impact from a complete vaccine schedule).

Indirect effects (i.e., the vaccination effect on unvaccinated population) considered in this analysis were limited to newly covered serotypes by PCV15 and PCV20, excluding those already included in PCV13, given its use in Taiwan for almost 10 y, and were assumed to gradually ramp-up over time after the new vaccine is introduced in the pediatric NIP. Input data for indirect effect were derived using published post-implementation data for PCV13 in Taiwan and some European settings with robust surveillance systems and similarly high vaccine uptake and extrapolated to newly covered serotypes of higher-valent PCVs, with adjustments for epidemiological differences. Model analyses

The base-case analysis (i.e., deterministic analysis) was based on the settings and inputs described above. To evaluate uncertainty in the model, probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), deterministic sensitivity analysis (DSA), and scenario analyses were conducted. The base-case analysis was conducted from both payer and societal perspectives over 10 y, whereas all sensitivity and scenario analyses were conducted from a societal perspective.

In addition, the public health and economic impacts of postponing PCV20 implementation into the pediatric NIP over periods of 1 and 2 y in Taiwan were assessed. These analyses quantified the potential burden of disease, both in terms of disease cases and deaths as well as disease-associated costs that would have been prevented with the introduction of PCV20 instead of the continued use of PCV13 or hypothetical implementation of PCV15.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the base-case results over a 10-y horizon. A PSA was performed to address the joint uncertainty of the underlying parameter estimates, using a standard error of 20%. The analysis was conducted with 1,000 iterations at a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of NT$1,230,781 per QALY gained, which was equal to one time Taiwan’s gross domestic product (GDP) per capita.59,60 Additionally, a DSA was performed by altering individual parameters to upper and lower values using a variance of 20%.

To further explore the robustness of the model conclusions to different assumptions, the following scenarios were evaluated: Serotype coverage: applying pre-COVID serotype coverage only (2018 to 2019) to explore the impact of variation in serotype distribution data on the base-case results, which were affected by underreporting issues during the pandemic.Pneumonia incidence: applying non-invasive disease incidence estimates from Huang et al. 2022, which reported relatively higher incidence than the study by Lu et al. 2020 used in the base case.3,22PCV20 3+1 vaccination schedule: a four-dose schedule (three primary doses administered at ages 2, 4, and 6 months, with a booster dose between ages 12 and 15 months) for PCV20, per the Taiwan FDA, was tested.10,61Serotype replacement: assuming serotype replacement in PCV15 and PCV20-specific serotypes, at an annual reduction of 5% (moderate replacement) and 10% (high replacement). While serotype replacement has been observed following the previous introduction of PCV7 and PCV13, the extent of serotype replacement following the new vaccines, such as PCV15 and PCV20, remains uncertain. Discussion

This is the first study investigating the cost and health impact of introducing PCV20 into Taiwan’s pediatric NIP. The goal of this economic evaluation was to assess the clinical and economic impact as well as the cost-effectiveness of switching from the current SoC (PCV13) to PCV20 into the Taiwan pediatric NIP. The study not only compared PCV20 with the current SoC but also compared it with a potential comparator (PCV15) from both a payer and a societal perspective. In addition, the study also estimated the potential short-term impact of delaying the implementation of PCV20 program in Taiwan.

PCV20 has been estimated to be a cost-effective alternative to current SoC vaccines across multiple countries.13–16 Consistent with studies in other countries, the current study demonstrated that the implementation of PCV20 would prevent a substantial number of pneumococcal disease cases and deaths, as well as result in substantial reduction in associated costs, vs. lower-valent comparators. PCV20 was found to be the dominant vaccination strategy (i.e., more effective and less costly) over both PCV13 and PCV15, all under a 2+1 vaccination schedule. These results were found to be robust across sensitivity and most scenario analyses.

In a scenario considering PCV20 under a 3+1 schedule, although the additional primary dose could increase the vaccination cost and total incremental costs compared with PCV13 and PCV15 under the base-case 2+1 schedule, PCV20 remained cost-effective vs. lower-valent options by reducing pneumococcal disease cases and fatalities at a higher overall cost that fell below the WTP threshold of one time Taiwan’s GDP per capita. These findings align with previous CEAs in which PCV20 was estimated to be dominant over PCV13 2+1 and PCV15 2+1 under both 2+1 and 3+1 schedules.15,62,63 In particular, a CEA from the payer perspective in Greece estimated that PCV20, under the 3+1 dosing schedule, was dominant over PCV13 and PCV15 under 2+1 schedules.63 In a scenario assuming a 2+1 schedule for all vaccines, PCV20 remained dominant, with similar findings to the current study and estimated lower total incremental costs under PCV20 2+1.63 Although the European Medicines Agency recommended the PCV20 for use in a 3+1 schedule, the Greek pediatric NIP recommends PCV20 under a 2+1 schedule for all healthy children aged 2 to 59 months, and under a 3+1 schedule for children at risk of pneumococcal infections, setting a precedent for broader scheduling guidelines for PCV20 implementation in Taiwan’s infant NIP.63

The findings from the current analysis of the short-term impact of delaying PCV20 adoption indicated that meaningful health gains and substantial medical cost savings would be lost even within the first 2 y of the delay when compared with maintaining the current SoC (i.e., PCV13) or using PCV15. For example, continuing with PCV13 for an additional year was estimated to lead to more than 3,000 potentially avoidable cases of pneumococcal disease, with the projected disease burden projected to be more than double by the second year; this would result in almost NT$47 million of cost-savings forgone in Year 1 and over NT$123 million by Year 2. A similar trend emerged in comparison with PCV15, where PCV20 was estimated to consistently deliver greater projected reductions in disease burden over 1 and 2 y.

These findings should, however, be interpreted in light of simplifying assumptions within the delay analyses, including a static epidemiological setting and immediate accrual of vaccine benefits. In the real world, serotype replacement and indirect effects evolve over time, with benefits accruing more gradually, as highlighted in some recent modeling studies.64,65 Hence, the estimated impact of delayed implementation likely represents an approximation of a substantial, yet preventable, burden of pneumococcal disease among Taiwanese children and a significant loss in medical cost savings potentially associated with deferring the transition to PCV20, of which the timing and magnitude of benefits may differ in reality.

The current study was not without limitations. First, the inclusion of PCV15 as a comparator is hypothetical, as it is not currently part of Taiwan’s pediatric NIP. While PCV15 assumptions were largely based on the current PCV13 data in Taiwan as well as being aligned with those used in comparable international modeling studies and tested key uncertainties through scenario analyses, results associated with PCV15 should be interpreted with caution. The PCV15 scenario is intended primarily to provide contextual comparison with PCV20 as a newly approved comparator rather than to predict precise real-world outcomes in Taiwan.

Secondly, the model used epidemiology and cost inputs based on Taiwan-specific data where possible; nonetheless, in the absence of Taiwan-specific data for EQ-5D, utility data from China were used as a proxy and adjusted using Taiwan population data.

In addition, as indicated in previous CEAs of PVC20 in other countries, challenges such as the absence of long-term real-world evidence for newer vaccines like PCV20 remained.13,14,66 In line with the approach used in these analyses, direct vaccine effect inputs of PCV20 were, therefore, extrapolated from earlier PCV evidence, including PCV13 and PCV7 studies, adjusted for serotype coverage. Similarly, indirect effects were based on post-PCV13 implementation data from both Taiwan surveillance study and non-local settings, which are subject to uncertainty, particularly for higher-valent vaccines where real-world evidence is limited. The use of IPD serotype distribution as a proxy for non-invasive diseases may not fully capture potential differences in serotype dynamics. While this approach is widely accepted in economic evaluations, it involves assumptions regarding the transferability of effectiveness across serotypes and disease states, as well as uncertainty in generalization. In addition, the differences in immunogenicity or serotype-specific performance between the higher-valent PCVs and the lower-valent PCVs may not be fully captured. However, extensive sensitivity and scenario analyses were conducted to explore these uncertainties, and the results were robust across a range of alternative assumptions, supporting the validity of the overall conclusions.

Other key limitations highlighted in previous analyses include limitations that are characteristic of the Markov model framework and difficulties in accurately capturing modeling serotype replacement.13–16 The model base case did not explicitly incorporate serotype replacement, as the extent of serotype replacement after the introduction of the higher-valent vaccines, such as PCV15 and PCV20, remains uncertain. As historical evidence from earlier vaccines (i.e., PCV7 and PCV13) has shown heterogeneous patterns across settings and over time, making robust modeling of serotype replacement challenging and potentially introducing considerable uncertainty. A conservative assumption of no replacement was, therefore, applied in the base case. To address this uncertainty, additional scenario assessments exploring moderate and high replacement assumptions were conducted, both of which yielded results consistent with the base case, supporting the robustness of the findings.

Conclusions

Under a 2+1 dosing schedule, vaccinating infants in Taiwan with PCV20 was estimated to be the dominant vaccination strategy vs. PCV13 and PCV15 from both the payer and societal perspectives. The implementation of PCV20 would also result in direct and indirect cost-savings, and QALY gains. While the 3+1 scenario was associated with higher costs, PCV20 was found to be cost-effective vs. both comparators. Delaying the adoption of PCV20 into the Taiwanese pediatric NIP was projected to result in substantial health and economic benefits forgone even in the short term, reinforcing the value of prompt introduction of the higher-valent vaccines. These findings could inform policymakers for future consideration of PCV20 implementation into Taiwan’s infant NIP.
