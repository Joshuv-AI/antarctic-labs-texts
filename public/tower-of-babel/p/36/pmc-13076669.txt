Prevalence of undernutrition among children on anti-retroviral therapy at comprehensive specialized hospital: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Habtu Adane Aytolgn, Daniale Tekelia Ekubagewargies, Mohammed Hassen Salih, Yewlsew Fentie Alle, Shimelis Seid Tegegne, Awoke Alemneh Ayalew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Children living with humanimmuno deficiency virus (CLHIV) are particularly prone to undernutrition. Both HIV/AIDS and undernutrition can lead to progressive deterioration of the immune system, leading to serious opportunistic infections. As a result, undernutrition with CLHIV is associated with a two-fold increase in morbidity and mortality. Evidence on the prevalence and factors associated with undernutrition among CLHIV was still insufficient in the study area. An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted on 391 CLHIV aged 6 months to 18 years at the anti-retroviral therapy clinic of the University of Gondar comprehensive specialized hospital. Data were collected using systematic random sampling from 1st March to 30th March 2022. The collected data were entered into Epi Info version 7 and exported to SPSS version 23 for analysis. Bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were computed. The overall prevalence of undernutrition was 56%. Undernutrition was significantly associated with a history of tuberculosis, the occupation of the caregiver being a merchant, duration of follow-up at ART clinics, poor dietary diversity and lower frequency of feeding. The prevalence of undernutrition among CLHIV was found to be high. Shorter duration of follow-up, occupation of caregiver being merchant, history of TB, low dietary diversity and lower frequency of feeding were significantly associated with undernutrition. We suggest that HIV/AIDS care services and ministry of health to give special concern, providing supplementary food to those vulnerable children. In addition, we suggest that those health organisations to deliver training and counselling to parents and caregivers regarding early initiation of ART, early identification of opportunistic infection and regular follow-up to clinic, nutritional support and feeding practice.

Introduction

Over 20 million childhood morbidities and deaths are thought to be caused by malnutrition1. People with HIV/AIDS frequently experience weight loss and undernutrition, perhaps decreasing survival, raising morbidity, and quickening the course of death2. Malnutrition and HIV frequently coexist in low- and middle-income nations3. According to an African study, 41.6% of children with HIV were malnourished4.

Undernutrition is defined as an insufficient intake of energy, protein, and other nutrients required for proper physiological function5.

The most common manifestations of undernourished CLHIV are weight loss, faltered linear growth (stunting), wasting and underweight6,7. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), HIV has four clinical stages, which are Stage 1 (asymptomatic), stage 2 (mild symptomatic), stage 3 (advanced symptoms) and stage 4 ( with severe symptoms)8,9. In places like Ethiopia, where HIV prevalence is high, children undernutrition may be made worse by HIV/AIDS10. Undernutrition has long-term, frequently permanent impacts on immunity, cognitive development, and physical growth in addition to its immediate clinical implications11.

The bidirectional and synergistic relationship between HIV infection and nutritional deficiencies makes CLHIV susceptible to undernutrition, morbidity and mortality6. Undernutrition in CLHIV usually caused by HIV/AIDS- induced immune impairment, worsens the risk associated with opportunistic infections12. Thus, HIV weakens the immune system and makes people more vulnerable to opportunistic infections, which further decreases food intake, hinders nutrient absorption, and raises metabolic demands13. Therefore, compared to HIV-negative peers, undernutrition among CLHIV significantly raises the risk of disease progression, treatment failure, and mortality often two fold the likelihood of adverse outcomes13–16. Therefore, for the best possible immunological recovery, cognitive growth, and overall treatment effectiveness in CLHIV, adequate and consistent dietary support is crucial17.

With over 56,000 children living with HIV as of 2018, Ethiopia continues to bear a heavy burden of paediatric HIV18. Undernutrition is still common, according to national survey data, with prevalence estimates of 24% for underweight, 36.6% for stunting, and 10% for wasting among children19. Therefore, early screening and management are crucial20.

In the same area there are some studies on non-HIV/AIDS infected children21,22, but there is still little data on the prevalence of undernutrition and the factors on HIV/AIDS positive children on ART, despite the acknowledged significance of nutritional care. In order to improve ART outcomes, strengthen clinical nutrition services, and inform targeted interventions, localised data are essential. For these, we aimed to assess the prevalence of undernutrition and its associated factors among CLHIV on ART follow-up at the University of Gondar comprehensive specialized hospital.

Methods

Study design, period, setting and population

Institution- based cross-sectional study was conducted from 1st March to 30th March 2022 at the University of Gondar comprehensive specialized hospital, paediatrics ART Clinic. The University of Gondar comprehensive specialised hospital is located in the central Gondar town administrative zone, Amhara National Regional State, which is about 750 km Northwest of Addis Ababa (the capital city of Ethiopia). Currently Gondar town has one comprehensive specialised hospital, one general hospital, eight government health centres, two private hospitals and nine private clinics. The University of Gondar comprehensive specialized hospital is a teaching hospital which serves more than five million people of the North Gondar zone and people of the neighbouring zones. The HIV/AIDS care service of the hospital was initiated in 2005 and has outpatient departments of Adult ART OPD (outpatient department) and Paediatric ART OPD.

All CLHIV who were followed up at the paediatrics ART clinic from the age of 6 months to 18 years during the study period were included. Exclusion criteria included consent refusal by caregivers and older children, language barriers and children with mental health conditions.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula determined by assuming a 42% prevalence rate of malnutrition (given the previous baseline proportion of malnutrition among CLHIV in Northwest Ethiopia23. By using a 95% confidence interval and a margin of error of 5%, the final sample size was determined as follows:

Sampling technique and procedure

The Systematic random sampling method was used to select those participants. After retrospectively observing the number of children attending ART clinic for one month, we got (N = 1236). So, by dividing this potential number of CLHIV for the sample size (N = 412), sampling interval k was equal to 3. Therefore, every K of CLHIV attending the ART clinic was included in the study.

Variable of the study

Undernutrition among CLHIV was the outcome variable of this study. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines undernutrition presenting in four forms: when the child is below two standard deviations (<-2SD) for weight-for-age (underweight), weight–for-height (wasting) and height-for-age (stunting) and lastly with micronutrient deficiencies24,25.

Sociodemographic factors (age, sex, residence, religion, birth order, educational and marital status of caregiver, children living with and family size); clinical factors (history of current or recurrent oral sores, diarrhea, history of tuberculosis, duration of ART clinic follow-up, baseline and current staging of WHO and CD4 count); dietary factors and environmental sanitation-related factors were independent variables.

Data collection Procedure and quality control

Data were collected using a combination of the current Federal Ministry of Health ART clinic intake and follow-up forms and anthropometric measurement by trained data collectors. The questionnaire was first prepared in the English language and translated into the Amharic language (the National as well as the public language in the study area) and then translated back to English. Two-day training was given for data collectors. The principal investigator and assigned supervisor controlled the data collection process. Data collectors met patients and their caregivers and gave appropriate information to the caregivers and children based on the child’s level of understanding. Both assent and consent were taken in children 12–18 years. Whereas, for under 12 years after they agreed orally up to their level of understanding, caregivers were involved in informed consent for assent26,27. This pretested questionnaire consisted of socio-demographic, dietary-related and environmental related data were collected from care givers while clinical related data were collected from patient’s medical record.

Anthropometric measurements were based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and WHO guidelines28,29.

Participants were weighed and measured once while wearing lightweight clothing. Children aged less than 2 years were laid horizontally and weighed using a children’s weighing scale with a precision of 0.05 kg. Similarly, their length was measured while stretched in a recumbent position using a measuring tape with a precision of 0.01 m. Whereas, Children aged 2–18 years were weighed standing unassisted and barefooted on a digital weight scale with a precision of 0.1 kg. Their height was also measured using a stadiometer with a precision of 0.01 m after standing straight on a smooth, flat horizontal surface with their heels together, eyes straight forward and touching the standing board at the heels, buttocks and the back of the head.

Dietary diversity was evaluated by a dietary diversity score calculated based on the seven food groups (according to the food agriculture organization dietary diversity score, 2011)30. Where caregivers were asked which of 16 food groups the children consumed in the past 24 h.

Poor dietary diversity was defined as when a child consumed a dietary diversity score < 4 food groups30, while good dietary diversity was defined as when child ate a dietary diversity score of ≥ 430.

The adherence status of the child to the ART was recorded as poor, fair and good based on the missed pill count. Adherence was poor when the child took less than 85% of the dose, fair when he/she took 85–94% of the dose and good when he/she took 95% and above of the dose31 according to the ART card summary guide.

Data processing and analysis

Data were cleaned, coded and entered into Epi-info version 7 and exported to the Statistical package for social science (SPSS) version 23 for analysis. Then, weights for age, weight for height, height for age and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) were determined using the software Anthro 3.2.2 and Anthro plus version. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and proportions were computed and presented using texts, graphs and tables. Both bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression models were carried out. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.2 in the bi-variable analysis were entered into the multivariable analysis. Finally, a p-value of less than 0.05 in the multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify variables significantly associated with undernutrition. Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with the 95% confidence interval was estimated to show the strength of associations.

Result

Socio demographics characteristics

The study included 391 CLHIV from a total of 412 samples, with a 95% response rate. About 21 children’s data were excluded because their records were incomplete. Almost half (51.2%) of them were female. While the mean age and (± SD) of children was 12.81 ± 5.8 years, ranging from 6 months to 17 years. The majority of study participants (92.1%) came from urban areas (Table 1). Most of them (89.8%) were living with their parents (Table 1). Furthermore, the majority of caregivers (76.7%) were married, and almost one-third (39.4%) had completed elementary school. Half of those CLHIV were in a family size of more than four (Table 1). In terms of income, about 58.8% of their family earned less than $22 USD each month ($). More than half (66.2%) of CLHIV were school-age groups (as shown in Table 1 below).

Prevalence of undernutrition among children living with HIV/AIDS

The prevalence of undernutrition among CLHIV was 219 (56%). The percentage of stunting, wasting and underweight were (45%, 20.5% & 6.9%) respectively (see Fig. 2).

Factors associated with under nutrition

Following multivariable analysis, undernutrition was found to be strongly correlated with the length of ART follow-up, the caregivers work as merchants, history of TB, dietary diversity and feeding frequency. Therefore, undernutrition was 1.7 times (95%, CI = 1.08, 2.61) more common in children with less than 25 months of ART follow-up than in those with a longer follow-up of 75 months.

Similarly, children with history of TB were 1.7 times more likely to be undernourished than those without TB (95%, CI = 1.02, 2.82). Additionally, compared to housewives, children whose caregivers were merchants had a two-fold higher risk of under-nutrition (95%, CI = 1.09, 3.40) (Table 5). Compared to children with adequate dietary diversity, those with poor dietary diversity were 2.8 times more likely to have undernutrition. Furthermore, children who were fed less frequently were nearly twice as likely to experience undernutrition as those who were fed more frequently (see Table 5).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated factors of undernutrition among CLIHV on ART follow-up at ART clinic in University of Gondar comprehensive specialized hospital, Northwest Ethiopia. The overall prevalence of undernutrition was found to be 56% (CI 51.2–61.1) with stunting, wasting and underweight (45%, 20.5% & 6.9%) respectively. The finding of the undernutrition specifically stunting was similar but higher wasting than studies conducted in the same study area on non-HIV/ADS infected children21,22. The higher wasting in this study might be due to HIV/AIDS infection, ART and related opportunistic infection. The other difference could be that in this study the age range was 6 months to17 years, while the other two studies aged 6 to 59 months. This finding was similar with studies conducted in the East and West Gojjam Zones and Southern Ethiopia 60.2% and 50%, respectively32,33 and Sudan34. The possible explanation might be the characteristics participants’ age category, similar socioeconomic and educational level of caregivers. In this study the prevalence of underweight was higher than studies conducted on general children in Ethiopia35, western Ethiopia36 and Northwest Ethiopia: 36.8%, 42% and 28%, respectively10,23. Moreover, the finding was higher than studies conducted in Zambia 27%37, Tanzania 48%38, Cameroon 38%39 and Ghana 28%40. This difference might be due to socio-economic difference, age difference, time difference and study settings. In this study the prevalence of stunting, wasting and underweight were 45%, 20.5% & 6.9% respectively which was lower than in other study6. The reason might be age difference and socio-demographic variation.

In the current study children who took ART for less than 25 months was more likely to develop undernutrition compared with those children who took it for more than 25 months. This is in line with another study in North Wollo, Ethiopia41. Studies show that CLHIV taking ART for more than 25 months had normal nutritional status with improved hemoglobin and albumin level than those taking it for less than 25 months42. Furthermore, longer duration of ART medication is associated with improved nutritional status through decreasing opportunistic infection43.

But in contrast to the current study, oropharyngeal disease and chronic diarrhea and poor adherence to ART were determinant factors of undernutrition41. The difference might be study design (cross-sectional vs. case-control), socio-demographic and sample size. In reality, oropharyngeal disease and chronic diarrhea cause undernutrition as oropharyngeal ulcers affects the children’s ability to eat or drink and persistent diarrhea is causes loss of body fluid and electrolytes. Poor adherence to ART also facilitates opportunistic infection which further mobilises body energy with subsequent undernutrition44.

History of TB was another factor associated with undernutrition which was supported by other studies in children16,23,41 and adults on ART follow-up45. The reason might be TB infection by itself causes loss of appetite and weight loss and most of the children on anti TB treatment have decreased appetite. Drug-to-drug interaction might also cause another adverse event related with gastrointestinal malabsorption, diarrhea and vomiting. Another possible reason may be anti-TB medication and ART drugs’ systemic toxicity46. The co-infection of tuberculosis with HIV increases production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, resulting in the breakdown of body fat and proteins which results in malnutrition termed as the “triple problem”46.

Undernutrition among children whose mother/caregiver’s occupation was merchant was twice (AOR = 2(1.09–3.42)) when compared to those whose occupation was a housewife. This is supported with other evidence: housewife caregivers practice increased frequency of feeding compared with mothers working outside the home who may feed the child less frequently47.

On the other hand, larger family size, lack of caregivers nutritional counselling and advanced WHO clinical staging were significant factor for undernutrition in other studies48,49 but not in this study. The probable reason for the difference might be differences in socio-demographics and sample size. In fact, nutritional counselling plays a key role for optimal feeding of children. Even though advanced WHO staging causes undernutrition as opportunistic infections cause energy loss, diarrhea and appetite loss, more than 90% of the children were stage Ⅰ in this study. In contrast to our study, low CD4 level is significantly associated with undernutrition50. The difference may be socio demographics: children vs. adults, hospital-based vs. facility-based. In fact lower CD4 count implies a weak immune system so they are highly susceptible to opportunistic infection and resulting undernutrition50.

In this study children having poor dietary diversity were 2.8 times more likely to develop undernutrition which is line with another study conducted in Eastern Ethiopia51 and Amhara region referral hospitals52. The reason might be parents and caregivers may not have awareness of the importance of dietary diversity. The other reason might also be lack of income to provide different food groups in a resource constrained area.

Furthermore, feeding less than four times per day was associated with undernutrition. This is consistent with another study in children49,53 and adults on ART medication45. The possible reason might be related with access to food due to resource constraints, as it is explained in the result that around 40% of their income is less than 1000 ETB. Studies show that malnutrition in CLHIV is associated with early death54. Thus, undernutrition in CLHIV is an indicator of impaired response to ART, which is also significant factor for impaired brain growth, reduced capability for education, increased hospital admissions and higher mortality rate55–57. Supplementary feeding intervention, including adequate protein, lipid, vitamins, carbohydrate and pharmacological appetite stimulants can significantly increase the responses for normal nutritional status in CLHIV12. Therefore, those children need nutritional support with nutrient-rich diversified food to normal growth, to boot immunity and to resist metabolic change related with ART42.

Strength and limitations

The strength of this study might be prospective and may be the first study in the study area.

However, the cross-sectional nature of the study limited the investigation to the level of the association between determinants and undernutrition. Another limitation might be the seasonal variation related to the intake of food was not considered and dietary data were reported by parents only within 24 h feeding habit.

Conclusion and recommendation

The prevalence of undernutrition among CLHIV attending ART follow-up at University of Gondar comprehensive specialized hospital was high. Duration of follow-up, occupation of the mothers/ caregiver, history of tuberculosis, dietary diversity and frequency of feeding were significantly associated factors. Therefore, we suggest that HIV/AIDS care services and the ministry of health to give special attention, providing supplementary food to these vulnerable age groups. In addition, we suggest that these health organizations to organize and deliver training and counselling to parents and caregivers regarding early initiation of ART, early identification of opportunistic infection and regular follow-up to clinic, nutritional support and feeding practice. Parents and caregivers need to understand the importance of early ART initiation, adherence, continued follow-up and look out for symptoms of concomitant infections like tuberculosis, diarrhea, loss of appetite, weight loss and cough and to seek clinic early. Furthermore, health education will help the parents and caregivers will understand the benefit of the use of different food groups in their diet, frequency of meals and food preparation to ensure that those children are getting adequate nutrition. We suggest future research follow up with large sample size incorporating dietary diversity (biochemistry).
