Integrated network pharmacology, molecular simulations, biophysical validation, and experimental validation to reveal the pharmacological effects and targets of Senkyunolide A against inflammation and oxidative stress
Authors: Yue Liu, Guang Yang, Xiansu Chi, Liuding Wang, Yunfan Zhang, Xiao Liang, Yunling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inflammatory response and oxidative stress interact with each other and are involved in the pathogenesis of various chronic diseases. Senkyunolide A (SenA) is a phthalide compound isolated from the traditional Chinese medicine Chuanxiong Rhizoma (Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort.). At present, the anti‑inflammatory and anti‑oxidative stress effects of SenA remain unclear. In this study, we adopted an integrated strategy combining network pharmacology, bioinformatics analysis, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, bio‑layer interferometry (BLI), and in vitro experiments to explore the anti‑inflammatory and anti‑oxidative stress effects and potential targets of SenA. Through network pharmacology and bioinformatics analysis, we identified four core target genes (Il1b, Ptgs2, Nos2, and Hmox1) of SenA against LPS‑induced inflammation in RAW264.7 cells. Direct binding of SenA to IL‑1β and PTGS2 was confirmed by molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and BLI assays. In vitro experiments showed that SenA pretreatment effectively inhibited LPS‑induced inflammatory response and oxidative stress in RAW264.7 cells, as evidenced by reduced expression of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑6, and IL‑1β), decreased levels of NO, ROS, and MDA, increased GSH levels, and alleviated cell swelling and mitochondrial damage. In addition, SenA pretreatment downregulated the mRNA expression levels of the core target genes Il1b, Ptgs2, Nos2, and Hmox1. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that SenA exerts significant anti‑inflammatory and anti‑oxidative stress effects and may serve as a candidate compound for the treatment of inflammation‑related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05459-3.

Introduction

Inflammation is a defensive response of the body to external harmful stimuli and a natural protective mechanism of the human body [1]. However, excessive and persistent inflammatory responses can cause damage to the body. Macrophages are key components of the innate immune system and can polarize into pro-inflammatory M1-type or anti-inflammatory M2-type macrophages under different microenvironmental stimuli [2]. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a major component of the gram-negative bacterial cell wall, acts as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) that drives macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype and induces the secretion of large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) [3].

RAW264.7 is a murine macrophage cell line and an ideal model for studying macrophage functions, inflammatory responses, and immunomodulatory mechanisms. Currently, RAW264.7 cells activated by LPS are the most commonly used inflammation model in in vitro studies [4–6]. Upon LPS-induced activation of RAW264.7 murine macrophages, toll-like receptor-4 (TLR-4) is triggered to identify and bind to LPS. This binding event further initiates the activation of numerous intracellular signaling cascades, including the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) pathways. Ultimately, this process promotes the synthesis and release of a variety of inflammatory signaling molecules, such as nitric oxide (NO), TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), thereby provoking a cytokine storm [7].

Inflammation and oxidative stress are interdependent and mutually amplifying pathological mechanisms. Under inflammatory conditions, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [8]. When ROS production is excessive or scavenging is insufficient, an overabundance of ROS accumulates locally, resulting in oxidative stress [9]. Excessive ROS production drives macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and thereby amplifying inflammation [10]. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress are major drivers in the initiation and progression of numerous chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, autoimmune conditions, and cancer [11–13]. Consequently, effectively suppressing inflammatory and oxidative responses has become a central therapeutic strategy and a primary focus of current research [14].

At present, conventional pharmacological therapies for chronic inflammation and oxidative stress mainly include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids and synthetic antioxidants [15]. Nevertheless, long-term administration of these drugs is often limited by severe adverse effects, such as gastrointestinal hemorrhage, renal injury and systemic metabolic disorders [15–17]. In addition, single-target synthetic antioxidants have limited bioavailability and fail to achieve satisfactory clinical outcomes. Natural products, owing to their potential anti-inflammatory activities, have emerged as a crucial source for the development of novel drugs against various diseases [18]. Senkyunolide A (SenA, PubChem CID: 3085257), a phthalide compound extracted from Chuanxiong Rhizoma (Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort.), has recently been reported to display a wide range of pharmacological actions, such as regulating autophagy, alleviating osteoarthritis, retarding atherosclerosis, ameliorating liver fibrosis [19–21]. However, while its general pharmacological potential is recognized, the specific molecular targets and the integrated regulatory network of SenA against LPS-induced macrophage activation remain insufficiently explored. In this study, we focus on and explore the potential anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative stress effects of SenA. We comprehensively employed multiple methods to explore the targets and mechanisms underlying the anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative stress effects of Sen A, including network pharmacology, bioinformatics, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, bio‑layer interferometry (BLI), and in vitro experiments. Materials and methods

Collection of the potential targets of Sen A

First, the full compound name “Senkyunolide A” was used as the search keyword to screen its potential targets on the Traditional Chinese Medicine System Pharmacology (TCMSP, http://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php) platform and the Human Gene Database (GeneCards, https://www.genecards.org/) platform. The canonical SMILES structure of Senkyunolide A was downloaded from the PubChem database and subsequently uploaded to the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and Targetnet database (http://targetnet.scbdd.com/) for target prediction and screening, with the screening threshold set at a predicted probability > 0. Second, all target data retrieved from TCMSP, GeneCards, SwissTargetPrediction and Targetnet were summarized and duplicates were eliminated to obtain the final dataset of potential targets of SenA for subsequent bioinformatics analysis. Searches covered all publications from database inception through May 14, 2025.

Microarray data retrieval

The keywords “LPS” and “RAW264.7” were used to search the expression datasets from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The selected dataset, identified as GSE159346, purified cytoplasmic, nucleoplasmic, and nucleolar fractions from macrophages for a time course (0, 2, 6, 12, or 15 h) of LPS (80 ng/ml) stimulation and performed RNA-seq.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes

We further analyzed the nucleolar differentially expressed genes (DEGs) data from RAW264.7 cells at 0 and 15 h post-LPS intervention. The “limma differential analysis” module on the online bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) was used to identify DEGs. The screening criteria for DEGs set as |Fold change (FC)| ≥ 2 and p < 0.05, ensuring that only genes with both statistical significance and biological relevance were included in the analysis. These screened differential genes are the genes that undergo significant changes after LPS stimulation of macrophages.

Subsequently, these DEGs were imported into the Metascape (http://metascape.org) for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses.

Intersection target acquisition and Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network construction

We imported the targets of SenA and the identified DEGs into the Draw Venn Diagram program (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/) to construct a venn diagram for visualizing intersection targets. To retrieve PPI network information, these genes were uploaded to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), with the minimum interaction score threshold set at 0.4 (medium confidence). Then, the PPI data was imported into Cytoscape 3.10.3 software for visual display, and six algorithms (MCC, DMNC, MNC, Degree, EPC, and Closeness) were used to calculate the “Node Scores”. For each algorithm, the top 5 genes with the highest scores were designated as key genes. Subsequently, these algorithm-identified key genes were intersected, and the genes present in all intersections were defined as “hub genes”. All online database and web-tool analyses in this study were conducted on May 20, 2025.

Molecular docking

The structures of receptor proteins were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank database (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org). The PyMOL software is used for protein receptor optimization, including the removal of water molecules and original ligands. Notably, essential cofactors required for protein activity were carefully preserved within the binding pockets to ensure the biological relevance of the docking environment. The 2D structural ligand was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and then imported into ChemDraw software to create the 3D chemical structures and minimize their energy. The receptor proteins in pdbqt format was imported into AutoDockTools 1.5.7 for the construction of docking binding pockets. Molecular docking and binding affinity calculations were performed using AutoDock Vina to identify the most favorable interactions. For comprehensive dissection of the aforementioned interactions, we utilized the Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP, https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index) to unravel the underlying binding forces between ligand molecules and target proteins. The visualization of the docking results was conducted via PyMOL.

Molecular dynamics

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022 software, and the force field parameters were generated via the pdb2gmx tool embedded in GROMACS 2022. The force field parameters for the ligand and receptor protein were assigned using the GAFF2 and AMBER99SB force fields, respectively. The system was solvated in a cubic TIP3P water box with an edge length of 1 nm. Counterions were added using the gmx genion tool to maintain overall charge neutrality. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, with the cutoff distance set to 1.0 nm. The LINCS algorithm was applied to constrain bond lengths in the system. Before the production simulation, the system underwent two steps of energy minimization. First, 3000 steepest descent steps were performed to quickly eliminate unfavorable contacts. Then, fine-tuning was conducted using the conjugate gradient method over 2000 steps. The simulation was conducted under NPT ensemble conditions at 310 K for 100 ns. Post-simulation, trajectory analysis was performed using the software’s built-in tools to calculate the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), the stability and dynamic characteristics of the system were evaluated.

Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI) for measuring binding kinetic

BLI is a highly sensitive, label-free technique that allows real-time monitoring of biomolecular interactions. Once target proteins are immobilized on the sensor, BLI can capture and screen their binding molecules via an affinity enrichment strategy [22]. To further validate the predicted interaction, we performed BLI experiment. All BLI assays were conducted on an Octet R2 (Sartorius, Shanghai, China) instrument. Briefly, freshly prepared recombinant IL‑1β (50101-M49H-B, SinoBiological, China) and PTGS2 (Ag24721, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) proteins were pre-coupled to Ni-NTA biosensors (Sartorius, Germany). SenA (ranging from 12.5 to 400 µM) were utilized to evaluate the dose-response dynamics of binding and dissociation. The binding and dissociation time was set to 60 s. Kinetic analyses were done using Octet Analysis Studio (13.0).

In vitro validation

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells were purchased from Wuhan Zishan Institutes for Biological Sciences (Wuhan, China) and cultured using Dulbecco’s Minimum Essential Medium (DMEM) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100U/mL penicillin. Cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37℃.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured at 37℃ and 5% CO2 for 24 h. When the cells were in the logarithmic phase, they were treated with different concentrations of SenA (B21461, Yuanye, Shanghai, China) solution (12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µM), and incubated for 24 h. Then, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, G4103, ServiceBio, Wuhan, China) solution was added to each well for 2 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured by a microplate reader (BioTek, Vermont, USA).

Determination of Nitric Oxide (NO) concentration

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Then they were pre-treated with different concentrations of SenA for 2 h, followed by treatment with the LPS (80 ng/ml, GC205009, ServiceBio, Wuhan, China) for 15 h. Pretreatment is a classic and widely adopted experimental design to evaluate the preventive and protective potential of natural products against inflammatory injury. In numerous in vitro studies exploring the anti‑inflammatory effects of natural products, a 1–2 h pretreatment duration ensures sufficient cellular uptake of the drug and achieves a steady intracellular concentration prior to LPS stimulation, thereby exerting effective regulatory functions [5, 23–25]. The supernatant of cell culture solution of each group was collected and detected according to the NO detection kit (S0021, Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α detection

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well. After culturing with the drug and LPS, The culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min. Finally, the cellular abundances of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were detected according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the ELISA kits (CEK1514, CEK1485, and CEK1565, Cohesion, UK).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH) assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well, and incubated with the drugs and LPS as previously described. The relative MDA concentration in cell lysates was determined by a MDA assay kit (G4300, ServiceBio, Wuhan, China). The relative GSH concentration in cell was measured using a GSH assay kit (G4305, ServiceBio, Wuhan, China).

Assessment of ROS

Intracellular ROS level was evaluated using the 2, 7-dichlorodihy drofluroresenin diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe (D6883, Sigma, USA). The RAW264.7 cells were prepared and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA solution at 37 ℃ for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium. The cells were observed under the full-field fluorescence scanning analyzer (CellAnalyzer Pro, lianhuazhizao, Wuhan, China), and photographs were taken in randomly selected fields of view.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Cell samples were collected and fixed in a solution containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde (G1102, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Afterwards, postfixation was performed using 1% osmium tetroxide, along with dehydration. The sections were then embedded in epon, and this was followed by staining with uranyl acetate. Images were taken using a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi).

Quantitative reverse transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (q-PCR) analysis

The mRNA expression levels of hub genes were measured using q-PCR. Total RNA was isolated with the TRIzol reagent (DP424, Tiangen, China). Subsequently, total RNA was prepared into cDNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (FSQ-201, TOYOBO, China). q-PCR was carried out using the 2×Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (G3326, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All primer sequences were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and were listed in Table 1. The level of expression of the target gene was calculated using 2^−ΔΔCT after q-PCR detection.

Statistical analysis

All data were shown as mean ±standard deviation. SPSS Statistics 25 software and GraphPad Prism 8.0 were employed for statistical analysis. Statistical analysis was performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by LSD test as a post hoc test and p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Prediction of potential targets of SenA

By combining the results obtained from TCMSP, GeneCards, SwissTargetPrediction and Targetnet, and removing the duplicate data, we finally identified 169 potential targets of SenA (Supplementary Information Ⅰ).

Screening and analysis of differential genes in RAW264.7 cells stimulated by LPS

From GSE159346, 1100 DEGs (657 downregulated, 443 upregulated) were identified and imported into Metascape for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, showing significant enrichment in 260 GO terms and 66 pathways (Supplementary Information Ⅱ). The top 20 enriched GO terms/pathways are displayed via bubble plots using the bioinformatics website (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/ ) (Figs. 2A, B ). Our results indicated that the target genes were highly enriched in a number of molecular functions (MF), including structural constituent of chromatin, catalytic activity, macromolecular conformation isomerase activity, ATP-dependent activity, etc. The biological processes (BP) that are highly enriched include mitotic cell cycle process, chromosome organization, chromosome segregation, positive regulation of defense response, etc. In the cell composition (CC) domain, the target genes were highly enriched in chromosomal region, nucleosome, condensed chromosome, protein-DNA complex, etc. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that inflammation-related pathways include TNF signaling pathway, nuclear transcription factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway.

Construction of PPI network and screening of hub genes

Subsequently, the 169 potential targets of SenA were cross-analyzed with the 1100 DEGs, and a total of 13 overlapped candidate genes were identified (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Information Ⅲ). Using the STRING database, the PPI network was constructed based on these 13 target genes (Fig. 2D). Next, We imported these 13 genes into the Cytoscape software for network visualization, with two free targets (Gpr35 and Abcc1) excluded at this point (Fig. 2E). Key genes in the PPI network were calculated using six algorithms of CytoHubba and subjected to cross-mapping, and finally Il1b, Ptgs2, Nos2, and Hmox1 were identified as hub genes (Supplementary Information Ⅳ).

Molecular dynamics simulation

RMSD is a reliable indicator for evaluating the conformational stability of proteins and ligands; the smaller the deviation, the better the conformational stability. As shown in the Fig. 4A, the IL1β-SenA complex system reached equilibrium after 65 ns and eventually fluctuated steadily around 0.25 nm; the PTGS2-SenA complex system stabilized after 10 ns with a final fluctuation of approximately 0.17 nm; the NOS2-SenA complex system achieved equilibrium at 75 ns and fluctuated around 0.22 nm; the HMOX1-SenA complex system reached equilibrium rapidly after 5 ns and remained stable at about 0.2 nm.Therefore, SenA exhibits high structural stability when binding to the target proteins IL-1β, PTGS2, NOS2, and HMOX1, respectively.

Rg can reflect the overall structural changes and characterize the compactness of protein structure. The IL1β-SenA, PTGS2-SenA, NOS2-SenA, and HMOX1-SenA complexes showed stable fluctuations throughout the dynamic simulation, indicating no obvious expansion or contraction occurred in these complexes during molecular motion ( Fig. 4B).

SASA is an indicator of protein quality surface area, this is the calculation of protein and small molecule surface area ( Fig. 4C), results are shown, after receptor-ligand binding, the SASA values of IL1β-SenA, PTGS2-SenA, NOS2-SenA and HMOX1-SenA complexes showed no obvious changes, further demonstrating stable binding between the small molecule and target proteins.

RMSF indicates the flexibility of the acid residues in the protein properties. As shown in Fig. 4D, the RMSF values of IL1β-SenA, PTGS2-SenA, NOS2-SenA and HMOX1-SenA complexes were all below 0.5 nm, suggesting low flexibility and high structural stability.

The Gibbs free energy graphic was utilized to depict the stability of the receptor-ligand complexes (Fig. 4E). RMSD and Rg were selected to construct landscape maps to detect and explore their steady-state structures. The blue regions signify a low-energy conformational state, indicating stable complex system. When the RMSD value was 0.24 and the Rg value was 1.46, the IL1β-SenA complex was in a relatively stable conformational state. When the RMSD value was 0.13 and the Rg value was 3.145, the PTGS2-SenA complex was in a relatively stable conformational state. When the RMSD value was 0.15 and the Rg value was 2.99, the NOS2-SenA complex was in a relatively stable conformational state. When the RMSD value was 0.14 and the Rg value was 2.38, the HMOX1-SenA complex was in a relatively stable conformational state.

In vitro experimental validation

Effects of SenA on RAW264.7 cell viability

We evaluated the effect of SenA on cell viability, RAW264.7 cells were treated with SenA in different concentrations (12.5 µM, 25 µM, 50 µM, 100 µM and 200 µM) for 24 h. The CCK-8 assays showed that the concentration of SenA at 200 µM had a significant effect on RAW264.7 cell viability, whereas no significant impacts on cell viability were observed at the other tested concentrations (Fig. 6A). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of SenA against RAW264.7 cells was calculated to be 184.5 µM.

Effects of SenA on NO release of LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells

To investigate the anti-inflammatory effects of SenA in vitro, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) - induced RAW264.7 cells were used as the inflammatory model. The results showed that compared with the control group, the level of NO in the cell culture supernatant of the model group was significantly increased; compared with the model group, the level of NO in the cell culture supernatant of the SenA groups were decreased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6B). Specifically, 25 µM, 50 µM and 100 µM of SenA significantly inhibited LPS‑induced NO release, whereas no statistically significant reduction was observed at 12.5 µM. Combined with the cell viability results mentioned above, two concentrations (25 µM and 50 µM) of SenA were chosen for further cell experiments.

Effects of SenA on secreted levels of IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells

As shown in Figs. 5C-E, the levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were significantly elevated in the LPS-stimulated model group compared with the control group. For IL-6 (Fig. 6C), only 50 µM SenA significantly reduced the cytokine level, while the 25 µM group showed a downward trend that did not reach statistical significance. For IL-1β (Fig. 6D), both 25 µM and 50 µM SenA significantly inhibited the production, with a slightly stronger effect at the 50 µM concentration. Similarly, TNF-α (Fig. 6E) was significantly reduced by both 25 µM and 50 µM SenA.

Effects of SenA on antioxidation in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells

To investigate whether SenA mitigates LPS-induced oxidative stress in cells, we determined the level of intracellular ROS in each group. As shown in Fig. 7A-B, LPS stimulation significantly increased ROS production, while pretreatment with SenA notably reduced ROS generation. Both 25 µM and 50 µM SenA significantly decreased the average fluorescence intensity of ROS.

Besides, we also detected the intracellular levels of glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA). Consistent with the ROS results, LPS stimulation markedly decreased intracellular GSH levels. Discussion

Inflammatory response and oxidative stress are interconnected and interact with each other. The inflammatory mechanism is accompanied by increased generation of ROS, and excessive ROS further cause DNA damage and protein denaturation, thereby activating inflammation-related signaling pathways [26]. Both inflammatory response and oxidative stress play a key role in the pathogenesis of many pathological conditions, including cardiovascular and neurological diseases [27, 28]. Therefore, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant strategies hold significant therapeutic application prospects in such cases.

Chuanxiong Rhizoma (Chuanxiong in Chinese) is a fundamental component of several classic Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) formulas, such as Chuanxiong Chatiao San, Buyang Huanwu Tang, and Chaihu Shugan San, which have demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy in treating various inflammatory diseases [29–31]. SenA, one of the primary bioactive constituents of Chuanxiong Rhizoma, has been reported to exert potent anti-inflammatory effects by modulating key signaling pathways, such as the NLRP3 inflammasome and TGF-β pathways [21, 32]. In the present study, we employed multiple methods to investigate the preventive and inhibitory effects of SenA on LPS-induced inflammation and oxidative stress.

Through the comprehensive retrieval and screening of multiple databases, we identified the 169 potential targets of SenA. Based on the GEO database, the microarray data of RAW264.7 cells stimulated by LPS was analyzed and 1100 DEGs were obtained. The intersection of 168 targets and 1100 DEGs yielded 13 overlapping gene targets, indicating that these genes may be the targets of the control of inflammation by SenA. Subsequently, 13 intersection genes were imported into the STRING database to construct a PPI network map. Through multiple algorithms of cytoHubba, Il1b, Ptgs2, Nos2, and Hmox1 were identified as hub genes. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation results showed that SenA exhibited good binding stability with IL-1β, PTGS2, NOS2 and HMOX1 proteins. Furthermore, BLI experiments further confirmed the direct binding between SenA and IL‑1β as well as PTGS2.

In addition, we demonstrated the potential intervention effect of SenA on inflammatory models through in vitro experiments. During SenA concentration screening, cell viability assays showed that SenA at 12.5 µM, 25 µM, and 50 µM exhibited almost no cytotoxicity, with cell viability maintained above 80%. In contrast, although 100 µM SenA did not show significant cytotoxicity, an obvious downward trend in cell viability was observed. Therefore, even though 100 µM SenA significantly inhibited NO release in RAW264.7 cells, we selected 25 µM and 50 µM for subsequent experiments. The pathogenesis of most inflammatory diseases is closely related to the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In this study, SenA(50 µM) significantly reduced the expression levels of the pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. Furthermore, SenA also exhibited a tendency to inhibit the mRNA expression level of Il1b. Ptgs2 encodes COX-2, which regulates inflammation and homeostasis by synthesizing prostaglandins and other lipids and promotes the development of inflammatory diseases [33, 34]. Our study validated that SenA (50 µM) obviously inhibited the mRNA expression level of Ptgs2. Nitric oxide synthase 2 (NOS2) is another important inflammatory marker that responds to various inflammatory stimuli and produces high levels of NO and ROS [35, 36]. The current results showed that LPS markedly increased the level of Nos2, but compared with the model group, SenA (25 µM) pretreatment significantly decreased the mRNA level of Nos2.

As described earlier, ROS plays an important role in oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. In this study, compared to the control group, pretreatment with SenA markedly reduced ROS abundance. GSH is an important antioxidant and free radical scavenger in the body, while MDA is a direct product of lipid peroxidation and reflects the degree of oxidative stress damage [37, 38]. We found that SenA pretreatment could reduce cellular MDA levels and increase GSH content. Electron microscopic observation showed that mitochondrial structure was restored and cristae increased in number, suggesting enhanced oxygen consumption and ATP production capacity. Heme-oxygenase 1 (Hmox1) is an essential stress response gene responsible for catalyzing the multistep oxidation process of heme [39]. While Hmox1 is recognized as a protective antioxidant enzyme, excessive upregulation of its expression leads to increased iron ion release, thereby exacerbating lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [40]. In our study, we observed that Hmox1 was significantly overexpressed in the model group, whereas SenA pretreatment markedly decreased its mRNA level. We speculate that the anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative stress activities of SenA may reduce the cellular compensatory response to oxidative stress, meaning cells no longer need to maintain redox balance through compensatory high expression of Hmox1. Meanwhile, consistent with our findings that SenA significantly reduced ROS and MDA levels and restored GSH levels, the downregulation of Hmox1 may also represent a secondary consequence of the improvement in oxidative stress and inflammation.

Taken together, our results revealed that SenA has significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant stress responses potential. These findings are expected to guide the application of SenA and further development of SenA in the treatment of inflammatory-related diseases. However, it should be mentioned that this study had some limitations. First, only a 2-hour pre-treatment regimen was adopted in the present study, and different time-phase intervention protocols including co-treatment and post-treatment were not systematically explored. This makes it difficult to further distinguish the preventive and therapeutic effects of SenA against inflammatory and oxidative stress injury. Second, we only preliminarily screened the potential functional targets of SenA underlying its anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative stress effects, while the intrinsic correlation and upstream–downstream causal regulatory relationship between inflammation and oxidative stress remain unclarified. Moreover, the in-depth molecular mechanism by which SenA exerts its protective effects needs further elucidation. Future research could further explore the molecular mechanisms through inflammation or oxidative stress-related signaling pathways, ferroptosis, and other aspects of SenA’s effects. In addition, although our study employed various public databases, advanced bioinformatics methods, and in vitro verification, additional in vivo experiments would further improve the reliability of the research conclusions and enhance the clinical translational value of SenA. Therefore, the validation of SenA’s pharmacological effects in animal models and deepening mechanistic exploration will be prioritized in our follow-up work.
