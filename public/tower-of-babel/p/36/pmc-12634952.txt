Calcium Channel α2δ Ligands Mirogabalin, Pregabalin, and Gabapentin: Advancements in Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathic Pain Therapeutics
Authors: Yiming Wu, Xiaohui Guo, Junqing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain (DPNP) is becoming increasingly prevalent as the global burden of diabetes continues to rise. DPNP manifests moderate-to-severe pain with burning, shooting, and tingling sensations, which increase clinical and economic burden and reduce the quality of life (QoL). α2δ ligands were developed on the basis of their mechanism of action involving the modulation of voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs). These ligands bind to the α2δ subunit of VGCCs, which reduces calcium influx and subsequently decreases the release of excitatory neurotransmitters. A majority of the clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of α2δ ligands in providing pain relief and improvement in the QoL for patients with DPNP. Furthermore, α2δ ligands have a tolerable safety profile, with somnolence and dizziness being the most frequently reported adverse events. Currently, most guidelines recommend calcium channel α2δ ligands as first-line treatment for DPNP. With the development of drug research, mirogabalin, an emerging novel α2δ ligand, was developed and validated. This review aims to summarize the latest status of α2δ ligand development and provide a comprehensive evaluation of α2δ ligands for the management of DPNP, emphasizing the potential mechanism of action, clinical efficacy, safety profile, and pharmacoeconomics. Introduction

Diabetes now ranks among the most important public health concerns in contemporary society. It is reported that the prevalence of diabetes was 11.1% globally, and China ranked first for number of adults, as 148.0 per million population in 2021 [1]. More than 50% of patients with diabetes face complications and concurrent conditions such as heart attacks, strokes, foot ulcers, amputations, vision loss, and neuropathy [2]. These complications substantially diminish the quality of life (QoL), shorten estimated lifespans, and further increase the current expenditure on treating patients with diabetes.

Approximately 50% of patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and 30% of patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) have diabetic polyneuropathy [3]. By far, the most common form of diabetic neuropathy (DPN) is distal symmetric polyneuropathy [4]. The Toronto Consensus Panel on Diabetic Neuropathy defines typical DPN as “a symmetrical length-dependent sensorimotor polyneuropathy attributable to chronic hyperglycaemia, associated metabolic derangements, cardiovascular risk covariates, and microvessel alteration” [5]. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) suggests that a simple definition for clinical practice is “the presence of symptoms and/or signs of peripheral nerve dysfunction in people with diabetes after the exclusion of other causes” [6]. DPN is characterized by the most common and earliest features: sensory symptoms [7]. It manifests as bilateral, symmetric, and length-dependent nerve fiber damage, which affects the longest and most susceptible nerve fibers first and progresses distally-to-proximally in a stocking glove pattern [8]. Negative sensations include numbness or a lack of sensation to feeling (attributable to injury of large, myelinated nerves); for small fiber neuropathy, it is specifically loss of pain and thermal sensation [7]. Positive signs reflect activity in nerve fibers of varying calibers, such as allodynia and hypersensitivity to sensory stimuli [7, 9].

Diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain (DPNP) is defined as pain caused by damage to the peripheral somatosensory system attributable to diabetes and manifests as sensory abnormalities in the innervation territory of the damaged nerves [10]. DPNP has a prevalence rate of 11% to 21% in patients with diabetes [3], and 57.2% in Chinese patients with T2DM and diabetic peripheral neuropathy [11]. The UK Pain in Neuropathy Study (PiNS) found that DPNP was associated with greater sensory impairment, particularly affecting small fibers, and a greater spread of proximal clinical signs compared with painless DPN [12]. Similar results were reported in two other studies, one of which further reported a dominant impairment in thermal sensation that correlated with both the intensity of neuropathic pain (NP) and the overall neuropathic burden [13].

DPNP presents no single conclusive symptom. Clinically, DPNP manifests as burning, shooting, and tingling sensations and a sensation similar to “pins and needles,” with most reporting as moderate to severe pain. Patients also report sensations of stabbing, stinging, or electric/burning/aching pain (injury from small nerve fibers), which intensify at night, and is accompanied by hyperalgesia and/or allodynia. In addition, they may experience numbness, tingling, or decreased sensation in the affected area (injury from large, myelinated nerve fibers) [14]. Patients with these sensations tend to endure greater clinical and economic burdens, such as sleep disturbances, mood issues, depression, and a decline in their QoL [15].

Voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) are among the key molecular mechanisms implicated in DPNP, which play a pivotal role in neuronal excitability and neurotransmitter release [16]. VGCCs are transmembrane proteins associated with various auxiliary subunits, including the α2δ subunits. These α2δ subunits are essential for functional calcium channel assembly and also serve as a binding site for gabapentin-like drugs. The upregulation of α2δ-1 subunit of VGCC has been linked to increased neuronal excitability and pain signaling, highlighting their crucial role in the pathogenesis of DPNP [17]. Through high affinity binding to α2δ subunits in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG), α2δ ligands attenuate pain transmission by reducing the fusing of the synaptic vesicle with the presynaptic membrane and subsequent neurotransmitter release into the synapses [18–20]. With the advancement of drug development, new α2δ ligands have been introduced, offering promising treatment options for DPNP.

In this study, we review the latest status of α2δ ligands development and summarize information on the mechanism, pharmacology, clinical pharmacokinetics (PK), and clinical efficacy and safety of α2δ ligands, namely gabapentin, pregabalin, and mirogabalin, to address the lacunae in understanding the improved clinical management of DPNP.

Materials and Methods

This narrative review is based on previously conducted studies and does not include any new research involving human participants or animals by the authors.

Literature Search Strategy

This narrative review was conducted on the basis of data from articles published from January 2002 to January 2024 using the PubMed and Scopus databases. The following keywords were used for the search of clinical studies: “diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain,” OR “DPNP” OR “painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy,” OR “diabetic peripheral neuropathy” AND “α2δ ligands” OR “mirogabalin” OR “mirogabalin besylate” OR “tarlige” OR “DS-5565” OR “pregabalin” OR “Lyrica” OR “gabapentin.” (Fig. 1) Original articles and abstracts including randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and critical reviews, such as systematic reviews and meta-analyses, were selected and examined for relevancy to clinical efficacy and safety of α2δ ligands. Studies investigating gabapentin, pregabalin, or mirogabalin in broader therapeutic contexts and patient populations not directly related to DPN or DPNP were also included. Case reports and editor letters were excluded. Two independent reviewers conducted a two-stage screening process of the retrieved citations. In the first stage, titles and abstracts were screened to identify potentially relevant studies. In the second stage, full-text articles were assessed against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Additionally, the reference lists of relevant reviews were manually screened to identify further eligible studies. Data extraction focused on baseline characteristics, study methods, and outcomes related to efficacy and safety. To ensure accuracy, a third author performed quality control on the extracted data. Current Treatment Options of DPNP

Consistent with NP treatment, the American Association of Neurology (AAN 2022) [21] and the ADA [22] recommend α2δ ligands, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) as first-line pharmacological treatment on DPNP. However, Chinese guidelines [23, 24] only recommend α2δ ligands and duloxetine as first-line treatment, while SNRIs (except for duloxetine) and TCAs are recommended as second-line treatments (Table 1). The calcium channel α2δ ligands were first developed as anticonvulsant agents before being widely used for NP relief. They bind to the α2δ-1 and α2δ-2 subunits located on presynaptic neurons in the DRG and spinal dorsal horn and reduce presynaptic Ca2+ influx, which decreases the release of excitatory neurotransmitters such as glutamate, substance P, and noradrenaline. These inhibition decreases DRG neuronal excitability and synaptic protein kinase C signaling pathway associated with DPNP [25–27]. Gabapentin was clinically used for pain relief in DPN but was not approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [28], whereas pregabalin has been approved for DPN by FDA for more than 20 years [29]. Mirogabalin was firstly approved in Japan in 2019 for the treatment of peripheral neuropathic pain (PNP) and recommended by the Japanese Society of Pain Clinicians for its use in DPNP [30]. TCAs, including amitriptyline, imipramine, desipramine and nortriptyline, inhibit the reuptake of noradrenaline and serotonin (5-HT) in presynaptic neurons and antagonizing N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptors in the dorsal horn, and alleviate pain associated with altered central sensitization in DPNP [31]. TCAs are also believed to affect the brain’s opioid system through serotonergic and norepinephrine neuromodulation [32]. However, the ADA recommends that TCAs should be used with caution given the higher risk of serious side effects [6, 33]—including metabolic disruptions, particularly in elderly patients and those with SNRIs (including duloxetine) work by increasing serotonin and norepinephrine levels in the central nervous system. This enhances the descending inhibition of pain modulation pathways, which can help in reducing central sensitization in DPNP [31]. In early 2004, the FDA approved duloxetine for the management of DPNP [35], followed by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in the same year [36]. However, its long-term use is reported to be associated with increased blood sugar levels [37–40]. Opioids are generally recommended as third /fourth line treatment for DPNP. Tapentadol was approved by the FDA for the treatment of DPNP in 2012 [44]. Topical agents such as capsaicin are recommended as third/fourth line agents for both NP and DPNP, and they work by depleting the neurotransmitter substance P from primary afferent neurons and loss of intraepidermal nerve fiber density.

Overall, these pharmacological treatments target different components of DPNP pathophysiology, including peripheral sensitization, central sensitization, and metabolic disruptions, providing comprehensive relief for patients suffering from this complex condition.

Mechanism of α2δ Ligands Binding

Structure of VGCC

VGCCs play a crucial role in several essential processes in excitable cells (particularly muscle, endocrine, neuronal, and cardiac cells), such as releasing neurotransmitters and facilitating muscle contractions, and changes in their properties play a causal role in the development of chronic pain, including NP [45].

VGCCs are constituted of a pore-forming α1 subunit, along with a transmembrane, disulfide-linked α2δ complex [46, 47], intracellular β subunit, and, in some instances, a transmembrane γ subunit. A total of ten distinct α1 subunits, four α2δ complexes, four β subunits, and two γ subunits have been identified [48]. The four isoforms of α2δ subunits include: the α2δ-1 subunit, mainly present in skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscles, along with neuronal cells and the DRG; and α2δ-2 found to be concentrated in the cerebellum (especially Purkinje fibers) [49]. The α2δ-3 subunits are located in both neuronal and nonneuronal tissues, [50, 51] whereas α2δ-4 is predominantly expressed in retinal neurons and certain nonneuronal tissues [52, 53].

VGCCs can be further subcategorized by the structural similarities of their channel-forming α1-subunit (CaV1, CaV2, CaV3) and by their distinct pharmacological sensitivities to various blockers (L, N, P/Q, R, and T-type). The high-VGCCs channels include L-type (CaV1.1, CaV1.2, CaV1.3, CaV1.4), P/Q-type (CaV2.1), N-type (CaV2.2), and R-type (CaV2.3) channels [54]. Of these, CaV2.2 N-type calcium channels are abundantly expressed in neurons involved in sensory signal processing, including spinal dorsal horn neurons, and DRG cell bodies [55]. The presence of the CaV2.2 N-type channels within pain-inducing neurotransmitters such as substance P, calcitonin gene-related peptide, and glutamate [56, 57], highlights the significant role of N-type channels in regulating synaptic transmission within pain pathways, particularly in C- and Aδ-nociceptors [58, 59]. However, it has been reported that N- or P/Q-type calcium channel Cavα2.1 or Cavα2.2 and β3 subunit expression were not co-upregulated with α2δ-1 subunit after nerve injury [60].

Mechanism of Analgesic Effects by α2δ Ligands

Of note, the α2δ-1 subunit plays an essential role in the development of NP (including DPNP), resulting from injury to sensory nerves. The α2δ-1 messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) is upregulated in the DRG, and the α2δ-1 protein is upregulated in the affected ganglia as well as the spinal cord dorsal horn after nerve damage [61–66]. Moreover, DRG neurons from transgenic mice overexpressing the Cavα2δ-1 gene, exhibit a hyperpolarizing shift in the voltage activation of VGCCs, increased peak conductance, and an increased inactivation [67]. This overexpression of α2δ-1 also increases the frequency of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents in the dorsal horn, suggesting that overexpression of α2δ-1 substantially contributes to NP [68] and DPNP [65, 66, 69–72].

The potential targets of α2δ ligands are the molecular sensitization mechanisms of peripheral nociceptors and actions in dorsal horns of spinal cord (Fig. 2). α2δ ligands inhibit the forward tracking of α2δ-1 and VGCC from DRG to the plasma membrane and its recycling from endosomal compartments, thereby reduce Ca2+ influx and excitatory neurotransmitters release [73]. Due to this inhibition of the accumulation of α2δ-1 in the dorsal horn, α2δ ligands reduce response to painful stimuli. α2δ ligands could also interfere with thrombospondin-mediated formation of excitatory synapses and may influence the uptake of glutamate through excitatory amino acid transporters (EAATs). Moreover, binding of the α2δ ligands to the α2δ-1 subunit inhibits the trafficking of the α1 pore forming unit, leading to downregulation of N-type calcium channels in the spinal cord and pain attenuating effects [63, 69, 74–76]. Indirect analgesic mechanisms of α2δ ligands are related to the modulation of descending pathways. Pregabalin act on a spinal–bulbo–spinal loop consisting of projection neurons in the superficial dorsal horn and brainstem descending faciliatory pathways mediated by 5-HT3 receptors [77, 78]. In the spinal nerve ligation (SNL) rat model of NP, gabapentin exerted its antihyperalgesic effects by indirect acting on the noradrenergic pathways and serotonergic pathways of the descending inhibitory system [79]. Gabapentin also induces the release of glutamate from astrocytes in the locus coeruleus in SNL rats [80]. Gabapentin and pregabalin also antagonizes the NMDA receptor and synaptic delivery, leading to analgesia [81–83]. Mirogabalin activates the descending noradrenergic system by binding to the α2δ-1 subunit of VGCCs to generate analgesic effects [84]. α2δ Binding–Dissociation and Selectivity

The α2 and δ subunits are held together by four disulfide bonds, and the δ subunit intramolecularly also carries two disulfide bonds. Four cache domains (amino acid and small nutrient molecule recognition domain typical in calcium channels and in a wide range of chemotaxis receptors) are identified in α2δ proteins [87]. The first double cache domain 1 (dCache_1) in the α2δ-1 protein is split by the von Willebrand factor A [88], and this latter also splits a consensus sequence (YxxxRxWY[x∼13–17]Y[x∼27–34]D) that is essential for amino acid and nutrient binding in chemoreceptors [89]. In addition, the R residue in the dCache_1 domain is the third arginine in the triple-arginine sequence that is essential for α2δ ligand biding [76, 90]. Furthermore, cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) data revealed that amino acid motif residues Y236, R241, W243, Y450, D452, T461, and D491 form the binding pocket for the carboxyl and amino groups, while W205, V207, Y217, W223, and L454 contribute to hydrophobic interactions facilitating mirogabalin binding [91].

Gabapentin, a first-generation α2δ ligand, binds to α2δ-1 subunit with greater affinity when compared with α2δ-2 (equilibrium dissociation constant [Kd] = 59 nM versus 153 nM) and has no affinity to the α2δ-3 subunit [92, 93]. Pregabalin, a second-generation α2δ ligand, binds with high affinity to recombinant α2δ-1 (Kd = 6.0 nM) and α2δ-2 subunit (Kd = 7.2 nM) with no subtype preference [94]. In R217A-mutant mice (expressing α2δ-1 protein with a single point mutation at residue 217), the Kd for gabapentin and pregabalin was largely decreased in the specific central nervous system (CNS) region expressing α2δ-1 such as the neocortex and spinal cord but sustained in the cerebellum region, where it expressed α2δ-2, suggesting α2δ-1 as the major binding protein [95]. Mirogabalin exhibits high binding affinity to the α2δ-1 and α2δ-2 subunits as Kd = 13.5 nM and 22.7 nM [96–98]. In contrast to pregabalin, which displayed an equal dissociation half-life (t1/2) of 1.4 h from both subunits, mirogabalin was reported to have a longer dissociation t1/2 for the α2δ-1 compared with the α2δ-2 subunit (11.1 h versus 2.4 h), with a dissociation rate constant [Koff] of 0.0627 h−1 for the α2δ-1 and 0.2837 h−1 for the α2δ-2 subunit. This selective and unique binding characteristics of mirogabalin with the α2δ-1 and α2δ-2 subunits are considered to contribute to the sustained analgesic effects with the required low dose in clinical applications (10–30 mg/day) and a potentially greater safety margin for side effects related to α2δ-2 expressing in CNS compared with previous generation of α2δ ligands [97].

Efficacy and Safety of α2δ Ligands

Gabapentin

Gabapentin (Fig. 3) was initially approved for epilepsy by the FDA in 1993 and was reviewed for a supplemental pain indication in 2001; thereafter approved for postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) in 2002. In 2004, the generic version of gabapentin became available worldwide [99]. In 2006, the EMA authorized the marketing of gabapentin across the European Union (EU) for NP, DPNP, and PHN [100]. Gabapentin is now considered a first-line treatment for various NP conditions in US, Japan, China and other countries. Its antiepileptic and anxiolytic effects are also well recognized, although it is often prescribed off-label for these indications [101]. Clinical Efficacy

In total, six clinical trials were conducted to validate the use of gabapentin for DPN pain relief, including one for NP (Table 3). Among them, a minimal clinical effectiveness of gabapentin 900–2400 mg/day was observed in two trials [102, 103] and a one-to-two-point improvement on the mean pain score (MPS; by numeric rating scale (NRS) and an 11-point Likert scale) was observed in two clinical trials of patients with DPNP [104, 105]. The trial of broader NP (including DPNP) reported a significant difference on the average daily pain score (ADPS) change of gabapentin 900–2400 mg/day compared with placebo [106]. A systematic review of three RCTs comparing the efficacy and safety of gabapentin versus duloxetine in DPNP reported no significant differences between the two drugs (mean difference of visual analog scale [VAS]: −1.23; 95% confidence interval [CI] −6.09, 3.62; P = 0.62) [108]. A study comparing the effectiveness of gabapentin and duloxetine for painful diabetic peripheral polyneuropathy reported no significant differences between the VAS scores of the two drugs (gabapentin: 39.43 ± 14.32 versus duloxetine: 36.78 ± 15.62; P = 0.357) [109]. Similarly, in patients with painful DPN, no significant differences in DPN symptoms (P = 0.578), DPN examination (P = 0.410), and neuropathic disability score (P = 0.071) were observed between gabapentin and duloxetine over 12 weeks. Moreover, both the drug groups had a comparable safety profile [110].

Safety

Somnolence/drowsiness, dizziness, fatigue, imbalance and peripheral edema were reported as the most common adverse events (AEs) of gabapentin [102, 111, 112]. Sandercock et al. reported that the incidence of dizziness and somnolence, which are frequently associated with gabapentin, was low in the treatment of DPN, with only 4% of patients discontinuing treatment due to AEs [104]. In cases of NP, gabapentin has been shown to cause dizziness (risk ratio [RR]: 3.11), somnolence (RR: 2.88), ataxia (RR: 4.47), and peripheral edema (RR: 3.30) in comparison with the placebo [107]. Confusion has also been reported as an AE associated with gabapentin in NP [105] (Table 4). Pregabalin

Pregabalin (Fig. 4), developed by Pfizer in 1991, got the first approval for DPN in 2004, subsequently for fibromyalgia in 2007, and for NP associated with spinal cord injury (SCI) in 2012 [99]. Pregabalin was approved in 2010 for general NP in Japan [113] and for PHN and fibromyalgia in China [114]; in 2014, the EMA approved it for NP, including PNP, such as DPNP and central NP [115]. In 2017, the FDA approved LYRICA® CR (pregabalin) for the management of NP associated with DPN.Fig. 4Skeletal structure of pregabalin

Clinical efficacy

Five studies were conducted between 2004 and 2008, the majority of which demonstrated the efficacy and safety of pregabalin in providing pain relief for patients with DPN [29]. A 5-week, double-blind, multicenter, placebo-controlled trial reported a significant improvement in the MPS in patients treated with pregabalin 300 (−1.26, 95% CI [−1.86, −0.65], P = 0.0001) and 600 mg/day (−1.45, 95% CI [−2.06, −0.85], P = 0.0001), accompanied by a higher percentage of patients with > 50% reduction in pain score from baseline (46%, 48% versus 18%). The sleep interference score, patient global impression of change (PGIC), clinical global impression of change (CGIC), short-form (SF)-McGill, and SF-36 scores also showed significant improvement [116]. In an 8-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial, pregabalin 300 mg/day significantly improved MPS, mean sleep interference score, PGIC, CGIC, short form of the McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ), tension-anxiety, and Total Mood Disturbance of Profile of Mood States (POMS) scores [117]. A 6-week, randomized, double-blind study on 246 patients with DPNP revealed that pregabalin 600 mg/day versus placebo significantly decreased MPS (P = 0.0002), and a higher percentage of ≥ 50% reduction from baseline (39% versus 15%, P = 0.0002) as well as VAS of SF-MPQ, sleep interference score, PGIC, and CGIC were observed [118]. In a 12-week RCT, only pregabalin 600 mg/day reached the primary endpoint of reduction in NRS (P = 0.0054), but 150 and 300 mg/day were not significantly different compared with placebo [119]. In Chinese patients with DPNP, pregabalin 300 mg/day resulted in an improvement in ≥ 30% of responders in MPS versus placebo (P = 0.0142); however, the primary endpoint of change in MPS from baseline to endpoint was not reached. Furthermore, no significant difference in VAS, pain intensity, and other QoL scales were observed [120]. A systematic review of 50 studies showed that NNT of pregabalin compared with gabapentin for pain intensity reduction of ≥ 30% and ≥ 50% were 8 versus 7 and 10 versus 8, with pregabalin having the highest RR (3.33–7.20) for cognition or coordination AEs [121]. Furthermore, it has been reported that the sustained-release formulation of pregabalin has comparable efficacy and safety to immediate-release pregabalin in DPNP. Both formulations significantly reduced the VAS and SF-MPQ descriptive scores, with a significant improvement in symptoms [122].

Safety

The most frequent AEs of pregabalin were reported as dizziness, somnolence, peripheral edema, dry mouth, and asthenia [29]. In a study by Lesser et al. (2004), patients receiving 300 and 600 mg/day doses of pregabalin reported dizziness (27.2% and 39%), somnolence (23.5% and 26.8%), and peripheral edema (7.4% and 13.4%), with CNS-related AEs as the most frequent AEs with mild–moderate intensities. In most cases, the AEs did not lead to discontinuation from the study. One patient receiving 75 mg/day and four patients receiving 600 mg/day experienced serious AEs, but no death was reported during the study [116]. Similar findings were reported by Richter et al. (2005), with dizziness (30.5%), somnolence (18.3%), and headache (13.4%) as the most common treatment-associated AEs in patients receiving 600 mg/day pregabalin and dizziness (6.3%), somnolence (5.1%), and asthenia (3.8%) in patients receiving 150 mg/day pregabalin. A total of nine patients discontinued the study owing to the AEs (six pregabalin 600-mg/day, one pregabalin 150-mg/day, and two placebo) [118]. Variations in the occurrence of AEs were reported among different racial groups as well. Notably, dry mouth and blurred vision were less common (≤ 1%) in the Asian population with no signs of euphoric mood, while somnolence, peripheral edema, weight increase, and constipation were higher in white and Black populations as well as other racial groups (comprising populations from Australia, Canada, Europe, Latin America, and the Middle East) [123]. The occurrence of dizziness, weight gain, dry mouth, blurred vision, and balance disorder was lower in Black patients compared with the other racial groups [123].

Mirogabalin (Fig. 5) was approved for PNP in Japan in 2019. In the same year, mirogabalin also received its approval for PHN and DPNP in Taiwan (China) [124] and for PNP (2020) and NP (2022) in Korea [125–127].Fig. 5Skeletal structure of mirogabalin

Patents for mirogabalin were also granted by the European Patent Office and US Patent and Trademark Office in September 2013 and May 2011 [128]. In June 2024, mirogabalin received its approval in China for treating DPNP due to its well-supported efficacy and safety profile in Chinese population [129].

Clinical Efficacy

A randomized, double-blind, phase 2 trial (NCT01496365) conducted in the USA reported the efficacy and safety assessment of varying doses (5−30 mg/day) of mirogabalin. A significant least square mean difference (LSMD) of ADPS from baseline to week 5 versus placebo was observed [130]. In patient-reported outcomes, reductions in the average daily sleep interference score (ADSIS) were significant in mirogabalin 15, 20, and 30 mg/day [131]. In a phase 2 study with 436 patients with DPNP, the ED50 for mirogabalin was estimated to be 20.5 mg (90% CI 10.1–41.7 mg). It also demonstrated that the 17.7 mg mirogabalin dose provided an ADPS effect equivalent to 300 mg pregabalin, with an ED50 of 20.5 mg (90% CI: 10.1–41.7 mg) for mirogabalin [132]. In a phase 3 trial (NCT02318706) reported by Baba et al. (2019), mirogabalin demonstrated a balanced efficacy and safety profile in Asian patients with DPNP. At week 14, the least square mean (LSM) change in the ADPS of 15, 20, and 30 mg/day mirogabalin versus placebo was −0.03 (P = 0.8773), −0.15 (P = 0.3494), and −0.50 (P = 0.0027); significant improvements of ≥ 50% response of ADPS, VAS, and ADSIS were observed in 30 mg/day [133]. A phase 3 trial (NCT04094662) enrolling Chinese patients reported that mirogabalin 15 mg twice a day (BID) compared with placebo demonstrated a significant improvement in the weekly ADPS with an LSMD of −0.39 (P = 0.0301), PGIC “much improved or better”, ADSIS, and EuroQol five-dimensional, five-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) index value, and VAS score [134].

Alyoubi et al. (2021) reported the results of meta-analysis in which mirogabalin was compared with pregabalin, showing a notably greater decrease in ADPS, specifically after treated for 3–5 weeks. The number of ≥ 30% and ≥ 50% reductions in ADPS were significantly higher in the mirogabalin group than in the pregabalin group [135]. Furthermore, in a study including 43 trials of patients with DPN, statistically significant differences in pain reduction of ≥ 50% were observed for duloxetine, mirogabalin, and pregabalin (odds ratio [OR]: 2.50, 3.25, 2.33) when compared with placebo [136]. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis of 16 studies reported that the maximum pure efficacy for mirogabalin and pregabalin in relieving DPNP and PHN were approximately −7.85% and −8.86%, respectively. The 95% CIs for the pure effect of mirogabalin and pregabalin showed a high degree of overlap, suggesting that the efficacy of the two drugs is comparable [137].

Kimura conducted a prospective, single-arm, open-label (MIROP) study to assess the efficacy and safety of switching from pregabalin to mirogabalin in patients with broader PNP (including DPNP). Effective doses were achieved in most patients (70%), and the mean VAS reduction was Δ15.7 mm (P < 0.0001) from day 1 to 29. Only mild AEs and no deaths were reported due to the mirogabalin treatment [138]. Two retrospective studies also reported clinical benefits of reduction in AEs and further improvement in the NRS after switching from pregabalin to mirogabalin [139, 140].

Safety

The most common AEs associated with mirogabalin are somnolence, dizziness, and headache; others (> 5%) are constipation, nausea, diarrhea, edema, weight gain, and fatigue [133, 134, 141, 142]. A pooled-analysis of two phase 3 studies reported that discontinuation due to somnolence, dizziness, edema, and peripheral edema was reported in < 1% of patients receiving 15-, 20-, and 30-mg/day mirogabalin (except for 1.9% of dizziness in 30 mg/day) [143]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis identified mirogabalin for the lowest rank of withdrawal rates from clinical trials (P = 0.58) in patients with DPNP [136]. A dose-dependent increase in the incidence of these AEs was reported with no change in their severity. Serious treatment emergent AEs (TEAEs) were reported in 2.4% of patients receiving 15 mg mirogabalin versus 3.3% of patients receiving placebo; no serious TEAEs were reported in more than one patient in the treatment groups, and most TEAEs resolved without any intervention [133].

Cost–Effectiveness

DPN imposes a significant economic burden on patients with DM and the national healthcare infrastructures. Comprehensive literature has quantified the financial burden of diabetes in China. Between 2016 and 2018, research from the Beijing Medical Claim Data for Employees database determined an average total expense of $1,712,000 for patients with diabetes-related complications and an extra cost of $883.90 for patients with DPN [144].

The difference-in-differences analysis conducted by Margarita et al. reported a notable increase in prescription costs with pregabalin (with an increase of $563; P < 0.0001); however, the preperiod to postperiod increases of $3081 for patients receiving pregabalin and $4684 for patients receiving gabapentin were similar (P = 0.8474) [145]. In the USA, a considerable difference in cost between generic gabapentin and pregabalin has been observed (a few dollars versus several hundred dollars/mo). This makes the interchangeability between the two drugs reasonable. However, only limited evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of the interchangeability is available [99].

An economic analysis conducted in Taiwan (China) using cost data from Taiwanese clinical experts and the Taiwan National Health Insurance Administration predicted that the use of 30 mg mirogabalin in DPNP was cost-effective compared with 300 mg pregabalin (incremental cost–effectiveness ratio [ICER]: 18,476 NT$/quality-adjusted life year [QALY] [$600/QALY]) [146]. A similar study conducted in mainland China suggested mirogabalin 30 mg as more cost-efficient than 300 mg pregabalin, displaying an ICER of CNY 49,057 per QALY, comfortably falling beneath China’s willingness-to-pay benchmark set at one time the gross domestic product per capita (CNY 80,976) [147]. This should help payers and healthcare providers to make informed decisions.

Expert Opinion and Future Perspectives

Although a plethora of drugs were clinically used to manage DPNP in China, there are still challenges in this field for Chinese patients, such as limited evidence on real-world studies, large populations, benefit balance on safety and efficacy profiles, long-term pain relief, and improvement on QoL [148].

In this review, we conducted a literature analysis for summarizing the clinical evidence of α2δ ligands for the treatment of DPNP. The α2δ ligands represent a unique class of Ca2+ channel inhibitors with pharmacologically antinociceptive effects. Gabapentin and pregabalin nonselectively bind to α2δ-1 and α2δ-2 subunits, whereas mirogabalin displays a greater selectivity and stronger binding affinity for the α2δ-1 subunit compared with the α2δ-2 subunit. Moreover, mirogabalin has a slower dissociation rate from the α2δ-1 subunit than the α2δ-2 subunit. This hypothesis made researchers believe that the weaker inhibition of the α2δ-2 subunit may contribute to fewer CNS side effects. A phase 2 study showed similar overall nervous system AEs between 15 mg/day mirogabalin and pregabalin 300 mg/day when the pain reduction evaluated using NRS was significantly lower in mirogabalin 15–30 mg/day. Studies have consistently found that switching from pregabalin to mirogabalin was tolerable in patients with PNP, as the somnolence, dizziness, and edema occurred less than switching before.

The data from various RCTs support the use of gabapentin and pregabalin to alleviate DPNP. Mirogabalin has been recently garnering much scientific attention as phase 3 RCTs in Asian patients with DPNP have shown promising results and a clinically significant analgesic effect, including a separate study trial on Chinese patients demonstrating a slightly smaller difference of ADPS −0.39. The ability of mirogabalin to provide quick and sustained pain relief while minimizing AEs, suggesting the potential for improved patient outcomes and enhanced treatment strategies for patients with DPNP.

Further attention has been focused on the role of α2δ ligands in effective management of DPNP. Real-world phase 4 studies of large population under various conditions of DPNP (such as renal impairment or pretreated) will be worth investigating. These research studies should primarily focus on exploring flexible dosages. It is also noteworthy to reveal the treatment pattern for patients with DPNP to further indicate the overview of the updated mapping in this field and risk factors related to insufficient therapy. Evidence on switching from one α2δ ligand to another could be applicable for clinical practice. It has also been found that while monotherapies of amitriptyline, duloxetine, and pregabalin significantly reduced pain, combination therapies provided even greater pain relief without notable differences among the combinations [149]. The add-on treatment of different pharmacologic analgesic drugs including α2δ ligands (such as pregabalin and crisugabalin) in a head-to-head comparison will draw some attention to DPNP management based on previous considerations on dosing-limited side effects.

A key strength of this review lies in its novelty, as providing an updated and comprehensive summary of the latest clinical efficacy and safety of gabapentin, pregabalin, and mirogabalin in a broad population of DPNP (including US, European, and Asian populations); the unique mechanism of α2δ ligands for management of DPNP was summarized. Moreover, the review adopts a hybrid systematic–narrative review approach, enabling broad coverage of relevant literature while preserving the contextual depth often lacking in traditional narrative reviews [150]. However, the review has certain limitations. The methodology did not include a formal critical appraisal and risk of bias, which may affect the interpretation of individual study findings. Hence, while the review offers a robust synthesis of available evidence, caution is warranted in drawing definitive conclusions regarding the comparative efficacy or safety of the α2δ ligands.

Conclusions

The effectiveness and tolerability of α2δ ligands in addressing DPNP signify a valuable addition to the therapeutic options available. This underscores the potential for optimizing individualized treatment approaches and improving the overall outcomes in managing DPNP.
