A statistical physics approach to integrating multi-omics data for disease-module detection
Authors: Xu-Wen Wang, Min Hyung Ryu, Michael H Cho, Peter Castaldi, Craig P Hersh, Edwin K Silverman, Yang-Yu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenes associated with the same disease frequently engage in mutual biological interactions, e.g., perturbation within a specific neighborhood in the molecular interactome, often referred to as the disease module. This has propelled the advancement of network-based approaches toward elucidating the molecular bases of human diseases. Although many computational methods have been developed to integrate the molecular interactome and omics profiles to extract such context-dependent disease modules, approaches that leverage multi-omics for disease-module detection are still lacking. Here, we developed a statistical physics approach based on the random-field O(n) model (RFOnM) to fill this gap. We applied the RFOnM approach to integrate gene-expression data and genome-wide association studies or mRNA data and DNA methylation for several complex diseases with the human interactome. We found that the RFOnM approach outperforms existing single omics methods in most of the complex diseases considered in this study.Keywords: multi-omics, disease module, human interactome

Introduction

Advancements in high-throughput technologies have facilitated the rapid generation of vast amounts of omics data, e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, at an unprecedented rate.1 These omics data enable the identification of disease-associated biomarkers.2 However, the alteration of molecules can also impact the expression of their partners in the context of cellular networks, where each node represents a molecule and each link represents the relationship between two molecules.3 This implies that each disease may be related to a “disease module,” e.g., a subgraph in the cellular network whose perturbation can be linked to a particular disease phenotype.4 For example, a new interaction partner, NSD3 for MYC (a frequently amplified global regulator of gene expression in tumors), was identified from the cancer-associated protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, and their interaction provided a new mechanism for NSD3/BRD4 chromatin complex regulation of MYC-driven tumors.5 Prior studies have also shown that disease-module detection can help to uncover new mechanistic knowledge by revealing unexpected contributors to the pathology of complex diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease,6 enhancing genome-wide association study (GWAS) interpretation and uncovering cell-type-specific mechanisms,7 revealing disease-disease relationships,8 and facilitating the discovery of therapeutic targets9,10,11 and stratification biomarkers.12,13

Unlike community detection approaches identifying densely connected subgraphs within a network, where nodes within a community exhibit a higher propensity for interaction among themselves than with external nodes, disease-module detection in human molecular networks involves the integration of omics data with biological networks. These methods typically assign an “activity” score to each node based on the omics data, quantifying the likelihood of the node being part of the disease module. Numerous methods have been proposed for disease-module detection. For instance, DIAMOnD14 gradually grows the disease module from a set of seed genes in a local agglomerative process. Hierarchical HotNet15 identifies a hierarchy of altered subnetworks and assesses their significance over various biological scales. DOMINO16 finds disjoint connected subnetworks in which the active, e.g., differentially expressed, are over-represented. MuST and randomized MuST (R-MuST) extract disease modules via the Steiner tree from the seed genes.17 ROBUST18 ensures robustness by enumerating pairwise diverse rather than merely pairwise non-identical disease modules and models disease modules by generalized Steiner trees. ROBUST outperforms existing methods, including DOMINO, MuST, R-MuST, and DIAMOnD, in terms of robustness, scalability, and functional relevance. We also previously developed a disease-module detection method based on the random-field Ising model (RFIM) by mapping the disease-module detection to the ground-state problem of RFIM, showing that RFIM outperforms existing methods, e.g., DIAMOnD and DOMINO, in GWASs.19

We emphasize that all these existing methods were developed by overlaying a single omics data type on a human molecular network. Integrating multi-omics could elucidate potential causative changes in disease processes that cannot be explained by one type of omics data.1,20 A computational method for disease-module detection that integrates multi-omics is still lacking. To fill this gap, we developed a method based on the random-field O(n) model (RFOnM), a classical statistical physics model where the spins can take values in an n-dimensional space and are subject to a random local magnetic field and a uniform global magnetic field.21 Each spin is represented by an n-component spin vector σi, with the αth component σi(α) representing the tendency of node i belonging to the disease module based on the omics data type α,α=1,…,n (see Figure 1 and STAR Methods). We applied the RFOnM approach to integrate gene-expression data and GWAS data, mRNA data, and DNA methylation of several complex diseases with the human interactome, finding that it outperforms existing single omics methods (in terms of the connectivity of disease modules, gene set enrichment, and disease association) for most of the diseases studied in this work.

Results

Disease-module detection

We applied RFOnM (with n=2) to identify the disease modules of four different complex diseases, namely Alzheimer’s disease, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and diabetes mellitus, by integrating the gene-wise p values derived from the publicly available gene-expression profile in Gene Expression Omnibus22 and GWASs, respectively. In addition to these four common diseases, we also applied RFOnM to five cancer datasets, breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), brain lower grade glioma (LGG), and ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), as well as a rare disease, frontotemporal dementia (Table 1). To compare RFOnM with other approaches, we applied ROBUST, DOMINO, and DIAMOnD separately to gene expression, GWAS data, mRNA, and methylation, respectively, given that they demonstrated superior performance over existing methods. In addition, we constructed two baseline models by selecting the most significant genes from gene expression, GWAS datasets, mRNA, and methylation, respectively, with sizes equivalent to RFOnM, without integrating the interactome.

Connectivity of the disease module

The disease-module hypothesis asserts that genes related to the pathogenesis of a particular disease tend to aggregate within specific neighborhoods of the interactome. To ensure the connectivity of the module is significant rather than a result of random chance, we examined the significance of the largest connected component (LCC) within the disease module by comparing the observed LCC to a distribution generated from randomly sampled gene sets of the same size. The results showed that RFOnM produced the highest Z scores in 9 of 12 diseases (Figure 2A). DIAMOnD modules based on GWAS and methylation data yield the highest Z scores in Alzheimer’s disease and colon adenocarcinoma, respectively, whereas DIAMOnD using DNA gene-expression data provide the highest Z scores in frontotemporal dementia associated with MAPT mutations.

Performance of disease-associated gene prediction

To evaluate whether the genes within the identified disease modules are indeed associated with the disease, we used the Open Targets Platform (OTP), an open-source and publicly available knowledge base that provides data and tools for systematic target-disease association as a reference.36 To quantify the network proximity between two gene sets while reducing the connectivity bias, e.g., due to hub genes, we calculate a percentile-based shortest-path distance metric. We first computed the matrix of shortest-path distances from each gene in the disease module to each gene in the top 100 most significantly associated genes in the OTP. For each gene set in the disease module, the distances to all top 100 genes in the OTP were converted to percentile ranks based only on finite (reachable) values. This step normalizes absolute distances by the connectivity structure of the network, reducing the impact of high-degree hub nodes that are inherently close to many other nodes. The final set-to-set proximity score was defined as the median of all percentile ranks between gene pairs, with values ranging from 0 (closest possible) to 1 (most distant). We found that RFOnM achieved shorter distances in 6 out of 12 diseases, which implies that the genes identified by RFOnM are closer to disease target genes (Figure S1).

Pathway enrichment analysis

We performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis to elucidate the biological pathways that are significantly enriched in the gene set within the disease module. This analysis enables us to understand the functional implications of the disease module, particularly in the context of their involvement in specific biological pathways. To ensure robustness in our findings, we employed the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method for p value adjustment to control for multiple hypothesis testing, with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold set at 0.05. After conducting the enrichment analysis, we compared the adjusted p values (on a log10 scale) associated with KEGG pathways derived from gene sets within the disease modules identified by different methods. Our findings indicate that the genes identified by RFOnM are associated with more significantly enriched KEGG pathways than those identified by ROBUST, DIAMOnD, and DOMINO (Figure 2B). For instance, RFOnM outperforms other methods in 7 out of 12 datasets, while the second-best method (DIAMOnD) shows superiority in 2 out of 12 diseases in each omics separately.

Disease-module and enriched pathways of COPD

We then focused on COPD, as it is the third leading cause of death worldwide37 and has the advantage of having both large-scale gene-expression and GWAS datasets available. We performed KEGG enrichment analysis to identify significantly over-represented biological processes among genes in the disease module of COPD with FDR < 0.05. We visualized these pathways, finding that most genes in the disease module are associated with cholesterol metabolism and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation (Figure S2A). Dysregulated lipid metabolism contributes to COPD pathogenesis by altering energy balance and promoting the synthesis of pro-inflammatory molecules within lung tissue.38 NET formation, amplified during viral infections such as rhinovirus, plays a functional role in COPD exacerbations by promoting inflammation through the release of double-stranded DNA, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.39 We also found that more pathways are enriched by module genes from integrated omics and that those pathways are more directly relevant to COPD pathophysiology (Figure S2A) than single-omics disease-module genes (Figures S2B and S2C). These include lipid metabolism, smooth muscle regulation, and neuroimmune interactions, mechanisms well established in COPD.

Validation of disease-module genes of COPD via larger studies

To examine whether identifying the disease module can facilitate the discovery of potential molecular biomarkers, we employed additional large-scale gene-expression data of RNA sequencing on lung tissues from 377 COPD individuals and 267 controls,40 and a large GWAS of 58,559 cases and 937,358 controls from the Global Biobank Meta-analysis Initiative.29 We applied RFOnM with and without a degree penalty and other disease-module detection to previous small-scale COPD datasets to identify the disease module associated with COPD. We then compared the Z scores computed from large-scale gene-expression data for those genes within and outside the disease module. We found that the Z scores of genes within the RFOnM module with the degree penalty are significantly higher than those of genes outside the module, regardless of gene-expression-derived Z scores (Figure 3A) or GWAS-derived Z scores (Figure 3B), while DIAMOnD and DOMINO show significance for modules identified from GWASs. Next, we focused on those genes with negative gene-expression-derived Z scores, which indicate a lower likelihood of association with COPD. For each of these genes, we calculated the maximum Z score between two Z scores computed from large datasets to assess its potential association with COPD in future studies. We found that Z scores of those genes within the COPD modules identified by RFOnM and DIAMOnD were significantly higher than genes not in the module (Figure 3C). This finding suggests that genes within the module, even if not significantly associated with the disease in smaller studies, may be associated with COPD in larger or subsequent studies. We also observed that these significant differences were absent when RFOnM was applied with the degree penalty. To investigate this, we compared the node degrees of genes within the identified modules to those outside the modules. We found that all methods, except RFOnM without the penalty, show significantly higher degrees for genes within the modules (Figure 3D). This suggests that degree bias plays a critical role in module validation and may inflate performance metrics if not properly controlled.

Discussion

A disease module refers to a well-defined neighborhood or subgraph of the molecular interactome linked to a complex disease, which assumes that if a gene or molecule is involved in a specific disease, its direct interactors might also be suspected to have some role in the same biochemical process.4 Unlike topological modules, often identified through community detection, integrating omics data with biological networks allows us to identify disease modules associated with a specific disease, also known as “active modules.”16 Here, we developed a disease-module detection method based on a statistical physics model RFOnM for multi-omics active disease-module detection. We applied our method to analyze four gene-expression datasets and four GWAS datasets covering Alzheimer’s disease, asthma, COPD, diabetes, and five cancers. We evaluated the disease modules identified by different methods using connectivity, enrichment analysis, and disease association. We found that RFOnM displayed superior performance than other methods in most diseases.

In addition to cholesterol metabolism and NET formation (Figure S2), chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species, generated from both cigarette smoke and endogenous inflammatory processes, are key drivers of COPD pathogenesis by inducing oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids, leading to epithelial cell injury and disease progression.41 Calcium signaling, essential for key epithelial functions such as mucus secretion, cilia movement, and tissue repair, is impaired in smokers and COPD patients, suggesting that disrupted Ca2+ homeostasis contributes to epithelial dysfunction and disease progression in COPD.42 The cyclic guanosine monophosphate-protein kinase G (cGMP-PKG) signaling pathway, mediated by soluble guanylyl cyclase, plays a protective role in the lung by maintaining barrier integrity and suppressing inflammation. In COPD and resistive breathing models that mimic airway obstruction, this pathway is downregulated, leading to lung injury, increased airway resistance, and inflammation.43 COPD is frequently associated with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and unfolded protein response (UPR) activation. The ER stress and UPR pathways represent promising therapeutic targets for slowing COPD progression and reducing the severity of exacerbations and related symptoms.44 Vitamin D deficiency is associated with increased risk and severity of COPD, and lower serum vitamin D levels correlate inversely with disease progression, highlighting the importance of vitamin absorption pathways and the potential for vitamin D as a modifiable factor in COPD management.45 These findings imply that RFOnM provides a framework for uncovering therapeutic pathways by highlighting context-specific network regulators that cannot be captured from individual gene-level analyses. In addition, the identification of overlapping modules across diseases offers mechanistic insights into comorbidities, while module-based network propagation enables gene prioritization even in the context of limited data, making it a promising approach for studying rare diseases.46

We examined the overlap between disease modules for Alzheimer’s disease, asthma, COPD, and diabetes, and found limited gene-level intersection among them (Figure S3A). This suggests that the identified modules capture largely disease-specific molecular mechanisms. In particular, an overlap between asthma and COPD is expected, as both diseases are characterized by airway inflammation. However, when the model’s degree penalty is removed, different modules exhibit substantial gene overlap across different diseases (Figure S3B).

We used two omics in our RFOnM approach. However, our RFOnM method can naturally integrate other omics data, e.g., DNA methylation, as long as those omics can yield gene-wise p values. Although multi-omics data are not always available for complex diseases right now, we anticipate that there should be more studies generating multi-omics data in the future. Additionally, instead of relying solely on two gene-wise p values, multiple omics data can be incorporated into the model. However, further research is needed to optimize the model’s configuration and to understand how integrating different omics types affects its overall performance. We integrated omics data with the PPI network from the STRING database. Using different interactomes, such as curated PPIs, can evaluate the robustness of disease-module detection methods across various interactome configurations, highlighting the need for further investigation. In summary, we provided an approach that can integrate multi-omics with the interactome, which holds potential for biomarker identification for complex diseases.

The identification of final disease modules using multi-omics approaches heavily depends on the overlap between different types of omics data. For example, genes that show significant associations in gene-expression data may not always exhibit similarly strong associations in GWASs, where p values could be higher. In the current method, a gene is included in the final disease module if it is suggested by either gene-expression data or GWAS data, which can lead to the inclusion of false-positive signals, particularly when constructing large disease modules. Therefore, understanding the extent of overlap between different omics data and developing methods to efficiently integrate these data types for robust disease-module identification warrants further investigation.

Limitations of the study

RFOnM integrates multi-omics data to identify disease modules, enabling the model to leverage cross-layer interactions between different omics types. Additionally, the incorporation of a hub gene penalty discourages the selection of genes from well-characterized highly connected pathways, promoting the discovery of novel disease mechanisms. Nevertheless, the RFOnM framework has certain limitations. First, its ground state cannot be solved exactly. Instead, the model approximates the optimal gene configuration by minimizing a global cost function. Due to the complex energy landscape and the presence of multiple local minima, the resulting modules may not be unique. In contrast, methods such as DIAMOnD produce deterministic module outputs. However, the Pearson correlation between the genes’ states for RFOnM across different realizations for each external field parameter H remains close to 1, indicating that our method is robust to the stochasticity of the optimization process (see Figure S4). In addition, we discretized the state of genes to obtain the approximate solution via simulated annealing. The exact solution of the ground state of RFOnM warrants the development of advanced methods, e.g., reinforcement learning. Second, although we only retained the largest module for simplicity, the disease module identified by RFOnM may consist of multiple disconnected submodules, and different submodules were enriched in distinct but biologically relevant pathways (see Figure S5). This occurs because RFIM defines the disease module through a global optimization problem across the entire interactome without requiring the module to be a single connected component within the human interactome. In addition, obtaining the ground state of RFOnM through simulated annealing requires a relatively longer time than other disease-module detection methods, particularly due to the combinatorial complexity of the RFOnM ground-state problem. Although we employed an efficient optimization strategy based on simulated annealing to solve the RFOnM ground-state problem on a large-scale PPI network, our formulation of the disease-module detection as a global optimization problem is fundamentally different and much more difficult than for other methods based on local agglomerative processes. Finally, RFOnM is sensitive to gene-wise p values, which may result in different modules for the same disease when these p values vary substantially. This is different from seed gene-based methods, which rely more on the relative ranking of genes rather than their absolute p values.

Method details

Disease-module detection using the random-field O(n) model

We formalized the disease-module detection problem as the ground state problem of the random field O(n) model as follows. We represent the state of node (gene) i as an n-component unit-length spin vector σi, where each component σi(α) represents the tendency of node i belonging to the disease module in omics type-α. Given a graph G=(V,E), i.e., the PPI network with a node set V (with node weights {hi}) and an edge set E (with edge weights {Jij}), we aim to find the optimal gene state {σi}, referred to as the ground state, that minimizes the following energy function:

Here, without the loss of generality, we focused on the case of two omics data types (i.e., n=2),where the spin vector of each node is represented as σi=[cosθi,sinθi]T. hi represents the intrinsic preference of nodes belonging to the disease module regardless of the states of their neighboring nodes, and the α-component hi(α) was computed from the Z-transformed p value of gene-i in omics data type-α, denoted as pi(α), i.e., hi(α)=Z(−log10pi(α)). Note that hi(α) is a monotonically decreasing function of pi(α) with hi(α)→+∞ as pi(α)→0 and hi(α)→−∞ as pi(α)→1. Nodes with very positive hi(α) (or equivalently, very low pi(α)) tend to belong to the disease module. Whether gene-i belongs to the disease module is determined by its spin vector σi in the ground state.

The first term in Equation 1 represents network coupling that encourages alignment between connected nodes, and the second external term pushes each node’s angular state θ to align with evidence derived from multi-omics data. The external field H=[H,H] is introduced to regulate the contributions of the two terms in Equation 1 and effectively control the size of the disease module (Figure 1). For example, if H is extremely low such that (H+hi)<0 for all the nodes, then all nodes will be inactive (i.e., σi(α)<0) in the ground state and there will be no disease module. With gradually increasing H, more nodes will be active (i.e., σi(α)>0). Eventually, if H is high enough such that (H+hi)>0 for all the nodes, then all nodes will be active in the ground state and the whole interactome will be the disease module. Hence, by controlling the parameter H, we can obtain a disease module with any desired size between 0 and N. For any given H and a random initial gene state {σi}, an optimization algorithm using simulated annealing was used to find the optimal gene state {σi} that minimizes the cost function H({σi}). To determine the final disease module, we evaluated a range of thresholds τ∈{0.85,0.86,⋯0.95} for sine and cosine-transformed gene scores derived from angular coordinates θ, selecting genes that exceeded a given threshold in at least one omics. For each candidate module size Nm∈{500,600,⋯1,000}, we iteratively identified the threshold that resulted in a gene set closest in size to the desired target and computed the mean disease relevance score using Z-scores across the genes in the largest connected component of the resulting subnetwork. The optimal module size and threshold were selected based on the combination that maximized the average Z score. Finally, we extracted the corresponding genes from the largest connected component of the gene interaction network as the final disease module.

Calculation of the ground state of RFOnM

We used the simulated annealing algorithm47 to approximately solve the ground state of the RFOnM. Note that simulated annealing is a heuristic algorithm for approximating the global optimum of a given objective function inspired by the process of annealing in metallurgy. To calculate the ground state of the RFOnM with n=2, we started from a high temperature T and a random initial state {σi} obtained by randomly selecting an angle θ for each node. We discretized the angle θ into 51 discrete values ranging from −π to π to ensure an equal distribution of positive and negative angles, with 0 positioned as the central value. We randomly pick a node and assign a new random angle θ to it and calculate the new energy H({σi′}). The new angle θ will be accepted if H(σi′)<H({σi}) or accepted with probability p=e−[H(σi′)−H({σi})]/T if H(σi′)≥H({σi}). Then, we systematically decrease the temperature T, and repeat the step until the system energy has converged.

Datasets

Interactome

For each disease, we overlaid the gene expression values and GWAS gene-wise p values onto the human interactome. For the human interactome, we used the STRING48 (9606.protein.links.v12.0.txt) database (http://string-db.org), which aims to provide a critical assessment and integration of PPIs, including both direct (physical) and indirect (functional) associations. We only focused on the physical interactions and removed the PPIs whose scores were lower than 400. The STRING Ensemble protein id (ENSP) is converted into the preferred gene name (e.g., Entrez gene ID) using the protein information (9606.protein.info.v12.0.txt) downloaded from the STRING database.

Data process and availability

We used MAGMA49 (Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation) to analyze the GWAS data. Reference data and gene location data were downloaded from https://cncr.nl/research/magma/. The GWAS data of Alzheimer was downloaded from https://cncr.nl/research/summary_statistics/. The GWAS data of diabetes was downloaded from http://diagram-consortium.org/downloads.html (version 2016). The GWAS data of COPD was collected in,27 which combines 26 cohorts containing 63,192 individuals (15,256 COPD cases and 47,936 controls). The p values and annotations of SNPs for diabetes, and COPD were obtained using MAGMA. For the asthma GWAS,50 the p values of SNPs were directly downloaded from http://jenger.riken.jp/en/result and SNPs were annotated to the genes in the summary results. The p value of each gene in all datasets was obtained using mean χ2 statistic of SNPs included in the gene and a known approximation of the sampling distribution of the mean χ2 statistic51,52 using MAGMA. Gene expression data were downloaded from GEO with accessions GSE63060, GSE69683, GSE47460, and GSE76894. p values were obtained by differential expression analysis using limma.53 mRNA and DNA methylation of five cancer datasets were downloaded from https://github.com/chenzRG/Cancer-Multi-Omics-Benchmark. p values were obtained using a t-test between cancer and non-cancer groups and adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. The frontotemporal dementia dataset was downloaded from https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Methylation_mValues/21257694?file=37676172. p values were obtained by differential expression analysis using DESeq2.54

Differential expression

We performed differential expression analysis using the limma package. Models were adjusted for age, race, sex, current smoking status, and smoking pack-years for LTRC data; sex for asthma and Alzheimer’s diseases; BMI and age for diabetes. The choice of these confounding factors depends on their availability in data. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to maintain a false discovery rate (FDR) < 5%.

Other disease-module detection methods

The ROBUST code was downloaded from https://github.com/bionetslab/robust. The parameters were set as follows: initial fraction value (init) of 0.25, reduction factor value (red) of 0.8, number of trees to be returned (trees) of 30, and threshold of 0.1. The top 10 most significant genes were selected as seed genes. The DOMINO code was downloaded from https://github.com/Shamir-Lab/DOMINO/. For each dataset, we selected the 100 most significantly expressed genes as active genes. If the modules of DOMINO using particular omics of some diseases do not include any genes, we used the module identified from other omics instead. The DIAMOnD was downloaded from https://github.com/dinaghiassian/DIAMOnD/. The 10 most significantly expressed genes were selected as seed genes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the enrichKEGG function from the clusterProfiler35 R package (v4.17.0), with organism set to Homo sapiens (hsa). p values were adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method, with significance defined at adjusted p < 0.05. Data are shown as boxplots (center line, median; box, interquartile range; whiskers, 1.5 × interquartile range) with individual data points displayed, or as bar plots representing a single data point per method (no error bars shown). Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.0). For group comparisons with replicates, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were applied (one-sided). A threshold of p < 0.05 was considered significant.
