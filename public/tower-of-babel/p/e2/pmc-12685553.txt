Two decades of filariasis in India: Insights into prevalence, diagnostic challenges, and strategies for elimination by 2027
Authors: Nandini Singh, Loick Pradel Kojom Foko, Jyoti Chhibber-Goel, Amit Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Lymphatic filariasis (LF) remains endemic in some Indian states.•Odisha reported the highest number of cases (17,038) in 2016.•Wuchereria bancrofti is the predominant LF-causing parasite.•Reliance on microscopy can miss atypical or amicrofilaraemic cases.•Zoonotic filariasis can hinder elimination.•Better diagnostics, treatments, and a One Health approach are vital.

Introduction

Lymphatic filariasis (LF), or filariasis, is one of the 13 neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) targeted for eradication and elimination out of 21 diseases and disease groups, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) (Global Report on NTDs 2024, WHO) [1]. It poses a significant global public health challenge, particularly in tropical and subtropical areas. According to the WHO 2024 estimates, ∼657 million individuals in 39 countries are still under the threat of LF, and this requires chemotherapeutic interventions to stop spread [1]. Helminths of the genera Wuchereria and Brugia have been implicated as the causative agents, the most common being Wuchereria bancrofti (Wb), Brugia malayi (Bm), and Brugia timori (Bt) for LF in humans [1]. These threadlike nematode worms are transmitted to humans by infected mosquitoes, mainly from the genera Aedes, Anopheles, Culex [1], and Mansonia [2]. In India, 99.4% of the LF cases are caused by Wb [2] and are predominantly transmitted by Culex quinquefasciatus [2,3]. In addition, 0.6% of the cases caused by Bm are transmitted by Mansonia spp. and Anopheles spp. [2].

The complex interplay between the parasite and its mosquito vector involves specific developmental stages within the mosquito, facilitating the transmission of infective larvae during blood meals to the human host [4]. The parasite matures into adult worms and resides in the lymphatic system, leading to characteristic clinical manifestations e.g., lymphedema, hydrocele and elephantiasis [4]. Unlike many common tropical parasitic infections, LF primarily affects adults rather than children or infants, although it may be acquired in childhood [1,5]. Interestingly, a significant number of individuals remain asymptomatic throughout their lives, and only a few go on to develop filarial disease [1]. Globally, ∼36 million individuals have chronic manifestations of LF according to WHO 2024 [1]. Affected individuals, especially those suffering from hydrocele and elephantiasis, along with other medical complications, often experience negative economic and psychosexual impacts [6]. The physical limitations imposed by limb swelling decrease work capacity, leading to income loss and financial instability, which is particularly evident in tropical countries where manual labor is the main source of livelihood [7]. Individuals with LF also experience altered body image, depression, anxiety, and reduced self-esteem due to their appearance, consequently affecting marital prospects and sexual relationships [7,8]. Moreover, in cultures where fertility and physical strength are highly valued, the implications of LF can extend to one's social standing and self-worth [8].

Although a 74% decline in LF infection has been documented since WHO launched the Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis (GPELF) in 2000 [1], persistent LF endemicity in countries such as India remains a concern [3]. In poor-resource settings and marginalized, difficult-to-reach communities, the epidemiological patterns contradict the recent gains and successes of the GPELF using yearly single-dose administration of antifilarial chemotherapy [9]. This indicates a complex challenge and worrying trend of LF that requires continuous and diversified efforts beyond the existing program’s scope in the context of countries where this disease remains endemic [9]. In addition, while the mass drug administration (MDA) endorsed by WHO has been instrumental in reducing the microfilaria (mf), antigenemia, and thus LF prevalence in many endemic regions, challenges such as the accessibility of remote areas, hard-to-reach populations, community awareness, and the potential for drug resistance pose obstacles in global efforts to control and eliminate the disease [9,10]. In a population of ∼1.4 billion [1], ∼0.619 million lymphedema and 0.126 million hydrocele cases have been noted [3]. Total of 339 districts from 20 states/Union territories (UTs) of India are infested with filarial parasites [3], thereby contributing 40% of the global burden of filariasis [3,11]. Currently, ∼650 million individuals in India face the threat of LF [3]. Since its record in the 6th century B.C. by Sushruta in Sushruta Samhita to the launch of the national MDA campaign with diethylcarbamazine citrate (DEC) in 202 districts/UTs in 2004, India has made steady and significant strides in controlling LF (Supplementary Figure 1) [3].

Still, along with the general challenges mentioned above, different issues persist, ranging from inconsistent drug delivery and treatment coverage, limited involvement of local leaders—which restricts social mobilization—to unavoidable delays in the availability of diagnostic tools such as the immunochromatographic test (ICT) cards (Alere Inc., Scarborough, ME, USA) [3]. The national MDA coverage increased to ∼69% in 2023 (GPELF). Many districts may still fall short of the ≥65% effective coverage required for interrupting transmission [1], [3]. Low compliance rates owing to fear of side effects, misinformation, and lack of awareness may also lead to suboptimal treatment coverage [12,13]. Stigma, limited awareness, and misdiagnosis by frontline workers may further hinder early detection [14].

Alongside is an emerging zoonotic threat, namely human dirofilariasis, caused by Dirofilaria repens (Dr), which remains underdiagnosed, misreported, and it generally lacks adequate surveillance and research [15]. Dirofilariasis is an infection of domestic and wild canines commonly caused by Dr (subcutaneous dirofilariasis) and Dirofilaria immitis (pulmonary dirofilariasis) [16]. Human dirofilariasis is accidentally transmitted via zoonosis, generally to individuals with close proximity to pets [16]. The vectors responsible for its spread are similar to those of filarial vectors, i.e., Aedes, Anopheles, and Culex, which aggravates the chances of transmission [21]. Since the first case of ocular dirofilariasis from Kerala, reported by Joseph et. al. in 1976 [17], and the subsequent first documented case of subcutaneous dirofilariasis from the same state by Senthivel and Pillai in 1999 [18], dirofilariasis is no longer considered rare, indicating endemicity. There are also imported cases that go undiagnosed due to the unavailability of a standard antibody detection test [19]. The population living in close proximity to pets has higher chance of accidental infection [16,20].

India’s LF elimination efforts face unique challenges, including a population of ∼1.4 billion, high migration rates (contributing to 10-15% of cases in non-endemic areas), and remaining gaps in diagnostics, morbidity management and disability prevention (MMDP) [14]. This study reviews two decades of LF data in India, addressing diagnostic complexities, zoonotic threats like dirofilariasis, and systemic barriers. We propose targeted solutions to strengthen control strategies in line with India’s goal to eliminate LF by 2027 [11].

Methods

Guidelines for the systematic review

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [22] 2020 guidelines were followed for the current study (Supplementary Figure 2).

Search strategy

We searched for relevant studies in the health-related electronic databases, namely PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, followed by a Google Scholar search at a later stage. The search was conducted between January 1, 2021 and December 2023. Included publications were limited to those in the English language, with a publication date range from January 1, 2001, to December 31, 2023. Our search strategy employed key terms and the Boolean operators ‘AND’ and ‘OR.’ Terms used to identify relevant literature were ``Filariasis AND India,'' ``Brugia malayi AND India,'' ``Dirofilaria immitis AND India,'' ``Dirofilaria repens AND India,'' and ``Wuchereria bancrofti AND India''. Also, “Filariasis AND India OR Brugia malayi AND India OR Dirofilaria immitis AND India OR Dirofilaria repens AND India OR Wuchereria bancrofti AND India.” The truncation element (*) was also used to refine the search strategy for eligible citations. Additionally, reference lists from the eligible studies were scrutinized to increase the number of potentially eligible studies.

Eligibility

Screening was conducted for eligibility of the studies and included if the study (a) assessed LF in human populations, dirofilariasis in human and dogs, regardless of diagnostic method (e.g., thick blood film, polymerase chain reaction); (b) was conducted in India or included Indians diagnosed during their travel or immigration to other nations; (c) reported LF prevalence in human populations; (d) reported figures for both the total tested group and the number of positive cases, or was an individual case report; (e) involved naturally infected subjects; (f) was a peer-reviewed publication; (g) was designed as cross-sectional, case-control, retrospective, or prospective; (h) was published between 2001 and 2023; and (i) as available in full text. Studies were also included if they assessed LF prevalence in both human and animal populations within the same study area. Those that did not fulfil the specified inclusion criteria were excluded from consideration. Hence, studies on experimental infections, drug compliance studies, MDA and their outcomes, preprints, conference papers, and papers with no full text, restricted access, or reviews were excluded.

Screening

The authors initially screened the retrieved articles by reviewing their titles and abstracts. Full-text versions of articles considered potentially relevant were then examined to determine final eligibility. Two authors (NS and JCG) independently identified and evaluated the studies, and any discrepancy was resolved through consensus and discussion with the lead author (AS). The data were collected using an extraction sheet, which was applied to the eligible studies and then cross-checked for errors. Covidence software [23] (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia; available at www.covidence.org), utilizing Cochrane's Risk of Bias 2 tool, was employed to screen the titles and abstracts of articles. The data collected included the name of the first author, year the data was published (if collection year was not available), Indian area (i.e., state or union territory), study design, LF species, prevalence (numerator and denominator), study population, clinical symptoms, atypical presentations, associated comorbidities and/or concurrent pathogens, and diagnostic methods. This was further organized via https://www.rayyan.ai/.

Data management

Data from case reports were analyzed to assess potential diagnosis obstacles, determine disease trends, contribute toward accurate disease mapping, and provide accounts of clinical presentation. The primary outcome focused on the distribution of LF cases in India by year and location from 2001 to 2023, and was integrated into a figure using mapchart.net and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA, www.graphpad.com). In our study, a case refers to an individual who showed symptoms of LF or tested positive for filariasis, as indicated by the presence of mf/filarial antigen/antibody against the filarial worm. If multiple datasets were provided in the study, then only confirmed cases of LF were included as cases. If a study was conducted in more than one state, but the numerator data were not given separately for each, we categorized the region as ‘not specified.’ The Chord diagram was created for the body systems affected with manifestations of diseases in terms of varied clinical symptoms and the body systems with affected organs, with the help of the “Circlize” package in R [24]. Since most studies did not specify the year of data collection, the year of publication was considered as the year of study to maintain consistency in mapping the cases (a potential limitation in the study design). For other missing information, we outlined it as ‘not specified’. Only case reports were analyzed for clinical manifestations because complete data were not available in the other 56 studies. One of the case reports was excluded from the analysis of affected organs and body systems because the entire body was affected [25].

Results

Our search identified 3,549 articles from four different databases (Supplementary Figure 2): PubMed (612), Embase (1263), Scopus (1270), and Google Scholar (404). Of these articles, 1,454 were included based on the year of publication being between January 2001 and December 2023, and the rest were excluded: PubMed (448 articles), Embase (658), Scopus (868), and Google Scholar (121). The articles were then screened and removed if (a) they were duplicates; (b) they did not have full text available, or (c) they were irrelevant to the study. A total of 319 articles were reviewed and processed to extract data for the current study, of which 157 were used for the analysis (Supplementary Figure 2). Studies included data for the numerator and denominator populations or individuals of the tested group.

Characteristics of the included studies

In 2001, only two publications were identified from the four relevant databases; none were identified for 2003. The number of publications increased markedly since 2009, peaking in 2013 and 2016 (Supplementary Figure 3a).

It is important to note that the publication year differs from the year of the sample survey or case reported in the study. All the articles collected were categorized into the following five categories: case reports (102), case series (14), prospective studies (36), retrospective studies (4), and transmission assessment surveys, TAS (1), cumulating to 157 studies (Supplementary Figure 3b). In the prospective studies, there were ∼22925 cases, of which 22610 (98.63%) were due to Wb. All human cases were due to Wb, and 251 (1.09%) were due to Bm. Bm was responsible for 39.0% cases in humans, and 61.0% cases in dogs. There were 64 cases (0.28%) caused by Dr in dogs . The lowest number of human cases (16) were reported in TAS, all of which were caused by Wb (Supplementary Figure 3b).

Diagnosis and treatment

Microscopy remains the gold standard for diagnosing filariasis, although it can be complicated by the absence of mf from peripheral blood smears due to the nocturnal periodicity of Wb in India. In such cases, other diagnostic tests, such as rapid diagnostic tests, specifically the ICT card test and the filarial test strip (FTS), play a crucial role in the timely detection of infections in low-resource point-of-care settings. Our analysis (Supplementary Figure 4a) showed that microscopy was the most used diagnostic method (2,342 cases), likely due to its accessibility in resource-limited settings, followed by the Indirect Hemagglutination (IHA) test (2,215 cases), which is effective for detecting antibodies in amicrofilaraemic cases. The least used was the filarial antibody test (1 case). An advanced technique, ultrasound scan, helps in visualizing the ‘Filaria Dance Sign,’ but it was used only in two cases (Supplementary Figure 4a). Triple antifilarial drugs—diethylcarbamazine (DEC), albendazole (ALB), and ivermectin (IVM) —provided relief in most cases, followed by the treatment of comorbidities, if any. The treatment modalities in 102 filariasis case reports showed that DEC (6 mg/kg) was used in 82.28% of cases, followed by ALB (400 mg) and IVM (200 µg/kg), either alone or in combination, in accordance with WHO antifilarial dosage protocols (Supplementary Figure 4b). Further analysis showed that 44.25% of patients treated with antifilarial drugs achieved full recovery, while mortality was reported in 2.66% of cases, primarily due to comorbid conditions (Supplementary Figure 4c).

Out of 1,488,432 examined, 1.19% were males, 1.06% were females, 0.13% were non-humans, and for 97.62%, gender was not specified (Supplementary Figure 5a). Analyses of a total of 23,192 positive cases showed that 47.26% were males, 45.18% were females, 0.98% were non-humans, and for 6.58%, the gender was not specified (Supplementary Figure 5b).

Epidemiology

From January 2001 to December 2023, data from 157 published studies cumulatively reported 23,192 cases out of total of 1,488,432 who were screened and suspected spanning 25 states/UTs (Supplementary Figure 6). Heatmap analysis of all cases from each state/UT from 2001 to 2023 revealed that Odisha reported the highest number of cases (17,038) in 2016, as it utilized the morbidity census survey data for lymphedema conducted there in 2005 (Supplementary Figure 6). Among reported positive cases (23,192), 22,938 were human filarial infections caused by Wb and Bm. Human dirofilariasis cases, caused by Dr numbered 26, and non-human filarial cases, caused by Bm, were 153, and Dr caused 75 cases of non-human (canine) dirofilariasis (Supplementary Figure 3b). Notably, Wb cases were reported from humans only.

GPPP

Studies with suspected/tested cases of more than 30 were included in prevalence analyses. LF prevalence was greatest in Puducherry (GPPP: 43.79%), likely due to the small sample sizes in the included studies (e.g., 500 examined vs. 1 million in Odisha) (Supplementary Figure 6 and Figure 1), suggesting sampling bias. Larger surveys are needed to confirm this high prevalence. Bihar (8.95%), Madhya Pradesh (5.76%), and Uttar Pradesh (5.33%) followed (Figure 1), reflecting known endemicity in these regions due to poor sanitation and high mosquito density [3]. Kerala reported a GPPP of 22.32% for canine dirofilariasis and 8.67% for Brugian filariasis in dogs, with additional cases in Odisha, highlighting the zoonotic risk in coastal areas with high pet ownership [26]. Indigenous cases from non-endemic states

It is crucial to note that our study reports numbers from published surveys conducted in states with indigenous cases, as well as reports from states free from indigenous cases of filariasis infection. For instance, the National Centre for Vector Borne Disease Control (NCVBDC) has reported indigenous cases from ∼339 districts in 20 States/UTs [3] and suggests that North-Western States/UTs — namely Chandigarh, Delhi, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Punjab, Rajasthan, and Uttaranchal—as well as the northeastern States of Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, and Tripura, are free from indigenous filarial infection [3]. Some reports suggest the presence of LF in the tribal areas of India [27]. Contrarily, published case reports analyzed in the current study have documented instances in states previously considered filariasis-free, such as Haryana [28,29], Himachal Pradesh [30], New Delhi [31,32], Rajasthan [33,34], and Sikkim [35,36]. These cases may reflect underreporting or transmission via migrant populations, with studies suggesting 10–15% of LF cases in non-endemic areas as linked to migration [14]. Notably, in addition to cases of bancroftian and brugian filariasis, instances of dirofilariasis have been reported from Gujarat [37], Haryana [38], Karnataka [21], Kerala [16,21,[39], [40], [41], [42]], (Figure 1) Maharashtra [19,20,43,44], and Tamil Nadu [45,46].

Clinical manifestations and atypical presentations

Analysis of 23,192 cases reported over the past two decades, from January 2001 to December 2023, suggests that the clinical manifestations of LF in India vary. From the 89 case reports analyzed for human LF, from the perspective of organs affected, the integumentary system exhibited the highest involvement (nine organs), followed by the reproductive (8), musculoskeletal (7), and endocrine systems (5). LF, lymphatic filariasis.

LF manifested as a lump/nodule/swelling as reported in 41 out of 89 LF cases (Figure 2b) and 6 out of 12 human dirofilariasis cases, followed by pain (40); lymphangitis was not seen in any of the above (Figure 2b). LF mainly affects the lymphatic system, the integumentary system, the genitalia, and the peripheral tissues, especially the limbs and scrotum [1]. However, in our study, the 89 case reports of LF due to Wb and Bm, showed cardiovascular (3), digestive (8), endocrine (4), integumentary (21), lymphatic (1), musculoskeletal (12), nervous (1), reproductive (11), respiratory (14), sensory (4), skeletal (7) and urinary (3) system involvement with varied manifestations (Figure 2b). Twelve case reports were of human dirofilariasis from Kerala, which showed digestive (1), integumentary (4), musculoskeletal (1) and sensory (6) system involvement. Infection at atypical sites is not rare but underreported. Human dirofilariasis: an emerging zoonotic concern

The incidence of human dirofilariasis in India is notable in states such as Kerala (Figure 1), coastal Karnataka, and Maharashtra, with fewer cases in North India, Odisha, and Assam [48]. Cases have been reported with Dr found in subcutaneous nodules [16], cheeks [16], neck [39], and eyes [19,20,37,38,45]. Usually, the infection is asymptomatic, amicrofilaremic, with no marked eosinophilia and the nodular appearance, leading to a misdiagnosis of malignancy [21]. There is concern that Dirofilaria species might adapt to humans if adult worms start producing microfilariae in human hosts [48]. This is further alarming, given recent findings of Bm adaptation to animals; for instance, a sample survey of 1,600 dogs in Kerala revealed an 8.1% infection rate with Bm mf [26], [48], [49] contributing to a GPPP of 8.67% when combined with other studies. Cats and dogs in Odisha also carry D. immitis and Dr [50].

Coinfections

Out of 157 studies, only three studies reported coinfections with other parasites and bacteria. In one of the studies analyzed, it was shown that in visceral leishmaniasis (VL) endemic regions, those with prior LF infection have a higher risk of progressing to VL, with a risk ratio of 2.57 [50]. This increased risk may be due to LF-induced Th2 immune responses, which impair Th1-mediated protection against VL [50]. Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium falciparum coinfection with Wb complicates diagnosis and treatment [51]. Bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Klebsiella oxytoca, Enterococcus sp., Escherichia coli, and Enterobacter sp. were found infecting the wounds of patients with filarial elephantiasis [52]. Coinfection with Wb in cases of bacterial infections and in background of leishmaniasis and malaria can significantly alter the clinical course of action and modulate the immune response.

Discussion - Gaps and the way forward

India in on target to eliminate filariasis via a five-pronged strategy of NCVBDC [3] (a) transmission interruption, (b) MMDP, (c) integrated vector control, (d) high-level advocacy for intersectoral convergence with allied departments, and (e) leveraging technology for alternate diagnostics and existing digital platforms. While remarkable progress has been made through MDA campaigns, India continues to face hurdles in achieving complete elimination.

The elimination goal requires addressing key gaps such as: (a) improving MDA compliance by deploying community health workers to counter misinformation, targeting a 90% coverage rate (currently 69.10%); (b) screening pregnant women for LF to prevent neonatal immune sensitization, and integrating LF testing into antenatal care; (c) enhancing pediatric surveillance following Shenoy et al. (2007) [53], with school-based screening programs to detect early lymphatic damage; (d) expanding surveillance in non-endemic states like Haryana and Sikkim, where cases suggest underreporting, using mobile diagnostic units; (e) developing filaria-specific diagnostics (e.g., polymerase chain reaction-based kits) to address atypical presentations like breast filariasis; (f) implementing One Health strategies, and screening pets in endemic areas like Kerala to curb dirofilariasis; (g) creating a national LF database integrating vector, incidence, and MDA data for predictive modeling, that is accessible via platforms like the NCVBDC’s website.

Culex quinquefasciatus is the predominant vector of Wb in India, particularly in urban and peri-urban environments [2], [3]. The ecological and biological interactions between the parasite and vector profoundly influence transmission dynamics [54]. Unlike Anopheles, which exhibits ‘facilitation’ (increasing infectivity with higher mf densities), Culex shows ‘limitation,’ meaning it can sustain high transmission efficiency even at low mf densities, implying a lower threshold density for ongoing transmission [54]. In Pondicherry, six annual MDA rounds reduced mf prevalence and intensity by ∼72-91%, resulting in ∼80-95% decline in transmission indices (annual transmission potential/annual infective biting rate); however, transmission was not fully interrupted [55]. Recent studies by Vasuki et al. (2025) detected residual vector infection rates of ∼0.05–1.85% post-MDA, highlighting how the Culex limitation phenomenon can undermine elimination efforts despite high MDA coverage [56]. Consequently, elimination programs based solely on MDA may become less effective [54].

In areas where the southern house mosquito is dominant, supplementary interventions such as targeted larval source management, environmental sanitation, and improved housing conditions do complement MDA. Similar observations have been made in urban African settings, such as Dar es Salaam, where this phenomenon necessitated integrated vector management strategies in conjunction with drug-based interventions [54]. Thus there is need to incorporate the understanding of vector-parasite relationships into program design to achieve and sustain elimination.

Addressing LF alongside comorbid infections is crucial for accurate diagnosis, effective management, and the development of integrated public health interventions. Tailored treatment strategies, novel therapies, and drug combinations can enhance patient outcomes. Strengthening surveillance systems is vital for monitoring disease prevalence and guiding targeted interventions. Integrated diagnostics combining clinical evaluation with advanced testing can improve early detection and treatment success. Public awareness and community engagement are key to prevention and control. A coordinated response involving government bodies, researchers, and global partners, backed by sustainable funding, is critical to achieving elimination goals and ensuring a lasting impact on LF control in India and globally.

Limitations

This review may be subject to publication bias, as studies with significant findings are more likely to be published. The use of publication year as a proxy for study year introduces temporal misalignment, which can potentially skew trends. Excluding non-English studies and preprints may limit the scope, while relying on case reports for clinical data may overrepresent atypical manifestations, underestimating common ones, such as lymphedema.

Conclusion

Filariasis remains a significant public health challenge in India, with high prevalence in regions like Puducherry and zoonotic threats such as dirofilariasis in Kerala. While dogs serve as the main reservoirs for D. repens, cats can also harbor Dirofilaria and zoonotic Brugia species, sustaining transmission. To achieve LF elimination, country can adopt a One Health approach, integrating human, animal, veterinary, and vector surveillance while addressing diagnostic gaps through advanced testing and public awareness campaigns. Cross-sectororal collaboration will be crucial for reducing infections and achieving sustainable elimination. Policymakers may prioritize sustainable funding for MDA, MMDP, and digital platforms to ensure a lasting impact on LF control.
