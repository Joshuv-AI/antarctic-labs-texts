The expanding landscape of the glucagon signaling network: mechanisms and outcomes
Authors: Ashish Foollee, Yuqin Wu, Patricia M Rusu, Adam J Rose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucagon is a peptide hormone mainly secreted by the alpha cells of the pancreatic islets in response to nutritional stimuli. Traditionally recognized for its hyperglycemic function counteracting insulin action, it mainly acts on the liver to affect glycogen, amino acid, lipid, and energy metabolism. Beyond its metabolic effects, glucagon also increases hepatocyte cell survival and reduces viral replication. Peptides with glucagon activity have recently emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate for obesity, type 2 diabetes, and associated liver disease, with several glucagon-based agents currently in phase 2/3 clinical trials. Despite this progression, and although discovered roughly the same time as insulin, how glucagon exerts its pleiotropic effects on metabolism and beyond remains relatively poorly understood. Knowledge of these signaling pathways could pave the way for further refinement of glucagon-based pharmacotherapy with more pronounced effects or reduced side effects. This review aims to address established literature and its limitations on glucagon signaling mechanisms and provides an update on the state of the art on glucagon signaling pathways to help in understanding the mechanisms behind glucagon action.

Importance of glucagon

Over the last decade, the peptide hormone glucagon has gained newfound importance as a potential pharmacotherapy due to its pleiotropic metabolic effects and its close link to type 2 diabetes and other metabolic disorders (1, 2). Yet, despite glucagon-based therapies becoming more popular in the scientific community and some currently in phase 2 clinical trials (3-5), due to glucagon's effects on multiple signaling pathways across various tissues, many of these pharmacotherapies have side effects. Further investigation of the signaling pathways affected by glucagon could lead to further refinement of glucagon-based therapies or discovery of novel pharmacotherapeutic drug targets with reduced risks, longer-lasting or robust effects, and accessibility to a wider patient cohort. This review aims to facilitate the understanding of glucagon signaling pathways and address the current knowledge gaps while providing an updated review of glucagon biology with a focus on the liver actions and signaling pathways.

Glucagon is a 29-amino acid-long peptide hormone secreted by the α-cells of the pancreatic islets (6). It delivers its effects through activating the glucagon receptor (GCGR) protein, a highly conserved mammalian transmembrane protein predominantly expressed by the liver. Glucagon was discovered in 1923 by Kimball & Murlin when they isolated a pancreatic extract that led to hyperglycemia in dogs and rabbits (7). They named the compound “glucose-agonist,” later abbreviated as glucagon. Initially, research on glucagon was limited owing to several factors, such as its low picomolar concentrations in circulating blood (8) and its derivation from the proglucagon gene, which can be cleaved into various functional peptides depending on the cell type, making the detection of glucagon even trickier due to cross-reactivity with the other derived peptides (9). It was not until nearly two decades later that Unger et al devised a glucagon-sensitive radioimmunoassay (10), thereby facilitating effective glucagon detection. At present, high-quality sandwich enzyme-linked immunoabsorbance assays are available, further facilitating glucagon detection (11). In 1994, Svoboda et al used RT-PCR to detect Gcgr mRNA expression in specific tissues, which led to the discovery that the glucagon receptor was not limited to the liver, which clearly displays the highest expression, but was also present in other cell types, including the kidney, heart, and brain (12).

Glucagon has a relatively short half-life in mammals (2, 5, and 7 minutes in rats, dogs, and humans, respectively), making it harder to study the rapidly degraded hormone (13, 14). With all these drawbacks hindering glucagon research, it is perhaps unsurprising that, following one century of its discovery, glucagon is still mostly only known as the “counterbalance” hormone to insulin, even though it displays pleiotropic effects across the body, albeit the mechanisms behind several of these phenotypes remain poorly understood.

Metabolic effects of glucagon

Glucagon is still widely known for increasing blood glucose levels through the stimulation of glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, as opposed to insulin (10). However, glucagon also affects other aspects of metabolism.

It has been shown that while the ingestion of glucose alone cannot increase glucagon secretion (15), the sole ingestion of either proteins (16) or certain lipids (17) (particularly saturated fats) elevates circulating glucagon levels. Glucagon also displays hypolipidemic effects through enhanced lipolysis and ketogenesis (18) and affects whole-body energy metabolism by increasing satiety and energy expenditure of cells, maintaining the rate of mitochondrial turnover (19) and thermogenesis (20). Furthermore, glucagon potently affects protein/amino acid metabolism, increases amino acid uptake by hepatocytes, and increases ureagenesis (21). This effect of glucagon on amino acid metabolism is believed to be further enhanced through glucagon's effect on autophagy (22). Autophagy is the process by which cells degrade and recycle their components, and this allows cells to adapt to stress. Enhanced cellular autophagy was first observed in the livers of starved animals, and in 1967, de Duve & Deter (23) demonstrated that it was glucagon that stimulated autophagy. The link between glucagon and autophagy was further strengthened when deletion of hepatic ATG7 (22), a protein essential for autophagy, impaired amino acid release and increased blood glucose levels in fasting mice, contributing to the simultaneous decrease of insulin and stable secretion of glucagon.

Glucagon signaling is also evident in the kidneys, as confirmed by studies performing kidney-specific glucagon receptor manipulation (24, 25). Wang et al (24) showed that knocking down renal GCGR in mice leads to multiple pathological events, including hyperaminoacidemia with impaired renal glucose filtration, fibrosis, cardiac hypertrophy, inflammation, fat accumulation in the kidney, and affected water, electrolyte, and nitrogen homeostasis. Liu et al (25), on the other hand, reported that injecting glucagon exacerbates renal dysfunction in diabetic mice, while renal tubular epithelial cell-specific glucagon receptor knockout or knockdown slows down diabetic kidney progression. While the two papers' results contradict each other, it should be considered that Wang et al (24). and Liu et al (25) studied glucagon in physiological and pathological contexts, respectively, which could present a more nuanced role of glucagon in kidney homeostasis, which is context-based. Another study (26) investigated the effects of chronic glucagon administration and antibody-mediated glucagon receptor antagonism on mice and their kidneys and found that sustained glucagon injection led to renal pathological events (from glomerular mesangial cell expansion to albuminuria), with an opposite effect seen in mice with glucagon receptor inactivation. Taken together, glucagon clearly influences the kidney, and further research is now required to decipher downstream signaling events involved in these outcomes.

These further showcase glucagon's non-glycemic functions in the body, and it is imperative to understand these functions and especially how glucagon causes these effects. These results also highlight the potential dangers of long-term glucagon therapy and why it is crucial to develop novel, more specific drug target sites to synthesize new drugs that do not present with an increased risk of pathologies in other organs.

Non-canonical effects of glucagon

Contrary to traditional characterization as a purely metabolic hormone, glucagon not only has systemic metabolic effects but also affects other cell processes such as autophagy (23). In 2008, Sinclair et al (27) showed, both in vivo (mouse) and in vitro (primary hepatocytes), that glucagon played a role in promoting hepatocyte cell survival through apoptosis inhibition, and this was reproduced in models of both acute and chronic liver injury. Several studies (28-32) investigated the effect of glucagon on a variety of cancers, revealing contradictory findings, with some suggesting a role for glucagon in suppressing tumor cell growth (28-30), while others suggest that glucagon enhances tumor growth (31, 32). However, it should be noted that the studies supporting a tumor-promoting role for glucagon were performed in vitro, which does not account for the microenvironment of the tumor, while the studies indicating glucagon reduced tumor growth rates were performed in vivo, with one study (30) even suggesting that glucagon inhibits tumoral angiogenesis to cause its tumor-suppressive effects. Relatively new findings (33) have demonstrated the importance of the glucagon receptor in halting tumor growth progression by showing that a triple agonist, which also targets glucagon, leads to a 17-fold reduction in tumor progression compared to agonists targeting GLP-1 receptors and/or glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptors. Furthermore, glucagon affects viral replication, whereby glucagon stimulation in host cells hinders viral replication (34), and GCGR knockout leads to a 50% increase in viral replication in vitro (35). Another non-metabolic function of glucagon involves its effects on cell fate and liver zonation; mice lacking the glucagon receptor have impaired liver zonation, a process critical for optimal liver functionality (36).

Since glucagon affects multiple processes in various cell types and affects numerous cellular signaling pathways to trigger its pleiotropic effects, for the sake of this review, we will only focus on the cell signaling pathways of glucagon in the liver, its main target organ.

Glucagon and pathophysiology

Glucagon has been associated with multiple pathological events, such as its upregulation in type 2 diabetic (T2D) patients (1) and chronic kidney disease (37). Elevated glucagon production is also observed in many other pathological events, such as sepsis and glucagonoma (38). T2D is typically described as the chronic elevation of blood glucose (7), and its pathogenesis is primarily attributed to insulin deficiency or resistance. However, evidence indicates that euglycemia can also be disturbed by hyperglucagonemia in both the fasting and postprandial states (7, 39, 40).

Glucagon exacerbates the hyperglycemia of T2D patients through continued gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis stimulation (40). The mechanisms through which T2D and hyperglucagonemia are related remain incompletely understood and yet to be elucidated as to whether hyperglucagonemia is causing type 2 diabetes or a mere consequence of alpha cell dysfunction. Their understanding might potentially be the key to novel drug targets for diabetes treatment. However, one study (41) showed that the deletion of the proglucagon gene (responsible for the expression of glucagon and glucagon-like peptides amongst others) in mice surprisingly resulted in normoglycemic mice, indicating the work of compensatory mechanisms to maintain glucose homeostasis in mice, independent of glucagon. In rare instances, the glucagonoma syndrome (42), a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, resulting from uncontrolled pancreatic alpha cell proliferation, causes the secretion of excessive glucagon levels leading to hyperglucagonemia, which might lead to other symptoms such as weight loss, a skin rash commonly referred to as necrotizing migratory erythema, anemia, thromboembolism, glossitis, and stomatitis. Persistent glucagon secretion can lead to a condition called glucagon resistance (43), where body cells (predominantly the liver) that express the glucagon receptor become unresponsive to glucagon secretion, leading to an impaired ability of glucagon to regulate glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism. In the context of metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes, glucagon resistance may lead to higher blood glucose levels and hepatic fat deposition, although the concept and definition of glucagon resistance are controversial (44-49). Further investigation of the etiology of glucagon resistance is important, and will help understand the limitations or provide insight into improving glucagon-based therapies.

Glucagon and pharmacotherapy

Currently, glucagon injections are used as an emergency procedure to treat severe hypoglycemia in patients who cannot ingest sugar or in diabetic patients who have received excess insulin (50). It is also administered before X-ray imaging of the gastrointestinal tract due to its ability to reduce gastric and intestinal muscle contractions, allowing for greater X-ray penetrance and resulting in better image contrast (51).

Based on its association with hyperglycemia in type 2 diabetes, glucagon receptor antagonism was considered as a therapeutic strategy for this condition, but was abandoned due to contraindications (52). In 2015, Finan et al (53) reported that injecting glucagon and glucagon-receptor agonists in obese and diabetic mice decreased the body weight of obese mice and reduced lipid accumulation in their livers. In addition, these injections did not exacerbate the existing hyperglycemia in these mice, showcasing the potential of glucagon to act as a therapy for obesity and the resulting fat deposition in tissues without aggravating diabetes, which is a common sequel to obesity. In 2023, these studies led the path to successful phase 2 clinical trials using a combination of glucagon and glucagon agonists to treat type 2 diabetes (3), obesity (4) and in 2024, a phase 2 clinical trial was approved to use a dual agonist of the glucagon receptor to treat patients with metabolic-associated steatohepatitis and fibrosis (5).

However, the pleiotropic nature of glucagon's actions across multiple organs contributes to a range of undesired effects associated with its therapeutic use. The mild effects include nausea, vomiting, and headaches, while more severe side effects, although very rare, include tachycardia, allergies, hypertension, muscle wasting (54), and other symptoms of hyperglucagonemia (43).

Glucagon signaling

Although glucagon can affect multiple aspects of metabolism, how it does so at the tissue-, cellular-, and molecular levels is not yet fully resolved. The following sections outline the role of the cell surface G-protein-coupled receptor and effector signaling nodes and proteins downstream that execute glucagon action. Since the majority of research has been conducted on liver and hepatocellular glucagon signaling, this will be a focus of this section. Defining how glucagon signaling exerts actions through shared and distinct signaling nodes will be important from a basic endocrinology and biochemistry perspective, but also for future efforts to refine glucagon-based therapeutic strategies.

G-protein-coupled-receptors

Glucagon targets the glucagon receptor protein (GCGR), which is expressed predominantly in the liver, pancreatic islets, and kidneys (55). This receptor is a 62 kDa protein belonging to the B-class of the G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) family (56). GPCRs are the largest group of protein-membrane receptors in eukaryotes, with a huge diversity in their structures, recognized signaling molecules, mode of downstream signal transduction, and functions performed. They represent a major target for pharmaceutical drug development (57). Despite their major structural diversities, all GPCRs share some common features that are conserved among eukaryotes. For instance, they all consist of a single polypeptide chain spanning across the plasma membrane seven times, with loops on either side of the membrane for both target binding and effective signal transduction (58).

Upon glucagon activating the GCGR receptor (Fig. 1), a conformational change occurs within the receptor, which leads to the activation of the G-proteins coupled with the receptor in the intracellular space (80). The major G-proteins activated by glucagon are the G alpha I, Gs proteins and, to a lesser extent, G alpha q, Gq proteins. These proteins are involved in downstream glucagon signal transduction through different mechanisms, with Gs being responsible for the activation of the cAMP-dependent Protein kinase A (PKA) pathway (81). Constitutive activation of the Gs in hepatocytes leads to a rise in cAMP levels and subsequent activation of the PKA-CREB pathway, which causes hepatic glycogen breakdown and hyperglycemia in both random-fed and fasted mice (82). Conversely, in hepatocyte-specific Gs subunit-KO mice, both PKA-CREB signaling and MAPK/ERK signaling, crucial for cell survival and proliferation, were impaired, leading to a reduced hepatic regenerative ability (83). Other metabolic phenotypes of hepatocyte-specific Gs subunit-KO mice include increased hepatic weight with elevated glycogen and reduced adiposity, enhanced glucose tolerance test with both increased insulin secretion and sensitivity in both muscles and liver. During free access to food, these mice had hypoglycemia and unchanged lipid metabolism, but upon fasting, similar blood glucose levels with reduced lipolysis and increased lipogenesis as wildtype mice were observed, showcasing the context-dependent effect of the subunit in both glucose and lipid metabolism (83). In mice with pancreatic-beta-cell-specific Gs subunit-KO (84), a reduced beta-cell mass was observed, along with severely impaired glucose tolerance, hyperglycemia, reduced insulin secretion and sensitivity, and poor survival rates, further demonstrating the crucial role of the GPCRs and their subunits in homeostasis.

The Gq subunit primarily mediates its downstream signaling through phospholipase C (PLC) (Fig. 1) and primarily uses the release of calcium ions as a secondary messenger molecule (85). In pancreatic cells, the Gq subunit is also responsible for alpha-cell responsiveness to hypoglycemia, and its activation is known to increase both glucagon and insulin secretion and increase glucose tolerance in mice (86).

Protein kinase A and glucagon

Following the conformational change in the Gs subunit due to glucagon stimulating the receptor, the alpha subunit of the activated G-protein exchanges its guanosine diphosphate component for a guanosine triphosphate (GTP) (81). This exchange leads to the dissociation of the alpha subunit from the beta and gamma subunits of the activated G-protein. This released subunit then binds to the enzyme adenylyl cyclase, which catalyzes the synthesis of the secondary messenger, 3′,5′-cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) (Fig. 1), a crucial molecule that triggers the activation of most of the downstream glucagon signaling in this pathway (87). Insulin can prevent glycogenolysis and thereby increase glycogen levels during rest through the Akt pathway by activating the enzyme phosphodiesterase 3B (PDE3B). This enzyme inhibits adenylyl cyclase and the subsequent rise in cAMP levels, blocking most of glucagon's downstream signaling (88). This highlights the importance of cAMP in glucagon signaling, together with a mechanism through which insulin dampens glucagon response.

Once cAMP levels increase, four molecules of cAMP can bind to the two regulatory sites of the PKA, which leads to the detachment of the regulatory sites and exposes the catalytic subunits of PKA, thereby activating the kinase, which can phosphorylate serine/threonine residues through an ATP-dependent reaction (89).

Once activated, PKA can alter the activity of target downstream proteins through phosphorylation and modulate their functions. Target proteins include enzymes, ion channels, and transcription factors with eventual impact on cellular protein expression levels. This enables PKA to ultimately regulate multiple processes, including glycogenolysis, glycolysis, mitochondrial biogenesis, and lipogenesis (64, 90-121) (see Fig. 1 for specific pathways for each effect). Glucose metabolism

Upon activation, PKA can stimulate glycogenolysis by inducing a phosphorylation cascade event, ultimately leading to the activation of glycogen phosphorylase kinase, which acts on glycogen to release the metabolically inactive molecule glucose-1-phosphate from glycogen, marking the initial reaction of glycogen breakdown to release glucose (90). Through PKA, glucagon also inhibits glycolysis through the phosphorylation of the enzyme pyruvate kinase (91) (at position Ser12 for the liver-specific isoform) and indirectly inhibits phosphofructokinase (92), with both enzymes being key regulators of the glucose oxidation pathway. This inhibition leads to a decrease in fructose-2-6-bisphosphate, which is a key substrate not only in the glycolytic pathway but also acts as an inhibitor to the enzyme fructose-1-6-bisphosphatase, a regulatory enzyme in the gluconeogenesis pathway. Thus, through the inhibition of a single molecule, PKA can simultaneously impair glycolysis and enhance gluconeogenesis (93). Moreover, inhibition of pyruvate kinase leads to a reduced breakdown of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to pyruvate (final reaction of glycolysis) to release ATP, and the subsequent build-up of PEP signifies a metabolic shift from glycolysis to gluconeogenesis (94).

PKA also influences glycolysis through the glucokinase enzyme, which, in the post-prandial state, can be released from its membrane-bound receptor and phosphorylate glucose to glucose-6-phosphate for glycolysis (95). PKA leads to the phosphorylation of the glucokinase at its receptor, preventing the release of the kinase from its receptor, thereby decreasing the rate of glycolysis. This occurs when PKA phosphorylates liver-kinase B1 (LKB1) at Ser431 (96), which mediates adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation through the phosphosite Thr172 (97), strengthening glucokinase binding to its receptor and decreasing glycolysis. AMPK can also detect cell energy level changes. During the starvation state (98), where glucagon secretion occurs, AMPK is more active, further enhancing the effect on glucokinase, besides sole PKA/LKB1 stimulation.

A major target of PKA in glucagon signaling involves the cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB) (See Fig. 1 for visual representation) (109), a transcription factor responsible for the upregulation of various gluconeogenic genes. By phosphorylating CREB at the Ser133 site, PKA enhances the transcription factor, leading to the upregulation of various gluconeogenic genes, such as glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), thereby promoting gluconeogenesis. Furthermore, CREB enhances the activity of histone-modifying co-activators, such as p300 and CREB-binding protein, which in turn amplify gluconeogenic gene expression (110).

A third mechanism through which CREB enhances gluconeogenesis involves its interactions with transcriptional co-activators, such as the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ co-activator 1α (PGC-1α) and CREB-regulated transcription co-activator 2 (CRTC2) (111). PGC-1α also transcriptionally induces Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), which, along with Sirtuin 5 (SIRT5), is involved in the deacetylation and activation of enzymes stimulating fatty acid oxidation and ureagenesis (112).

Due to its potent action in inducing gluconeogenesis, CREB inhibition has been considered a potential therapeutic target for reducing hepatic glucose secretion into the bloodstream, thereby dampening diabetes (109). Transgenic mice expressing solely a dominant form of CREB called A-CREB in the liver, which has mutations rendering its transcriptional functions inactive by altering CREB's ability for DNA binding, displayed severe hypoglycemia upon fasting, along with reduced gluconeogenic gene expression, thereby showcasing CREB's crucial role in glucose homeostasis (113). However, studies (114) using liver-specific CREB knockout mice reported that the mice had euglycemia in both the fasting and fed states, with no altered glucose, insulin or glucagon tolerance and no effects on the key gluconeogenic genes. This could imply that A-CREB has off-target effects other than direct binding to DNA, which may have led to the observed hypoglycemia, as CREB may interfere with other bZIP family of transcription factors (115). Therefore, the phenotypic effects observed with A-CREB may be through the inhibition of non-CREB transcription factors, an event absent in the CREB-knockout mice.

Moreover, in liver-specific knockout mice for CRTC2 (116) (a transcriptional co-activator downstream of the PKA/CREB pathway), hepatic glucose output and blood glucose levels remain within the normal range under both the fasting and fed state, with only a slightly impaired glucagon-stimulated glucose production. These results align with the liver-specific CREB knockout studies and may imply that the effects of CREB on glucose metabolism might be more convoluted than expected, and further research on CREB is required to clarify the specific role of the hepatic CREB-CRTC2 pathway in glucagon-mediated hepatic glucose production.

Lipid metabolism

PKA-induced AMPK activation also hinders lipogenesis (see Fig. 1 for visual pathway) through inhibitory phosphorylation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 and 2 (ACC1 and ACC2) at Ser79 (99) and Ser212 (100), respectively. This eventually impairs the levels of malonyl-CoA, a substrate for fatty acid synthesis. This phosphorylation also enhances the activity of the mitochondrial oxidative enzyme carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 (CPT1) (101), the rate-limiting enzyme for beta oxidation. This enzyme facilitates the entry of fatty acids into the mitochondrial inner membrane due to decreased inhibition by malonyl-CoA. GCGR−/− mice exhibit impaired fatty acid oxidation with no increase in beta oxidation genes such as CPT 1/2 or CACT compared to wild-type mice, suggesting a role for glucagon in increasing the expression of these beta oxidation-promoting genes (102). Through CPT1, mitochondrial beta oxidation is elevated, which results in more acyl-CoA molecules being generated as byproducts. This increase in acyl-CoA allows mitochondrial ketogenesis rates to rise. Through indirect suppression of the effects of the liver X receptor alpha (LXR-α) (103), PKA also leads to decreased lipogenesis through subsequent inhibition of the Sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP)-1c, a transcription factor promoting the expression of lipogenesis-stimulating genes. While the exact mechanism remains elusive, AMPK also indirectly leads to a decrease in Diglyceride acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1) (103, 104), an enzyme catalyzing the last step of the lipogenesis pathway, thus showing that glucagon hinders lipid production by targeting multiple sites of the lipogenesis pathway. AMPK also suppresses the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) Complex-1 pathway (105) through phosphorylation of the Raptor protein and activating the Tuberous Sclerosis Complex 2 (TSC2) through multiple phosphorylation sites, including Ser1387 (106). This inhibition leads to multiple metabolic events, such as decreased lipid and protein synthesis (thus affecting protein metabolism as well) and ketogenesis. However, the effects of glucagon on ketogenesis remain controversial, as deletion of the glucagon receptor in mice does not correlate with an impairment in ketogenesis. Few studies (107, 108) even propose the hypothesis that glucagon's effects on ketogenesis are a mere consequence of impaired insulin signaling.

Amino acid metabolism

Glucagon also affects amino acid metabolism by promoting the uptake and metabolism of amino acids in livers of rats, particularly the amino acids lysine, arginine, glycine, alanine, and glutamine (117). Administration of glucagon to human patients resulted in decreased plasma amino acid levels (122). Through PKA/CREB activation, glucagon enhances the expression of amino acid transporters (including SLC7a2, SLC25a15, and SLC38a5) for their transport into hepatocytes and clears them from circulation (64). These amino acids can then be deaminated or transaminated to form alpha-keto acids, which can either enter the Krebs' cycle directly or indirectly via glycolytic intermediates such as pyruvate and acyl-CoA (118, 119). During prolonged fasting states, together with glycogen and lipids, amino acids can also be used as an energy source, where amino acid catabolism gives rise to increased pyruvate and oxaloacetate levels, which shift hepatic metabolism toward gluconeogenesis, and the increased acyl-CoA levels promote ketogenesis (120). Amino acid catabolism involves the formation of ammonia as a byproduct, which is toxic to cells (121). Urea cycle enzyme N-acetyl glutamate synthetase (NAGS) catalyzes the conversion of glutamate (a byproduct of amino acid catabolism) to N-acetyl glutamate (123), which stimulates enzyme carbamoyl-phosphate synthase (CPS1). This converts ammonia into carbamoyl-phosphate, which can enter the urea cycle and be transported to the kidneys safely for excretion (124).

Non-metabolic functions

PKA is also responsible for some of the non-metabolic functions of glucagon. It has been shown that glucagon's effect of halting viral replication (34) in hepatocytes is reproducible with cAMP-promoting agents such as forskolin and dibutyryl-cyclic AMP, and since cAMP is a second messenger leading to the activation of PKA, the cAMP-dependent PKA pathway is likely responsible for this effect.

cAMP/Epac pathway

Aside from activating PKA, glucagon-stimulated cAMP production also activates the cAMP-gated exchange factors, specifically the Rap1 guanine nucleotide exchange factor (Epac) (125). cAMP directly activates Epac through its guanine nucleotide exchange factor domain to activate the small GTPase Rap1. Mammals possess two isoforms of Epac: Epac1 & Epac2 (126). In the liver, Epac2 is the dominant isoform with minimal Epac1 expression (125), and the Epac2-Rap1 signaling pathway regulates the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (127), inhibits IL-6 signaling (128), reduces apoptosis (129) and promotes the secretion of the fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) (130) which has multiple effects on metabolism and whole-body energy expenditure. A recent study (131) using the Epac-specific inhibitor ESI-05 showed that the inhibitor could not fully reverse glucagon's inhibition of glycogenesis. Instead, it led to a partial reduction of glucagon-induced glucokinase translocation and an upregulation of gluconeogenic gene levels (131). These findings further highlight that glucagon mediates its effect by affecting multiple signaling pathways, and targeting downstream pathways might not be enough to fully counteract glucagon's action on metabolism.

A patch-clamped study (132) in rat liver cells revealed a crucial role of Epac in glucagon-induced calcium (Ca2+) and chloride (Cl−) channel activation. Increases in intracellular Ca2+ concentrations are believed to play a critical role in glucagon-mediated metabolic regulation. The increased Ca2+ concentration activates glycogen phosphorylase (PYGL), which catalyzes glycogen breakdown and also activates protein kinases ERK1/2 (132). These then lead to CREB phosphorylation and activation of its downstream signaling pathways (133). Additionally, increased Ca2+ concentration activates the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), which regulates the transcriptional activity of the Forkhead box protein O1 (FoxO1) through phosphorylation of the Ser276 site, which also regulates gluconeogenesis (134). Several studies (124, 135) have reported that CaMKII is essential for glucagon-stimulated hepatic glucose production, with glucagon being unable to increase hepatic glucose production in CaMKII-knockout mice. Moreover, increases in Ca2+ concentrations have been reported to affect lipid metabolism by promoting lipogenesis gene expression and assembly of very-low-density lipoproteins (136).

These findings highlight the multifaceted and pathway-dependent roles of calcium signaling in glucagon-mediated metabolic regulation. However, much of the current literature and research on Epac's role in glucagon signaling focuses on pharmacological agonists and inhibitors. Recently, one paper showed that, upon hepatic Epac knockdown, mice did not exhibit any changes in plasma amino acids or glucagon levels and α-cell mass, suggesting that Epac has no role in that pathway. Further research in this area, using similar approaches (137), is essential to clarify Epac's function in glucagon signaling, as pharmacological agents have their own drawbacks (eg, due to off-target effects or reduced target affinity), which can result in unreliable outcomes compared to genetic functional studies.

cAMP-independent pathway

Upon the action of glucagon on the glucagon receptor, the Gq protein of the GPCR activates the ion channel phospholipase C-inositol triphosphate channel (PLC), independent of cAMP (85). PLC then acts on its target plasma phospholipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, a reaction that yields diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol 14,5-triphosphate (IP3) as end-products. This increase in DAG stimulates certain isoforms of protein kinase C (PKC) (138). However, the effect of this stimulation remains poorly investigated, with one study (139) showing that PKC-knockdown in rat livers with NAFLD led to decreased insulin resistance and another (140) showing that full-body PKCɛ knockout, but not liver-specific knockout, results in mice protected from glucose intolerance and insulin resistance. Currently, no study has investigated the hepatic PKC on glucagon-mediated effects, marking a potential for future studies and potential breakthroughs.

IP3 synthesis activates the endoplasmic reticulum receptor IP3R, which stimulates the efflux of calcium (Ca2+) ions (136) from the ER to the cytosol. Additionally, PKA leads to the phosphorylation of IP3R at Ser393, further increasing hepatocytes' Ca2+ concentrations, mediating downstream glucagon signaling as described previously. Studies (141) have indicated that IP3R knockdown reduces glucose synthesis in mice following glucagon treatment. Insulin also leads to inhibitory phosphorylation of IP3R via Akt, resulting in a decrease in glucose production by glucagon, and this glucose drop can be restored if the phosphorylation is blocked, further emphasizing the important role of IP3R in mediating glucagon's hyperglycemic actions (142). IP3R also promotes fatty acid oxidation by enhancing the activity of calcium-dependent alpha-ketoglutarate (143).

Glucagon and other protein post-translational modifications

While phosphorylation remains a major post-translational modification (PTM) through which glucagon mediates its effects, the hormone can trigger other PTMs as part of its signal transduction. Glucagon plays a pivotal role in chromatin remodeling (144, 145) through its impact on acetylation mechanisms. By causing the dephosphorylation of CRTC2 to allow its nuclear translocation, where it binds to CREB, glucagon can facilitate the recruitment of histone acetyltransferase KAT2B and chromatin regulators like WDR5. KAT2B mediates the acetylation of H3K9 (146), which leads to a less condensed chromatin structure that facilitates the binding of CREB and CRTC2 to upregulate gluconeogenic genes. Moreover, glucagon has been reported to regulate hepatic class IIa histone deacetylases (HDACs) (147) by promoting their dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation through LBK1's inhibition of salt-inducible kinases (148). These HDACs interact with gluconeogenic gene promoters such as G6P and Pck1 to enhance FoxO and CREB interactions to increase gluconeogenesis (147).

Under fasting conditions, the transcription factor Foxa2 (149) plays a critical role in lipid metabolism and ketone body generation. Foxa2 acylation at Lys259, regulated by HDACs I-III and co-regulated by transcription factors p300 and SIRT1 (150), is induced by both fasting and glucagon stimulation. Mutational studies (151) demonstrated that this acetylation enhances Foxa2's activity and promotes fatty acid oxidation and ketogenesis, with adenoviral-transduced expression of the acetylation-mimicking mutation K259Q significantly upregulating fatty acid oxidation and ketogenesis-promoting genes.

Apart from acetylation and phosphorylation, glucagon also influences methylation. Arginine methylation (152), mediated by PRMT5, is critical in regulating gluconeogenesis. Glucagon-stimulated PRMT5 also mediates histone modifications such as the dimethylation of arginine at position 2 of histone H3 (H3R2me2), which further enhances the expression of gluconeogenic genes through an open chromatin configuration facilitating transcription. Furthermore, glucagon impacts ubiquitination and causes an increase in total protein ubiquitination and at specific peptide regions, like increased K48-linked ubiquitination, which leads to reduced K63-linked ubiquitination (153). SENP2, a small ubiquitin-like modifier-specific protease, was shown to be crucial in glucagon's regulatory role, where its loss led to reduced glucose production and its overexpression exacerbated high-fat diet-induced hyperglycemia (154). Restoring SENP2 levels to normal in SENP2-KO mice restores glucagon-induced gluconeogenesis to normal rates (154).

Collectively, these findings emphasize glucagon's critical role in orchestrating chromatin remodeling and transcriptional regulation through diverse mechanisms, including acetylation, deacetylation, phosphorylation, and methylation, thereby integrating metabolic pathways, such as gluconeogenesis, lipid oxidation, and ketogenesis to maintain cellular energy supplies during fasting.

Glucagon signaling and intracellular trafficking: spatial control of hepatic metabolism

Glucagon orchestrates hepatic metabolism by activating signaling cascades that control gluconeogenesis, glycogen breakdown, lipid mobilization, and amino acid metabolism. While much of the focus has traditionally been on proximal cAMP–PKA signaling, spatial organization of these signals within hepatocytes could also critical for determining which metabolic pathways are engaged. Intracellular vesicle trafficking provides this spatial layer by regulating receptor localization, organizing signaling complexes, and controlling access of metabolic effectors to substrates. Understanding intracellular trafficking therefore provides mechanistic insight into how glucagon signaling is translated into specific metabolic outcomes.

There is emerging evidence from genetic and phosphoproteomic studies that intracellular trafficking influences glucagon signaling both at the level of receptor localization and in shaping downstream metabolic outcomes (155, 156). Vps37a, a key endosomal sorting protein, plays a pivotal role in the intracellular trafficking of the GCGR receptor to ensure prompt signal termination by facilitating the receptor's transport to lysosomes for degradation (156). Loss of Vps37a results in endosomal GCGR accumulation and overactivation of the glucagon signaling pathways. Consistent with impaired signal termination, Vps3a-deficient mice display a chronic increase in hepatic glucose output, resulting in hyperglycemia, decreased liver glycogen, and upregulated gluconeogenic genes, illustrating how dysregulated receptor trafficking directly translates into excessive gluconeogenic drive. Importantly, restoring GCGR to the plasma membrane reverses these effects, confirming that the metabolic dysregulation seen in the Vps3a-KO mice could be explained by impaired GCGR translocation. This accentuates Vsp37a's role in maintaining GCGR localization and regulating glucagon signaling (156). These findings demonstrate that vesicular trafficking of GCGR is a key determinant of hepatic glucose production by constraining glucagon signaling strength and duration.

While intracellular trafficking is known to influence glucagon signaling at the proximal level, recent studies, using a rat liver perfusion system, examined the effects of glucagon treatment on liver phosphopeptide profiles and identified the vesicle trafficking protein SEC22B as playing a role beyond proximal receptor interactions (155). SEC22B was identified as a critical intracellular regulator of glucagon signaling to downstream metabolic pathways, primarily through its phosphorylation site Ser137, with mutational studies highlighting the importance of this phosphorylation site in modulating glycogen, lipid, and amino acid metabolism (155). Notably, hepatocyte-specific deletion of SEC22B does not impair proximal glucagon signaling pathway, as demonstrated by similar phosphorylation responses of PKA target proteins in SEC22B-deficient mice and wild-type mice following glucagon injections. However, the SEC22B silencing leads to disrupted lipid and amino acid metabolism, characterized by hepatic triglyceride and cholesterol accumulation, reduced hepatic glycogen storage, and altered circulating amino acid levels. Mechanistic investigations suggest that this phosphorylation modulates protein-to-protein interactions involved in autophagy, protein metabolism, and lipid pathways, thereby influencing metabolism and glucagon's actions. Collectively, these findings position SEC22B-Ser137 as a central hepatic signaling hub for the diverse metabolic actions of glucagon (155).

Collectively, these examples illustrate that intracellular trafficking is not only involved in receptor turnover but also a regulator that links glucagon receptor dynamics to the execution of diverse metabolic programs. Deciphering how trafficking proteins affect spatial and temporal organization of signaling to determine hepatocyte metabolic responses, remains poorly understood, and should be a focus of future research.

Conclusion

Glucagon has multi-faceted cell-signaling mechanisms through which it can mediate its pleiotropic effects (see Fig. 1), even when only the liver, its major target organ, is being considered. As exemplified in this review, the signaling processes involved in glucagon's action on glucose and glycogen metabolism are presently the most well-described. In contrast, the cellular signaling processes of how glucagon can affect amino acid and lipid metabolism, as well as non-metabolic functions, remain less well understood.

With glucagon gaining traction as a promising pharmacotherapeutic target, understanding and bridging the knowledge gaps is key to targeting the pathways responsible for the desired effects while repressing or avoiding the pathways that are responsible for the unwanted effects of glucagon. This knowledge will also help gain better insight into poorly defined or controversial roles of glucagon action, such as its effects on cancer development or hepatoprotection, amongst others. As seen in Fig. 1, numerous pathways have been uncovered through which glucagon mediates its various functions. This could be considered to be only “the tip of the iceberg” as there is still a multitude of questions left unanswered. In particular, the signaling pathways conveying the non-metabolic effects of glucagon, for example, remain a mystery. Using novel technologies such as high-throughput cell-based screening and phosphoproteomics (155), novel proteins have been linked to glucagon signaling and can help direct future research, but the relationship between most of these proteins and the hormone is still largely unexplored. Moreover, while the effect of glucagon on protein phosphorylation has been a major focus of research, there exist multiple other protein post-translational modifications (157), such as palmitoylation and malonylation, to name a few, where their relation to glucagon has either been poorly studied or not studied at all. With the emergence of a new era of “omics analyses (genomic, metabolomic, etc.) and high-throughput screening and AI prediction tools available, more avenues are now available to dig even deeper and further our knowledge regarding the signaling pathways of glucagon in the liver and beyond.
