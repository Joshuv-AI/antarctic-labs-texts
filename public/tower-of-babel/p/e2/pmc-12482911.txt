Management of low back pain among Medicaid beneficiaries: modalities, patterns, and perspectives across states
Authors: Kayla N Tormohlen, Christie Lee Luo, Anam Ahsan, Brian C Coleman, Patience M Dow, William C Becker, Tamara Haegerich, Emma E McGinty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLow back pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide and Medicaid beneficiaries are disproportionally impacted. No studies have comprehensively examined patterns of treatment among Medicaid beneficiaries with low back pain.MethodsWe quantitatively described modalities of treatment and low-value care received following a low back pain diagnosis among Medicaid beneficiaries across the United States. We then qualitatively explored factors that influence treatment patterns by interviewing chronic pain experts.ResultsOn average, 39.6% of patients received a prescription opioid in the 12 months following diagnosis and 41.2% received conservative therapies—noninvasive, nonpharmacological methods including physical, manual, or psychological therapies. Prescription nonopioid analgesic medications were the most common modality received first (57.8%) and across the 12 months following diagnosis (74.1%). On average, 8.9% of patients received high-dose, long-term opioid therapy (>120 morphine milligram equivalents/d for ≥90 days) and 31.7% received early imaging; both indicators for low-value care. Chronic pain experts highlighted challenges related to Medicaid coverage for conservative therapies, limited access to pain specialists, and social and economic factors influencing treatment access and utilization.ConclusionBarriers, including Medicaid coverage limitations, provider access challenges, and economic factors, likely impact patterns of low back pain treatment among individuals enrolled in Medicaid.

Introduction

Low back pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide.1 Nearly 10% of the world's population experiences low back pain, and this could increase to 36% by 2050.2,3 Most adults will experience at least 1 episode of low back pain in their lifetime, and in the United States, 39% of adults report current acute or chronic back pain.4 Chronic low back pain is associated with reduced quality of life, limitations on life and work activities, as well as a higher likelihood of other co-occurring medical conditions, including high blood pressure and depression.5,6 Additionally, the societal and economic impacts of low back pain are substantial, with billions of dollars spent on associated health care costs and approximately 265 million workdays reported to be lost each year due to chronic low back pain among persons in the United States.3

Treatment guidelines for low back pain include a multifaceted approach with emphasis on conservative therapies—noninvasive, nonpharmacological methods that use a more holistic approach to reducing pain and restoring function—including physical (eg, exercise and physical therapy), manual, and psychological therapies,7 and less emphasis on surgical interventions and high-risk pharmacological treatments, including prescription opioid medications, as first-line interventions.8 Guidelines suggest that the use of early imaging (within the initial 6 weeks), without the presence of specific serious conditions, so-called “red flag” conditions,9,10 is generally not warranted, as early imaging is not associated with improved outcomes but is associated with increased health care costs.11 Additionally, the use of opioid therapy is discouraged,8 particularly the use of high-dose and long-duration opioid treatments due to the limited evidence of beneficial effects and increased risk of adverse health outcomes, such as opioid use disorder and overdose.8,12-15 Despite these consistent recommendations, low-value care for low back pain, including inappropriate use of imaging and opioid pharmacologic therapy, has historically been common.16

While recent reports highlight a potential reduction in low-value care among commercially insured patients with low back pain,17 no studies, to our knowledge, have comprehensively examined patterns of treatment among Medicaid beneficiaries with low back pain, across the 50 US states and the District of Columbia (DC). The prevalence of chronic pain, including chronic low back pain, is higher among adults insured by Medicaid compared with adults with commercial insurance.18 Previous work identified differences in state Medicaid policies in regards to coverage and utilization management strategies for both pharmacological and nonpharmacological treatments used for low back pain, potentially leading to differences in treatment patterns across Medicaid beneficiaries in the United States.19,20 Medicaid enrollees have been documented to experience opioid prescribing incongruent with clinical guidelines for back pain specifically, including receipt of opioids in a higher percentage of visits for acute and chronic back pain, compared with persons enrolled in commercial insurance.21 Additionally, compared with patients enrolled in commercial insurance, patients enrolled in Medicaid are less likely to receive conservative therapies for chronic pain treatment.22

This study first aimed to quantitatively describe patterns of treatment, including overall receipt, first treatment received, and low-value care among Medicaid beneficiaries with low back pain in the United States. We then qualitatively explored chronic pain experts’ perceptions of factors that influence treatment patterns among persons with low back pain insured by Medicaid.

Data and methods

Quantitative analysis

Data

Data for the quantitative component of this study included Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System (T-MSIS) analytic files (TAF). The TAF include claims data from 50 US states and DC, with enrollment and demographic information, as well as service use and prescription drug claims for Medicaid beneficiaries in the United States. We used medical and pharmacy claims data from January 1, 2018, through December 31, 2019. Based on quality indicators from DQ Atlas,23 3 states (Maryland, Texas, Utah) were excluded from analyses including procedure measures described below (see Supplement S1 for inclusion and exclusion criteria).

We identified patients enrolled in Medicaid in each month for at least 6 months prior to and 12 months following a low back pain index diagnosis. A low back pain index diagnosis was defined as an inpatient or outpatient claim with a low back pain International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis code (Supplement S2), without any claims with a low back pain diagnosis in the prior 6 months. Patients were excluded if they had claims in more than 1 state within the 12 months following their index claim because we were interested in state-specific treatment patterns or if they were dually eligible for Medicaid and Medicare because linked Medicare claims were unavailable.

Measures

For all patients with a low back pain diagnosis, we described the proportion receiving any low back pain treatment and the volume of treatment modalities received within 12 months of the low back pain index diagnosis, as well as the first modality received following the index diagnosis. Low back pain treatment measures included prescription opioid medications, prescription nonopioid analgesic medications, and procedures.24 Three procedure categories were operationally defined and used: (1) conservative therapies (noninvasive, nonpharmacological methods that use a more holistic approach to reducing pain and restoring function; eg, physical, manual, and psychological therapies), (2) interventional procedures (minimally invasive and targeted interventions; eg, intramuscular injections and nerve blocks), and (3) surgical interventions (surgical procedures; eg, spinal fusion and laminectomy) (see Supplement S2 for a full list of procedures and codes). For opioid medications, we excluded opioid-agonist medications primarily used to treat opioid use disorder as opposed to pain (Bunavail, buprenorphine-naloxone, probuphine, suboxone, Subutex, Zubsolv). Among beneficiaries who received a prescription opioid, we described the dose and duration of the prescription opioid medications received by calculating the average morphine milligram equivalents (MME) per day and days’ supply in the 12 months following the low back pain index, adjusting for overlapping prescriptions. For the MME outcome, patients with an average MME per day greater than 1000 were excluded given the possibility that data entry errors contributed to these outliers. Last, we examined possible indicators for low-value care. Receipt of high-dose, long-duration prescription opioids was defined as the proportion of individuals who received prescription opioid medications with a daily dosage greater than 120 MME for 90 or more consecutive days.12 Early imaging was defined as the proportion of patients with an imaging claim within the first 6 weeks of their index claim without any red flag conditions (ie, specific serious conditions that might warrant early imaging; see Supplement S2) within 14 days of the imaging claim.9-11 The identification of red flag conditions in proximity to an imaging claim would therefore exclude such instances from being categorized as low-value care, as imaging could be necessary to evaluate these more serious underlying issues. All outcomes were calculated at the state level to understand across-state variation and averaged across states to examine patterns overall. State-specific results are shown in Supplement S5.

Qualitative interviews

Participant recruitment

We conducted semi-structured qualitative interviews with chronic pain experts across the United States to understand key factors influencing chronic pain treatment utilization among Medicaid beneficiaries. Our sample frame included experts from 6 states (Kansas, Louisiana, Maine, Nevada, North Carolina, South Carolina) with variation in Medicaid expansion status and prevalence of opioid treatment for low back pain. Among states that adopted Medicaid expansion, based on preliminary analyses, we selected those with the highest (Louisiana and Nevada) and lowest (Maine and North Carolina) proportion of Medicaid beneficiaries with low back pain who received a prescription opioid medication. We used the same process for states that have not expanded Medicaid with the highest (Kansas) and lowest (South Carolina) proportion of patients receiving a prescription opioid (Supplement S3). In addition to state experts, we recruited national experts with knowledge in chronic pain treatment and policy. Recruitment focused on individuals with professional expertise in chronic pain treatment and policies and guidelines impacting chronic pain treatment, including state Medicaid experts, health systems leaders, and members of professional associations and boards and chronic pain advocacy groups. Our initial set of experts was identified based on public information indicating relevant expertise and experience and purposively recruited via email. We then solicited additional experts by asking experts to identify people they believed to be a good fit (snowball sampling). Our recruitment protocol involved an initial invitation email and 5 follow-up emails sent weekly. We contacted 73 experts, 32 of whom did not respond after completing our recruitment protocol and 28 who declined to participate.

Data collection

Experts were invited to participate in a 1-hour interview. After obtaining verbal consent, a single team member conducted interviews using a semi-structured interview guide. The interviewer and analyst (A.A.), a master's level research analyst, has extensive qualitative interview training and experience. The interview guide was iteratively developed based on the study's aim to understand experts’ perspectives of factors that influence chronic pain treatment, with a particular focus on how policies may impact chronic pain, including low back pain, and treatment access for patients insured by Medicaid. The guide included questions to obtain perspectives in the following domains: (1) policies that influence treatment delivery for chronic pain, overall and for different modalities; (2) most influential policy barriers to chronic pain treatment; and (3) policy considerations that influence chronic pain treatment for Medicaid beneficiaries. We indicated to experts that we defined policies broadly (eg, Medicaid policies, state department of health policies, state waiver authorities); this definition led to broader conversations about related factors that influence chronic pain treatment for Medicaid beneficiaries.

Interviews were conducted from July through November 2024 via Zoom and lasted an average of 50 (range: 37–58) minutes. Interviews were conducted in tandem with quantitative analyses. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed for analysis, with any personal identifying information redacted. No incentives or rewards were offered to study participants. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, the point at which no new themes or concepts emerged from subsequent interviews. Interviews were analyzed using summative content analysis to develop memos summarizing perceptions of important factors influencing chronic pain treatment among Medicaid beneficiaries. The first 3 memos were developed by 2 study team members (K.N.T. and A.A.) and compared as part of an evaluative process to ensure impartiality and consistency in the development across interviews. Following this process, memos were created by a single study team member (A.A.) and discussed among the study team to identify key themes. Interview transcripts were then reviewed to refine themes and identify illustrative quotes.25-27 This research was reviewed and approved by the Weill Cornell Medicine Institutional Review Board.

Results

Quantitative findings

Our sample included 2 349 246 Medicaid enrollees across the 50 US states and DC with a low back pain diagnosis. The average age across Medicaid enrollees with low back pain in all states was 39.1 years old and 66.7% were female.

Receipt of low back pain treatment modalities

Low back pain treatment received within the first 12 months of a patient's index diagnosis varied across patients and states (Table 1, Figures 1 and 2, and Supplement S5). Overall, an average of 89.5% of patients received any treatment in the 12 months following their low back pain index diagnosis (95% CI, 87.7%–91.4%; range across states, 60.6% [Florida (FL)] to 96.9% [Minnesota (MN)]). On average across states, 39.6% of patients received a prescription opioid medication (95% CI, 37.5%–41.8%; range, 20.0% [North Carolina (NC)] to 55.2% [Utah (UT)]) and 74.1% of patients received a prescription nonopioid analgesic medication within the first year of their index diagnosis (95% CI, 71.3%–76.8%; range, 45.8% [FL] to 86.5% [Montana (MT)]) (Table 1). Across states, an average of 41.2% of patients with low back pain received conservative therapies (95% CI, 36.7%–45.7%; range, 8.4% [Oklahoma (OK)] to 72.3% [MN]), 11.5% received interventional procedures (95% CI, 10.9%–12.1%; range, 7.1% [Hawaii (HI)] to 15.4% [Colorado (CO)]), and 6.1% of patients received surgical interventions (95% CI, 5.5%–6.6%; range. 2.4% [Louisiana (LA)] to 10.8% [Alabama (AL)]) (Figure 1).

Volume of low back pain treatment modalities

Among those receiving at least 1 prescription opioid medication, patients received an average of 5.1 prescriptions (95% CI, 4.9–5.3; range, 3.2 [Tennessee (TN)] to 6.8 [Missouri (MO)]) in the first 12 months following their index diagnosis. The average MME per day was 35.1 (95% CI, 33.6–36.6; range, 26.1 [DC] to 46.6 [Wisconsin (WI)]) and the average days’ supply was 84.2 days (95% CI, 78.6–89.9; range, 46.5 [North Dakota (ND)] to 131.8 [Nevada (NV)]) (Table 1). Among patients who received at least 1 prescription nonopioid medication, patients received an average of 8.2 prescriptions (95% CI, 7.8–8.6; range, 4.6 [NC] to 12.4 [MO]) (Table 1). Among patients who received each type of procedure category, patients had an average of 10.0 conservative therapy visits (95% CI, 9.5–10.5; range, 7.0 [West Virgina (WV)] to 15.0 [NV]), an average of 1.8 interventional procedure visits (95% CI, 1.8–1.9; range, 1.7 [OK] to 2.2 [MO]) and an average of 1.6 surgical procedure visits (95% CI, 1.6–1.7; range, 1.2 [Oregon (OR)] to 2.1 [MO]) in the 12 months following low back pain index diagnosis (Figure 1).

First low back pain treatment modality received

An average of 57.8% received a nonopioid analgesic prescription medication first (95% CI, 54.3%–61.3%; range, 30.4% [South Dakota (SD)] to 76.5% [LA]), followed by 25.1% receiving a conservative therapy first (95% CI, 21.2%–29.1%; range, 53.6% [OK] to 57.3% [ND]). Twelve percent (12.1%) of patients received a prescription opioid medication prior to any other modality (95% CI, 11.0%–13.2%; range, 6.2% [ND] to 23.5% [OK]), 3.0% received an interventional procedure (95% CI, 2.4%–3.5%; range, 0.8% [Nebraska (NE)] to 8.9% [FL]), and 2.0% of patients received a surgical intervention first (95% CI, 1.6%–2.4%; range, 0.5% [HI] to 6.9% [AL]) (Figure 2).

Low-value care indicators

Of people who received opioid treatment, 8.9% of patients had at least 90 consecutive days with more than 120 MME per day (95% CI, 8.1%–9.8%; range, 2.2% [DC] to 16.5% [OK]). On average, 31.7% of patients received imaging, without a red flag condition, within the first 6 weeks of their index diagnosis (95% CI, 30.3%–33.1%; range, 22.1% [ND] to 43.4% [AL]).

Qualitative findings

We conducted interviews with 13 experts, 7 of whom were experts in the targeted states (Kansas, Louisiana, Maine, Nevada, North Carolina) and 6 were national experts. All experts were knowledgeable in chronic pain treatment and policy and represented a range of professional roles including Medicaid officials, health systems leaders, and members of professional associations or boards; 9 were clinicians.

Themes centered around factors that influence chronic pain, including low back pain, and treatment for persons insured by Medicaid. Three key themes emerged from the interviews: (1) coverage limits of chronic pain treatments, (2) provider access, and (3) economic and social barriers to treatment. Coverage limits for chronic pain treatments

Experts discussed limitations around Medicaid coverage for certain types of chronic pain treatment. Lack of coverage for, and utilization management of, conservative therapies was the most salient. While considered to be preferred treatments, as highlighted by experts, access to conservative therapies is limited in many states because of examples such as lack of Medicaid coverage for acupuncture and utilization management strategies for chiropractic care, including prior authorization and visit limits. Experts discussed that coverage limitations create large barriers for Medicaid beneficiaries because the out-of-pocket cost for these types of treatments are often cost-prohibitive. Experts also mentioned that utilization management strategies do not align with the chronicity of pain that patients experience, as a limited number of visits for physical therapy, for example, confines the possible beneficial effects for patients experiencing chronic pain or requires waivers for additional coverage of clinically effective care, as described in Table 2 and the following quote.

“I will tell you that, for chiropractic services, Medicaid has very limited coverage for patients. They often will require a PCP [primary care provider] referral, and that PCP referral does not always go for chiropractic care and then they will have, like I said, a very limited number. When we're talking about patients in chronic pain, we usually define chronicity at 6 months, but you know a patient that is in pain for 6 months for at least half the days is not going to get well in a short, acute course of care. They [Medicaid] will often only cover in a short, acute course of care, thereby making it unaffordable for a lot of Medicaid patients, especially to get regular care.” —Expert 4-1

Provider access

Experts mentioned that many pain specialty providers do not accept Medicaid, limiting access to treatment for individuals insured by Medicaid. This is particularly prominent for conservative therapies; as expressed in interviews, this could be influenced by the difficulty providers experience working with Medicaid programs in terms of coverage and reimbursement. Experts also mentioned lower Medicaid payment rates for pain specialists as compared with Medicare and commercial insurance plans, and that low provider payment rates could decrease the likelihood of accepting Medicaid patients, and, in turn, limit access to treatment and contribute to poor outcomes, as described in Table 2 and the following quote.

“It would be helpful to improve Medicaid reimbursement for pain specialists because access to them is quite limited, and so it leaves most of us in primary care without substantial access to pain specialists.” —Expert 5-2

Economic and social barriers to treatment

Experts mentioned that Medicaid beneficiaries often grapple with many challenges beyond their chronic pain conditions and emphasized the importance of addressing social determinants of health for improved health and well-being. Experts highlighted that access to chronic pain treatment, both starting and continuing care, can be hindered by many barriers, including mobility challenges due to pain, financial strains, access to technology, inadequate transportation, and social support networks, as described in Table 2 and the following quote.

“I think a lot of it is also what we call social determinants of health. A lot of it is going to depend on whether people can get time off from work and whether people can get adequate transportation to do things, and the community and social supports they have. There are just lots and lots of things that go into this that are bigger than policy, and that particularly affect the Medicaid population because of income limitations or disability.” —Expert 7-6

Discussion

Treatment guidelines encourage maximizing the use of nonpharmacologic and nonopioid pharmacologic therapies and considering opioid therapy only when the benefits are anticipated to outweigh the risks.8,15 Our study found that almost 40% of Medicaid beneficiaries received a prescription opioid medication in the 12 months following diagnosis; only approximately 4 in 10 patients received a conservative therapy, aligning with expert perspectives highlighting major utilization barriers to these types of treatments. Experts noted the continued use of opioid medications for pain management while emphasizing the limited access to nonpharmacologic, conservative treatments, which could contribute to continued opioid prescribing. Most patients received nonopioid prescription medications as their first treatment following their diagnosis. Recommendations also emphasize that the use of low-value care, including early imaging and high-dose, long-duration opioid treatment, should be limited and is not associated with improved health outcomes.11,12 In our sample, 1 in 3 patients received early imaging and approximately 10% received high-dose, long-term opioid therapy. We found low-value care remaining common in a sample of Medicaid patients, whereas a study examining low-value care in a commercially insured sample with low back pain found reductions in the use of opioids and imaging from 2011–2019.17 Results from qualitative interviews suggest that several factors influence low back pain treatment, as Medicaid policies, including coverage and utilization management strategies, influence patterns of care that patients receive, and payment policies impact provider networks and access to pain specialists. Medicaid patients may also face socioeconomic challenges that impact treatment access.

Chronic pain experts consistently highlighted Medicaid coverage–related barriers to chronic pain treatment for beneficiaries. Medicaid policies vary by state, and limited coverage and strict utilization management strategies may limit the use of certain treatment modalities, particularly conservative therapies for low back pain. As we observed, only an average 41.2% of patients received this treatment modality; this ranged from 8.4% to 72.3% across states, suggesting variation in Medicaid policies and treatment access. A prior study examined coverage and utilization management policies for conservative therapies for low back pain. Among the 15 state Medicaid plans examined, most plans covered some conservative therapies, including physical and occupational therapies, but treatments including acupuncture and psychological therapies were either not covered or the plan documents lacked information about coverage.19 Utilization management strategies, including prior authorization, condition requirements, visit limits, and referral requirements, were common for conservative therapies for low back pain; across Medicaid plans, 92% included visit limits for chiropractic care and 100% had visit limits for physical and occupational therapy.19

While most patients in our study received some form of treatment in the year following their low back pain diagnosis, this was predominantly driven by pharmacologic interventions rather than guideline-recommended conservative treatments. The factors identified by experts may influence an individual's ability to initiate and sustain needed low back pain treatment. In addition to Medicaid coverage policies, Medicaid beneficiaries face other barriers to care that could influence treatment patterns. Previous work suggests that persons enrolled in Medicaid may have more limited access to treatment due to pain specialty clinics not accepting Medicaid.28,29 Findings from our qualitative interviews regarding limited provider access align with these findings indicating that lack of acceptance of Medicaid patients is a significant problem, particularly for providers delivering conservative therapies. Lastly, Medicaid enrollees with low back pain face challenges beyond Medicaid coverage and provider availability, more so than individuals with other forms of coverage.30 More individuals insured by Medicaid do not have access to a vehicle, creating challenges related to transportation, and Medicaid enrollees may face higher financial stress, leading to challenges such as affording childcare.30

Limitations

The quantitative component of this study utilized medical and pharmacy claims TAF data, and only captures health care services delivered and covered by Medicaid. Our sample was limited to patients enrolled in Medicaid at least 6 months prior to and 12 months following a low back pain index diagnosis. This requirement helps ensure we can fully observe a person's health care encounters. However, this inclusion criterion may affect the generalizability and interpretation of our findings. The direction of the potential bias is not straightforward. On one hand, individuals with gaps in coverage may have poorer health due to limited access to care.31,32 As a result, our sample may disproportionately include individuals with more stable health care access and potentially better health outcomes, leading to an underestimation of treatment utilization. On the other hand, because loss of coverage reduces access to care,33 excluding individuals without continuous enrollment could lead to an overestimation of treatment utilization. Due to this requirement, our results cannot be generalized to other groups, such as those experiencing coverage gaps or those who are uninsured. Patients may have received services not covered by Medicaid (ie, paid out-of-pocket); this may be particularly relevant for some conservative therapies such as acupuncture and massage. Additionally, not all symptoms of low back pain conditions (and red flag conditions) are regularly coded in submitted claims, therefore leading to possible underestimates of these diagnoses. We could not assess pain severity and clinical appropriateness or the quality of treatment services captured in our study. Additionally, while we developed our measures to identify treatment for low back pain, it is possible that a patient received a treatment for another co-occurring condition. Qualitative interviews were conducted with a convenience sample of chronic pain experts and may have been subject to response bias due to self-selection of those willing to participate. To capture a range of perspectives, our sample included experts with differing affiliations, including those from state agencies, health systems, academic institutions, and professional associations or boards. Notably absent from our selection of experts were patients who may have additional or contradictory perspectives to those identified by policy and clinician stakeholders. Additionally, to enhance the reproducibility and rigor of our qualitative methods and avoid researchers’ biases in results interpretation, we used a common interview guide and standard coding process across all interviews.

Conclusion

More than 50% of Medicaid beneficiaries did not receive conservative therapies, which are recommended as first-line treatments for low back pain. Additionally, low-value care was relatively common, with approximately 30% of patients receiving early imaging and approximately 10% receiving high-dose, long-term opioid therapy. Barriers, including Medicaid coverage limitations, provider access challenges, and social and economic factors, likely impact access to this care. Expansion of coverage for conservative therapies and increased reimbursement rates for providers who treat pain could help enhance provider networks and increase access to needed treatment.
