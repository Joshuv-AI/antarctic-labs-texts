Epidemiology of Progressive Supranuclear Palsy in Navarre, Spain: A Population‐Based Study
Authors: P. Arrondo Gómez, E. Vicente, G. Martí‐Andrés, J. Sánchez Ruiz de Gordoa, I. Gastón, P. Clavero, R. Valentí, J. Delfrade, M. E. Erro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEpidemiological studies carried out on progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) provide heterogeneous data. Our aim has been to analyze incidence, point prevalence, survival, and time to diagnosis of PSP in Navarre, a northern Spanish region.MethodsThis is a population‐based observational retrospective study from 2010 to 2022. Data from various sources of health information were reviewed to identify all potential diagnoses of PSP, validated through medical records reviews. Patients were included if they fulfilled the 2017 Movement Disorder Society (MDS) criteria and were classified into the different PSP phenotypes.ResultsA total of 226 prevalent PSP cases were identified. Age‐standardized point prevalence rate at January 1, 2023, was 7.52/100,000 inhabitants, and age‐standardized annual incidence rate (IR) for the period was 2.41/100,000 person‐years. PSP‐Richardson syndrome (RS) phenotype showed significantly higher prevalence and incidence rates compared to PSP non‐RS phenotypes. Joinpoint regression for overall incidence showed a significant increase until 2018 and thereafter a significant steep decline. Joinpoint regression for prevalence showed a statistically significant annual decline from 2018 onwards. Mortality rate experienced a peak in 2020. Median time to diagnosis was 37 months, and the median survival from clinical symptom onset was 89 months. PSP‐RS was associated with reduced time to diagnosis and reduced survival compared to the PSP non‐RS phenotypes.ConclusionsThis study represents the first population‐based epidemiological study of PSP in Spain and the first with application of the new MDS diagnostic criteria in Europe, providing detailed incidence, prevalence, and mortality data that are in accordance with other European studies.

Introduction

Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) is a heterogeneous and progressive adult‐onset neurodegenerative disorder that is characterized pathologically by the accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein within neurons (neurofibrillary tangles) and glial cells (tufted astrocytes and coiled bodies) [1].

It is the most common form of primary tauopathy, and the diagnosis is based on clinical criteria reformulated by the Movement Disorders Society (MDS) in 2017 [2]. Besides Richardson syndrome (PSP‐RS), the MDS‐PSP criteria have defined other various PSP phenotypes based on the different combinations of four clinical domains: Ocular motor, postural instability, akinesia, and cognitive dysfunction. PSP‐RS is the most frequent clinical phenotype, representing about 50% of PSP cases [3]. The rest of the phenotypes are PSP‐predominant postural instability (PSP‐PI); PSP‐predominant ocular motor dysfunction (PSP‐OM); PSP‐predominant parkinsonism (PSP‐P); PSP‐progressive gait freezing (PSP‐PGF); PSP‐predominant corticobasal syndrome (PSP‐CBS); PSP‐predominant frontal presentation (PSP‐F); and PSP‐predominant speech/language disorder (PSP‐SL) [2]. There are differences in survival among the different phenotypes, being lower in PSP‐RS, which is around 5–8 years, compared to the other phenotypes [4]. On the contrary, the time to diagnosis is longer in non‐RS phenotypes, and it may be as long as three years [5].

There are several worldwide epidemiological studies on PSP, mostly conducted in Europe and the US, but few studies have investigated the epidemiology of PSP in Spain. A recent meta‐analysis reported that prevalence for PSP ranged from 1.00 (0.9–1.1) to 18 (8–28) per 100,000 inhabitants, while incidence rates (IR) for PSP ranged from 0.16 (0.07–0.39) to 2.6 per 100,000 person‐years [6]. The age of onset has been generally estimated at the late 60s, without significant gender differences [7].

Based on the highly heterogeneous findings on the epidemiology of PSP reported by studies, our aim has been to perform an epidemiological study of PSP in Navarre (a northern Spanish region) for 13 years (2010–2022) and to analyze incidence, prevalence, survival, mortality, and time to diagnosis in our region, applying the most recent PSP diagnostic criteria.

Material and Methods

Study Population

This is a thirteen‐year population‐based observational retrospective study from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2022, which included all residents in Navarre, a Spanish region with 672,155 (52.52% women) inhabitants on January 1, 2023, comprising 1.4% of the Spanish population [8]. Navarre has a single large public tertiary hospital (Navarre University Hospital ‐HUN‐), which is the referral hospital for the entire region. Neurologist ratio per inhabitant in Navarre is 4.65/100,000 inhabitants [9]. Since 2010, the HUN has provided movement disorder consultations attended by specialized neurologists.

The Spanish National Health System (S‐NHS) is based on the principles of universality, free access, equity, and fairness of financing, and is mainly funded by taxes [10]. Over 98% of Navarre's citizens have an individual health card with a unique 8‐digit personal identification code (called CIPNA), which allows them to have access to the public health system. It contains information on birth date, sex, and other socio‐demographic conditions, and enables linking across databases through a unique identifier.

Data Sources

The S‐NHS provides an infrastructure for recording community‐wide medical information and medical diagnoses, and other procedures are coded using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). A population‐based Rare Disease Registry (RERNA) [11, 12] was implemented in Navarre in 2013, with the aim of having the most complete possible census of patients diagnosed with rare diseases in the region. This registry collects cases from all available Health Information Systems (HIS), in addition to direct medical reporting, to record both retrospectively and prospectively, prevalent cases from 2000. The HIS used by RERNA to collect PSP cases were: The Minimum Basic Data Set at Hospital Discharge (from the tertiary hospital–HUN– and two regional public hospitals), the Temporary Work Disability Registry, and the Mortality Registry [13, 14, 15]. In addition, the Electronic Clinical Records from all Neurology hospital Departments (including inpatients, outpatients, and two private hospitals) and from the Navarre Biobank were explored and reviewed.

The diagnostic codes from the International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10) used to identify cases from RERNA were: With the 9th Revision (ICD‐9) (until 2015) 333.0 (“other degenerative diseases of the basal ganglia”) and potentially relevant ICD‐10 codes from 2016 were G23.1 (Progressive supranuclear ophthalmoplegia [Steele‐Richardson‐Olszewski]) and G23.8 (“other specified degenerative diseases of basal ganglia”).

The procedures carried out were in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2000, and the study was approved by the Navarre Ethical Committee of Clinical Research.

Ascertainment and Data Collection

We identified potential PSP cases in a first screening phase that required validation by clinical confirmation in a second phase. In the first phase, the information from the various data sources was reviewed by two data managers, members of the research group (PA and EV), to identify all potential diagnoses of PSP during the study period. Databases were cross‐checked to identify duplicate patients included in more than one data source. In the second phase, case validation was performed by senior neurologists (GMA, JRG, and MEE), experts in movement disorders who reviewed information from medical records. A detailed chart review was carried out, and pertinent information was extracted from each chart. We collected demographic data, date of clinical symptom onset, date of PSP diagnosis, and date of death if applicable. Time to diagnosis was defined as the duration from clinical symptom onset to diagnosis. Diagnosis date was defined as the date on which the PSP diagnosis was first recorded. Key clinical features for the application of the MDS‐PSP criteria were reviewed, as well as the first diagnosis established by a neurologist.

Patients were included if they fulfilled the MDS‐PSP criteria for the diagnosis of the three degrees of diagnostic certainty of PSP (probable, possible, and suggestive), and PSP was classified into the following phenotypes: PSP‐RS, PSP‐PI, PSP‐OM, PSP‐P, PSP‐PGF, PSP‐CBS, PSP‐F, and PSP‐SL.

Data Analysis

The cases selected to calculate the point prevalence at the end of the period had to be alive on January 1, 2023. The prevalence onset date was considered the date of diagnosis of each case. Cases residing in Navarre at any time from 2010 to 2022 were considered prevalent.

Point prevalence (global and sex‐specific) of PSP was calculated using the number of PSP individuals per 100,000 inhabitants, resident in Navarre, both at the end of the period (on January 1, 2023) and on January 1 of each year. Incidence and mortality rates were defined as the number of newly diagnosed PSP cases and the number of PSP‐registered deaths, respectively, per 100,000 inhabitants and per year. Mean annual incidence and mortality rates per 100,000 were analyzed for two periods: 2010–June 2016 and July 2016–2022. For age‐standardized rates, we used the direct method and the 2013 World Health Organization (WHO) European Standard Population (ESP) as reference [16], the 2010 U.S. (United States) Census [17], and the 2016 Spanish Population [8]. Overall trends were analyzed for the three epidemiological indicators.

Demographic features were summarized using descriptive statistics, such as median and ranges for continuous variables and absolute and relative frequencies for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared by sex or PSP phenotype using t‐test or U Mann–Whitney test, and Chi‐square test and Fisher's test for categorical variables. Median survival time and 95% CI were computed using Kaplan–Meier estimators, and comparisons between sexes were performed using the log‐rank test. Age‐sex‐specific prevalence and incidence rates were calculated with 95% confidence intervals. Joinpoint Regression models were used to analyze annual trends and to calculate Annual Percent Changes (APC) for prevalence and incidence. Statistical analyses were performed with Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 25, and a significance level of p < 0.05 was chosen.

Results

The search strategy from multiple sources, after removing duplicates and deaths before 2010, yielded 867 potential cases of atypical Parkinsonism that required diagnostic verification. After the review of digital medical records, we identified 237 potential PSP cases (Figure S1). We excluded 9 cases because not enough information was available. Finally, we analyzed data from 226 PSP prevalent cases (51.8% male), between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2022.

Prevalence and Incidence

The point prevalence on June 30, 2016, was 11.25 (95% CI: 8.55; 13.95) cases/100,000 inhabitants. On January 1, 2023, 51 patients were alive, giving a crude point prevalence of 7.68 (95% CI: 5.57; 9.79) cases/100,000 inhabitants (Table 1). The prevalence standardized to the WHO ESP was 7.52 (95% CI: 5.43; 9.60). The point prevalence ratio of PSP‐RS compared to the PSP‐non RS was 2.35 (1.30; 4.24), resulting in statistically significant differences. The mortality rate during the period 2010–2022 was 2.15 person‐years/100,000 (95% CI: 1.83; 2.48). For the period 2010–2016, the mortality rate was 1.46 (95% CI: 1.09; 1.84), and for the period 2016–2022 it was 2.53 (95% CI: 2.06; 3.00).

Clinical Features

Main clinical features are summarized in Table 4. Median age at symptom onset was 73 (range 72–88) years old, 72 years old for men (54–86) and 74 years old for women (52–88). There were no significant differences in sex, age at onset, or age at diagnosis between PSP‐RS and PSP‐non RS groups. The most frequent symptom leading to neurology consultation was gait disturbance and falls in the PSP‐RS group (45.1%, 69/153), and it was as frequent as Parkinsonism (27.4%, 20/73) in the PSP‐non RS group. The diagnosis certainty level of probable was achieved more frequently in PSP‐RS than in the PSP‐non RS group (p < 0.0001), while any case with the diagnosis certainty level of suggestive corresponded to PSP‐RS. Those cases with a suggestive certainty diagnosis level corresponded to: 3 PSP‐F, 2 PSP‐PI, 1 PSP‐P, and 1 PSP‐CBS. In 13.7% of patients (31/226), diagnosis was confirmed postmortem. In this group, the diagnostic success rate during life was 90.3% (28/31). The patients who were not diagnosed with PSP during their lifetime had PSP‐P (n = 2) and PSP‐F (n = 1) phenotypes. In 26.5% (60/226) of patients, the diagnosis of PSP was established at the first neurological visit, corresponding in 91.7% (55/60) to the PSP‐RS phenotype. In 16.3% (37/226) of the cases, the first diagnosis was atypical Parkinsonism. The most frequent misdiagnosis was Parkinson's disease in 23.4% (53/226) of the cases. Other first misdiagnoses were frontotemporal dementia (8.8%, 20/226), Alzheimer's disease (6.1%, 14/226), and other diagnoses (18.5%, 42/226) that included normotensive hydrocephalus, vascular Parkinsonism, and miscellaneous.

Time to Diagnosis and Survival

The median duration from onset of symptom to PSP diagnosis was 37 months (34; 40), without differences between sex or age at diagnosis (Table 5). The median of survival was 89 months (80.0; 97.7) (Table 5), without sex or age differences. PSP‐RS was associated with reduced time to diagnosis (p < 0.0001) and survival (p < 0.0001) compared with the rest of the phenotypes. Moreover, PSP‐P was associated with longer survival when compared with the other phenotype groups, excluding PSP‐RS (p = 0.005) (Table 5). Discussion

In this work, we provide a detailed description of the prevalence, incidence, and mortality of PSP in Navarre, stratified by sex, age, and clinical phenotypes, and represent the first population‐based epidemiological study of PSP in Spain.

Our country had participated in a study to analyze the prevalence, incidence, and clinical determinants of Parkinson's disease (PD) in Europe [18], which included five substudies, one of which was carried out in Navarre, but which did not provide specific data on PSP. A door‐to‐door study designed primarily to determine the prevalence of PD in Guipuzcoa was carried out, in which the prevalence of other types of Parkinsonism was 1.1/100,000 inhabitants, without specifying data on PSP [19]. The incidence of PSP in Girona (1.5/100,000 person‐years) has been published as part of a non‐population study aimed at describing the incidence of rare dementias from a registry (Registry of Dementia of Girona) [20]. Finally, a recent population‐based cross‐sectional study in Catalonia provides data on the epidemiology of PD and atypical parkinsonisms (incidence 46.4/106 person‐years and prevalence 209.4/106 inhabitants) [21].

In other countries, more epidemiological studies on the prevalence of PSP have been conducted than on its incidence, with different methodologies and data that vary significantly from one to another, as reflected in the results of two recent meta‐analyses [6, 22]. The standardized point prevalence of PSP in our region (7.52/100,000 inhabitants) is higher than the combined prevalence of 4.95/100,000 inhabitants obtained in one of the meta‐analyses, based on six studies carried out in Europe, and lower than the pooled prevalence of 12.85/100,000 inhabitants obtained in the same meta‐analysis from three studies conducted in Japan [6]. The prevalence rates for PSP in three recent studies carried out in the U.S. (1.89/100,000 inhabitants) [23], Scotland (2.46/100,000 inhabitants) [24] and Israel (5.3/100,000 inhabitants) [25] are lower than in Navarre, while it is close to the prevalence in a Swiss study (8.3/100,000 inhabitants) [26] that applied similar methodology to ours.

In the present study, the incidence of PSP was 2.41/100,000 person‐years, higher than that obtained in the studies published up to 2020 [6], including the Swiss one, where the incidence was 1.9/100,000 person‐years [26], and higher than the incidence in Israel of 1/100,000 person‐years [25]. The incidence of PSP in Navarre is slightly lower than that from Olmsted County, Minnesota (2.6/100,000 person‐years) [27], a population‐based study with a methodology similar to ours, and that also applies the most recent PSP diagnostic criteria [2]. The increased diagnostic sensitivity resulting from the application of the MDS‐PSP criteria [28] may explain the higher incidence rates.

In our study we found no differences in prevalence or incidence of PSP according to sex, in line with the majority of publications, except for a higher incidence in men in Olmsted County [23], but we have shown that the prevalence of PSP‐RS (5.27/100,000 inhabitants) is significantly higher than that of the other clinical phenotypes (2.24/100,000 inhabitants), and the same is true for incidence, which is 1.62/100,000 person‐years for PSP‐RS compared to 0.8/100,000 person‐years for the other clinical phenotypes. We found only one paper reporting prevalence data on different phenotypes, where PSP‐RS was also the most frequent, followed by PSP‐P and PSP‐PGF [29]. With respect to age, both the prevalence and incidence of PSP in Navarre are higher in the 70–79 age group, as is the case in most studies [23, 24, 26].

We attribute the decline in incidence from 2018 onwards to the COVID‐19 pandemic that prevented patients with neurodegenerative diseases from attending face‐to‐face consultations. There was a slight decrease in incidence in 2019 compared to 2018, but the decrease was primarily from 2020. This decline in incidence from 2020 onwards has also occurred in other neurodegenerative diseases [30]. The trend towards a decrease in prevalence beyond 2018 may be due to the incidence decrease and the increase in mortality rate due to the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020.

The median of survival in our study was 7.4 years, similar to previous studies [27, 31]. PSP‐RS phenotype has a significantly shorter survival (6.5 years), in comparison with the rest of the phenotypes (9.7 years), as it was already known, and PSP‐P has a significantly longer survival (12.7 years) than the rest of the non‐RS phenotypes. No differences were found in survival between men and women, in line with other studies [32].

Time to diagnosis in our series is in line with other studies [29, 33] or shorter [25]. We also found significant differences according to phenotypes (2.3 years in PSP‐RS and 5.5 years in the rest of the phenotypes). The longer time to diagnosis in PSP‐non RS phenotypes is already known, primarily in the PSP‐P phenotype [34]. Only in 26.5% of patients, PSP diagnosis was established at the first neurology visit; most of these had the PSP‐RS phenotype, which is explained by the fact that early falls and ophthalmoparesis have a high specificity for the diagnosis of the classic form of PSP. PD was one of the most common first misdiagnoses, as has been found by others [23], among a great variety of other diagnoses, which reflects the difficulty of establishing a precise and early diagnosis in the absence of biomarkers at early stages of the disease.

The mean age of onset of PSP (73 years) is similar to other reports [35, 36] but higher than PSP in South Asian origin patients [37] or in MSA [38]. The most frequent presenting symptoms were gait disturbance and falls in the PSP‐RS phenotype, while gait disturbance/falls and Parkinsonian syndrome were the most frequent in the PSP non‐RS group. Regarding the frequency of PSP non‐RS phenotypes, PSP‐P was the second most frequent form, although by age range, PSP‐F was the most frequent non‐RS phenotype in the 60–69 age group. The PSP‐CBS and PSP‐PGF phenotypes were less frequent than the rest, unlike what was found in patients of Asian origin, where both phenotypes have a higher representation [36]. We have not found that the PSP‐SL phenotype is more frequent in young patients, but in line with a previous study, we have found that the PSP‐PGF phenotype is more prevalent in groups over 70 years of age [39]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the representation of phenotypes other than PSP‐RS or PSP‐P is small and does not allow conclusions to be generalized.

The main limitations of our study are its retrospective nature and the difficulty of diagnostic certainty intrinsic to neurodegenerative diseases. Since this was a retrospective study, the medical history data were not collected in a systematized manner but as part of routine clinical practice. Regarding these points, expert neurologists in movement disorders performed a systematic review of the medical charts focusing on predefined clinical domains according to the MDS‐PSP criteria [2], and patients with poor‐quality medical reports were excluded. This has allowed us to improve diagnostic sensitivity and stratify by clinical phenotypes, although it should be noted that recent studies suggest that application of the MDS‐PSP criteria may not be useful for accurately differentiating the different clinical phenotypes and tend to over‐represent the classical form of the disease [40, 41], as is the case in our study. Moreover, we explain the small number of cases with the diagnostic certainty level of suggestive and the absence of unclassifiable cases in our series, by the application in most cases, of the MDS‐PSP criteria at the final stage of the disease [5, 41]. We are aware that a limitation lies in direct rate comparisons with other studies when the underlying populations have different characteristics.

Another limitation is that the percentage of pathological confirmation of PSP in our series is not high, although most patients had a diagnostic certainty category of probable, and the percentage of patients with correct diagnosis during their lifetime was over 90%, which is higher than in other series [42].

We consider that our study has many strengths: Recruitment of cases was performed by searching through extensive sources of health information, and covers a long period. The Spanish healthcare system involves a high degree of use of public resources. Despite this, the bases of private clinics have also been reviewed. Therefore, a few patients may have been missed. In addition, unlike other population‐based studies, all PSP phenotypes have been included in this one, which may lead to more accurate incidence and prevalence results.

In conclusion, our work represents the first population‐based epidemiological study on PSP in Spain and the first with application of the new MDS diagnostic criteria in Europe. It provides detailed incidence, prevalence, and mortality data for PSP that are in accordance with other European studies and may be useful for the appropriate management of health resources. Comparison with studies from other geographic areas may provide clues to the etiology of this disease.
