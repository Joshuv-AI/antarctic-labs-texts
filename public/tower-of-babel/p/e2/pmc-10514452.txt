Monitoring effects of hydrodynamic cavitation pretreatment of sodium oleate on the aggregation of fine diaspore particles through small-angle laser scattering
Authors: Weiguang Zhou, Xinran Liu, Yufeng Long, Guangyuan Xie, Yanfei Chen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Hydrodynamic cavitation (HC) enhanced fine particle aggregation could be largely due to the generation of tiny bubbles and their role in bridging particles. However, the lack of adequate characterizations of aggregates severally limits our further understanding of the associated aggregation behaviors. In this study, the aggregation of fine diaspore particles was comparatively investigated in sodium oleate (NaOl) solutions with and without HC pretreatment through the small-angle laser scattering (SALS) technique in a shear-induced aggregation (SIA) system. Results showed that HC pretreatment caused the formation of bulk nanobubbles (BNBs), which significantly modified the particle interactions and thereby modified the size and mass fractal dimension (Df) of aggregates under different SIA conditions. Although HC pretreatment did not noticeably alter the gradual change trend of aggregate size and structure characteristics under specific variables, BNBs bridging facilitated the aggregation process towards the diffusion-limited cluster aggregation model, resulting in the formation of larger but looser aggregates. This effect was more pronounced under relatively high NaOl concentrations. Apart from BNBs, the aggregation was also affected by cavitation bubbles formed during shear cavitation, which was more significant under high stirring intensity conditions (i.e., 1800 rpm) than the low stirring intensity conditions (i.e., 600 rpm).

Introduction

Aggregation behavior is one of the most common phenomena of fine particles, which can be observed in various fields, such as papermaking, dyeing, and wastewater treatment [1], [2]. Over the past few decades, with the development of aggregation theory, different models of aggregation have been reported, including diffusion-limited cluster aggregation (DLCA), reaction-limited cluster aggregation (RLCA), and so on [3]. It has been recognized that aggregates formed under different driving models exist varying morphological and mechanical characteristics [4]. As many characteristics of suspensions (such as the optical, rheological, and adsorptive properties) are closely related to the geometrical properties of aggregates [5], fully verifying the morphological characteristics of aggregates holds great economic and academic value.

During the solid-liquid separation process, the morphology of aggregates plays a crucial role in determining the separation efficiency [6]. Factors such as aggregate size and structure have significant impacts on this outcome. While research on aggregate size variations during aggregation is extensive, relatively little attention has been paid to aggregate structures. On the other hand, it has been found that aggregates involved in different coagulation processes often have fractal structure characteristics. The fractal dimension is the most important parameter for quantitatively describing the fractal structure of aggregates. Currently, there are several methods for measuring the fractal dimension of aggregates, mainly including image analysis, settling, and light scattering [7]. Particularly, image analysis is one of the simplest and most widely used methods. However, a two-dimensional image can distort the three-dimensional morphology of aggregates, causing problems in accurately presenting final data [8]. The settling method also has limitations in characterizing the structure of aggregates because the assumption of impermeable spheres used does not accurately relate to aggregate size, porosity, and settling velocity for highly porous and fractal aggregates [9]. Additionally, both image analysis and settling require a significant amount of time to acquire data and are only suitable for offline characterization. In contrast, light scattering based on the Rayleigh-Gans-Debye (RGD) approximation provides opportunities for the in-situ measurement of the mass fractal dimension (Df) of aggregates, which parameter is often inferred from scattering experiments that exhibit a power law correlation between scattered intensity and the scattering vector [10]. Light scattering, meanwhile, has obvious advantages in monitoring the fragile and deformable properties of aggregates [11], [12]. More precisely, light scattering methods include laser scattering, X-ray scattering, and neutron scattering. Among them, laser scattering requires low equipment demands and has a relatively long wavelength, making it suitable for measuring aggregates with larger sizes. The small-angle laser scattering (SALS) technique has been employed for Df determination in various monodisperse systems, like alumina particles, ferric hydroxide particles, etc. [13], [14]. To kaolin, coal, and other inorganic matter, it is reported that the SALS technique can be well adapted for the structure characterization of aggregates with tens of microns in size [15], [16], [17]. Furthermore, SALS has demonstrated its suitability in determining the Df of aggregates formed not only under the model of DLCA but also under the model of RLCA, showing its broad adaptability [3].

Focusing on the field of mineral processing, particle aggregation shows significant impacts on ultrafine minerals processing. Various factors that induce the aggregation of fine particles, such as energy input [18], polymer flocculant [19], salt [20], [21], and other parameters [22], [23] have been extensively investigated in previous studies, revealing close coupling relationships between aggregate characteristics and processing responses. As a current research hotspot, the role of hydrodynamic cavitation (HC) pretreatment and related tiny bubbles in particle aggregation has been widely explored [24], [25], while quantitative characterization of corresponding aggregates is still lacking. Although some reports indicate that particle aggregation in solutions pre-subjected to HC differs from that in conventional solutions [26], changes in aggregate properties can be multifaceted [27]. Despite these findings, comprehensive research on particle aggregation and multi-dimensional characterization of aggregate in cavitation systems remains limited.

The −38 μm particle size fraction accounts for more than 30% of China’s bauxite resources, but the conventional flotation and desilication technology currently in use has a low comprehensive recovery efficiency for this portion of resources [28]. Additionally, the processing of fine bauxite slimes poses challenges such as settling, filtering, and dewatering in practice [29]. In contrast, HC technique has demonstrated technical superiority in strengthening the aggregation and subsequent benefication of fine bauxite ores [30], while the underlying mechanism is still pending. Thus, in this study, fine diaspore particles were chosen as the objective mineral for research. Sodium oleate (NaOl) was used as the sole surfactant. Firstly, HC was triggered by jetting NaOl solutions through a specific venturi tube circularly to accomplish cavitation pretreatment. Then, stirring-induced aggregation (SIA) of fine diaspore particles in NaOl solutions with and without HC pretreatment was launched, with the aggregation of solids under different conditions monitored through SALS accordingly. This study aims to provide insights into the mechanism of fine particles aggregation assisted by HC more comprehensively.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

Basic mineralogical information of the tested diaspore sample has already been described previously [31]. NaOl (AR) was purchased from Beijing Dingguo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Reagent grade HCl and NaOH acted as pH adjustments. Deionized ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ•cm, was pre-filtered by 0.22 μm microporous membranes before final utilization in all tests.

Bulk nanobubbles generation and characterization

Fig. 1 schematically shows the HC-SIA system, in which BNBs are produced during the HC processing. In each test, pure water was initially pumped through a peristaltic pump (WT-600EAS/353Y, JIHPUMP, China) into a venturi tube with the liquid velocity of the tube throat kept near 16.2 m/s (a velocity enough for initiating cavitation inception [32]). Then, the solution was cyclically treated in the close-circuit system for 3 min, while no extra aeration was imposed during the processing, because 2–2.5% original dissolved gas within the water was found to be sufficient to provide substantial gas nuclei for gas nucleation [33], [34]. After HC treatment, the “milky” gas-liquid mixture stored in the “Cavitation reactor” was stood for 5 min to make the larger microbubbles burst/breakup, thus forming the BNBs solution. Thereafter, BNBs solution was transferred into a nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; NS300) for visual observation (Direction A2). All tests were launched at room temperature and in an open ambient environment.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of HC– SIA system.

Schematic diagram of HC– SIA system.

Aggregates formation and characterization

Before the SIA experiments, NaOl solutions prepared at pH 10 with varying concentrations were pretreated through the HC system (All the operating variables are the same as mentioned in section 2.2). After being processed and standing for 5 min, NaOl solutions containing BNBs were prepared. For each SIA test, 5 g fine diaspore powder and 95 ml NaOl solution with and without BNBs containing was firstly mixed to make the slurry with a solid mass concentration of 5% in a beaker (Direction A1). Then, the beaker was placed onto a magnetic stirrer and the SIA test was carried out. During the tests, aggregation of diaspore particles was mainly investigated at two different stirring intensities (SIs), i.e., 600 rpm and 1800 rpm. The focus was to reveal the size and structural characteristics of diaspore aggregates as a function of NaOl concentration and stirring time (ST).

The size and structure features of aggregates were analyzed through the SALS, which was effective for characterizing aggregates with low refractive indices and loose structures [35]. The instrument used here was a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). In each measurement, a small quantity of well-treated slurry was carefully transferred using a 0.5-cm ID pipette to the measuring beaker filled with 1L of ultrapure water. This was done gently thus to prevent any possible aggregates from breaking apart. The system’s light shielding degree was monitored until it reached a range of 10±2%, after which the measurement started. Each sample was measured 5 times successively, with the average recorded as the final reported data. Notably, the size data of tested particles could be directly output through the supporting software, while the corresponding Df could only be obtained after special calculation and analysis based on the extracted scattering angle and scattering light intensity data.

Df can be normally used to describe structure characters of aggregates. The low Df indicates that aggregates are amorphous and loose, whereas aggregates that are more compact should have higher Df. Meanwhile, for any mass fractal aggregates, the mass, m(R), of the aggregates is directly proportional to its radius, R, raised to a power equal to Df, according to(1)m(R)∝RDf

Results and discussion

Visual observation and stability characterization of BNBs

It is well known that “milky” gas-liquid mixture appears when HC starts, with numerous tiny bubbles being produced simultaneously. Once the HC processing stops, most of the larger microbubbles tend to move upward and disappear at the liquid-air interface as time passes, leaving most nanoscale bubbles stagnating in the bulk phase thus forming BNBs solutions. BNBs are thought to be incredibly stable in aqueous solutions and can remain long enough for the non-real-time detection [39]. Here, in order to clarify the characteristics of BNBs generated during HC processing, a presentative signal graph of BNBs stored in ultrapure water subjected to HC treatment and subsequent 5-minute deposition was present in Fig. 2(a). Obviously, some nanoscale round and bright spots appeared. Given that almost no impurities in the pure water, these detected nano-size spots were likely to be the BNBs formed by gas homogeneous nucleation during HC processing [40]. Fig. 2(b) illustrated the concentration of BNBs in the BNBs solution over time. Evidently, after HC processing, the concentration of BNBs could reach up to 108/ml, indicating a highly efficient production process. Additionally, the concentration of BNBs decreased slowly with only an 8.2% reduction after 2 h, confirming their remarkable stability.Fig. 2(a) Intuitive pictures of BNBs generated by HC (b) The concentration of BNBs as a function of settling time.

(a) Intuitive pictures of BNBs generated by HC (b) The concentration of BNBs as a function of settling time.

In the presence of NaOl, smaller BNBs are likely to be produced during HC processing, as much smaller interface tension caused by NaOl benefits the prevention of bubbles’ coalescence and breakup [41]. Meanwhile, the strong affinity between NaOl molecules and BNBs promotes the accumulation of organic groups on BNBs surfaces, forming organics-armored BNBs. The hydrophobic group of oleate anions inserts into the interior of BNBs while the hydrophilic group faces outward, resulting in highly negatively charged BNBs [38]. So, it is convinced that more and smaller BNBs can be obtained if NaOl is pre-added into the solution before the HC operation. The steady deposition of BNBs in NaOl solutions may potentially affect the later aggregation of fine particles as that reported previously [39], which will be further explored in the following studies.

Size and structure characteristics of aggregates formed under the low stirring intensity

As all known, particle’s aggregation is strongly related to the flow field environment, with SI a key factor [42]. Plus, the presence of BNBs can indeed enhance the aggregation of particles through capillary bridging [43], while detailed characteristics of BNBs-assisted aggregates are still pending. Therefore, the aggregation of diaspore particles was carried out here under two different SIs, from which the influences of HC pretreatment on diaspore aggregation could be exposed. Notably, SALS technique was employed to unveil the size and Df characters of diaspore aggregates. As the refractive index of gas is much lower than that of diaspore, the interfere of BNBs in aggregate characterizations is likely very insignificant [35], [44]. In other words, the RGD scattering theory was still of high applicability in determining the properties of aggregates in this study.

Fig. 3 shows the apparent size of diaspore aggregates in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 600 rpm. Evidently, in the presence of bare NaOl (Fig. 3(a and c)), the SD curves of aggregates shifted to the right generally as NaOl concentration rise. This could be attributed to the NaOl adsorption, which increased the surface hydrophobicity of diaspore and then promoted the particles aggregation through hydrophobic attraction [45]. At higher concentrations of NaOl, the hydrophobicity association between non-polar hydrocarbon chains of NaOl molecules also came into play, further strengthening the aggregation of solids [46].Fig. 3Size of aggregates formed in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 600 rpm (ST = 3 min).

Size of aggregates formed in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 600 rpm (ST = 3 min).

The HC treatment of NaOl could also boost the right-shift of the SD curves of diaspore aggregates (Fig. 3(b)), which was more significant at relatively high NaOl concentrations. It soundly indicated that BNBs generated in the HC process are indeed conductive to the aggregation of diaspore particles. In details, when the concentration of NaOl was low, both the hydrophobicity of minerals and the number of newly-formed BNBs were low. At this time, there would be only few BNBs stably entrapping on the defects (e.g., pores, cavities, cracks, etc.) of solids due to the geometric heterogeneity of mineral surfaces [24], resulting in the formation of gaseous bridges which ensured a cohesion between particles. But the bridging effect was supposed to be rather limited under this condition, the size of diaspore aggregates was only slightly enlarged consequently. By contrast, as NaOl concentration raised, more stable BNBs were produced and higher surface hydrophobicity could be achieved after NaOl-diaspore interaction. High hydrophobic surfaces were of relatively low surface energy which allowed more BNBs to accumulate on the solid/liquid interfaces. As a result, BNBs induced capillary bridging between particles became more significant, which therefore leaded to more noticeable differences in the aggregation of fine diaspore between the sole SIA system and the HC-SIA system. The influence of NaOl concentration and HC treatment on aggregate size could also directly reflect from the comparative results of the volume average size (D43) values (Fig. 3(c)). Particularly, at a concentration of 10−3M, the D43 of diaspore aggregates increased from 20.44 μm to 27.51 μm when NaOl solution subjected to HC pretreatment.

Variations in Df of diaspore aggregates as a function of NaOl concentration are shown in Fig. 4. According to the classic aggregation kinetics theory, aggregates typically formed through a sequence of transformations from primary particles, to clusters, and finally into aggregates [47]. When subjected to low SI (i.e., 600 rpm), the slurry flow smoothly and the aggregation process was primarily affected by colloidal physicochemical interactions, including electrostatic repulsive forces, van der Waals forces, hydrophobic attraction as well as hydrophobic association of NaOl molecules, all of which were closely related to the dosage of NaOl added. In details, when the concentration of NaOl was low (≤10−4M), only a few small superhydrophobic spots (known as “active sites”) appeared on diaspore surfaces after NaOl adsorption. These active sites played a crucial role in aggregate growth by serving as starting points for the formation of fine clusters with a compact nucleus, which ultimately resulted in the formation of aggregates with a higher Df value [26]. When the NaOl concentration was within the range of 10−4M to 2×10−4M, hydrophobic attraction induced by NaOl adsorption might periodically predominate, which would accelerate the aggregation process and eventually leaded to the formation of larger but looser aggregates [48]. As the NaOl concentration increased to a level between 2×10−4M and 6×10−4M, the high adsorption of NaOl remarkably enlarged the electrostatic repulsion between diaspore particles, making the hydrophobic attraction between particles relatively weakened. As a result, the diaspore particles faced a greater energy barrier to interacting with each other, leading to slower aggregation and the formation of more compact aggregates. Lastly, when the NaOl concentration surpassed 6×10−4M, a large amount of NaOl might adsorb onto the diaspore surfaces. The hydrophobic attraction between hydrophobized solids and the hydrophobic association caused by the hydrocarbon chains of NaOl might exceed the repulsive forces, thereby substantially accelerating the aggregation process. Consequently, the apparent size of diaspore aggregates increased while the corresponding Df normally decreased [28].Fig. 4Df of aggregates formed in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 600 rpm (ST = 3 min).

Df of aggregates formed in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 600 rpm (ST = 3 min).

The HC pretreatment of NaOl solution prior to SIA did not alter the Df trend observed in diaspore aggregates concerning varying NaOl concentration (Fig. 4(b and c)). However, the NaOl concentration required for the formation of the most compact aggregates decreased from 2×10−4 M to 10−4 M. Importantly, a comparison between Df values of diaspore aggregates obtained under these two systems (i.e., sole SIA and HC-SIA systems) indicated that HC pretreatment of NaOl solutions consistently yielded smaller aggregate Df. Moreover, the gap became more pronounced as the NaOl concentration increased. Based on the above analysis, changes in aggregation behavior could be likely attributed to the production of BNBs during HC processing and their role in bridging solids [26], [49]. Specifically, when the NaOl concentration was low (i.e., ≤10−4M), there were only a few BNBs entrapped on solids that promoted particles aggregation via capillary bridging. As a result, some compact diaspore clusters might form in slurries, leading to larger Df values. When the concentration of NaOl was relatively high (＞10−4 M), more BNBs and more hydrophobic diaspore particles could be obtained, which indisputably benefited the attachment of BNBs onto solids and therefore triggered more intensive bridging between different clusters/particles. However, the presence of BNBs could also cause an effect similar to steric hindrance and strength the electrostatic repulsion between BNBs and particles, making it difficult for compact aggregates to form during the bridging process. In brief, BNBs played a vital role in diaspore aggregation and tended to generate more aggregates with lower fractals, consistent with our previous study where we observed more gel-like aggregates in slurries being pretreated by HC [26], [27], [50].

In addition, the measured Df values suggested that the aggregation process of fine diaspore in bare NaOl solutions generally conformed to the DLCA model, while was not the case when the NaOl concentration reached to 6×10−4M. By contrast, pretreating NaOl solutions with HC leaded to lower Df for aggregates, indicating that HC pretreatment facilitated the aggregation process more towards the DLCA model [3]. This could be attributed to the BNBs’ capillary bridging. Interestingly, at a NaOl concentration of 6×10−4M, there was a relative increase in the Df of aggregates, likely due to their breakup and reorganization during the SIA process. The rupture of aggregates caused small particles/clusters to fill inside, thereby increasing the density and ultimately leading to an increase in the measured Df values [51].

Fig. 5 depicts how diaspore aggregates size changes over ST in NaOl solutions at a SI of 600 rpm. In the sole SIA system (Fig. 5(a and c)), SD curves of diaspore largely overlapped as ST prolonged until it reached to 16 min, at which point the SD curve noticeably shifted leftward. These observations suggested that hydrophobized diaspore could reach aggregation equilibrium quickly at the low SI. However, when stirred for longer period (e.g., 16 min), significant breakup and reorganization of preformed aggregates occurred. Compared with the sole SIA system, larger aggregates were generated in the HC-SIA system when the ST was very short (Fig. 5(b and c)), suggesting that particles adhesion through BNBs bridging occurred rapidly [52], [53]. However, the aggregates could be quickly re-destroyed with the SD curve significantly left shifted as stirring continued. Moreover, the SD curves of diaspore aggregates exhibited minimal changes with ST longer than 6 min. Nevertheless, the average D43 of aggregates in the HC-SIA system was always greater than that of the sole SIA system throughout the entire ST range. These results indicated that BNBs generated in HC processing could indeed promote the formation and survival of clusters/aggregates in slurries regardless of ST.Fig. 5Size of aggregates as a function of ST with a SI of 600 rpm (C(NaOl) = 6×10−4M).

Size of aggregates as a function of ST with a SI of 600 rpm (C(NaOl) = 6×10−4M).

At a SI of 600 rpm, the variations in Df of aggregates as a function of ST are shown in Fig. 6. For the sole SIA system, there was a noticeable trend of a slight increase followed by a decrease in the Df of aggregates. In details, when the ST reached 3 min, the Df of diaspore aggregates peaked, indicating its most compact state. However, with prolonged stirring, the Df of aggregates gradually decreased in general, likely due to the breakup of aggregates leading to a looser structure. Based on the mentioned above, it could be concluded that in the sole SIA system, compact aggregates were formed in the initial aggregation stage. As stirring continued, the size of aggregates would increase slowly at the expense of reduced aggregate compactness. When comparing the HC-SIA and sole SIA systems, we observed similar changes in aggregates Df with increasing ST, suggesting that the presence of BNBs in NaOl solutions did not alter the Df transformation pattern of aggregates under varying STs. However, we found that the Df of aggregates was relatively smaller when NaOl underwent HC pretreatment, probably still due to the bridging of BNBs.Fig. 6Df of aggregates as a function of ST with a SI of 600 rpm (C(NaOl) = 6×10−4M).

Size and structure characteristics of aggregates formed under the high stirring intensity

Unlike situations with low SI, influences of hydrodynamics on aggregate formation and property changes become more substantial when dealing with high SI. According to published researches, this influence can be mainly reflected from three aspects. First, the associated strong turbulent environment can provide more dynamics for the particle interaction within the slurry, which helps to overcome the energy barrier for particles adhesion and form more compact aggregates [54]. Secondly, shearing cavitation can normally be triggered near the wall of mixer or on hydrophobic solid surfaces, especially in gas-saturated environment. Once cavitation occurs, cavitation bubbles (CBs) are likely to precipitate in the slurry, which can certainly promote particle aggregation as reported previously [24], [55]. Thirdly, the strong mechanical shearing force of fluid benefits the fragmentation of aggregates, which frequently results in larger aggregates disintegrating into smaller ones or even clusters due to their fragile internal bonds [56]. Overall, the aggregation of diaspore particles under high SI is exceedingly intricate. Here, we tried to comprehensively examine the impact of high SI on aggregation process and aggregate properties under different systems.

Fig. 7 illustrated the apparent size of diaspore aggregates under different NaOl concentrations at a SI of 1800 rpm. It was manifest that the SD curves of diaspore aggregates shifted gradually to the right with the increase of NaOl concentration, irrespective of whether the NaOl solution was pretreated by HC or not (Fig. 7(a and b)). This trend was similar to that observed at the low SI. Additionally, the D43 value in HC-SIA system was consistently larger under a given NaOl concentration, confirming that the presence of BNBs promoted the survival of larger cluster/aggregates. Moreover, there were some intriguing distinctions in the aggregate size characteristics between SIs of 1800 rpm and 600 rpm. Specifically, in the SIA system, when compared with D43 values of aggregates at 600 rpm, the D43 values of aggregates decreased at 1800 rpm when NaOl concentrations were low, while they increased to be higher levels at higher NaOl concentrations. The larger aggregate size observed at 600 rpm under low NaOl concentrations could typically be attributed to the gentle stirring environment that prevented fragile aggregates from breaking down [57]. On the other hand, it was probably that the collision and adhesion between solids and CBs formed during shearing cavitation were more dominant in determining aggregate formation under strong hydrodynamics [58], especially at higher NaOl concentrations. These ultimately resulted in larger D43 values for the final aggregation products at the SI of 1800 rpm. As for the HC-SIA system, changes in SI appeared to have a great impact on aggregate size. In other words, to attain aggregates of comparable size to those produced at 600 rpm, a higher critical NaOl concentration was necessary in the HC-SIA system with stirring at 1800 rpm than in the sole SIA system.Fig. 7Size of aggregates formed in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 1800 rpm (ST = 3 min).

Size of aggregates formed in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 1800 rpm (ST = 3 min).

At a SI of 1800 rpm, variations in Df of diaspore aggregates as a function of NaOl concentration are shown in Fig. 8. Generally, the aggregates Df exhibited a trend of initially decreasing, gradually increasing and finally decreasing again with increasing NaOl concentration. In particular, in the absence of NaOl, aggregation between solids was mainly driven by the fluid dynamics. Under the high SI condition, the strong shearing force of fluid could expel liquid from possible small aggregates, causing fine particles/clusters to be embedded into the voids of aggregates and a relatively large Df of aggregates finally. In cases where NaOl concentrations were low (e.g., 10−4 M), the hydrophobic attraction between solids induced by NaOl adsorption was only minimally enhanced. However, the scatter-adsorbed NaOl and tiny bubbles pinned onto solids provided numerous active sites for particles adhesion. This increased the likelihood of particles forming larger aggregates following rapid aggregation, which in turn leaded to a decrease in the Df of aggregates. Afterwards, as the NaOl concentration increased from 10−4 M to 6×10−4 M, hydrophobic attraction and capillary bridging induced by tiny bubbles between solids might boost the particles aggregation. But meanwhile, strong mechanical shearing had the potential to accelerate the breakup and reorganization of aggregates. As a result, relatively large and compact aggregates could be obtained. Besides, when the NaOl concentration exceeded 6×10−4M, the hydrophobic attraction between solids, the hydrophobic association between NaOl molecules, and the bubble’s bridging of solids were expected to be quite strong, partially weakening the mechanical dispersion process. Thus, the aggregation process reaccelerated, leading to an increase in aggregates size but a slight reduction in their compactness.Fig. 8Df of aggregates formed in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 1800 rpm (ST = 3 min).

Df of aggregates formed in different NaOl solutions with a SI of 1800 rpm (ST = 3 min).

The HC pretreatment of NaOl solutions prior to SIA did not alter the general trend of the Df of diaspore aggregates under varying NaOl concentrations (Fig. 8(b and c)). However, at low NaOl concentrations (i.e., <10−4M), the measured Df values were smaller than those of sole SIA system, due to stronger capillary bridging caused by BNBs. As the NaOl concentration increased, both attraction (caused by NaOl adsorption and tiny bubbles bridging) and electrostatic repulsion among different components were speculated to be stronger in the HC-SIA system than that in the sole SIA system [59]. Therefore, within a relatively wide range of NaOl concentration (i.e., 10−4M to 6×10−4M), the Df of aggregates slight increased before reaching a plateau. With further increase in NaOl concentration (i.e., above 6×10−4M), the Df of aggregates slightly decreased again, which could also be attributed to the hydrophobic association between NaOl molecules. Overall, under the same NaOl concentration condition, the newly-formed aggregates during sole SIA and/or HC-SIA processing were generally of smaller size and Df at 1800 rpm compared to that at 600 rpm. This observation could be partially attributed to the fact that slurries at 1800 rpm subjected to more significant hydrodynamics than those at 600 rpm, leading to the formation of numerous smaller gas-solid clusters/aggregates.

Fig. 9 illustrates the changes in diaspore aggregates size as a function of ST at a SI of 1800 rpm. In the sole SIA system (Fig. 9(a and c)), the apparent size of aggregates initially increased and then decreased as ST extended. This behavior was probably attributed to the tendency of hydrophobized solids to aggregate under the strong shear conditions. In other words, particle aggregation occurred more rapidly than aggregate rupture at this stage, resulting in a gradual increase in size of diaspore aggregates. When ST reached 10 min, the apparent size reached its maximum, suggesting that the aggregation and fragmentation of aggregates achieved a dynamic equilibrium. Afterwards, the apparent particle size began to decrease as stirring continued, implying that the aggregate rupture speed gradually exceeded the aggregation speed.Fig. 9Size of aggregates as a function of ST with a SI of 1800 rpm (C(NaOl) = 6×10−4M).

By contrast, the size of diaspore aggregates showed an initial decrease followed by a noteworthy increase as the ST prolonged in the HC-SIA system. At the initial stage of aggregation, the presence of BNBs in slurry promoted the rapid aggregation between particles, leading to a sharp increase in the apparent size of aggregates within a short ST [60]. However, with the extension of ST, the already formed aggregates were quickly disrupted and their size rapidly decreased, reaching a minimum value after stirring for 3 min (D43≈23.27 μm). Subsequently, as stirring continued, the apparent aggregate size increased incredibly again, which was thought to be closely related to the precipitation of increasingly more CBs in slurries during the long-period shear cavitation. With the joint assistance of BNBs, CBs and hydrophobized solids, noticeable large aggregates were formed in slurries.

Regarding the structural characteristics of diaspore aggregates, it was evident from Fig. 10 that at a SI of 1800 rpm, the Df of aggregates initially increased slightly and then rapidly decreased with prolonged ST, with the highest Df values obtained after 3 min of stirring in both two systems. Moreover, changes in aggregate size and Df indicated that in the sole SIA system, the breakup and reorganization of the already existing aggregates in slurries might predominant when the ST ranged between 1 and 3 min, resulting in smaller but more compact clusters/aggregates. However, when the ST exceeded 3 min, some medium-size aggregates might undergo diffusion-controlled aggregation, leading to larger size and reduced Df of aggregates. In comparison, relatively large and compact aggregates formed in slurries when stirred for 1 to 3 min in the HC-SIA system, probably due to the combining bridging role of BNBs and CBs during this period. After 3 min of shearing, the aggregates size continued to increase, while the aggregates Df gradually decreased. This suggested that the aggregation of particles was mainly accomplished by bridging solids and small clusters/aggregates with those BNBs and CBs [61].Fig. 10Df of aggregates as a function of ST with a SI of 1800 rpm (C(NaOl) = 6×10−4M).

Overall, significant progress was made in quantitatively characterizing the aggregates induced by HC pretreatment in a NaOl-SIA system through the SALS technique. This research built upon our previous studies, further confirming that HC pretreatment had a remarkable effect on fine particles aggregation and the multidimensional properties of associated aggregates. This impact was most likely due to the generation of newly-formed BNBs during HC processing. The presence of these BNBs altered the electrostatic repulsion and bridging attraction between particles, ultimately leading to changes in overall particle-particle interactions within the entire system [62], [63], [64]. Consequently, particle aggregation and aggregate characters changed, with variables of NaOl concentration, SI and ST also showing specific influences.

Conclusions

The aggregation of fine diaspore particles was studied in a NaOl-SIA system with and without HC pretreatment through the SALS technique. The following conclusions can be drawn based on the results.1)BNBs were successfully produced during the HC processing in this study, exhibiting an impressive concentration range of 108/ml. After being left to stand for 2 h, there was only a slight 8.2% decrease in the overall concentration, highlighting their excellent stability.2)Regardless of the SI, increasing NaOl concentration leaded to a gradual increase in the size of aggregates, but the size corresponding to a higher SI was relatively small. The HC pretreatment prior to SIA did not alter the general trend of the size and Df of aggregates under varying NaOl concentration. However, under the HC pretreatment and high SI conditions, aggregation was promoted towards the DLCA model. This promotion could be attributed to the accumulation of BNBs and CBs in the slurry, which fostered the formation of larger but looser aggregates via capillary bridging, particularly when the NaOl concentration was high.3)At an SI of 600 rpm, particles aggregation could quickly reach equilibrium within a short ST. While the aggregate size in the sole SIA system showed little change with an increase in ST, the aggregate size in HC-SIA system decreased markedly before reaching an equilibrium state and the corresponding aggregate size was consistently larger than that in the sole SIA system. This indicated that although stirring caused aggregate breakage, the presence of BNBs still promoted the retention of some aggregates in slurries. Furthermore, with prolonged stirring, the Df of aggregates slightly increased then gradually decreased. HC pretreatment did not change the Df transformation pattern of aggregates under varying ST, while leading to relatively small Df values under the role of BNBs bridging.4)At an SI of 1800 rpm, the aggregate size in the sole SIA system first increased and then decreased as ST prolonged, while the aggregate size in the HC-SIA system first slightly decreased and then sharply increased. Throughout the entire time interval, the aggregate size in the HC-SIA system remained consistently larger under given ST conditions. Regardless of HC pretreatment, the Df of aggregates first slightly increased and then gradually decreased. This could be attributed to the synergistic bridging effect of BNBs and CBs, which strengthened the particles aggregation and thereafter promoted the formation of more large and loose aggregates.
