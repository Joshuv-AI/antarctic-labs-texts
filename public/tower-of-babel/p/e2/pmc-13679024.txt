Elucidating metabolic dysfunction induced by titanium dioxide in intestinal epithelial cells using an integrative approach of metabolomics and transcriptomics
Authors: Xinchen Wang, Shilong Jiang, Zhengyu Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTitanium dioxide (TiO2) is widely present in a variety of food and personal care products, leading to frequent human exposure primarily via oral ingestion. Although the oral bioavailability of TiO2 nanoparticles is generally considered low, the gastrointestinal tract represents the primary site of exposure and may undergo local molecular and metabolic disturbances following repeated contact.MethodsIn this study, we investigated the impact of TiO2 exposure on gene expression and cellular metabolism in intestinal epithelial cells using integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic approaches.ResultsTranscriptomic analysis identified differentially expressed genes associated with disruptions in cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes, collectively pointing to mechanisms underlying TiO2-induced cytotoxicity. Metabolomic profiling further revealed that TiO2 exposure perturbed key metabolic pathways, as evidenced by significant alterations in critical metabolites—including acetylcholine, glutathione, cytosine, deoxyadenosine, and pantothenic acid—indicative of broad metabolic dysfunction. Notably, integrative correlation analysis demonstrated that TiO2 disrupts lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, energy metabolism, and redox homeostasis.ConclusionsIn conclusion, our findings elucidate the metabolic mechanisms driving TiO2-induced intestinal toxicity. Moreover, this work underscores the power of integrated transcriptomic-metabolomic analysis as a robust strategy for mechanistic toxicological evaluation and risk assessment of nanomaterial exposure.Keywords: Titanium dioxide, Toxicology, Food toxicology, Intestinal

Introduction

Titanium dioxide (TiO2), an odorless white powder renowned for its formidable coloring efficacy, serves as a prevalent pigment in sweets, chocolate, chewing gum, beverages, and various foodstuffs [1]. Its utility extends beyond the food fields to encompass a spectrum of industries, including cosmetics, coatings, pharmaceuticals, and allied sectors. The estimated annual global production of nano-sized titanium dioxide was approximately 38,000 tons [2], with the reported annual usage of titanium dioxide in the United States being around 2,780 tons [3]. The widespread utilization of titanium dioxide, alongside the increased likelihood of daily exposure, has prompted heightened scrutiny regarding its probable effects on human health and the ecological environment. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) imposes stringent regulations on the concentration of TiO2 NPs permissible in food, limiting it to less than 1% [4]. In 2022, the precautionary principle adopted by the EU resulted in public policies that prohibited the utilize of food-grade titanium dioxide (fg-TiO2) [5]. Despite that, the use of fg-TiO2 is still authorized in countries outside Europe. Thus, it is urgent to carry out additional studies to further explore the probable adverse impacts of TiO2 in utilization.

There has been extensive research into the fate of TiO2 within the body following oral ingestion, as well as their potential implications for health [6, 7]. Following oral exposure, TiO2 enter the gastrointestinal tract where they exhibit low solubility in acidic gastric fluids, leading to their aggregation and limited dissolution. Consequently, they tend to accumulate in the stomach in small quantities [8]. After oral exposure, no titanium dioxide nanoparticles were found to be distributed in major tissues or organs [9]. The majority of TiO2 nanoparticles were not absorbed and were excreted via feces [10]. The digestive organs (gastrointestinal tract) are therefore the main organs contacted by titanium dioxide and are more susceptible to the effects of titanium dioxide. In vitro, TiO2 led to oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage in Caco-2 cell line [11]. Moreover, nano-sized TiO2 particles affected enterocyte viability and induced the pro-inflammatory cytokines production in vitro intestinal epithelial model [12]. Alongside with in vitro studies, several published animal studies have provided evidence on the adverse effects of TiO2, including induction of intestinal inflammation and promotion of inflammatory bowel disease [13–15]. Therefore, the risk of intestinal health after TiO2 exposure cannot be ignored. Nevertheless, the mechanism of TiO2 exposure leading to intestinal damaging effects is still unclear, and in-depth studies are urgently needed to explore the underlying biological processes.

In recent decades, advancements in biotechnology have revolutionized the field of multi-omics including transcriptomics, metabolomics, proteomics, etc. The adoption and implementation of high-throughput sequencing technology have enabled researchers to obtain vast amounts of experimental data. These data are pivotal for researchers investigating the mechanisms through which pollutants exert detrimental effects on health [16–18]. In this study, 25 μg/mL TiO2 and 50 μg/mL TiO2 were administered to NCM460 cell line to understand the effects of TiO2 on intestinal epithelial cells. The results showed that exposure to TiO2 at a concentration over 50 μg/mL could significantly reduce cell viability. And transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses showed disruption of cellular component and several metabolic processes, with disrupted metabolites and genes related to purine metabolism, which has unique biochemical roles and is critical for a diverse array of physiological processes and for the fitness of various systems. Thus, our dataset explores the genetic, metabolic regulation of human intestinal epithelial cells following titanium dioxide exposure. The integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis data offers valuable clues into the molecular mechanisms underlying the impacts of titanium dioxide exposure on organisms. This information can guide researchers in delving deeper into understanding how this exposure impacts biological processes at a molecular level. By analyzing various omics data, such as genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, we can uncover a more comprehensive picture of the mechanisms at play. Furthermore, a more integrated and comprehensive understanding of biological responses to titanium dioxide exposure is possible using a multi-omics approach. By combining data from different omics levels, researchers can identify potential pathways and interactions that may have been overlooked with a single-omics approach. This comprehensive view enables a more thorough investigation into the intricate relationship between titanium dioxide exposure and biological processes. In conclusion, Analysis of multi-omics data yields plausible insights into elucidating the in vivo response mechanisms to titanium dioxide exposure. This approach offers a more in-depth understanding of how biological processes are influenced by exposure to this substance, ultimately leading to new insights and discoveries in this field of study. Through the integration of various omics data, researchers can gain a more comprehensive perspective on the intricate workings of titanium dioxide exposure on living organisms.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of titanium dioxide

Titanium dioxide was purchased from Xianfeng Nano Co., Ltd. (China). Transmission electron microscopy was used to confirm the size of Titanium dioxide particles. Briefly, TiO2 was suspended in ethanol and then added it to a copper carbon-coated gird. The copper grid was dried in a ventilated area at room temperature. Sample images were captured by a transmission electron microscope and the accelerating voltage of electron microscope was 80 kV.

Cell culture and titanium dioxide treatment

NCM460 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells were exposed to varying concentrations of TiO2 nanoparticles (5, 10, 25, 50, 100, and 250 μg/mL) for 24 hours. Following treatment, cellular morphology was examined and imaged using an optical microscope.

Cytotoxicity assays

The NCM460 cells were subjected to cytotoxicity assessment following treatment with TiO2 using a CCK-8 assay (Aoqing Biotechnology company, Beijing) based on the provided instructions. Briefly, NCM460 cells (5 × 103 cells per well) were planted in 96-well plates with 100 μL Serum-containing cell culture medium, and were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours. The cells were then subject to different concentrations of TiO2 (0, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250 μg/mL) in DMEM for an additional 24 hours. We set five replicates for each concentration. Following this, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the cells were further incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours. The absorbance of each well at 450 nm was subsequently assessed using a microplate reader.

Non-targeted metabolome analysis

Sample preparation

We treated NCM460 cells (1 × 107 cells) with 25 and 50 μg/mL TiO2 for 24 hours, and we set up six biological replicates. 200 μL extract solution (acetonitrile: methanol: water = 2: 2: 1) containing isotopically-labelled internal standard mixture was added to each sample. Each sample undergoes vortex mixing for 30 seconds, followed by three cycles of freeze-thaw using liquid nitrogen. Next, the samples were subjected to ultrasonic treatment in an ice water bath for 10 minutes. Afterward, the samples were placed at −40 °C for 1 hour, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes. Then the supernatant was transferred to a new glass vial for LC/MS analysis. The supernatants from each sample were pooled in equal volumes to generate the quality control (QC) sample.

LC-MS/MS analysis

The experiment was carried by a UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) and connected to a Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo). The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 ammonia hydroxide at pH 9.75 (A) along with acetonitrile (B). Auto-sampler was kept at a temperature of 4 °C, and 2 μL injections were made. The decision was made to utilize the QE HFX mass spectrometer due to its capability in acquiring MS/MS spectra via information-dependent acquisition mode with the Xcalibur acquisition software (Thermo). In this mode, the Xcalibur acquisition software evaluates the full scan MS spectrum systematically. The ESI source parameters were established with a sheath gas flow rate of 30 Arb, an auxiliary gas flow rate of 25 Arb, a capillary temperature of 350 °C, full MS resolution set to 60,000, MS/MS resolution at 7500, collision energy in NCE mode ranging from 10 to 60, and a spray voltage of 3.6 kV (positive) or −3.2 kV (negative).

Metabolomic data analysis

Utilizing ProteoWizard, the primary data underwent conversion to the mzXML format and were subsequently analyzed through an in-house program created in R, utilizing XCMS for functions including peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration. Afterward, metabolite annotation was performed utilizing the in-house MS2 database named BiotreeDB, applying a cutoff of 0.3 to ensure annotation accuracy.

RNA extraction and transcriptome analysis

The NCM460 cells (1 × 107 per sample) were treated with TiO2 (25 and 50 μg/mL) for 24 h and then total RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (ThermoFisher, 15,596,018) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quantity and purity of total RNA were assessed using the Bioanalyzer 2100 and the RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit (Agilent, CA, USA, 5067–1511). Samples for sequencing library construction were selected based on high-quality RNA criteria, specifically requiring RIN numbers exceeding 7.0. DESeq2 software was used for conducting differential expression analysis of genes between two distinct groups, and edgeR was employed for comparing two samples. Genes meeting the criteria of a p-value less than 0.05 and an absolute fold change greater than 1.5 or less than 0.67 were identified as differentially expressed. These differentially expressed genes were subsequently analyzed for enrichment in Gene Ontology (GO) functions and KEGG pathways.

Integration analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying TiO2-induced intestinal toxicity, the interplay between gene expression and metabolic alterations was analyzed using R software. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to investigate potential associations between differentially expressed genes and dysregulated metabolites.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 24.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and all experiments were independently repeated at least three times. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was used, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Characterization of TiO2

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanoparticles underwent comprehensive physicochemical characterization prior to use. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed irregularly shaped particles that aggregated into clusters resembling grape-like bunches (Fig. 1A). The average primary particle size was determined using ImageJ software, and the resulting size distribution histogram showed a mean diameter of approximately 69.55 nm (Fig. 1B). Additionally, dynamic light scattering (DLS) was employed to assess the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of TiO2 in suspension. In distilled water, the nanoparticles exhibited a mean hydrodynamic size of 458.7 nm and a zeta potential of −14.1 mV, indicating moderate colloidal stability.

Cytotoxicity caused by TiO2

We evaluated the cytotoxic effects of titanium dioxide (TiO2) on NCM460 cells across a range of concentrations and monitored morphological alterations using an optical microscope. As shown in Fig. 2A, increasing TiO2 doses induced progressive morphological changes: cells became rounded and shrunken, exhibited reduced adhesion to the culture substrate, and displayed signs of cell death. To quantitatively assess cytotoxicity, we performed a CCK-8 assay. The results demonstrated that TiO2 exposure significantly suppressed NCM460 cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. A statistically significant reduction in viability was first observed at a concentration of 50 μg/mL (Fig. 2B). Based on these findings, 50 μg/mL was selected as the high-dose exposure level because it represented the lowest concentration that induced notable cytotoxicity while retaining sufficient cell viability for downstream transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. In contrast, 25 μg/mL was selected as the low-dose exposure level to evaluate early molecular and metabolic alterations under a relatively low-cytotoxicity condition.

Transcriptome characteristics of NCM460 cells exposed to TiO2

To systematically investigate the impact of TiO2 exposure on the transcriptome of intestinal epithelial cells, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) on NCM460 cells treated with 25 μg/mL (low dose, L) or 50 μg/mL (high dose, H) TiO2, alongside untreated controls (C). Differential expression analysis revealed that, compared to the control group, the 25 μg/mL TiO2 treatment induced 89 upregulated and 111 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). As the TiO2 concentration increased to 50 μg/mL, the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) markedly rose, with 274 genes upregulated and 365 genes downregulated relative to the control (Fig. 3A), indicating a dose-dependent transcriptional response. A hierarchical clustering heatmap illustrated the distinct expression patterns of DEGs across the control, low-dose, and high-dose groups (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, volcano plots were used to visualize the global distribution of DEGs, highlighting both the magnitude and statistical significance of transcriptional changes in the low-dose (Fig. 3C) and high-dose (Fig. 3D) groups compared to the control.

The identified DEGs from L and H groups were then subjected to enrichment analysis by using Gene Ontology (GO) database. The data illustrated that 25 μg/mL TiO2 treatment-affected genes were notably enriched in biological processes associated with the tissue homeostasis, anatomical structure homeostasis, positive regulation of T cell proliferation, T cell costimulation process. DEGs were also enriched in molecular functions such as aspartic-type endopeptidase activity, aspartic-type peptidase activity, cholesterol transfer activity, sterol transfer activity (Fig. 4A). In the group treated with 50 μg/mL TiO2, the different expressed genes were enriched in biological progresses such as tissue homeostasis, anatomical structure homeostasis, heart process. These DEGs were also enriched in molecular function structural constituent of cytoskeleton (Fig. 4B). Moreover, pathway enrichment analysis using the KEGG database showed notable enrichment of Protein digestion and absorption, Fat digestion and absorption, Ovarian steroidogenesis, Cholesterol metabolism, Glycerolipid metabolism signaling pathway among the identified DEGs in group L (Fig. 4A). While DEGs from 50 μg/mL TiO2-treated group were accumulated in Mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis, B cell receptor signaling pathway, Pentose and glucuronate interconversions, T cell receptor signaling pathway, Gap junction tight junction pathway (Fig. 4B).

The OPLS-DA findings in Figs. 5B and 6B revealed significant variations in metabolic profiles between groups L (25 μg/mL), H (50 μg/mL), and the control. By conducting 200 permutation tests, we assessed the OPLS-DA model to ensure its reliability and avoid oversfitting (Figs. 5C and 6C). Volcano plots (Figs. 5D and 6D) illustrated the differential distribution of metabolites in the 25 μg/mL and 50 μg/mL TiO2 groups. Additionally, the hierarchical clustering heatmap depicted the disrupted expressions of metabolites in response to low and high doses of TiO2(Figs. 5E and 6E). As showed in Figs. 7 and 8, in the positive iron mode a greater number of metabolites exhibited discernible differences between groups L and H compared to control.

Integration analysis of transcriptomics and metabolomics in NCM460 cells following TiO2 exposure

In order to further understand the potential mechanisms underlying TiO2 induced intestinal epithelial cell toxicity, we performed joint transcriptome–metabolome pathway analysis using MetaboAnalyst and mapped significantly altered genes and metabolites onto KEGG pathways. Results showed that TiO₂ exposure disturbed multiple metabolic pathways, with partially distinct profiles detected in negative and positive ion modes of metabolomics assay. In the 25 μg/mL TiO₂, negative iron mode mainly affected pyrimidine metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, amino acid-related metabolism, and glutathione metabolism, whereas positive iron mode was associated with alterations in glycerophospholipid metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, histidine metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, and arginine/proline metabolism (Fig. 10A). In the 50 μg/mL TiO₂, negative iron mode primarily disturbed nucleotide metabolism, the TCA cycle, amino acid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, and glutathione metabolism, while positive iron mode mainly affected histidine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, glutathione metabolism, mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis, arginine biosynthesis, and nitrogen metabolism (Fig. 10B). Overall, these findings indicate that TiO₂ exposure broadly perturbs lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, energy metabolism, and redox homeostasis.

Discussion

TiO2 exposure primarily occurs through three main routes in the human body: ingestion via the digestive tract, inhalation through the respiratory system, and contact with the skin. Subsequently, TiO2 traverses the circulatory system, disseminating throughout diverse organs and tissues. Although the oral bioavailability of TiO2 nanoparticles is generally considered low, repeated or chronic exposure may still result in local intestinal retention and limited translocation to extra-intestinal organs, potentially contributing to organ-specific pathophysiological effects. Moreover, extensive investigations have underscored that following inhalation or oral ingestion of TiO2 particles, murine models exhibit notable TiO2 particle accumulation in vital organs, including but not limited to the lungs, gastrointestinal tract, liver, cardiac muscle, spleen, and kidneys, thereby precipitating discernible organ pathophysiology [19, 20].

The precise mechanisms through which exposure to TiO2 causes alterations in the body are not yet fully understood and remain largely ambiguous. Despite ongoing research, the current body of evidence remains sparse and inconclusive. In this context, the utilization of transcriptomic and metabolomic technologies arises as a potent approach to investigate the molecular mechanisms triggered by emerging contaminants. Multi-omics methodologies, combining diverse data types like genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, provide a comprehensive framework for understanding intricate biological responses [21–23]. In this study, TiO₂ nanoparticles with an average primary diameter of approximately 69 nm because this size falls within the nanoscale range and is relevant to nanosized fractions reported in commercial or food-grade TiO₂ materials [24]. Nanoparticles in this size range possess a relatively high surface area and may interact effectively with intestinal epithelial cells [25]. Moreover, omics methodologies were employed to examine the extensive biological alterations in NCM460 cells following exposure to TiO2. The integrated assessment of metabolomic and transcriptomic data from the responses to TiO2 exposure facilitated the understanding of molecular events and mechanisms underpinning the response process in intestinal epithelial cells. These discoveries yield novel insights into the intricate regulation of gene expression and fundamental metabolic pathways subsequent to TiO2 exposure.

Transcriptomic techniques are extensively utilized in toxicology research to elucidate the toxicological mechanisms of substances, identify potential biomarkers, and formulate preventive and therapeutic strategies [26]. The analysis of enriched differentially expressed genes indicated that biological progresses such as tissue homeostasis, anatomical structure homeostasis were influenced by TiO2 exposure (Fig. 4.). Moreover, these DEGs were involved in epithelial structure maintenance and intestinal epithelial barrier. The monolayer of epithelial cells in the intestine forms both a transcellular and paracellular barrier, effectively separating the contents in the intestinal lumen from the surrounding interstitial space [27]. The paracellular barrier is a complex structure of intercellular junctions primarily regulated by apical tight junction proteins and their associated counterparts [28]. One way in which the intestinal epithelial monolayer maintains its barrier function is through the tight junctions between cells, which form the paracellular barrier. These junctions effectively seal the gaps between cells, preventing the passage of molecules between them. Additionally, the transcellular barrier, which consists of cell membranes and intracellular junctions, further reinforces the barrier by controlling the movement of substances through individual cells. This protective barrier can be compromised by various harmful events that disrupt the tight junction complex. Exposure to exogenous substances can lead to damage to the intestinal epithelial barrier, as these substances may trigger inflammation, oxidative stress, and cell apoptosis in the intestinal mucosa. These responses can result in damage to the tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells, such as the degradation or rearrangement of tight junction proteins, thereby increasing the permeability of the intestinal epithelial barrier. It has been shown in vitro experiments that TiO2 exposure induces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in Caco-2 cells, leading to intestinal epithelial cell damage [11, 12]. Interestingly, we noticed that TiO2 exposure disturbed the genes related to Protein digestion and absorption, Fat digestion and absorption, Ovarian steroidogenesis, Cholesterol metabolism (Fig. 4), suggesting that changes in gene expression following TiO2 exposure may further affect cellular metabolic processes.

Metabolomics has emerged as a valuable tool for probing interactions among metabolites, aiding in unraveling the complexity of entire metabolomes [29]. We conducted metabolomic analyses to identify distinct metabolic pathways, aiming to delve deeper into the impact of titanium dioxide exposure on intestinal epithelial cells. We conducted enrichment analyses on metabolites identified in positive and negative ion modes at each TiO2 concentrations. The effects on cell metabolism were found to be more significant at the higher exposure concentration. The findings from the enrichment analyses indicated that pathways experiencing notable disturbances consisted of pyrimidine metabolism, biosynthesis of aminoacyl-tRNA, metabolism of glycine, serine, and threonine, as well as histidine metabolism. Anomalous pyrimidine metabolism could potentially be linked to the oxidative harm inflicted upon the DNA within cells [30]. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, found universally across all organisms, are pivotal in deciphering genetic instructions vital for protein synthesis [31]. The renewal and repair of intestinal mucosal cells necessitate substantial protein synthesis. Consequently, the normal function and provision of aminoacyl-tRNA are paramount for maintaining the health and functionality of intestinal mucosal cells. Therefore, disruption of these metabolic pathways by TiO2 exposure has the potential to influence intestinal health, potentially affecting overall gastrointestinal function and well-being.

Correlation analysis between transcriptomics and metabolomics revealed that genes enriched in purine metabolism pathway such as ENTPD2 and GUCY2F were negatively related to purine metabolite deoxyadenosine (Fig. 10). ENTPD2 is a nucleotidase localized to the plasma membrane and involved in the hydrolysis of ATP to adenosine [32]. Deoxyadenosine, derived from the nucleoside adenosine, consists of adenine connected to a deoxyribose moiety through a N9-glycosidic bond. The absence of oxygen at the 3’ position of its ribose ring distinguishes deoxyribose from ribose. At elevated concentrations, deoxyadenosine functions as both an immunotoxin and a metabolic toxin [33]. Metabolic toxins are endogenous metabolites that, when present in elevated concentrations over an extended duration, can adversely impact the health of various body systems. Therefore, the observed fluctuations in metabolites and gene expressions resulting from TiO2 exposure provide a foundational basis for conducting more thorough and comprehensive exposure assessments in the future.

The biological effects of orally ingested TiO2 nanoparticles are closely related to their absorption, distribution, and clearance kinetics [34]. Most TiO2 particles are thought to remain within the gastrointestinal tract and be eliminated through feces, whereas a small fraction may cross the intestinal barrier via M-cell-mediated transport, transcellular uptake, or paracellular routes and subsequently distribute to extra-intestinal organs [35]. Particle size, aggregation state, surface charge, protein corona formation, and interaction with the intestinal mucus layer may further influence their local retention and biological availability [36]. These kinetic characteristics support the relevance of intestinal epithelial cells as a primary target for evaluating TiO2-induced toxicity [37].

Consistent with this concept, our NCM460 cell model showed that TiO2 exposure induced concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, with notable toxicity beginning at 50 μg/mL. Moreover, transcriptomic analysis revealed changes in adhesion- and extracellular matrix-related pathways, indicating potential disruption of epithelial barrier-associated functions. Metabolomic analysis further showed alterations in oxidative stress- and nucleotide metabolism-related metabolites, including glutathione and deoxyadenosine. Therefore, even if systemic absorption of TiO2 is limited, local intestinal exposure may be sufficient to induce epithelial stress responses, metabolic disturbance, and purine metabolism dysregulation.

We recognized the limitations of our research. Our primary focus was on examining the effects of TiO2 exposure on NCM460 cells over a 24-hour period. However, it is crucial to emphasize that variances in TiO2-responsive metabolites and genes, as documented in the literature, may arise from variations in exposure durations or cellular responses across different models or cell types. The outcomes of long-term studies exploring the impacts of TiO2 exposure across different time frames and its effects on various organs, such as the spleen or kidneys, should not be overlooked. Our research was predominantly centered on the NCM460 cell line in an in vitro setting. Future research should concentrate on comparing the biological responses between mice and humans after TiO2 exposure in order to identify similarities and differences between the two organisms. This will enhance our knowledge of the underlying health effects of TiO2 exposure in humans, drawing on insights gained from mouse studies. Moreover, the comprehensive transcriptomic and metabolomic data provided in our study could potentially serve as a valuable resource for future research endeavors, including epidemiological inquiries into the mechanisms linked to TiO2 exposure. In addition, the TiO2 concentrations used in vitro may exceed typical environmental or dietary exposure levels and cannot be directly extrapolated to human exposure scenarios. Nevertheless, as the gastrointestinal tract is the primary contact site after oral exposure, local accumulation or interaction with the intestinal mucus layer may result in relatively higher intestinal exposure. Therefore, the selected concentrations were used to investigate mechanistic responses rather than to directly reproduce real-world exposure. Future low-dose, long-term, and in vivo studies are needed to validate the physiological relevance of these findings.

Conclusion

Collectively, TiO2 exposure in NCM460 cell line disturbed the transcriptional mapping. The different expressed genes clustered in pathways related to cell conjunction, cell adhesion and cell metabolism. Besides, metabolism analysis indicated that cell metabolism was significantly perturbed by TiO2, especially nucleotide metabolism. Therefore, our findings offer a fresh perspective on the mechanism behind TiO2-induced toxicity in intestinal epithelial cells and enhance our comprehension of the potential toxicity of food additives.
