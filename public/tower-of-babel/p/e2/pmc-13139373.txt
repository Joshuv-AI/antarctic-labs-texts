Overlying strata movement characteristics and water conducting fracture zone height prediction for deep coal mining in arid Western China
Authors: Qiu Du, Guangli Guo, Huaizhan Li, Jingchao Sun, Zhenqi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe deep mining of coal seams in the central Ordos Basin, a critical arid region of western China, is governed by the unique mechanical behavior of ultra-thick, weakly cemented sandstones of the Cretaceous Zhidan Group, which challenge classical overburden control theories. To elucidate the deformation mechanisms of this special overburden and assess its implications for groundwater protection, this study integrates physical similarity simulation, laboratory rock-mechanical testing, microstructural analysis, and distinct-element numerical modeling (UDEC). A 1:500 similarity model simulated the stratigraphic sequence and mining progression. Results reveal that during single-panel extraction, the main roof initiates deformation at a 150 m face advance, with subsidence reaching 5.55 m at a 300 m mining width. Under multi-panel continuous mining, the high-position weakly cemented sandstones exhibit large-scale coordinated bending and plastic flow, forming a distinctive “subsidence–rotation” deformation pattern that propagates upward. Triaxial tests confirm the rock’s low strength and high confining-pressure sensitivity, with peak strength ranging from 27.8 to 71.1 MPa, cohesion of 6.7 MPa, and an internal friction angle of 38.7°. Failure modes transition from tensile splitting at low confinement to shear and plastic flow at higher pressures. Microstructural analyses (SEM/EDS) identify well-rounded quartz grains, extensive intergranular pores, and weak calcite–feldspar cements, explaining the observed high deformability and low bearing capacity. UDEC simulations of multi-panel mining captured progressive horizontal separation, stress redistribution, and composite tensile–shear failure. The water-conducting fracture zone (WCFZ) is shown to develop upward to the base of the Jurassic Anding Formation, with a maximum height of 186 m when the width-to-depth ratio exceeds 1.5. These findings characterize the deformation features of weakly cemented overburden and establish a mechanical basis for predicting the WCFZ, providing critical insights for overburden management and groundwater resource protection in deep coal mining under arid conditions.Keywords: Weakly cemented sandstone, Deep coal mining, Overburden movement, UDEC numerical simulation, Water-conducting fracture zone heightSubject terms: Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Under the global energy transition, coal remains a crucial primary energy source, and its safe, efficient, and green mining continues to be a practical challenge for many regions worldwide. The Ordos Basin in China, endowed with abundant coal resources, serves as a core base for national energy security. As shallow resources diminish, mining activities are progressively shifting to deeper coal seams with more complex geological conditions1–3. A defining geological characteristic of the central mining area in the basin is the widespread development of ultra-thick and weakly cemented sandstone from the Cretaceous and Jurassic systems overlying the coal seams4,5. Due to their late diagenesis and short geological history, these rock masses are primarily cemented by argillaceous, calcareous, or porous materials, resulting in low cementation strength and exhibiting significant “weakly cemented” properties6—characterized by low strength, high porosity, susceptibility to weathering, and a tendency to soften and disintegrate upon contact with water. This stands in fundamental contrast to the typically high-strength, competent overburden commonly found in other major global coal basins, such as the Appalachian Basin in the USA7 or the Ruhr region in Germany8. Under the influence of deep mining, the movement, fracturing, and stress evolution of such weakly cemented overburden are highly distinctive. Traditional overburden control theories based on competent rock masses experience a significant decline in explanatory power and predictive accuracy under these specific geological conditions, making it difficult to effectively guide engineering practices related to water prevention9–11, ground control12,13, roadway support14–16, and ecological protection17–19. Therefore, gaining an in-depth understanding of the dynamic response mechanisms of thick, weakly cemented overburden under deep mining disturbance is not only a pressing technical challenge for the safe extraction of deep resources in the Ordos Basin but also a frontier scientific issue in international mining rock mechanics concerning the behavior of special weak rock masses.

Extensive and fruitful research on overburden movement has been conducted over the years, leading to the establishment of classical theoretical frameworks such as the Key Stratum Theory20,21, the Voussoir Beam Structure, and the Transfer Rock Beam Theory. However, these theories are primarily founded on geological conditions involving rock masses of relatively high strength. For the ultra-thick, weakly cemented sandstone and mudstone of the Cretaceous and Jurassic systems widely distributed in the Ordos Basin, their physical and mechanical properties—low strength, high porosity, and significant rheological behavior—coupled with their thick-bedded structural features, often hinder the formation of stable “beam”-like structures under mining influence. Instead, they are more prone to large-scale coordinated bending, rapid closure of bed separation, and the development of unique fracture patterns, reflecting nonlinear deformation behavior22,23. While existing research has noted the particularity of weakly cemented strata, it has predominantly focused on shallow mining conditions24. Studies on the overburden movement mechanisms under the coupled effects of deep coal seam mining and ultra-thick, highly positioned weakly cemented overburden remain unsystematic. Specifically, the controlling mechanisms of weakly cemented rock mass deformation on strata pressure behavior and surface subsidence under deep mining conditions are not yet fully revealed. Moreover, the spatiotemporal evolution of internal deformation and failure within weakly cemented overburden remains unclear. These unresolved key scientific questions hinder the development of accurate prediction methods for water-conducting fracture zones and effective ground control technologies tailored to these specific geological conditions25,26.

The macroscopic mechanical behavior of rock is fundamentally governed by its microstructure and mineral composition27,28. Deciphering this “genetic code” is essential for understanding overburden movement patterns. Internationally, significant research has been conducted on the behavior of weak rocks, but it has largely focused on mudstone, shale, or severely weathered rock masses. Research on the specific weakly cemented sandstone found in the Ordos Basin—characterized by late diagenesis yet great thickness—is still in its nascent stages. Existing findings indicate that at the microscopic scale, the weakly cemented sandstone in the Ordos Basin typically exhibits irregular, angular mineral grains (primarily quartz and feldspar) with small contact areas between them. Cements, mainly composed of clay minerals such as kaolinite, illite, and montmorillonite, or calcite, are often insufficient or unevenly distributed, forming extensive networks of micropores and microcracks. This loose structure makes the rock more susceptible to particle rolling, shear slippage along cements, and pore collapse under triaxial compression, manifesting macroscopically as low peak strength, significant strain-softening, plastic flow characteristics, and high sensitivity to confining pressure and moisture content. However, a comprehensive link between the mechanical properties of this weakly cemented sandstone and its overburden response to mining-induced stress has not yet been conclusively established in the literature.

To systematically address these multi-scale challenges, our study designed and implemented an integrated research scheme combining “physical simulation, microscopic mechanistic analysis, and numerical simulation.” First, based on detailed geological drilling and logging data, a geomechanical model of the study area was established, serving as the foundation for designing and constructing a physical similarity simulation experiment. The experiment employs accurately proportioned similar materials (e.g., sand, gypsum, calcium carbonate) to replicate the mechanical properties of the weakly cemented strata, providing a visual and dynamic recreation of the entire process of longwall panel advance in a deep coal seam. The primary objective is to capture the spatiotemporal evolution of the overburden movement, including the sequential caving of strata from bottom to top, the initiation and development of bed separations, fracture propagation, and ultimate surface subsidence. This approach is particularly focused on revealing the overall deformation mode, the location of key strata, the angle of break, and the developmental height of the water-conducting fracture zone, thereby establishing a comprehensive macroscopic understanding. Second, to delve into the microscopic world, a series of triaxial compression tests were conducted on intact rock samples collected from the field to obtain complete stress-strain curves and failure modes of the weakly cemented sandstone under varying confining pressures. Concurrently, high-resolution observations using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) were performed on rock samples before and after testing to qualitatively and quantitatively analyze changes in their microstructure. Furthermore, Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) was employed to determine the distribution of major elements, aiding in the identification and estimation of primary mineral constituents. This multi-faceted analysis aims to fundamentally explain the genesis of the specific mining response observed in the weakly cemented sandstone of the Ordos Basin. Consequently, by integrating macro- and micro-scale perspectives, this study seeks to elucidate the coupled driving mechanisms and spatiotemporal sequence of deformation, failure, and stress field evolution in the weakly cemented overburden.

The findings of this research are intended to provide a direct scientific basis for the safe and efficient mining of deep coal resources in the central Ordos Basin. They will specifically guide the optimization of panel layout, support design, roof management, water intrush prevention, and strategies for mining-induced subsidence control and ecological restoration. Ultimately, this work aims to contribute to the coordinated development of coal resource extraction and environmental protection in this arid region. Furthermore, the insights gained are expected to hold significant reference value for mining activities in other global regions with similar weakly cemented overburden conditions, such as parts of Central Asia, Australia, and the Western basins of North America.

The structure of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the geological setting of the study area and elaborates on the design and monitoring scheme of the similar material simulation experiment. Section 3 systematically presents the results of this simulation, unveiling the macro-dynamic process of overburden movement and failure. Building upon this, Sect. 4 delves into the underlying mechanisms, from micro to macro scales, governing the mining-induced movement and failure of the ultrathick weakly cemented overburden, through integrated rock mechanics experiments and microstructural analysis. This section also employs UDEC numerical simulation to further quantitatively analyze the spatiotemporal evolution of the internal displacement field, stress field, failure zones, and the water-conducting fractured zone under full-seam mining conditions. Finally, a predictive formula for the height of the water-conducting fractured zone applicable to this region is proposed. The findings aim to provide a significant theoretical foundation and practical reference for water inrush prevention and groundwater resource protection under deep mining conditions in the central mining area of the Ordos Basin.

Materials and methods

Engineering and geological background

The study area is located in the central Ordos Basin, characterized by flat beaches and dune landforms, and experiences a mid‑temperate semi‑arid continental climate. Precipitation is highly unevenly distributed throughout the year, with the majority concentrated between July and September.

According to the comprehensive geological map provided by Yingpanhao Coal Mine (see Fig. 1), the regional stratigraphic sequence, from oldest to youngest, comprises: the Upper Triassic Yanchang Formation (T3y), the Middle‑Lower Jurassic Yan’an Formation (J1‑2y), the Middle Jurassic Zhiluo Formation (J2z) and Anding Formation (J2a), the Lower Cretaceous Zhidan Formation (K1zh), and the Quaternary aeolian sand layer (Q4). The #2‑2 coal seam, currently the main mining target in the area, was formed during the Jurassic period and is hosted in the third member of the Yan’an Formation. It has a burial depth ranging from 660.38 m to 783.68 m, averaging 722.88 m, with an average dip angle of approximately 1°. The seam thickness varies between 5.21 m and 7.33 m, with an average of 6.41 m. Similar material simulation experiment scheme

This study utilized the constant temperature and humidity two-dimensional plane stress similarity simulation test system from the Mining Subsidence Research Institute at China University of Mining and Technology. The test platform measures 5 m in length, 0.3 m in width, and has a maximum height of 1.5 m. Considering the high fluidity of the aeolian sand layer in the study area, which makes it difficult to maintain stability in a two-dimensional similarity model, and the height limitation of the model frame, the aeolian sand layer and part of the Cretaceous Zhidan Group strata were omitted during the actual model construction. Instead, they were replaced by 60 iron blocks applied as a uniformly distributed load, with each block weighing 10 kg.

The geometric similarity ratio of this similar material model is 1:500, with corresponding time ratio 1:22.36 and mechanical property similarity ratio 1:800 accordingly. Based on these similarity ratios, the main physical and mechanical parameters of the model and the mixture ratios of the similar materials were designed. The similar materials are required to possess stable mechanical properties, be relatively close to the prototype in terms of physical and mechanical behavior, be easily accessible with low cost, and be convenient for preparation and handling. This study selected yellow sand as the aggregate, a mixture of gypsum and calcium carbonate as the cementing agent, and mica powder as an additive to enhance material plasticity and adjust the bulk density of the mixture. Additionally, purified water mixed with an appropriate amount of borax (serving as a retarder) was used to thoroughly mix and mold all similar materials. Based on the simplified stratigraphy of the study area and the designed main physical and mechanical parameters for each model layer under the established similarity ratios, mixture ratio experiments were conducted. The final determined similar material mixture ratios for each stratum in the model are presented in Table 3.

Monitoring scheme

Digital Close-range Industrial Photogrammetry (DCIP) for deformation monitoring can be employed to acquire spatial information at different locations of a physical simulation model during simulated mining processes, enabling quantitative analysis of the overburden displacement field distribution under varying degrees of mining influence. In this study, the Xi’an Jiaotong University Digital Photogrammetry (XJTUDP) system was utilized to monitor the deformation of the physical simulation model. As shown in Fig.s 3, the XJTUDP monitoring system consists of scale bars, coded control points, non‑coded monitoring points, and digital cameras. The scale bars and coded control points are arranged around the model frame to provide a unified scale reference and spatial datum for each measurement. Non‑coded monitoring points are regularly arranged on different areas of the simulation model using pins.

As shown in the Fig. 3, a total of 12 horizontal observation lines were arranged on the similar material model. Each line contained 29 measurement points spaced at equal intervals. The observation lines were labeled in top-down order as A, B, …, L. Among them, lines D, G, and K were located at the bottom of the Cretaceous Zhidan Group thick weakly cemented sandstone layer, the Jurassic Anding Formation, and the Jurassic Zhiluo Formation sandstone layer, respectively. Above line D, lines C, B, and A were arranged at 10 cm intervals. Within the Jurassic Anding Formation, lines E and F were set at the upper and lower boundaries of the intermediate fine-grained sandstone layer to distinguish it from the overlying and underlying sandy mudstone layers. Three additional observation lines—H, I, and J—were evenly distributed inside the Zhiluo Formation sandstone layer to enhance monitoring density. Finally, line L was placed between the uppermost sandy mudstone layer and the medium-grained sandstone layer of the Jurassic Yan’an Formation to monitor the displacement and deformation of the main roof.

Deformation monitoring of similar material model based on digital close-range industrial photogrammetry.

During monitoring, the camera was positioned approximately 5 m in front of the model. The shooting locations were arranged in a horizontal arc divided equally into 4 columns, with an interval of about 3 m between adjacent columns. In the vertical direction, each column was further divided into upper, middle, and lower tiers, resulting in a total of 12 viewing angles that provided overlapping image coverage. During actual image acquisition, a minimum of three photographs were taken from each viewing angle to mitigate the adverse effects of data loss caused by defocusing during subsequent data processing.

The monitoring accuracy of the XJTUDP system was evaluated. After stabilization, three repeated observations were conducted. The root mean square error (RMSE) of the coordinates for all monitoring points was calculated and analyzed statistically by Eq. (1). The results (Fig. 4) show that the mean error of horizontal coordinates is concentrated within 0.15–0.55 mm (maximum: 0.98 mm; median: 0.26 mm). The mean vertical error is within 0.15–0.32 mm (maximum: 0.57 mm; median: 0.25 mm). This demonstrates that the XJTUDP system achieves sub-millimeter-level monitoring accuracy, meeting the requirements for quantitative research on similar simulation experiments.

Results

The similar material simulation experiment carried out in this study belongs to the 2-D plane strain model, which can analyze the movement characteristics of overlying strata under the following two mining conditions : (1) the movement characteristics of overlying strata on the main section of the main section during the mining process of a single working face with an actual mining width of 300 m and a total mining length of less than 300 m; (2) the movement characteristics of overlying strata on the inclined main section after continuous mining of three adjacent working faces with an actual mining width of 300 m.

Simulation results of single working face mining

The simulation results show that there is no obvious deformation and failure of the main roof when the working face is mined in the range of 0 ~ 150 m. The following only analyzes the vertical displacement curve on the L-line when mining 150 m.

Figure 5 shows the vertical displacement of each point on the L line after conversion according to the geometric similarity ratio of 1:500 when mining 150 m. Combined with the sub-millimeter monitoring accuracy estimated above, it can be seen that if 1 mm is used as the minimum displacement that can be monitored, the geometric similarity ratio of 1:500 is converted to the engineering scale of 500 mm. Therefore, the observed vertical displacement within 500 mm can be regarded as the measurement error. The vertical displacement of the measuring point above the solid coal on the L-line fluctuates within 500 mm. It can be seen that there is no vertical displacement in this area at this time. There are three points with vertical displacement exceeding 500 mm above the mining area of the working face. This shows that when the simulated mining length reaches 150 m, the vertical displacement of the main roof above the mining area of the working face has begun to appear, which is consistent with the actual situation.

Vertical displacement curve of L line under simulated mining of 150 m widths.

When the mining length exceeds 150 m, the overburden begins to break from bottom to top. The direction of the arrow vector at each measuring point in the figure represents the displacement direction of the overburden rock, and the size, length and color of the arrow vector represent the displacement of the overburden rock.

Overburden displacement field under simulated mining length of 175 to 150 m. (a) 175 m (b) 200 m (c) 225 m (d) 250 m (e) 275 m (f) 300 m.

When the working face is mined at 175 m (Fig. 6(a)), the rock layer under the basic roof is broken for the first time. The displacement vector length of the measuring point at the suspended roof is slightly larger than that of the surrounding measuring points. This indicates that the rock strata in the hanging roof area have begun to move, and the accumulation of elasticity in the rock strata can prepare for the release of subsequent breaking.

When the working face is mined for 200 m(Fig. 6(b)), the whole rock layer of the basic roof is broken. The two measuring points in the broken range of the L survey line have a large downward displacement. The measuring points on the K line are attached to the bottom of the Zhiluo Formation sandstone layer, so there is no obvious displacement.

In Fig. 6 (c), (d) and (e), the displacement of several measuring points at the edge of the L-line above the mining area of the working face is increasing, while the displacement of the remaining measuring points does not change significantly. This shows that the mutual occlusion between the cantilever beam in front of the working face and the rear masonry beam inhibits the rapid expansion of the displacement influence range to the upper strata to a certain extent.

When the mining length of the working face increases to 300 m (Fig. 6(b)), above the mining area of the working face, not only the displacement of some measuring points on the L measuring line in the main roof is increased, but also the displacement of some measuring points on the I, J and K measuring lines in the lower part of the Zhiluo Formation sandstone layer is also greatly increased. This shows that the breaking of the cantilever beam structure in front of the working face weakens the supporting effect on the upper rock layer, and the influence range of the overlying rock displacement is greatly expanded upward.

We extract the vertical displacement of measuring points in the influence range of overburden displacement and near the L line, and draw the evolution process of vertical displacement curve as shown in Fig. 7. When the rock layer under the basic roof is broken for the first time, the maximum subsidence on the L survey line is 0.98 m. When the main roof is broken, the maximum subsidence on the L survey line increases sharply to 5.55 m. Subsequently, the maximum subsidence remained basically unchanged, indicating that the roof span had touched the coal seam floor. However, as the mining length continues to increase, the displacement of some L measuring points in front of the working face increases to varying degrees, so that the vertical displacement curve of the L measuring line shows a spoon-shaped ' skewed ' feature. Combined with the structure formed after the basic roof is broken, it can be seen that the vertical cracks are continuously generated in the basic roof in front of the working face, which makes the relevant rock strata break. There is a bite contact between the broken blocks, which constitutes a typical masonry beam structure, thus weakening the displacement of the roof, so that the displacement curve of the front roof appears skewed.

Simulation results of 3 adjacent working faces continuous mining

When the excavation width of the model reaches 600 mm, the adjacent continuous mining of multiple working faces is simulated. Figure 8 is the distribution map of the whole overburden displacement field when simulating the continuous excavation of three working faces (mining width 300 m).

Overburden displacement field under simulated continuous mining of 3 working faces. (a) mining 1 working face (b) mining 2 working faces (c) mining 3 working faces.

Figure 8(a) shows the displacement field of overlying strata when excavating the first working face. On the whole, the influence range of overburden displacement caused by mining 1 working face is relatively small in the whole overburden. There is almost no obvious displacement in the high-position thick Cretaceous Zhidan Formation weakly cemented sandstone layer far from the working face.

Figure 8(b) shows the displacement field of overlying strata when the second working face is excavated continuously. At this time, the low-lying strata in the overlying strata of the goaf are displaced, and the far-field high-level thick weakly cemented strata appear with an order of magnitude of about 5 mm displacement. By observing the distribution characteristics of the displacement vectors of the measuring points on different strata, the following two phenomena that need special attention can be found :

The displacement vectors of overlying strata in different layers above the mining area have different directional distribution characteristics. The displacement of the measuring point on the high thick weakly cemented sandstone layer points to the center of the goaf. The displacement direction of the measuring point on the sandstone layer of the Middle Jurassic Anding Formation is approximately vertical downward. The displacement direction of the measuring points on the sandstone layer of the Zhiluo Formation at the relatively low level is spread from the center of the goaf to both sides; the displacement direction of the measuring points on the roof of the lowest layer of Yan ‘an Formation is relatively disordered, but it basically points to the lower goaf.

The displacement of the overlying strata in the triangle area surrounded by the fault lines on both sides below the Cretaceous Zhidan Group sandstone layer above the working face 1 is significantly larger than that in other areas. However, the interior of the Cretaceous Zhidan Group is not affected by this non-uniform displacement field, but still has symmetry. This shows that the high Cretaceous Zhidan Group can significantly weaken the ' skewness ' characteristics occurring in the low-level medium-hard Jurassic strata.

Figure 8(c) shows the displacement field of overlying strata when the excavation is continued to the third working face. Compared with the previous stage, the displacement magnitude of the overlying strata directly above the excavation area has been greatly improved. The maximum displacement of the ultra-thick weakly cemented sandstone layer of the far-field high Cretaceous Zhidan Group has exceeded 7 mm. This shows that under this mining degree, the whole overburden will have a large area and high strength overburden movement phenomenon.

In addition, there are three phenomena that need special attention :

The basic overlying rocks above Working Face 1 and Working Face 3 have undergone high-intensity displacement, but the displacement of the overlying rock layer above working Face 2 is relatively small. The relatively small displacement area of the overlying rock on the upper part of Working Face 2 is interconnected with the displacement area of the same magnitude of the overlying rock of the Zhiluo formation on the upper part of Working Face 3, forming a “displacement arch” structure. The structure reflects that the roof of the working face 2 has a supporting effect on the overlying rock after contacting the coal seam floor.

The displacement vectors of overlying strata in different regions have different directional distribution characteristics. According to the directional distribution characteristics of the displacement vector, the overburden movement field can be divided into three regions along the horizontal direction. The middle part is the ' subsidence zone ‘, and the displacement direction of overburden rock in different layers in the area is vertical downward. The left and right sides are the ' rotary zone ‘. The displacement direction of the upper overburden in the area points to the center of the goaf, the middle part is vertical downward, and the lower part points to the outside of the goaf.

The boundary between the ' subsidence zone ' in the middle and the ' rotary zone ' on both sides is approximately ' hyperbolic ‘. The left and right hyperbolic boundaries are symmetrically distributed with the center line of the goaf as the axis of symmetry. The ' subsidence zone ' is surrounded by two left and right hyperbolic dividing lines to form an inverted horn cross-sectional shape with a small upper opening and a large lower opening. The lower part of the hyperbolic boundary coincides with the fracture line of the overburden strata.

In summary, under the geological and mining conditions of the study area, when the width-to-depth ratio of the underground mining area reaches 1.22, the high-level thick weakly cemented Cretaceous Zhidan Group sandstone layer has undergone a large-scale continuous deformation, rather than a fracture-like failure deformation similar to the immediate roof. This ' plastic flow ' reflects the unique strata movement and failure mode of the high-position ultra thick weakly cemented sandstone layer under deep mining conditions in the central mining area of the Ordos Basin.

It can be seen from Fig. 9 that all curves can be divided into two types according to its morphological evolution characteristics. (1) Low-level mining sensitive curves: for each additional working face, the vertical displacement of the measuring points on the L and K measuring lines increases rapidly, and the vertical displacement curve shows obvious skewed characteristics; (2) Medium-high stationary curves : G, D and A survey lines showed a relatively gentle growth rate, and the skewness of the vertical displacement curve under each mining stage gradually weakened from bottom to top. Discussion

Mechanical properties test of weakly cemented sandstone

The movement characteristics of overlying strata caused by underground coal seam mining are closely related to its physical and mechanical properties. In this section, rock mechanics test methods such as conventional triaxial compression test, scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis and X-ray energy spectrum analysis are used to analyze the physical and mechanical properties of the extremely thick weakly cemented sandstone in the study area.

The conventional triaxial compression test

The conventional triaxial compression test instrument used in our study is the TATW-2000 rock triaxial test system. The system is jointly developed by China University of Mining and Technology and Changchun Chaoyang Test Instrument Co., Ltd., which can meet the dynamic and static mechanical tests of cylindrical and cylindrical rock samples under uniaxial and triaxial stress conditions. As shown in Fig. 10, TATW-2000 rock triaxial test system consists of a testing machine, a confining pressure control system and a workstation equipped with professional servo control software. The results of the triaxial compression test show that the sandstone of the the Cretaceous Zhidan Formation is low mechanical strength and weak bearing capacity. Its failure mode first changes from vertical splitting type to inclined shear type and finally to plastic flow type as the confining pressure increases. These characteristics are consistent with the ' plastic flow ' failure phenomenon of overlying strata in similar material simulation experiments. This explains the phenomenon observed in Fig. 8(c), where the overlying strata in the middle of the mined-out area undergo a uniform downward movement, while the overlying strata on both sides exhibit a rotational movement characteristic.

SEM and mineral composition analysis

In order to analyze the microstructure characteristics and mineral composition of weakly cemented sandstone, we cut the sandstone into cube specimens with growth, width and height of 1 cm (Fig. 15). After cutting, they were placed in a cool and ventilated place for 7 days. Before scanning electron microscope observation, the loose mineral particles on the surface of the specimen were blown off, and the surface of the specimen was sprayed with gold to enhance the conductivity to improve the observation effect. In order to reduce the accidental error, five regions were selected on the surface of the sample by random method, and the representative and high-definition visual field was photographed and recorded, and the elemental composition of some regions was analyzed by surface scanning and mineral composition analysis.

Microstructure characteristics of weakly cemented sandstone

Figure 16 are the scanning electron microscope images of Cretaceous Zhidan Formation sandstone under different observation ratios. Figure 16(a) clearly shows the shape and size characteristics of sandstone particles at millimeter scale. From Fig. 16(a), it is easy to know that the particles composed of weakly cemented sandstone are well sorted and highly rounded, and the particle size is basically in the range of 100 - 400 . There are many pores between particles and good connectivity, while there are few cements filling pores, which is obviously in line with the definition of weakly cemented sandstone.

Figure 16(b) and (c) show the skeleton particles of the weakly cemented sandstone and the cement state between the particles at the scale of hundreds of microns. The cementation type between the skeleton particles is pore liner cementation. The cement is attached to the contact position of the particles in a mylonite shape. The larger skeleton particles in the middle also have a crack that penetrates up and down. This phenomenon indicates that the internal microscopic particles of weakly cemented sandstone can also break when it is damaged by force.

Figure 16(d) show the morphology and scale characteristics of weakly cemented sandstone cements at a 20 scale. Weakly cemented sandstone cements are basically debris with extremely poor roundness, and the size is basically within 10. The surface of the skeleton particles is a honeycomb-like uneven interface, and the contact between the particles and the cement is weak. This makes the bearing capacity of macro-scale weakly cemented sandstone weak.

SEM observations of the weakly cemented sandstone in the Cretaceous Zhidan Formation sandstone reveal that its macroscopic flow-type failure under mining-induced stress is attributed to two key microstructural characteristics: relatively coarse grain size and insufficient cementation between particles.

Analysis of numerical simulation experiment

UDEC simulation experiment scheme

The thickness of each stratum and the physical and mechanical parameters of the block in the UDEC numerical model are consistent with Tables 1 and 2, respectively. The aeolian sand layer with strong fluidity in the uppermost part of the overburden is regarded as a uniform load, which is applied to the top of the ultra-thick and weakly cemented sandstone layer of the Cretaceous Zhidan formation. In the model, 1 km wide solid coal pillars are set on the left and right sides of the coal seam excavation area to avoid boundary effect. The left and right boundaries of the model are fixed with horizontal displacement, and the lower boundary is fixed with horizontal and vertical displacement.

UDEC relies on the reasonable division of joint simulation level, joint surface, fracture surface and other rock mass structural planes in advance in the block to simulate the rock mass fracture or fracture effect when the rock mass is destroyed by force. The strata in this study area are relatively stable, and structural planes such as large faults are not exposed. At the same time, the main research object of this section is the deformation and failure characteristics of the whole overburden strata, and the failure form of small-scale rock block is not deeply studied. Therefore, the pre-designed weak joint surface is mainly the horizontal level between strata of different ages. The mechanical parameters of these joints are uniformly set to the values shown in Table 8.

The stress distribution characteristics inside the overburden

Figure 23 is the horizontal stress distribution diagram of overlying strata in the process of continuous mining of 6 working faces. When the number of mining faces is less than 3, the horizontal stress field is characterized by relatively simple compression of the tensile center on both sides of the upper layer and the compression center on both sides of the lower layer. When the number of mining faces exceeds 3, the distribution characteristics of horizontal stress field in overlying strata are more complex. From bottom to top, the roof of the Jurassic Yan ‘an Formation in the lowest layer is basically in a horizontal tensile state, and the tensile stress area is mainly located in the overlying strata in front of the mining area. The horizontal tensile zone in the center of the bottom of the Jurassic Zhiluo Formation and the horizontal compressive stress zone in the front of the Jurassic Zhiluo Formation move forward with the position of the front working face. The tensile stress zone of the previous stage is transformed into a compressive stress zone. The change of horizontal tensile and compressive stress zone at the top of the rock layer of the Zhiluo Formation is just the opposite of that at the bottom. The higher-level Anding Formation sandstone layer has been in a horizontal tensile stress state. The range of the tensile stress zone continues to expand along the front of the working face. The higher Cretaceous Zhidan Group strata show the characteristics of the bottom center tensile zone and the front compression zone continuously shifting along the increasing direction of the working face. The change of tensile and compressive stress zone at the top of Zhidan group is opposite to that at the bottom.

Figure 24 is the vertical stress distribution diagram of overlying strata in the process of continuous mining of 6 working faces. When the third working face is mined, the pressure relief zone and the internal pressurization zone begin to appear simultaneously in the overlying strata above the goaf. With the increase of the number of working faces, the pressure relief area is continuously transferred to the front of the mining area, while the range of the internal pressurization area is gradually expanded and its number is increasing discontinuously. In Fig. 20 (c)-(f), the original pressure relief area and the pressurized area behind the mining area are no longer transferred after they are formed, such as the pressure relief area in the goaf of working face 1 and the pressurized area in the goaf of working face 2. In addition, when the third working face is mined, the vertical stress value exceeds 60 MPa on both sides of the whole mining area, especially in the solid coal pillar area in front of the working face. With the working face forward, the coal pillar pressurization area in front of the mining area will become the pressure relief area. The above phenomena show that under the condition of increasing mining degree, the low-position Zhiluo Formation and the high-position Zhidan Formation in the overlying strata will be disturbed by compression and tensile stress successively. This kind of disturbance with different properties will cause ' fatigue effect ' to the rock strata in the two regions, reduce its mechanical properties and increase the damage degree of mining overburden.

Conclusions

Deep excavation in the central Ordos Basin induces a fundamentally different overburden response due to the presence of ultra-thick, weakly cemented sandstone. Physical similarity modeling demonstrates that the main roof remains stable until a 150 m advance, after which deformation rapidly intensifies. By 300 m of extraction, roof subsidence reaches 5.55 m. Under multi-panel continuous mining, the Cretaceous Zhidan Group does not exhibit brittle fragmentation but instead undergoes large-scale, coordinated bending and plastic-flow deformation, forming a coupled “subsidence zone–rotation zone” structure that propagates through multiple stratigraphic units.

Deep excavation in the central Ordos Basin induces a fundamentally different overburden response due to the presence of ultra-thick, weakly cemented sandstone. Physical similarity modeling demonstrates that the main roof remains stable until a 150 m advance, after which deformation rapidly intensifies. By 300 m of extraction, roof subsidence reaches 5.55 m. Under multi-panel continuous mining, the Cretaceous Zhidan Group does not exhibit brittle fragmentation but instead undergoes large-scale, coordinated bending and plastic-flow deformation, forming a coupled “subsidence zone–rotation zone” structure that propagates through multiple stratigraphic units.

The macroscale deformation mode is governed by intrinsic mechanical weakness and microstructural characteristics of the Zhidan Group sandstone. Triaxial testing reveals low peak strength (27.8–71.1 MPa), low cohesion (6.7 MPa), and a strong dependence on confining pressure, with failure transitioning from splitting to shear and eventually to plastic flow. SEM and EDS analyses show well-rounded quartz grains, pervasive pores, discontinuous calcite–feldspar cements, and widespread microcracks, collectively producing a rock mass highly susceptible to ductile deformation under mining-induced stress redistribution.

Numerical simulations delineate a clear stratified deformation and failure evolution in the overburden. UDEC modeling of six sequentially mined panels identifies preferential development of horizontal separations at the base of the ultra-thick weakly cemented sandstone and within the Zhiluo Formation. The results clarify the deformation patterns of the overlying strata and the development law of the water-conducting fracture zone, which are jointly controlled by specific lithological combinations and mining-induced disturbances.
