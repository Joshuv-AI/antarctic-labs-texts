Anthocyanin-functionalized selenocysteine nanotherapeutics alleviate cisplatin nephrotoxicity by inhibiting oxidative stress and ferroptosis
Authors: Hongyan Zhu, Xingyuan Wu, Yulin Tan, Lei Shi, Weibin Bai, Xiaoling Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough cisplatin (CDDP) remains a widely used chemotherapeutic agent in clinical cancer treatment, severe oxidative stress and inflammatory responses induced by CDDP are still major causes of acute kidney injury (AKI) in patients. Our previous studies have demonstrated that anthocyanins and selenocysteine (SeC) exhibit potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Here, we successfully synthesized a synergistic nanotherapeutic, cyanidin-3-O-glucoside-functionalized SeC nanoparticles (C3G@SeC NPs), which significantly alleviated CDDP-induced damage in HK-2 cells by inhibiting oxidative stress and ferroptosis. In vivo, C3G@SeC NPs alleviated AKI by regulating the MAPK and ferroptosis pathways. Furthermore, C3G@SeC NPs regulated the populations of M2 macrophages, Treg cells, CD4+ T cells, MDSCs, and NK cells disrupted by CDDP in the kidney and spleen tissues of AKI mice, ultimately inhibiting the inflammatory response. In summary, this study synthesizes a synergistic nanotherapeutic and systematically elucidates its anti-AKI mechanisms, indicating C3G@SeC NPs may be an ideal nanoparticle for alleviating CDDP nephrotoxicity.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03741-z.Keywords: Cisplatin, Acute kidney injury, Cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, Selenocysteine, Biomaterials, Ferroptosis

Introduction

Accumulating evidence highlights cisplatin (CDDP) as a cornerstone anticancer agent for solid tumors, including lung, bladder, thyroid, and ovarian cancers [1–3]. Its antitumor activity primarily results from the formation of DNA-cisplatin adducts, which disrupt DNA replication and transcription [4, 5], ultimately inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Despite its significant clinical efficacy, CDDP-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) remains a major limitation to its dosage and therapeutic outcomes. CDDP nephrotoxicity is characterized by acute tubular necrosis (ATN) [6], with underlying mechanisms including oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and ferroptosis [7–9]. While vigorous hydration therapy before and after CDDP administration remains a critical preventive measure for AKI [10], many patients exhibit poor adherence to the hydration protocol. This non-compliance may exacerbate electrolyte imbalances, thereby undermining the efficacy of hydration therapy.

Anthocyanins are a class of natural water-soluble phenolic pigments widely present in plants and classified as flavonoid compounds. The anthocyanin molecule contains multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups, which can donate electrons to neutralize free radicals and have been proven to be highly effective antioxidants [11]. Previous studies have reported the ameliorative effect of anthocyanins against AKI [12, 13]. Among them, cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G) represents one of the most frequently studied anthocyanins, exhibiting diverse biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, reproductive-protective, and metabolic regulatory properties. Recent studies have demonstrated that C3G scavenges excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby reducing oxidative stress-mediated cellular damage. Furthermore, C3G can inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [14] and improve renal function parameters in nephritis models [15]. These findings have highlighted its potential protective role against AKI. However, the phenolic hydroxyl groups in C3G make it prone to oxidation and photodegradation under light exposure, which diminishes its antioxidant efficacy and limits its long-term therapeutic applicability.

Selenium (Se) is an essential trace element for humans, widely distributed in various tissues and organs, with particularly high concentrations in the liver and kidneys. In the human body, Se primarily exists in the form of selenocysteine (SeC), which constitutes the active center of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px). It directly participates in scavenging free radicals and protecting cells from oxidative damage [16]. Compared to inorganic Se, SeC exhibits higher bioavailability and lower toxicity [17], thereby facilitating better intestinal absorption and metabolic utilization. In recent years, nano-SeC synthesized via nanotechnological approaches has demonstrated distinct advantages over traditional SeC compounds, including excellent biocompatibility, negligible toxicity, anti-ferroptosis activity, and enhanced cellular uptake [18]. The nanoencapsulation of SeC with natural compounds via nanotechnology-based strategies represents an emerging research frontier [19]. This approach integrates the unique physicochemical properties of SeC with the bioactivity advantages of natural compounds, offering substantial therapeutic potential. The co-encapsulation of SeC and C3G can significantly enhance the structural stability of both compounds, thereby enabling better preservation and functional manifestation of their bioactivities (Fig. 1). This study aimed to integrate the natural antioxidant C3G with the bioactive SeC into a single nanosystem through a simple stirring method mediated by PAA-PVP copolymer. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted to systematically evaluate the potential of these nanoparticles in alleviating CDDP-induced AKI. The protective mechanisms were comprehensively investigated from multiple perspectives, including antioxidant effects, anti-inflammatory responses, and inhibition of ferroptosis. The findings of this study are expected to provide a theoretical foundation for the development of novel chemotherapeutic adjuvants against CDDP nephrotoxicity.

Results

C3G alleviates CDDP cytotoxicity

The cytotoxicity of CDDP was measured using the CCK-8 assay in vitro. As shown in Fig. 2A, CDDP significantly reduced HK-2 cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner, decreasing cell viability by approximately 54.7% at 6 µg/mL. Therefore, a concentration of 6 µg/mL, which is closest to the IC50 value, was utilized in subsequent experiments to establish a model of CDDP-induced cytotoxicity. There are three types of anthocyanins used to evaluate the protective effects in this study: C3G, malvidin-3-O-glucoside (M3G), and peonidin-3-O-glucoside (P3G) (chemical structures shown in Fig. 2B). The different substituents on the B-ring of these anthocyanins confer distinct colors, stability, and biological activities upon them. Notably, only C3G showed significant protective efficacy, with 40 µg/mL C3G treatment rescuing cell viability loss from 49.0% to nearly 36.5% (Fig. 2C). Structurally, the ortho-dihydroxy structure of C3G exhibits stronger radical scavenging capacity compared to the monohydroxy structure of P3G and the structure of M3G (which contains two methoxy groups at the 3’ and 5’ positions plus a single hydroxyl group) [20]. This structural feature may account for C3G’s stronger antioxidant activity and resistance to CDDP cytotoxicity.

(C) CCK-8 analysis of HK-2 cell viability after treatment with anthocyanins and CDDP. Flow cytometry analysis of cell cycle distribution after treatment with anthocyanins and CDDP (D), including the populations of cells in the G0/G1 phase (E) and Sub-G1 phase (F). Data are presented as means ± SD (n = 3)

Previous studies have confirmed that CDDP can cause cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [21]. PI staining was further employed to assess anthocyanin-mediated modulation of cell cycle distribution. The results of flow cytometry indicated that CDDP induced marked G0/G1 phase arrest in HK-2 cells, which was mitigated by C3G treatment (Fig. 2D and E). By contrast, M3G and P3G showed no significant effects. Additionally, CDDP significantly increased the Sub-G1 population (Fig. 2F), and this increase was inhibited by C3G. Notably, C3G reduced the Sub-G1 population to 8.3%, which was significantly lower than CDDP alone (19.7%) or CDDP + M3G (12.5%). Treatment with C3G, M3G, or P3G alone did not affect cell viability or cycle distribution. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that C3G serves as a promising adjuvant therapeutic candidate for alleviating CDDP cytotoxicity.

Simple synthesis and characterization of C3G@SeC NPs

Application of nanotechnology in disease therapy may increase the solubility and bioavailability of natural compounds or other bioactive compounds. In the present study, we employed a series of polymeric matrices (PLGA-PEG, PF127/TA, and PAA/PVP) to fabricate SeC- and C3G-loaded nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 3A, PAA/PVP-based C3G@SeC NPs exhibited superior encapsulation efficiencies, achieving 10% for SeC and 60% for C3G, resulting in a SeC: C3G mass ratio of 1: 25. In contrast, PLGA-PEG and PF127/TA formulations showed negligible SeC encapsulation (< 1%). Comparative analysis revealed 75% and 67% encapsulation efficiencies for C3G and SeC in single-loaded NPs (Fig. 3B), respectively, while dual-loaded C3G@SeC NPs maintained 61% of C3G encapsulation and 10% of SeC encapsulation. This decrease in SeC encapsulation efficiency is likely attributed to competitive binding between C3G and SeC for negatively charged carboxyl groups within the PAA/PVP matrix. Dynamic light scattering analysis (Fig. 3C-E) indicated hydrodynamic diameters of 1400 ± 120 nm (C3G NPs), 240 ± 30 nm (SeC NPs), and 570 ± 45 nm (C3G@SeC NPs). The zeta potential was determined to be −32.0 ± 0.6 mV for C3G@SeC NPs, −18.3 ± 0.6 mV for C3G NPs, and − 13.6 ± 0.5 mV for SeC NPs, with all particles exhibiting a negative zeta potential (Fig. 3F). Stability assessments under aqueous conditions confirmed < 5% change in hydrodynamic diameter over 7 days (Fig. 3G), suggesting excellent structural integrity and potential for sustained release of nanoparticles [22].

Protective activities of C3G@SeC NPs in vitro

To evaluate the protective effects of the newly synthesized nanoparticles against CDDP cytotoxicity, we pretreated HK-2 cells with nanoparticles before CDDP exposure. As shown in Fig. 4A, all nanoparticles rescued CDDP-induced viability loss in a dose-dependent manner, with only C3G@SeC NPs (5 µg/mL) significantly restoring cell viability, increasing it from 68.0% to nearly 86.6%. When HeLa and HepG2 cells were treated with CDDP at 3 µg/mL, the cell viability decreased by approximately 22.3% and 22.0%, respectively (Fig. 4B and C). Notably, treatment with C3G@SeC NPs (5 µg/mL) alone also reduced the viability of HeLa and HepG2 cells by approximately 20.8% and 22.5%. When administered in combination, C3G@SeC NPs synergistically enhanced the antitumor activity of CDDP, decreasing cell viability by nearly 26.8% and 30.0%, which indicated that C3G@SeC NPs treatment did not affect the antitumor efficacy of CDDP. ROS production was measured using the DCFH-DA probe, and the fluorescence images were captured by confocal microscopy. The result indicated that C3G@SeC NPs treatment for 12 h significantly reduced CDDP-induced ROS production (Fig. 4D), and the analysis results of DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity over time are shown in Fig. 4E. CDDP treatment markedly increased ROS levels by approximately 101.7% compared to the control. Relative to the CDDP group, subsequent treatment with C3G@SeC NPs, SeC NPs, or C3G NPs reduced ROS levels by about 19.8%, 15.7%, and 11.4%, respectively, with only the C3G@SeC NPs group showing a statistically significant difference. Furthermore, the ROS level in the C3G@SeC NPs-alone treatment group even decreased by 14% compared to the control.

Previous studies have reported that ROS can disrupt mitochondrial structure and function, thereby exacerbating nephrotoxicity [5]. We further analyzed mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) by flow cytometry with the JC-1 probe. The results showed that CDDP treatment induced a significant increase in JC-1 (green) positive cells, indicating significant MMP depolarization, which was mitigated by C3G@SeC NPs, C3G NPs, and SeC NPs (Fig. 4F and G). C3G@SeC NPs exhibited the optimal MMP recovery effect. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the synthesized nanoparticles, particularly C3G@SeC NPs, effectively alleviate CDDP cytotoxicity by suppression of ROS-mediated mitochondrial damage in vitro.

C3G@SeC NPs inhibit ferroptosis in vitro

Accumulating evidence indicates that CDDP induces AKI by disrupting iron homeostasis [23], leading to massive iron ion accumulation, which subsequently triggers lipid peroxidation-mediated ferroptosis [24]. We employed systematic pharmacological interventions to investigate the ferroptosis-inhibitory potential of C3G@SeC NPs. As shown in Fig. 5A, pretreatment with the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO), ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) [25], or C3G@SeC NPs (5 µg/mL) effectively rescued CDDP-induced HK-2 cell viability reduction from 43.32% to 31.51%, 35.87%, and 27.89%, respectively, alleviating CDDP cytotoxicity. Moreover, C3G@SeC NPs (5 µg/mL), DFO, and Fer-1 significantly rescued RSL3(4 µg/mL)-induced cell viability reduction from 53.57% to 23.23%, 37.56%, and 40.64%, respectively, thereby alleviating ferroptosis in HK-2 cells (Fig. 5B). RSL3 is a reported ferroptosis activator [26–28]. Strikingly, C3G@SeC NPs exhibited the most potent protective efficacy compared to DFO and Fer-1.

Considering the impact of ferroptosis on mitochondrial dysfunction [28], we measured MMP using a JC-1 probe. As expected, RSL3 treatment induced severe MMP depolarization, which was mitigated by C3G@SeC NPs and DFO (Fig. 5C and D). Mito-Tracker imaging further confirmed these results (Fig. S1). Furthermore, the RhoNox-1 probe (red) was employed to detect RSL3-induced iron ion accumulation [29], and RSL3 treatment induced intense red fluorescence compared to the control, which was significantly attenuated by C3G@SeC NPs or DFO (Fig. 5E and F). Subsequently, quantitative analysis of Liperfluo fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5G) and corresponding imaging (Fig. S2) showed that C3G@SeC NPs and DFO effectively inhibited RSL3-induced lipid peroxidation.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) is one of the end products of lipid peroxidation, and the results showed that C3G@SeC NPs inhibited RSL3-induced MDA elevation (Fig. 5H). Additionally, C3G@SeC NPs rescued RSL3-induced depletion of the antioxidant proteins GSH (Fig. 5I) and GPX4 (Fig. 5J) [30]. Collectively, C3G@SeC NPs alleviated RSL3-induced lipid peroxidation-dependent ferroptosis, reduced MDA production, and restored the expression of antioxidant proteins.

C3G@SeC NPs alleviate CDDP-induced AKI in vivo

To evaluate the nephroprotective efficacy of nanoparticles against CDDP-induced AKI in vivo, we established an AKI model in mice (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B, mice pretreated with saline (control), C3G@SeC NPs, C3G NPs, or SeC NPs for 4 days showed no significant difference in body weight compared to the control. However, intraperitoneal CDDP injection (15 mg/kg) resulted in a significant reduction in body weight across all treatment groups. Notably, oral administration of C3G@SeC NPs, C3G NPs, or SeC NPs partially attenuated CDDP-induced weight loss. Histopathological analysis of H&E staining revealed severe renal damage in the CDDP group (Fig. 6C), characterized by severe tubular epithelial cell necrosis, glomerular congestion, and interstitial edema [31], and treatment with nanoparticles significantly improved renal tissue injury. Furthermore, the elevated renal biochemical markers, including UREA, CREA, and UA, were reduced in response to the nanoparticle treatment (Fig. 6D–F). C3G@SeC NPs (2 mg/kg) exhibited the most potent protective effects. Hepatic function markers (AST, ALT) were similarly reduced by nanoparticle treatment (Fig. 6G and H).

C3G@SeC NPs regulate immune cells in the kidney and spleen tissues

The literature on AKI has highlighted that during CDDP treatment, the infiltration of immune cells into the kidney elicits inflammatory responses, which may exacerbate tissue damage and contribute to the progression of AKI. Massive immune cells are recruited to the injured kidney tissues, where macrophages play crucial roles in the innate immune system [32]. Among these, the M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory/reparative) phenotypes represent two classical polarization states that play opposing roles in the process of kidney injury. In the early stages of renal injury, M1 macrophages dominate, releasing large amounts of inflammatory cytokines and recruiting more immune cells to the site of damage [33]. During the middle and late phases of kidney injury, macrophages gradually polarize toward the M2 phenotype. These M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, which not only suppress excessive inflammatory responses but also inhibit tubular cell apoptosis [34]. T cells, including CD4⁺ T cells, can differentiate into distinct subtypes [35], such as Th1 cells or Th2 cells, and secrete cytokines to sustain inflammation. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are essential for immune suppression, which can attenuate inflammation and regulate oxidative stress [36]. Moreover, NK cells exacerbate kidney damage through cytotoxic mediators, such as perforin and granzymes, which target injured tubular epithelial cells [37].

In the present study, we analyzed the distribution of different types of immune cells in kidney tissues using flow cytometry (Fig. S3A). As shown in Fig. 7B and C, the populations of M2 macrophages (CD45⁺CD11b⁺F4/80⁺CD206⁺) and Treg cells (CD3⁺CD25⁺FOXP3⁺) were significantly reduced in the CDDP group compared to the control. Notably, treatments with C3G@SeC NPs, C3G NPs, or SeC NPs partially restored the populations of M2 macrophages and Treg cells, with C3G@SeC NPs (2 mg/kg) exhibiting the most potent recovery. Furthermore, the population of CD4⁺ T cells (CD3⁺CD4⁺) (Fig. 7E) was extremely increased in the CDDP group, and C3G@SeC NPs treatment significantly reduced it. No significant differences in the populations of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (CD45⁺CD11b⁺Gr-1⁺) (Fig. 7A) and NK cells (CD45⁺CD3⁻NK1.1⁺) (Fig. 7D) were observed across all the groups.

The spleen serves as an important reservoir for immune cells to maintain immune homeostasis. Flow cytometric analysis of immune cells in spleen tissues (Fig. S3B) indicated that the population of M2 macrophages (CD45⁺CD11b⁺F4/80⁺CDCD206⁺) showed no significant differences among all groups (Fig. 7G). However, the populations of MDSCs (Fig. 7H) and NK cells (Fig. 7F) were significantly reduced following CDDP treatment, which was reversed by C3G@SeC NPs treatment. Furthermore, we assessed the expression of inflammatory cytokines in kidney tissues. ELISA analysis indicated that the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, were significantly up-regulated in the CDDP group compared to the control, and these inflammatory levels were significantly attenuated by C3G@SeC NPs (Fig. 7I-J, L). No significant differences in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 7K) were observed across all groups. In addition, treatment with C3G@SeC NPs alone did not affect immune cell populations or inflammatory cytokine production. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that C3G@SeC NPs alleviate AKI by regulating immune cell infiltration and inflammatory responses.

C3G@SeC NPs inhibit CDDP-induced oxidative stress by regulating the MAPK pathway

Excessive renal oxidative stress induced by CDDP is a key mechanism of AKI, contributing to pathological processes including mitochondrial dysfunction, ferroptosis, and inflammatory cell activation. To characterize this, we first measured antioxidant protein expression in kidney tissues. As shown in Fig. 8A and B, CDDP treatment significantly reduced GSH and SOD levels compared to the control, whereas C3G@SeC NPs (2 mg/kg) restored them to near-normal levels. ELISA analysis further indicated that CDDP treatment significantly downregulated the expression of TrxR1, GPX4, and GPX1 in kidney tissues (Fig. 8C-E), which was reversed by C3G@SeC NPs. Furthermore, Western blot (WB) analysis (Fig. 8F) showed that C3G@SeC NPs rescued CDDP-induced reductions in SOD2 (Fig. 8G) and CAT (Fig. 8H) protein expression.

(F) WB analysis of CAT, SOD2, p-p38, p38, p-JNK, JNK, p-ERK, ERK, and β-actin protein expression levels in kidney tissues. Quantitative analysis of SOD2 (G), CAT (H), p-p38/p38 (I), p-JNK/JNK (J), and p-ERK/ERK (K) levels in Fig. 8F. Data are presented as means ± SD (n = 3)

To explore the protective mechanisms of C3G@SeC NPs, the phosphorylation levels of JNK, ERK, and P38 were analyzed by WB (Fig. 8F). As shown in Fig. 1, the MAPK pathway would be activated under oxidative stress conditions and participates in the regulation of antioxidant responses and cellular damage [38]. Strikingly, C3G@SeC NPs significantly inhibited CDDP-induced phosphorylation of p38, JNK, and ERK (Fig. 8I-K). C3G@SeC NPs treatment alone did not affect baseline antioxidant proteins or MAPK-related protein expression.

C3G@SeC NPs inhibit CDDP-induced ferroptosis in vivo

Ferroptosis has been reported to induce AKI via iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [39]. DAB-enhanced Prussian blue staining showed significantly elevated renal iron levels in the CDDP group, which were reduced by C3G@SeC NPs (Fig. 9A). Immunohistochemical staining for 4-HNE (a lipid peroxidation marker) showed a similar trend to iron accumulation, and the lipid peroxidation levels were increased in the CDDP group and reduced by nanoparticle treatment. Furthermore, ELISA analysis of MDA levels was upregulated in the CDDP group and restored to near-control levels by nanoparticle treatment, particularly C3G@SeC NPs (Fig. 9C). As shown in Fig. 1, CDDP induced ferroptosis by regulating iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and related proteins [40]. Thus, WB analysis was employed to evaluate ferroptosis-associated proteins, and the results indicated that CDDP treatment significantly downregulated the expression of Nrf2, HO-1, xCT, and GPX4 (Fig. 9B and D), and C3G@SeC NPs rescued the expression of these proteins. Conversely, CDDP treatment significantly increased the expression of Keap1 and ACSL4 (Fig. 9B and D). Keap1 is reported as a regulator of Nrf2, and ACSL4 promotes ferroptosis by enhancing arachidonic acid incorporation into membrane phospholipids. Both Keap1 and ACSL4 were downregulated by C3G@SeC NPs, further confirming the role of C3G@SeC NPs in alleviating AKI via ferroptosis inhibition.

Se exists in the human body primarily as SeC and selenomethionine (Se-Met) [41, 42], and it can inhibit AKI by regulating intracellular redox homeostasis. Specifically, SeC regulates iron metabolism by modulating ferritin and transferrin receptor expression, thereby inhibiting iron overload-induced Fenton reactions and ferroptosis [43]. Measured by high-performance liquid chromatography-inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (HPLC-ICP-MS), SeC levels in the kidney decreased by nearly 50% in the CDDP group compared to the control (Fig. 9E and F) and significantly increased after C3G@SeC NPs, SeC NPs, and C3G NPs treatment. Notably, C3G@SeC NPs treatment increased SeC levels by approximately 4-fold compared to the CDDP group. This restoration of SeC levels may be critical for alleviating CDDP-induced AKI. In addition, C3G@SeC NPs treatment alone did not affect the baseline expression of ferroptosis-related proteins, MDA levels, or SeC content.

Preparation of nanoparticles

Add 3 mL of polyacrylic acid (PAA) (4 mg/mL in water), 3 mL of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) (6 mg/mL in water), and 1.2 mL of Sect. (16.7 mg/mL in 1 M NH3-H2O). Adjust the pH to about 4.8 with 0.8 mL Sect. (13 mg/mL dissolved in 1 M HCl). Add 0.8 mL TA (3.5 mg/mL in water), adjust pH to 4 with HCl, and stir at 250 rpm for 12 h. Centrifuge the mixture at 12,000 rpm for 15 min and suspend in 6 mL of H2O to obtain the SeC NPs material.

Add 1.5 mL of PVP (6 mg/mL), 400 µL of TA (3.5 mg/mL), and 2.5 mL of C3G (10 mg/mL), and stir the mixture at 250 rpm for 10 min. Centrifuge the mixture at 12,000 rpm for 15 min and resuspend in 2 mL of H2O to obtain the C3G NPs material.

Take 6 mL of the mixture containing the above synthesized SeC NPs, make it up to 18 mL with H2O, adjust the pH to about 4 with HCl, add 6.9 mL of C3G (10 mg/mL), and stir it at 250 rpm for 3 min to obtain the mixture containing C3G@SeC NPs. Centrifuge the mixture at 12,000 rpm for 15 min and resuspend in 2 mL of H2O to obtain pure C3G@SeC NPs [44].

Determination of encapsulation efficiency

The nanoparticles were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant containing free C3G was collected. The amount of C3G in the supernatant and wash solution was determined by the pH difference method. The encapsulation efficiency of C3G was calculated by adding potassium chloride buffer solution (pH 1.0) and sodium acetate buffer solution (pH 4.5), and measuring the absorbance at 520 nm and 700 nm in the supernatant and wash solution. The precipitates and supernatants of SeC NPs and SeC@C3G NPs were digested with aqua regia and analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) to determine their contents, and the SeC encapsulation efficiency was calculated.

Characterization of nanoparticles

The hydrodynamic particle size and zeta potential of various nanosystems were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano. Subsequently, the morphology and size of the nanoparticles were assessed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of samples stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.0) [45, 46].

Evaluation of the particle size stability of nanoparticles

Each type of nanoparticle (SeC NPs, C3G NPs, and C3G@SeC NPs) was suspended in water, and their particle sizes were measured daily using a Malvern particle size analyzer over 7 days to assess stability.

Cell culture and cell viability assay

Human kidney-2 cells (HK-2), human cervical cancer cells (HeLa), and human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). HK-2 and cancer cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 and DMEM medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To study the effects of different drugs on cell viability, 1 × 104 cells/well were seeded into a 96-well plate. After 24 h, the cells were pre-incubated with different materials for 3 h and then treated with CDDP or RSL3 for 24 h. The cell culture medium was removed, and 100 µL of CCK-8 solution was added and incubated for 1–2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cell viability was calculated relative to control cells [47].

Detection of cell cycle distribution

HK-2 cell cycle assays were performed as previously described [48, 49]. Briefly, HK-2 cells were incubated with anthocyanin (40 µM) or DMSO for 3 h and then treated with CDDP or control (water) for 24 h. Cells were collected, washed three times with cold PBS, centrifuged, resuspended in pre-cooled 70% ethanol, fixed overnight at −20 °C, centrifuged again to discard the supernatant, and washed twice with pre-cooled PBS. Cells were then incubated with propidium iodide staining (PI) solution for 30 min at 4 °C before analysis by flow cytometry (Epics XL, Beckman Coulter). 10,000 events were recorded per sample, and each experiment was repeated at least three times. Data were analyzed using Multicycle software.

Detection of cellular ROS

HK-2 cells were seeded into 96-well black plates and pre-incubated with different nanoparticles for 4 h. After treatment with 6 µg/mL CDDP for 24 h, cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA probe for 30 min at 37 °C, washed twice with PBS to remove excess probe, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader at excitation 488 nm and emission 525 nm. Images of ROS were captured using a fluorescence microscope [50].

Mitochondrial morphology and membrane potential analysis

HK-2 cells were pretreated with protective agents for 3 h and then exposed to CDDP (6 µg/mL) or RSL3 (3 µg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were washed once with PBS and incubated with JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 20 min. After washing once with PBS, 10,000 events were analyzed by flow cytometry using FL1 (green) and FL2 (red) channels to detect JC-1 monomers and aggregates [51], respectively.

HK-2 cells treated with protective agents and RSL3 were incubated with Mito-Tracker for 2 h at 37 °C, washed twice with warm PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min. Hoechst 33,342 (1 µg/mL) was added, and cells were incubated in the dark for 15 min. After three washes with PBS, fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope with 488 nm (Mito-Tracker) and 405 nm (Hoechst) lasers.

Detection of intracellular lipid peroxides

HK-2 cells were pretreated with protective materials for 3 h and subsequently treated with RSL3 for 24 h. Following this, the cells were incubated with Liperfluo (5 µM at 37 °C for 30 min). After removing the staining solution, cells were incubated with Hoechst 33342 (1 µg/mL) for 15 min. The cells were then washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, and fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope to capture multiple fields of view.

Detection of cellular iron ions

HK-2 cells were treated with the protective materials and RSL3 as previously described, followed by 1 µM RhoNox-1 treatment for 30 min [29]. Cells were then incubated with Hoechst 33342 dye for 20 min, washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, and fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope across multiple fields of view.

Animal experiments

Male C57/BL6J mice (6 weeks old) were randomly divided into seven groups based on body weight: Control group, CDDP group, CDDP + C3G@SeC NPs (Sect. 1 mg/kg), CDDP + C3G@SeC NPs (Sect. 2 mg/kg), CDDP + SeC NPs (Sect. 2 mg/kg), CDDP + C3G NPs (C3G 50 mg/kg), and C3G@SeC NPs (Sect. 2 mg/kg) alone. During the 8-day experimental period, mice received saline or the corresponding protective agents via oral gavage daily. On day 4, mice were administered CDDP (15 mg/kg) or saline, and body weight changes were recorded daily. Mice were sacrificed on day 9, and tissues were collected for subsequent analyses.

Nephrotoxicity analysis in vivo

Serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (UREA), serum creatinine (CREA), and uric acid (UA) were measured using a biochemical analyzer. In addition, SOD, GSH, and MDA levels in kidney tissue and HK-2 cells were determined using commercial kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and Prussian blue staining of kidney tissues

Enhanced Prussian blue staining was used to detect iron deposition in kidney tissue as described previously [52]. Paraffin tissue Sect. (4 μm thickness) were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100 − 70%), washed with PBS, incubated in a mixture of equal parts 2% hydrochloric acid and 2% potassium ferrocyanide for 30 min, washed again with PBS, counterstained with nuclear fast red, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Iron deposition was observed by light microscopy.

For immunohistochemical analysis, sections were incubated overnight with 5% bovine serum albumin and the 4-HNE target antibody, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies. Finally, the distribution of labeled 4-HNE on the sections was observed by light microscopy.

Immune cell analysis

Immune cell analysis was performed as previously described [53]. The freshly harvested spleen and kidney tissues were homogenized, lysed, and then washed with PBS. Subsequently, both the cell surface and the intracellular components were stained using antibodies specific to CD3, CD4, CD45, CD11b, Gr-1, CD25, FoxP3, NK1.1, F4/80, and CD206 for 30 min at 4 °C. Samples were analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, and data were processed using Cytexpert software.

WB analysis

WB analysis was performed as previously described [54]. Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30–50 µg) were mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer, boiled at 95 °C for 5 min, separated by 10–12% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies (diluted in blocking buffer) overnight at 4 °C, washed with TBST, and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, and band intensities were quantified using the Clinx ChemiScope imaging system and associated software. β-actin was used as an internal control.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Student’s t-test was used to analyze the differences between the two groups. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed using SPSS v17.0 (Windows). Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 vs. control group or #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001, ####P < 0.0001 vs. CDDP group.

Discussion

The CDDP metabolized in the kidneys can induce excessive ROS production through multiple pathways, which is the core trigger for AKI. On one hand, ROS can attack polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes, triggering lipid peroxidation reactions that directly lead to renal tubular epithelial cell dysfunction or even death. Meanwhile, ROS-mediated lipid peroxidation products (MDA) and Fe²⁺ accumulation synergistically induce ferroptosis, further exacerbating renal tissue damage [55, 56]. On the other hand, ROS and cell damage signals activate a strong inflammatory response, recruiting a large number of immune cells to infiltrate the kidneys, forming a vicious cycle of renal injury.

Previous studies have confirmed that C3G possesses potent antioxidant activity and can effectively inhibit lipid peroxidation-dependent ferroptosis [57]. SeC is an indispensable trace element in human tissues, primarily serving as the active center component of antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidases (GPXs) and thioredoxin reductases (TRs), which can specifically scavenge hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxides [58]. To address the clinical challenge of limited treatment options for CDDP-induced AKI during cancer chemotherapy [59], this study successfully synthesized novel functional nanoparticles (C3G@SeC NPs) that combine the antioxidant properties of anthocyanins with the biological functions of SeC through a stirring method mediated by PAA-PVP copolymers. Characterization using various techniques confirmed that these nanoparticles achieve synchronous loading of C3G and SeC. Further experiments demonstrated that C3G@SeC NPs exhibit good stability in aqueous solution, laying the foundation for subsequent studies on bioactivity and mechanisms of action. In vitro cell experiments showed that C3G@SeC NPs significantly alleviated CDDP-induced decrease in viability of human HK-2 cells by scavenging intracellular ROS, reducing mitochondrial damage, and regulating cell cycle arrest. Furthermore, these nanoparticles also inhibited RSL3-induced iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and MDA production in HK-2 cells, thereby blocking ferroptosis-mediated apoptosis.

In vivo studies further clarified the therapeutic potential of C3G@SeC NPs in AKI. In a CDDP-induced AKI mouse model, treatment with C3G@SeC NPs led to a significant recovery trend in biochemical markers of renal function and markedly reduced renal histopathological damage. Mechanistic investigations revealed that, compared to the CDDP group, C3G@SeC NPs maintained renal oxidative homeostasis by regulating the phosphorylation levels of MAPK pathway proteins. Within the ferroptosis pathway, C3G@SeC NPs not only significantly reduced renal iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and MDA generation but also upregulated the expression of ferroptosis-related protective proteins such as Nrf2, HO-1, xCT, and GPX4 [60], while downregulating the expression of Keap-1 and ACSL4. These results suggest that C3G@SeC NPs alleviate AKI by reducing iron accumulation and inhibiting lipid peroxidation-dependent ferroptosis. Notably, C3G@SeC NPs also further reduced renal injury in AKI mice by downregulating the number of CD4⁺ T cells involved in immune response and inflammation regulation in renal tissues, while simultaneously significantly upregulating the proportions of Treg cells and M2 macrophages. In addition to the immune cells analyzed in the paper, CDDP-induced AKI may also affect the distribution of neutrophils, CD8⁺ T cells, and B cells, which could participate in renal injury through mechanisms such as immune infiltration, direct cytotoxic effects, or antibody production [61]. This is also worthy of further investigation.

Although nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems show great potential in alleviating CDDP nephrotoxicity, nanoparticles still face numerous challenges in practical applications. First, the metabolic pathways and degradation products of nanoparticles within the body remain incompletely understood, necessitating more long-term and systematic safety evaluations. Additionally, the majority of nanomedicines suffer from low targeting efficiency and poor specificity, failing to demonstrate improved therapeutic efficacy in clinical practice [62]. Therefore, further research is urgently needed to overcome the potential limitations of nanoparticles.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully synthesized a novel functional nanoparticle aimed at alleviating CDDP nephrotoxicity during cancer treatment. In vitro experiments demonstrated that C3G@SeC NPs treatment reduced damage in HK-2 cells and significantly restored cell viability compared to the CDDP group. In vivo studies further confirmed that C3G@SeC NPs treatment markedly alleviated renal tissue damage and improved biochemical markers of renal function in AKI mice. Mechanistically, the protective effects of C3G@SeC NPs were primarily associated with the amelioration of oxidative stress, regulation of MAPK pathway protein phosphorylation, modulation of ferroptosis-related protein expression, and influence on the renal immune microenvironment. This functional nanomedicine intervention strategy, based on the synergistic regulatory mechanism of C3G and SeC, provides a highly promising therapeutic approach for the prevention and treatment of CDDP-associated AKI.
