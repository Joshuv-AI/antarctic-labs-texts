Comparing the impact of video feedback and group discussion on patient education performance among medical interns: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Seyed Esmaeil Managheb, Neda Pouralimohamadi, Kaveh Taghipour, Seyedeh Nastaran Managheb, Hossein Hosseini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective patient education is critical in managing chronic diseases, improving patient outcomes, and reducing healthcare burdens. This study aimed to compare the impact of video feedback (VFB) and group discussion (GD) training on the patient education performance of medical interns.MethodIn this double-blind randomized controlled trial, 78 medical interns at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (2019) were assigned to VFB or GD groups using closed-envelope randomization. Both groups attended six 2-hour workshops with standardized scenarios. Performance was assessed using the Objective Structured Long Examination Record (OSLER) checklist before (OSLER1) and one month after the intervention (OSLER2). Primary outcome was the difference in mean post-intervention OSLER scores. Analyses included Mann-Whitney U and Wilcoxon tests.ResultSeventy-six interns completed the study. No significant differences in demographic features (p > 0.05) or baseline scores (p = 0.21). Both groups demonstrated significant improvements in OSLER scores after the intervention (P < 0.001). The GD group’s mean OSLER1 score increased from 4.38 ± 1.04 to 8.43 ± 1.85 (p < 0.001), while the VFB group’s scores rose from 5.10 ± 2.09 to 17.44 ± 1.10 (p < 0.001). The VFB group significantly outperformed the GD group in post-intervention scores (p < 0.001), with a mean score improvement of 12.33 ± 2.53 compared to 4.05 ± 2.05 in the GD group (p < 0.001).ConclusionBoth training methods improved interns’ patient education performance, with VFB showing substantially greater gains. Video feedback may be a more effective approach for enhancing communication skills in clinical training. Further research is warranted to assess long-term impact and broader applicability.Trial registrationA clinical trial number is not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07748-w.Keywords: Video feedback, Patient education, Performance assessment, Group discussion, Medical educational technology

Introduction

Effective patient education is a cornerstone of clinical practice, directly influencing patient adherence, understanding, and health outcomes [1]. Despite its critical importance, the optimal methods for training medical interns in patient education remain a topic of debate, particularly with the advent of innovative teaching tools such as video feedback and group discussions [2, 4]. Traditional face-to-face teaching methods are increasingly being complemented by novel strategies aimed at enhancing clinical competence, communication skills, and patient interaction [5].

Video feedback has gained considerable attention as an educational tool for improving clinical skills. For example, a 2024 study by Wang et al. [6] highlighted the benefits of combining video feedback with peer role-playing, reporting substantial improvements in learning efficiency, interpersonal communication, and clinical competencies among medical students. Similarly, Lehmann et al. [7] examined various applications of video feedback in surgical training, noting its potential to enhance technical skills. However, the study found that the advantages of video feedback over traditional oral feedback were not consistently evident, suggesting that its effectiveness may depend on the context and type of skills being taught. This underscores the need for further investigation into the specific benefits of video feedback, particularly in areas such as patient education.

Conversely, group discussion has long been recognized as a key element of interactive learning, fostering collaborative exploration of clinical cases, knowledge exchange, and reinforcement through dialogue [8, 10]. Studies such as those by Shafian et al. [11] and Chamberland et al. [12] emphasized the value of structured discussions in promoting critical thinking, reflection, and diagnostic accuracy. Despite its benefits, group discussion is not without limitations. Medical trainees often report receiving feedback that is untimely, insufficiently detailed, or inadequately structured in clinical settings, highlighting the need for improved feedback delivery methods to maximize its educational potential.

While both video feedback and group discussions have shown promise in improving educational outcomes, discrepancies in their comparative effectiveness remain unresolved. For instance, Wang et al. [6] demonstrated the advantages of video feedback in enhancing communication and clinical skills, whereas Zhang et al. [13] raised concerns about the variability in quality and accuracy of peer-generated feedback, which can impact its efficacy. Similarly, although group discussions foster deeper understanding and collaborative learning, they may not provide the same level of individualized, targeted feedback that video-based methods offer [10]. These conflicting findings reveal a critical gap in the literature, particularly regarding their effectiveness in enhancing patient education performance, a domain that demands both refined communication skills and clinical competence.

This study aims to address this gap by comparing the effectiveness of video feedback and group discussions in improving patient education performance among medical interns. It is hypothesized that video feedback, through its ability to provide individualized and visual feedback on real-time interactions, may offer superior benefits for refining patient communication skills. However, the collaborative nature of group discussions, which promotes peer-to-peer learning and reflection, may also provide unique advantages in enhancing patient education. By employing the Objective Structured Long Examination Record (OSLER) as an objective measure of educational outcomes, this study seeks to provide evidence-based insights into the relative efficacy of these two teaching methodologies.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This interventional, double-blind, prospective study was conducted among medical interns at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences in 2019. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (ID: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1398.304). Due to the educational nature of the interventions, the randomized controlled trial (RCT) design was approved locally by the same committee on July 28, 2019 (ID: 1398.304). Hence, a clinical trial number is not applicable. The study adheres to the CONSORT 2010 guidelines to ensure transparency and rigor in reporting. The primary objective was to compare the effectiveness of video feedback and group discussion training on the patient education performance of medical interns.

The sample size was calculated as 34 participants per group, using a confidence level of 95%, a test power of 80%, and an effect size of 0.7. With two groups, a total of 78 participants were included. Initial recruitment utilized convenient sampling, after which randomization into two groups was performed by an independent statistician, using a closed-envelope method to ensure allocation concealment.

Eligible participants were medical interns at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences who had completed internal medicine rotations and had at least three months remaining in their internship. A total of 78 interns provided informed consent and were randomized into two groups of 39 each. Interns unable to fully participate in the training sessions were excluded (n = 2). The Group Discussion (GD) group attended interactive workshops focused on group discussions, while the Video Feedback (VFB) group participated in workshops that incorporated video feedback on patient interactions.

Baseline patient education skills were assessed using a modified version of the Objective Structured Long Examination Record (OSLER). The assessment involved a standardized patient presenting with type 2 diabetes (see Additional file 1). Following the baseline assessment, participants were randomized into the two intervention groups.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed by an independent statistician using a closed-envelope method. To minimize potential bias, both groups attended workshops held in the same location, where hidden cameras recorded sessions. Participants were unaware that cameras were recording, ensuring study integrity and reducing the Hawthorne effect [10]. Both participants and assessor were blinded to group allocations.

Assessment tools

The effectiveness of patient education methods was evaluated using the modified OSLER, a validated instrument for assessing clinical performance. The OSLER checklist included 20 binary items, with each task scored as either 1 (completed) or 0 (not completed) [14, 15]. Total scores ranged from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating better performance.

To ensure the validity of the modified OSLER, its content and face validity were reviewed by a panel of ten expert professors. After incorporating their feedback, the checklist achieved a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 95%. The final version of the checklist is provided in see Additional file 2.

The assessments utilized standardized patients (SPs), who were amateur actors trained to reproduce a scripted scenario. These SPs were matched to the age, sex, height, and weight specifications of the scenario to maintain realism. Interns conducted history taking and physical examinations on the SPs, while their performance was observed and scored by an assessor using the OSLER checklist.

All assessments were conducted by a trained evaluator from the Education Development Center (EDC) at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. This evaluator, an experienced professional in clinical education assessments, underwent rigorous training on the standardized application of the OSLER checklist. The use of a single assessor ensured consistency and eliminated the need for inter-rater reliability analysis. The assessor’s selection criteria included professional qualifications from the EDC and prior experience in medical education assessments.

Interventions

Participants in the VFB group attended workshops that incorporated video-based feedback, while those in the GD group participated in workshops focused on structured group discussions. Both training methods utilized the same educational content, which was derived from credible sources [16, 18], and employed standardized scenarios approved by the university’s internal medicine department. Group Discussion (GD) workshops

The GD group participated in six two-hour sessions, each involving four participants per session. Sessions began with participants sharing their experiences with patient education, allowing the group to identify effective practices and common challenges. The instructor then delivered a brief presentation on essential patient education skills.

Role-playing exercises followed, in which one participant acted as the physician, another as the patient, and the remaining participants served as observers. After each scenario, observers provided peer feedback, and the instructor offered guidance using a patient-education feedback checklist (see Additional file 3). This process was repeated with different participants assuming alternate roles in new, predesigned scenarios. Each session comprised 15 min of scenario role-play by participants, followed by 15 min of group discussion and instructor feedback based on the patient-education feedback checklist.

Video Feedback (VFB) workshops

The VFB group also participated in six two-hour sessions with four participants per session. Similar to the GD workshops, the sessions covered the same educational content and utilized standardized scenarios. However, these sessions incorporated video recording and review as a key component of the training.

During each session, one participant acted as the physician, another as the patient, and the remaining two participants observed. The interactions were recorded using a pre-installed camera. After each role-play, participants independently reviewed the video footage of their interactions to identify their strengths and areas for improvement. They also assessed their own performance using the patient-education feedback checklist provided in the workshops (see Additional file 3).

Each scenario lasted 30 min: 15 min for role-play and 15 min for video review and feedback. The instructor was present to clarify the workshop patient-education checklist but was not involved in directly assessing participants or completing the checklist. The participants were responsible for independently evaluating their performance and completing the checklist.

Post-intervention assessment

At the end of the month, upon completion of the workshops, participants from both groups underwent a second OSLER test (OSLER2). This assessment involved a standardized patient presenting with primary hypertension, as described in Additional file 4. Participants were evaluated by an expert assessor who observed their performance and completed the OSLER2 checklist (see Additional file 1). To maintain objectivity, the assessor was unaware of the participants’ group allocations throughout the study.

The effectiveness of the interventions was determined by comparing OSLER1 and OSLER2 scores. All workshops and assessments were conducted in the conference hall of the Rasoul Akram Family Medicine Clinic in Shiraz, ensuring a consistent testing and training environment.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the difference in mean post-intervention OSLER scores between the two groups, measured one month after workshop completion (OSLER2). The secondary outcome was the change in mean OSLER scores from baseline to post-intervention (OSLER2 – OSLER1), reflecting the relative improvement in patient education performance attributable to the training methods. Both assessments utilized standardized patients presenting with predefined clinical scenarios (Additional files 1 and 4), approved by the university’s internal medicine department.

The anonymized dataset generated and analyzed during the current study is provided as supplementary material (see Additional file 5).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23. Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) summarized the data. Inferential analyses, including Fisher’s exact test, Mann-Whitney U test, and Wilcoxon signed-rank test, were employed to compare group outcomes. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed with nonparametric tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

As summarized in Table 1, there were no statistically significant differences between the group discussion (GD) and video feedback (VFB) groups in baseline demographic variables. The mean age was 25.38 ± 1.74 years in the GD group and 24.95 ± 0.39 years in the VFB group (p = 0.183). The GD group consisted of 62.2% males and 37.8% females, while the VFB group included 46.2% males and 53.8% females (p = 0.242). Internship duration did not significantly differ between groups (GD: 8.05 ± 3.64 months; VFB: 7.13 ± 3.69 months; p = 0.188). Baseline OSLER scores (OSLER1) were also comparable between the groups (GD: 4.38 ± 1.04; VFB: 5.10 ± 2.09; p = 0.213).

The mean OSLER1 scores were 5.10 (95% CI: 4.43–5.78) in the VFB group and 4.38 (95% CI: 4.03–4.72) in the GD group.

Discussion

This study evaluated the comparative effectiveness of video feedback (VFB) workshops and group discussions (GD) in improving patient education skills among medical interns at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. Our findings indicate that both interventions significantly enhanced interns’ patient education skills. However, the VFB group demonstrated substantially higher post-intervention OSLER scores, highlighting its superiority. These results align with the growing body of evidence advocating for the integration of innovative, technology-driven teaching methods in medical education [6, 12, 19, 20].

The remarkable improvement observed in the VFB group highlights the transformative potential of video feedback. Previous studies, such as Wang et al. [6], have shown that combining video feedback with peer role-playing enhances medical students’ interpersonal skills and learning efficiency. These findings corroborate our results, emphasizing that active learning strategies foster deeper engagement and retention of clinical skills. Furthermore, Wang et al. highlighted enhanced communication skills and professional confidence, suggesting a broader impact of video feedback interventions [6].

Video feedback offers distinct advantages, particularly its ability to facilitate personalized learning [12, 19, 20]. Hibbert et al. [21] emphasized that video feedback promotes self-reflection and self-regulation by allowing participants to assess their strengths and weaknesses in a structured manner. The visual nature of this feedback enables learners to contextualize their actions, fostering better retention and application of both technical and non-technical skills [21]. Additionally, Karkera et al. [22] noted that video feedback encourages self-directed learning by enabling learners to compare their performance against benchmarks, a feature mirrored in our study’s findings.

Despite these strengths, video feedback is not without limitations. One concern is cognitive overload due to the volume of information presented, which may overwhelm learners and impede their focus on key areas for improvement [22]. This challenge is supported by Karkera et al. [22], who observed that learners might fixate on minor details, hindering broader skill development. Moreover, while video feedback is a powerful visual tool, it lacks the interactivity and real-time engagement of group discussions, where immediate peer and facilitator feedback enhances understanding [23].

Group discussions, on the other hand, offer unique advantages. They foster critical thinking and active participation through peer-to-peer interactions. Badge et al. [23] emphasized the benefits of collaborative learning environments, where interns are exposed to diverse perspectives and are encouraged to critically evaluate clinical cases. Similarly, Naamati-Schneider et al. [4] demonstrated that group discussions enhance knowledge retention and promote deeper reflection, contributing to a more comprehensive clinical education.

However, the efficacy of group discussions can depend heavily on the facilitator’s skills and the level of participant engagement. Variability in participation and discussion quality may lead to unequal learning experiences among students. Karkera et al. [22] noted that poorly facilitated discussions could hinder the learning process, limiting their overall effectiveness. In contrast, video feedback provides a standardized and consistent learning experience, enabling participants to observe and learn from both their own performances and those of their peers [22].

An essential component of both workshops in our study was the incorporation of role-play, which allowed participants to simulate real-life clinical scenarios in a safe and structured environment. Role-playing facilitated active learning, enabling interns to practice essential communication skills, such as delivering complex medical information or navigating difficult patient conversations [24]. This experiential learning approach, combined with structured feedback from peers and facilitators, enhanced reflection and skill refinement. Existing literature supports the notion that role-playing is a valuable educational strategy in clinical training, facilitating active learning, enhancing retention, and preparing interns for real-world clinical challenges [24, 26]. These findings advocate for the broader integration of role-play within medical curricula to complement other active learning strategies, such as video feedback, and to enhance clinical competency across diverse educational contexts.

Our findings challenge previous studies that questioned the additional value of video feedback in clinical education. For instance, Lehmann et al. [7] reported no significant advantage of video-assisted feedback over oral feedback in surgical skill acquisition. However, our results suggest that the effectiveness of feedback depends on the context and the nature of the skill being taught. While video feedback may not significantly improve technical surgical skills, it appears to be more impactful in patient education, where communication and interaction are central. This finding underscores the need to tailor feedback methods to specific educational goals, a topic that warrants further investigation.

The study by Zhang et al. [13] supports our findings, confirming the efficacy of peer video feedback in skill-based learning despite students’ mixed feelings about its quality. An important feature of our intervention was the structured and controlled environment in which video feedback was provided, likely mitigating concerns about feedback accuracy, as noted in previous studies. By employing standardized scenarios and a systematic feedback process, we enhanced the reliability and effectiveness of this teaching method.

The significant improvement in scores observed in the VFB group compared to the GD group raises questions about the specific elements contributing to its effectiveness. One plausible explanation is the immediate and visual nature of video feedback, which allows participants to directly observe their interactions and make timely adjustments [12, 27]. Chamberland et al. [12] highlighted that immediate feedback enhances diagnostic performance, suggesting that timely, relevant feedback is critical in clinical education. This contrasts with the more abstract nature of feedback in group discussions, which may lack the same level of engagement and clarity.

The potential for video feedback to bridge gaps in clinical education, particularly in resource-constrained settings, has been demonstrated in other contexts as well. George et al. [27] reported that video demonstrations were non-inferior to bedside tutorials in teaching pediatric clinical skills, supporting the broader applicability of video-based learning across various medical disciplines. Likewise, Tantiphlachiva et al. [28] showed that video-recorded feedback was just as effective as face-to-face feedback in improving surgical skills, highlighting the feasibility of using video feedback as an alternative in settings where real-time instructor availability is limited.

Clinically, the implications of our study are significant. Effective patient education is a cornerstone of quality healthcare, especially in managing chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, where patient engagement and adherence are critical. Enhancing medical interns’ communication skills through video feedback can help cultivate a workforce of healthcare professionals better equipped to engage patients in shared decision-making and deliver patient-centered care [11]. Based on our findings, we recommend incorporating video feedback into standard medical curricula, particularly in areas emphasizing patient communication and education. A hybrid learning model that integrates video feedback with other active learning strategies, such as simulations and peer-assisted learning, could address diverse student learning needs while ensuring the retention of critical clinical competencies.

Study limitations and future directions

This study acknowledges several limitations. The small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences between groups, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, participants who opted to engage more actively with the video feedback may have been inherently more motivated, introducing potential selection bias. External factors such as prior training in communication or patient education skills were not assessed or controlled for and could have influenced the outcomes. Moreover, the absence of a standardized checklist for feedback evaluation and the lack of dedicated spaces for conducting workshops further constrained the study. Future research should aim to include larger, more diverse cohorts, utilize validated assessment tools, and control for background training and motivation to better understand the efficacy of video feedback. Tailoring feedback delivery to specific educational contexts may enhance its impact across various medical disciplines.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study suggests that video feedback workshops may contribute to the improvement of patient education skills among medical interns. While the findings are promising, they should be interpreted with caution in light of the study’s limitations. Future research should explore the long-term retention of these skills, their impact on patient outcomes, and the integration of video feedback into broader educational curricula. Embracing innovative and evidence-informed teaching methods remains essential to better prepare future healthcare professionals for the complex demands of modern medical practice.
