Hot spots and frontiers in autistic traits in the general population from 2008 to 2023: A bibliometric analysis
Authors: Yue Wang, Yuan Zhao, Wan Suraya Binti Wan Nik, Chenghui Tan, Feng Lu, Qiqi Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Autistic traits are increasingly conceptualized as continuously distributed characteristics present across the general population rather than being confined to clinically diagnosed autism spectrum disorder. Over the past 2 decades, research on autistic traits has expanded rapidly, yet a comprehensive overview of its knowledge structure, developmental trajectory, and emerging frontiers remains lacking. This study aims to systematically map the research landscape on autistic traits in the general population and to identify key themes, evolutionary patterns, and research frontiers.Methods:A bibliometric analysis was conducted using publications indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection from 2008 to 2023. CiteSpace and VOSviewer were employed to analyze co-authorship, co-citation, keyword co-occurrence, and burst terms. Standard parameter settings were applied to ensure analytical robustness, and exploratory adjustments confirmed the stability of the overall network structure. Thematic clusters and temporal evolution were examined to reveal the conceptual organization and dynamic development of the field.Results:The results indicate a steady growth in publications on autistic traits in the general population since 2008. Four major thematic domains were identified: measurement of autistic traits and individual differences, autistic traits and comorbidity, biological and genetic mechanisms, and cognitive and behavioral characteristics. Burst term analysis revealed a shift in recent years toward outcome-oriented and socially relevant topics, with sex differences and quality of life emerging as prominent research frontiers. These trends reflect increasing attention to sex-sensitive measurement, psychosocial functioning, and lived experience beyond categorical diagnosis.Conclusions:This bibliometric study provides a comprehensive overview of the intellectual structure and evolving frontiers of research on autistic traits in the general population. The findings highlight a conceptual transition from trait measurement and mechanistic exploration toward functional outcomes, well-being, and sex-sensitive perspectives. These insights may inform future research directions, measurement refinement, and the development of more inclusive frameworks for understanding autistic traits across diverse populations.

1. Introduction

Autistic traits are not exclusive to individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) but are also widely distributed across the general population, forming a continuous spectrum. Individuals exhibiting higher levels of these traits without a clinical ASD diagnosis are often referred to as the high autistic traits group or the subclinical autistic group.[1-4] Baron-Cohen et al[5] proposed that autistic traits exist along a continuum, meaning that everyone may exhibit certain autism-related characteristics – such as social difficulties, communication challenges, restricted interests, and repetitive behaviors – to varying degrees. To assess these traits, they developed the autism spectrum quotient (AQ). Furthermore, research has indicated that individuals with high levels of autistic traits in the general population share notable similarities with those diagnosed with ASD.[6] These similarities include reduced sensitivity to social cognition,[7] poor cognitive flexibility,[8] and decreased automatic processing of emotional prosody.[9] However, unlike individuals who are formally diagnosed with ASD, those with autistic traits typically do not experience severe functional impairments. Instead, these traits tend to manifest in milder forms within the general population.[10]

Autistic traits refer to a set of behavioral characteristics and cognitive patterns associated with ASD that are also present in the general population. These traits are widely distributed and exist along a continuum.[11,12] Meng et al,[13] through a questionnaire survey involving 1000 college students, found that with an average AQ score of 22.01, approximately 6.4% of the students exhibited high levels of autistic traits. These findings suggest that autistic trait levels among college students are moderate, with a relatively high proportion exhibiting elevated traits. Similarly, multiple studies have estimated the prevalence of high levels of autistic traits in the general population to range from 5% to 25%, with the highest rates observed in young adults.[14,15] In contrast, the prevalence among children is significantly lower, ranging from 1.4% to 3.31%.[16,17] Additionally, recent research has indicated an increasing trend in the prevalence of high levels of autistic traits among the general population.[17] Given these trends, reviewing the historical development of research on autistic traits in the general population, identifying key research topics, and analyzing emerging trends are essential.

Over the past 15 years, researchers have sought to understand how subclinical autistic traits influence daily functioning, interpersonal relationships, and learning outcomes. Significant progress has been made in psychology, education, cognitive behavioral science, and public health regarding this topic. However, systematic reviews and analyses of research trends remain relatively limited. Bibliometric analysis is widely used for evaluating scientific research, tracking discipline development, and mapping knowledge structures. This method quantitatively examines the volume, quality, structure, distribution, and development trends of literature, offering insights into the current state, key areas, and frontiers of academic research in a given field.[18] To provide a comprehensive and scientifically grounded overview of research on autistic traits in the general population, this study employed CiteSpace and VOSviewer software to conduct a visual analysis of the key aspects of literature, including authorship, institutional affiliations, and keyword distributions. The aim was to systematically map the development and research hotspots in this field over the past 15 years, thereby offering a theoretical reference for future research directions and scholarly contributions on a global scale.

2. Research methods and data sources

2.1. Data sources

The data for this study were retrieved from the Social Sciences Citation Index and the Science Citation Index Expanded, both of which are integral components of the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC). The literature search spanned the period from 2008 to 2023. In bibliometric research, a temporal window of approximately 15 years is widely regarded as methodologically and empirically appropriate, as it strikes a balance between citation maturity, the identification of thematic evolution, and analytical feasibility.[19-21] Prior to 2008, research on autistic traits in the general population remained relatively limited and fragmented, with sparse publication records in certain years, thereby limiting the formation of stable citation networks required for robust bibliometric analysis. Since 2008, the increasing adoption of dimensional assessment frameworks, particularly the AQ, has coincided with a sustained growth in research output and the gradual consolidation of thematic and collaborative structures. Accordingly, selecting 2008 as the starting point enables a more reliable examination of both the longitudinal development and emerging research frontiers in this field. On December 21, 2024, the literature search was independently conducted by 2 researchers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with 2 additional researchers. 424 publications related to autistic traits in the general population were identified and included in the bibliometric and visual mapping analyses. 2.2. Research methods

2.2.1. Research tools

This study employs CiteSpace 6.4.1 (Advance) and VOSviewer 1.6.20 to conduct a visual bibliometric analysis of the literature. CiteSpace, developed by Professor Chaomei Chen at Drexel University, is a widely used scientometric and visualization tool designed for academic literature analysis. Based on the Java platform, it is primarily applied to co-citation analysis, keyword co-occurrence analysis, keyword clustering, burst detection, and journal dual-map overlays analysis. Through the analysis of large-scale bibliographic data, CiteSpace allows researchers to systematically identify the developmental trajectory, research hotspots, and emerging trends within a specific field. It has therefore been extensively adopted in scientific research evaluation and intelligence analysis.[22] VOSviewer, developed by Van Eck and Waltman at Leiden University, is another prominent bibliometric and knowledge visualization tool. Also implemented in Java, VOSviewer focuses on the construction and visualization of scientific knowledge networks and is widely applied in bibliometrics,[23] scientometrics, and information science research.[24] According to Hu et al,[25] although VOSviewer and CiteSpace share overlapping analytical functions, VOSviewer demonstrates greater efficiency in processing large-scale datasets and offers more refined graphical visualization capabilities. However, its types of visual analytical maps are comparatively limited. Therefore, this study integrates CiteSpace and VOSviewer to construct a more comprehensive and complementary knowledge map of research on autistic traits in the general population.

2.2.2. Data analysis methods

In the visual knowledge maps generated by CiteSpace and VOSviewer, the size of each node represents its frequency of occurrence, while the links between nodes indicate co-occurrence relationships. The thickness of these links reflects the the frequency or strength of co-occurrence. In CiteSpace, centrality is a key metric used to evaluate the structural importance of a node within the network. It is commonly employed to assess the influence and significance of a node within the knowledge map. Higher centrality values indicate greater structural significance. Nodes with centrality values exceeding 0.1 are generally regarded as hub (key) nodes and are visually emphasized with purple rings. Centrality is automatically computed for all nodes, and no user-defined threshold is required during the calculation process. Instead, centrality values are applied at the interpretation stage to identify structurally important nodes that connect different thematic clusters. Adjusting the interpretive threshold primarily affects visual emphasis rather than altering the underlying network structure.

The term “burst terms” refers to keywords or phrases that exhibit a sudden surge in usage within a specific period. Such terms are often associated with research hotspots, emerging topics, or major academic developments, thereby reflecting dynamic shifts within a research field. Their occurrence suggests rapid knowledge expansion and may signal emerging research directions or trending issues. Importantly, burst terms are not merely high-frequency keywords observed over a short interval. Instead, they demonstrate strong temporal significance and sustained relevance. Their abrupt emergence and persistence provide valuable insights into the evolving research landscape. In burst detection, the γ parameter was employed to regulate the sensitivity of the burst detection algorithm. Lower γ values tend to identify a larger number of burst terms, including those with shorter duration or lower intensities, whereas higher γ values preferentially capture more prominent and temporally sustained bursts.

Additionally, this study applies the log-likelihood ratio algorithm to classify and rank keyword clusters. Within this framework, smaller cluster label numbers correspond to larger and more significant clusters, thereby offering stronger explanatory power for the research domain. Cluster “#0” typically represents the most important or mainstream research theme. The cluster label is determined by the keyword with the highest log-likelihood ratio value within that cluster. In CiteSpace knowledge map analysis, Modularity Q and Silhouette S are 2 critical metrics for assessing clustering quality and the structural validity of the map. A Modularity Q score above 0.3 indicates a well-structured network with clearly differentiated clusters. A Silhouette S score above 0.5 reflects acceptable clustering quality, indicating high intra-cluster similarity and improved interpretability. A Silhouette S score above 0.7 indicates highly reliable clustering, meaning strong internal consistency among nodes. Conversely, a Silhouette S score below 0 suggests poor clustering results, indicating low reliability.

4. Discussion

In this study, publications on autistic traits in the general population from 2008 to 2023 were analyzed using CiteSpace and VOSviewer based on the WoSCC database. Both annual publication output and citation counts tended to increase, reflecting sustained scholarly interest and an increasing diversity of research directions within this field.

4.1. General information

The publication trends can be categorized into 3 distinct phases. The first phase, from 2008 to 2011, represents the initial stage, characterized by a relatively low number of studies, with annual publications ranging from single digits to approximately 10. For example, only 2 articles were published in 2008, increasing to 11 in 2011. This limited research output may be attributed to the fact that the AQ scale, developed by Professor Simon Baron-Cohen and his team in 2001 to assess autistic traits in the general population, was still undergoing validation for different cultural and demographic contexts.[29–31] Consequently, research on autistic traits in the general population had just begun to gain traction. The second phase, spanning 2012 to 2016, marked a period of slow growth. The number of publications increased from 8 in 2012 to 20 in 2015. Although the number of publications slightly declined to 13 in 2016, the overall trend remained upward. The third phase, from 2017 onward, signifies a period of accelerated growth, with a notable surge in research output, particularly in 2018 and 2019, when the number of annual publications exceeded forty. By 2023, the number of studies reached 64, nearly equaling the total number of papers published from 2008 to 2012. This surge suggests a growing academic interest, an expansion in research scope and depth, and the gradual maturation of autistic traits as an independent research field. The increase in societal discussions on autism and the widespread use of assessment tools such as the AQ scale likely contributed to this growth. Researchers have increasingly recognized that a substantial proportion of the general population exhibits high levels of autistic traits, further fueling academic interest. Research on autistic traits has gradually gained attention in multiple disciplines, especially education, psychology, and neuroscience.[32–35] As the understanding of autistic traits in the general population continues to deepen, future research may continue to grow, especially as breakthroughs in neuroscience and psychology research further advance the field.

The observed growth in publications likely reflects the interaction of multiple driving forces rather than academic interest alone. First, the conceptual shift toward a dimensional and spectrum-based understanding of autistic traits has encouraged researchers to assess such traits across the general population, rather than restricting investigations to clinically diagnosed individuals, a development facilitated by the increasing maturity and diversification of trait-based measurement instruments. Second, increasing evidence suggests that higher levels of autistic traits in the general population are associated with anxiety, depression, impaired social functioning, and related psychosocial outcomes,[36–38] thereby extending research attention beyond clinical diagnosis to issues of mental health, quality of life (QoL), and the identification of strengths. Third, the growing prominence of neurodiversity discourse has increased scholarly and public interest in the subclinical manifestations and everyday functional expressions of autistic traits.[39]

At the same time, increases in bibliometric indicators should not be interpreted as direct evidence of changes in population prevalence. Methodological and structural factors, including database indexing practices, shifts in research funding priorities, and improved access to large-scale datasets, may also contribute to the apparent expansion of the literature.[40,41] These considerations provide essential contextual grounding for interpreting the publication trends identified in this study.

From an authorial perspective, several scholars have made significant contributions to the development of this research field. Professor Simon Baron-Cohen is the most influential figure, ranking first in terms of the H-index, citation count, and publication volume. Since 2008, he has authored 16 articles, making groundbreaking contributions to the study of autistic traits in the general population. His development of the AQ scale provided a critical framework for assessing individual autistic traits.[5,29] The scope of his research encompasses prevalence rates, cognitive differences, gender variations, social implications, and the potential mental health impacts of autistic traits.[42–44]

The second most prolific researcher, Angelica Ronald, has also published 16 papers, focusing on the genetic basis of autistic traits and their association with neurodevelopment and social behavior.[45,46] Henning Tiemeier has contributed 11 publications, primarily exploring the relationship between autistic traits in infants and maternal factors during pregnancy.[47,48] Andrew J.O. Whitehouse has authored 12 papers examining the influence of autistic traits on morphology, their manifestation in infant development, and their relationship with testosterone levels.[49,50] John N. Constantino has published 9 papers, emphasizing the genetic nature of autistic traits. His development of the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) has provided a widely used assessment tool for measuring autistic traits in research settings.[51,52]

From a geographical perspective, research collaboration in this field is highly active. The United Kingdom has the highest collaboration strength, particularly with the United States, Australia, China, and the Netherlands.[53] However, some exceptions exist. For instance, although Japan has produced a significant number of publications, its link strength with other countries remains relatively low, with only 17 recorded connections. This limited collaboration may stem from ethical, cultural, and demographic factors that pose challenges to international research partnerships.[54] Strengthening cross-border collaboration between countries and institutions could further advance research in this field.

From the perspective of the dual-map overlays, research in this field shows a significant trend toward multidisciplinary integration. Psychology and education address the issue of “phenomenon and practice” and drive applied research on autistic traits in the general population.[55] Moreover, biology and genetics explore “mechanisms and foundations” to promote scientific progress from mechanism to intervention.[56] This collaborative effort may further catalyze integrated research initiatives encompassing genes, brain science, and social practice. Concurrently, the findings of this analysis underscore the necessity of recognizing the synergistic effects that emerge from interdisciplinary collaboration. Specifically, the integration of biological mechanisms and psychosocial needs emerges as a pivotal consideration when formulating interventions and policy support.

4.2. Research topics

The following categories reflect the core content and development direction of research topics, as classified through keyword clustering analysis of the aforementioned 10 clusters.

4.2.1. Measurement of autistic traits and individual differences

These clusters involve primarily the exploration of measurement tools for autistic traits, individual differences, and related cognitive characteristics. #0 Systematizing quotient: keywords include systematizing quotient, asperger syndrome, empathy quotient (EQ), individual differences, reflecting research on individual differences in systematizing tendencies, and empathy in autistic traits in the general population.[10] Simon Baron-Cohen’s “systematizing and empathy theory” has made important contributions to this cluster.[57] #2 AQ: keywords include autism-spectrum quotient (AQ scale), emotion recognition, and eye-movements, indicating research on the application of measurement tools for autistic traits (such as the AQ) in the recognition of social cognitive functions such as emotions and eye movements.[58] #6 ASD: keywords include ASDs, reward decision-making, and familial risk, studies involving studies on the risk characteristics of ASDs in the general population and decision-making behavior.[59] The crux of this research lies in the utilization of scale tools, such as the AQ, EQ, and systemizing quotient, to quantify autistic traits within the general population. This endeavor aims to delve deeper into the intricacies of individual differences and their performance in emotion recognition and decision-making behavior.[60]

4.2.2. Autism traits and comorbidity

This cluster focuses on the comorbidity of autism traits with other psychological and behavioral characteristics, especially in the general population. #1 ADHD: keywords include attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), eating disorders, twin study, and deficit hyperactivity disorder, indicating research on the comorbidity of autism traits with ADHD and eating disorders (such as anorexia nervosa).[61] #9 Suicidality: keywords include suicidality, risk markers, and non-suicidal self-injury. This cluster studies the risk markers of autistic traits and suicidal tendencies and self-injury behaviors.[62] #4 Cognitive empathy: keywords include cognitive empathy, anxiety, and alexithymia, indicating that cognitive empathy and emotional awareness disorders (such as aphasia) are important manifestations of autistic traits.[10] This category reveals the comorbidity of autistic traits with ADHD, anxiety, eating disorders, and self-injury behaviors, providing an important basis for understanding the mental health risks of autistic traits.

4.2.3. Biological mechanisms and genetic research

This category emphasizes the biological basis, such as the role of genetics, neurophysiology, and the immune system in autistic traits. #8 Immune system: keywords include immune system, polymorphism, and prenatal, focusing on the relationship between the immune system and autistic traits, such as the influence of genetic polymorphisms and prenatal environmental factors.[63] #3 Spectrum: keywords include spectrum and twins, which are related to twin studies and indicate the importance of genetic factors in autistic traits.[64] #5 Selection: keywords include savant skills, eye gaze, and the Siblings Research Consortium, which explores the relationship between exceptional skills and genetic selection, as well as research on siblings.[65] These studies reveal the genetic basis and immunobiological mechanisms of autistic traits, and further explore the differences in performance under the interaction of genetics and the environment.

4.2.4. Cognitive and behavioral characteristics

This category focuses on research on the cognitive functions and behavioral manifestations of individuals with autistic traits: #7 Creativity: keywords include creativity and social behavior, involving studies that explored the relationship among autistic traits, creativity and social behavior. #10 Attention: keywords include attention, eye gaze processing, and temporal-binding window, involving studies that explored the relationship between attention functions and autistic traits, especially their impact on visual attention and temporal perception.[66] This category focuses on the unique performance of individuals with autistic traits in terms of cognitive and behavioral functions such as attention, social interaction, and creativity.

These clusters reflect the trend of multidimensional development of research on autistic traits in the general population, from measurement tools and comorbidities to genetic mechanisms and behavioral characteristics, demonstrating the depth and breadth of the field.

4.2.5. Conceptual interactions among the four research topics

The 4 research topics identified in this study are analytically distinct yet conceptually interconnected, collectively constituting a multilevel framework for understanding autistic traits in the general population. Research on the measurement of autistic traits and individual differences, primarily through instruments such as the AQ, EQ, and systemizing quotient, provides a methodological foundation by enabling the systematic quantification of trait variation and individual differences in social cognition and decision making.

Building upon this foundation, studies examining biological and genetic mechanisms offer explanatory insights into the neurobiological and heritable bases underlying variability in autistic traits. Research on cognitive and behavioral characteristics in turn translates these trait differences into observable functional expressions, including patterns of attention, social behavior, and creativity. Investigations focusing on autistic traits and comorbidities further extend this framework by situating cognitive–behavioral profiles within broader psychosocial contexts, thereby linking elevated trait levels to mental health outcomes and overall well-being.

These clusters form a coherent and mutually reinforcing conceptual structure, progressing from measurement and underlying mechanisms to behavioral expression and psychosocial implications, which clarifies the integrated and multidimensional nature of research on autistic traits.

4.3. Hotspots and frontiers

A keyword burst analysis was conducted to reveal the development trajectory of the field and the evolution of research hotspots. The outcomes of this analysis in the domain of autistic traits in the general population can be categorized into 3 phases according to the time, strength, and duration of keyword occurrence. This categorization is largely consistent with the results of the analysis based on the number of publications, thereby revealing the research focus and developmental trend of the field. The initial stage, spanning from 2008 to 2012, is characterized by an initial interest in the field. The term “brain science” exhibited a high prominence intensity (5.07) from 2009 to 2017, signifying a notable interest among researchers in the relationship between autistic traits and brain structure or function. The prominence of fetal testosterone (2011–2016) signifies a shift in research focus toward investigating the correlation between fetal hormone levels and autistic traits during this period. This observation underscores the significance of a biological perspective in the study of autistic traits.[67] For example, Whitehouse et al[50] explored the relationship between testosterone concentrations in cord blood and autistic-like traits in the general population by collecting 707 cases of children’s cord blood and 374 completed AQ questionnaires in early adulthood. The period from 2008 to 2012 was characterized by a significant increase in research focusing on autism in childhood and its biological underpinnings. Research gradually expanded to include autistic traits in the general population, thereby laying the foundations for the field through neuroscientific and hormonal studies.

The subsequent phase, spanning from 2013 to 2017, witnessed significant advancements in the field. A 2013 face study underscored the correlation between autistic traits and the recognition and processing of facial emotions, thereby initiating a deeper exploration into individual behavioral characteristics. In 2014, the keyword “deficit hyperactivity disorder” emerged as a prominent discourse catalyst, signifying a growing focus among researchers on the coexistence of autistic traits and other neurodevelopmental disorders.[68] In 2015, the AQ scale gained prominence, signifying an advancement in the standardization of measurement tools for autistic traits and their widespread utilization for screening and assessing autistic traits in the general population. Concurrently, research expanded from the biological basis to behavioral characteristics and comorbidities, while standardized measurement tools (e.g., the AQ scale and SRS) were introduced, thereby promoting the quantitative development of research on autistic traits in the general population.

The third stage encompasses the period from 2018 to 2023. The term “attention” experienced a notable surge in prominence during the span from 2018 to 2020, signifying a notable increase in the level of research activity concerning the relationship between autistic traits and attention function within the general population. The emergence of eating disorders and anorexia nervosa in 2020 signified a shift in focus among researchers toward the potential association between autistic traits and eating disorders. For example, on the basis of 3559 SRS questionnaires and the Children’s Eating Behavior Questionnaire, Van’t Hof et al[69] found that among girls, autistic traits were associated with increased levels of emotional overeating and emotional undereating, whereas no such association was found among boys.

The emergence of sex differences as a Burst in 2021 underscores the distinct manifestations and mechanisms of autistic traits in men and women.[70] As accumulating evidence suggests that autistic traits are expressed, perceived, and identified differently across sexes, research adopting a sex-sensitive perspective has expanded rapidly. Large-scale population-based studies of adults in the United Kingdom (involving more than 7000 participants) have demonstrated that nearly all items of the AQ exhibit sex-related measurement bias, with only 2 items functioning equivalently across males and females. For the remaining items, response probabilities were systematically influenced by sex, with females being more likely to endorse items related to social skills and communication.[71] These findings raise important concerns regarding the sex fairness and measurement invariance of commonly used screening instruments when applied to the general population. In parallel, meta-analytic evidence based on large nonclinical samples indicates that compared with females, males tend to show higher mean levels and greater variability of autistic traits and that such sex differences are more pronounced in the general population than in clinical samples.[72] Collectively, these findings suggest that sex influences not only diagnostic identification but also the population-level distribution and measurement of autistic traits, thereby reinforcing the need for sex-sensitive measurement and interpretation in future research.

The term QoL emerged as a prominent subject in 2021, signifying a shift in research focus towards the subjective well-being and QoL of individuals with high autism traits within the general population.[73] Informed by the neurodiversity perspective and positive psychology, contemporary research increasingly prioritizes whether individuals are able to live well, rather than the extent to which they deviate from normative functioning, thereby positioning subjective well-being and life satisfaction as central outcomes. Within nonclinical contexts, autistic traits are conceptualized as variations in cognitive and social styles rather than indicators of pathology,[74] rendering QoL a more appropriate and ecologically valid criterion than symptom-based measures. At the same time, a growing body of mechanism-oriented research has established QoL as an integrative construct for examining how autistic traits translate into everyday experiences. For example, in a large general population adult survey, higher levels of autistic traits were associated with lower QoL across all WHOQOL-BREF domains (physical, psychological, social, and environmental), as well as with reduced self-determination.[75] Moreover, analyses combining autistic and non-autistic adults similarly demonstrated that autistic trait levels were negatively associated with QoL across domains, highlighting QoL as a robust outcome sensitive to trait-related variation across the full continuum.[76]

From 2018 to 2023, academic research on autistic traits has shown a trend of diversification, expanding into areas such as attention, eating disorders, gender differences, and QoL. This further revealed the broad impact of autism traits on an individual’s daily functioning. Concurrently, the enhancement of research tools has emerged as a reliable measurement method for research.

This progression mirrors the profound and multifaceted expansion of research on autistic traits within the general population, encompassing diverse levels ranging from biological underpinnings to extensive applications in domains such as individual behavior, comorbidity, and QoL. In the future, research in this field may expand to explore effective interventions that can alleviate the impact of autistic traits on various social behaviors in the population and to test the application of artificial intelligence in this field.

5. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data were collected exclusively from the WoSCC database and included only articles and reviews published in English. As a result, the analysis may not fully capture all relevant research in this field. Second, while this study employed 2 widely used bibliometric analysis tools, VOSviewer and CiteSpace, and efforts were made to minimize bias, some subjectivity may persist due to differences in algorithmic approaches and varying researcher perspectives on the same dataset.

6. Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed and visualized nearly 15 years of research on autistic traits in the general population. By examining publication trends, leading authors, institutions, countries, keywords, and co-citation networks, it offers a comprehensive overview of the field’s developmental trajectory and major research domains. The findings are intended to assist researchers in identifying current research trends, synthesizing existing evidence, and informing future investigations.. Additionally, recent studies have increasingly highlighted the multifaceted impact of autistic traits on various aspects of daily functioning within the general population. Moving forward, interdisciplinary approaches, combining education, psychology, and occupational studies with advancements in AI, may represent a promising direction for future research. Furthermore, enhancing collaboration among scholars, institutions, and countries will be crucial for promoting the continued advancement of research on autistic traits in the general population.
