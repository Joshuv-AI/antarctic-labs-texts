Gap in knowledge and improvement after an infectious disease training program for Ugandan healthcare workers
Authors: Antonella Cingolani, Riccardo Serraino, Francesca Cagnoni, Marta Chiuchiarelli, Gabriella Monolo, Federico Frondizi, Rony Reginald Bahatungire, Maurizio Destro, Carlo Torti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAchieving international accreditation standards, including those defined by the Joint Commission International (JCI), requires strong infection prevention and control (IPC) and antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programs to limit antimicrobial resistance. In Uganda, healthcare workers (HCWs) still face challenges in infectious disease (ID) management due to limited diagnostic resources, standardized protocols, and structured IPC training. This study aimed at assessing possible gaps in knowledge at baseline and short-term improvements after a context-adapted IPC and AMS training program in improving infectious disease management competencies among healthcare workers in Uganda.MethodsWe conducted a pre–post interventional study between July 21st, 2025, and July 30th, 2025 involving 56 healthcare workers (nurses, midwives, and clinical officers) from rural and peri-urban health facilities in Northern Uganda. The curriculum, which aligns with the World Health Organization, Ugandan Ministry of Health, and JCI standards, covered tuberculosis, HIV, malaria, sepsis, pneumonia, hepatitis, and emerging infections, as well as key IPC and AMS principles. Teaching combined interactive lectures, case-based discussions, and simulation exercises. Knowledge and practice were assessed before and after training using multiple-choice questions (MCQs), clinical case scenarios (CCSs), and Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) surveys. Paired t tests were used to compare pre- and post-training scores.ResultsThe 56 participants had a mean age of 34.5 ± 5.2 years and an average of 9.2 ± 4.6 years of professional experience. Post-training performance improved significantly across most thematic domains. The largest increases in the proportion of correct responses were observed in HIV/STI, malaria, viral hepatitis, and bone and joint infections. The mean MCQ score increased from 0.61 ± 0.12 to 0.85 ± 0.08 (p < 0.001), representing a 25% mean gain. CCS scores increased from 0.59 to 0.70 (p < 0.001). KAP-A scores rose from 0.90 to 1.03. Variability in performance decreased post-training, suggesting more uniform understanding. No significant correlation was found between improvement and participants’ age, sex, or education level. Qualitative feedback confirmed greater confidence and adherence to hand hygiene, PPE use, and environmental sanitation practices.ConclusionsThe program significantly improved both knowledge and IPC competences across all professional groups. Translating international standards into locally applicable practices strengthened frontline capacity of HCWs’ for infection prevention, clinical safety, and antimicrobial stewardship. Our study was limited to assess improvement at the conclusion of the intervention, therefore refresher sessions, mentorship, and integration with IPC monitoring tools are likely to be useful to sustain this level of knowledge and contribute to foster long-term health system resilience in Uganda.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13269-8.

Introduction

Infectious diseases remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Uganda, placing a substantial burden on an already resource-limited health system [1]. Healthcare workers (HCWs) often face significant challenges in diagnosing and managing infections due to limited access to standardized protocols, diagnostic tools, and continuous professional development opportunities [2, 3]. Infection prevention and control (IPC) practices and antimicrobial stewardship programs are frequently underdeveloped, contributing to the persistence of healthcare-associated infections and the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [4–6].

Training interventions aimed at improving infectious disease management in low- and middle-income countries generally include knowledge-based approaches, skills-based training, and competency- or behaviour-oriented models that integrate technical skills with attitudes and adherence to protocols. Evidence indicates that stand-alone didactic training has limited impact, whereas interactive, case-based and simulation-oriented approaches are more effective in improving clinical reasoning, IPC compliance, and antimicrobial stewardship practices [7, 8].

Although national IPC guidelines, hand hygiene campaigns, and antimicrobial stewardship recommendations exist in Uganda, their implementation is often inconsistent across facilities due to resource constraints, limited training opportunities, and gaps in supervision and monitoring [4–6]. Moreover, the lack of training contributes to significant variability in the quality of care, increasing the risk of medical errors, healthcare-associated infections, and poor patient satisfaction.

Establishing national and regional centres of excellence in patient care, education, and research presents an opportunity to strengthen these critical domains. To achieve these goals, structured and context-adapted training for frontline HCWs is essential. Educational interventions tailored to local epidemiology and resource availability can enhance clinical competencies, standardize infection management, and foster sustainable improvements in patient care. However, evidence on the effectiveness of structured infectious disease training programs adapted to local healthcare contexts in Uganda remains limited. Most initiatives have focused on single diseases or short-term outcomes, with limited evaluation of comprehensive, multi-disease training programs aligned with international accreditation standards.

To address these gaps, this study evaluated a structured, context-adapted infectious disease training program integrating IPC, AMS, and clinical reasoning, aligned with World Health Organization, Ugandan Ministry of Health, and Joint Commission International standards. Using a mixed-methods pre–post design, the study assessed changes in HCWs’ knowledge, clinical decision-making, attitudes, and self-reported practices, while incorporating qualitative feedback to contextualize quantitative outcomes. This approach aimed at better understanding the gaps in knowledge at the same time contributing evidence on scalable training models capable of strengthening frontline healthcare capacity and improving quality and safety of care in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a pre–post interventional study conducted between June and July 2025, with data collection taking place during the training period from 21 to 30 July 2025. The study involved HCWs from rural and peri-urban health facilities in northern Uganda as part of the capacity-building program of the International Specialized Hospital of Uganda (ISHU), a tertiary-level referral, research and teaching hospital currently under construction in Lubowa, Wakiso District near Kampala (Uganda). The facility is being developed through a public–private partnership, in which private developers manage operations for a concession period (approximately ten years) before transferring ownership to the Ugandan government. Designed to provide highly specialized medical and surgical care, including organ transplantation, oncology, neurosurgery, cardiac surgery, orthopaedics, and advanced diagnostic such as nuclear medicine, ISHU aims to reduce medical tourism and retain the country’s capacity to deliver complex, high-quality healthcare within Uganda and the surrounding region. The intervention aimed to strengthen competencies in infectious disease management, infection prevention and control (IPC), and antimicrobial stewardship (AMS), in accordance with standards and recommendations from the World Health Organization (WHO) [9, 10], the Ugandan Ministry of Health (MoH) [11, 12], and the Joint Commission International (JCI) [13].

Participants and training intervention

Participants were selected following a structured interview conducted by two of the authors (F.C. and M.D.) that assessed predefined personal and professional attributes, including clinical experience, motivation, teamwork attitudes, and the relevance of the participant’s role to infectious disease management and IPC practices. Participant selection was determined by operational and institutional priorities related to capacity building for the ISHU rather than statistical power considerations; therefore, this study represents a comprehensive evaluation of all enrolled trainees rather than a sample-based inferential analysis.

Because participants were selected through a standardized review and interview process using predefined criteria, potential selection bias was minimized. Eligible participants included nurses, midwives, and clinical officers actively involved in patient care and willing to participate in the training program. There were no additional exclusion criteria. All HCWs who met these criteria and consented to participate after the interview process were enrolled.

The structured training course was delivered over seven days (21–30 July 2025) and combined interactive lectures, case-based discussions, small-group activities, and simulation exercises. Baseline assessments were administered on the first day of the course (21 July 2025), and post-training assessments were conducted on the final day (30 July 2025), immediately after completion of the training.

The curriculum covered major infectious diseases of public health relevance in Uganda, including tuberculosis, HIV, malaria, sepsis, soft tissue infections, pneumonia, hepatitis, and emerging infections. IPC modules emphasized hand hygiene, appropriate use of personal protective equipment (PPE), patient isolation procedures, and environmental sanitation. The educational approach was grounded in adult learning principles, promoting active participation and application of international guidelines to local healthcare contexts.

Ethical considerations, consent, and data protection

The study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles for research involving human participants. All participants were clearly informed about the purpose of the study prior to participation, and written informed consent was obtained through signature on a consent form.

Site authorization to conduct the training and evaluation activities was obtained through the coordinating institution. Personally identifiable information (e.g., names or contact details) was not recorded in the study database and was not used for data analysis. Any personal information collected during the initial selection process was used solely for training coordination and eligibility assessment and was not retained or linked to study outcomes. All analyses were conducted on anonymized data.

The same instrument was administered immediately before and after the training to measure changes in knowledge and behavioural indicators. All the data were anonymized and assigned a unique participant code.

Knowledge was assessed through the MCQs, clinical case scenarios, and true/false items, with each correct answer assigned one point. Total knowledge scores were expressed as the sum of correct responses. In addition, we ranked the proposed questions (including MCQs, CCS, KAP-A) into ten thematic domains to estimate the level of knowledge for each domain measured as proportion of correct responses. Performance for each domain was measured as proportion of correct responses over the total number of expected responses (number of participants × number of questions for each domain), thereby accounting for both incorrect and missing answers. This descriptive analysis was performed at baseline to assess possible gaps in the specific topics, and it was repeated at the conclusion of the educational intervention to explore whether a possible improvement could differ among the topics of interest. Attitude and practice items were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree/never, 5 = strongly agree/always), allowing quantification of participants’ perceptions and self-reported behaviours. For analysis, mean scores were calculated for each domain, and higher values indicated more favourable attitudes or better adherence to recommended practices.

Statistical analysis

No formal sample size calculation was performed, as the study included the entire cohort of healthcare workers enrolled in the infectious disease training program as part of the institutional capacity-building strategy for the International Specialized Hospital of Uganda (ISHU). Participant inclusion was therefore determined by operational and institutional priorities rather than by sampling for inferential purposes, and the analysis represents a comprehensive evaluation of all enrolled trainees.

Quantitative data were entered into IBM SPSS Statistics and analysed using standard descriptive and inferential methods. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of pre- and post-training score distributions for multiple-choice questions (MCQs), clinical case scenarios (CCS), and composite Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) domain scores. No significant deviations from normality were observed for the primary outcome measures (all Shapiro–Wilk p values > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric statistical tests. For the analysis of the thematic domains, differences between pre-test and post-test proportions were assessed using chi-square tests, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using a normal approximation.

Pre- and post-training differences in MCQ and CCS scores were evaluated using paired-sample t tests, with results reported as mean differences and corresponding SD. Composite scores for the KAP domains (KAP-A, KAP-B, and KAP-C) were calculated as mean Likert-scale values, and changes over time were assessed using paired-sample t tests. Internal consistency of the KAP instrument was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

Associations between score improvements (ΔMCQ, ΔCCS, and ΔKAP) and participant characteristics (age, sex, profession, educational level, and years of experience) were explored using Pearson correlation coefficients for continuous variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for categorical variables. Given the relatively small sample size and the exploratory nature of these analyses, multivariable regression models were not performed to avoid potential overfitting and unstable estimates.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative evaluation

At the conclusion of the course, participants completed an individual questionnaire that included an open-ended feedback section addressing perceived changes in confidence, relevance, and applicability of the training content to daily clinical practice. No focus groups or interviews were conducted. Qualitative data were analysed using a thematic content analysis approach. The identified themes were used to complement and contextualize the quantitative findings.

Results

A total of 56 healthcare workers, which represented the entire predefined group, completed the infectious disease training program, consisting of nurses (46.4%), midwives (32.1%), and clinical officers (21.4%) from rural and peri-urban health facilities in northern Uganda. The mean self-reported participant age was 34.5 ± 5.2 years, with a mean of 9.2 ± 4.6 years of professional experience. Baseline scores were broadly comparable across demographic groups (see supplementary material S2). No statistically significant differences were observed between male and female participants in MCQ, CCS, or KAP domain scores. Similarly, baseline scores did not differ significantly across age groups.

Modest differences were observed across professional categories, with clinical officers demonstrating slightly higher MCQ and CCS scores compared with nurses and midwives, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. Overall, these findings suggest relatively homogeneous baseline knowledge and attitudes across participant groups.

Training outcomes and post-intervention effects

Post-training performance improved significantly across most thematic domains (Fig. 1). The largest increases in the proportion of correct responses were observed in HIV/STI, malaria, viral hepatitis, and bone and joint infections. Improvements were also observed in endocarditis, sepsis, SSTI, and antimicrobial resistance/antimicrobial stewardship. In contrast, no significant changes were observed in IPC or tuberculosis.

The mean multiple-choice questionnaire (MCQ) score increased from 0.61 ± 0.12 to 0.85 ± 0.08 (p < 0.001), corresponding to an average relative gain of 25% (Fig. 2). Similarly, the mean score for case-based clinical scenarios (CCS) rose from 0.59 ± 0.10 to 0.70 ± 0.09 (p < 0.001), indicating an enhanced capacity to apply theoretical knowledge to clinical decision-making. Scores from the KPA-A domain also demonstrated a modest yet statistically meaningful improvement, increasing from 0.90 to 1.03, reflecting greater awareness and commitment to IPC principles. Following the training intervention, participants showed a measurable improvement in both the KAP-B and KAP-C domains. The mean KAP-B score increased from 4.35 (± 0.47) to 4.81 (± 0.33) (t = 2.16, p = 0.037), while that for KAP-C increased from 4.69 (± 0.35) to 4.84 (± 0.27) (t = 2.16, p = 0.037).

Qualitative findings

The thematic content analysis of open-ended responses identified consistent themes across the four explored domains. Overall, qualitative findings complemented and contextualized the quantitative results.

Clarity of the course

Participants generally perceived the course as clear and well structured, with 45 out of 56 participants (80.4%) reporting being “very satisfied.” The logical progression of topics and the use of accessible language facilitated understanding of complex clinical concepts and supported participants’ confidence in applying the content to daily practice.

Competence of the trainers

Trainers were widely described as competent and knowledgeable. Participants highlighted their clinical expertise, ability to address questions effectively, and capacity to link theoretical content with real-world clinical experience, enhancing the credibility of the training.

Relevance of clinical example

Clinical cases were considered highly relevant to participants’ professional settings. Respondents emphasized the practical nature of the examples and their immediate applicability to routine clinical work, which supported the translation of knowledge into practice. Overall, 48 out of 56 participants (85.7%) reported being “very satisfied” with the clinical case component.

Overall satisfaction

Overall satisfaction with the course was high, with 47 out of 56 participants (83.9%) reporting being “very satisfied.” Participants frequently linked their positive evaluation to the combination of clear content delivery, trainer competence, and practical relevance. Several respondents also reported increased confidence and motivation to implement the acquired knowledge in their clinical activities.

Discussion

This study evaluated a structured infectious disease training program delivered to HCWs from rural and peri-urban health facilities in Uganda. Overall, baseline analysis revealed moderate theoretical knowledge and clinical reasoning scores, together with relatively positive attitudes toward infection prevention practices. Importantly, baseline scores were broadly comparable across sex, age, and professional groups, indicating a relatively homogeneous cohort of participants with similar competencies before the course.

The present study suggests that a structured, context-adapted infectious disease training program may enhance knowledge, clinical reasoning, and IPC competencies of HCWs in northern Uganda. While the pre–post design does not allow to derive definitive causal attribution, the consistency of improvements across multiple assessment domains suggests that the educational intervention was associated with short-term gains in knowledge and clinical reasoning. Taken together, these findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the potential value of structured, context-tailored training approaches in strengthening frontline healthcare capacity.

The baseline assessment provided important contextual information about the educational needs of the participating healthcare workforce. Regarding the thematic domains, our analysis demonstrated a overall improvement, but no statistically significant changes were observed for IPC and tuberculosis themes, probably because knowledge in these fields was already very good.

Although overall MCQ scores indicated moderate theoretical knowledge, performance in clinical case scenarios was lower. Moreover, the absence of statistically significant differences in baseline scores across sex, age, and professional groups suggests that knowledge gaps were not restricted to specific demographic categories but were distributed across the healthcare workforce. This finding supports the relevance of multidisciplinary training approaches that address common educational needs among healthcare workers with diverse professional backgrounds.

The 25% mean increase in knowledge scores and the consistent gains in case-based clinical scenario performance suggest that participants not only acquired new information but were also able to apply it to clinical decision-making. These findings are consistent with previous evidence indicating that experiential and interactive learning approaches improve the retention and translation of IPC and antimicrobial stewardship concepts into clinical practice [7]. The modest but statistically significant increase in KAP scores (KAP-B and KAP-C) further suggests a positive shift in attitudes and confidence towards infection control, reinforcing the behavioural aspects essential for sustainable practice change. This alignment with prior literature strengthens the plausibility of the observed effects and supports their potential relevance beyond the study setting.

Importantly, the absence of correlation between knowledge improvement and demographic variables such as age, profession, or years of experience may indicate the inclusivity and adaptability of the training model. This uniformity of outcomes suggests that the educational intervention was accessible and beneficial to HCWs across different levels of expertise, supporting its potential scalability to other regions and healthcare settings.

Although modest, the sample size of the study provided sufficient statistical power to detect significant differences in both the knowledge (i.e., MCQ) and clinical reasoning (i.e., CCS) domains. The observed improvements were statistically robust, with relatively narrow confidence intervals suggesting acceptable internal precision. However, the relatively small sample size should be considered when interpreting external validity and generalizability. Moreover, while the magnitude of improvement in MCQ and CCS scores was substantial, the absence of a comparator group prevents exclusion of alternative explanations such as test familiarity or short-term recall effects.

While the improvement in KAP-A did not reach statistical significance, the results for KAP-B and KAP-C suggest positive behavioural and attitudinal changes consistent with infection control principles. The internal consistency of the KAP instrument, verified by a Cronbach’s α > 0.8, support good reliability of the self-reported measures. The relatively small magnitude of change is consistent with the use of self-reported Likert-scale measures and the short duration of the intervention and does not necessarily reflect limited educational impact. Moreover, Raw mean scores were retained rather than standardized scores to preserve interpretability within the local context and facilitate comparison with similar IPC training evaluations conducted in low-resource settings.

Qualitative feedback supported the quantitative findings, with participants reporting increased confidence in implementing key IPC measures, including hand hygiene, the use of PPE, environmental sanitation, and isolation procedures. These self-reported behavioural changes are particularly relevant in the Ugandan context, where healthcare-associated infections and antimicrobial resistance remain persistent challenges. By translating international standards into locally feasible practices, the program may have addressed critical gaps in the existing healthcare delivery system and may have contributed to building a safer and more resilient healthcare environment. These observations highlight the potential system-level relevance of training interventions that integrate global standards with local implementation feasibility.

The results of this study are relevant since structured IPC training is not universally available. Studies show that only a minority of healthcare workers have received recent training in IPC or disease-specific management (e.g., Ebola), and training is often not integrated into pre-service curricula. High staff turnover and a lack of ongoing mentorship further undermine the sustainability of IPC knowledge and skills [15, 16]. These structural constraints highlight the importance of embedding training initiatives within broader workforce development and retention strategies.

Emerging evidence from outbreak-response and public health workforce studies suggests that high staff turnover is closely linked to substantial occupational stress and burnout. Studies among Field Epidemiology Training Program trainees and field epidemiologists report high burdens of role overload, adverse working conditions, and role ambiguity, with more than half of respondents expressing turnover intention [17, 18]. These findings indicate that while competency-based IPC and AMS training is essential, its long-term impact may depend on parallel investments in supportive supervision, manageable workloads, and retention strategies that address occupational stress alongside technical capacity building. This broader perspective suggests that educational interventions should be viewed as one component of multifaceted health system strengthening efforts rather than as stand-alone solutions.

Competency-based and interprofessional training approaches, such as the East Africa IPC Learning Network, have led to improvements in IPC compliance and occupational health competencies among health care workers in tertiary hospitals across Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda. For example, median IPC competency scores increased from 2.5 to 3.5 at baseline to ≥ 4.5 at the endpoint, and appropriate PPE use rose from 65% to 92% (P < 0.006) after the implementation of collaborative quality improvement projects and routine training sessions [8].

The findings of our study are consistent with previous evidence from sub-Saharan Africa. In particular, data from Uganda further support the effectiveness of similar training initiatives in improving healthcare performance. Educational outreach and integrated infectious disease management programs have been associated with increased triage rates, more appropriate hospital admissions, and improved prescribing practices for malaria and pneumonia, with effects sustained over time following both immediate and delayed on-site support [7, 19–21].

At a broader system level, field epidemiology training programs, such as the Uganda Public Health Fellowship Program, have played a critical role in strengthening outbreak response capacity, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, by enabling rapid deployment of well-trained personnel integrated within the Ministry of Health [16, 21–23].

At the same time, evidence from integrated disease surveillance and response training highlights that, although healthcare workers report improvements in surveillance, reporting, and outbreak management, persistent structural challenges (including workforce shortages, limited funding, and high staff turnover) continue to constrain the long-term sustainability of these gains [24]. These findings suggest that while structured training is an effective tool to enhance competencies and performance, its impact is maximized when embedded within broader health system strengthening strategies, including mentorship, supportive supervision, and integration into pre-service education.

This study has strengths, but it is affected by several limitations. First, we used a pre–post design without a control group, which limits causal inference and does not allow definitive attribution of the observed improvements to the training intervention alone. Second, outcomes were assessed immediately after the intervention and therefore reflect short-term educational gains rather than sustained improvements in clinical practice. Third, self-reported KAP measures may be subject to social desirability bias. Future studies should incorporate controlled designs, longer follow-up periods, and objective indicators of IPC performance or antimicrobial prescribing practices. Lastly, the number of participants was small, but the study was able to include all those who were pre-selected as candidate to be employed by the ISHU.

Conclusions

These findings suggest that structured, context-adapted training programs may improve short-term knowledge and self-reported infection prevention practices among healthcare workers in resource-limited settings. However, further studies with longitudinal follow-up, employing multivariable regression models to more robustly assess predictors of training effectiveness, are required to assess long-term retention of competencies and their impact on clinical outcomes. The approach implemented within the ISHU framework offers a replicable model for capacity building and quality improvement aligned with international accreditation standards and national health priorities. However, given the short follow-up period, caution is warranted in interpreting the long-term sustainability of these findings, which should be confirmed through studies with extended follow-up. Sustained mentorship, periodic refresher training, and institutional support will be critical to maintaining the observed gains and fostering a culture of safety and antimicrobial stewardship in Uganda’s healthcare system.
