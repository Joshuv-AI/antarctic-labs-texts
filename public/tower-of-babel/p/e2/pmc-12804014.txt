Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy Triggered by Electrical Cardioversion and Complicated by Ventricular Septal Rupture
Authors: Syed Ahmad, Amro Taha, Harshith Thyagaturu, Harsh A. Patel, Ruby Havistin, Daniel Brito Guzman, Sudarshan Balla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TTS), or apical stress-induced cardiomyopathy, was initially described in 1990 in Japan.1 Stress-induced cardiomyopathy is a transient deterioration in myocardial function and wall motion, secondary to stressors of various etiologies. Triggers, including acute medical illness, noncardiac trauma, or emotional distress, have been described.2 Although the exact mechanism remains unclear, the prevailing hypothesis suggests that excess circulating catecholamines lead to coronary dysfunction, myocardial stunning, or direct myocardial injury.3

Although initially reported as involving the left ventricular apex, other variants have subsequently been described, such as reverse takotsubo or basal variant, midventricular variant, and segmental variants.4 The apical variant of stress-induced cardiomyopathy, TTS, is characterized by echocardiographic findings of ventricular apical ballooning, electrocardiographic findings such as ST-segment elevation or T-wave inversions, troponin elevations, brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) elevations, and a potential preceding stressful triggering event. Stress-induced cardiomyopathy can occur without an inciting event, and acute coronary syndrome (ACS) should be ruled out when considering the diagnosis.

Stress-induced cardiomyopathy is generally associated with a favorable long-term prognosis.2 The rate of recurrence, myocardial infarction (MI), need for ventilator support, and all-cause mortality are low for stress-induced cardiomyopathy without mechanical complications.4 While most patients recover left ventricular function, mechanical complications from stress-induced cardiomyopathy are rare but carry high mortality.5 We present a rare case of synchronized electrical cardioversion–induced TTS, complicated by cardiogenic shock and ventricular septal rupture (VSR). We emphasize the imaging-based diagnostics in the workup and the learning points.

Case Presentation

A 77-year-old female patient with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, history of tachyarrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy (with recovered left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] to 65%), hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, left atrial appendage occlusion device placement, and major depressive disorder presented to the emergency department (ED) with a chief complaint of dyspnea and palpitations. The patient had recently been started on therapeutic anticoagulation in anticipation of an elective transesophageal echocardiography– (TEE-) guided electrical cardioversion for symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

On presentation to the ED, the heart rate was 135 beats per minute, blood pressure was 119/72 mm Hg, and jugular venous pressure was normal. Pulmonary auscultation revealed normal breath sounds throughout, and cardiac examination revealed an irregular tachycardic rhythm without a murmur. A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. The initial laboratory workup revealed a normal troponin level, BNP of 314 pg/mL (100-400 pg/mL, mildly elevated), and unremarkable complete blood count, basic metabolic panel, and thyroid function tests.

Previous echocardiograms were reviewed, including one that showed the history of tachyarrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy 3 years prior to this presentation. At that time, the patient was in atrial fibrillation and had an LVEF of 40% with global hypokinesis of the left ventricle. No significant valvular abnormalities were noted. Heart failure therapy included spironolactone, metoprolol succinate, and enalapril. The subsequent echocardiogram, performed approximately 18 months later, showed an LVEF of 65% with no significant wall motion abnormalities (Video 1). A single-photon emission computed tomography myocardial perfusion study showed no signs of ischemia.

On day 1 of hospitalization, the patient underwent TEE, which revealed the left atrial appendage occlusion device with no evidence of thrombus or peridevice leak and mildly reduced left ventricular function (Figure 1, Video 2). The patient received moderate sedation for the procedure with fentanyl and midazolam. The adequacy of sedation was checked; the patient was not arousable to verbal and light tactile stimulation, and airway and breathing were maintained. Synchronized electrical cardioversion was performed with 360 joules of biphasic energy. The patient reported a mild, transient episode of chest pain soon after the delivery of the electrical shock, but this resolved prior to discharge. Medications at the time of discharge included apixaban, empagliflozin, atorvastatin, enalapril-hydrochlorothiazide, metoprolol succinate, and spironolactone.

The patient returned to the ED approximately 22 hours postdischarge with complaints of chest pain, generalized fatigue, and gastric discomfort. Troponin was elevated at 170 ng/L, and BNP was 1,915 pg/mL. Emergent invasive coronary angiography with left and right heart catheterization revealed no culprit lesion but demonstrated slow flow in all 3 coronary arteries. Right heart catheterization data showed a right atrial mean pressure of 23 mm Hg, right ventricular pressures of 43/17 (mean, 24) mm Hg, pulmonary artery pressures of 46/30 (mean, 36) mm Hg, and mean pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 33 mm Hg. The Qp/Qs ratio was calculated to be 2.3 (Fick principle), suggesting a left-to-right shunt. Additional findings included elevated biventricular pressures (left ventricular end diastolic pressure 32 mm Hg), a reduced cardiac index (1.6 L/min/m2), and reduced cardiac output (3.2 L/min) consistent with cardiogenic shock. A microaxial flow pump was placed for hemodynamic support to treat cardiogenic shock.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed new-onset apical variant stress-induced cardiomyopathy with severely depressed right and left ventricular function (Video 3) and a newly developed VSR with a left-to-right shunt (Figures 4 and 5, Video 4). Clinically, the presentation was suggestive of troponin-positive nonobstructive coronary artery disease, which has a broad differential. Ischemic injury resulting in MI with nonobstructive coronary artery, nonischemic insults such as stress-induced cardiomyopathies and myocarditis, and noncardiac etiologies such as pulmonary embolism should be considered. The patient remained critically ill in the intensive care unit, requiring vasopressor support and invasive ventilation due to pulmonary edema. Subsequently, multiorgan failure developed with worsening renal failure, shock liver, and lactic acidosis. The patient suffered cardiac arrest due to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, achieving return of spontaneous circulation after 30 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Discussion

In summary, this was a unique case of iatrogenic stress-induced cardiomyopathy precipitated by electrical cardioversion complicated by VSR. The diagnosis was based on the constellation of ECG, echocardiogram, and laboratory findings, and stress-induced cardiomyopathy was identified as the cause of the acute left ventricular and right ventricular failure leading to cardiogenic shock. Invasive coronary angiography for ACS evaluation showed nonobstructive disease, and prior to cardioversion, there were no signs of acute heart failure. Echocardiographic data reviewed from the prior years did not show any evidence of VSR; similarly, the TEE performed prior to cardioversion did not show a VSR. These findings support the conclusion that stress-induced cardiomyopathy was precipitated by electrical cardioversion, leading to VSR formation and cardiogenic shock.

Despite 3 decades of research, stress-induced cardiomyopathy remains poorly understood. The current literature suggests the underlying pathophysiology involves elevated catecholamine levels and a proinflammatory state that contributes to cardiac dysfunction. Our patient had several conditions that predisposed to stress-induced cardiomyopathy, including being female, elderly, and postmenopausal and having comorbid depression. We suspect that electrical cardioversion was the precipitating trigger for TTS, given the temporal association and proximity of electrical cardioversion with the return to the hospital within 24 hours.

Electrical cardioversion is considered safe with low risk of complications. Commonly reported complications of electrical cardioversion include skin burns, arrhythmias, temporary chest pain, and thromboembolism.6 Stress-induced cardiomyopathy has been reported as a complication of electrical cardioversion. Thangjui et al.,7 using the national readmission database from 2018, reported the incidence of TTS within 30 days postcardioversion at 2.7 events per 10,000 procedures, or less than 0.03% of procedures. The authors also performed a systematic review of 13 published reports of electrical cardioversion-associated TTS.7 Our case report shares some similarities with the patients included in the systematic review. The presentation of TTS occurred within 48 hours of electrical cardioversion, a pattern also observed in our case. The presentation of cardioversion-associated TTS in the systematic review included acute respiratory distress with pulmonary edema due to heart failure (84.6%), chest pain (15.4%), pulseless electrical activity cardiac arrest (15.4%), and cardiogenic shock (30.8%). Of the variants of stress-induced cardiomyopathy, the apical variant was noted in 12 of 13 reports. Our patient also presented with acute heart failure and cardiogenic shock with apical variant stress-induced cardiomyopathy. Only 1 patient died among the 13 published reports included in the systematic review. Our report adds to the literature that concludes that electrical cardioversion is a trigger for stress-induced cardiomyopathy. Although the outcomes from the prior report had a favorable prognosis, there is a risk of mortality when stress-induced cardiomyopathy is complicated by cardiogenic shock, with an even higher risk of mortality when complicated by a mechanical complication of VSR, which we highlight in this case.

All-cause mortality from stress-induced cardiomyopathy is estimated to be 5.6% per patient-year. Stress-induced cardiomyopathy is associated with low rates (<2%) of MI, cerebrovascular events, or recurrence. In-hospital complications, including ventilator support or catecholamine use, were reported in 12% to 14% of cases.4 Cardiogenic shock occurs in near 6% of stress-induced cardiomyopathy admissions.8 In-hospital mortality is nearly 6-fold higher in stress-induced cardiomyopathy patients with cardiogenic shock compared with those without. Interestingly, patients who otherwise are considered less likely to develop stress-induced cardiomyopathy, such as younger, male patients, are more likely to develop cardiogenic shock from stress-induced cardiomyopathy. Potential causes of cardiogenic shock in stress-induced cardiomyopathy include extensive left ventricular wall motion abnormality (i.e., pump failure), right ventricular involvement, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, and acute mitral regurgitation.9 A less commonly noted cause of cardiogenic shock from stress-induced cardiomyopathy includes VSR formation or free wall rupture.

The current literature has rare reports of VSR or free wall rupture from stress-induced cardiomyopathy and none from electrical cardioversion-induced TTS.5 The reason for these potential mechanical complications may be from the myocardial injury, dilation of the ventricles, proinflammatory state, or increased myocardial stress during the acute heart failure event. As with post ST-elevation MI complications, mortality rates with VSR or free wall rupture are very high, and clinicians should be aware of these complications in the stress-induced cardiomyopathy setting as well. Our case differs from other cases of VSR or TTS due to the uniqueness of the etiology of VSR secondary to TTS. Our diagnosis of the entity is supported by echocardiographic and coronary angiographic data. Additionally, we highlight practical clinical learning points to increase awareness of this rare complication.

Our case highlights the following points in the care of patients during the peri- and postcardioversion phase. First, clinicians should ensure adequate sedation during the electrical cardioversion procedure to minimize the potential of electrical cardioversion triggering stress-induced cardiomyopathy. Second, postcardioversion chest pain should be evaluated with an ECG and/or imaging to identify potential stress-induced cardiomyopathy or ACS. Third, stress-induced cardiomyopathy should be in the differential diagnosis postcardioversion in patients with signs of respiratory distress or chest pain. Lastly, rare but potentially fatal mechanical complications should be considered in hemodynamically unstable patients with stress-induced cardiomyopathy.

Conclusion

Electrical cardioversion can potentially trigger the development of stress-induced cardiomyopathy, especially in patients with predisposing risk factors. Uncommonly, stress-induced cardiomyopathy patients can present with cardiogenic shock. Comprehensive echocardiographic evaluation to identify the etiology of cardiogenic shock and, although rare, an assessment for mechanical complications such as VSR should be sought in stress-induced cardiomyopathy patients complicated by cardiogenic shock.
