Low-Cost and Eco-Friendly Fabrication of Diphenylamine-Based Hyper-Cross-Linked Polymer for Water Softening
Authors: Shahid Nazeer, Rimsha Khalid, Saqlain Raza, Abdulaal Zuhayr Al-Khazaal, Isham Areej, Amin Abid, Tariq Javed, Bien Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pure, clean, and safe drinking water availability has become a burning challenge globally, especially considering that many people are consuming hard water, leading to several health concerns. This study focused on developing an efficient, cost-effective, and eco-friendly adsorbent, DPA-HCP, to remove hard water ions and improve water quality for drinking. The synthesis of DPA-HCP utilized an external cross-linking strategy, resulting in a high specific surface area (S BET) of 611.99 m2/g, indicating a significant number of available adsorption sites. Comprehensive characterization techniques such as Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were employed for the analysis of the structure, as well as the morphology of the adsorbent. There was a reduction up to 84% in water hardness by DPA-HCP under operating conditions of pH 8, with a contact time of 100 min and an adsorbent concentration of 1.2 g/L. A maximum adsorption capacity of 100.51 mg/g was identified for hard water ions, whereas kinetic analysis reveals that this adsorption process is pseudo-second order (R 2 = 0.9981), showing that the process is an exothermic and spontaneous process. The experimental data showed a very good fit with the Langmuir isotherm model with an R 2 value of about 0.984. Thermodynamic analysis gave an enthalpy change (ΔH o) of −21.52 kJ/mol, an entropy change (ΔS o) of −59.89 J/mol·K, and a Gibbs free energy change (ΔG o) of −4.306 kJ/mol. Such results accentuate DPA-HCP as a promising candidate for addressing hard-water-related problems, thus providing hope for improving access to safe and clean drinking water all over the globe. Furthermore, its eco-friendliness, easy synthesis, and low cost make it a strong candidate to replace costly adsorbents such as ion-exchange resins.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 60% of the world’s water supply is classified as hard water. In the United States, about 85% of households have hard water, with varying degrees of hardness depending on the region. The degree of water hardness varies significantly by region, with certain areas experiencing particularly high levels due to geological factors. Hard water is generally considered harmful to health. Some specific issues associated with hard water include skin irritation and dryness, hair damage, cleaning issues, dental issues, and scale formation. Hard water affects individuals, particularly those with sensitive skin conditions such as eczema or dermatitis. Hard water can make hair feel dry, brittle, and dull. The minerals present in hard water can bind to the hair shaft, causing it to become less flexible and more prone to breakage. Besides, there is a potential link between hard water and dental health issues. It may contribute to dental erosion, particularly in areas with high fluoride concentrations. Additionally, the minerals in hard water can contribute to the formation of dental calculus (tartar), which can increase the risk of gum disease and tooth decay. The salts present in hard water can accumulate in pipes, appliances, and fixtures, leading to scale formation. This scale can reduce the efficiency and lifespan of water heaters and boilers. Soaps and detergents are rendered less effective in the presence of hard water. The minerals present in hard water, when in contact with soap molecules, produce insoluble compounds, which are called soap scum. This often results in soap residues on surfaces and stiffening of fabrics.

To remove the total hardness of water, agricultural waste products and residues, such as peels and industrial byproducts, are utilized as effective and sustainable adsorbents. , One of the most important adsorbents is chitosan. This compound contains amino (NH2) as well as hydroxyl groups (OH). Such groups are very active against various pollutants and hard water ions. − Nano adsorbents are now used, which are based on nanomaterials. Nanomaterials/nanocomposites, as well as hydrogels, are used for water purification and softening. , Also, carbon-based materials like graphene, graphene oxide, and nanocomposites of graphene are studied, which are efficient for water contamination removal. ,

The innovative and sustainable methodology of Pentamwa et al. employs waste plastic materials for the removal of water hardness using groundwater as a medium, hence promising to tackle the water quality problem. The waste plastic was collected and modified to form cationic resins. The results show that the hardness removal efficiency was 43%. In recent times, polymeric materials have gained popularity for their dual advantages of effectively removing pollutants and hard water ions, while being highly cost-effective. Among these polymers, porous polymeric materials are used extensively. As far as porous materials are concerned, many advantages exist over their nonporous materials, particularly because they exhibit increased porosity, allowing a greater surface area and higher efficiency toward the removal of water hardness. − Of them all, hyper-cross-linked polymers (HCPs) are one of the highly appreciated and highly efficient porous polymeric adsorbents. With a small pore size and a very high surface area, they can adsorb both pollutants and hard water ions. Moreover, they have significant implications not only in science but also in our day-to-day life, such as in environmental applications, drug delivery systems, and renewable energy sources. ,

Thus, Davankov and co-workers first synthesized HCPs in the 1970s, with polystyrene serving as the precursor of polymerization. Linear polystyrene chains were subjected to Friedel–Crafts reactions to give rise to structural bridges between neighboring aromatic rings while maintaining the remainder of the chains in a swollen state. This methodology then initiated the development of HCPs in their capacity as extremely efficient porous materials. The resulting product features HXL-type structures with micropores, contributing to a high surface area. , Subsequently, Jerabek and Sherrington advanced the work on HXL materials by employing an internal electrophile-based method. They introduced vinylbenzyl chloride (VBC) as a comonomer and proceeded with chloromethylation via Friedel–Crafts alkylation. Friedel–Crafts reactions remain a predominant method for synthesizing HCPs, which can be produced through condensation reactions using small building blocks or monomers. ,−

The vast availability of aromatic compounds allows for the synthesis of diverse hyper-cross-linked polymers, enhancing their applicability in various fields. , Xu et al. developed a novel compound called POP-TCE-15 by incorporating triptycene with crown ether 15. These alterations aimed to enhance both the rigidity and the porosity of the polymer structure. Preparation of the above-mentioned HCP was accomplished through a Friedel–Crafts alkylation reaction between triptycene and crown ether-15, leading to the formation of the desired porous organic polymer. The researchers found that the modified compound exhibited remarkable effectiveness in water treatment applications and demonstrated good recyclability. Even after five cycles, POP-TCE-15 retained its efficacy. The reported removal efficiency for methyl blue was 787.4 mg/g, while that for RhB was 421.9 mg/g. − Jiang et al. developed a novel polymer called Noria-POP-1 by incorporating Noria as a monomer. The researchers aimed to enhance the desired characteristics and adsorption capacity of the organic polymers. The synthesis reaction for the preparation of Noria-POP-1 involved Noria and aryl diamine via diazo coupling. The modification of the polymer gave it high functionality and porosity for adsorption applications, especially for dye removal from wastewater. The adsorption capacities reported for methyl blue, Rhodamine B, and neutral red were 2434, 855, and 590 mg/g, respectively. Rahimpour et al. described further studies regarding the synthesis of a membrane material for water softening applications based on poly­(ether sulfone) and polyamide. Under the influence of poly­(ethylene glycol), poly­(vinylpyrrolidone), acrylic acid, and tritone, poly­(ether sulfone) membranes were fabricated by immersion. The thin films of polyamide were treated with 1,3-phenylenediamine and trimesoyl chloride. The outcome of the study showed that the poly­(ether sulfone) membranes removed MgSO4 and CaSO4 from water effectively under various experimental conditions. Interestingly, the polyamide-based membrane showed a higher ability to eliminate MgSO4 (200 mg/g) in comparison to the poly­(ether sulfone) membrane (101 mg/g). ,,

In this research, we report a unique DPA-HCP that consists of diphenylamine as a monomer. In contrast to traditional HCPs that mainly employ polystyrene or vinylbenzyl chloride (VBC) monomers, DPA-HCP have extra nitrogen functionalities (−NH groups). These functional groups are crucial for enhancing the adsorption affinity for hard water ions (Ca2+ and Mg2+), acting through strong electrostatic interactions and surface complexation mechanisms. Furthermore, DPA-HCP is synthesized via an external cross-linking route using carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) and ferric chloride (FeCl3) as a catalyst, allowing for better control of the porosity and surface area of the polymer material. This change in structure leads to a remarkably high surface area (611.99 m2/g), which also gives a much higher number of active adsorption sites. DPA-HCP showed a much greater adsorption capacity of 100.51 mg/g and an 84% reduction of water hardness under optimized conditions. One of the key distinguishing features of DPA-HCP is its remarkably high Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area of 611.99 m2/g, which exceeds many reported HCPs, usually in the range of 200–500 m2/g. Such a high surface area and optimum pore size distribution (2.6 nm) create active adsorption sites that consequently lead to an increased adsorption capacity (100.51 mg/g) and removal efficiency of hard water ions (84%) under optimum conditions. The DPA-HCP has excellent stability, reusability, and easy regeneration, which make it a good adsorbent among the existing materials for water softening applications. These properties make it a promising candidate for large-scale water treatment applications. In addition, regeneration efficiency has been recorded as 79.6% of its original adsorption capacity even after five adsorption–desorption cycles with mild acidic treatment (1 M HNO3). The estimated cost of synthesizing DPA-HCPs is approximately $5–10 per kg, primarily due to the use of inexpensive reagents, simple synthetic methodologies, and mild reaction conditions, which yield high-surface-area materials with efficient cross-linking. A combination of high stability and reusability qualifies it for continuous water softening systems in industrial wastewater treatment, desalination plants, and the purification of boiler feedwater.

Scaling up the synthesis of diphenylamine-based HCPs involves addressing critical factors, such as cost, raw material availability, and process optimization. The costs associated with scaling up are influenced by raw material prices, energy consumption, and reactor design. Diphenylamine and carbon tetrachloride, commonly used as cross-linkers, are commercially available; however, their prices vary depending on purity and supplier. The Friedel–Crafts alkylation process employed for cross-linking utilizes Lewis acid catalysts like FeCl3, which are relatively inexpensive but can become significant cost contributors when used in large quantities. Continuous flow synthesis has emerged as a promising method to enhance productivity, boasting space-time-yield (STY) values that are 32–117 times higher than traditional batch processes. This improvement reduces operational costs by minimizing the reaction times and energy consumption.

Raw material availability is generally sufficient for large-scale production, but ensuring consistent quality and supply chain reliability is essential for industrial applications. The choice of solvent plays a pivotal role in maintaining the microporosity and adsorption properties of HCPs. Solvents such as dichloroethane are commonly used in synthesis. Optimizing the synthesis parameters is crucial for achieving high yields and maintaining product quality during scale-up. Continuous flow reactors offer significant advantages over batch methods by enabling precise control over parameters such as the residence time, substrate concentration, reagent ratios, and temperature. For instance, increasing the weight ratio of substrate to catalyst and cross-linker from 1:3:3 to 1:5:5 significantly enhances the micropore volume (1493%) and STY (47.5%). Similarly, elevating reaction temperatures by 10 K increases the micropore volume by 84.8%, although further increases may not enhance STY.

Pore Size Distribution

Nitrogen-containing materials are highly attractive for their ability to adsorb calcium and magnesium ions, primarily due to their electron-rich nature. This property facilitates the formation of bonds between electron-deficient centers, such as calcium and magnesium ions, and electron-rich sites on the adsorbent, thereby enhancing ion adsorption. − DPA-HCP, in particular, exhibits several advantageous properties that make it suitable for this purpose. It possesses a significant surface area, a narrow distribution of pores, and a hierarchical structure of pores, all of which contribute to its effectiveness as an adsorbent.

The BET surface area of DPA-HCP is measured at 611.99 m2/g, which indicates a high availability of active sites for adsorption. DPA-HCP has a total pore volume of approximately 0.4123 cm3/g and an average pore size of around 2.6 nm. These values contribute to its effectiveness in capturing hard water ions. The narrow pore size distribution ensures that the pores are optimally sized for facilitating the diffusion of calcium and magnesium ions, while the hierarchical structure allows for efficient transport within the material. Additionally, the presence of Lewis basic sites within DPA-HCP increases its affinity for calcium and magnesium ions, making it even more effective in adsorption processes.

The adsorption isotherms for DPA-HCP were systematically measured at 273 K and 1 bar, as illustrated in Figure . These measurements provide critical insights into how DPA-HCP interacts with hard water ions under controlled conditions. The large surface area with appropriate pore structure and functional chemical properties makes DPA-HCP a promising material for applications aimed at removing calcium and magnesium ions from water sources, ultimately contributing to improved water quality for drinking purposes.

Application of DPA-HCP

DPA-HCP can serve as an effective adsorbent to remove the hard water ions (calcium and magnesium) to make it soft. DPA-HCP has some unique properties that enable it to be used as an adsorbent. Some unique properties include a high surface area, selective adsorption, specific functional groups, stability, and easy synthesis. However, the DPA-HCP is an amine-based polymer and has some health effects when inhaled; therefore, its complete removal from water after adsorption by filtration is necessary.

The application of newly synthesized DPA-HCP (diphenylamine HCP) was studied to remove the water hardness. The adsorption study shows that DPA-HCP actively adsorbed the hard water ions from hard water and made it suitable for drinking purposes. Furthermore, the different factors that affect the rate of adsorption were also studied in batch mode and reported. The adsorption isotherm (Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherms) was also studied and reported.

Mechanism of Adsorption

The adsorption mechanism of DPA-HCP for hard water ions, specifically Ca2+ and Mg2+, is deeply rooted in molecular-level interactions that are facilitated by its unique chemical structure and functional groups. A crucial role is played by the amine functional groups (−NH) derived from the diphenylamine monomer, which effectively trap positively charged calcium and magnesium ions through electrostatic interactions. These interactions are enhanced by the nitrogen-rich framework of DPA-HCP, which provides electron-donating sites that strongly interact with electron-deficient metal ions. This nitrogen-rich framework not only enhances selectivity for hard water ions but also provides highly polar conditions that facilitate strong electrostatic interactions with divalent cations like Ca2+ and Mg2+. The structural and chemical properties of DPA-HCP, including its high porosity and exposed functional groups, significantly improve ion-exchange efficiency and promote hydrogen bonding. This versatility allows for the efficient removal of hard water ions while maintaining stability in aqueous conditions. Moreover, the nitrogen content in DPA-HCP can be controlled by varying the monomers used, enabling modified selectivity toward specific ions. This adaptability is crucial for optimizing the removal of hard water ions in different water treatment scenarios. Beyond electrostatic interactions, the π-electronic cloud of the benzene ring in DPA-HCP also plays a significant role in developing cation-π interactions with the positively charged metal ions (Ca2+ and Mg2+). These interactions further enhance the adsorption capacity of DPA-HCP, making it a versatile platform for water treatment applications. Factors Affecting the Rate of Adsorption

Effect of Contact Time

The study of the impact of contact time on the % removal of hardness of water lodged the adsorption time from 5 to 120 min while keeping the rest of the factors constant (pH 8, initial dose of adsorbent 1.4 g, temperature 25 °C, and concentration of hard water ions 100 mg/L). Initially, in the first 50 min, the % removal was marked by a rapid rise as a large number of vacant sites were available. Subsequently, the adsorption slows down, as fewer adsorption sites are available. Maximum adsorption occurred after a contact time of 100 min, with about 84% removal of hardness ions, and then remained constant. The plot of time effects on adsorption is shown in Figure a.

Effect of the Weight of the Adsorbent

The impact of changes in the adsorbent weight between 0.1 and 1.4 g, with the other parameters kept constant at pH 8, temperature 25 °C, contact time 100 min, and concentration of hard water ions 100 mg/L, was studied. Effect of pH on Adsorption

The experiments to find out the pH influence on the efficiency of hardness removal in water were conducted at pH ranging from 1 to 12, while other parameters, such as initial adsorbent dose (1.4 g), temperature (25 °C), contact time (100 min), and initial number of ions (100 mg/L), were kept constant. The initial drop in removal efficiency was noted between pH values of 1 and 2, while it showed an increase between pH values of 3 and 7. The maximum adsorption was reported at pH 8, with 84% hardness removal being achieved. Removal efficiency decreased from pH 9 to 12 beyond pH 8. At very low pH (1, 2), the highly acidic environment leads to the protonation of adsorbent active sites, limiting its ability to effectively adsorb the metal cations. As the pH increased from 3 to 7, the adsorbent surface acquired a more negative charge, enhancing the adsorption of positively charged metal ions like Ca2+ and Mg2+, thereby improving removal efficiency. Enhanced adsorption at pH 8 is likely attributed to a neutral state of surface charge of the adsorbent, which is optimal for metal ion attraction. However, increased pH (9–12) resulted in excessive negative charge on the adsorbent surface, leading to repulsion with metal hydroxide precipitates. Also, increasing the solubility of these metal hydroxides at an elevated pH must have hindered adsorption efficiency. The graph is shown in Figure c.

Effect of Temperature

The study examined the impact of the temperature on the adsorption of hard water ions onto DPA-HCP, a specific adsorbent material. The experiments were conducted within a temperature range of 0 to 60 °C, with an initial ion concentration of 100 mg/L, a contact time of 100 min, an adsorbent dose of 1.4 g, and a constant pH of 8.0. The results showed an indirect relationship between temperature and adsorption efficiency, with a gradual decrease in adsorption percentage as the temperature rose. The maximum adsorption occurred at low temperatures, with the percentage removal varying significantly from 84% at 10 °C (283 K) to 12% at 60 °C (333 K). However, it was observed that the adsorption efficiency began to decrease at temperatures above 40 °C. This decline is attributed to the exothermic nature of the adsorption process. In exothermic adsorption, heat is released during the binding of adsorbate molecules to the adsorbent surface. At higher temperatures, the increased thermal energy disrupts these interactions, causing adsorbed molecules to desorb from the surface. This weakening of the attractive forces between the adsorbate and the adsorbent reduces the adsorption capacity. Moreover, higher temperatures can shift the equilibrium of the adsorption process, favoring desorption over adsorption. Therefore, maintaining an optimal temperature is crucial for maximizing adsorption efficiency. The graph of this parameter is shown in Figure d. Effect of Interfering Ions

Experiments were conducted to investigate how interfering ions and acid and base treatments affect the adsorption capacity of DPA-HCP for hard water ions, specifically calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+), while maintaining constant parameters. The interfering ions used in these experiments included sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), iron (Fe2+), and calcium (Ca2+). The results showed that all of these ions reduced the adsorption capacity of DPA-HCP, with iron chloride (FeCl2) having the most significant negative impact, while sodium chloride (NaCl) had the least effect. This trend suggests a relationship between ionic strength and interference, where higher ionic strength leads to stronger interactions between the interfering ions and the DPA-HCP surface, hindering the adsorption of Mg2+ and Ca2+.

The presence of interfering ions like Na+ and Fe2+ can impact the selectivity of DPA-HCP for Ca2+ and Mg2+ by competing for binding sites. Monovalent ions such as Na+ may reduce the efficiency of divalent ion adsorption due to their higher mobility and lower hydration energy, allowing them to occupy active sites more readily. Divalent ions like Fe2+ can also disrupt selectivity by forming different interactions with the polymer’s functional groups. These competitive effects are influenced by factors such as ionic strength and binding site geometry, which can be optimized to mitigate interference. The selectivity of DPA-HCPs can be maintained by adjusting the polymer’s structure and operational conditions to favor Ca2+ and Mg2+ adsorption.

Moreover, the experiments examined the impact of acid and base treatments on the adsorption capacity. Acid treatment imparted positive charges to the DPA-HCP surface, which repelled the positively charged Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions, leading to decreased adsorption. Conversely, basic treatment increased the negative charges on the surface, thereby attracting Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions through electrostatic forces, resulting in enhanced adsorption. This indicates that the surface charge of DPA-HCP plays a crucial role in its adsorption capacity for hard water ions. Thus, the adsorption capacity of DPA-HCP for Mg2+ and Ca2+ can be significantly influenced via both interfering ions and surface charge alterations. Langmuir adsorption isotherm has the following mathematical form qe=QOKLCe1+KLCe The linear form of the above equation can be used for calculating Langmuir parameters. 1qe=1QoKLCe+1Qo The Langmuir isotherm model is fundamental to any adsorption process. The Langmuir isotherm pertains to the relationship between the adsorbate concentration in a solution and the quantity adsorbed onto the surface of a solid material. According to the Langmuir model, the equilibrium concentration of adsorbate (C e) is associated with the amount of adsorbed metal per gram of adsorbent at equilibrium (q e). Two important parameters derived from experimental data are the maximum monolayer adsorption capacity (Q o) and the Langmuir constant (K L).

The values of K L and Q o can be obtained from the graph plotted between 1/C e versus 1/q e on the y-axis. The slope of the graph gives the value of K L, and the intercept provides the value of Q o. Another important aspect of the Langmuir isotherm is that it depends on the equilibrium parameter R L; this is a constant that represents the separation factor. The value of K L can be calculated using the following equation 1KL=11+(1+KLCo) where C o represents the initial concentration. Adsorption characteristics are depicted by the R L value; when its value is greater than 1, adsorption is unfavorable. When its value is equal to one, then adsorption is linear. But when its value is between 0 and 1, the adsorption is favorable.

According to the graph of the Langmuir adsorption isotherm for DPA-HCP shown in Figure a, the R L value falls between 0 and 1, which indicates favorable adsorption, which obeys the Langmuir model. The q max monolayer coverage capacity was calculated as 100.51 mg/g, whereas the Langmuir isotherm constant (K L) was evaluated as 0.054 L/g. The separation factor (R L) was 0.27, which confirms that equilibrium adsorption is favorable, while the R 2 value of 0.984 supports good sorption data fitting with the Langmuir model of the isotherm.

Thermodynamics Study of Adsorption

In exothermic processes, Gibbs free energy (ΔG o), entropy (ΔS o), and enthalpy (ΔH o) have negative values, governed by the relationship. ΔG°=ΔH°−TΔS°

Value of Entropy (ΔS o) and Enthalpy (ΔH o)

The thermodynamic behavior of the adsorption process of Ca2+ and Mg2+ onto DPA-HCP is governed by the interplay of enthalpy (ΔH o), entropy (ΔS o), and Gibbs free energy (ΔG o), which collectively determine the efficiency and feasibility of the adsorption mechanism. The plot between ln K c and 1/T to adsorb Ca2+ and Mg2+ onto DPA-HCP is shown in Figure . The negative enthalpy value ΔH o = −21.52 kJ/mol indicates that the adsorption process is exothermic, meaning heat is released during the binding of ions to the adsorbent surface. This exothermic nature suggests that as temperature increases, the adsorption capacity decreases because the equilibrium shifts to favor desorption, consistent with Le Chatelier’s principle. The negative ΔH o also confirms that the adsorption process is energetically favorable at lower temperatures, making DPA-HCP more effective under cooler conditions.

Thermodynamics of adsorption.

The entropy value ΔS o = −59.89 J mol–1 K–1 being negative reflects a reduction in disorder at the adsorbent–adsorbate interface during adsorption. This indicates that when Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions are adsorbed onto DPA-HCP, they form stable and ordered interactions with the functional groups on the polymer surface. This ordering stabilizes the adsorbate–adsorbent complex but reduces the randomness in the system, contributing to selective ion capture. The Gibbs free energy (ΔG) values further validate this thermodynamic behavior. The negative ΔG o values confirm that the adsorption process is spontaneous, while their decrease with increasing temperature suggests easier adsorption at lower temperatures. The nonlinearity of van’t Hoff plot for DPA-HCP suggests that the enthalpy change is not constant over the temperature range due to temperature-dependent structural changes in polymer structure, changes in hydration, and the presence of multiple binding sites on polymers. This inverse relationship between ΔG and temperature demonstrates that lower temperatures enhance adsorption efficiency by promoting stronger electrostatic and chemical interactions between DPA-HCP and hard water ions.

Regeneration of the Adsorbent

The adsorbent’s effectiveness for use at an industrial level is particularly dependent on its regeneration capacity. Acidic media was used to study the regeneration of adsorbate. In this case, with 0.5 g of DPA-HCP loaded with hard water ions, a 1 h agitation in a 1 M nitric acid solution was carried out. The integration of nitric acid lowered the pH, thereby switching the surface charge of DPA-HCP to positive. This change in surface charge facilitated the breaking of bonds between the hard water ions and the DPA-HCP surface due to the electrostatic force of repulsion present between the positively charged DPA-HCP surface and the divalent calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+) ions. DPA-HCPs demonstrate robust stability across multiple regeneration cycles with a slight decrease in adsorption efficiency from 84% initially to 49.4% after 14 cycles (Figure ). This minimal degradation suggests that DPA-HCPs retain their structural integrity and functional groups effectively. Despite some reduction in efficacy, DPA-HCPs remain viable for industrial water treatment applications due to their ability to maintain a high level of performance across cycles. The slight decline in adsorption efficiency after each cycle is manageable, indicating that DPA-HCPs are suitable for repeated regeneration processes. Eventually, their durability supports their potential as sustainable adsorbents in industrial settings.

Conclusions and Future Prospects

The purpose of this study was to synthesize a cost-effective, efficient, and eco-friendly diphenylamine-based hyper-cross-linked polymer (DPA-HCP) and to evaluate its characterization and adsorption properties for hard water ions, specifically calcium and magnesium, to enhance its suitability for drinking purposes. The successful synthesis of DPA-HCP was achieved through an external cross-linking strategy. Characterization of the polymer was performed using various spectroscopic techniques, including FTIR, BET, SEM, and TEM. The cross-linking among monomer molecules was confirmed by FTIR. The synthesized DPA-HCP demonstrated an excellent adsorption capacity for hard water ions. The study revealed that DPA-HCP operates optimally at a pH of 8 and reaches equilibrium after 100 min, achieving a maximum hardness removal efficiency of 84%. However, the presence of interfering ions in real water samples resulted in a removal efficiency of 78%. Unlike traditional adsorbents, the DPA-HCP has unique features, especially high surface area, chemical stability, thermal stability, mechanical strength, rapid adsorption kinetics, eco-friendliness, and economic synthesis, which make it novel from other adsorbents. These properties, along with high efficiency, qualify it for continuous water softening systems in industrial wastewater treatment, desalination plants, and the purification of boiler feedwater. Besides, in industrial relevance, DPA-HCP can be used as a sustainable alternative to conventional, costly ion-exchange resins. The adsorption process was best fitted by the Langmuir adsorption isotherm model, with a separation factor (R L) of 0.27, indicating favorable equilibrium adsorption. Also, the adsorption isotherms suggest that the reaction obeys the pseudo-second order kinetic model and is exothermic.

These findings demonstrate the potential of DPA-HCP as an effective solution for mitigating the adverse effects of hard water consumption, contributing to ensuring access to clean and safe drinking water. The results of this study encourage further research to evaluate the DPA-HCP performance under continuous flow conditions and real-world temperature fluctuations. After multiple cycles, the DPA-HCP may be degraded due to various factors like oxidative cleavage, hydrolysis, and mechanical degradation, which may affect the adsorption capacity of DPA-HCP. Besides, this study also encourages further research on the estimation of long-term stability, enhancing the porosity of DPA-HCP, and exploring its applications in heavy metal removal, dye removal, and energy storage.

Materials

Diphenylamine (monomer) 99%, dichloroethane (DCE) (solvent) 99%, carbon tetrachloride (cross-linker) ≥99.5%, ferric chloride (FeCl3) (catalyst) 97%, eriochrome black tea 99%, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) ≥99.5%. All chemicals used in this study were procured from Sigma Organics and used without any further purification.

Synthesis of HCP of Diphenylamine

10 mL of the solvent, i.e., dichloroethane, 0.3 g of monomer (diphenylamine), 1 mL of CCl4, and 0.3 g of ferric chloride (FeCl3) were taken in a round-bottom flask with a condenser. The whole setup was placed in the oil bath (Paraffin Oil), and the temperature was set first at 45 °C for 4–5 h. After 5 h, the temperature was increased from 45 to 80 °C and maintained for 18–20 h until the formation of the product was complete. The thick appearance of the materials was an indication that the product had formed. Once the product was formed, it was washed with ethanol to remove excess solvent, unreacted monomer, and catalyst (washing continued until the color of FeCl3 disappeared). The product was filtered and collected in a china dish. The resulting product was transferred to an oven and dried at 105 °C until completely dry. After that, the product is ground into a fine powder. The final product is a hyper-cross-linked polymer of diphenylamine (DPA-HCP) on which different analysis was performed. , The chemical equation of synthesized DPA-HCP is shown in Figure .

Adsorption Parameters Optimizations

The adsorption properties of the polymer for hard water ions were thoroughly examined through batch studies designed to optimize the contact time, dosage, and pH levels. The first step involved fixing the contact time. The contact time allowed for the adsorption was from 5 to 120 min. Dosage optimization was achieved by testing various weights ranging from 0.2 to 1.4 g. Additionally, the pH was varied across a range of 1 to 12 to assess its impact on adsorption efficiency. The adsorption isotherm study, covering three important models, namely, the Temkin, Langmuir, and Freundlich isotherms, was applied to best study the adsorption behavior.
