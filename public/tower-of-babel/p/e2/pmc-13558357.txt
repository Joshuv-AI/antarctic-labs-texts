Colorectal Cancer Outcomes Among Disaggregated Asian Subgroups: a 2006–2020 SEER Analysis
Authors: Matthew Y Zhao, Jayraan Badiee, Christina P Wang, Folasade P May
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite varying colorectal cancer (CRC) outcomes across distinct Asian American subgroups, Asian individuals are often assessed in aggregate. We performed disaggregated analyses to assess for heterogeneity in CRC outcomes across the six largest Asian origin groups in the United States.MethodsWe identified adults with a primary diagnosis of CRC using surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER) registry data from 2006 to 2020. Individuals from the six largest Asian origin groups and a referent non-Hispanic White (NHW) group were included. We identified predictors for regional or distant stage CRC at the time of diagnosis using multivariable logistic regression and predictors for CRC related death using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsOur cohort included 25,379 Asian Americans, who identified as Chinese (6585 [25.9%]), Filipino (6399 [25.2%]), Japanese (4309 [17.0%]), Vietnamese (3047 [12.0%]), Korean (2964 [11.7%]), and Asian Indian/Pakistani (2075 [8.2%]), in addition to 234,938 NHW individuals. In our multivariable logistic regression, Vietnamese (aOR 1.23; 95% CI 1.14–1.32), Korean (aOR 1.20; 95% CI 1.11–1.30), and Asian Indian/Pakistani (aOR 1.10; 95% CI 1.01–1.21) individuals had increased odds of regional or distant stage at time of diagnosis compared to NHW individuals. In our multivariable Cox model, Chinese (aHR 0.92; 95% CI 0.88–0.97) and Asian Indian/Pakistani (aHR 0.88; 95% CI 0.80–0.97) individuals had decreased likelihood of CRC related death compared to NHW individuals. Lower neighborhood-level socioeconomic status by Yost Index was associated with worse CRC outcomes in both multivariable models.ConclusionsIn this national cancer registry analysis, marked heterogeneity was observed in CRC stage and survival across disaggregated Asian American groups. Disaggregated analyses are key to accurately survey CRC trends, identify groups at highest risk, and develop culturally relevant approaches to CRC prevention and care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10620-026-10083-9.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Epidemiology, Health disparities, Disaggregation

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer related death in the United States, imposing a great burden on public health infrastructure with notable differences in outcomes by race and ethnicity groups [1, 2]. Asian American populations are traditionally understood to have favorable CRC outcomes. The most recent American Cancer Society CRC Facts and Figures Report notes that Asian Americans and Pacific Islanders have the lowest CRC incidence rates and the highest survival rates despite having the lowest screening uptake [1]. This is particularly salient as cancer remains the leading cause of death among Asian Americans, in contrast to other major racial and ethnic groups [3]. Moreover, conventional CRC disparity research tends toward assessing Asian populations in aggregate, overlooking important distinctions that exist between subgroups in lifestyle and health behaviors which shape CRC risk [2, 4–7].

Investigating CRC outcomes in this population through a disaggregated lens is a critical step towards optimizing public health strategies, as Asians represent the fastest growing race/ethnicity group in the United States, and are projected to double by 2060 [8]. A prior study examining CRC incidence across Asian Americans revealed elevated rates among Japanese and Southeast Asian groups comparable to those observed in non-Hispanic Black populations [9]. A recent study evaluating disaggregated Asian American mortality records demonstrated substantial heterogeneity and increasing CRC mortality rates among Korean and Vietnamese populations [10]. Although valuable, these findings were limited by death certificate misclassification and lack of individual-level clinical data. Existing studies on CRC outcomes using more robust data sources like surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER) incorporate disaggregation by Asian origin group, but are limited in their reliance on broad regional categorizations (eg, East Asian and Southeast Asian). Moreover, prior studies assess CRC outcomes but largely do not integrate multi-level determinants to explain observed differences. Thus, we aimed to perform a comprehensive population-based analysis using SEER registries to characterize variation in stage at diagnosis and CRC related death using individual- and neighborhood-level factors among the six largest Asian origin groups. Our study aims to provide more granular estimates of CRC outcomes within disaggregated Asian American groups, with the goal of informing targeted cancer prevention and care strategies for this rapidly growing and diverse population.

Methods

Population and Data Sources

We assessed CRC outcomes among disaggregated Asian subgroups using SEER 22 registry data (17 registries spanning 2006 to 2020) including census tract-level socioeconomic status (SES) attributes obtained via SEER*Stat version 9.0.4 [11]. We included individuals aged ≥ 18 with a primary diagnosis of CRC, with histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, or signet-ring cell carcinoma. We excluded individuals with other concurrent or prior cancer diagnoses. We included individuals from the six largest Asian origin groups in the United States: Chinese, Filipino, Japanese, Vietnamese, Korean, and Asian Indian/Pakistani, with non-Hispanic White (NHW) individuals serving as the referent group.

Statistical Analyses and Study Outcomes

We used a multivariable logistic regression model to assess patient- and neighborhood-level factors associated with regional or distant stage at CRC diagnosis, as well as a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model to assess factors associated with CRC related death. For the Cox model, survival time was defined as the number of months from diagnosis to last known follow-up, with a minimum survival time of 0.5 months assigned to individuals with 0 months of follow-up. Failure was defined as death attributed to CRC, and individuals were censored at last known follow-up if death did not occur. Our model violated the proportional hazards assumption, therefore the analysis was performed using robust standard error estimates. In both models we controlled for age, sex, Asian origin group, urban residence, tumor location, as well as neighborhood-level SES by Yost index and persistent census tract poverty status. The Yost index is a composite measure including median household income, median house value, median rent, percent less than 150% of the federal poverty level (FPL), education level, percent in working-class occupations, and percent unemployed [12]. Census tract-level persistent poverty is defined as residing in a county for which at least 20% of the population has lived below the FPL for at least 30 years [13]. Tumors in the cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and transverse colon were designated as right sided; tumors in the splenic flexure, descending colon, and sigmoid colon were designated as left sided; and tumors at the rectosigmoid junction or in the rectum were designated as rectosigmoid. To allow for direct comparisons across Asian American subgroups, we conducted a supplementary analysis restricted to Asian American individuals, using Chinese individuals as the reference group. We also generated survival curves from a Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for age, sex, stage at diagnosis, tumor location, Yost Index, persistent census tract poverty, and urbanicity to demonstrate differences in survival by Asian origin group. A log-rank test was performed to compare survival across Asian origin groups. Data were managed and analyzed using Stata/MP 19.0 (StataCorp LLP, College Station, TX). P-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort Demographics and Characteristics

Among adults diagnosed with CRC included in SEER data from 2006 to 2020, 25,379 Asian individuals identified as Chinese (6,585 [25.9%]), Filipino (6399 [25.2%]), Japanese (4309 [17.0%]), Vietnamese (3047 [12.0%]), Korean (2964 [11.7%]), and Asian Indian/Pakistani (2075 [8.2%]). An additional 234,938 NHW individuals were included as the referent group. Among all individuals included for analysis, median age was 66.0 (56.0–76.0), 138,049 (53.0%) were male, 32,404 (12.6%) fell within the lowest Yost Quintile (most disadvantaged), and 15,044 (5.8%) lived in persistent poverty (Table 1). At the time of CRC diagnosis, 106,319 (40.8%) had localized disease, whereas 105,417 (40.5%) and 48,591 (18.7%) were found to have regional and distant stage CRC respectively. CRC anatomic location was distributed as 103,086 (39.6%) right sided, 70,529 (27.1%) left sided, 81,682 (31.4%) rectosigmoid, and 5030 (1.9%) location not specified. Treatment modalities, urban residence, survival months, and CRC related death are also detailed in Table 1.

Excluding individuals with unspecified CRC anatomic location (1.9%), NHW individuals had the greatest proportion of right sided CRC (40.7%), whereas Filipino individuals had the greatest proportion of left sided CRC (37.8%), and Asian Indian/Pakistani had the greatest proportion of rectosigmoid CRC (42.5%) (Fig. 1A). All Asian subgroups had greater rates of left sided and rectosigmoid CRC relative to NHW individuals. Japanese individuals had the greatest proportion of localized disease at time of CRC diagnosis (41.4%), whereas Vietnamese individuals had the greatest proportion of both regional (44.7%) and distant stage (19.8%) (Fig. 1B). Korean individuals had the greatest proportion living in persistent poverty (9.0%), followed by Vietnamese individuals (6.9%) and NHW individuals (5.9%). Whereas NHW individuals had the greatest proportion within the Yost index 1st quintile (13.2%), Asian Indian/Pakistani individuals had the greatest proportion within the Yost index 5th quintile (53.0%) (Fig. 1C). Among Asian subgroups, Vietnamese individuals had the highest rates of CRC related death (28.2%), followed by Korean (28.0%) and Japanese individuals (26.5%). Asian Indian/Pakistani individuals had the lowest rates of CRC related death (21.0%).

Regional or Distant Stage at CRC Diagnosis

Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that Vietnamese (aOR 1.23, 95% CI 1.14,1.32), Korean (aOR 1.20, 95% CI 1.11,1.30), and Asian Indian/Pakistani (aOR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01,1.21) individuals had increased odds of regional or distant stage at the time of CRC diagnosis relative to NHW individuals (Table 2). Lower neighborhood-level SES by Yost index was also associated with increased odds of regional or distant stage at time of diagnosis, in which quintiles 1 through 4 were associated with regional or distant stage relative to quintile 5 (Yost index quintile 1 [aOR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06,1.13], quintile 2 [aOR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06,1.12], quintile 3 [aOR 1.08, 95% CI 1.06,1.11], quintile 4 [aOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03,1.07]). Compared to left sided CRC, right sided tumors were associated with increased odds of regional or distant stage at time of diagnosis (aOR 1.14, 95% CI 1.12,1.17), whereas rectosigmoid tumors were associated with decreased odds of regional or distant stage (aOR 0.97, 95% CI 0.95,0.99). Urban residence (aOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.08) was associated with modestly increased odds of regional or distant stage at presentation. Baseline characteristics of Asian-only subgroups are presented in Supplementary Table 1, and results of the logistic regression analysis using Chinese individuals as the reference group are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

CRC Related Death

In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, both Chinese (aHR 0.92, 95% CI 0.88,0.97) and Asian Indian/Pakistani (aHR 0.88, 95% CI 0.80, 0.97) individuals had decreased risk of CRC related death compared to NHW individuals (Table 2). Yost index quintiles 1 through 4 were associated with increased risk of CRC related death compared to quintile 5, and demonstrated a stepwise increase in risk of CRC related death with worsening SES (Yost index quintile 1 [aHR 1.30, 95% CI 1.26,1.34], quintile 2 [aHR 1.20, 95% CI 1.17,1.23], quintile 3 [aHR 1.14, 95% CI 1.12,1.17], quintile 4 [aHR 1.09, 95% CI 1.07,1.12]). Moreover, residence in a persistent poverty census tract was also associated with increased risk of CRC related death (aHR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01,1.09). Both right sided (aHR 1.14, 95% CI 1.12,1.17) and rectosigmoid tumor location (aHR 1.15, 95% CI 1.12,1.17) were associated with increased risk of CRC related death compared to left sided CRC location. Regional (aHR 3.40, 95% CI 3.32,3.49) and distant (aHR 20.40, 95% CI 19.93,20.88) metastatic disease were associated with increased risk of CRC related death compared to localized CRC. Whereas increasing age (aHR 1.03, 95% CI 1.03,1.03) was associated with increased risk of CRC related death, female sex (aHR 0.93, 95% CI 0.92,0.94) was associated with decreased risk of CRC related death. Results of the Cox proportional hazards analysis restricted to Asian American subgroups, with Chinese individuals as the referent, are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Discussion

In this population-based analysis using SEER registries, we identified distinct differences in CRC burden and death across the six largest Asian origin groups in the United States, with variation observed across individual- and neighborhood-level characteristics. Vietnamese, Korean, and Asian Indian/Pakistani individuals with CRC had increased odds of presenting with regional or distant stage relative to NHW individuals. Chinese and Asian Indian/Pakistani individuals had lower risk of CRC related death compared to NHW individuals, whereas Vietnamese individuals had the lowest CRC survival among all groups. In a supplementary analysis restricted to Asian American subgroups, Vietnamese individuals also had increased risk of CRC related death compared to Chinese individuals. Lower neighborhood-level SES was associated with increased odds of presenting with regional or distant stage, while residence in low-SES or persistent poverty census tracts was associated with an increased risk of CRC related death.

Our findings in Vietnamese Americans are consistent with prior smaller studies demonstrating more advanced disease and worse cancer survival trends in this population. In a prior SEER analysis, Vietnamese individuals were found to have the highest rates of metastatic disease for colorectal, breast, prostate, and lung cancer combined [14], while recent studies demonstrate rising CRC mortality that is projected to exceed that of NHW individuals [10]. These findings are concerning given low CRC screening uptake [15, 16] and increasing CRC incidence observed in Vietnamese populations [17]. Importantly, Vietnamese Americans experience multiple sociocultural factors that impact cancer screening uptake and cancer care, including language differences, culturally rooted beliefs about illness, and challenges navigating the healthcare system as immigrants [18]. Vietnamese Americans have among the highest rates of limited English proficiency [19–21], moreover a recent Pew Research Center report revealed Vietnamese Americans have lower education attainment and household income relative to Asian Americans overall [22]. Additional obstacles include cancer fatalism, namely the belief that cancer is unavoidable, and limited self-efficacy to complete CRC screening [23]. Altogether, these factors likely contribute to delays in diagnosis and underscore the need for linguistically and culturally tailored interventions to address gaps across the cancer continuum in Vietnamese American populations.

Our finding that Korean Americans had increased odds of presentation with regional or distant stage is consistent with existing studies [24, 25]. Prior studies have also demonstrated increasing CRC incidence and mortality rates among Korean Americans [10, 26, 27]. Altogether, these findings are concerning given that CRC is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among Korean Americans [3], and that Korean Americans have some of the lowest CRC screening rates [15]. While language differences and low health literacy have been identified as barriers, fear regarding familial burden as a consequence of cancer detection may represent a culturally-salient obstacle [28–30]. Moreover, cancer stigma appears to be particularly relevant in Korean communities, manifesting as fear that cancer diagnoses reflect negatively on an individual and their family [31]. In light of these considerations, community based interventions partnering with Korean churches have achieved notable success in improving CRC screening uptake [32, 33].

Despite having increased odds of presentation with regional or distant stage CRC, our finding that Asian Indian/Pakistani individuals had lower risk of CRC related death compared to NHW individuals is supported in the literature [16]. Asian Indians have the highest median household income among Asian Americans, and although we controlled for neighborhood-level SES, unmeasured individual-level factors may contribute to observed survival differences [34, 35]. Additionally, while numerous studies affirm higher CRC survival among Asian Indian/Pakistani Americans than NHW individuals [16, 36, 37], literature on stage at diagnosis is mixed. A recent SEER analysis suggested upward trends in regional and distant stage rectal adenocarcinoma in Asian Indian/Pakistani individuals [37], and indeed, we found this group to have the greatest proportion of rectosigmoid CRC. Asian Indians have consistently demonstrated low CRC screening uptake, therefore initiatives to improve cancer prevention efforts in this population are critical [3, 38, 39].

Our findings that neighborhood-level SES measures are associated with regional or distant stage and worse survival align with extensive literature on CRC outcomes among under-resourced populations [40–42]. Low SES by Yost index has also specifically been linked to both worse CRC survival and presentation with metastatic disease [4, 43]. We found that the odds of presentation with regional or distant stage and risk of CRC related death increased in a stepwise fashion with worsening Yost quintile. Our models included both Yost Index and persistent census tract poverty, and although both measures share conceptual overlap as area-level SES measures, collinearity diagnostics did not suggest meaningful multicollinearity. Moreover, the Yost Index reflects a composite cross-sectional measure of disadvantage, whereas persistent census tract poverty captures sustained longitudinal disadvantage. Neighborhood-level disadvantage reflects structural barriers such as income and educational attainment, but may also extend to reduced primary and specialty care availability, transportation constraints, and limited access to culturally concordant care that may impair screening and delay CRC diagnosis. Prior studies have revealed increased stomach and liver cancer risk in Asian Americans residing in areas characterized by not only greater socioeconomic disadvantage, but also lower levels of acculturation [44, 45]. Additional studies have explored the potential intersection of nativity with neighborhood SES context, where foreign-born Chinese, Filipino, Korean, and Vietnamese individuals with CRC were more likely to reside in low SES neighborhoods [46].

Our study features notable limitations. Although patients in SEER are generally representative of the broader population, SEER does not reflect a full representation of the United States [47]. The retrospective nature of this data lends itself to inherent selection bias. Moreover, although SEER includes important patient-level variables, it lacks insight on individual level socioeconomic factors, such that our analyses were limited to surrogate estimates of disadvantage based on neighborhood characteristics from census data. Treatment was also excluded as a covariate given that SEER encodes treatment in broad categorical terms that would not accurately characterize its role in CRC-related death, and future studies using datasets with more granular treatment data will be better positioned to examine treatment as a mediator of survival differences. Urban residence was associated with increased odds of regional or distant stage CRC in our multivariable model, however the association is weak and lacks clinical significance. The limited variability in urban residence across our cohort suggests this association is likely driven by sample size rather than a meaningful underlying relationship. In addition to data driven limitations, our study chose to focus on the six largest Asian origin groups but is not comprehensive in capturing all Asian American populations due to limited case counts. Future studies will benefit from more granular patient-level data, as well as dedicated analyses and methods for evaluating smaller subgroups.

This study represents one of the largest and most comprehensive analyses of CRC outcomes among disaggregated Asian Americans. SEER contains a high proportion of Asian Americans that enables disaggregated analyses that would not otherwise be feasible in smaller or less diverse datasets [47]. In addition to the scale of disaggregation, our analyses are notable for the integration of area-level metrics for a more nuanced evaluation of the impact of neighborhood disadvantage in the context of racial disparities. Collectively, our study highlights marked heterogeneity across CRC outcomes within Asian Americans, and provides the foundation for future research and tailored public health efforts.

Asian Americans represent one of the fastest growing populations in the United States, however research around their health outcomes have traditionally been evaluated in aggregate masking important differences in cultural beliefs, health behaviors, risk profiles, and immigration patterns [8]. As demonstrated in our study, more granular data collection by origin group is essential to understanding population health needs and for informing tailored, community-specific strategies to address cancer disparities. Indeed, considerable efforts have been made to improve CRC outcomes in Asian American communities using targeted, culturally relevant approaches [23, 28, 32, 33, 48–51]. Efforts such as these benefit from strategic optimization for the specific communities they serve, and are key to designing interventions that meet the needs of diverse Asian communities facing CRC.
