Estimating and discovering heterogeneous treatment effects using machine learning in epidemiological studies: a practical guide
Authors: Toshiaki Komura, Falco J Bargagli-Stoffi, Onyebuchi A Arah, Kosuke Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine learning-based heterogeneous treatment effect (HTE) estimation and discovery have recently received substantial attention in the healthcare literature. In particular, meta-learner frameworks and causal forests have been widely used in estimating the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). Such advances in HTE estimation and discovery have allowed researchers to assess HTE patterns in their data. Here, we provide a comprehensive and practical guide as well as statistical codes for researchers to implement these models effectively. Specifically, we provide an overview of core motivations for HTE analysis. Then, we describe a methodological overview of popular machine learning algorithms for CATE estimation and how to calibrate their model fit. After demonstrating an application example using a national sample of US older adults, we discuss some critical and practical considerations of HTE analysis with highly granular CATE. Finally, we discuss the assessment of HTE, including the scale and reference point, as well as the interpretation of CATE. Overall, this paper aims to equip researchers with both the conceptual understanding and practical tools necessary to apply machine learning-based HTE analysis in epidemiological research, including both randomized controlled trials and observational studies.Keywords: heterogeneous treatment effect, effect-measure modification, conditional average treatment effect, machine learning, causal inference, epidemiology

Introduction

Investigating heterogeneous treatment effect (HTE) is essential to understand why a treatment works or does not work and who benefits or is harmed from the treatment. Departing from a 2-by-2 table, analytic approaches for HTE have evolved over recent decades. For example, risk modeling examines HTE by stratifying samples based on their baseline risk of outcome [1]. More recently, an increasing body of statistical literature has proposed various effect modeling strategies, which estimate treatment effects for individuals or subgroups in the study, conditioning on a multi-dimensional set of covariates [1, 2]. Such a strategy, often powered by flexible semi- or non-parametric models, allows us to assess HTE across various characteristics simultaneously. Yet, there remains a need for comprehensive and practical guidelines for researchers to apply these techniques in epidemiological studies appropriately and effectively. This article introduces a step-by-step practical guide on the machine learning approaches for estimating and discovering HTE, including (i) motivations, (ii) causal estimands, (iii) estimation techniques, (iv) analytical steps, and (v) key considerations.

Motivations for HTE estimation

The bulk of traditional causal analyses in epidemiology has focused on average treatment effects (ATE) at the population level. However, ATE can mask important variation in causal effects across subgroups or individuals. HTE is the variation of treatment effects across individuals or subgroups. In epidemiology, HTE is of particular interest in (but not limited to) the following contexts: (i) effective resource allocation via targeted intervention, (ii) understanding pathways through which the interventions affect the outcome, (iii) hypothesis generation, (iv) health equity, and (v) generalizability and transportability (Table 1). First, targeted resource allocation is a common aim of HTE analysis. The central premise is that understanding characteristics associated with larger (or smaller) benefit (or harm) enhances decision-making for treatment effectiveness and prioritization. For example, if individuals with a family history of cancer experience the largest reduction in cancer mortality from screening, targeting this subpopulation might deserve high priority. In addition, even if ATE is null, some subpopulations may receive benefit, although the ATE is diluted by other subpopulations that did not benefit from the treatment [3]. HTE could also be discussed in between-treatment comparisons (e.g. drug A versus drug B); researchers might be interested in a more effective treatment option for a particular condition, often in the context of precision medicine [4]. Second, HTE insights help us to understand the mechanism through which treatment affects the outcome. For example, the Women’s Health Initiative study revealed a protective effect of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) on cardiovascular outcomes among younger postmenopausal women, but also an elevated risk for cardiovascular outcomes was observed among older women [5]. This HTE contributed to understanding the role of effect-measure modification in MHT. Third, this example also illustrates hypothesis generation; the inconsistent relationships between MHT and cardiovascular outcomes across age groups led to the “timing hypothesis,” which posits that the time since menopause is an important determinant of the cardiovascular effect of MHT [5]. Fourth, policymakers might consider HTE in terms of health equity. Greenspace, for example, could promote physical health among individuals with low socioeconomic status (SES) more than among those with high SES, indicating that the intervention could reduce existing health disparities [6]. Instead, health disparities might be exacerbated when population intervention provides more benefits to individuals with high SES than to those with low SES [7]. Such insights into health inequality could be overlooked from naive population-level ATE estimates. Lastly, HTE is critical for evaluating the generalizability and transportability of treatment effects from study sample to target population [8, 9]. Such extrapolation could be hindered when estimates are based on samples where individuals from the target population are underrepresented or not included and the study includes an inadequate set of effect modifiers [8]. Compared to conventional linear regression models, machine learning algorithms allow for more flexible model specification, including better handling of nonlinearities, bias-variance trade-off, and higher-order interactions between high-dimensional covariates (including continuous ones) and treatment. Furthermore, data-driven effect modeling can help discover heterogeneous subgroups characterized by unknown or complex combinations of covariates, especially when prior knowledge of HTE is limited.

Causal estimands for HTE

A causal estimand commonly used to describe HTE is the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) based on causal assumptions such as conditional exchangeability, consistency, and positivity [10]. CATE is the treatment effect conditional on a given set of covariates. It can be described as E[YiA=1-YiA=0|Xi=x], where i indexes each individual, Y represents the outcome, A is the binary treatment, YA=a is the potential outcome had A been set to a, and x is a vector of covariates. HTE is considered to be present if E[YiA=1-YiA=0|Xi=x] is not constant across levels of Xi—that is, for two generic covariate values, x′≠x″, we have that E[YiA=1-YiA=0|Xi= x′]≠E[YiA=1-YiA=0|Xi=x″]. These variables need to be incorporated as effect modifiers, but not other types of variables (e.g. mediators or colliders). A highly granular CATE characterized by several or numerous covariates is sometimes described as an “individual treatment effect”—formally, E[YiA=1-YiA=0|Xi=xi]—as it represents a causal estimand corresponding to the covariates observed for each individual in the study. However, such an individual treatment effect cannot be observed in the data [11]. Instead, CATE can be extended to more detailed causal estimands. For example, group average treatment effect (GATE) is a type of CATE that is aggregated by a specific subgroup [12]. When CATE is estimated using 30 characteristics, their values can be summarized using a simpler group. Therefore, GATE is a less granular form of CATE. Because highly granular CATE from machine learning algorithms often return considerably wide confidence intervals (CIs) in practice, representation of results with GATE might yield more stable contrasts of HTE [13]. Conversely, individualized average treatment effect (IATE) refers to CATE in the most granular form. In the context of the present paper, IATE is identical to highly granular CATE but can be applied to complement GATE [14]. These extended versions of CATE could offer descriptions of HTE in more detailed ways. They should be selected in a way that best describes relevant research questions.

HTE estimation using machine learning

A conventional approach for HTE is subgroup analysis, where researchers examine effect-measure modification with prespecified covariates, typically one variable at a time. HTE is detected by assessing the degree of variation in treatment effect across the specified strata (e.g. male versus female). Although the implementation and interpretation of this analysis are intuitive, there are potential risks for false-positive and false-negative findings due to multiplicity and inadequate statistical power [15]. In contrast, HTE analysis using highly granular CATE could provide detailed and comprehensive insights, particularly for exploratory questions (i.e. whether HTE is present and what constitutes HTE) rather than hypothesis-driven questions (i.e. whether a certain variable modifies the treatment effect). In this section, we briefly introduce common statistical and machine learning methods for estimating CATE.

Meta-learners refer to algorithms that fit models using a subset of functions called base learners (outcome prediction model) [16]. Base learners can incorporate various models, including logistic regression and random forest. The meta-learner is not a specific statistical model but a framework where models converge. Commonly used meta-learner algorithms include the S-learner, T-learner, X-learner, DR-learner, and R-learner (Table 2 and Figure 1) [2, 17]. Bayesian causal forest is another useful method, which can be seen as an extension of S-learner [18]. While all models can be applied in randomized controlled trial data, X-, DR-, R-learners, and Bayesian causal forests are effective approaches in observational settings because they adjust for the unbalanced distribution of covariates between treatment and control groups (i.e. confounding). Causal forest is a popular approach for highly granular CATE estimation in healthcare literature [2, 19]. Causal forest is an algorithm constructed with multiple “causal trees” [20]. A causal tree is a single tree algorithm that splits samples using if-then rules (Figure 2A shows sample splits based on age, gender, and body mass index). The top node is known as the “root node,” and the following child nodes further divide the samples. Then, leaf nodes, the terminal nodes, indicate the predicted CATE. The sample split is designed to maximize the heterogeneity of CATEs across leaf nodes [20]. See Supplementary Method 1, in the supplementary material, for further details. To aid in inference robust to sample selection and overfitting and obtain valid CIs after model fitting, causal tree adopts an “honest” estimation approach, where a random half of the entire sample is used to build nodes, and the other half is used for CATE prediction (Figure 2B) [20]. For causal interpretation of the results obtained, the identification assumptions, including conditional exchangeability, consistency, and positivity, must be satisfied in each terminal node [21]. After repeating this process with random subsamples via bootstrapping, to mitigate overfitting, the causal forest estimates the final CATE based on the average of multiple causal trees [20]. To reduce further potential overfitting, k-fold cross-fitting can be applied (Figure 2C). Here, the total sample is divided into K subsamples, and CATE estimation for samples in fold k is performed using the remaining samples in k-1 folds. The CATE of the entire sample is estimated by repeating this prediction K times [22]. Potential confounders and effect-measure modifiers should be included as covariates [13].

Illustration of causal tree (A), honesty (B), and cross-fitting (C). SE, standard error.

It is also important to note that, in the causal_forest function of the grf package in R and the CausalForest function of the EconML package in Python, causal forest is applied using the R-learner framework by default [23]. In this approach, a causal forest model is constructed using random forest, which estimates outcome values by averaging the results of multiple decision trees. In contrast to causal trees, which are optimized for CATE in each leaf node, each decision tree in a random forest predicts the outcome using a random subsample.

Application example

We explored heterogeneous associations between spousal onset of stroke and individuals’ subsequent depressive symptoms using data of 1,835 US adults aged ≥50 from the Health and Retirement Study. We aimed to elucidate subpopulations who may benefit from support after a spousal stroke. Data-driven exploration is effective here, as the HTE of their relationships has been scarcely reported but is plausible, given the psychosocial complexity. Supplementary Figure 1 and Table 1, in the supplementary material, show the sample flow chart and how each variable was coded, respectively. Supplementary Table 2, in the supplementary material, provides the characteristics of the analytic samples. We applied default parameters [13] with cross-fitting of 10 folds [22]. The calibration performance of the trained causal forest model was first assessed by BLP analysis. Then, the sample was divided into quintiles based on the estimated CATE, computing ATE and 95% CI of each quintile via augmented inverse probability weighting [24]. To explore potential sources of HTE, we compared the characteristics of each CATE quintile. Variable importance was also assessed by the frequency with which each variable was applied to split trees in the causal forest model. Details of the study population, study design, and variable ascertainment are in Supplementary Methods 3, in the supplementary material.

BLP analysis showed reasonable model fit according to mean forest [1.05 (95% CI = 0.04-2.06)] and differential forest [0.81 (0.33–1.29)]. The quintile plot showed a monotonically increasing trend of ATEs according to the CATE quintiles (Supplementary Figure 2, in the supplementary material). Compared to CATE Q1 (estimated to be resilient to the exposure), primary participants in CATE Q5 (estimated to be vulnerable to the exposure) were more likely to be older (68.8 versus 75.0), less insured (92% versus 18%), and have lower SES (household wealth: $792,350 versus $324,120) (Supplementary Table 3, in the supplementary material). Similarly, these characteristics showed higher variable importance (Supplementary Figure 3, in the supplementary material). These results indicate that older individuals with lower SES and less access to healthcare resources might experience higher detrimental effects on their mental health after spousal stroke events. These subpopulations might particularly benefit from interventions, including counseling and home care services.

Discussion

We have introduced an overview of HTE analysis with highly granular CATE thus far. In this section, we will further discuss additional points that deserve consideration in practice.

Heterogeneity of HTE

Just as the motivation for HTE is diverse, the measure of HTE could vary. First, HTE is dependent on the chosen effect-measure scale. While HTE can be expressed using relative and absolute scales, it might be detected only on one of these scales [10]. For example, suppose males and females have baseline mortality risks of 10% and 5%, respectively. If a treatment reduces mortality risks by 2% on an absolute scale [risk difference (RD)], the relative risks [risk ratio (RR)] will be 0.80 for males and 0.60 for females. Similarly, if another treatment uniformly results in an RR of 0.50, RDs are −5% and −2.5% for males and females, respectively. In other words, variation in treatment effects across levels of specified covariates could exist on at least one effect-measure scale in this example. Because different effect measures could thus conceal HTE, understanding the distribution of baseline outcome risk as well as the benefit (or harm) could aid a nuanced interpretation of HTE findings. Likewise, it would also be important to assess HTE on both absolute and relative scales wherever possible.

Moreover, HTE is an inherently relative concept, and hence, defining a reference of comparison deserves careful consideration. When analysis is motivated by an effective allocation of limited resources, comparisons could be made between individuals with certain characteristics (i.e. who deserves higher priority than the others?). Conversely, if the main interest is the safety of treatment, HTE analysis should address characteristics associated with harmful treatment effects. In this scenario, the focus of comparison is no longer other individuals but the direction of the treatment effect. Furthermore, decision-making in the real world could involve balancing between benefits and harms. Importantly, the absence of benefit does not usually mean harm on the same outcome (e.g. side effects as harm resulting from unintended other outcome(s), while benefit might be avoiding severe illness from the primary outcome condition). While the reference group in conventional subgroup analysis is typically fixed by prespecified variables, highly granular CATE allows more flexible comparisons. Therefore, the selection of the comparison group should be explicitly discussed based on the motivation and the audience who would benefit from the HTE insights.

Interpretation of highly granular CATE

CATE represents effect modification, but not causal interaction (which arises in the case of multiple treatments) [10]. Manipulating characteristics associated with HTE is not guaranteed to affect the degree of CATE. For example, even if individuals with high body mass index (BMI) are predicted to have particularly low CATE from the treatment, the reduction in their BMI might not increase their CATE.

Moreover, highly granular CATE holds fundamental dilemmas of interpretability, although this may not be a critical issue if analysis is motivated solely by CATE estimation [25]. First, each single CATE becomes harder to understand due to the higher granularity resulting from the complex interplay of multiple characteristics. This structural issue poses a challenge to profiling subgroups or individuals tractably and meaningfully [25, 26]. Second, the information richness does not necessarily align with a decision-making process by humans. In contrast to the highly granular CATE that reflects complex information, human audiences tend to seek a simpler explanation [26]. Third, even if researchers identify characteristics that contribute to HTE, they might not be relevant to practice or policy audiences [25]. For example, although propensity scores may be informative in disentangling HTE, presenting HTE patterns by propensity score is unlikely to facilitate effective communication. These fundamental issues are troublesome when researchers attempt to identify specific characteristics associated with HTE and communicate these with decision-makers. While an emerging body of literature has proposed techniques to synthesize interpretable insights from highly granular CATE [14, 25], further investigations are needed in this field.

Conclusions

In this tutorial, we introduced a practical overview of machine learning approaches for HTE analysis, including motivations, methodological characteristics, steps in analysis, and important considerations. While further methodological development and applications in epidemiology are warranted, these approaches enable flexible detection and comparison of HTE patterns, supporting a better understanding of cause-and-effect relationships and more effective health promotion in future precision medicine and public health.
