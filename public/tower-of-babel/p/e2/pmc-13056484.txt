Body Mass Index: A Key Factor in Surgical Site Infections After Kidney Transplantation?
Authors: Mohamed El‐Mahrouk, Raphaela Sorgnitt, Gerold Schwantzer, Robert Karitinig, Derar Jaradat, Zoltan Mathe, Robert Sucher, Daniela Kniepeiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTKidney transplant recipients are at increased risk of surgical site infections (SSIs) due to procedural complexity and immunosuppression. This retrospective single‐centre study examines the influence of BMI on SSI risk and identifies common pathogens. A total of 230 renal transplant patients were included in this analysis 2017 and 2019. All patients received a cephalosporin for prophylaxis, and wound swabs were taken from those who developed SSIs. SSIs occurred in 45 patients (19.6%) and were not significantly associated with age, sex or donor type. The most common organisms were coagulase‐negative staphylococci (26.7%), Staphylococcus epidermidis (24.4%), Enterococcus faecalis (22.2%) and Candida albicans (22.2%). SSIs were significantly linked to surgical revision (p < 0.001) and higher BMI (p = 0.027). A BMI ≥ 28.1 was associated with threefold higher odds of SSIs (OR = 3.0; p = 0.001). Each one‐unit increase in BMI was associated with a 7.2% increase in the odds of SSI occurrence (OR = 1.072; p = 0.020). Staphylococcus (p = 0.019) and Enterococcus (p = 0.048) infections were more common in patients with BMI > 28.1. Therefore, SSIs are a frequent complication posttransplant and are strongly associated with high BMI. Standard antibiotic regimens may not cover all relevant pathogens in obese patients, underscoring the need for tailored prophylactic strategies.Keywords: antimicrobial prophylaxis, BMI, kidney transplantation, obesity, surgical site infections

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) occur in approximately 0.5%–3% of patients following a surgical procedure. These infections significantly prolong hospitalisation, extending the average length of stay by 7–11 days [1]. As a result, SSIs pose a considerable economic burden. A retrospective cross‐sectional study analysing data from German hospitals between 2010 and 2016 found that patients with an SSI incurred median costs approximately €10 000 higher than those without an infection (€19 008 vs. €9040) [2].

SSIs represent a significant clinical challenge, particularly among immunosuppressed patients, due to their strong association with increased morbidity and mortality [3, 4]. The incidence of SSIs varies substantially depending on the type of organ transplanted, with reported rates ranging from 3% to 53%. In the context of kidney transplantation, the incidence is estimated to be between 3% and 11% [5]. Although SSIs is a common postoperative complication, the KDIGO guidelines do not provide clear recommendations on which antibiotic should be used to reduce the risk of SSI in kidney transplantation [6, 7]. In contrast, the ‘American Society of Transplantation Infectious Diseases Community’ and the IDSA/ASHP/SIS/SHEA guidelines recommend an antibiotic prophylaxis with a first‐generation cephalosporin for ≤ 24 h [5, 8].

Studies have demonstrated that SSIs are significantly associated with an increased body mass index (BMI) [9, 10]. Nevertheless, there are few studies specifically investigating the effect of BMI on SSIs in the context of kidney transplantation. However, the current body of evidence suggests that BMI plays a crucial role to develop SSIs [11, 12, 13].

This study aims to identify the predominant pathogens causing SSIs and assesses? potential shortcomings in existing antibiotic regimens. Furthermore, it seeks to explore the role of BMI as a contributing risk factor in the occurrence of SSIs.

Methods

In this retrospective study, 230 patients who underwent kidney transplantation at our institution between 1 January 2017, and 31 December 2019, were included. This study focused on postoperative SSIs after kidney transplantation until the discharge.

The key characteristics, including age, gender, BMI (kg/m2), complications, spectrum of pathogens and type of donation, were retrospectively collected from medical records.

Surgical Technique

The surgical approach to the pelvic loop was performed via a paramedian arc incision. The vascular anastomoses were constructed as classical end‐to‐side anastomoses and sutured using a continuous Prolene suture. The ureter‐to‐bladder anastomosis was performed in Lich‐Gregoir technique to ensure an antireflux mechanism [14, 15, 16].

Anti‐Infective Prophylaxis

For antimicrobial prophylaxis, ceftriaxone 2 g was administered preoperatively and continued postoperatively until the surgical drain was removed. Valganciclovir was administered as prophylaxis against cytomegalovirus (CMV) for either 3 or 6 months, depending on the CMV serostatus match. Trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole was given for 6 months to prevent Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia.

Immunosuppression

All patients undergoing kidney transplantation received corticosteroid therapy, which was subsequently tapered, in combination with immunosuppressive treatment consisting of tacrolimus and mycophenolate mofetil. Depending on the patient's immunological risk profile, Thymoglobulin was additionally administered as induction therapy.

All patients who developed an SSI underwent a wound swab to identify the bacteria and fungi responsible for the infection.

Statistical Analysis

To assess differences in metric parameters between patients with and without SSI we used the Mann–Whitney U test and the Chi‐squared test for categorical parameters, both of them exact and two‐sided. ROC‐curve analysis was applied to get a cut‐off value for BMI that best discriminates patients with SSI and patients without. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the effect of the predictors age, sex, BMI, surgical revision and type of organ donation on developing a SSI. Correlations between metric variables were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficient (r s). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29 (Release 29.0.0.0 (241) 2022. Armonk (NY), USA: International Business Machines Corporation). A p < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. Metric data are presented as mean with bracketed standard deviation (SD) and categoric data as frequency with bracketed percentage.

Results

Patient Cohort and Clinical Outcomes

Between January 2017 and December 2019, a total of 230 kidney transplant recipients were included in this retrospective analysis. The cohort had a mean age of 53.74 (SD 12.79) years and an average BMI of 27.06 (SD 5.28). Of the patients, 150 (65.2%) were male and 80 (34.8%) were female.

Living donor transplantation was performed in 41 patients (17.8%), while 189 patients (82.2%) received kidneys from deceased donors. Surgical revision was required in 37 cases (16.1%), and graft nephrectomy during the same hospital stay was necessary in four patients (1.7%).

A total of 45 patients (19.6%) developed a SSI, comprising 14 (31.1%) females and 31 (68.9%) males. Among these, 28 patients (62.2%) were colonised with a single pathogen, 12 (26.7%) with two pathogens, two (4.4%) with three pathogens and three (6.7%) with four or more pathogens. The most frequently isolated organisms were coagulase‐negative staphylococci (26.7%), Staphylococcus epidermidis (24.4%), Enterococcus faecalis (22.2%) and Candida albicans (22.2%) (Table 1).

We found that patients with SSI showed a significantly higher BMI (p = 0.027) than patients without SSI. To determine an optimal BMI threshold for classifying patients with a higher risk for SSI, we performed a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis using Youden's J statistic. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.606 (95% CI: 0.509–0.703; p = 0.027), indicating modest discriminative ability. The maximum value of Youden's J (Jmax = 0.264), corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.556 and a specificity of 0.708, was observed at a BMI cut‐off of 28.1.

Patients who developed an SSI were significantly more likely to require vacuum‐assisted closure (VAC) therapy for wound management (p < 0.001) (data not shown).

No significant difference was observed in the length of hospital stay between patients with and without SSIs (p = 0.068) (data not shown).

Regression Analyses

In univariable logistic regression, BMI (p = 0.020), surgical revision (p < 0.001) and graft explantation (p = 0.027) were significantly associated with increased odds of microbial colonisation.

Each one‐unit increase in BMI was associated with a 7.2% increase in the odds of developing an SSI (odds ratio [OR] = 1.072; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.011–1.138; p = 0.020). Patients with a BMI ≥ 28.1 had a threefold higher odds of developing a SSI compared to those with a BMI < 28.1 (OR = 3.0; 95% CI: 1.6–5.9; p = 0.001).

Patients undergoing surgical revision had a more than fourfold higher odds of SSI (OR = 4.3; 95% CI: 2.0–9.2; p < 0.001), while graft explantation was associated with an even higher odds (OR = 13.1; 95% CI: 1.3–129.5; p = 0.027) (Table 3).

Multivariable regression confirmed the significance of BMI and surgical revision as independent risk factors for SSI. However, graft explantation was no longer statistically significant in the multivariate model.

Association With Number of Pathogens

A weak significant positive correlation was observed between the number of isolated pathogens and both BMI (r s = 0.14, p = 0.037) and length of hospital stay (r s = 0.13, p = 0.044). No significant associations with the number of pathogens were identified for age (p = 0.821), sex (p = 0.540) or donor type (p = 0.181). However, patients with a higher number of isolated pathogens were significantly more likely to have required surgical revision (p < 0.001), VAC therapy (p < 0.001) and graft explantation (p = 0.002) (data not shown).

Furthermore, we analysed whether patients with a BMI > 28 had a significantly higher rate of infections with specific pathogens. This subgroup showed a significantly increased incidence of Staphylococcus (p = 0.019) and Enterococcus (p = 0.048) infections. No significant differences were observed for Lactobacillus species (p = 0.316), Klebsiella species (p = 0.612) or Candida infections (p = 0.500).

Discussion

SSIs remain a common postoperative complication in kidney transplant recipients, with an incidence of nearly 20% in our cohort. While the increased susceptibility due to immunosuppression is well recognised, few studies have systematically investigated specific risk factors contributing to SSI in this population. There is growing evidence from studies that BMI is a significant contributor to the risk of SSIs [4, 12, 13].

In our analysis, BMI emerged as a significant predictor of SSI in univariable (p = 0.020) and multivariable (p = 0.034) regression models. A weak but statistically significant correlation was observed between BMI and the number of isolated pathogens (r s = 0.14, p = 0.037). Notably, while standard BMI classifications define obesity at ≥ 30 kg/m2 [17], our findings indicate that even preobese individuals with a BMI above 28 exhibit a significantly elevated SSI odds. These results underscore the importance of considering elevated BMI as a modifiable risk factor in the perioperative management of kidney transplant recipients.

These findings prompt the question of how the incidence of SSIs in obese kidney transplant recipients can be effectively reduced. In our cohort, one shot ceftriaxone was routinely administered for perioperative prophylaxis and continued postoperatively until the surgical drain was removed. However, current KDIGO guidelines provide limited and nonspecific recommendations regarding the optimal choice and dosing of perioperative antibiotics in kidney transplant recipients. Obesity is associated with an increased risk of complications, as outlined in the guidelines. While weight reduction is specifically recommended in this context, no adjustments to antibiotic prophylaxis are currently suggested [6]. The lack of specificity may be through regional variability in antimicrobial resistance patterns. In contrast, the ‘American Society of Transplantation’ guidelines recommend perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis with cefazolin. However, in obese patients, only a weight‐adjusted dosage is advised—for example, cefazolin 3 g for patients weighing more than 120 kg [5]. As a result, to our knowledge there is currently no evidence on a modified antibiotic prophylaxis in obese patients.

With regard to antifungal treatment, both the KDIGO and the American Society of Transplantation guidelines recommend its use only in high‐risk patients [5, 6]. In our cohort, C. albicans was isolated in 22.2% of patients with SSIs, indicating that fungal pathogens contributed to more than one in five cases. A review demonstrated that fungal infections caused by Candida species occur in 1%–2% of all kidney transplantations and are associated with a mortality rate of 10%–40% [18]. C. albicans has the ability to form biofilms, which are highly resistant to antifungal drugs, immune responses and environmental stresses—making infections difficult to treat [19]. These findings underscore the potential value of targeted antifungal prophylaxis in high‐risk populations, such as obese kidney transplant recipients. Well‐designed prospective studies are needed to assess the effectiveness and clinical benefit of prophylactic antifungal therapy in this subgroup.

Among patients who developed SSIs, S. epidermidis was identified in approximately one in four cases (24.4%). Notably, this pathogen is not covered by the antimicrobial spectrum of ceftriaxone. One study showed that S. epidermidis biofilms impair wound healing by delaying reepithelialisation in a murine model [20]. A further study showed that S. epidermidis contributes to delayed healing in chronic wounds through its multidrug resistance, robust biofilm formation and pro‐inflammatory properties [21]. E. faecalis , associated with 22.2% of SSIs, similarly to S. epidermidis , shows a gap in susceptibility to ceftriaxone. A mouse model demonstrated that E. faecalis infection induces an anti‐inflammatory state in keratinocytes and fibroblasts, impairing immune responses and delaying wound healing [22]. Ultimately, colonisation with both bacterial species appears to have a detrimental impact, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. As a result, the recurrent isolation of these organisms, particularly in patients with a BMI > 28, raises the question of whether the standard prophylactic regimen should be broadened in high‐risk individuals.

In our study, no significant associations were found between SSI occurrence and patient age, sex or type of donor organ. However, patients who developed SSIs were significantly more likely to undergo graft explantation (p = 0.024), surgical revision (p < 0.001) and require VAC therapy (p < 0.001). The association between SSI and the increased need for revision surgery and VAC therapy is clinically plausible and consistent with the severity of the infectious complications observed.

Based on these findings, a broadened perioperative antimicrobial regimen—for example, including piperacillin/tazobactam and ketoconazole—may be reasonable in patients with a BMI over 28. However, the efficacy and safety of this approach should be evaluated in a prospective clinical trial.

Limitations

This study is retrospective in nature and therefore inherently subject to the limitations of this study design, including potential selection and documentation biases. Detailed demographic data, including comorbidities, were not collected, as the study focused specifically on BMI. Nevertheless, data collection for the variables included was largely complete, with minimal missing data.

The most significant limitation is that the observation period was restricted to the in‐hospital postoperative course, which may have led to an underestimation of SSIs occurring after discharge. The average length of hospital stay in our cohort was 12 days, whereas wound healing in immunosuppressed patients typically extends over several weeks. As a result, late‐onset SSIs may not have been captured in this analysis.

Additionally, although BMI was analysed as the primary exposure, the observational design limits causal inference. The selection of covariates for adjustment in multivariable logistic regression was constrained by the small number of events (N = 45) and the low frequency of certain subgroups (e.g., graft explantation). As a result, the adjusted models may not fully account for potential confounders, and the wide CIs reflect this uncertainty. Therefore, associations between BMI and SSI should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal, and future studies with larger cohorts and carefully selected confounders are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

SSIs remain a common complication following kidney transplantation. Our findings indicate that BMI is a significant risk factor for the development of SSI. These results suggest that patients with an elevated BMI may benefit from a modified perioperative prophylactic regimen, potentially including antifungal agents. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and optimise infection prevention strategies in high‐risk transplant recipients.
