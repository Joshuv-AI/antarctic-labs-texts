Ppb-level formaldehyde sensor utilizing a compact 3D-printed differential photoacoustic cell and a 320 nm UV laser
Authors: Xiu Yang, Biao Li, Xiao Geng, Tianbai Zhao, Qiwen Yu, Jiajia Hou, Dacheng Zhang, Yize Liang, Kaijie Xu, Hongpeng Wu, Xukun Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Precise measurement of formaldehyde (H2CO) is a vital defense line for health, crucial for risk warning and prevention of major diseases like leukemia and cancer. The cross-interference of commercial electrochemical and metal oxide semiconductor sensors is grievous for H2CO sensing. Spraying disinfection alcohol, culinary steam, or even perfume may mistakenly trigger warnings. In this work, a low-cost 3D-printed differential photoacoustic cell (PAC) with a ultraviolet (UV) laser is developed for trace H2CO detection based on the photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) technology. A 3D-printed differential PAC is an integrated structure composed of two differential channels, two gas buffer chambers, a gas inlet and a gas outlet. Two steel tubes with identical length and an internal diameter of 4 mm are inserted into two differential channels to enhance the photoacoustic signal, respectively. Consequently, the differential PAC has a resonant frequency of 3775.5 Hz and a Q-factor of 27, with a minimal gas sample requirement of only 7.3 mL and a weight of 32.4 g. A 1σ detection limit of 1.03 ppm is achieved using a 320 nm 10 mW UV laser with an integration time of 1 s. An Allan-Werle deviation analysis indicates that the detection limit can be improved to 68.5 ppb at the optimal integration time of 969 s.

Introduction

Formaldehyde (H2CO) is a hazardous chemical widely present in human daily lives, often released from sources such as decorative materials and household products [1], [2]. Its adverse effects on human health are substantial, particularly concerning its carcinogenic potential [3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has classified H2CO as a carcinogen and teratogen, as well as one of the potential strong mutagens, exhibiting potent carcinogenic and cancer-promoting effects [4]. The WHO stipulates that the concentration of H2CO in residential areas must not exceed 82 part-per-billion (ppb) within 30 min [5]. Therefore, accurate measurement of indoor H2CO concentration is crucial to human health. Currently, the primary methods for H2CO detection include spectrophotometer [6], [7], colorimetric [8], [9], gas chromatography analysis [10], [11] and electrochemical analysis [12], [13]. However, these conventional techniques are generally unsuitable for online real-time monitoring of H2CO concentration, owing to their inherent limitations such as operational complexity, prolonged analytical procedures, and lack of continuous measurement capabilities.

With the rapid development of laser-based absorption spectroscopic techniques, the online real-time monitoring of H2CO concentration has long been achieved [14], [15], [16], [17]. In 2022, G. Li et al. [18] reported a H2CO detection limit of 140 part-per-trillion (ppt) with an 88 s integration time based on wavelength modulation spectroscopy and off-axis integrated cavity output spectroscopy (WM-OA-ICOS). The following year, K. Duan et al. [19] developed a battery-driven, portable optical H2CO sensor employing a mid-infrared ICL with a central wavelength of 3.6 μm and a dense pattern multi-pass cell with the effective absorption path length of 26.4 m, achieving a detection limit of 1.42 ppb with an integration time of 0.2 s. Same year, B. Fang et al. [20] utilized a compact improved spherical mirror multi-pass cell with an optical absorption path length of 50.6 m to reach a detection limit of 650 ppt. Despite its highly sensitivity, laser-based absorption spectroscopy has not been widely applied, primarily due to the high system cost.

Among various laser-based absorption spectroscopic technologies, photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) [21], [22], [23], [24], [25] has garnered significant attention due to its simple structure and relatively low cost. Photoacoustic spectroscopy employs a photoacoustic cell (PAC) [26], [27], a quartz tuning fork [28], [29], [30], [31], or a cantilever to capture photoacoustic signal for the detection of target gases. To further reduce the cost of PAS-based gas detection systems, a conventional practice is to adopt LEDs as low-cost alternatives to laser sources [32], [33], [34]. However, this approach often leads to compromised detection performance. With the increasing maturity of 3D printing technology [35], [36], a novel solution has emerged for system cost reduction, which is replacing traditionally customized stainless-steel photoacoustic cells with 3D-printed counterparts [37], [38]. In 2022, G. Zhang et al. [39] proposed a 3D-printed resonant PAC with the air volume of only 10.2 mL, achieving a detection limit of 141 ppb for acetylene (C2H2). To further demonstrate the performance of 3D-printed PAC, U. Dwivedi et al. [40] conducted a performance comparison between a 3D-printed photoacoustic gas senser and a commercial quartz enhanced photoacoustic spectrometer in 2023, demonstrating a favorable signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the 3D-printed PAC. Advancing precision in 3D printing technology has driven the emergence of 3D-printed PAC with intriguing morphologies. In 2025, R. Cui et al. [41] designed a novel 3D-printed photoacoustic sensor for high-sensitivity carbon dioxide (CO2) detection, which combined multi-pass cell and Helmholtz PAC, achieving a minimum detection limit of 330 ppb with an average time of 48 s.

In this work, a differential resonant PAC fabricated via 3D printing is designed, integrating with a 320 nm 10 mW ultraviolet (UV) laser, for online real-time monitoring of H2CO. The 3D-printed differential PAC features an integrated structure consisting of two differential channels, two gas buffer chambers, one gas inlet, and one gas outlet, characterized by its lightweight and compact size. To enhance the photoacoustic signal, two steel tubes with identical length and an internal diameter of 4 mm are inserted into two differential channels, respectively. Employing this 3D-printed differential PAC as the core sensing unit, the detection system achieves highly sensitive online real-time measurement of H2CO with a minimal gas sample requirement of 7.3 mL.

Photoacoustic cell design

When the PAC operates in resonance mode, the photoacoustic signal SPA generated within it can be calculated using the equation(1)SPA=CcellαPCwhere Ccell represents the constant of the PAC, α is the absorption coefficient of the target gas, P is the power of the excitation laser, and C is the target gas concentration. The expression for Ccell is given by(2)Ccell=−(γ−1)Qjωj∭pj*(r→)g(r→,ω)dVVcwhere γ is the isobaric and isovolumetric heat capacity ratio, Qj is Q-factor of the PAC, ωj is the light modulation frequency, Vc is the PAC volume, and ∭pj*(r→)g(r→,ω)dVis the overlap integral between the laser beam and the acoustic wave. Essentially, Ccell reflects the signal amplification capability of the PAC under specific conditions, which is solely determined by the PAC geometry, volume, Q-factor and modulation frequency. Therefore, a specific design of the PAC is necessary to realize a large cell constant, hence achieving an improved SNR.

A differential PAC is designed and fabricated via 3D printing using ABS engineering plastic. It requires a minimal gas sample volume of 7.3 mL and has a weight of 32.4 g. It features an integrated structure consisting of two differential channels, two gas buffer chambers, one gas inlet, and one gas outlet, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). Two differential channels are identical cylindrical resonant cavities, each with a length of 40 mm and a radius of 3 mm. Two steel tubes with an inner radius of 2 mm are inserted into two differential channels, respectively. At both ends of channels is a gas buffer chamber with a length and radius of 10 mm. Two UV-fused silica windows (OW22-UV, LBTEK) are mounted on the outer side of each chamber (opposite the differential channel) with cover plates to form a seal. The acoustic field distribution and resonance frequency of the designed 3D-printed differential PAC are simulated using the COMSOL Multiphysics. The physical model, shown in Fig. 1(b), is simplified to reduce complexity and computational cost. This simplified model includes only the two differential channels (40 mm in length and 2 mm in radius) and the two buffer chambers (10 mm in length and 10 mm in radius). Selection of light source for formaldehyde detection

Laser-based absorption spectroscopic technology necessitates that the wavelength of the excitation light source align precisely with a characteristic absorption line of the target gas. To achieve optimal detection performance, it is essential to select a strong absorption line specific to the target gas while minimizing potential cross-interference from other gas species. Consequently, the appropriate selection of the target gas absorption line constitutes a critical aspect in the design and implementation of spectroscopic gas detection systems. In 2000, Meller et al. [42] measured the ultraviolet absorption cross section of H2CO over the wavelength range of 225–375 nm at 298 K, with a spectral resolution of 0.025 nm using a diode array detector. H2CO exhibits distinct absorption cross section in the ultraviolet region of 225–375 nm. However, the absorption features of nitrogen dioxide (NO2) are also present in this spectral range, primarily between 320 nm and 360 nm [43], as indicated by the blue curve in Fig. 3. To ensure the performance of the H2CO detection system while avoiding cross-interference from NO2, a 320 nm 10 mW UV laser (UV-FN-320–10mW, Changchun New Industries Optoelectronics) is selected as the excitation light source. The laser power of the UV laser after it transmitted through the 3D-printed PAC is shown in Fig. 4, where the gray and red lines denote the output power under a constant drive current and after modulation by a chopper, respectively. At a drive current of 3.16 A, the output power transmitted through the 3D-printed PAC is 21.74 mW without modulation and attenuated to 11.06 mW after chopper modulation. The establishment of the sensing system

The schematic diagram of H2CO detection system based on 3D-printed differential PAC is shown in Fig. 5. A 320 nm 10 mW UV laser serves as the excitation source, aligned with one channel of the 3D-printed differential PAC. The 3D-printed differential PAC is an integrated structure consisting of two differential channels, two gas buffer chambers, one gas inlet and one gas outlet. Two identical electret condenser microphones are positioned at the midpoint of each differential channel and connected to a differential circuit to effectively suppress ambient noise and enhance the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), thereby outputting the photoacoustic signal (Mic1 - Mic2). To further enhance the photoacoustic signal, two steel tubes with identical length and an internal diameter of 4 mm are inserted into two differential channels, respectively. An optical chopper (MC2000B, Thorlabs Inc.) is placed in the optical path between the UV laser and the 3D-printed differential PAC to achieve intensity modulation of the excitation light. A function generator (SPF05, SP Co.) supplies a TTL signal to the chopper controller, driving the chopper to rotate at the resonant frequency of the PAC. A lock-in amplifier (SR830, Stanford Research Systems) demodulates this signal using the TTL reference signal provided by the chopper controller. The demodulated signal is acquired by a computer (PC) via a USB-to-RS232 interface connected to the lock-in amplifier. A distribution system (GB100, MCQ instruments) is used to generate H2CO at various concentrations from a 47.31 ppm H2CO:N2 gas mixture (Dalian Special Gases Co.) and pure N2. The blended gas is delivered to a mass flow controller (MFC) (MC-500SCCM, Alicat), whose outlet is connected to the gas inlet of the 3D-printed differential PAC. After passing through the PAC, the gas subsequently enters a pressure controller (EPC) (EPC100A-1X, Shaanxi Yidu Intelligence). The outlet of the EPC is connected to a pump (N920G, KNF Co.) that finally vents gas outdoors. Throughout the experiment, the gas flow rate and pressure inside the PAC are regulated by the MFC and EPC, respectively.Fig. 5The schematic diagram of H2CO detection system based on 3D-printed differential photoacoustic cell.Fig. 5

The schematic diagram of H2CO detection system based on 3D-printed differential photoacoustic cell.

Experimental results and discussion

3D-printed photoacoustic cell resonant frequency

The PAS system requires the modulation frequency of the excitation light source should correspond to the resonant frequency of the PAC [44], [45], [46]. The frequency response curve of the 3D-printed differential PAC is experimentally measured using a 47.31 H2CO:N2 gas mixture, with a gas flow rate of 50 sccm and a pressure of 700 Torr. As shown in Fig. 6, the experimentally measured frequency response curve exhibits strong consistency with the simulation results in Fig. 2(b), demonstrating that the differential PAC enhances the photoacoustic signal while suppressing the in-phase noise. The resonant frequency of the 3D-printed PAC is determined to be 3775.5 Hz, which deviates from the simulation result. This discrepancy is primarily due to the influence of ambient pressure and temperature on the resonant frequency, as well as slight deviations between the actual machining dimensions of the 3D-printed PAC and the simulated dimensions. The Q-factor of the PAC is calculated to be 27 by taking the ratio of the resonant frequency to the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the frequency response curve [47], [48]. In subsequent experiments, the chopper frequency is set to 3775.5 Hz to achieve intensity modulation of the UV laser, and the lock-in amplifier is configured to the 1f-mode for demodulation of the photoacoustic signal.Fig. 6The frequency response curve of 3D-printed differential photoacoustic cell under 47.31 ppm H2CO:N2 gas mixture. Flow rate optimization

The relationship between the photoacoustic signal and the gas flow rate within the 3D-printed differential PAC has been investigated using a 47.31 ppm H2CO:N2 gas mixture. The relationship is presented in Fig. 7. As the gas flow rate increases from 50 sccm to 400 sccm in increments of 50 sccm, the photoacoustic signal fluctuates around 16 μV. Analysis of the standard deviation (1σ) at different flow rates, represented by the green dots in Fig. 7, shows that 1σ standard deviation increases with rising gas flow rates. This indicates that higher flow rates introduce greater noise into the system. Therefore, in the system performance evaluation experiments, the gas flow rate is set to 50 sccm to minimize the introduction of flow-induced noise.Fig. 7The relationship between the photoacoustic signal and the gas flow rate.Fig. 7

The relationship between the photoacoustic signal and the gas flow rate.

Pressure optimization

Pressure influences the Q-factor of the PAC, the relaxation rate and the absorption line strength of the target gas. Therefore, the effect of pressure on the photoacoustic signal must be considered. A 47.31 H2CO:N2 gas mixture is filled into the 3D-printed differential PAC, and the pressure is increased from 200 Torr to 700 Torr with the increments of 100 Torr. The photoacoustic signals are recorded at each pressure for 200 s. As shown in Fig. 8, the photoacoustic signal increases with rising gas pressure. This enhancement is attributed to the increased frequency of molecular collisions at higher pressure. In the following experiment, the pressure is maintained at 700 Torr to evaluate the performance of H2CO detection system based on 3D-printed differential PAC.Fig. 8The relationship between the photoacoustic signal and the pressure.Fig. 8

Performance evaluation

The detection sensitivity of H2CO detection system based on 3D-printed differential PAC and a UV laser is characterized by progressively increasing the H2CO concentration in the H2CO:N2 gas mixture. H2CO:N2 gas mixture with concentrations ranging from 4.73 ppm to 47.31 ppm are prepared and introduced into the PAC. The photoacoustic signals for each concentration are recorded over a duration of 200 s with a 1-s averaging time. The resulting photoacoustic signals are shown in Fig. 9(a). The average values of the photoacoustic signals at different H2CO concentrations are plotted in Fig. 9(b) to illustrate the linear response of the H2CO detection system over a range of concentrations. A linear fitting applied to these averaged values yields an R-squared value of 0.98658. When the 3D-printed differential PAC is filled with pure N2 gas, the calculated background noise level (1σ) is 0.34692 μV. The linear fitting result in Fig. 9(b) indicates a photoacoustic signal response slope of 0.33708 μV/ppm with respect to H2CO concentration. The detection limit of the H2CO detection system is therefore determined to be 1.03 ppm by dividing the background noise level by the response slope.Fig. 9(a) Photoacoustic signals at different concentrations of H2CO:N2 gas mixture. (b) Photoacoustic signal as a function of H2CO concentration.Fig. 9

(a) Photoacoustic signals at different concentrations of H2CO:N2 gas mixture. (b) Photoacoustic signal as a function of H2CO concentration.

Allan-Werle deviation analysis is employed to evaluate the long-term stability of the H2CO detection system. The photoacoustic signal is measured continuously for two hours in a pure N2 background. Allan-Werle deviation is calculated from this data, as shown in Fig. 10. Allan-Werle deviation curve exhibits an initial increase followed by a decrease, corresponding to an inverse trend in the system’s detection sensitivity. Specifically, the sensitivity initially decreases and then increases with longer integration times. The initial decline is attributed to a reduction in the signal level, while the subsequent improvement results from the effective suppression of the noise as the integration time is extended. The Allan-Werle deviation analysis shows that when the integration time reaches 969 s, the detection limit can be further improved to 68.5 ppb.Fig. 10Allan-Werle deviation analysis of H2CO detection system based on 3D-printed differential photoacoustic cell.Fig. 10

Conclusions

A 3D-printed differential resonant PAC has been designed for highly sensitive, online real-time detection of H2CO, with performance analyzed from both theoretical and experimental perspectives. The 3D-printed differential PAC features an integrated structure that includes two differential channels, two gas buffer chambers, a gas inlet and a gas outlet, with a gas sample requirement of only 7.3 mL and a weight of 32.4 g. Two steel tubes with an internal diameter of 4 mm are inserted into two differential channels of the PAC to enhance the photoacoustic signal, respectively. A 320 nm 10 mW UV laser serves as the excitation light source, achieving a 1σ detection limit of 1.03 ppm with an integration time of 1 s. Allan-Werle deviation analysis indicates that the detection limit can be improved to 68.5 ppb at an optimal integration time of 969 s, demonstrating the excellent long-term stability of the H2CO detection system. The designed 3D-printed differential PAC is fabricated from ABS engineering plastic, which signifies substantial reductions in weight, manufacturing cost, and machining complexity compared to traditionally customized stainless-steel counterparts, while maintaining comparable system sensitivity. With the continuous improvement of 3D printing technology, its applications in laser-based absorption spectroscopic technologies are expected to diversify significantly. Future improvements to the H2CO detection system include replacing the excitation source with an LED to further control the system cost and reducing the system complexity, thus enhancing the potential for practical production and application of gas detection system based on absorption spectroscopy technology.
