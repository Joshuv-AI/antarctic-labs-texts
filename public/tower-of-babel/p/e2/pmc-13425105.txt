Possible Mechanisms Underlying the Effects of Fecal Microbiota Transplantation in Patients With Irritable Bowel Syndrome
Authors: Magdy El-Salhy, Tora Skarvatun, Dag Arne Lihaug Hoff, Johan Lunding, Elisabeth Kjelsvik Steinsvik, Odd Helge Gilja, Jan Gunnar Hatlebakk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsThis study aims to identify the mechanisms underlying the effects of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT).MethodsThis study investigated 93 patients included in our previous clinical trial: 24 received 30 g of their own feces (placebo group), while 47 and 22 received 30 g and 60 g of donor feces, respectively. The patients underwent sigmoidoscopy with biopsies, provided fecal samples, and completed questionnaires to assess irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) symptoms, fatigue, and quality of life at the baseline and at 1 month following FMT. The biopsy samples were immunostained for Musashi-1, neurogenin-3, and enteroendocrine and immune cells. The cell densities were quantified by computerized image analysis. Fecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) were measured by gas chromatography, and fecal bacteria were measured by 16S ribosomal RNA polymerase chain reaction DNA amplification.ResultsThe densities of stem cells, enteroendocrine cell progenitors, serotonin, glucagon-like peptide 1, and peptide YY (PYY) cells increased following FMT in the 30-g and 60-g treated groups but not in the placebo group, and they were inversely correlated with both IBS symptoms and fatigue. The densities of submucosal immune cells and mast cells decreased following FMT in the 30-g and 60-g treated groups but not in the placebo group, and they were correlated with IBS symptoms and fatigue. The fecal level of butyric acid increased in patients treated with donor feces, and this was inversely correlated with IBS symptoms and fatigue.ConclusionsStem cells, enteroendocrine progenitors, serotonin, and glucagon-like peptide 1 as well as low-grade inflammation may play roles in IBS symptom manifestations. The effects of FMT are probably due to the amelioration of these abnormalities.Keywords: Glucagon-like peptide 1, Low-grade inflammation, Mast cells, Serotonin, Stem cells

INTRODUCTION

Studies of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) as a treatment for irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) have produced diverse results ranging from high long-term efficacy to no effect at all.1-4 These diverse results of FMT interventions in IBS are probably due to differences in the FMT protocols used and in the included patient cohorts. The mechanisms underlying the effects of FMT on IBS symptom relief are unknown. Understanding these mechanisms may clarify the pathophysiology of IBS and lead to new therapeutic opportunities.

The densities of stem cells in the intestine and their early progeny as well as the densities of early intestinal endocrine cell progenitors are lower in IBS patients than in healthy subjects.5 It has been speculated that abnormal clonogeny and differentiation progeny of the stem cells in the intestine and an abnormal secretory lineage differentiation progeny could play roles in the pathophysiology of IBS.5

Patients with IBS exhibit gastrointestinal dysmotility, visceral hypersensitivity, and abnormal secretion.6 The enteroendocrine cells secret several hormones that control gastrointestinal motility, visceral sensitivity, and gastrointestinal secretion.6 The density of enteroendocrine cells is reportedly lower in patients with IBS than in healthy subjects.6

Intestinal mucosal low-grade inflammation is considered to be one of the factors contributing to the pathophysiology of IBS.7,8 Among the immune cells, an increase in submucosal mast cells appears to be associated with the activation of IBS symptoms.7,8

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are the main metabolites of the bacterial fermentation of undigested and unabsorbed fiber and carbohydrates in the intestine.9,10 SCFAs affect intestinal contractility, visceral pain, epithelial proliferation, epithelial barrier function, and host immunity.11

In our previous randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study we found that FMT decreased the symptoms and increased the quality of life (QoL) of IBS patients.1 The present study involved a large proportion of the IBS patients included in that study, and was designed to establish whether the improvement following FMT is associated with changes in the densities of intestinal stem cells and their early progenitors, secretory lineage differentiation progenitors, enteroendocrine cells, submucosal immune cells, and fecal SCFAs, as well as the composition of fecal bacteria.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Randomization of Patients

The design of this study has been described in detail previously (Supplementary Protocol).1 Briefly, the patients were randomized to receiving 30 g of their own feces (placebo group) or 30 g or 60 g of feces from a donor (30-g and 60-g groups, respectively) at a ratio of 1:1:1. Patients underwent sigmoidoscopy, and completed 3 questionnaires to assess their symptoms and QoL at the baseline and at 1 month after FMT. During sigmoidoscopy, 5 biopsy samples were obtained at different sites of the sigmoid colon about 30 cm to 35 cm from the anus.

Patients

This study included 93 of the 164 patients who participated in our previous clinical trial and agreed to undergo sigmoidoscopy.1 When the study was completed and opened, there were 24 patients in the placebo group, 47 patients in the 30-g group, and 22 patients in the 60-g group. Fecal Sample Collection, Preparation, and Administration

The procedure for performing FMT has been described in detail earlier.1 Shortly, donor fecal material was frozen immediately and was kept at –80°C, and thawed at 4°C for 2 days before transplantation. The fecal samples were mixed with 40 mL of sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) and filtered. The transplant was administered into the duodenum via a gastroscope.

Symptom and Quality of Life Measurements

IBS symptoms and fatigue were measured by the IBS Severity Scoring System (IBS-SSS) and Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS) questionnaires.12,13 QoL was measured using the IBS-QoL questionnaire. 14 Response to FMT was defined as a decrease in IBS-SSS total score of ≥ 50 points.

Immunocytochemistry and Computerized Image Analysis

After fixation of the biopsy samples in 4% buffered paraformaldehyde, they were embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5-μm-thick sections. Immunocytochemistry was done using the avidin-biotin-complex (ABC) method with the Vectastain Elite ABC Plus kit and peroxidase (horse anti-mouse/anti-rabbit IgG) (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Morphometric measurements were performed using a microscope with a camera linked to a computer. Olympus cellSens imaging software was used. The objective used in the analysis was ×40. Using this objective, each field on the monitor represented a tissue area of 0.14 mm2. Immunostained sections were coded, and the co-authors that performed measurements were not aware of identities of the slides. Measurements were made in 10 randomly chosen fields. The method used has been validated earlier.15

The numbers of Musashi-1 and neurogenin-3 cells were measured by manually counting the number of immunoreactive cells and the number of crypts by pointing and clicking the computer mouse in each field. The density of Musashi-1 cells was expressed as the number of cells per crypt, and that of neurogenin-3 as the number of cells per 10 fields. The density of enteroendocrine cells was determined from the number of enteroendocrine cells and the area of the epithelial cells. The density of enteroendocrine cells was expressed as the number of endocrine cells/mm2 of epithelium. The number of immune cells was determined by counting the number of immune cells in the lamina propria manually in each field. The density of immune cells was expressed as the number of immune cells per field of view.

Determination of Fecal Short-chain Fatty Acid Levels

Each fecal sample was weighed, homogenized with 2.5 mL of 3 mmol/L 2-ethylbutyric acid/0.5 mmol/L H2SO4, and distilled in vacuum. The SCFAs level and the levels of acetic, propionic, iso-butyric, butyric, iso-valeric, valeric, iso-capronic, and capronic acids were measured using gas chromatography (Agilent 7890A; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).16 The levels of SCFAs are expressed in mmol/kg wet weight.

Bacterial Analyses

Fecal bacterial contents were analyzed as described in detail previously by 16S ribosomal RNA polymerase chain reaction DNA amplification covering variable regions V3-V9, and by DNA probe hybridization of 48 bacterial markers.17

Statistical Methods

To test differences between the placebo, 30-g, and 60-g groups in response, sex, IBS subtypes, and medication Fisher’s exact test was used. The Kruskal-Wallis test with a Dunn’s multiple-comparisons post-hoc test with adjusted P-values were used to assess differences between placebo, 30-g, and 60-g groups at the baseline in IBS duration, IBS-SSS, and FAS total scores. The nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was used to analyze differences between the baseline and at 1 month following FMT in the placebo, 30-g, 60-g, responder, and nonresponder groups. Correlations were analyzed using the nonparametric Spearman test, and P-values were adjusted for multiple correlations using Bonferroni correction. Differences between IBS subtypes regarding IBS-SSS, FAS, and IBS-QoL scores and the densities of Musashi-1, neurogenin-3, enteroendocrine cells, and immune cells were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with a Dunn’s multiple-comparisons post hoc test, where adjusted P-values were recorded. Cohen’s d was used to determine the effect size of FMT on IBS-SSS, FAS, and IBS-QoL total scores, and the densities of Musashi-1, neurogenin-3, enteroendocrine cells, and immune cells as well as the SCFAs levels.

Ethics

The study was approved by the West Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics, Bergen, Norway (Approval No. 2017/1197/REK vest). This study was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03822299).

Differences Between Irritable Bowel Syndrome Subtypes

There was no difference in the response to FMT between IBS subtypes (P = 0.900). There was no difference between IBS subtypes regarding IBS-SSS, FAS, and IBS-QoL scores either at the baseline or at 1 month after FMT (Supplementary Fig. 13). There was also no difference between the IBS subtypes regarding the densities of Musashi-1, neurogenin-3, chromogranin A, serotonin, PYY, and GLP-1 cells at the baseline and at 1 month after FMT (Supplementary Fig. 14). There was no difference between the IBS subtypes regarding total immune cells or mast cell densities, or the fecal level of butyric acid either at the baseline or at 1 month after FMT (Supplementary Fig. 15).

DISCUSSION

The density of stem cells is lower in IBS patients than in healthy subjects, and it has been speculated that the low density of enteroendocrine cells in IBS patients could be caused by abnormal clonogeny and differentiation progeny of the intestinal stem cells.5 In the present study, the density of intestinal Musashi-1-expressing cells increased in IBS patients who received donor FMT and in the FMT responder group, but not in the placebo or nonresponder groups. Furthermore, the density of Musashi-1-expressing cells was inversely correlated with the IBS-SSS total score and the scores for all of its items. These findings indicate that the increases in the densities of stem cells and their early progenitors play roles in the symptom improvement seen after FMT. The density of Musashi-1-expressing cells was also inversely correlated with the FAS total score and the scores for its items, suggesting that the increases in the densities of stem cells contribute to the improvement in fatigue seen after FMT. The present observations suggest that transplantation of epithelial organoids containing stem cells could be a useful therapeutic approach for IBS in the near future.18-20

The density of neurogenin-3 is lower in IBS patients than that in healthy subjects.5 The present study found that density of neurogenin-3-expressing cells increased in donor-treated IBS patients and in the FMT responder group, but not in the placebo or nonresponder groups. Although the density of these cells was inversely correlated with the IBS-SSS total score, it was only inversely correlated with the score for the item of dissatisfaction with bowel habits. This suggests that this increase in the density of neurogenin-3-expressing cells only affects intestinal motility. The density of these cells was not correlated with the FAS total score or the scores for its items, indicating that the changes in neurogenin-3 cells after FMT do not affect fatigue in IBS patients. Mutation in neurogenin-3 results in a life-threatening condition called enteric anendocrinosis, which is associated with severe malabsorption and diabetes.21 This condition is accompanied by the almost total loss of small- and large-intestine enteroendocrine cells.21 In experimental animals, changes in intestinal neurogenin-3 expression result in a shift in the secretory lineages, with more goblet cells and fewer enteroendocrine cells.21 Moreover, a low neurogenin-3 expression is associated with the low density of enteroendocrine cells seen in autosomal recessive disorder, congenital malabsorptive diarrhea, small-intestine allograft rejection, and NEUROG3-knockout mice.22-24

The density of cells expressing the common marker for enteroendocrine cells (chromogranin) in the colon has been reported to be lower in IBS patients than in healthy subjects.6 In the present study, the density of these cells increased after FMT in the donor-treated groups and in responder group, but not in the nonresponder or placebo groups. The density of chromogranin-A-expressing cells was inversely correlated with the IBS-SSS total score. These observations indicate that FMT probably exerts its effects by affecting the density of enteroendocrine cells that regulate gastrointestinal motility, visceral sensitivity, and secretion. Moreover, the density of chromogranin A was inversely correlated with the FAS total score and the scores for its items, indicating that enteroendocrine cells may also play a role in the improvement of fatigue recorded after FMT.

This study found that the density of serotonin cells increased in the IBS patients who received donor FMT and in responder group, but not in the placebo or nonresponder groups. The density of these cells was inversely correlated with the IBS-SSS total score and the scores for its 4 items. The density of serotonin cells in the large intestine has been reported to be lower in IBS patients than in healthy subjects.6 However, the density of these cells was not correlated with the FAS total score, suggesting that serotonin does not affect fatigue in IBS patients. Serotonin plays an important role in visceral sensitivity by activating the submucosal branch of the enteric nervous system.6 Serotonin may exert its effects on the central nervous system through an endocrine or a synaptic pathway from neuropods (axon-like long basal processes extending to the lamina propria) to neurons. Serotonin is believed to stimulate 5-hydroxytryptamine 1-7 receptors in circumventricular brain nuclei (hypothalamus and area postrema) via an endocrinal mechanism.21 Furthermore, serotonin stimulates colonic motility, accelerates small- and large-intestine transit, and inhibits gastric emptying.6 The importance of serotonin in manifesting IBS symptoms is already recognized, and led to the use of serotonin receptor antagonists and selective serotonin-reuptake inhibitors as symptomatic pharmacological treatments for IBS.25

The density of PYY cells in the large intestine has been found to be lower in IBS patients than in healthy subjects.6 The density of PYY cells increased significantly at 1 month after FMT in the 30-g, 60-g, and responder groups, but not in the placebo or nonresponder groups. The density of these cells was inversely correlated with the abdominal pain score, but not with the IBS-SSS total score or the scores for its other 3 items. PYY modulates serotonin release from colonic enterochromaffin cells via the endogenous NK2/NK3 cascade system.26 This could explain the inverse correlation between the density of cells expressing PYY and abdominal pain observed in the present study. The density of PYY-expressing cells was not correlated with the FAS total score or the scores for its items in the present study.

The density of GLP-1-cells increased in the IBS patients treated with donor FMT and in responder group in this study, but not in the placebo or nonresponder groups. Moreover, the density of these cells was inversely correlated with the IBS-SSS total score and the scores for its items, indicating an association between IBS symptoms and GLP-1. The density of GLP-1-expressing cells was also inversely correlated with the FAS total score, suggesting a role for GLP-1 in fatigue observed in IBS patients. GLP-1 inhibits gastric and pancreatic secretion, delays gastric emptying, is a major mediator of the ileal brake, and exerts some incretin effects.6,27,28 GLP-1 acts through a hormonal and synaptic neuropod mode of action on GLP-1 receptors located in the hypothalamus, nucleus of the solitary tract, and area postrema.21 GLP-1 analogue ROSE-010 have been shown to reduce abdominal pain in IBS patients, to delay gastric emptying and to accelerate colonic transit.29,30 It has been suggested that ROSE-010 has an optimal effect on IBS-C and IBS-M.31 The present study showed that there was no difference in the increased density of GLP-1 expressing cells between the 3 major IBS subtypes, namely diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D), constipation-predominant IBS (IBS-C), and mixed IBS (IBS-M). This observation advocated that GLP-1 analogue can also be used in treating IBS-D. The GLP-1 analogues are used currently for treating diabetes and for weight loss.21 The present observations confirm the previously published results that GLP-1 analogues could be of value in treating IBS symptoms and probably the associated fatigue.

The density of total immune cells decreased after FMT in the lamina propria in the donor-treated groups and responder group, but not in the placebo or nonresponder groups. The density of submucosal immune cells was correlated with the IBS-SSS total score and with the scores for abdominal distension and dissatisfaction with bowel habits, but not with the score for abdominal pain. These findings support the suggestion by Barbara and colleagues that low-grade inflammation plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of IBS.8 The density of immune cells was also correlated with the FAS total score and the scores for its sub-items, indicating that submucosal immune cells play a role in fatigue associated with IBS. This finding suggests that low-dose 5-aminosalicylic acid or budesonide could be useful in treating IBS.

The density of submucosal mast cells decreased in the IBS patients who received donor FMT and in responder group, but not in the placebo or nonresponder groups. The density of these cells was correlated with the IBS-SSS total score and the scores for its items including abdominal pain and dissatisfaction with bowel habits scores. Mast cells are capable of producing serotonin, and possess serotonin receptors.32 Submucosal mast cells lie close to enteric nerves, and by releasing inflammatory mediators they affect the enteric nervous system and muscle function.8 The present observations underline the importance of mast cells in IBS symptom manifestation and support the hypothesis of Barbara and colleagues that mast cell–nerve interactions could be important for IBS pathophysiology.33 Barbara and colleagues proposed controlling mast-cell functions using pharmacological drugs such as anti-IgE antibodies, inhibitors of the intracellular protein tyrosine kinase Syk, second-generation antihistamines, and proteinase-activated receptor antagonists.33

The present observations that the fecal level of butyric acid increased in IBS patients who received 30-g or 60-g of the donor feces and in the responder group, but not in the placebo or nonresponder groups are in agreement with previously published results. 34 Furthermore, the present findings that the level of butyric acid was inversely correlated with the IBS-SSS total score and the scores for its items are also in agreement with previous published observations.34 Butyric acid provides colonic epithelial cells with energy and regulates gastrointestinal motility, contractility, visceral sensitivity, epithelial proliferation, epithelial barrier function, and local immunity.35 The important role played by butyric acid in manifesting IBS symptoms is supported by previous observations of the butyric acid levels being inversely correlated with intestinal transit time in healthy subjects, and inversely correlated with colon transit time and positively correlated with stool frequency in IBS patients.35,36 Furthermore, treating IBS patients with microencapsulated sodium butyrate improved their abdominal pain, diarrhea, and constipation. Butyric acid regulates serotonin expression by intestinal enterochromaffin cells and also regulates the expression of PYY and GLP-1 by activating the free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2), FFAR3, G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43), and GPR41 receptors.10,37-42 In the present study, the level of butyric acid was inversely correlated with the FAS total score and the scores for its items. The possible mechanisms underlying these effects remain to be investigated.

The bacteria whose abundance was increased in IBS patients who received FMT included Alistipes spp., Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Eubacterium biforme, and Lactobacillus spp. Alistipes spp. hydrolyses the serotonin precursor tryptophan into indole.43 They also produce the anti-inflammatory acetic, succinic, and propionic SCFAs.43,44 Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Eubacterium biforme are the main producers of butyric acid in the large intestine.45-51 Faecalibacterium prausnitzii also produces an anti-inflammatory single 15-kDa protein that blocks the nuclear factor kappa B pathway, and increases the expression of serotonin by affecting major genes regulating serotonin expression.52,53 Lactobacillus spp. regulate epithelial barrier function and modulate immune responses by increasing the expression of mucin by intestinal cells, and blocking the adherence of bacteria, fungi, and parasites.54,55 These findings raise the question whether probiotics containing Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Eubacterium biforme, Alistipes, and Lactobacillus spp. could be useful in treating IBS and fatigue.

The main strengths of this study were that it included IBS patients belonging to the 3 main IBS subtypes (ie, IBS-D, IBS-C, and IBS-M) as well as both female and male patients with IBS. The main limitations of the study were that it did not include the fourth IBS subtype, it was dominated by female IBS patients, and it showed only short-term changes and not the long-term changes as well as the sample size of the patients’ cohort did not allow analysis of the difference between IBS-subtypes.

In conclusion, the densities of stem cells, enteroendocrine cell progenitors, serotonin, GLP-1, and PYY cells increased after FMT in IBS patients treated donor´s transplant. The densities of these cells were inversely correlated with IBS symptoms and fatigue, indicating their roles in manifesting IBS symptom and fatigue. The abundance of bacteria that produce butyric acid increased after FMT in IBS patients who received donor feces and hence the level of butyric acid also increased. Moreover, the densities of submucosal immune cells and especially, mast cells decreased after FMT in IBS patients who received donor’s transplant. The densities of these cells were positively correlated with IBS symptoms and fatigue. Although these observations showed association rather than causation or mechanistic proof, they may suggest new treatment approaches for IBS. Furthermore, these parameters can be used as predictive biomarkers for FMT response in IBS and eventually durable effects.
