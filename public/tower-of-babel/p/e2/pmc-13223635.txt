US multicentre randomised controlled trial protocol: Comparing Analgesic Regimen Effectiveness and Safety after surgery trial for Kids (CARES for Kids)
Authors: Jenna Sulgi Lee, Mark C Bicket, Sara A Mansfield, Jennifer A Rabbitts, Adam Van Horn, Erin E Perrone, Kao-Ping Chua, Terri Voepel-Lewis, Jennifer Waljee, Chad Brummett, Carey Hutmacher, Yi Li, Lorraine I Kelley-Quon, CARES for Kids Investigators

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute pain is an expected symptom for adolescents after outpatient surgery. In the USA, postoperative analgesic regimens frequently include prescription opioids. Increasing attention from clinicians, patients and other healthcare leaders has been directed toward non-opioid strategies, such as combining non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) plus acetaminophen, as potential first-line options for managing postoperative pain. However, the effectiveness and safety of home regimens that include versus exclude opioids for adolescents are unclear. The Comparing Analgesic Regimen Effectiveness and Safety after surgery for Kids study evaluates the effectiveness and safety of NSAIDs plus acetaminophen alone (NSAID regimen) versus NSAIDs and acetaminophen plus a low-dose opioid regimen (opioid regimen).Methods and analysisThis study is a pragmatic, multicentre randomised controlled clinical trial recruiting 900 patients aged 12–20 years undergoing three common outpatient surgeries (tonsillectomy, laparoscopic cholecystectomy, knee arthroscopy) across four health systems. We will recruit patients prior to surgery and individuals will be randomised 1:1 with stratification to receive prescriptions for either the NSAID regimen or the opioid regimen. The primary effectiveness outcome is patient-reported pain intensity, while the primary safety outcome is adverse medication-related symptoms both assessed over the first 2 weeks after surgery. Secondary outcomes include quality of recovery, healthcare-related quality of life and rates of problematic substance use and chronic prescription opioid use, assessed up to 1 year after surgery.Ethics and disseminationThe study incorporates stakeholder collaboration, including patient partners, surgeons, professional organisations., and health insurance payors, to ensure ethical conduct and relevance. This study is overseen by a single institutional review board with certificate of confidentiality. Findings will be disseminated through academic publications, conferences and community outreach to inform patients, parents, surgical teams and policymakers about optimal pain management strategies for adolescents after surgery.Trial registration numberNCT06671002.Keywords: Adolescent, Paediatric anaesthesia, Pain management, SURGERY, Paediatric surgery

Introduction

Each year, 4 million surgeries are performed on US children and adolescents, including both outpatient and inpatient procedures.1 Acute pain after surgery is an expected symptom that adolescents and their families need guidance, support and optimised analgesic regimens to manage at home. Even with procedures considered ‘low risk’ for surgical complications, over 80% of children and adolescents experience pain after surgery and nearly half report moderate or severe pain.2 3 Despite this high burden of pain, adverse effects from commonly used analgesic regimens are frequent yet often under-recognised, adding to the challenges families face during recovery.

Postoperative analgesic regimens to treat pain have historically included prescription opioids. Recent guidelines from the American Pediatric Surgical Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend that opioids should be prescribed only when necessary and should be limited to less than 5 days of opioid pills after low-risk surgery.4 5 In light of the current ongoing US opioid epidemic, there is growing concern that exposure to prescription opioids during adolescence may influence future patterns of substance use and risk. Recent surgery is the number one reason for surplus prescription opioids at home which may increase risk of future non-medical prescription opioid use in adolescents.6 A previous study which reviewed national prescription dispensing data indicates that dentists and surgeons together accounted for approximately 61% of opioid prescriptions dispensed to children and adolescents in 2019.7 These ongoing concerns have prompted new consideration to emphasise non-opioid medications such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and acetaminophen as first line therapy. Even with the recent national reduction in overall opioid prescribing, for most adolescents, opioids remain one of the most commonly prescribed pain medications after low-risk surgery.7,12

Research comparing pain medications has not progressed alongside current medical practices. As such, absence of evidence is the key driver for the present variation in postoperative analgesic regimens. Only four trials in adults have compared opioids to NSAIDs for pain after discharge from surgery, motivating current efforts to better evaluate these regimens through ongoing studies in adult surgical populations.13,17 However, no prospective randomised studies compare these postoperative analgesic regimens for adolescents.18

This randomised controlled trial called Comparing Analgesic Regimen Effectiveness and Safety after surgery (CARES) for Kids will compare two commonly prescribed home-going analgesic regimens: (1) NSAIDs with acetaminophen (the NSAID regimen) or (2) a low-dose opioid regimen plus NSAID and acetaminophen (the opioid regimen). We will compare pain control as the primary outcome after common low-risk paediatric surgeries. Secondary outcomes will include short-term adverse effects and long-term harms related to analgesic use. Our goal is to identify which approach is the most efficacious and safest for our target population.

Methods and analysis

Study design overview

CARES for Kids is a multicentre, patient-level, parallel group, pragmatic, prospective, randomised controlled trial comparing two common analgesic regimens prescribed to adolescents (12–20 years old) after three low-risk outpatient surgeries across four hospital systems. Patients and their families will be randomised to one of two treatment options: (1) NSAIDs and acetaminophen (referred to as the ‘NSAID’ regimen) or (2) NSAIDs and acetaminophen plus low-dose opioid medications (the ‘opioid’ regimen). By evaluating pain management strategies under usual clinical conditions, this pragmatic clinical trial design captures real-world prescribing practices and reflects patient variability. The intervention and data collection protocols are the same for both treatment arms as discharge prescribing assignment is the only difference between groups. The CARES for Kids study group is described in online supplemental appendix.

Patient, public and physician engagement

We designed this trial with significant input from a multidisciplinary stakeholder advisory board that includes patients, caregivers, anaesthesiologists and surgeons. Further, we have included stakeholders from community-based organisations, professional groups, research organisations, health insurers and members from data and safety monitoring board (DSMB). Stakeholders have been and continue to be involved in the design, conduct, reporting and dissemination plans of this research. The stakeholder advisory board has been engaged through individual and group interviews followed by quarterly stakeholder group meetings, with a mix of virtual and in-person activities. Engagement will continue throughout the duration of the trial, focusing on study conduct and disseminating study results.

Site selection

This study will be performed across paediatric healthcare systems. Four enrolment sites were chosen based on their case volume, diversity of patient population, commitment to multicentre research and existing trial enrolment infrastructure maturity. Participating sites currently include the University of Michigan Medical Center, Children’s Hospital Los Angeles (CHLA), Nationwide Children’s Hospital and Lucile Packard Children’s Hospital at Stanford Medicine Children’s Health. These institutions represent academic health systems with children’s hospitals of varying sizes (ranging from 348 beds to 673 beds) and substantial surgical case volumes (figure 1). Each includes both hospital and ambulatory surgical centres and represents a range of real-world surgical care settings. In combination, these institutions have racially, ethnically and geographically diverse service areas enabling the enrolment of a patient sample representative of the US adolescent population.

Study population

We will enrol 900 adolescents aged 12–20 years if they have no significant analgesic medication use before surgery and undergo one of the three common low-risk outpatient procedures, including tonsillectomy, laparoscopic or robotic cholecystectomy and knee arthroscopy. These three procedures are among the most common surgeries in adolescents with a significant national divide in practice regarding who receives opioids versus no opioids, helping maintain equipoise for the trial (figure 2).119,22 For this study, we initially defined significant analgesic medication use before surgery as prescriptions reported by the child or caregiver for opioid or NSAID medications in the past 90 days. With publication of the AAP guidelines, significant analgesic medication use before surgery was revised to prescriptions for six or more days of opioid medications in the last 90 days.5 The three low-risk surgical procedures are tonsil removal (tonsillectomy with or without adenoid removal), gallbladder removal (laparoscopic or robot-assisted cholecystectomy) and knee scope (diagnostic arthroscopy). Additionally, patients admitted from the emergency department for inpatient cholecystectomy will be included if they are discharged within a day following surgery. Patients and caregivers who speak English or Spanish will be included in this study. All study materials—including consent forms, questionnaires and informational documents—were translated into Spanish by certified medical interpreters to ensure linguistic accuracy. Patients will be excluded from the study if they anticipate another surgery within 12 months, anticipate life expectancy <12 months, do not have legal guardians and have complex chronic conditions per the Pediatric Medical Complexity Algorithm.23 Patients will also be excluded if they have comorbidities that preclude assignment to either treatment regimen such as allergies to study medications, liver disease, estimated glomerular filtration rate <60, heart failure, peptic ulcer disease, stroke, anticoagulation, heart surgery, acute psychiatric instability, substance use disorder not in remission or treatment and history of diversion of controlled substances.

Subpopulations of interest

The demographic subpopulations of particular interest that we will perform sub analysis on are sex, age (12–16 years vs 17–20 years) and ethnicity (Hispanic vs non-Hispanic). Sex-based differences in pain perception have been reported with females reporting higher pain levels compared with males which may contribute to variations in postoperative management strategies.24 25 Also, mid-adolescence marks a period of ongoing physiological, psychological and social maturation, with age of 16 years representing a common point along this broader developmental transition.26 Age is a significant determinant of postoperative opioid use, with older adolescents more likely to receive higher quantities of opioids compared with younger patients.27 Stratifying the analysis by age may identify significant differences in pain perception, coping mechanisms and opioid use behaviours. The paediatric Hispanic population is one of the fastest-growing demographics in the USA and CHLA serves a substantial portion of this community.28 29 Within this community, cultural differences in pain perception and medication management have been documented which may influence opioid prescribing and usage patterns.30

Study procedures

Screening and enrolment

The primary enrolment method uses a digital strategy including phone calls, emails, phone texts and electronic medical record (EMR) based patient portal messaging with a secondary enrolment strategy of outpatient clinic visits. Research staff will query electronic health records of patients after initial pre-surgery appointment (~1–6 weeks before surgery) for cohort discovery. The research staff will also review the operating room schedule once surgery has been scheduled to confirm eligibility and timing of enrolment. Once an eligible patient is scheduled to undergo one of the three surgeries included in the trial, research staff will review the EMRs for exclusion criteria and then will contact potentially eligible patients and caregivers. For patients who meet eligibility criteria and express interest, research staff will conduct a follow-up phone call to review the screening questions in detail. If the patient remains eligible, staff will inform them of their eligibility status and invite them to participate in the study. If the patient and their guardian agree to proceed, a convenient time is arranged to complete the consent and assent process electronically (online supplemental exhibit). The MyDataHelps platform,31 hosted at the University of Michigan, will be the primary interface for enrolment sites. Enrolment sites will use this web-based application to document patient screening, recruitment, consenting and patient questionnaire administration.

Retention plan

Strategies for optimising retention will focus on ease of patient participation. Research staff will provide technical support to download MyDataHelps platform and study app used for the self-administered tools and patient-reported outcomes and will check for completion of the assessments. Based on stakeholder feedback, research staff will provide mobile devices with data plans to participants who do not have access to one. In the interim, they will call patients directly to complete assessments with those waiting for their phones to arrive. Additionally, the MyDataHelps smartphone application notifications will remind participants to complete assessments. The automated notifications via text will occur weekly. Participants will be instructed that all questionnaires must be completed by the patients themselves rather than their caregivers to ensure that the data accurately reflect the patients’ experiences and perspectives. Prior research has demonstrated a divergence between patient-reported and caregiver-reported outcomes, with discrepancies often observed in pain perception, symptom reporting and overall treatment experiences.32 33 Participants will also have the option to complete assessments on a parallel web version (MyDataHelps online portal). Parents or caregivers of participants aged 12–17 years will also be notified via text if assessments are missing.

Randomisation, masking and allocation concealment

Once a patient is registered into MyDataHelps.org, the patient will be randomised with a 1:1 allocation ratio into one of the two treatment arms, NSAID or opioid arm. Randomisation occurs after the ‘Randomization’ survey is completed in coordinator mode the day before surgery or the day of surgery. To ensure a balanced distribution of participants undergoing each procedure, randomisation of patients will be stratified by site, sex, age (<16 years or ≥16 years) and type of surgery via permuted block randomisation with a variable block size.

Both analgesic regimens will be operationalised by the surgical team, who will provide the intervention in the form of analgesic medications prescribed to the patient on the day of surgery. Participants will not be masked to treatment arm assignment due to the complexity of the masking process and participant/caregiver desire to know which analgesic medication they are taking. The supervising clinical investigators who implement the interventions at the site will also be aware of the treatment assignment. To maintain allocation concealment, randomisation will be conducted using the online study data management system, using an algorithm prepared by the study statistician. Both study staff performing outcome assessment and data analysis will be masked to the treatment assignment. This masking and the structured nature of outcome measures will also minimise potential biased outcome ascertainment.

Interventions

The treatment regimens are developed from evidence-based recommendations from prescribing guidelines and systematic reviews.4 5 In both treatment arms, the surgical team will implement the prescribing strategy by providing the intervention analgesic medications on the day of surgery. The choice and dosage of both NSAID and opioid prescriptions are titrated based on the patient’s weight and recommended dosing. This pragmatic clinical approach reflects real-world prescribing practices, where treatments are tailored to individual patient needs and outcomes using evidence-based principles.

The NSAID analgesic prescribing strategy includes prescriptions for an NSAID and acetaminophen. Subjects randomised to the NSAID regimen will be prescribed 40 doses of only one of the following:

Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg (max 600 mg) by mouth every 6 hours around the clock for 2 days then as needed for pain.

Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg (max 600 mg) by mouth every 6 hours around the clock for 2 days then as needed for pain.

Celecoxib 6 mg /kg (max 400 mg) by mouth once; then 3 mg/kg (max 200 mg) every 12 hours around the clock for 2 days then as needed for pain after.

Celecoxib 6 mg /kg (max 400 mg) by mouth once; then 3 mg/kg (max 200 mg) every 12 hours around the clock for 2 days then as needed for pain after.

Participants will be additionally prescribed acetaminophen 15 mg/kg up to 1000 mg by mouth every 6 hours around the clock for the first 2 days after surgery and then as needed after (40 doses). Options for liquid formulations for these medications are also available.

The opioid analgesic prescribing strategy includes prescriptions for an opioid medication as well as an NSAID and acetaminophen. Subjects randomised to the opioid regimen will be prescribed 10 doses of only one of the following opioid medications:

Oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg (max 5 kg) by mouth every 4–6 hours as needed for pain.

Oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg (max 5 kg) by mouth every 4–6 hours as needed for pain.

Hydromorphone 0.04 mg/kg (max 2 mg) by mouth every 4–6 hours as needed for pain.

Hydromorphone 0.04 mg/kg (max 2 mg) by mouth every 4–6 hours as needed for pain.

Of note, patients randomised to the opioid arm are not required to fill the opioid prescription and may choose not to consume any of the opioid. Similar to the NSAID regimen, participants in the opioid regimen will additionally receive acetaminophen 15 mg/kg up to 1000 mg by mouth every 6 hours around the clock for the first 2 days after surgery then as needed after (40 doses) as well as ibuprofen 10 mg/kg (max 600 mg) by mouth every 6 hours around the clock for 2 days then as needed (40 doses). A low dose opioid-only arm was considered but not implemented due to patient and clinical stakeholders’ concern for excessive exposure to opioids and misalignment with current postoperative pain management guidelines which stress a multimodal approach.

Safety and adverse events

The study will be monitored by the full steering committee and a DSMB with respect to accrual, baseline demographic characteristics, withdrawal rates, questionnaire completion and major adverse events (AEs). The research team will monitor the study for adverse events. All serious AEs will be reported to the Institutional Review Board and Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute according to stipulations. If such problems are considered potentially related to the trial, then they will also be referred to the DSMB. The members of the DSMB will have no direct involvement in the conduct of the trial, and they will not have financial, proprietary or professional conflicts of interest, which may affect the impartial, independent decision-making responsibilities of the DSMB. Participants will be informed of potential interactions and safety considerations, in line with standard of care, at the time of analgesic regimen prescribing. At study assessments, we will evaluate the use of prescribed and over-the-counter pain medications. All participants will receive closer monitoring than is typical in usual practice, with daily assessment of pain and adverse effects for the first 2 weeks.

The study team will follow-up reports of side effects from patients. Recommendations to prevent constipation will be provided to all participants in both study arms as per the practice at each study site. Important AEs (eg, bleeding) will be evaluated by a study physician and reported to the surgical team. Potentially urgent AEs (eg, difficulty breathing) will be referred for immediate evaluation in the local clinic or emergency room. Clinically significant new or worsening psychiatric symptoms will be reported to the surgical team.

Data collection

Baseline data reported from patients will be collected at enrolment via interview and medical record review. Assessments are timed after randomisation on the day of surgery for baseline assessment, daily for 0–14 days after surgery, 1 month after surgery and every 3 months thereafter (3 months, 6 months, 9 months and 12 months after surgery). Financial incentives in a graduated amount will be given for each assessment period to offset costs of participation, totalling at most US$245. Patient-reported outcomes will be measured at baseline and after discharge from surgery through follow-up surveys and are displayed in table 1. The local study team will review charts to assess for clinically important AEs for up to 12 months after surgery. Although this study involves a comprehensive overview of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), we consider the assessment protocol to be appropriately balanced in scope and respondent burden. This approach was endorsed by stakeholder and patient partners and is consistent with multiple previous symptom management trials, where similar-length assessments were well tolerated by patients with postoperative pain.

Outcomes

The primary and secondary outcomes and time points at which they will be assessed are listed in table 1. Although participants will be asked to complete multiple patient-reported measures in this proposed study, we believe this outcome assessment protocol is both appropriately comprehensive and reasonable in terms of respondent burden. Patient partners were involved in selecting key outcomes which include pain intensity, function, improvement, symptoms and AEs. These domains also align with national recommendations specific to paediatric clinical trials in acute pain specifically the pediatric working group of the Initiative on Methods, Measurement, and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials guidelines34 and the National Institutes of Health Helping to End Addiction Long-term Pediatric Common Data Elements.19 Each pain outcome domain will be assessed using validated patient-reported measures.

Primary outcome

The primary effectiveness outcome is acute postoperative pain intensity reported by the patient each day for up to 14 days after surgery using the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) Short Form. Specifically, we will use BPI worst pain intensity at the site of surgery, assessing worst pain intensity in the past 24 hours with responses indicated on an 11-point numeric rating scale (0, ‘no pain’, 10, ‘pain as bad as you can imagine’). The BPI is a validated, preferred tool endorsed by national organisations to measure acute pain after paediatric surgery.19 35 36 The primary safety outcome is AEs from analgesic medications. Following the established standards for clinical trials in pain management, an adverse effects symptom checklist will proactively screen patients for their report of AEs from analgesic medications over the first 14 days after surgery.37 The validated symptom checklist assesses the number and severity of common symptoms and participants’ beliefs about whether or not they are medication-related.38 The list will be tailored according to the Cochrane review to include the most common side effects from oral NSAIDs and opioids for postoperative pain.39

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes include sleep disturbance, global impression of change in pain, pain interference, quality of recovery, health-related quality of life, depression and anxiety, substance use, prolonged opioid use and surgical complications. PROMIS Pediatric Sleep Disturbance V.1.0 has been validated as a reliable tool for measuring sleep problems.40 A seven-grade, patient-reported global impression of change scale will be used to evaluate patients’ perceptions of pain improvement or worsening.41 The PROMIS Pediatric Pain Interference Scale (Short Form-4a) is a validated and reliable tool designed to measure pain-related interference in function.42 To evaluate for depression and anxiety, the Patient Health Questionnaire and Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale will be used.43 44 One question from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health will assess for new prolonged opioid use after surgery.45 46 The local study team will review charts to assess for clinically important AEs such as unplanned postoperative clinical interactions related to pain and emergency room visits, hospitalisations and 30-day complications after discharge using the American College of Surgeons’ National Surgical Quality Improvement Program Pediatrics definitions.47

Trial data sources

All study data will be collected on the MyDataHelps digital platform,31 a secure, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-compliant application developed by CareEvolution. MyDataHelps enables real-time data capture through PROMs, which participants complete using their personal smartphone or device. Participants will also have the option to complete assessments on a parallel web version (MyDataHelps online portal) if they do not have a mobile device. The platform supports data integration, medication tracking and survey completion which allow for longitudinal data collection that mirrors pragmatic recovery experiences. MyDataHelps includes customised, data-driven electronic case report forms to capture data gathered by research coordinators at each site. Standardised data forms will be used to capture data from the perioperative period including immediately before, during and after surgery to minimise bias and missing data, as well as to permit for recording and reporting of reasons for dropout and missing data via MyDataHelps strict audit trail. All collected data will be stored securely within the platform and study staff will have access to de-identified responses for monitoring and analysis.

Prevention of missing data

Several strategies will be implemented to minimise missing data throughout the study. Participants will be engaged using a smartphone-based platform (MyDataHelps), allowing for flexible, remote completion of surveys. Other features include frequent automated reminders, real-time monitoring of survey completion and phone call reminders by study coordinators when participants miss key assessments. To minimise missed data points, reminders are strategically clustered in the afternoon to align with times when participants are to be home from school. The pragmatic design of the study allows the interventions to be flexible and the prescribing strategies can be tailored to specific surgical procedure and individual patient needs.

Sample size and power

The sample size was calculated to test the superiority of NSAID versus opioid analgesic regimens on effectiveness for acute pain on postoperative day one. The trial is powered to test the superiority for each one of the three types of low-risk surgeries included in CARES for Kids (ie, tonsillectomy, cholecystectomy, knee arthroscopy). To account for multiple comparisons, a two-sided type I error of 0.0166 after applying a Bonferroni correction was used to control the family-wise error rate of 0.05 with three procedure types. The 90% power testing was used for superiority (two-sample t-test) at the day 1 assessment but achieved power in the generalised estimating equations superiority models will be improved by the repeated outcome measures. The outcome measure for the Numeric Rating Scale pain intensity has a mean of ~3.4 (SD=2.1) in prior samples of low-risk surgical patients, and past studies suggest a difference of 1.0 represents a minimally important difference.48 49

For the superiority analysis, calculations estimated that 244 patients (or 122 per arm with 1:1 allocation ratio) are required to detect a difference in means of ≥1. Based on prior work, we conservatively assume a 15% crossover or loss to follow-up. This requires enrolling 288 patients per type of surgery. To account for three surgery types, we will recruit a total study population of 900 (300 patients per type of surgery).

Statistical analysis plan

All analyses will follow a prespecified statistical analysis plan that will be finalised and registered prior to analysing the effect of treatment assignment on outcomes. We plan to define treatment by randomised assignment to the NSAID-based regimen versus the opioid-based regimen and conduct separate analyses within each surgery type, that is, tonsillectomy, gallbladder removal and knee arthroscopy, which is consistent with the powering strategy. The plan will also detail analytical approaches to account for repeated measures of outcomes data collected during postoperative days 0–14, longitudinal analysis of other outcomes and subgroup analyses to examine potential effect modification by sex, age, group (12–16 years vs 17–20 years) and ethnicity (Hispanic vs non-Hispanic), among other details. Analyses will be performed using validated statistical software such as R or SAS.

Ethics and dissemination

The CARES for Kids trial was approved by institutional review boards at participating sites. The University of Michigan IRBMED approved the study (HUM00252793) and serves as the single IRB (sIRB), with participating institutions entering into reliance agreements with this sIRB. A certificate of confidentiality from the NIH has been obtained to protect the privacy of study participants by limiting the disclosure of identifiable, sensitive research information except as required by law under specific circumstances. The trial was registered as NCT06671002 and first posted in November 2024. The first patient was recruited in January 2025. Enrolment is expected to continue until 2028. A model consent form is included in the online supplemental material.

The trial results will have significant potential for reproduction, dissemination and implementation. The CARES for Kids study has been carefully designed to include characteristics that maximise the ability to replicate study findings and translate findings into practice in diverse care settings. The two treatment arms (NSAID and opioid) represent readily available medication options as prescribed by surgeons to paediatric patients after low-risk surgery. The flexibility built into the two regimens increases their ability to be used by more patients and in more care settings. By including three different types of low-risk surgeries, the ability to apply this work to varied types of surgeries and groups of paediatric patients is enhanced.

There are unique opportunities to disseminate findings from the CARES for Kids study beyond the traditional academic routes. Existing infrastructure includes the Michigan Overdose Prevention Engagement Network (OPEN) to support dissemination. Federal entities, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and national quality organisations such as the Leapfrog Group, reference OPEN opioid prescribing recommendations for surgical care. OPEN also collaborates with the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services, Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan and state legislators. OPEN meets with these stakeholders through biannual in-person meetings, which serve as an additional platform to disseminate our findings. Through the Institute for Healthcare Policy and Innovation at the University of Michigan, a team of established liaisons regularly engage with federal and state policymakers. Study findings will be shared through one-on-one meetings, organised events and coordinated policy briefings facilitated by these channels. The CARES for Kids stakeholder advisory board will contribute to the design of digital multimedia communication materials and support broader dissemination through community events. Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan has committed to promoting the CARES for Kids study findings, similar to our previous opioid stewardship initiatives.50,52 CHLA is a partner institution with the University of Southern California and the investigator team maintains a longstanding history of collaboration, publication and community engagement with the Institute for Addiction Science (IAS). The affiliation with IAS at the University of Southern California will also extend the dissemination of our findings through IAS platforms, including its website, newsletters, social media, podcasts and press releases.

Potential barriers to dissemination and implementation of the study findings have been recognised. Some patients may be hesitant to accept findings favouring one analgesic regimen over another due to the personal nature of pain and/or sociocultural perception on pain management. To address this issue, the dissemination strategy will emphasise patient-reported outcomes for pain, AEs and important secondary outcomes after surgery. The results will be contextualised across diverse patient populations by collaborating with the stakeholder advisory board, including patient and caregiver partners, to present findings that are accessible and meaningful to the millions of patients who undergo low-risk surgeries each year. Because surgical providers may selectively adopt findings for certain types of procedures, CARES for Kids intentionally includes three low-risk procedures that vary by patient characteristics such as age, sex and body region. Trial findings for NSAIDs versus opioid may differ among patient subgroups or procedure types so the study will adapt messaging accordingly to reflect subgroup-specific evidence and ensure tailored recommendations.

Discussion

For the millions of paediatric patients who undergo surgical procedures every year, recent surgery remains a primary driver of opioid prescribing. Prescription opioids are associated with significant side effects with potential for long-term use or diversion. This multicentre, pragmatic randomised controlled study addresses a critical gap in knowledge in acute pain management for adolescents following surgery, particularly in the context of the ongoing opioid epidemic in the USA. By comparing the efficacy and safety of a regimen consisting of NSAIDs and acetaminophen to a low-dose opioid regimen combined with NSAIDs and acetaminophen, this study aims to provide valuable insights into optimal postoperative pain management for children and adolescents.

The design and approach for this study introduce several trade-offs that merit consideration. First, the study is designed as a pragmatic trial so adherence to the assigned, randomised analgesic regimen may vary across the participating adolescents; adherence will be important as a measure for the study. Although the two treatment arms are specified and standardised, adolescents or caregivers may not fill or use the prescribed medications. Additionally, adolescents may deviate from the assigned analgesic regimens if there is an AE or unexpected hospital readmission. Further, crossover may occur, most likely from the arm without opioids to the one with opioids, which will be viewed as an important measure. Next, adolescents and caregivers might use non-pharmacologic pain management strategies at home such as heat or ice packs, physical therapy or other supportive measures. While common instructions are provided, their use may not be standardised so this will be measured via patient-reported assessments. The use of adjunctive non-pharmacologic therapies also may affect postoperative pain management and recovery outcomes, which have the potential to introduce unmeasured variability within this study. Finally, while risk of non-medical prescription opioid use and abuse is multifactorial and not solely determined by the perioperative prescription, the study includes baseline opioid-related and substance-related risk assessment using screening instruments, enabling these factors to be incorporated into study analyses.

In summary, the CARES for Kids study compares the efficacy and safety of opioid vs non-opioid analgesic regimens in treating pain after low-risk surgery for adolescents. The data collected from this study will provide valuable insights to patients, providers, payors, policymakers and researchers regarding optimal postoperative pain management for children and adolescents. The findings in this study will guide surgical teams to make informed decisions that balance effective pain relief and minimising risks associated with prescription opioids.
