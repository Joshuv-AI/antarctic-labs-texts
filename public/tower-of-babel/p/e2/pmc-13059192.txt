A French national remote peer tutoring initiative: the gastroenterology course example
Authors: Guillaume Le Cosquer, Clément Pastier, Nathalie Ganne Carrie, Jérémie H. Lefevre, Olivier Palombi, Kevin Chevallier, Benoît Heid-Picard, Alexandre Degachi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccess to preparatory resources for the French National Ranking Exam (NRE) varies widely among medical students, contributing to heterogeneous preparation experiences. To promote equity, we created TACFA, a resident-led association providing free, nationwide remote peer-tutoring courses. This study evaluates the feasibility and acceptability of a national Gastroenterology preparatory lecture.MethodsThe lecture was designed by resident tutors and proofread by medical professors. It included interactive clinical cases and open discussion. Participant perceptions, including satisfaction and perceived usefulness were assessed using pre- and post-course surveys.ResultsAmong 2,395 regular TACFA participants, 1,453 (60.7%) attended the Gastroenterology lecture, and 1,322 (55.2%) completed the post-course survey. Among them, 1,227 were fifth-year students, accounting for 12.8% of the 9,600 expected NRE candidates in 2025. Participants represented all 35 French medical faculties, with a median age of 22 years, and 61.4% identifying as women. Despite 78% of students engaging in regular private preparatory activities, the TACFA lecture garnered positive feedback. Main motivations for private preparation were perceived program effectiveness (58.9%) and fear of missed opportunities (57.4%).The Gastroenterology lecture attracted 200 live attendees and 6,800 YouTube views at 6 months. Students rated the lecture 6.9/10 (SD: 1.7), and 83.6% reported that the lecture helped them to gain skills and knowledge necessary to prepare for the NRE. Students gave an average recommendation score of 8.9/10 (SD: 1.4).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the feasibility and acceptability of a national remote peer-tutoring lecture in Gastroenterology. Resident-led, accessible educational initiatives such as TACFA are well received by students and may support scalable approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08911-7.

Introduction

The French medical education system culminates in the National Ranking Exam (NRE), a high-stakes examination taken at the end of the sixth year of medical school [17]. The NRE determines both specialty choice and residency location, making it a pivotal milestone in medical training. Performance on this examination has long-term consequences for professional trajectories and geographic distribution of the medical workforce. As such, preparation for the NRE represents a central focus of the final year of undergraduate medical education in France [21, 24].

Preparation strategies for the NRE rely on a combination of institutional teaching, individual study, and supplementary preparatory activities, including mock examinations and thematic lectures. Several studies have shown that engagement in structured preparatory activities is associated with improved performance on the NRE, particularly through repeated exposure to clinical reasoning exercises and examination formats [2, 3, 7, 16, 17]. To cope with this challenge, medical schools offer diverse pedagogical resources for the NRE training.

However, the availability, intensity, and pedagogical diversity of these resources vary considerably across French medical schools. Geographic disparities in faculty staffing and access to senior academic clinicians have been documented, with medical schools possessing greater teaching resources demonstrating higher NRE success rates [14, 23]. Despite a standardized national examination framework, these differences contribute to heterogeneous preparation contexts nationwide.

In parallel with institutional resources, many students turn to private preparatory courses to complement their training. Although these courses are widely perceived as beneficial, no clear evidence has demonstrated their superiority over structured peer-supported or tutor-led approaches in terms of examination outcomes [29]. Reliance on external preparatory resources further reflects variability in local training ecosystems rather than a uniform national standard. The cost associated with private preparation may represent an additional constraint for some students, particularly those from low-income socio-economic backgrounds [6]. International studies have suggested that financial strain during medical training is associated with increased psychological distress [22].

In response to this heterogeneity in preparation contexts, we established a resident-led association, the Tutorat Associatif pour des Conférences en Formation Approfondie en Sciences Médicales (TACFA). The association's mission is to provide free, nationally accessible remote courses for the sixth-year medical students applying for the French NRE through peer tutoring. This initiative has received support from the National Conference of Medical Deans, the Scientific Council in Medicine of the NRE Centre and the National Coordination of Medical Teaching Colleges. These endorsements allow all clinical cases to be proofread by medical professors specializing in each discipline, ensuring consistency, scientific accuracy and educational rigor.

Within this broader program, the present study focuses on the development and assessment of the feasibility and acceptability of the Gastroenterology and Hepatology course, endorsed by the national teaching colleges for Gastroenterology and Hepatology (CDU-HGE), and Digestive Surgery.

Methods

Gastroenterology and hepatology course description

We conducted a free 150-min online course on October 30th of 2024, offered in the late afternoon via YouTube® (www.youtube.com/@tacfa-conferencenationalee8260) and Twitch® (m.twitch.tv/tacfa_medecine) platforms. As previously mentioned, this session was part of a broader national educational initiative by the TACFA. Membership in this resident-led association is voluntary and open to medical students. Regular updates regarding available programs, including content details and schedules, are shared through email, as well as TACFA's official channels on X® (www.x.com/tacfa3), Instagram (www.instagram.com/tacfa_asso), and Facebook® (www.facebook.com/tutotacfa) and TACFA’s website (www.tacfa.odoo.com). Participation in these programs is entirely optional for medical students.

The course featured a lecture structured around five clinical cases, followed by an open discussion session between medical students and peer tutors. Participants had one week to complete the clinical cases online in advance, using the free national platform of the Digital University in Health and Sport (“Université Numérique en Santé et Sport,” UNESS). This platform is a university-funded initiative that provides free and open access to validated resources for NRE preparation.

The pedagogical design was grounded in a competency-based approach aligned with recent reforms of French medical education, emphasizing clinical reasoning, multidisciplinary knowledge integration, and active learning through case-based discussion [13]. The educational framework and assessment design for these clinical cases complied with the new design of the NRE, launched in 2023 [9]. Each clinical case included 5–8 questions using various formats: Multiple Choice Questions (MCQ), Single Answer Questions (SAQ), one best answer questions (i.e. all suggested options are correct, but one holds greater importance in clinical decision-making), short and open answer questions, questions with a long menu (maximum of 25 possible answers), questions with zone selection on iconography (i.e. interactive questions requiring specific region identification on an image) [18]. The MCQ and SAQ formats presented clinical scenarios with one or multiple correct answers among the suggested options.

The course relied on principles of case-based learning and adult learning theory, promoting active engagement through clinical case resolution, in line with competency-based reforms of French medical education and the NRE framework [15, 28]. The choice of a live, remote format was informed by documented student preferences and aimed to ensure nationwide accessibility while fostering interactive learning [10].

The course content, including the correction of the clinical cases, was delivered collaboratively by a digestive surgeon (Clément Pastier) and two gastroenterologist residents (Guillaume Le Cosquer and Kilian Dobigny). Following the lecture, dedicated time was set aside for an interactive discussion between the audience and the tutors, facilitated via chat on both YouTube® and Twitch® platforms.

National peer tutoring program development

To develop a training course on the Gastroenterology and Hepatology tailored to the French national context, we recruited tutors among hepato-gastroenterology and digestive surgery residents (years 7 to 12 of medical training). Two lead tutors (GL and CP) were responsible for compiling clinical cases and ensuring their scientific accuracy and educational relevance.

To further ensure content quality, an anonymous external review was conducted by professors specialized in hepato-gastroenterology and digestive surgery from across the country. This collaborative approach ensured the highest standards of educational and medical rigor, aligning the course with the needs of medical students.

Study objectives

The program was evaluated through participant-reported outcomes focusing on satisfaction and perceived usefulness. Evaluation relied on pre- and post-course questionnaires developed specifically for this national peer-tutoring initiative (see Supplementary Document 1 and 2) [26]. The questionnaires were designed by resident tutors in collaboration with senior faculty members, guided by best practices for survey use in medical education [20]. They were conceived as formative evaluation tools rather than summative assessments, in order to explore learners’ preparation strategies, expectations, and perceived usefulness of the program. To ensure feasibility and maximize response rates in a large-scale national setting, the surveys were intentionally concise, with an estimated completion time of less than five minutes.

The pre-course questionnaire was made available on the UNESS digital platform one week prior to the live online correction of the clinical cases. It was designed to evaluate medical students' use of NRE preparation materials and to gauge their engagement with our national educational initiative.

An anonymous web survey was conducted on the UNESS platform one month after the Gastroenterology and Hepatology course. This post-course questionnaire aimed to assess participants' reactions to the program and the perceived usefulness of the course in supporting their learning.

In addition, an exploratory analysis was conducted to describe differences in demographic characteristics and NRE preparation habits between students who attended private preparatory lectures and those who did not. These analyses were intended solely to illustrate the variability in preparation strategies among participants.

Data collection

The following participant characteristics were recorded: gender, age, medical school year, use of private and public NRE preparation materials, and engagement with our national educational initiative. Additionally, an open-ended question was included to gather suggestions for improving the program.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as medians and ranges (or interquartile) and compared with Student's t-test (or the Mann–Whitney Wilcoxon test for variables with non-normal distribution). Categorical variables were reported as proportions and percentages and compared with chi-squared test (or Fisher’s exact tests when appropriate).

Two-sided statistical tests were used for all analyses and a p-value < 0.05 were considered significant. The statistical analyses were performed with the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29.0.1.0).

Results

Population characteristics

Of the 2,395 regular participants (defined as attending at least 3 lectures per year) in the TACFA preparation courses, 1,453 students (60.7%) participated in the Gastroenterology and Hepatology course and completed the pre-course questionnaire. Participants represented all 35 French medical faculties. Among respondents to the pre-course questionnaire, 61.4% identified as women, 36.3% as men, and 2.3% declined to disclose their gender. In terms of academic level, 149 students (10.3%) were in their fourth year, 1,227 (84.4%) in their fifth year (representing 12.8% of the 9,600 NRE expected candidates in 2025), and 77 (5.3%) in their sixth year. The median age of respondents was 22 years (range: 18–40 years).

National Ranking Exam (NRE) preparation habits

Among participants, 873 (60.1%) reported attending local medical schools’ preparatory lectures, and 235 (16.2%) participated in local peer tutoring programs. Additionally, 1,373 students (94.5%) stated that they engaged in at least one private preparatory activity, such as mock exams or lectures. Of these, 1,071 (78%) attended regular private preparatory lectures.

The primary reasons for following private preparatory programs were: belief in their effectiveness (n = 631; 58.9%), fear of missing opportunities (n = 615; 57.4%), fear of being disadvantaged without them (n = 424; 39.6%), lack of confidence in the university’s ability to adequately prepare for the NRE (n = 290; 27.1%), and feeling incapable of succeeding independently (n = 199; 18.6%). Conversely, the main reasons for not participating in private preparatory lectures were: lack of time (n = 281; 73.6%), financial constraints (n = 205; 53.7%), and perceived ineffectiveness of private programs (n = 98; 25.7%).

Regarding the materials used for NRE preparation, participants reported using: the reference book from the Professors College of Digestive Surgery (n = 910; 62.6%), the reference book from the Professors College of Hepato-Gastroenterology (n = 586; 40.3%), online resources from the Professors College of Hepato-Gastroenterology (n = 376; 25.9%), nationally validated contents provided by the Professors Colleges provided by UNESS (“Livret de suivi des apprentissages”, LiSA)(n = 949; 65.3%), and other private, non-official documents (n = 351; 24.2%).

Exploratory description of preparation strategies

In this exploratory descriptive comparison, students attending private preparatory programs showed lower reported use of official reference materials from the Professors College of Hepato-Gastroenterology (38.7% vs. 45%, p = 0.029) and Digestive Surgery (56.3% vs. 64.9%, p = 0.003). Additionally, these students reported lower participation in local university preparatory lectures (57.3% vs. 67.8%; p < 0.001) and local peer tutoring programs (14.7% vs. 20.4%; p = 0.009). The gender ratio also differed significantly between the two groups, with a higher proportion of men (39.3%) among those attending private lectures compared to non-attendees (27.7%; p < 0.001).

Reaction at 1 month

Among the 1,322 respondents to the post-course questionnaire, 62.9% identified as women, 35.9% as men, and 1.2% declined to disclose their gender. In terms of academic level, 210 students (15.9%) were in their fourth year, 1,048 (79.3%) in their fifth year, and 64 (4.8%) in their sixth year. The median age of respondents was 22 years (range: 18–40 years).

Regarding attendance at TACFA lectures, 194 respondents (14.7%) reported that this was their first lecture, 831 (62.9%) had attended one to five lectures previously, 177 (13.4%) had attended six to ten lectures, and 120 (9.1%) had attended more than ten lectures. When asked whether they would recommend TACFA lectures to other medical students on a scale of 0 (not at all) to 10 (highly recommend), students gave an average score of 8.9 (SD: 1.4).

Among the 841 respondents who attended the Gastroenterology and Hepatology course, 63% felt the lecture adequately covered the topics of Digestive Surgery, Gastroenterology and Hepatology. However, 259 students (30.8%) reported that some clinical case points remained unclear. On a scale of 0 (very poor) to 10 (excellent), students rated the overall quality of the Gastroenterology and Hepatology lecture at 6.9 (SD: 1.7). A major criticism regarding the course was the difficulty in interpreting imaging, particularly CT scans. Many students felt that these imaging interpretations were beyond the capabilities of pre-clinical medical students (particularly in diagnosing bowel obstruction).

Participant-reported perceived usefulness of the course

Among the 841 respondents who attended the Gastroenterology and Hepatology course, 703 (83.6%) reported that they perceived the lecture as helpful to gain skills and knowledge necessary to prepare for the NRE. Furthermore, 602 students (71.6%) reported confidence in their ability to apply perceived learning from the lecture to clinical practice during their internships.

Discussion

This study highlights the feasibility and acceptability of a free national, resident-led, remote peer-tutoring initiative designed to support students preparing for the NRE. While the program was developed within a broader context of concerns regarding heterogeneity in NRE preparation resources, the present evaluation was intentionally limited to feasibility and participant-reported acceptability. The findings support the viability of implementing such an initiative at a national scale.

The audience predominantly consisted of 5th-year students, reflecting the timing of examinations early in the 6th year under the new design for NRE set in 2023 [9]. Women constituted two-thirds of our cohort, mirroring broader trends in medical school demographics [27]. Most participants reported regular use of private preparatory resources. This observation should not be interpreted as a lack of relevance or impact of the present intervention, as preparation strategies are typically well established, cumulative, and influenced by long-standing perceptions of risk and performance optimization [29]. As such, any shift in students’ reliance on private preparatory resources would be unlikely to emerge or be detectable during the first year of implementation of a new educational initiative.

Our exploratory analysis revealed differences between students who attended private preparatory lectures and those who did not. These descriptive findings highlight variation in reported preparation strategies with a potential preference for materials provided by private programs over official resources. Furthermore, the lower engagement in university-organized and local peer-tutoring programs among private program attendees might suggest that private resources may be considered as substitutes for institutional contents. The observed overrepresentation of men among private program attendees aligns with existing studies that have identified gender-based disparities in educational preferences, likely shaped by cultural and societal norms [1].

Although financial constraints were not the most frequently cited reason for non-participation in private preparatory lectures, more than half of the students nonetheless identified cost as a limiting factor. This finding aligns with national survey data from 2023, which showed that 58% of French medical students preparing for the NRE experience financial anxiety, and 37% report holding a part-time job during their studies, underscoring the persistence of financial pressure despite widespread engagement with private preparatory resources [5].

Participant-reported outcomes indicated a high level of perceived usefulness and a strong willingness to recommend the course, despite a more moderate overall quality rating. This discrepancy may reflect the demanding nature of the content, including the breadth of topics covered and the integration of imaging interpretation, rather than limited acceptability. Similar programs in medical education have demonstrated that collaborative and interactive learning environments significantly enhance student success [25]. Peer-tutoring programs have also been shown to improve both learning outcomes and confidence by fostering a sense of community and mutual support [8].

The originality of our initiative lies less in the pedagogical format itself than in its national scale and coordination, alignment with the official examination framework, faculty-reviewed resident delivery, and integration with open digital platforms. Most peer-tutoring initiatives described in the literature are local or single-institution programs, whereas this intervention reached students from all 35 French medical faculties and engaged a substantial proportion of a national cohort. While the NRE is context-specific, the implementation framework may be transferable to other educational systems facing heterogeneous local training environments.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be noted. First, the evaluation of the program relied on self-reported data. As self-assessment of learning is known to be unreliable, perceived usefulness should not be interpreted as evidence of objective knowledge or skill acquisition [11, 11, 12, 12, 19]. In addition, questionnaire anonymity prevented linkage to NRE outcomes, and the instruments were not psychometrically validated [4]. Moreover, the limited disciplinary scope and multifactorial determinants of examination performance (e.g. individual psychological factors and heterogeneous local training environments) preclude any inference regarding impact on NRE results. Future studies should therefore incorporate a broader range of items and objective outcome measures, such as performance on standardized national mock exams, to more accurately evaluate the educational impact of this peer-tutoring initiative. Second, while our sample included students from all 35 French medical faculties, enhancing the generalizability of our findings, voluntary participation may have introduced selection bias, favouring more motivated or engaged students. Finally, the focus on a single specialty (Gastroenterology and Hepatology) limits the generalizability of our results to other medical disciplines.

In conclusion, this study represents the first national peer-tutoring initiative in French medical education. Our pilot study demonstrates the feasibility and acceptability of this approach, particularly in the context of a targeted Gastroenterology and Hepatology course. Building on this initial experience, we plan to expand the program to other specialties, incorporating student feedback to refine and enhance the content. Future longitudinal studies will be needed to explore the potential educational impact of this nationally coordinated initiative.
