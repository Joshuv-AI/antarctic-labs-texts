A novel mass–heat exchange network analogy, regression, and synthesis method for mass exchanger networks
Authors: Yuan Xiao, Guomin Cui
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Synthesis of mass exchanger network (MEN) is significant to save the material and reduce the pollution for process industries. Considering its small magnitude and concentration constraints, the optimized configuration of MEN can easily violate the concentration constraints, leading to a reduction in the structural diversity during the optimization process compared to that of a heat exchanger network (HEN) of the same size. Moreover, the constraint on the mass transfer driving force based on the equilibrium composition may also have major effects on the optimization process. To address these issues, this study presents a process–equipment–system analogy between the MEN and the HEN, proposing a novel mass–heat exchange network analogy, regression, and synthesis method. First, the generalized HEN was established by using an analogy considering the MEN based on either the original composition or the equilibrium composition. In this regard, a coordination coefficient was introduced to correct the deviation of the analogy relationship between the heat transfer and the mass transfer processes. Then, the generalized HEN was optimized by employing the random walk algorithm with compulsive evolution (RWCE). A regression method was proposed to regress the optimized HEN solution to the MEN expression, allowing the continuous optimization by using the RWCE with finer parameters. The final optimal solution obtained was found to be lower than those reported in the literature with enhanced structural diversity, demonstrating the effectiveness of the presented method and providing new ideas for further synchronous optimization of combined heat and mass exchanger networks.

Introduction

Heat exchanger networks (HENs) and mass exchanger networks (MENs) are two typical types of process system synthesis that have been extensively studied and utilized widely in various applications in the chemical industry, aiming at achieving energy saving and pollution reduction [1,2]. In this regard, the optimization technique for MENs was developed later than that for HENs, and thus the methodology of the former was built on top of the latter. In general, the physical forms of the two networks are similar, as shown in Fig. 1, where the characteristics of mass transfer in the MEN (as Fig. 1(b)) are analogous to those of heat transfer in the HEN (as Fig. 1(a)).Fig. 1Description of problems associated with HEN and MEN, (a) HEN, (b) MEN.Fig. 1

Description of problems associated with HEN and MEN, (a) HEN, (b) MEN.

When characterizing the HEN synthesis problem, a number NH of hot and NC of cold process streams to be cooled and heated, respectively, are considered. The main task of the HEN synthesis is to achieve the minimum total annual cost (TAC) of the network by the heat recovery among hot and cold streams [3]. The supply and target temperatures, heat capacity flowrates, and heat transfer coefficients of process streams are also provided [4]. Besides, the hot and cold utilities with the corresponding supply and target temperatures are optional to supplement the heat, aiming to satisfy the target temperatures of the cold and hot process streams [5,6]. Nevertheless, the operating costs of the hot and cold utilities and the heat transfer coefficients of the employed heaters and coolers are known, as are the capital cost evaluations for heat exchangers [7].

Similarly, the MEN synthesis problem can be described by the numbers NR of rich streams and NS of lean streams, including process and external mass separating agents (MSAs) [8,9]. The task of MEN synthesis is to achieve the minimum network TAC by the mass recovery among rich and lean streams. In the analysis, the flowrates and the supply and target compositions of each rich stream are also given. Furthermore, the supply and target compositions and the mass transfer equilibrium relations for each lean stream are also provided. The process lean streams are freely available, while the external MSAs are purchased from the market [10]. The flowrate of each lean stream is to be determined by the synthesis task, while their upper bounds are considered constraints. Moreover, the annual operating time, size details, and cost information of mass exchangers, as well as the annual capital costs (ACC), are given [4].

Furthermore, the methods applied in HEN synthesis are more developed and advanced than those used in MEN synthesis, as highlighted in Fig. 2. Short and Isafiade [11] presented a systematic review covering MEN synthesis applications for over around thirty years. Due to the analogy between the two networks, they reported that most of the synthesis methods used in MEN applications have been adopted from existing synthesis techniques for HENs [11]. Therefore, the existing methods for HEN and MEN syntheses belong to the same category. This includes sequential synthesis techniques mainly based on pinch technology, and simultaneous synthesis techniques based on mathematical models and optimization algorithms.Fig. 2Number of publications targeting synthesis methods applied in HEN and MEN applications in ScienceDirect and CNKI from August 1990 to April 2023.Fig. 2

Number of publications targeting synthesis methods applied in HEN and MEN applications in ScienceDirect and CNKI from August 1990 to April 2023.

The pinch technology for MEN synthesis was developed on top of the method for HEN synthesis, mainly employing the rich and lean streams composite curves [12] or the grand composite curve [13]. Gadalla [14] presented a graphical method involving the plotting of rich compositions against the equivalent lean compositions and avoiding mass transfer across the pinch. Moreover, Yanwarizal et al. [15] applied the stream composition against the plotting of mass load and the mass allocation network for simultaneous targeting of minimum MSAs and network design. Other sequential methods based on mathematical programming individually set different targets for the minimum number of units, usage of MSA, and capital cost of network design [16].

Furthermore, simultaneous approaches can minimize the TAC based on the formulation of a mixed integer nonlinear programming (MINLP) model and establishment of algorithms for its efficient solving. As an analogy to such models for HEN synthesis, the hyperstructure [17] and the stage-wise superstructure (SWS) models [18] have been successfully adapted into the synthesis of MENs in previous investigations. In this regard, Szitkai et al. [19] developed an MINLP formulation for single-component systems transferring mass through packed columns, based on iso-compositional mixing assumption, to improve the computational efficiency of model solving. In connection with this, an interval-based MINLP superstructure (IBMS) [20] was developed, capable of reducing the number of binary variables and saving computational time for initialization and solution generation. Further, Azeez et al. modified the MEN-SWS and derived models based on the supply-based superstructure (SBS) [4], the supply and target-based superstructure (S&TBS) [21], the target and supply-based superstructure (T&SBS) [21], by defining the intervals of superstructure with the use of the rich streams' supply compositions and the lean streams’ target compositions. Liu et al. [22] modeled the mass exchangers as interceptors and applied linearization, disjunction, and relaxation to establish a formulation for multiple components. Amelia and Siemanond [23] developed the original SWS to overcome the combined heat and mass exchanger network by using a more accurate formula. In general, these non-convex models are difficult to solve when using combinatorial optimization solvers. Recently, stochastic methods, including the genetic algorithm (GA) [24], the particle swarm algorithm (PSO) [25], and the random walk algorithm with compulsive evolution (RWCE) [26] have been successfully used in the optimization of MENs. In addition, Xu et al. [27] proposed a two-step optimization method combining two non-structural models using the mass loads and the number of trays for the tray columns, respectively, to improve the performance of MEN synthesis. To make the non-structural model adaptive to multi-component MEN synthesis, Xiao et al. [28] presented a node-based vertical non-structural model (NV-NSM) and optimized the model using the RWCE and fine-search strategy (FS).

However, the optimization performance of the above-mentioned synthesis methods is shown to be inefficient during the late evolution period. This is related to the following two main aspects. On the one hand, the large gap between the magnitudes of variables for the MEN and HEN cases restricts the efficiency of HEN synthesis methods in the structural evolution and global optimization for MEN synthesis problems. On the other hand, the strict constraints of the outlet compositions and equilibrium compositions used for the driving force of mass transfer lead to the production of infeasible solutions that violate the constraints during the MEN synthesis. This, in turn, restricts the solution domain.

Therefore, this study aims to relate the MEN synthesis to the generalized HEN (GHEN) synthesis by making use of the analogy between the two problems and directly applying the advanced HEN synthesis technologies. The GHEN was established based on either the equilibrium or initial compositions of lean streams to expand the solution domain. The optimized GHEN was then regressed into the MEN expression and further optimized to search for the global optimum.

Methodologies

Process, equipment, and system analogies between heat and mass transfer

To overcome the restrictions of the small magnitude of composition on the optimization performance of the MEN, the MEN was transformed to GHEN by employing the multi-scale analogies between heat and mass transfer, involving the process, equipment, and system levels.

Analogy between heat and mass transfer process

According to the transfer principle, the transfer of momentum, mass, and heat follows the same physical mechanism. In general, two or more processes are considered analogous if they are governed by the same dimensionless equation [29]. In this regard, the heat and mass transfer processes can be analogized by comparing the boundary and layer equations of heat and concentration in their dimensionless form along with the boundary conditions in the y-direction [29], as shown in Equations (1), (2).(1)u*∂T*∂x*+v*∂T*∂y*=1ReLPr×∂2T*∂y*2T*(x*,0)=0,T*(x*,∞)=1(2)u*∂CA*∂x*+v*∂CA*∂y*=1ReLSc×∂2CA*∂y*2CA*(x*,0)=0,CA*(x*,∞)=1

Moreover, C0 can balance the different costs of the two heat and mass exchange equipment, leading to more realistic system optimization results. The introduction of the coordination coefficient also aids in facilitating the description of the comparative relationship between heat and mass transfer processes. This helps to realize the comparative analysis of the total heat and mass transfer coefficients for the corresponding equipment and then obtain the generalized form of the heat exchanger corresponding to the monomer mass exchange equipment.

Analogy of HEN and MEN

The analogies of the heat and mass transfer processes and equipment are summarized in Table 1. The results indicate that an MEN with NR rich streams and NS lean streams can be transformed into a GHEN with NR hot streams and NS cold streams, employing dimensionless compositions and temperatures. Noteworthy, substituting the MEN problem for the obtained GHEN can not only widen the magnitude of variables, but also enhance the flexibility of modeling and optimization. Modeling of GHEN based on non-structural model

The node-based non-structural model (NNM) with a stream splitting [30] was employed in this study for modeling GHEN. The NNM generates stream matches by randomly connecting the nodes set in the hot and cold streams [31], as shown in Fig. 6(a). In this regard, any stream match satisfying the temperature constraint is allowed in the NNM as shown in Fig. 6(b), making such a model more flexible than the SWS model, which presets the “stage” and fixes matching order. On the other hand, the NNM can include stream matches outside the solution space expressed by using the SWS.Fig. 6Illustration of the NNM for GHEN, (a) Empty structure, (b) Generated stream matches.Fig. 6

Illustration of the NNM for GHEN, (a) Empty structure, (b) Generated stream matches.

Furthermore, the external MSA of the MEN is analogized to the last process cold stream of the GHEN. In this case, different from the original HEN problem, there are no hot or cold utilities to supplement the heat requirements of each stream. Notably, the process lean streams of the MEN are freely available; therefore, their maximum flowrates are used for the synthesis of GHEN. Moreover, the flowrate of the last process cold stream is determined by the synthesis task while satisfying the upper bound.

In addition, NsgH and NsgC stream-split groups are set in the hot and cold streams, respectively, and respective NbH and NbC branches are set in the hot and cold stream-split groups. Noteworthy, the number of nodes determines the solution space of the NNM. For instance, Xu et al. [32] employed serial numbers to denote all the nodes located in the hot and cold streams. However, the positions on the stream, the stream-split group, and the stream branch must be recognized every time as part of the cost simulation. This leads to major losses in terms of the computational time and resources. To directly recognize the positions along the process streams, the heat loads on the preset hot and cold nodes are expressed as QHi,im,ib(i∈NR,im∈NsgH,ib∈NbH) and QCj,jm,jb(j∈NS,jm∈NsgC,jb∈NbC), respectively. Optimization of GHEN-NNM by using the basic RWCE

The optimization variables of the proposed GHEN-NNM include QHi,im,ib, SPHi,im,ib, SPCj,jm,jb, and FCpNS. The RWCE method was selected for the optimization of the GHEN-NNM due to its feasibility in the simultaneous synthesis of integer and continuous variables. Moreover, this method possesses a global search ability beneficial for the mutation mechanism of accepting imperfect solutions. The optimization workflow of the RWCE is shown in Fig. 7. The main steps in the optimization process include: 1) initialization of the GHEN with the heat loads, split ratios, and heat capacity of the last cold stream; 2) proportional evolution of heat loads and split ratios of the generated HE units and the elimination based on the minimum heat load and split ratio; 3) generation of new HE units; 4) evolution of the heat capacity of the last cold stream; 5) comparative analysis of the TACs of the initial and evolved structures, in addition to the selection and mutation; 6) output of the optimal solution when the terminal condition is satisfied. Conclusion

This study establishes a method for process, equipment, and system analogies between heat and mass transfer processes. Based on the proposed method, the GHEN is analogized to the MEN to modify the magnitude of the problem, where the coordinate coefficient and the maximum temperature can be adjusted to control the scale of the analogy. The GHEN is optimized by using a non-structural model along with a random algorithm to improve the global optimization performance. Then, the optimal GHEN is regressed into the MEN expression and optimized further by the FS-RWCE method. The proposed approach application in the case study yielded multiple optimal solutions that are lower than the best solution published in the literature, with a maximum reduction of TAC of 3.59 ‰. Moreover, the diverse stream matching relationships in the obtained optimal configurations demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in expanding the stream matching space and enhancing the structural evolution of the MEN synthesis by analogizing the problem to the GHEN synthesis.

Further, when setting a proper maximum temperature Tmax, the proposed method was shown to perform well in the global optimization and structural evolution of the GHEN and MEN. Based on this, it is recommended that Tmax should not be very high. Nevertheless, the initial compositions of the lean streams in the MEN can be analogized to the temperatures in the GHEN, which, in turn, can expand the heat transfer space and the solution space for the GHEN, in particular, when Tmax is set relatively high.

Overall, the proposed mass–heat analogy, regression, and synthesis method can provide a novel and effective approach for the global optimization of MEN synthesis problems. In addition, it can lay the foundation for the furtherly simultaneous synthesis of the combined heat and mass exchanger networks in the future, and can aid in attaining efficient energy savings as well as pollution reductions for process systems.

Limitations of the study

In this study, the proposed mass–heat exchange network analogy, regression, and synthesis methods were implemented only in the synthesis of an MEN employing continuous packed-columns as mass exchangers. In its current status, the method is still not feasible for implementation in the case of MENs employing tray-columns as mass exchangers. Thus, undeniably, a lot more systematic explorations are further demanded to further develop this method in order to make it applicable to the MEN problems with multi-equipment.

In addition, the effects of the key analogy parameters were studied herein by employing only a univariate analysis. In future investigations, an adaptive analogy strategy needs to be established to determine the parameters, considering the needs of different case studies and applications. Finally, the analogy parameters should be further linked to the key parameters of the algorithm to fully utilize the potential of the proposed method.
