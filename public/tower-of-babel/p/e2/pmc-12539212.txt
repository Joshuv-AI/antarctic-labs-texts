Targeted modification of cis-elements in the CUL3 gene to restore exon 9 inclusion for treating Gordon syndrome
Authors: Xiaomeng Shi, Shanshan Lu, Qian Tang, Xiangzhong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe weak splice acceptor site (AS) of exon 9 underlies almost all pathogenic variants of Cullin3 (CUL3) causing exon 9 skipping in Gordon syndrome, emphasizing the need for splicing-targeted therapeutic strategies. This study explored universal therapeutic targets to modulate AS and investigated their potential and mechanisms for restoring normal splicing.ResultsThrough bioinformatic prediction, minigene assays, EMSA, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated construction of mutant cell lines and RIP, three rescue sites in the polypyrimidine (Py) tract of intron 8 were identified, including A(-9)T, A(-10)T and AA(-9, -10)TT, with AA(-9, -10)TT most effectively promoting exon inclusion by extending the Py-tract to increase U2AF2 binding. Additionally, previous candidate target A18G was confirmed to rescue exon 9 skipping by weakening hnRNP A1 splicing inhibition in endogenous cell models.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the therapeutic potential of AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G in CUL3-related Gordon syndrome, suggesting the targeted modification of cis-elements could be an ideal and universal strategy to develop treatments for splicing-related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00820-y.Keywords: Cullin3 gene, Exon skipping, Splicing regulation, Gordon syndrome, Pathogenic variant, Potential therapeutic site

Background

The process of converting precursor messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) transcribed from genes into translatable mRNAs by removing noncoding introns and ligating protein-coding exons is crucial for regulating gene expression and ensuring protein diversity [1, 2]. Approximately 95% of human multiexon genes are estimated to have more than one mode of pre-mRNA splicing, known as alternative splicing [3, 4]. The precise excision of eukaryotic pre-mRNA introns relies on the accurate regulation of cis-acting elements and trans-acting factors [5]. Disruption of the splicing mechanism can result in protein dysfunction, leading to organ dysfunction and a range of diseases [6]. With increasing knowledge of splicing variants, increasing studies have demonstrated that aberrant splicing is the major pathogenic mechanism of cancer and various genetic diseases, such as neurological, cardiovascular, and renal disorders [7–11]. Notably, approximately 25–50% of point variants cause disease by affecting normal splicing, and exon skipping is the most common form of mis-splicing, accounting for approximately 30–40% of abnormal splicing events [12–14]. Therefore, a deep understanding of the occurrence and regulatory mechanism of aberrant splicing is necessary for exploring potential therapeutic strategies for splicing disorders.

Pre-mRNA splicing depends on the binding of the spliceosome and RNA-binding proteins to cis-acting elements and involves a series of RNA‒RNA, RNA‒protein, and protein‒protein interactions [15, 16]. Disruption of cis-acting and trans-acting elements can lead to aberrant splicing patterns [17]. Cis-acting elements on pre-mRNAs include conserved splice sites (5’ splice donor sites and 3’ splice acceptor sites), branch points, polypyrimidine tracts (Py-tracts), exonic/intronic splicing enhancers (ESEs/ISEs) and silencers (ESSs/ISSs). Trans-acting elements include activators and repressors that bind to splicing enhancers and silencers, such as members of the serine/arginine-rich (SR) protein family and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) family, respectively [17–19]. Splicing is the process of spliceosome assembly, rearrangement, and catalytic activation [20]. The spliceosome is an intricate assembly composed of five small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs: U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6) and approximately 200 associated proteins. The U2-type spliceosome is the major complex responsible for the recognition and excision of most introns, with specific conserved splicing factors such as splicing factor 3b subunit 1 (SF3B1), U2 small nuclear RNA auxiliary factor (U2AF), and serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 2 (SRSF2) indispensable for this process [17–20]. Studies have shown that most splicing variants cause disease by affecting conserved splicing signals or disrupting the balance of splicing regulatory elements to interfere with the effective recognition and function of nearby splice sites [21–24]. As the understanding of mis-splicing mechanisms has advanced, scientists have developed various therapeutic strategies based on distinct aberrant splicing mechanisms for disease treatment, such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), by blocking or inducing targeted exon skipping, for treating spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), and base-editing therapy targeted to correct the splicing protein RBM20 variant for treating dilated cardiomyopathy [22, 25–28]. Restoring proper splicing to obtain normal functional proteins may represent the most direct and effective strategy for some specific diseases, especial for those exon skipping-related diseases.

The CUL3 (MIM: 614496) gene is the causative gene associated with Gordon syndrome, a rare genetic disease, and CUL3 mutations typically lead to the most severe disease phenotypes. Notably, the vast majority of variants identified occur in exon 9 and flanking introns, causing exon 9 exclusion in mRNA and generating a pathogenic protein lacking amino acids 403–459 [29, 30]. Owing to the weak AS (BDGP 0.51) in intron 8 of CUL3, the splicing of exon 9 is susceptible to interference by the adjacent splicing environment [30]. As proposed in our previous study, developing a therapeutic strategy to rescue exon 9 skipping of CUL3 could provide a promising treatment option for this disease. By performing a minigene screening based on two pathogenic synonymous variants, c.1221 A > G (A15G) and c.1236G > A (G30A), we identified a potential candidate site, c.1224 A > G (A18G), which could significantly rescue exon 9 skipping induced by both tested variants [31]. However, whether the candidate site works well in vivo remains unknown. Are there more suitable sites for rescuing exon 9 skipping, especially in the intron region? Can the weak AS strength be improved? These questions arouse our interest in further exploration.

In this study, we first performed a stepwise nucleotide substitution in the intron 8 region (from c.1207-3 to c.1207-10) and estimated the effect of a specific single nucleotide substitution on adjacent AS by BDGP, and we identified that the nucleotide substitutions of c.1207–9 A > T (A(-9)T) or c.1207–10 A > T (A(-10)T) and their double substitution of AA(-9, -10)TT significantly increase AS scores. Using in vitro minigene experiments, we demonstrated that these three nucleotide substitution forms could rescue almost all exon 9 skipping caused by variants near or in the AS region. Of these, AA(-9, -10)TT had the best rescue effect. Then, using the endogenous mutant cell lines constructed via homology-directed repair (HDR)-mediated clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9), we validated the rescue effects of AA(-9, -10)TT and the previously identified candidate site A18G, the function of which was not verified in vivo. As expected, both the AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G nucleotide substitutions had good rescue effects on exon 9 skipping. Furthermore, through mechanistic exploration via electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) and RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays, we demonstrated that, by extending the surrounding Py-tract region, AA(-9, -10)TT substitutions enhanced the binding of Py-tract-associated splicing factor U2AF2 with CUL3 pre-mRNAs to enforce exon 9 inclusion. A18G weakens the splicing inhibition of hnRNP A1 to restore exon 9 inclusion, in accordance with our previous in vitro results. Through this study, we present a new splicing-targeted therapeutic approach with the potential to serve as a universal strategy for Gordon syndrome.

Methods

Bioinformatics analysis

All CUL3 point variants were collected from the Human Gene Mutation Database and ClinVar (August 2022). Bioinformatics software was used to predict the effects of these variants on pre-mRNA splicing. Given its demonstrated reliability in predicting human splice sites, its ability to provide canonical splice site types along with corresponding sequences and strength scores, and its convenient and widespread application in previous studies, BDGP (https://fruitfly.org/seq_tools/splice.html) was employed to explore the potential impact of variants on the consensus 5’ donor or 3’ acceptor sites and the generation of new sites. The Human Splicing Finder (HSF, https://www.genomnis.com/access-hsf) was used to assess the influence of variants on classical splice sites and splicing regulatory elements. The prediction of RNA secondary structures was performed using RNAfold (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at//cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi). AlphaFold 3 (https://alphafoldserver.com) was used to predict the interactions between the 475-amino acid sequence of U2AF2 (retrieved from NCBI) and the RNA sequences of WT-RNA (AUCUUAAUUUUG) and Mut-RNA (AUCUUUUUUUUG), respectively. An inter-protein Template Modeling score (ipTM) greater than 0.6 was considered indicative of a meaningful interaction. DeepClip analysis (https://deepclip-web.compbio.sdu.dk) was used to predict the effect of nucleotide substitutions on protein–RNA binding.

Plasmid construction and site-directed mutagenesis

The wild-type (WT) minigene plasmid of CUL3 (pSPL3-CUL3-WT) was previously constructed, and point mutant plasmids were prepared via the QuikChange™ Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA) [30, 31]. The U2AF2 expression plasmid was constructed according to the methods of our previous study [31]. The cDNA from 293T cells was used to amplify the coding sequence of U2AF2 via RT‒PCR, and the PCR products were subsequently cloned and inserted into the expression vector pcDNA3.1-3×flag at the BamHI and XhoI restriction sites (BamHI: CGCG∧GATCC; EcoRI: CCGG∧AATTC). All the plasmids were confirmed using Sanger sequencing, and all the primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S1.

Cell culture, transfection and splicing analysis

Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) and HeLa cells were cultured as described in previous studies [10, 31]. The cells were seeded into a 24-well plate. After they reached 70–80% confluence, the empty pSPL3-control (EV), pSPL3-WT and pSPL3-Mut plasmids were transfected individually via the Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were collected, total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Japan). PCR amplification was performed via the vector-specific primers SD6 (forward primer: 5’-TCTGAGTCACCTGGACAACC-3’) and SA2 (reverse primer: 5’-ATCTCAGTGGTATTTGTGAGC-3’), as previously described [10]. Finally, the PCR products were analysed via 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE) and semi-quantification to assess the transcript patterns, and the target DNA bands corresponding to the transcripts were verified by sequencing.

Protein purification and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

EMSAs were performed to investigate the interaction between target RNA fragments and the U2AF2 protein [32, 33]. CY5.5 fluorescence-labelled wild-type and mutant RNA probes (WT-RNA and Mut-RNA) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. U2AF2 protein was prepared via overexpression and purification in 293T cells. Briefly, the pcDNA3.1-U2AF2-3×Flag plasmid (30 µg) was transfected into 293T cells (2 × 107) via Lipofectamine 2000 as described above. Seventy-two hours after transfection, 3×Flag-tagged U2AF2 protein was purified with Flag Affinity beads (Merck, CELLMM2) and detected by Western blotting (WB) using an anti-Flag antibody (Sigma, F1804). For binding, the samples were incubated for 30 min at room temperature based on the following system specifications: 1 µL of 10× protein‒RNA binding buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 0.25 µL of RNase inhibitor (Takara, Japan), 1 µL of purified U2AF2 protein at 5 µg/µL, 1 µL of 5 µM fluorescent RNA probe, and 6.75 µL of DEPC water. Finally, the RNA‒protein complexes were separated via electrophoresis on a 6% polyacrylamide gel in 0.5× TBE buffer (Beyotime, China) and visualized using a LI-COR Odyssey Imager (LICOR, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Construction of endogenous cell lines via HDR-CRISPR/Cas9

Three 293T-based cell lines containing G30A, G30A&AA(-9, -10)TT and G30A&A18G mutations in CUL3 were generated via HDR-based CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing technology. Specific small guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting different protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs) and single-stranded oligo DNA nucleotide donors (ssODN donors) built for HDR were designed and synthesized (sequences provided in Table S2). Nucleofection was performed according to the protocol of the Amaxa™ Cell Line Nucleofector™ Kit V (Lonza, CH). In brief, 293T cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS. Then, 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 90 µL of room-temperature nucleofector solution. One microlitre of sgRNA (100 µM) and 1 µL of Cas9 (62 µM) were added to 6 µL of nucleofector solution for 10 min, and 2 µL of the ssODN donor (100 µM) was added for another 2 min. Then, the mixture was incubated with the cell suspension and transfected in the electroporation cuvette using program A-023. After electroporation, the cells were transferred to a 6-well plate and grown in 2 mL of complete Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) for 2 days. Colonies derived from single cells were then obtained via the limiting dilution method. Genomic DNA was extracted from the cell lines via a TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China). DNA fragments spanning the targeted region of the CUL3 gene were amplified using PCR, and Sanger sequencing was subsequently conducted to ensure that the colonies had targeted mutations. The sequencing primers used are listed in Table S3.

RNA binding protein Immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

The overexpression plasmids of the target protein were transfected into WT and endogenous mutant cells as described above [31], and the nuclear extract (200 µL) was prepared after 72 h using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, China). Immunoprecipitation was performed with Pierce™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the instructions. A total of 200 µL of nuclear extract was incubated with 5 µg of primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by a 2-hour incubation with 30 µL of prewashed magnetic beads at 4 °C. After the pellets were washed with PBS, 1 mL of PBS was added to resuspend them. Then, 1/4 of the solution was used to verify the precipitated proteins using WB, and 3/4 of the solution was used to elute coprecipitated RNA. RT‒PCR and AGE were conducted to assess RNA enrichment as detailed above, and the primers used are shown in Table S4.

Statistical analysis

The experimental bands were accurately quantified via ImageJ software. The percentage of exon skipping (%) = (lower band/(lower band + upper band))×100. For EMSA, the relative binding affinity was normalized to that of the WT. The relative enrichment in RIP was normalized to that of the corrected control group (relative enrichment = (IPexp − IgGexp)/(IPctrl − IgGctrl); exp, the experimental group; ctrl, the control group). In the RIP assay, to evaluate U2AF2-mediated enrichment of CUL3 pre-mRNAs in the context of G30A or G30A&AA(-9, -10)TT mutation, G30A cells were used as the control group. To evaluate the enrichment of CUL3 pre-mRNAs by hnRNP A1 in wild-type (WT) 293T cells and mutant cells carrying G30A mutation or G30A&A18G mutation, WT cells were used as the control group. The data are presented as the means ± SEMs (n = 3). Statistical analysis and graphing were performed by using GraphPad Prism 9.0, employing the independent t test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (****P < 0.0001, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05, ns = nonsignificant).

Results

Intronic rescue targets for exon 9 skipping in the adjacent AS region were predicted by BDGP

Among the CUL3-related pathogenic variants (20 total variants, Human Gene Mutation Database and ClinVar; up to August 2022), almost all variants lead to exon 9 skipping except the deletion variant (CUL3Δ474–477) [34]. The majority of the variants (sixteen) were point variants and were exclusively located in exon 9 and its flanking introns (Fig. 1 and Table S5) [30, 35–38]. As noted in our previous report, exon 9 has a strong donor splice site (DS, 1.00) but has a relatively weak acceptor splice site (AS, 0.51) that might contribute to the propensity for exon 9 skipping [31]. Moreover, evidence has shown that pathogenic variants around the AS further weaken or even abolish the splice site. These findings suggest that enhancing the strength of the splice site to restore exon inclusion may represent a potential therapeutic method for treating Gordon syndrome, especially patients with AS-related pathogenic variants. Given this, we first performed a stepwise single nucleotide substitution in the adjacent intron region (from c.1207-3 to c.1207-10, intron 8) of the AS. For example, if the site was A, it would be changed to G, C, or T. Then, the new sequence was estimated using BDGP. Through this series of single nucleotide substitutions, a total of 24 predicted outcomes, with the original AS still intact, were acquired. Notably, the nucleotide substitutions of A(-9)T and A(-10)T could increase the AS score from 0.51 to 0.91, suggesting an improved splicing strength of the AS (Table 1). The double nucleotide substitution c.1207–9 A > T & c.1207–10 A > T (AA (-9, -10) TT) was also predicted, and the AS score reached 0.98, indicating a stronger splicing effect.

We subsequently tested the effects of nucleotide substitution on AS by introducing A (-9)T, A(-10)T and AA(-9, -10)TT into the pathogenic variant. A total of 15 point variants were analysed, with the exception of c.1207-1G > A, which is located at the key site of the AS. As shown in Fig. 1; Table 2, we found that the three nucleotide substitutions had significant effects on the AS. After the introduction of A(-9)T or A(-10)T, almost all the BDGP scores improved to different degrees (0.66–0.94 and 0.68–0.94, respectively). Notably, the introduction of the double nucleotide substitution AA(-9, -10)TT led to a maximum increase in all BDGP scores, reaching up to 0.81–0.98, indicating great rescue potential.

The nucleotide substitutions A(-9)T, A(-10)T, and AA(-9, -10)TT effectively rescue exon 9 skipping at the minigene level

Among the aforementioned fifteen point variants of the CUL3 gene, nine point variants were located around the AS region, and six point variants were adjacent to the DS. Although the three nucleotide substitutions A(-9)T, A(-10)T, and AA(-9, -10)TT improved the AS scores of all these fifteen point variants, we noticed that the nine AS-related point variants possessed weaker AS scores. In contrast, the six DS-related point variants had normal AS scores that were the same as those of the WT (0.51) (Table 2).

Next, to validate the effects of the candidate nucleotide substitutions A(-9)T, A(-10)T and AA(-9, -10)TT on exon 9 skipping induced by different variants, in vitro minigene splicing assays were performed as described above. We first focused on nine AS-related point variants (A15G, G30A, A32G, T(-28)G, A(-26)G, T(-12)G, T(-6)G, T(-5)A, and C(-3)T), the minigenes of which were constructed on the basis of the pSPL3-CUL3-WT minigene [30, 31]. As such, rescue minigenes containing the candidate site (A(-9)T, A(-10)T, or AA(-9, -10)TT) were generated on the basis of the wild-type and mutant minigenes (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 2A and B, the three rescue sites had no obvious effect on exon 9 splicing in the WT group. However, for the mutants, A(-9)T and A(-10)T showed good abilities to rescue exon 9 exclusion. As expected, AA(-9, -10)TT had a significant restoring effect. The result of minigene splicing was confirmed using Sanger sequencing (Figure S1).

In 293T cells, A(-9)T could better rescue exon 9 skipping caused by six point variants, with the exceptions of T(-28)G, A(-26)G and T(-5)A. The average exon skipping rate decreased from 87.97% (75.33%-95.24%) to 39.73%, and the exon skipping rate of A15G was reduced to 0.00% to achieve full recovery. In HeLa cells, A(-9)T successfully restored normal splicing for all tested variants, with the average percentage decreasing from 89.84% (78.24%-93.71%) to 50.19%, and exon 9 skipping caused by A15G was still fully restored. A(-10)T effectively restored exon 9 inclusion caused by 7 point variants except T(-6)G and T(-5)A in 293T cells, decreasing the average skipping percentage to 20.02% and achieving complete recovery for the A15G and T(-12)G variants. A(-10)T also successfully restored splicing for all variants except T(-5)A in HeLa cells, with an average percentage of 16.62%, and A15G was still completely restored. Satisfactorily, AA(-9, -10)TT demonstrated the most effective restoration for both 293T and HeLa cells, significantly reducing skipping for all variants, with mean skipping percentages of 9.77% and 2.85%, respectively.

We subsequently tested the effect of the preferred candidate site AA(-9, -10)TT on exon 9 skipping induced by six DS-related point variants involving c.A1376G, c. G1377A, c.G1377C, c.G(1377 + 1)C, c.T(1377 + 2)C, and c.A(1377 + 3)G). AA(-9, -10)TT had no restorative effect on exon 9 skipping caused by these variants, except for a slight restoration observed in Group A (1377 + 3)G (Figure S2), implying the importance of the distance effect for the cis-elements.

The RNA sequence containing AA(-9, -10)UU exhibited a stronger affinity for U2AF2 compared to WT RNA

We wanted to further elucidate the underlying mechanism by which the AA(-9, -10)TT nucleotide substitution rescues exon 9 skipping. Based on analysis of the sequence of the AS region, we hypothesized that the substitution AA(-9, -10)TT enhances exon inclusion through exerting an effect on the surrounding Py-tract in intron 8 to function. Previous studies have shown that the Py-tract is an important cis-element for normal splicing of pre-mRNAs. At the initial stage of splicing, each cis-element is recognized by its corresponding trans-acting factor (Fig. 3A), and the Py-tract is recognized and bound by U2AF2, a conserved splicing-associated factor [5, 17, 39]. AA(-9, -10)TT substitution could extend the local Py-tract region, leading to the hypothesis that it may enhance the binding affinity between the Py-tract and U2AF2. Molecular docking predictions were then performed using AlphaFold 3 between wild-type RNA (WT-RNA) and U2AF2, as well as mutant RNA of AA(-9, -10)UU (Mut-RNA) and U2AF2 (Fig. 3B). The results indicated that both WT-RNA and Mut-RNA bind to U2AF2, and more intermolecular bonds were noted between Mut-RNA and U2AF2, forming a tighter spatial structure and suggesting a stronger affinity (Figure S3 A, B). Consistently, DeepClip predicted that AA(–9, − 10)UU substitution increased U2AF2 binding, with the score rising from 0.97 to 1.00 (Figure S4).

Next, the binding affinity of WT or Mut with U2AF2 was validated using EMSA. Two fluorescence-labelled RNA probes that ensured that the Py-tract was located in the primary loop portion of the stem loop were used in the experiment (Fig. 3C) [32, 33]. The U2AF2 protein was prepared and purified using a eukaryotic expression system and validated by WB (Fig. 3D). As shown in Fig. 3E and F, the EMSA results indicated that both probes could bind to U2AF2, and Mut-RNA showed a stronger binding affinity to U2AF2 than WT-RNA did, as expected.

Restoring exon 9 inclusion in endogenous cell lines through enhanced U2AF2 binding by AA(-9, -10)TT and inhibited hnRNP A1 binding by A18G

To further verify the restorative effects and mechanisms of AA(-9, -10)TT on exon 9 skipping, in vivo experiments were performed in endogenous point mutant cell lines generated via HDR-CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Moreover, another previously identified candidate site, A18G, was also explored in this study. Considering the technical restrictions of CRISPR/Cas9 and the feasibility of site mutants, three endogenous cell lines, with site mutants G30A, G30A & AA(-9, -10)TT (referred to as G30A/(-9, -10)) or G30A & A18G (G30A/A18G), were generated and verified using Sanger sequencing (Fig. 4A and B). G30A is a known pathogenic point variant that causes absolute exon 9 skipping and can be restored with an A18G nucleotide substitution by abolishing hnRNP A1 inhibition, as confirmed in our previous study [31]. G30A is an ideal model site and was used for in vivo experiments. As expected, the splicing analysis results of the G30A, G30A/(-9, -10) and G30A/A18G cell lines revealed that G30A indeed led to exon 9 skipping in vivo, as noted based on the 507 bp band in Fig. 4C. Interestingly, AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G restored 100% normal exon 9 splicing, as shown by the 678 bp band in Fig. 4C. All the corresponding splicing products were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure S5).

Next, to further investigate the rescue mechanism of AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G in vivo, RIP assays were performed to verify our in vitro findings (Fig. 4A), and both U2AF2 and hnRNP A1 were successfully immunoprecipitated (Fig. 4D ad). The RIP results of U2AF2, showed that U2AF2 enriched more pre-mRNAs of CUL3 in G30A/(-9, -10) cells than in G30A cells (Fig. 4D bc), confirming that AA(-9, -10)TT promoted U2AF2 binding. The RIP results of hnRNP A1 shown that the splicing inhibitor hnRNP A1 enriched more CUL3 pre-mRNAs in the G30A group than in the WT group (Fig. 4D ef), indicating that G30A enhanced hnRNP A1 binding. Moreover, hnRNP A1 was enriched with less CUL3 pre-mRNA in the G30A/A18G group than in the G30A group, suggesting that A18G weakened the splicing inhibition of hnRNP A1 (Fig. 4D ef).

Discussion

With the development of high-throughput sequencing technology, a growing number of aberrant splicing events of pathogenic genes were revealed to contribute to a diversity of diseases [17, 21, 22]. Targeting the abnormal splicing, many therapy strategies have been developed in recent years, such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), engineered snRNP complexes, and small molecule drugs [22, 40–42]. However, these drugs do not work permanently, require multiple doses, and are often expensive [43, 44]. With the development of gene editing technology, gene editing therapy has shown broad application prospects in many fields such as genetic diseases and tumors [28, 45–47]. While, in most cases, this therapy was confined to correcting a single specific variant, and cannot be widely applicated in the majority of patient groups. Thus, by targeting the abnormal splicing mechanism of specific diseases, developing innovative strategies that benefit most patients is an effective method to further improve and expand the application of gene therapy in associated diseases.

Gordon syndrome, also known as pseudoaldosteronism type II (PHAII) or familial hyperkalemic hypertension (FHH), is a rare Mendelian disorder with variable onset, ranging from birth to adulthood. It is classically characterized by high systolic blood pressure, hyperkalaemia, and hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis [30, 48]. To date, no effective preventive measures or targeted treatments are available, only conservative treatments for symptomatic management, most of which require lifelong medication. In addition, the prognosis depends on blood pressure levels. This disease is associated with pathogenic mutations in the WNK1, WNK4, CUL3, or KLHL3 genes, with the most severe phenotype caused by autosomal dominant mutations in CUL3 [29, 35, 49]. Notably, no missense or nonsense mutation in exon 9 has been found to cause this disease, and exon 9 skipping is the primary pathogenic form of CUL3-related Gordon syndrome, leading to the loss of 57 essential amino acids in the functional domain of CUL3 [31, 35]. Therefore, identifying universal targets that can restore splicing is an innovative therapeutic strategy for treating the pathogenesis, with significant potential for clinical application.

Although our previous study identified the synonymous mutation A18G in exon 9 as a potential candidate target, considering that A18G is located in the exon region, our current focus shifted to seek potential sites that could enhance weak classical AS (0.51, BDGP score) in the conserved sequence of intron 8 [31]. A total of twenty-four single nucleotide substitutions at eight sites (from c.1207-3 to c.1207-10) were analysed using BDGP. Three forms of nucleotide substitutions—A(-9)T, A(-10)T and AA(-9, -10)TT—were identified as candidate sites to dramatically improve the BDGP score of the original AS without altering its position. In the subsequent minigene assays performed in 293T and HeLa cells, both A(-9)T and A(-10)T effectively rescued exon 9 skipping caused by nine AS-related CUL3 pathogenic variants, resulting in rescue rates of 6/9 and 7/9, respectively. Satisfactorily, AA (-9, -10)TT had a greater effect on restoring exon 9 inclusion than did A(-9)T or A(-10)T alone, with rescue rates of 9/9, suggesting that AA (-9, -10)TT would be a better candidate site for further exploration.

To validate the effects of the candidate sites AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G in vivo, we used HDR-mediated CRISPR/Cas9 technology to construct mutant endogenous cell models [50–52]. We selected the pathogenic variant G30A as a model site for further exploration in vivo according to previous in vitro results [31]. G30A mutant cells were successfully generated and confirmed to cause exon 9 skipping in vivo. Two cell lines containing the candidate sites AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G were subsequently constructed in G30A mutant cells, and these two candidate sites indeed restored exon 9 splicing in mutant cells. However, endogenous validation was not conducted for all AS-related CUL3 pathogenic variants in this study, and only G30A was used as a model site to verify our hypothesis for several reasons. First, this site is located just within the PAM (TGG), the best ideal site for HDR-CRISPR/Cas9, avoiding repeated cutting. Second, this site is located within the appropriate distance to A18G and AA(-9, -10)TT for effective HDR. Third, like the other tested variants, it is located in the conserved region near the weak acceptor splice site, leads to exon 9 skipping, and exhibits similar splicing-disruptive and splicing-rescue mechanisms. Although HDR-mediated CRISPR/Cas9 is a powerful tool for gene editing, the technical restrictions, such as appropriate PAM selection, the feasibility of site mutation and the efficiency of HDR, limit the application of this technology. With the rapid development of high-precision base editing, scientists could achieve almost any specific base editing for different purposes [53–56]. These new editing technologies provide more choices for further verification of the effectiveness of the candidate sites AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G at more sites.

The strong splicing recovery effect of the candidate site AA(-9, -10)TT led us to explore its rescue mechanism. We noticed that sites − 9 and − 10 of intron 8 are located precisely in the Py-tract, and the sequence of AA(-9, -10)TT was altered from UCUUAAUUUU to UCUUUUUUUU after transcription. The Py-tract is essential for the recognition of the 3’ splice site and efficient utilization of the branch point. It is recognized and bound by the associated factor U2AF2 at the initial stage of splicing [5]. As a special functional sequence, the Py-tract is mainly composed of C and U nucleotides, and its composition and length can affect its combination with U2AF2, which is crucial for the assembly of the spliceosome. The strength of the cis-element Py-tract is determined by the number of consecutive Us present, with a weak Py-tract containing four consecutive Us and a strong Py-tract containing nine or more consecutive Us [57, 58]. The difference in binding affinity between RNA substrates and U2AF2 is partially attributed to the different conformations of the RNA recognition motif (RRM) domains of U2AF2, which possesses two RRM domains and adopts a closed “inactive” conformation in the presence of a weak Py-tract or an open “active” conformation in the presence of a strong U-rich Py-tract. The tandem RRM domains of U2AF2 not only serve as binding scaffolds but also play an active role in splice site recognition and spliceosome assembly [5, 57, 59]. Therefore, we hypothesized that AA(-9, -10)TT could extend the Py-tract to increase U2AF2 binding activity and promote exon 9 splicing, which is consistent with the findings of a study in which the C was mutated to T in the Py-tract to restore downstream exon inclusion [60]. The following EMSA and RIP experiments confirmed that the binding affinity of the RNA sequence with the candidate site (AA(-9, -10)TT) to U2AF2 was indeed enhanced by increasing the length of the Py-tract. Moreover, the distance between the branch point and the Py-tract is crucial for effective branch formation [5]. Our nucleotide substitution strategy decreased the distance from the Py-tract to the branch point by extending the Py-tract upstream, which might also promote branch point activity to increase exon splicing.

In addition, to further confirm the in vivo rescue mechanism of A18G that we previously investigated [31], an endogenous cell-based RIP assay was performed. The results revealed that the G30A variant enhanced hnRNP A1 binding to inhibit exon 9 splicing and that A18G weakened binding by interfering with cis-elements to counteract splicing inhibition and restore normal splicing. Thus, the rescue mechanisms of the two candidate sites, AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G, have been comprehensively elucidated (Fig. 5). The splicing of vulnerable exons with weak splice sites depends on the balance between splicing regulatory elements like hnRNPA1 binding silencers and the Py-tract strength, with optimization of the Py-tract capable of compensating for splicing mutations [61, 62].

The pathogenic mutation can cause exon 9 skipping. AA(-9, -10)TT enhances binding affinity to U2AF2 to promote exon 9 splicing (a), and A18G inhibits hnRNP A1 binding affinity to restore exon 9 inclusion (b)

In the presence of the pathogenic variant, whether normal exon 9 splicing caused by our candidate sites (AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G) could result in the restoration of the functional protein is a question worth exploring. The recovery of intact exon 9 was achieved only by enhancing the activity of the AS through the intronic nucleotide substitution AA(-9, -10)TT or counteracting the splicing inhibition caused by pathogenic variants through the exonic synonymous substitution A18G. The two candidate sites retained the original splice site and could not activate cryptic splice sites to generate new splicing products. In addition, six variants are located in the intron that is not translated, and two of the three variants located in exon 9 (A15G, p.Glu407Glu and G30A, p.Leu412Leu) are synonymous variants. Thus, the restoration of exon 9 splicing would not result in any amino acid changes. Although the missense variant A32G would lead to Asp to Gly at position 413 after restoring exon 9 splicing, it was much better than the 57 amino acid deletion caused by exon 9 skipping. Taken together, our study revealed that the Py-tract could be used as a potential intronic target to promote exon inclusion, especially for the pathogenic mechanism with a weak AS. Additionally, introducing a synonymous mutation in the exon is a potential universal rescue strategy to regulate splicing by affecting regulatory elements (ESE and ESS). Notably, editing intronic elements is better and safer than editing exonic elements [63]. With the development of gene-editing technology, it is possible to precisely modify conserved cis-elements, providing opportunities for long-acting and personalized treatment strategies.

Despite verifying the effect of the two potential sites for treating CUL3-related Gordon syndrome in endogenous cell models. A further validation needs to be addressed in animal models. Additionally, how the findings could be translated into specific therapeutic approaches is still a challenge for us, hence, future research should aim to develop specific therapeutic modalities such as splice-switching antisense oligonucleotides, gene editing, or other approaches to leverage the identified sites for clinical translation.

Conclusions

In summary, our study identified two novel potential sites (AA(-9, -10)TT and A18G) and proposed promising universal strategies targeting cis-elements to regulate splicing for treating Gordon syndrome, which will provide an important basis for the development of new strategies for splicing-related diseases in the future.
