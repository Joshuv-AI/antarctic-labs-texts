The problem with “borrowed” anatomy faculty: why medical anatomy isn’t enough for dental education
Authors: Joe Iwanaga, Norio Kitagawa, R. Shane Tubbs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As dental schools increasingly share anatomy curricula with medical programs, a growing mismatch is emerging between what dental students need and receive. While head and neck anatomy is essential for all health professionals, dental students require significantly more detailed, region-specific anatomical knowledge, especially regarding the oral cavity, neurovascular structures, and surgical landmarks. Yet, this level of instruction is often delivered by medical school faculty unfamiliar with the unique clinical demands of dentistry. This commentary highlights the limitations of the “borrowed” faculty model and argues for dedicated, dental-aware anatomy educators actively engaged in anatomical research to ensure competent, confident clinical performance by future dentists.

Introduction

As an oral surgeon and anatomist teaching in a medical and dental schools primarily US and Japan, the first author of this article has observed firsthand how anatomy instruction, though fundamental to all health professions, diverges drastically in its application between medicine and dentistry. Increasingly, dental schools integrate their basic science curriculum with medical programs, particularly in the first or second year (US, Japan, and Korea). While this shared model has administrative and curricular benefits, it presents a critical flaw: the assumption that medical-level head and neck anatomy is sufficient for dental education. This approach often leads to a situation where anatomy faculty from the medical school are “borrowed” to teach dental students despite having little to no experience with the depth and specificity required in anatomy in dentistry [1-3]. A growing educational mismatch risks leaving future dentists underprepared for clinical practice.

Review

The myth of equivalence: head and neck≠oral anatomy

One of the most common assumptions in integrated curricula is that “head and neck anatomy” is synonymous with “oral anatomy.” It is not [1]. Medical students are typically taught the broad features of the skull, cranial nerves, neck triangles, and airway structures. Dental students, however, require microanatomical precision, such as fascial spaces and their surgical implications, neurovascular pathways essential for nerve blocks and implant surgery, muscular insertions and fiber directions relevant to flap design, and variations in foramina and canals that influence anesthesia or pathology.

Just as medical students are taught to locate the internal jugular vein for central venous catheter insertion, dental students must learn the anatomy of the mentalis and buccinator muscles for proper complete denture impression. This level of nuance is neither required nor commonly covered in standard medical anatomy courses [3]. When anatomy instruction is unified across programs, dental students are expected to absorb clinical-level head and neck anatomy from instructors who may not fully appreciate its clinical relevance in dentistry [4, 5].

The limits of “borrowed” faculty

Borrowed faculty—PhD anatomists or MDs teaching for both medicine and dentistry—often excel in their discipline but are rarely trained in the clinical applications of dental anatomy. This leads to several issues:

1. Incomplete emphasis: key areas such as the pterygomandibular space, mandibular canal variations, and the anterior superior alveolar canal are often neglected or superficially addressed [1, 2].

2. Lack of clinical relevance: without context, students memorize structures but fail to connect them to procedures like local anesthesia, extractions, or surgical flaps.

3. Assessment mismatch: dental students are often tested on medical-style questions that do not reflect the practical knowledge needed for dentistry.

4. Student frustration: dental students frequently express anxiety over gaps in their anatomical knowledge, particularly when entering clinical training in oral surgery, periodontics, or prosthodontics [6]. This challenge may be mitigated when anatomy is taught by faculty with direct expertise in dental practice or who has been specifically trained for teaching dental related anatomy to the dental students [7]. Just as a medical student aspiring to become a neurosurgeon may feel encouraged and understood when taught by a neurosurgeon, dental students are more likely to feel supported and inspired when taught by faculty with relevant dental clinical experience.

The case for dental-trained anatomists

Despite the presence of skilled educators and experienced clinicians, much of what dental students and dentists need to understand about oral anatomy has only recently been uncovered through focused anatomical research within dentistry itself. While traditional anatomy faculty and many current surgeons offer valuable instruction, the emerging anatomical reality—such as the detailed soft tissue composition of oral mucosa or the spatial relationships of neurovascular branches—is still largely unknown or underappreciated in mainstream curricula [8, 9]. Periodontists, for instance, rarely address the tissue-level structures underlying the mucosa, though these are critical to grafting, flap design, and implant placement [10, 11]. As a result, the most accurate and clinically useful anatomy is increasingly defined not by textbooks but by the latest anatomical research conducted within dental science.

Recommendations for curriculum leaders

1. Appoint dental-trained anatomy faculty: oral surgeons, periodontists, or anatomists with dental surgical backgrounds should actively participate in gross anatomy teaching and curriculum design.

2. Use hybrid teaching models: continue collaboration with medical faculty for general systems, but ensure head and neck instruction is led by or co-taught with dental experts.

3. Incorporate procedure-based learning: anatomy labs should include simulated injections, surgical landmarks, and dissection-based case discussions rooted in dentistry.

4. Establish independent learning outcomes: dental students should be assessed on gross structure and functional and clinical anatomy related to their scope of practice.

5. Enhance procedural and clinical relevance through integration: the call for more procedural and clinically oriented anatomical teaching is compelling. However, because anatomy education typically occurs before formal clinical training begins, this gap can hinder students from fully appreciating its relevance. To bridge this disconnect, dental schools should implement strategies such as early clinical exposure, integration with simulation-based learning, and case-based discussions that contextualize anatomical knowledge. These approaches can help students connect anatomical structures to practical procedures earlier in their training, thereby reinforcing both comprehension and long-term retention.

Conclusion

Dental students require anatomical instruction that surpasses what is typically delivered in medical education. Relying solely on borrowed faculty—however skilled in general anatomy—risks under-preparing future dentists for procedures that demand precise, context-rich anatomical knowledge. A robust dental anatomy curriculum must be taught by those who understand both the science and the surgery. Naturally, there are many anatomy faculty members, though not specialized in dentistry, who are capable of effectively teaching dental students. However, in many cases, such faculty have reached that level through individual effort and dedication. This should not be left to personal initiative alone; the issue must be addressed at a systemic level. Realigning teaching roles with professional relevance and drawing on active dental anatomy research can restore depth, confidence, and clinical competence in the next generation of dentists.
