The protective effects of naringin against cisplatin-induced pulmonary toxicity in rats: Insights into the modulation of ER stress and SIRT1/Nrf2 pathway
Authors: Nihal Turkmen Alemdar, Selim Demir, Esin Yulug, Nadire Sevdenur Erdogan, Elif Ayazoglu Demir, Ahmet Mentese, Yuksel Aliyazicioglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCisplatin (CIS) is a chemotherapeutic agent which is frequently utilised in the treatment of ovarian, breast, testicular and lung cancer. Nevertheless, the potential for severe and irreversible toxicity to the lungs restricts its utilisation. Naringin (NRG), a flavanone glycoside found in significant quantities in citrus fruits, has been demonstrated to exert protective effects against oxidative tissue damage. The present study aimed to assess the protective effect of NRG against CIS-induced pulmonary toxicity.MethodsThe administration of NRG (50 and 100 mg/kg) was conducted orally over a period of seven days. CIS (7.5 mg/kg, IP) was administered on day 2 to rats, and lung samples were collected on day 8.ResultsThe CIS application induced lipid peroxidation (LPO), inflammation, and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) in the lung, resulting in the emergence of histopathological findings. However, the administration of NRG was found to be capable of preventing lung injury by suppressing LPO, inflammation and ERS levels in a dose-dependent manner. Furthermore, the suppression of CIS-induced sirtuin1(SIRT1)/nuclear factor-erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) signaling was abolished by NRG supplementation.ConclusionsThe present study has shown, for the first time, that NRG has a potential for protection against lung injury by modulating SIRT1/Nrf2/HO-1 signalling in the context of CIS-induced lung injury.Keywords: Cisplatin, ER stress, inflammation, lung toxicity, naringin, Nrf2, SIRT1

Background

Cisplatin (CIS) is a chemotherapeutic drug widely used to treat ovarian, breast, testicular and lung cancer [1]. CIS enters cells by a variety of mechanisms, including diffusion, facilitated diffusion mediated by cation carriers, and endocytosis, and is transported to the cell nucleus where it exerts its cytotoxic effects [2]. CIS exerts cytotoxic effects by inducing apoptosis with increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), while it arrests the cancer cell cycle by forming cross-links through intercalation [3]. Despite its recognised efficacy as a cancer drug, it is associated with significant toxic effects on kidney, lung, and heart tissues [1]. As is the case with other chemotherapeutic agents, the use of CIS produces selective cytotoxic effects on cancer cells, while it can also have toxic effects on healthy lung cells. Pulmonary toxicity due to CIS is an uncommon occurrence. Nevertheless, the condition is a grave pathology with the potential to have catastrophic consequences [2]. Although the aetiopathogenesis of CIS-associated pulmonotoxicity is not fully known, it has been documented that increased oxidative stress (OS) and inflammation damage pulmonary epithelial cells and blood vessels, leading to progressive damage from deoxygenation to fibrosis [2, 3]. Lung fibrosis is a fatal disease in which the lungs become scarred and stiff, resulting in permanent shortness of breath and death within 5 years of diagnosis. Unfortunately, no medications are available to reverse pulmonary fibrosis, and the only option for patients are lung transplantation [1]. CIS toxicity is a complex process initiated primarily by OS and inflammation and involves a number of cellular signals [2]. CIS induce OS through an increase in ROS production and depletion of glutathione (GSH) and other antioxidant enzymes. Unneutralised ROS can damage lipids, leading to lipid peroxidation (LPO), enzyme inactivation and DNA damage [3]. OS associated with CIS also activates the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, a master regulator of inflammatory processes, leading to the induction of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), and myeloperoxidase (MPO), burst as a result of leukocyte infiltration [4]. Consequently, CIS-induced subacute OS and inflammation results in activation of traditional apoptosis pathways and cell death [3]. Over the past few years, it has been established that CIS toxicity leads to endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) by reducing the protein folding capacity of ER, and in this case cell death occurs via ER-associated apoptosis [4].

Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a NAD+-dependent deacetylase found in almost all mammalian tissues, has been identified as an important metabolic sensor [5]. SIRT1 has been demonstrated to fulfil a pivotal function in the modulation of a broad spectrum of physiological processes, encompassing metabolic rate, OS, inflammation, and DNA repair [6]. The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory processes controlled by nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) have been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in preventing degenerative cell damage [7]. SIRT1 and Nrf2 have been demonstrated to act as positive regulators of each other, thereby increasing the resistance of cells to changing physiological conditions [8]. Recent reports have emphasised the significance of repressed SIRT1/Nrf2 signalling in the context of CIS-induced tissue toxicity [9–11]. Therefore, the investigation of the protective capacity of natural antioxidants, such as polyphenols, which may exhibit antioxidant and SIRT1/Nrf2 modulator properties, against the toxicity of CIS has been one of the most popular areas of research in recent years [2, 12].

Citrus fruits are among the most frequently cultivated natural products on the world, consumed both as a fresh fruit and in the form of fresh fruit juice, as well as being used in the production of other food products [7]. Citrus fruits are natural products rich in flavonoids, and naringin (NRG) is one of the flavanone glycoside found most abundantly in citrus fruits [13]. Notably, it is distinguished by its multifaceted properties, which encompass anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, antioxidant, and ulcerative effects [14]. The NOAEL value of 6 months of oral administration of NRG to rats has been determined to be greater than 1250 mg/kg/day [13]. This finding effectively eliminates any biosafety concerns, thus facilitating its extensive utilisation in both the pharmaceutical and nutraceutical sectors, owing to its favourable effects on health [7]. The antioxidant properties of NRG primarily result from its capacity to chelate metal ions, impeding the Fenton reaction and thereby enhancing the activities of antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) [15]. Furthermore, recent studies have indicated that NRG’s capacity to modulate SIRT1/Nrf2 signaling may also be implicated in the molecular mechanism of its tissue-protective activity [13, 16]. The protective effects of NRG against D-galactose [16], lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [17] and bleomycin [18] induced lung injuries have been demonstrated in previous experimental reports. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, no study has yet investigated the protective effect of NRG against CIS-associated pulmonotoxicity. This study aimed to investigate the effects of NRG on OS, inflammation and ERS in a CIS-induced lung toxicity model, while also revealing the role of the SIRT1/Nrf2 axis.

Methods

Drugs, chemicals and kits

The CIS, NRG, carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) tablet, thiobarbituric acid (TBA), sulphuric acid, formaldehyde, ethanol, xylene, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) solution were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The total oxidant status (TOS) kit was procured from Rel Assay Diagnostics (Gaziantep, Türkiye). All enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits employed for biochemical quantification were obtained from Fine Biotech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and the measurement process was undertaken in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Animals and treatments

The present study was conducted using 30 female Sprague-Dawley rats (180–190 g) obtained from the Surgical Application Center of Karadeniz Technical University. The rats were housed in the same centre under standard conditions free of pathogens (12 h light-dark cycle and 24 ± 2 °C). Following a period of acclimatisation spanning seven days, the rats were randomly divided into five groups: Normal control, NRG control (100 mg/kg), CIS, CIS + NRG treatment (50 and 100 mg/kg) groups. CIS and NRG were prepared by dissolving in physiological saline [19] and 0.5% CMC [20], respectively. On the second day of the experiment, all groups containing CIS were injected with CIS (7.5 mg/kg), while the groups not containing CIS were injected with physiological saline. The dose of 7.5 mg/kg of CIS was previously determined using experimental models of CIS-induced organ toxicity with a minimum experimental duration of 5 days [19, 21]. The administration of NRG was conducted via gavage to all groups containing NRG, while 0.5% CMC was administered via gavage to groups devoid of NRG. The doses of NRG were selected on the basis of previous reports of its beneficial biological activity of oral NRG in experimental models of acrylamide-induced renal toxicity and paclitaxel-induced reproductive toxicity [22, 23]. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Karadeniz Technical University (Protocol number: 2024/40), and all methods used in this study were implemented in strict accordance with the guidelines set out in the ARRIVE guidelines. All possible measures were taken to minimise the suffering of the animals and the total number of animals utilised. The rats were observed daily for toxic symptoms, mortality, or any abnormal findings. On the morning of the eighth day of the experiment, euthanasia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of overdose of sodium pentobarbital (140 mg/kg) and lung tissues were rapidly extracted for further experimentation [24]. Animals that exhibited no signs of respiration and response to systemic stimuli were designated as deceased [25].

Histopathological analysis

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 48 h and then embedded in paraffin blocks after follow-up procedures. Five-micrometers-thick sections taken from the blocks were stained with H&E, examined, photographed under a microscope by a researcher unaware of the groups, and scored. Subsequently, the photographs were re-evaluated by a second researcher who was unaware of the groups and the semi-quantitative scores. The results were presented by means of calculation of the mean of both scores [26, 27]. Five different areas of each specimen were scanned and scored for the following parameters: alveolar epithelial cell damage, haemorrhage, alveolar wall thickening, inflammatory cell infiltration, and interstitial edema. Previously defined damage scores were used, ranging from 0 (none) to 4 (severe and widespread pathology) [28, 29].

Tissue sampling

The lung tissues were thoroughly washed with ice-cold PBS to ensure the removal of any extraneous contaminants. Subsequently, the tissues were then homogenised in a PBS buffer (10% w/v; pH: 7.4) employing a homogeniser. Following the acquisition of homogenates, centrifugation was initiated at 1800 g, at a temperature of 4 °C, for a period of 15 min. Subsequently, the protein content present within the collected supernants was determined by means of the bicinchoninic acid method [30]. Subsequently, the supernatants were utilised for biochemical analysis.

Analysis of pulmonary oxidant indicators

The levels of tissue LPO were determined through the measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA) using a manual spectrophotometric method that had been developed previously [31]. In summary, 1 mL of the supernatant mixed with TBA and sulphuric acid, following which the mixture was heated at 100 °C for 1 h. Subsequent to the cooling period, the samples were subjected to a centrifugation process at 1800 g for a duration of 10 min. The spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, CA, USA) was utilised to measure the absorbances of the samples at a wavelength of 532 nm. Levels of LPO in the tissues were expressed as nmol MDA/mg protein [32]. Levels of TOS present within lung tissues were detected by means of a colorimetric kit (Cat no: RL0024, Gaziantep, Türkiye) in accordance with the kit instructions.

Analysis of pulmonary antioxidant indicators

The GSH level in the lung tissues was determined by means of an Ellman reagent-based manual colorimetric kit (Cat no: EU2640, Fine Biotech Co. Ltd., Wuhan, China) [33]. In summary, 100 µL of the supernatant and Ellman’s reagent were thoroughly amalgamated and the mixture was left to incubate at 25 °C for 5 min. Subsequent to this, a spectrophotometer was utilised to ascertain the optical density of the sample at 412 nm. GSH concentrations in tissue samples were expressed as µmol/mg protein.

The levels of rat lung tissue SOD (Cat no: ER0332), GPx (Cat no: ER0274), SIRT-1 (Cat no: ER1338), Nrf2 (Cat no: ER0666) and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1, Cat no: ER1041) were measured using anti-rat ELISA kits according to manufacturer’s instructions (Fine Biotech Co. Ltd., Wuhan, China).

Analysis of pulmonary inflammation indicators

The levels of rat lung tissue NF-κB p65 (Cat no: ER1187), IL-6 (Cat no: ER0042) and MPO (Cat no: ER0142) were measured using anti-rat ELISA kits according to manufacturer’s instructions (Fine Biotech Co. Ltd., Wuhan, China).

Analysis of pulmonary ERS and apoptosis indicators

The levels of rat lung tissue binding immunoglobulin protein (BIP, Cat no: ER0562), activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6, Cat no: ER1645), growth arrest and DNA damage inducible gene 153 (GADD153, Cat no: ER0694) and cleaved caspase-3 (CASP3, Cat no: ER0143) were measured using anti-rat ELISA kits according to manufacturer’s instructions (Fine Biotech Co. Ltd., Wuhan, China).

The following steps were taken in order to perform a classical ELISA: Initially, a series of standard solutions were obtained by serial dilution of the standard sample provided by the manufacturer. Subsequently, equal volumes of standards and samples were added to a 96-well microplate that had been coated with the primary antibody. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Upon completion of this period, the contents of the plate were removed and a biotin-labelled antibody solution was added. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Once this period had concluded, the contents of the plate were removed and a streptavidin-conjugated HRP solution was added to each well. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for half an hour. Subsequent to the termination of the designated period, the contents of the plate were extracted, the TMB substrate solution was introduced, and the mixture was subjected to incubation at 37 °C for a duration of half an hour. Subsequent to the termination of the designated period, the stop solution was added to each well, and the absorbances of the wells were measured at 450 nm. The quantity of analyte present in the samples was calculated by constructing a standard curve by plotting the absorbance values against the concentration of the standards [34].

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as the mean ± SEM (n = 6) and statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0. Data were analyzed using one way ANOVA followed by post hoc Tukey test. Values ​​of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect of NRG on OS and antioxidant system parameters

While total oxidant and LPO levels in lung tissue were evaluated by TOS and MDA, respectively, the antioxidant system was appraised using reduced GSH, SOD and GPx as markers. As demonstrated in Fig. 1, there was a significant increase in TOS (~ 3.5-fold) and MDA (~ 5.6-fold) levels in the lung tissue of rats administered only CIS. This was accompanied by a dramatic depletion in GSH (~ 4.0-fold), SOD (~ 5.2-fold), and GPx (~ 4.6-fold) levels. However, the administration of NRG supplements, particularly in high dose, exerted a substantial regenerative effect on the antioxidant system within the lung tissue, resulting in the suppression of LPO and TOS levels.

Effect of NRG on SIRT1, Nrf2 and HO-1 levels

In rat lung tissue, levels of SIRT1 (~ 7.4-fold), Nrf2 (~ 3.2-fold) and HO-1 (~ 3.4-fold) decreased significantly only following administration of CIS (Fig. 2). Conversely, NRG supplementation resulted in a substantial augmentation of CIS-induced suppression of SIRT1 (~ 5.7-fold), Nrf2 (~ 2.7-fold) and HO-1 levels (~ 3.3-fold), particularly at the high dose.

Effect of NRG on inflammatory parameters

As demonstrated in Fig. 3, the administration of CIS led to a significant augmentation in the levels of NF-κB p65 (~ 3.5-fold), IL-6 (~ 3.8-fold) and MPO (~ 3.1-fold) in the pulmonary tissue of rats, culminating in a substantial inflammatory response. However, the supplementation of NRG was found to have a significant effect on the suppression of inflammatory biomarker levels [NF-κB p65 (~ 3.8-fold); IL-6 (~ 4.5-fold); and MPO (~ 2.9-fold)], which was found to be dose-dependent, with a greater effect observed at the high dose.

Effect of NRG on ERS and apoptosis parameters

The levels of ERS and apoptosis in lung tissue were evaluated using parameters of BIP, ATF6, GADD153 and CASP3. As demonstrated in Fig. 4, treatment of rats with CIS led to significant upregulation of BIP (~ 6.3-fold), ATF6 (~ 6.5-fold), GADD153 (~ 4.1-fold) and CASP3 (~ 7.0-fold). Nevertheless, the supplementation of the NRG was determined to be efficacious in ameliorating the levels of ERS and apoptosis within the lungs, with a more pronounced effect evident at the high-dose administration setting.

Effect of NRG on histopathological findings

The Fig. 5; Table 1 present histological images and semi-quantitative damage scores of lung tissue from the experimental groups, respectively. The lung tissues of the control and NRG (100 mg/kg) groups exhibited normal lung histological architecture, characterised by a fairly regular alveolar structure (Fig. 5A and B). However, severe damage to the alveolar epithelial cells, alveolar wall thickening and haemorrhage, as well as moderate inflammatory cell infiltration and interstitial edema, were observed in some places in the lung tissues (Fig. 5C) of the CIS group (as shown by higher damage scores in Table 1). Although there was decreased cell damage to the alveolar epithelium in the CIS + NRG (50 mg/kg) group compared to the CIS group, there was still mild to moderate alveolar wall thickening, haemorrhage and interstitial edema (Fig. 5D). In the CIS + NRG (100 mg/kg) group, lung architecture approaching normal was observed, despite the presence of occasional mild interstitial edema and alveolar wall thickening (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

The NRG is a flavanone glycoside that is particularly abundant in citrus fruits [13], and its pulmonary protective effect has been demonstrated in various experimental injury models in previous studies [16–18]. However, the potential protective effects of NRG against CIS-induced lung injury remain unclear. This study is the first to focus on NRG’s protective effects against CIS related lung injury. In this study, it was shown that the protective effects of NRG against lung damage caused by CIS are due to the reduction of OS, inflammation, ERS, and cell death. In addition, our research highlights that regulating the SIRT1/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway plays a role in this protective effect of NRG in the lungs.

The present study revealed that CIS administration resulted in increased signs of haemorrhage, inflammatory cell infiltration, edema, alveolar wall thickening and epithelial cell damage. These findings were consistent with previous reports identifying the destructive effects of CIS on lung tissue [26, 35]. However, the administration of NRG supplements was found to significantly improve the impairment to lung architecture induced by CIS, with the high-dose supplementation proving more effective. Consistent with our findings, Turgut et al. reported that NRG may exhibit lung-protective effects by improving the infiltration of inflammatory cells and alveolar wall thickening in a bleomycin-induced experimental lung toxicity model [18].

The mechanism of CIS-induced pulmonotoxicity is complex. It is known that ROS, which are increased by CIS, cause severe OS by increasing LPO formation [3]. Conversely, SIRT1 and Nrf2 function as sensors of OS and metabolism, and work in a reciprocal regulatory relationship to modulate each other’s activities. Consequently, they supervise the regulation of proteins that protect cells against oxidative and inflammatory damage [8]. It has been demonstrated that, over time, excessive ROS from CIS ultimately results in the inhibition of the SIRT1/Nrf2 axis, thereby rendering cells vulnerable to OS and inflammation [9, 10]. Consistent with the existing literature, the present study found that the administration of CIS led to the inhibition of SIRT1/Nrf2 signalling in the lungs of rats, an increase in LPO and TOS levels, and a weakening of the antioxidant defence system. In contrast, the application of NRG, particularly in high the dose, has been demonstrated to have a counteractive effect on OS. This is evidenced by the restoration of antioxidant defence components and the decrease in LPO and TOS levels. The observed effect can be attributed to the previously demonstrated metal-chelating properties of NRG, which have been shown to inhibit the Fenton reaction and exhibit radical-scavenging activities [15]. Furthermore, NRG treatment restored the suppressed levels of SIRT1, Nrf2 and HO-1 in the CIS group, which can be explained at least in part by its ability to modulate the SIRT1/Nrf2 signalling pathway and exert lung-protective effects. This hypothesis is in line with earlier reports that NRG can have a protective effect by regulating SIRT1/Nrf2 signaling in models of tissue injury caused by D-galactose [16] and acetaminophen [36].

Inflammation represents a pivotal component in the sequence of events that ultimately results in tissue damage in cases of CIS [3]. As is well documented, NF-κB signalling is activated in response to CIS-derived ROS, which leads to a rapid increase in cytokine levels, such as IL-6 [4]. As a secondary consequence, inflammatory cell migration to the damaged tissue accelerates, causing a burst of MPO [37]. Consistent with this, our results showed that CIS administration causes widespread inflammation in lung tissue, as indicated by high levels of NF-κB p65, IL-6 and MPO. Conversely, the inflammation induced by CIS was found to be significantly reduced in rats treated with NRG, as evidenced by reduced levels of NF-κB, IL-6, and MPO. It is well known that the SIRT1/Nrf2 axis regulates the activity of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory genes [8]. Furthermore, SIRT1/Nrf2 signalling is one of the most important mechanisms that inhibit NF-κB [6]. Higher SIRT1/Nrf2 levels in the NRG treatment groups than in the CIS group may also contribute to NRG’s anti-inflammatory activity. Consistent with our findings, it has previously been reported that NRG exhibits anti-inflammatory activity by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway in experimental models [38, 39].

The decline in the protein folding capacity of the ER in conditions, such as calcium imbalance, OS and low ATP levels is referred to as ERS [40]. Recent reports have demonstrated that CIS upregulates a number of ERS biomarkers, activates CASP3, and promotes apoptosis [4]. Increased ERS subsequently triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR) in cells. The UPR is a series of processes, including the activation of ER-associated degredation (ERAD) to remove misfolded proteins and the increase in chaperone synthesis, that aim to either correctly fold unfolded proteins in the ER or eliminate them in the short and medium term [41]. The accumulation of numerous misfolded proteins in the ER promotes the dissociation of the chaperone BIP from ATF6 [4]. Once activated in the Golgi, the released ATF6 migrates to the nucleus, where it induces the expression of genes, such as GADD153. This alleviates ERS in the acute phase and directs apoptosis in the chronic phase [40]. In line with this, our results showed that the administration of CIS induced ERS biomarkers and CASP3 levels in lung tissue. By contrast, NRG supplements were found to significantly improve CIS-induced ERS and apoptosis levels, with the high dose proving to be more effective. It is known that the SIRT1/Nrf2 axis inhibits cellular apoptosis in response to ERS by increasing the expression of ERAD components [42, 43]. This was confirmed by higher SIRT1/Nrf2 levels and lower ERS levels in the NRG groups. Consistent with our findings, a previous study demonstrated that NRG could reduce ERS and apoptosis by decreasing BIP and GADD153 levels in a model of bleomycin-induced pulmonary toxicity [44].

The present study is not without its limitations. Firstly, the study utilised the conventional NRG and CIS doses that have been widely documented in the extant literature. Subsequent to a 7-day treatment period, biochemical and histological experiments were conducted. It is recommended that future studies concentrate on the testing of NRG and CIS at varying doses and in different forms, and that long-term results be evaluated. Secondly, the research team’s available laboratory and financial resources allowed for biochemical measurements to be performed using spectrophotometric and ELISA-based methods. In future studies, the cytoplasmic (and, in some cases, nuclear) levels of these and the other proteins involved in cell signalling should also be evaluated using detailed molecular methods, such as Western blot, RT-PCR, and immunohistochemistry. Thirdly, in this study, lung tissues of rats were evaluated using biochemical and histological methods. In subsequent studies, lung function assessment tests such as spirometry should be incorporated into experimental designs to evaluate the protective efficacy of NRG from a physiological perspective.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that oral administration of NRG may provide protection against CIS-induced lung toxicity. The prevailing evidence suggests that CIS administration primarily causes lung damage by inducing OS, inflammation, ERS, and apoptosis. It is hypothesised that NRG mitigates this tissue damage through its in vivo antioxidant activity and by modulating SIRT1/Nrf2 signalling. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the potential of NRG as a therapeutic agent against chemotherapeutically induced lung injury, emphasising the necessity for further optimisation to ensure its clinical usability.
