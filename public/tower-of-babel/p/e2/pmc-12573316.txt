Effect of a portable therapeutic baby nest designed for neonates on physiological parameters, comfort and pain levels during venous blood sampling: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Aylin Arıkan, Figen Işık Esenay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Turkey, paediatric emergency departments often lack the specialised environments required for invasive procedures on neonates. Given their developmental vulnerability, it is crucial to use interventions that reduce discomfort and support physiological stability. This study introduces the portable therapeutic baby nest (PTBN), designed to provide developmentally supportive care aligned with the principles of the Newborn Individualized Developmental Care and Assessment Program.MethodsThis two-phase study included 80 term neonates (40 per group). First, the PTBN was developed from February to April 2022. Second, its effectiveness was evaluated through a randomized controlled trial conducted from May to September 2022. The experimental group underwent venous blood sampling in the PTBN, and the control group on a standard stretcher. All procedures were video-recorded and assessed by two blinded nurses using the Physiological Parameter Monitoring Form, COMFORTneo and Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS).ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar across groups (p>0.05). During and after sampling, the PTBN group had significantly lower heart and respiratory rates, higher oxygen saturation and lower NIPS and COMFORTneo scores compared with controls (p<0.05).ConclusionThe PTBN improved physiological stability, comfort and pain outcomes during venous sampling in neonates.Trial registration numberNCT05442619.Keywords: emergency nursing, Randomized Controlled Trial, pediatrics

Introduction

The neonatal period is marked by heightened sensitivity to environmental factors and increased susceptibility to infections.1 Common conditions like jaundice, respiratory distress and vomiting frequently require visits to paediatric emergency departments (EDs).2 However, ED environments often intensify neonatal distress due to bright lights, loud sounds and unfamiliar stimuli, particularly during invasive procedures such as venous sampling, injections and catheterisation.3 4 These interventions trigger physiological, behavioural and metabolic responses, including increased heart rate, blood pressure and cortisol levels, alongside visible discomfort.5 Prolonged stress exposure may alter pain perception and stress regulation, leading to long-term neurological and behavioural consequences.6 7

In response to these challenges, Heidelise Als8 developed the Neonatal Individualized Developmental Care and Assessment Program, which aims to reduce stress and promote comfort by aligning care with neonates’ developmental needs. According to this framework, optimal sensory conditions include sound levels ≤45 dBA during the day and ≤35 dBA at night,9 and lighting between 10 and 600 lux.10 However, in Turkey, and likely many departments worldwide, structural and operational constraints prevent paediatric EDs from meeting these standards. This situation suggests the need for practical and cost-effective strategies to improve neonatal care in EDs—without requiring major infrastructural modifications.

Several non-pharmacological interventions, including oral sucrose, non-nutritive sucking, swaddling, facilitated tucking, kangaroo care and music therapy, have been shown to reduce neonatal pain and improve physiological stability.11 A systematic review by Shen et al confirmed their effectiveness.11 However, their integration into paediatric EDs is often limited by factors such as infrastructure constraints, staff training requirements and varying clinical practices. While interventions like oral sucrose and swaddling primarily focus on pain relief, they provide limited support in managing environmental stressors that contribute to neonatal distress.

We developed a portable therapeutic baby nest (PTBN) for neonates undergoing venous blood sampling. Given that neonatal distress is influenced by both procedural pain and sensory factors, PTBN was designed to provide multidimensional support in the ED setting, minimising sensory overload and promoting postural support. It is a portable lightweight, disinfectable structure which features a single-use sterile roll cover for hygiene, cotton-filled support pads that maintain a fetal position and passive noise-reducing headphones to shield neonates from excessive environmental noise. A semi-open shade reduces bright light exposure while ensuring visibility for clinical procedures.

We designed a randomised controlled trial (RCT) to test the benefits of the PTBN. The study hypotheses are as follows: compared to the control group, neonates in the PTBN group will exhibit (H11) lower respiratory rates, (H12) lower heart rates, (H13) higher oxygen saturation levels, (H14) lower pain levels and (H15) higher comfort levels.

Methods

Study design

This study comprised two phases: (1) the development of the PTBN and (2) a single-blind RCT evaluating its effects on physiological parameters, comfort and pain during venous blood sampling.

The portable therapeutic baby nest

The PTBN was developed between 1 February and 30 April 2022.

Design phase: The PTBN was designed by the researcher and advisor with input from five paediatricians and five neonatal nurses. The goal was to shield neonates from excessive light and noise while supporting optimal posture. Dimensions were determined based on neonatal anthropometry, and a technical drawing by an animation designer was reviewed for functionality and comfort.

Material selection: Hygiene, durability and neonatal safety were prioritised. The outer layer is 100% cotton canvas, and the inner lining is soft, breathable cotton. Additional components include a semi-open shade for light reduction, polar headphones for noise reduction and support rolls for fetal positioning.

Production phase: The PTBN was made foldable and portable with carrying handles and an integrated mattress for comfort. It can be stored in a compact bag for easy reuse after disinfection.

Standardisation: Light and noise reduction were measured hourly over 3 days using a lux and decibel metre using a light and sound standardisation form. External light levels of 1044 lux and 294 lux were reduced to 71 lux and 24 lux inside the PTBN. External noise levels of 105 dB and 57 dB were lowered to 45 dB and 25 dB. These findings confirmed the PTBN significantly reduced sensory exposure (p<0.05), aligning with neonatal care standards.

Preliminary application: A pilot study with four neonates assessed feasibility. Physiological parameters, pain and comfort were evaluated. The PTBN group had significantly lower Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS) scores and higher COMFORTneo scores (p<0.001) compared with the control group. Additionally, oxygen saturation was higher, while respiratory and heart rates were lower, indicating greater physiological stability. No modifications were needed after pilot testing. Evaluation of the PTBN

The RCT phase was conducted from 1 May to 30 September 2022, in the blood sampling unit of a paediatric ED in Turkey. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05442619).

Participants

We included neonates aged 37–42 weeks gestational age, ≤28 days old and weighing 2500–4000 g. who had venous blood work ordered by their treating ED physician.We excluded neonates who had received analgesics within 4 hours before the procedure, had congenital anomalies, respiratory distress, clinical instability, suspected/confirmed COVID-19, vision/hearing impairments or a history of hospitalisation.

Neonates were withdrawn from the study by parental request, if removed from the PTBN during the procedure, or after unsuccessful blood sampling on the first attempt.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using G*Power V.3.1 software based on mean NIPS scores reported by Pekyiğit and Açıkgöz12 during venous blood sampling in term neonates. Assuming an effect size (d) of 0.80, a statistical power of 94% (β=5.8%) at α=0.05 was achieved, resulting in a required sample of 80 neonates (40 per group).

Randomisation and allocation

To ensure balanced group sizes and control for confounding factors such as gender13 and prior exposure to painful procedures,7 block randomisation was used. Blocks were structured based on these variables and repeated ten times. Sealed envelopes were prepared in advance and placed in designated bags according to gender and procedural exposure (eg, female exposed: ‘FE’ bag, male not exposed: ‘MNE’ bag). Parents randomly selected a sealed envelope, the contents of which were not visible to them, thereby preventing researcher influence on group assignment. Allocation (eg, FEE (Female-Expose-Experimental), MNEC (Male-Not Expose-Control), FNEE (Female–Not Expose–Experimental), MEE (Male–Expose–Experimental)) was revealed only after the envelope was opened.

Although block randomisation follows a structured sequence, selection bias was eliminated, as parents—not researchers—selected the envelopes, ensuring that the final allocation within each block remained unknown at the time of recruitment. This process maintained unpredictability and ensured rigorous methodological integrity.

Data collection materials

To evaluate PTBN effectiveness, a smartwatch stopwatch recorded respiratory rates, a hospital-calibrated pulse oximeter measured saturation and heart rates and a compact camera with a tripod recorded the venous sampling procedure.

Study procedures

With the exception of the person performing the venous blood sampling, all neonates received standard clinical care at the discretion of the treating physician.

Venous blood sampling was performed by a researcher with 13 years of clinical experience on designated duty days to maintain procedural consistency. Blood sampling was conducted from the cephalic or basilic veins using a 20 G sterile needle after antiseptic skin preparation, following standard protocols.

Preprocedure: The researcher introduced themselves to the parents, explained the study and obtained written informed consent. Neonatal demographic and clinical data were recorded using the Introductory Information Form, and parents randomly selected a sealed envelope for group allocation. The blood sampling room was prepared according to institutional guidelines.

Control group: Neonates underwent routine blood sampling on a hospital trolley-bed without additional interventions. Video recording commenced, with clothing intact except for the left foot, where a pulse oximeter was placed. Parents were informed that neonates would remain in position for 2 min postprocedure for recovery assessment before recording ceased.

Control group: Neonates underwent routine blood sampling on a hospital trolley-bed without additional interventions. Video recording commenced, with clothing intact except for the left foot, where a pulse oximeter was placed. Parents were informed that neonates would remain in position for 2 min postprocedure for recovery assessment before recording ceased.

Experimental group: The PTBN was placed on the trolley-bed with a sterile roll sheet, and parents positioned the neonate inside. Support rolls ensured a flexed posture, while polar headphones and a semi-open shade minimised auditory and visual stimuli. A pulse oximeter was attached, and video recording commenced. The procedure followed the same protocol as in the control group. Neonates remained in the PTBN for 2 min postprocedure for monitoring before recording ceased. The PTBN was disinfected for reuse (figure 2).

Postprocedure: Video recordings were independently evaluated by two neonatal nurses blinded to the study’s objectives, methodology and group allocations. To maintain assessment integrity, recordings were intentionally framed to focus solely on the neonate’s face and upper body, thereby excluding overt procedural markers and minimising the likelihood of revealing group allocation. The nurses, who routinely use NIPS and COMFORTneo, independently assessed pain and comfort at three standardised time points: 2 min before, during and 2 min after the procedure. No additional training was required, but to ensure consistency and inter-rater reliability, they were provided with a standardised scoring guideline.

Data analysis

Sample size was calculated using G*Power V.3.1 software. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.24. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means and SD. Normality was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since parametric assumptions were met, independent t-tests and χ² tests were used for between-group comparisons.

A one-way mixed-design analysis of variance was conducted to evaluate the main effects of group (intervention vs control), time (2 min before, during and 2 min after the procedure) and the group×time interaction. The group effect tested whether overall outcome measures (eg, physiological parameters, pain and comfort) differed between the two groups. The time effect assessed whether there were changes across the three time points regardless of group. The group×time interaction tested whether the pattern of change over time differed between the groups.

In addition, within-group comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise tests to evaluate time-related changes within each group. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Comparison of defining characteristics of neonates by groups

The study included 80 neonates (50% male, 50% female). All participants completed the study as planned, with no exclusions or withdrawals after randomisation, ensuring full adherence to the study protocol. Participant flow is illustrated in the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) diagram (figure 3).18 Neonates’ median postnatal ages were 13 days in the experimental group and 12 days in the control group; median gestational age was 39 weeks in both groups. Most neonates had no prior exposure to non-pharmacological interventions (table 1). The mean duration of venous blood sampling was 371.45±6.35 s in the experimental group and 384.05±8.02 s in the control group, with no significant difference (p>0.05).

CONSORT flow diagram of the study. CONSORT, Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials.

Comparison of the mean physiological parameters of neonates

Baseline vital signs, recorded 2 min before the procedure, were similar in the two groups. During and 2 min after the procedure, the control group had higher respiratory and heart rates and lower oxygen saturation (p<0.05). Within-group comparisons using Bonferroni-adjusted tests revealed significant changes over time. In the experimental group, respiratory and heart rates significantly decreased during and after the procedure (p=0.001), while in the control group, these rates increased (p<0.05). Oxygen saturation remained unchanged in the experimental group (p>0.05) but decreased significantly in the control group (p=0.001) (table 2).

Discussion

Venous blood sampling, a routine yet painful procedure in paediatric EDs, often triggers physiological and behavioural stress responses in neonates.19 Given that EDs have limited ability to provide developmentally supportive environments, efforts to reduce procedural distress are essential. This randomised study demonstrated the PTBN’s ability to stabilising physiological parameters, enhance comfort and reduce pain during venous blood sampling compared with the usual blood sampling procedure.

Neonates exhibit physiological responses to painful interventions, including increased respiratory and heart rates and decreased oxygen saturation, influenced by biopsychosocial factors.5 These parameters serve as key indicators of pain levels.20 Baseline data were collected to assess changes over time within and between groups. However, during and 2 min postprocedure, the experimental group had lower respiratory and heart rates and higher oxygen saturation than controls, supporting hypotheses H11, H12 and H13. Sarhangi et al20 reported improved physiological stability in neonates exposed to maternal heartbeat sounds during arterial sampling. In contrast, Avcin and Küçükoğlu21 found no significant heart rate changes with breastfeeding and kangaroo care during heel sticks, possibly due to baseline physiological differences and methodological variations. These findings suggest that Individualized Supportive Developmental Care (ISDC)-supported blood sampling enhances physiological stability.

Exposure to bright light and noise can heighten neonatal pain sensitivity,22 while inadequate ISDC practices may exacerbate distress.12 This study demonstrated that PTBN effectively reduced pain, supporting hypothesis H14. No prior study has evaluated a compact intervention like PTBN that simultaneously minimises environmental stimuli and optimises postural support for term neonates. However, Shykhveisi et al22 found that combining eye protection and earplugs during venous sampling in preterm neonates was more effective in pain reduction than either method alone. Similarly, Pekyiğit and Açıkgöz12 reported that white noise combined with a flexed position provided greater relief than either approach independently. Consistent with these findings, our results suggest that reducing sensory stimuli while ensuring proper positioning significantly alleviates neonatal pain during blood sampling.

During the procedure, the PTBN group exhibited significantly lower NIPS scores, with a 4-point difference from the control group, indicating a meaningful reduction in procedural pain. As the procedure ended and pain stimuli ceased, distress levels naturally declined in both groups, reducing the NIPS score difference to one point after 2 min. This decline underscores the transient nature of procedural pain while highlighting PTBN’s effectiveness in alleviating pain during its peak intensity.

This study also found that PTBN significantly improved neonatal comfort during venous blood sampling, supporting hypothesis H15. No previous research has evaluated a compact ISDC-based intervention like PTBN for this purpose. However, existing studies suggest similar benefits. Çiftçi and Yayan23 found fetal positioning effective for comfort during venous access in preterm neonates, while Kahraman et al6 reported that white noise, maternal voice and MiniMuff increased comfort during heel sticks. Additionally, Alemdar24 demonstrated that incubator covers improved comfort. These findings highlight the potential of ISDC interventions to enhance neonatal well-being by minimising environmental stressors during invasive procedures.

Strengths and limitations

Sociodemographic characteristics were comparable between groups, minimising bias. Since factors such as gender,13 gestational age, health status and previous pain experiences7 influence pain perception, ensuring group comparability was critical. Similarly, vital signs, comfort and NIPS scores were similar at baseline between the groups. Parents chose the envelope which mitigates against selection bias even with sealed envelopes. Although block randomisation theoretically allows for the prediction of the final participant’s allocation, each envelope was randomly selected by parents who were unaware of its contents, and participants were admitted to the ED at different times. This procedure effectively eliminated the risk of selection bias. In addition, the RCT design conducted in a paediatric ED, the use of a context-specific and easily applicable intervention, and the evaluation of outcomes using validated instruments by experienced neonatal nurses enhanced the methodological rigour and clinical relevance of the study.

This study has several limitations. Although outcome assessors were blinded, they may have indirectly inferred group allocation from contextual cues in the video recordings. While the camera was positioned to capture only the neonates’ faces and upper bodies, certain visual details—such as the presence of support pads—may have revealed group assignment. Nevertheless, pain and comfort were assessed using structured and validated scales by experienced neonatal nurses, minimising the risk of subjective interpretation. Additionally, the number of blood samples collected per neonate was not standardised, which may have led to slight variations in procedure duration.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that PTBN is an effective and practical solution for improving neonatal care during invasive procedures in paediatric EDs. Its design allows for practical use by nurses, shielding neonates from excessive noise and light while enhancing comfort through therapeutic touch and postural support. By effectively reducing pain and stabilising physiological parameters, PTBN offers a significant advancement in neonatal pain management and procedural care, providing a feasible and impactful approach for fast-paced clinical environments.
