The impacts of chronic infections on shaping cellular senescence
Authors: Ashley Johnson, Troy Rought, Jacob Aronov, Princess Pokharel, Angelina Chiu, Azadeh Nasuhidehnavi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCellular senescence is a fundamental biological process characterized by stable cell cycle arrest, genomic instability, and the acquisition of a proinflammatory secretory phenotype. While senescence is traditionally associated with aging, growing evidence reveals that chronic infections such as viral, bacterial, and protozoan parasites can serve as powerful inducers of senescence, contributing to premature aging and long-term tissue damage. This review explores the diverse mechanisms by which persistent pathogens trigger or sustain senescence in host cells. We highlight how these chronic infections manipulate host DNA repair, mitochondrial dynamics, telomere maintenance, oxidative stress, and immune function to promote senescence and immunosenescence. Emerging findings also reveal how pathogens hijack the host cellular machinery to induce senescence across various tissue types. In many cases, senescence not only enables pathogen persistence but also drives pathological outcomes such as fibrosis, neurodegeneration, cardiomyopathy, and immune exhaustion. Collectively, this emerging evidence highlights a unifying strategy among diverse pathogens: the exploitation of cellular senescence to support chronic infection and promote disease. Understanding how infectious agents drive senescence offers new insights into age-related pathologies and highlights potential therapeutic targets, such as senolytic and senomorphic agents, to mitigate the long-term impacts of chronic infections.Keywords: Senescence, Chronic infection, Viral infection, Bacterial infection, Protozoan parasites

Introduction

Senescence is a cellular biological process that induces stable cell cycle arrest along with specific characteristics, including chromatin remodeling, mitochondrial dysfunction and the secretion of a complex proinflammatory secretome [1]. One of the most significant consequences of senescence is its contribution to chronic inflammation, particularly through a secretory phenotype that sustains tissue-damaging inflammatory responses chronologically. Interestingly, during persistent infections, we face a similar inflammatory state in which the purpose is to fight pathogens; however, the immune response to pathogens can become dysregulated, characterized by continues activation of immune cells, excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and failure to return to immune homeostasis. Over time, this unresolved inflammation contributes to chronic, low-grade inflammatory state leading to tissue damage and development of age-related and inflammatory diseases [2, 3]. Emerging research increasingly supports a connection between chronic infections and accelerated aging. These effects may be driven by both the prolonged immune response of the host and direct mechanisms employed by the pathogens themselves. Here, we discuss how chronic infectious diseases are linked to the acceleration of aging-related hallmarks, shedding light on the molecular and cellular pathways through which persistent pathogens contribute to premature aging and age-associated decline.

Emerging hallmarks of infection-driven senescence

Cellular senescence is a fundamental stress response that serves as a protective mechanism against uncontrolled cell proliferation. It contributes to tissue repair and regeneration and helps maintain overall tissue homeostasis [1]. Conversely, senescence is linked to the accumulation of molecular and cellular damage that, over time, contributes to physiological deterioration, increased risk of age-related diseases, and eventual organismal death [4]. Emerging evidence now suggests that chronic infections may act as accelerants of aging, contributing to its progression through immunosenescence and tissue stress. While cellular responses to pathogens are vital for antimicrobial defense, they can also act as triggers for cellular senescence under certain conditions. This senescence, which is initially protective, may become maladaptive when sustained, promoting tissue dysfunction. In the following sections, we highlight key characteristics of senescent cells that are particularly relevant to infectious diseases.

Senescence can be identified by increased activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal), which reflects high lysosomal content in senescent cells [5]. Detection of β-galactosidase activity at pH 6, through biochemical assays, is indicative of cellular aging and is distinct from the acidic β-galactosidase activity observed at pH 4 in non-senescent cells [6]. It is important to note that SA-β-Gal is not an exclusive marker of senescence under all conditions. For example, certain immune cells, such as macrophages, can upregulate SA-β-Gal as part of their normal physiological response to immune stimuli, independent of cellular senescence [7]. Thus, interpretation of this marker requires careful consideration of the biological context.

Senescent cells are also characterized by a state of stable proliferation arrest, in which they become trapped in either the G1 or G2 phase of the cell cycle [8]. This arrest is driven primarily by the activation of tumor suppressor pathways, most notably through the upregulation of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors. Two key molecular pathways are commonly involved: the p53 pathway, which induces the expression of its downstream effector p21, and the p16 pathway, which targets and regulates the retinoblastoma protein [9, 10]. In addition to undergoing cell cycle arrest, senescent cells exhibit characteristic changes in nuclear architecture, including the loss of Lamin B1, a key structural component of the nuclear envelope [11]. This loss facilitates chromatin remodeling and contributes to the formation of senescence-associated heterochromatic foci (SAHFs) [12]. SAHFs are enriched in heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1) and are known to silence genes critical for cell cycle progression, including E2F target genes and other transcriptional activators [13]. These molecular and structural changes reinforce durable cell cycle arrest, effectively preventing the re-entry of senescent cells into the proliferative cycle.

Another well-established marker of cellular senescence is telomere shortening [14]. Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences (TTAGGG) located at the ends of linear chromosomes that serve as protective caps to prevent the loss of essential genetic information and shield chromosomes from being misidentified as damaged DNA [15]. With each round of cell division, telomeres progressively shorten due to the end-replication problem, a limitation of DNA polymerase, which is unable to fully replicate the 3’ ends of linear DNA strands. Thus, during each round of cell division, telomeres progressively shorten. Telomerase helps counteract this loss by adding telomeric repeats, but some shortening still occurs with each replication cycle. In this context, telomeric regions are particularly susceptible to mutations and are inefficiently repaired. Damage in these regions can trigger persistent DNA damage responses, activating cell cycle inhibitors such as p21 and p16, which lead to cellular senescence [15].

The activation of the DNA damage response (DDR) in response to various stimuli, such as telomere attrition and oxidative stress, can further reinforce senescence. The DDR is activated by DNA lesions such as single- and double-strand breaks and aims to maintain genomic integrity. One of the mechanisms of DDR is the phosphorylation of histone H2AX and the formation of γ-H2AX, which helps in the assembly of DNA repair complexes [16]. In cells subjected to persistent DNA damage, particularly senescent cells, γ-H2AX foci can accumulate and remain unresolved [17]. A wide range of pathogens have been shown to induce DNA damage and promote γ-H2AX formation, suggesting a potential role in driving host cell senescence.

A defining feature of senescent cells is the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which is characterized by the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, growth factors and proteases [18, 19]. These inflammatory mediators include interleukins (such as IL-6, IL-8, and IL-1β), interferons (e.g., IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factors (e.g., TNF-α) and chemokines (such as CXCL-8 and CXCL-10) [20]. These inflammatory factors are predominantly derived from two canonical transcription factors: nuclear factor-kB (NF-kB) [21] and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein β (C/EBPβ) [22]. Among these, NF-κB, plays a central role, acting as a master regulator of inflammatory signaling. The NF-κB family includes five related proteins: NF-κB1 (p50), NF-κB2 (p52), RelA (p65), RelB, and c-Rel. These proteins work by forming pairs, either with each other or with themselves, and binding to specific regions of DNA called κB enhancers to turn target genes on. NF-κB can be activated through two main pathways: the canonical and non-canonical pathways. Both play important roles in controlling immune and inflammatory responses, though they work in different ways. In the canonical NF-κB pathway, stimulation activates the IKK complex, which phosphorylates the inhibitor protein IκBα. This leads to IκBα’s degradation, allowing NF-κB proteins to enter the nucleus and activate genes involved in inflammation and immune responses [21].

While SASP factors can play beneficial roles in acute tissue remodeling and immune surveillance, their persistent expression disrupts local tissue architecture, promotes chronic inflammation, and alters the function of neighboring healthy cells [14]. Given the role of senescence in chronic inflammation and age-related pathologies, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating this process are under active investigation. Senomorphics represent a therapeutic strategy for mitigating SASP-mediated inflammation without eliminating senescent cells. These agents suppress the harmful activities of senescent cells by selectively inhibiting key signaling pathways, such as NF-κB, mTOR, JAK/STAT, and p38 MAPK, that drive and sustain deleterious effects [23, 24]. Senomorphics have demonstrated protective effects in age-related pathologies, including kidney injury and ischemia-induced cardiac damage [25, 26]. In addition, approaches such as senolytic drugs (which selectively eliminate senescent cells), epigenetic reprogramming [27]and metabolic modulation [28] offer promising avenues to mitigate the harmful effects of the SASP, restore tissue function, and delay the onset of aging-related decline [29].

Early-phase clinical investigations have provided encouraging evidence for the therapeutic potential of senolytic agents, particularly the combination of dasatinib and quercetin (D + Q), across a range of age-related and inflammatory conditions. In a pilot study involving diabetic kidney disease patients, a brief three-day course of D + Q led to a notable reduction in senescent cell burden in adipose and skin tissues, accompanied by decreased levels of key circulating SASP cytokines such as IL‑1α and IL‑6 [30]. Similarly, in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, intermittent administration of D + Q over three weeks was found to be feasible and well-tolerated, with preliminary evidence suggesting potential improvements in physical function, though larger trials are needed to confirm efficacy [31]. Senolytics are also being considered in acute inflammatory conditions and infections. Planned clinical trials will evaluate the effects of fisetin, a natural flavonoid with senolytic properties, for its potential to mitigate the progression of sepsis in older adults (NCT05758246). Additionally, another study will investigate the use of fisetin to reduce systemic inflammation in patients with COVID-19 (NCT04476953), aiming to reduce disease progression and dampen inflammatory complications. These investigations reflect growing interest in modulating inflammation-driven senescence in the context of infection. While inflammation plays a vital role in host defense, promoting the recruitment of immune cells and clearance of pathogens, chronic or excessive inflammatory signaling may also contribute to the accumulation of senescent cells. Understanding how specific inflammatory mediators trigger or modulate cellular senescence during infections represents an important and evolving area of research.

Cells can undergo senescence in response to mitochondrial damage. Mitochondria are central to both cellular energy production and the regulation of oxidative stress. During oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondria naturally generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which serve signaling roles at low levels but, in excess, can damage proteins, lipids, and DNA [32]. This oxidative stress is a known trigger of cellular senescence [32] and plays a critical role in the onset and progression of many age-related diseases [33]. In the context of infection, a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria, viruses and intracellular parasites, often provoke a burst of ROS as part of the host innate immune defense [34, 35]. While they are initially protective, the long-term accumulation of ROS can harm host tissues, leading to cellular senescence [36].

Significant alterations in the mitochondrial network and structure occur during the senescence process. Mitochondrial health is tightly regulated by dynamic processes such as fission and fusion. Fission, which is controlled primarily by dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), allows damaged mitochondria to be isolated for degradation, whereas fusion, which is mediated by mitofusins (MFN1 and MFN2) and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1), enables mitochondrial networks to join and maintain functional integrity [37, 38]. Mitophagy, the selective autophagic degradation of damaged mitochondria, is also critical for mitochondrial quality control. Upon mitochondrial damage, PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane and recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin, which tags damaged mitochondria for degradation. However, with age, these systems become dysregulated, mitochondrial networks become increasingly fragmented, mitophagy becomes less efficient, and dysfunctional mitochondria accumulate [39]. Recent evidence suggests that severe infections lead to the dysregulation of mitophagy [40]. This decline in mitochondrial quality control impairs cellular metabolism, elevates oxidative stress, and contributes to the onset and progression of cellular senescence and aging [41]. Experimental evidence suggests that improving mitochondrial fusion can restore mitochondrial function and prolong cellular longevity [42]. Therefore, the disruption of mitochondrial dynamics and oxidative stress resulting from infection not only contributes to acute pathogenesis but also accelerates long-term cellular aging processes.

Chronic viral infections and senescence

Viral infections can trigger a wide range of host cellular responses that mirror key hallmarks of cellular senescence. In particular, persistent viral infections have been shown to promote cellular aging through mechanisms such as the induction of cell cycle arrest, accumulation of DNA damage, and sustained secretion of proinflammatory cytokines [43]. These processes not only impair normal cellular function but also contribute to the development of a senescent phenotype. Viruses manipulate host cellular pathways to support their persistence, but as a consequence of this, they induce senescence-associated phenotypes, marked by DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and secretion of proinflammatory SASP factors, which can accelerate tissue degeneration and systemic aging. This persistent senescent state not only facilitates viral survival but also exacerbates age-related comorbidities and immune exhaustion in infected individuals. In this context, we explore how certain viruses, particularly those capable of evading the immune system and establishing chronic infections, can drive senescence in host cells.

Compared with healthy individuals, chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection gradually damages the liver and promotes hepatocellular senescence through the upregulation of key cell cycle inhibitors, including p16, p21, and p27 in patients chronically infected with HCV [44]. Mechanistically it is not clear how chronic HCV infection leads to upregulation of cell cycle inhibitors during chronic infection. An in vitro study using human hepatoma cells showed that HCV core protein can downregulate p16 expression via promoter hypermethylation, mediated by the upregulation of DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) and DNMT3B, ultimately enhancing cell proliferation [45]. In contrast, HCV core protein upregulation of p21 and p27 [45], however the functional consequences of this upregulation, particularly in relation to cellular growth arrest or senescence, have not yet been clearly defined. These findings point out the complexity of HCV infection dynamics with cell cycle regulators and highlights the need for further investigation on mechanistic link between HCV infection and the induction of cellular senescence.

This senescent phenotype is further supported by increased hepatic SA-β-gal activity in approximately 50% of individuals with chronic HCV, indicating widespread liver cell senescence [46]. In addition, elevated levels of DNA damage markers, such as γ-H2AX and phosphorylated HP1γ, are observed in the liver tissues of HCV-infected patients and are particularly localized within fibrotic regions [47]. In addition to hepatocytes, chronic HCV infection also contributes to immunosenescence, as evidenced by telomere shortening in CD4+ T cells, leading to premature T-cell aging [48, 49]. Notably, telomere attrition in T cells is associated with greater degrees of hepatic fibrosis [48]highlighting a potential link between immune cell senescence and fibrotic progression. Collectively, these findings underscore the close association between senescence-related cellular changes and fibrosis in chronic viral hepatitis.

HCV infection also promotes mitochondrial fission and Parkin-mediated mitophagy by increasing the expression and phosphorylation of DRP1, facilitating its translocation to the mitochondria [50, 51]. Mechanistically, the HCV core protein promotes the activity of cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1)/cyclin B complex, which phosphorylates DRP1 at Serine 616 (Ser616) and facilitates its recruitment to the mitochondria membrane [50]. Interestingly, these alterations in mitochondrial dynamics are critical for HCV pathogenesis. Silencing DRP1 or Parkin not only impairs viral propagation but also enhances apoptotic signaling, as indicated by increased cytochrome c release and elevated caspase-3 activity [50, 51]. These studies suggest that HCV actively manipulates the host mitochondrial architecture to support its persistence, a strategy that may simultaneously drive a senescence-like phenotype in infected cells.

Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), which is a life-long infection that can cause severe infection in immunocompromised patients, is another well-documented example of viral infection-induced senescence. In vitro studies have shown that HCMV infection increases SA-β-Gal activity in both primary human fibroblasts and primary renal proximal tubular epithelial cells [52, 53]. HCMV also modulates the cell cycle, as evidenced by the upregulation of p53 and p16 in fibroblasts and the downregulation of the proliferation marker Ki67 in renal epithelial cells [52, 53]. HCMV immediate-early (IE) genes, particularly IE2, are essential components in inducing this senescence phenotype. HCMV-induced senescence arises independently of viral DNA replication, as shown by its persistence in the presence of a polymerase inhibitor and its absence in cells exposed to UV-inactivated virus, indicating that early gene expression alone is sufficient to trigger the senescence program [52].

Moreover, HCMV induces a senescence-associated gene expression profile and enhances the production of the SASP secretome, including the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-8, in infected renal epithelial cells [53]. Interestingly, inhibition of IL-6 signaling with tocilizumab significantly reduced the spread of DNA damage, as indicated by fewer γH2AX-positive cells, and suppressed NF-κB activation, without altering extracellular IL-6 levels [53]. In addition to this autocrine effect, paracrine IL-6 released from HCMV-infected cells impairs cell proliferation and induces senescence in neighboring bystander cells [53]. These senescence-associated changes also extend to the immune system. In individuals with latent HCMV infection, compared with uninfected controls, CD8+ T cells exhibit significant telomere shortening, suggesting accelerated immunosenescence [54]. Together, these findings highlight the multifaceted role of HCMV in driving both tissue- and immune-related senescence.

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) promotes the expression of cellular senescence biomarkers across various cell types through the activity of viral proteins. While current antiretroviral therapy effectively reduces the HIV load and extends life expectancy, this increased longevity is often accompanied by signs of accelerated aging and age-related comorbidities [55]. One key factor contributing to this phenomenon is the HIV transactivator of transcription (Tat) protein. HIV-Tat is an essential regulatory protein that enhances viral transcription and is actively secreted by infected cells into the extracellular environment. Once outside the cell, Tat exerts effects on neighboring cells, including the induction of inflammatory chemokine production [56]. In vitro, Tat expression drives the upregulation of senescence marker expression in several cell types. In CD4+ T-cell lines, Tat induces the expression of canonical senescence markers such as p21, p16, and γ-H2AX [57]. In adipose stem cells, Tat elevates the levels of IL-6, IL-8, p16, and p53 [58]. In primary mouse microglia, Tat increases SA-β-gal activity, promotes the expression of p16 and p21, reduces cell proliferation, and stimulates the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [59]. Similarly, endothelial cells exposed to both Tat and HIV glycoprotein 120 (gp120) exhibit increased SA-β-gal activity, further indicating the induction of senescence [60]. In human CD4+ T cells, HIV-Tat inhibits telomerase activity by reducing the nuclear level of telomerase reverse transcriptase [61]. This impairment may limit the replicative capacity of CD4+ T cells, contributing to immune decline in HIV patients. Thus, HIV-Tat may contribute to the premature aging phenotype observed in HIV-infected individuals by promoting virus-induced cellular senescence.

In addition to promoting senescence through inflammatory and transcriptional changes, HIV disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis, particularly in neurons, contributing further to cellular aging. HIV-1 proteins, including Tat and gp120, promote mitochondrial fragmentation via the upregulation of DRP1 and its translocation to mitochondria in primary human neurons [62]. In addition, these viral proteins increase Parkin-mediated mitophagy, which is characterized by increased PINK1 accumulation, Parkin translocation from the cytosol to the mitochondria, and mitochondrial turnover [62]. Furthermore, in primary rat neurons, Tat increases mitochondrial calcium levels, causing membrane depolarization and activation of the fission protein DRP1, whereas the levels of the fusion protein MFN2 remain unchanged, leading to mitochondrial fragmentation [63]. These disruptions in mitochondrial dynamics and quality control contribute to neuronal dysfunction and may underlie the early onset of cognitive decline observed in aging individuals with HIV. Thus, both viral protein-induced senescence and mitochondrial dysregulation play critical roles in HIV-associated premature aging.

Herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1), like other human herpesviruses, establishes lifelong latency, primarily in the peripheral nervous system. For decades, this persistence was thought to be mostly harmless beyond occasional reactivation. However, a growing body of research has begun to shift that perspective, suggesting a more insidious role for HSV-1, particularly in the brain. HSV-1 DNA has been detected in the brains of individuals with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), as well as in some healthy controls, raising intriguing questions about its potential involvement in neurodegeneration [64, 65]. In vitro studies further elucidated this link, showing that HSV-1 infection of neuronal and glial cells leads to the accumulation and secretion of β-amyloid proteins, one of the pathological hallmarks of AD [66]. Repeated HSV-1 infection modifies the histone acetylation profile in primary neurons, suggesting a role in inducing a senescent-like phenotype [67]. Most recently, evidence from a human brain model revealed that repeated brain injury can reactivate latent HSV-1, triggering not only β-amyloid buildup but also tau phosphorylation, another defining feature of AD pathology [68]. Despite these compelling findings, further in vivo studies are needed to elucidate the mechanism behind repeated HSV-1 infection in the brain and the induction of AD hallmarks.

Summary of the impact of viral, bacterial, and parasitic infections on cellular senescence

Bacterial infection induces cellular senescence

Bacteria are also infectious agents that drive cellular senescence and accelerate aging. Bacteria-induced senescence can have several harmful consequences for host tissues and overall immune function. While senescence initially serves as a protective response, halting the proliferation of damaged or infected cells, bacteria-induced senescence can have numerous detrimental effects on host tissues. This process can be triggered by chronic anti-microbial inflammation, oxidative stress, direct genotoxicity and activating specific pathways of the immune system that maintain the SASP, including Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), NF-kB, p53 and p21 [43]. As accumulation of senescent cells is accompanied by various geriatric disorders [69] bacterial infections may do more than causing acute illness, they may also act as a catalyst for degenerative diseases. Consequently, bacteria-induced senescence contributes to chronic inflammation, impaired tissue repair, and increased susceptibility to secondary infections. Some bacterial pathogens, such as Escherichia coli, Helicobacter pylori, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Borrelia burgdorferi, have been found to stimulate senescence and cause chronic infection [43].

Some strains of Escherichia coli that harbor the genotoxin colibactin, which is encoded by the polyketide synthase (pks+) genomic island, have been shown to induce cellular senescence in host cells. In vitro infection with pks + E. coli causes double-strand DNA damage and subsequently increases γH2AX foci and triggers cell cycle arrest, which is marked by the upregulation of p21 and p16 [43]. These strains also contribute to colorectal cancer pathogenesis by causing DNA double-strand breaks, mutations, and genomic instability, which arrest the cell cycle at the S and G2/M phases [70]. The resulting senescence is associated with the SASP, which is characterized by elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α and IFN-γ that can promote a tumor-supportive microenvironment in colorectal cancer models [70]. Additionally, E. coli-infected senescent cells can undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, where they acquire cancer stem cell-like properties that drive tumor progression and resistance to chemotherapy [71]. Mechanistically, pks + E. coli triggers senescence through ROS production and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, notably without activating caspase-dependent apoptosis in both normal and cancerous colon epithelial cells [70]. These findings indicate that pks + E. coli strains drive senescence, rather than apoptosis, in affected epithelial cells.

Helicobacter pylori, a major cause of chronic gastritis and peptic ulcers, induces persistent cellular senescence primarily in gastrointestinal (GI) epithelial cells, with senescent phenotypes remaining even after successful eradication of the infection [72]. Chronic H. pylori infection promotes telomere shortening in the gastric epithelium, not through direct damage but rather via inflammation-induced ROS production, which causes DNA double-strand breaks [73, 74]. These breaks activate poly (ADP‒ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP-1) and increase alternative nonhomologous end joining, contributing to genomic instability and cellular stress [74]. This environment drives premature senescence, which is further exacerbated by virulence factors such as cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA), which are known to induce redox imbalances and DNA damage [75]. Additionally, H. pylori enhances the expression of long noncoding RNAs that drive the SASP. This process is characterized by increased levels of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and IL-8, and is sustained through prolonged phosphorylation of NF-κB p65, which in turn activates key inflammatory pathways [76]. In addition to affecting the GI tract, H. pylori-induced senescence also affects skin fibroblasts and keratinocytes, potentially contributing to chronic skin conditions such as psoriasis, urticaria, and rosacea [75].

Persistent inflammation during chronic Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection is driven by the sustained production of proinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ, along with eicosanoids and microRNAs [77]. These factors influence host cell death and immune responses, ultimately promoting tissue damage and pathogen persistence [77]. Senescence pathways are induced in part by this persistent inflammatory environment. M. tuberculosis causes extensive DNA hypermethylation and disrupts epigenetic regulatory networks in both humans and guinea pigs, especially in genes that control aging and immune responses [78]. As a result of these alterations, biological aging is accelerated; M. tuberculosis-infected patients exhibit elevated levels of SASP proteins such as TNF-α, CXCL9, and CXCL10, as well as an average increase in the epigenetic age of 12–14 years [78]. Additionally, M. tuberculosis infection is linked to CD4+ T-cell senescence and exhaustion, which is characterized by an increase in the expression of inhibitory receptors such as T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain 3 (TIM-3) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), which further restrict T-cell function as well as a decrease in the production of polyfunctional cytokines [79]. The inability of these faulty T cells to regulate bacterial replication results in a vicious cycle of inflammation, immunological dysfunction, and disease progression. CD4+ T-cell transcriptional analysis during chronic infection revealed enrichment of gene sets linked to cellular stressors such as the p53 pathway, UV damage, and hypoxia [79]. These results highlight how M. tuberculosis-induced immunological dysregulation and inflammation encourage early cellular senescence, which contributes to long-term health decline even after the infection appears to have cleared.

Borrelia burgdorferi, the spirochetes responsible for Lyme disease, profoundly alter host cellular processes, causing persistent symptoms in patients and creating a physiological state reminiscent of inflammaging. One hallmark of this process is accelerated cellular senescence, as evidenced by a significant increase in SA-β-Gal activity in the serum of Lyme disease patients compared with healthy individuals [80]. A recent study revealed additional mechanisms that may contribute to this persistent inflammatory state. The tracking of fluorescently labeled B. burgdorferi peptidoglycan (PG) cell walls revealed that PG can accumulate and remain long-term in the livers of mice, where it is associated with liver dysfunction [81]. This lingering bacterial material was retained in Kupffer cells and hepatocytes and was shown to stimulate PBMCs [81]. These findings suggest that residual bacterial antigens, even in the absence of active infection, may drive chronic immune activation and inflammation. In addition, basic membrane protein A (BmpA), a major virulence factor of B. burgdorferi, can potently activate various host immune cells, which could be a potential mechanism for persistent inflammation. For example, when human microglia are exposed to BmpA, they produce a wide range of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines [82]. These include elevated levels of IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, CCL2, CCL5, CXCL1, CXCL10, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-1Ra, which are molecules that play central roles in inflammation and immune signaling [82]. Importantly, PBMCs from patients with disseminated Lyme disease, when stimulated with B. burgdorferi, produce high levels of IL-1Ra, IL-1β, and IL-6 [85], reinforcing the idea that the immune system remains hyperresponsive long after initial exposure. These findings paint a compelling picture that B. burgdorferi initiates not only an acute immune response but also a chronic, senescence-linked inflammatory state that may underlie the persistent symptoms and complications observed in many Lyme disease patients.

The impacts of protozoan parasitic infections on the senescence process

Protozoan parasites, a group of single-cell organisms that invade other living cells, are master manipulators of their host cell biology. These parasites finely tune their cellular processes to create a favorable environment for their survival and replication. They modulate a wide range of host functions, including cell cycle progression, apoptosis, ROS production, and cellular metabolism [84, 85]. These parasite-driven modifications can contribute to the development of a senescence-like phenotype in host cells. In the following sections, we explore several examples of well-characterized intracellular protozoan parasites known to induce cellular senescence, highlighting the mechanisms they employ and the implications for host‒pathogen interactions.

Toxoplasma gondii, the causative agent of toxoplasmosis, is an obligate intracellular parasite capable of establishing lifelong infections in its mammalian hosts. To thrive within host cells, T. gondii exploits oxidative stress, manipulating cellular pathways to create a favorable niche for its replication. Infection leads to increased production of ROS, which in turn causes DNA double-strand breaks, as evidenced by elevated γH2AX expression in infected HeLa cells [86]. This DNA damage interferes with normal cell cycle progression, arresting host cells predominantly in the S and G2/M phases across a variety of cell types [87–90]. The accumulation of DNA damage and persistent cell cycle arrest ultimately drive host cells into a senescent state, an outcome that provides the parasite with a stable, nonproliferative environment conducive to long-term survival.

T. gondii promotes the G1/S transition and then halts progression to the G2 phase by downregulating cyclin B1, effectively blocking mitotic entry [88]. This strategy supports parasite replication, as G1-arrested cells are less permissive to parasite growth. In immune cells such as primary human neutrophils, T. gondii delays apoptosis by suppressing caspase-8 and caspase-3 activation while upregulating DNA repair pathways and dampening leukocyte activation, thereby extending cell survival and blunting immune responses [86]. More recent studies have demonstrated that T. gondii also induces cellular senescence in gastrointestinal tissues, particularly in the mouse colon [91]. This senescence is characterized by increased oxidative stress, elevated expression of p16 and p21, and reduced Lamin B1, alongside the upregulation of SASP factors such as IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and matrix remodeling enzymes [91]. These inflammatory mediators contribute to chronic tissue inflammation and disrupt colon epithelial integrity [91]. Structural damage to the mucus barrier and decreased expression of tight junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin further illustrate how T. gondii-induced senescence undermines mucosal defense [91]. Interestingly, senolytic treatment reduces cellular senescence and improves colon barrier integrity in T. gondii-infected mice [91]. Together, these findings underscore the role of senescence not only in facilitating chronic infection but also in driving pathological tissue remodeling. Importantly, therapeutic interventions targeting senescent cells, such as senolytic agents, offer a potential strategy to mitigate the long-term consequences of this persistent parasitic disease.

Plasmodium species, the etiological agents of malaria, often cause acute symptoms; however, the infection can persist in the liver and cause a chronic recurrent state [92]. In a controlled human malaria infection study, healthy volunteers were deliberately infected with P. falciparum to investigate the effects of malaria on cellular aging [93]. The infection led to increased telomere attrition and p16 levels along with the downregulation of anti-oxidant genes in peripheral blood cells, which was largely restored by day 64 following the final antimalarial treatment. Infection also triggers an increase in the levels of proinflammatory cytokines; however, while the levels of most of these cytokines decrease after treatment, the levels of certain cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-8, and IL-18, remain modestly elevated [93].

Cerebral malaria (CM) is a life-threatening neuroinflammatory condition increasingly associated with cellular senescence in the brain. Postmortem analyses of CM patients revealed elevated expression of the senescence marker p21, particularly within astrocytes [94]. Additionally, in an experimental mouse model of CM, astrocytes were identified as key contributors to the senescent phenotype, showing increased levels of p21, p53, SA-β-Gal activity, and the proinflammatory chemokine CXCL-10 [96]. Notably, senolytic treatment significantly reduces the burden of senescent astrocytes and decreases CXCL-10 levels in both brain tissue and plasma [94]. Overall, these findings suggest that parasite- and inflammation-driven mechanisms may drive cellular aging.

Trypanosoma cruzi, the causative agent of Chagas disease, establishes a persistent chronic infection that, if left untreated, leads to cardiomyopathy in approximately one-third of infected individuals. One of the emerging mechanisms contributing to this pathology is the acceleration of cellular senescence, particularly in cardiomyocytes. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that T. cruzi infection induces a senescent phenotype in cardiomyocytes characterized by increased SA-β-Gal activity, elevated ROS production, and reduced contractile function [95]. ROS accumulation in cardiomyocytes is correlated with structural and functional damage, including elevated cardiac troponin I levels and impaired contractility [95]. Moreover, T. cruzi-induced oxidative stress causes DNA damage, which is marked by increased DNA breaks and a reduction in S-phase progression in human cardiomyocytes [96]. Similarly, T. cruzi infection suppresses cell proliferation and induces senescence, as evidenced by increased SA-β-Gal activity and activation of senescence-related pathways in fibroblasts [97]. This senescent shift supports parasite survival by reducing host apoptosis and limiting immune-mediated clearance [97, 98]. Together, these findings underscore how T. cruzi exploits oxidative stress and senescence pathways to establish a permissive niche, contributing to the progression of chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy.

Leishmaniasis, caused by chronic infection with Leishmania species, can also present symptoms commonly associated with aging, particularly in immune cells. Leishmaniasis manifests in two major clinical forms: cutaneous leishmaniasis, characterized by localized skin lesions and nodules, and visceral leishmaniasis, a more severe systemic form impacting internal organs such as the liver, spleen, and lymphatic system [99]. Leishmaniasis can trigger chronic immune activation and inflammation, which may contribute to premature immunosenescence. In patients with cutaneous leishmaniasis, skin lesions exhibit an immunosenescence gene signature characterized by the upregulation of key markers such as p21, p16, and p38, as well as altered expression of differentiation-associated genes such as EOMES and T-bet [100]. Importantly, this immunosenescence profile does not correlate with patient age [100]suggesting that the senescence-like immune phenotype is disease-driven rather than age dependent. Furthermore, circulating natural killer (NK) cells in these patients present several senescence-related characteristics, including reduced proliferative capacity and telomere attrition [101]. These immune cells also express elevated levels of CD57 and inhibitory killer cell lectin-like receptor G1 (KLRG1) but lack the costimulatory marker CD27 [101]. These features have previously been defined as a senescent phenotype in immune cells [102, 103]. Notably, the accumulation of senescent NK cells with high cytotoxic potential has been associated with increased skin lesion size [101]. Peripheral T cells from cutaneous leishmaniasis patients exhibit a similar senescent profile characterized by high expression of CD57 and KLRG1, increased levels of the DNA damage marker γH2AX, and shortened telomeres [101]. The accumulation of these senescent T cells has also been correlated with larger skin lesions, suggesting that immune cell senescence may contribute to disease severity and tissue damage [101]. Interestingly, senescence not only occurs as a consequence of Leishmania infection but also may play a contributory role in disease pathogenesis. A recent analysis of skin biopsies from patients with localized cutaneous leishmaniasis revealed that higher senescence scores were associated with an increased risk of disease progression to the more severe mucosal form [104]. These findings suggest that cellular senescence could serve as both a marker and a potential driver of disease severity in leishmaniasis.

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

Emerging evidence suggests that, across a wide range of pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and protozoan parasites, the induction of cellular senescence is not simply a consequence of infection but rather a deliberate strategy used by pathogens to create and maintain a favorable niche within the host. By driving senescence, these pathogens manipulate host cellular functions to support their own survival, replication, and persistence. The resulting senescent phenotype not only aids in immune evasion and chronic infection but is also closely linked to immunosenescence and accelerated biological aging in the host.

It is noteworthy to note that senescence may play a beneficial role in host antimicrobial defense to infections. For example, senescent fibroblasts exhibit resistance to vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) infection and secrete SASP factors that further enhance antiviral defenses in the surrounding environment [105]. Similarly, induction of senescence in the lung has been shown to reduce viral load in VSV-infected mice in the early stage of the infection [105]. These findings support the view that, in certain contexts, senescence may serve as a protective mechanism against specific infections by limiting pathogen replication and enhancing immune activation.

As research in this area has advanced, targeting cellular senescence has emerged as a promising approach to mitigate the long-term pathological consequences of infection. Strategies such as eliminating senescent cells or modulating senescence-related pathways have therapeutic potential to disrupt persistent infections, promote tissue repair, and improve overall health outcomes. However, a critical area that still requires investigation is the long-term impact of pathogen-induced senescence on host health. It remains unclear whether these senescent states are reversible or if they leave lasting damage even after the infection is resolved. A deeper understanding of how infections drive senescence and how this process contributes to disease progression could pave the way for innovative therapies that address not only the infection itself but also the chronic conditions that may follow.
