N-Halosuccinimide enables cascade oxidative trifluorination and halogenative cyclization of tryptamine-derived isocyanides
Authors: Jun-Yunzi Wu, Long-Ling Huang, Jia-Luo Fu, Jia-Yi Li, Shuang Lin, Shuang Yang, Zhi-Shu Huang, Honggen Wang, Qingjiang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoth the pyrroloindoline core and N–CF3 moiety hold significant importance in medicinal chemistry. However, to date, no instances of constructing N–CF3-containing pyrroloindolines have been reported. Herein, we present a robust and operationally simple approach to assembling such intriguing skeletons from tryptamine-derived isocyanides through a cascade sequence, which includes an oxidative trifluorination and a subsequent halogenative cyclization. Key to the success lies in the development of a facile conversion of isocyanides to N–CF3 moiety with commercially available reagents N-halosuccinimide and Et3N·HF. The protocol features mild reaction conditions, broad functional group tolerance, good to excellent yields, and high diastereoselectivities. In addition, we demonstrate that the halide substituent within the products serves as a versatile functional handle for accessing diverse C3-quaternary-substituted N–CF3-pyrroloindolines.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Synthetic chemistry methodology, Reaction mechanisms

Introduction

Pyrroloindolines, especially those quaternary-substituted at the C3 position, have garnered significant attention from the synthetic chemistry community due to their presence in a wide variety of structurally complex natural indole alkaloids and bioactive molecules1–4. Their high conformational rigidity and well-defined spatial arrangement of substituents established them key structural elements in medicinal chemistry. To date, the SciFinder database search5 has revealed that nearly three thousand pyrroloindoline-containing compounds have been investigated for their biological activities (Fig. 1a)6–8. For example, (-)-physostigmine and its analogs have been used as drugs for treating myasthenia gravis and glaucoma and as potential drug candidates for relieving symptoms of Alzheimer’s disease9. The introduction of the trifluoromethyl (CF3) group has been widely utilized to adjust the biophysical properties of small molecules, such as lipophilicity, permeability, and metabolic stability10–13. In contrast to the C–CF3-14–16, O–CF3-17–19, and S–CF3-containing20–23 compounds, which have been well-studied, bioactive molecules featuring N–CF3 moieties24–27 have drawn increasing interest from the medicinal chemistry community in recent years (Fig. 1b)28–30. The lack of efficient methods to prepare N–CF3-containing compounds remains a significant limitation for exploring their bioactivity. For instance, N–CF3-containing pyrroloindolines may hold promise for potential applications in medicinal chemistry. However, no synthesis of such intriguing molecules has been reported.

Tryptamine-derived isocyanide (TDI), a versatile building block with multiple reactive sites, has demonstrated notable efficiency in cascade dearomative spirocyclization reactions. Representatively, the nucleophilic addition of the isocyanide to a suitable electrophile generates a highly electrophilic nitrilium ion species, which is subsequently trapped by the indole C3 position to yield spiroindoline products (Fig. 2a, mode I). In the absence of a C2 substituent, subsequent intra- or intermolecular nucleophilic addition takes place, leading to the formation of more complex spiroindolines. In this context, various hard electrophiles, such as Michael acceptors, imines, and others, have been explored by Ji31,32, Feng33,34, Ruijter35, Liu36, Shi37, and Li and Zheng38. Particularly, Ruijter et al. recently achieved an elegant iodospirocyclization of TDIs mediated by a soft electrophile, N-iodosuccinimide39. Liu40, Ji41, and Ruijter42 independently developed an alternative spirocyclization reaction involving the interaction of an isocyano group with in-situ generated metal carbene or nitrene species, respectively (mode II). Recently, Wang and co-workers achieved an efficient spiroindoline synthesis through a Pd-catalyzed insertion reaction of TDIs into aryl and alkenyl iodides (mode III)43. Very recently, the group of Ji reported a Mn(III)-promoted dearomative spirocyclization of TDIs with aryl boronic acids via a radical tandem process (mode IV)44. It is noteworthy that in all these spirocyclization reactions, only the carbon atom within the isocyano group serves as the reaction site; the involvement of both carbon and nitrogen atoms as reaction sites remains largely underexplored32.

We have been interested in organofluorine synthesis45–48 and very recently reported an efficient method for constructing C–CF3 bonds49. Inspired by the recent advance of N–CF3 chemistry50–59 and previous studies on the halogenation of isocyanides60,61, we hypothesized that the potentially bioactive N–CF3-containing pyrroloindoline framework could be synthesized from TDI through a cascade sequence (Fig. 2b). This sequence begins with an oxidative fluorination of the isocyano group, followed by a halogenative cyclization. In the presence of suitable electrophilic halogenating agents, the electrophilic activation of the isocyanide gives the corresponding halonitrilium ion. When the N1 position of the indole core is protected by an electron-withdrawing substituent, the spirocyclization process may not proceed immediately. Instead, the addition of a fluoride source leads to multiple fluoride additions to the halonitrilium ion, forming an intermediate where the former isocyanide nitrogen atom exhibits nucleophilic character. In addition, the interaction of the electrophilic halogenating agent with the indole core could also induce the formation of an electrophilic halonium intermediate, which could be quenched through the intramolecular nucleophilic addition by the isocyanide nitrogen atom, leading to the desired N–CF3-containing pyrroloindoline product. Notably, this reaction demonstrates the accessibility of the N–CF3 moiety from isocyanides under operationally simple conditions. In a seminal work reported in 1980, Ruppert achieved the synthesis of N–CF3 amines from isocyanides62. However, the use of deleterious fluorine gas and anhydrous hydrogen fluoride limited its widespread synthetic applications. Herein, we disclose that the designed transformation was successfully effected under conditions employing commercially available N-halosuccinimide and Et3N ∙ HF. The protocol proceeds under mild conditions, exhibiting broad functional group tolerance, good to excellent yields and high diastereoselectivity. By leveraging both atoms of the isocyano group as reaction sites, the method provides an efficient route to N–CF3-containing 3-halo-pyrroloindolines in a convenient one-pot manner. In addition, downstream manipulations of the installed halide functionality can readily convert the resulting products to a series of complex C3-quaternary-substituted N–CF3-containing pyrroloindolines.

Results

Reaction optimization

Initially, the cascade oxidative trifluorination and halogenative cyclization of tryptamine-derived isocyanide 1a was explored by evaluating various fluorine sources in the presence of NBS (N-bromosuccinimide, 3.5 equiv) as both the oxidant and halogenating agent in DCM (dichloromethane) at room temperature (Table 1). While the utilization of AgF, previously employed by Schoenebeck50, Wilson51, Yi and Wang52, and Tlili53, resulted in no reaction (entry 1), the use of Py·HF or Et3N·HF as the fluorine source successfully produced the desired N–CF3-containing pyrroloindoline 2a as a single diastereoisomer in good yields (entries 2–3). The latter afforded a higher yield of 74%. The structure and stereochemistry of the product were confirmed through X-ray diffraction analysis of products 2a and 2 m (see below). The solvent screening revealed that a less polar solvent was beneficial for the transformation, with the original DCM being the optimal choice (entries 3–7). More polar solvents such as N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and tetrahydrofuran (THF) were found to be ineffective. The loadings of both fluorine sources and NBS were also examined. Decreasing the NBS loading to 2.5 equiv delivered a better yield (79%, entry 8), whereas further decreasing to 2.0 equiv resulted in a lower yield (70%, entry 9). The decrease of Et3N·HF loading to 6.0 equiv gave a diminished yield of 68% (entry 10), while increasing the loading to 12.0 equiv led to a clean conversion and an excellent isolated yield of 91% (entry 11). Remarkably, the reaction was completed within 10 min, and the presence of air did not affect the reactivity, demonstrating the practicability and operational simplicity of the protocol.

Substrate scope

With the optimized conditions in hand (Table 1, entry 11), we next focused on exploring the generality of this cascade reaction. The effect of the R substituent on the benzene ring was first examined using NBS as the oxidant and halogen source. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the transformation was found to be very general and robust. A variety of commonly encountered substituents, regardless of their electronic nature, at different positions of the benzene ring were well tolerated, affording the corresponding N–CF3-containing 3-Br-pyrroloindoline products 2b-2l in good to excellent yields with high diastereoselectivities. Substituents such as halides (2b, 2c, 2d, 2f, 2j, and 2k), methyl (2e, 2i, and 2l), methoxy (2g), and cyano (2h) served as valuable functional handles for further derivatization. Notably, substituents at the C4 or C7 position did not interfere with the reactivity (2j, 2k, and 2l). Next, we evaluated the impact of the R1 substituent at the C2 position and the R2 substituent on the bridging ethylene linker. Sterically demanding C2 aryl- (1m, 1n, and 1o), methyl- (1r), and even long-chain alkyl-substituted (1p and 1q) TDIs underwent the reaction smoothly, giving the corresponding products in moderate to good yield. α-Branched isocyanide (1s) and tryptophan-derived isocyanide (1t) (The enantiomerically pure isonitrile 1t was prepared following Danishefsky’s protocol. For details, see ref. 63) were also compatible with the reaction conditions, in which a modest stereochemical induction was observed. Furthermore, even rigid phenylene-bridged isocyanides were amenable to this protocol, furnishing the corresponding asymmetric bipyrroloindolines in moderate yields (2u and 2v). We also investigated the compatibility of various N-protecting groups and found that in addition to tosyl (Ts), TDIs featuring other sulfonyl groups such as benzenesulfonyl (2w), p-bromobenzenesulfonyl (Bs, 2x), p-methoxybenzenesulfonyl (2y), and p-nitrobenzenesulfonyl (Ns, 2z) underwent the cascade reaction with moderate to excellent yields ranging from 57% to 98%. Moreover, commonly encountered acetyl (Ac, 2aa), t-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc, 2ab), carbobenzyloxy (Cbz, 2ac), 2,2,2-trichloroethoxycarbonyl (Troc, 2ad), and allyloxycarbonyl (Alloc, 2ae) groups were also tolerated. It is worth noting that the protocol was not limited to NBS as the oxidant and halogen source. Simply replacing NBS with NCS (N-chlorosuccinimide) or NIS (N-iodosuccinimide) under standard conditions, the corresponding chlorinated and iodinated products were successfully obtained in reasonable yields (3a and 4f). Interestingly, the reaction of the homologous isocyanide 1af with NBS did not produce the desired brominated pyridoindoline product. Instead, pyridoindole 5, presumably resulting from a dehydrobromination process, was isolated in 57% yield, and its structure was confirmed by X-ray crystallographic analysis. Unfortunately, attempts to use unprotected N-H (1ag), N-Ph (1ah), N-Me (1ai) TDIs, other homologous isocyanides with long alkyl chains (1aj and 1ak), or pyrrole-derived isocyanide (1al) failed to give the desired product.

Synthetic applications

To showcase the synthetic practicality of this protocol, a gram-scale synthesis was performed (Fig. 4a). Consequently, 1.22 g of N–CF3-containing pyrroloindoline 2a was obtained in 88% isolated yield on a 3.0 mmol scale under the standard conditions without erosion of efficiency, demonstrating the robustness of the method. Subsequent synthetic manipulations of the C-X bond within the products allowed for the synthesis of diverse N–CF3-containing pyrroloindolines. As mentioned earlier, C3-quaternary-substituted pyrroloindolines are prevalent in numerous naturally occurring products and biologically active molecules. Encouragingly, the incorporated halide functionality can serve as a valuable handle for accessing these intriguing skeletons. As depicted in Fig. 4b, in the presence of DTBP base, a silver(I)-mediated nucleophilic substitution reaction64 of bromide 2a with diverse carbon- (6, 7, and 8), nitrogen- (9 and 10), sulfur- (11), and oxygen-centered (12 and 1365) nucleophiles could bring about tremendous diversity to the N–CF3-containing pyrroloindoline scaffold. It should be noted that all these nucleophilic substitutions proceeded with complete stereospecificity, showing a preference for cis-fusion on the 5,5-ring system. The observed stereoselectivity might result from (1) the silver(I)-promoted Friedel-Crafts-like substitution potentially proceeding via an SN1 mechanism66, where nucleophilic addition occurs to an intermediate like Int-1 (Fig. 4b); and (2) the formation of the cis-diastereoisomer being relatively thermodynamically favored due to lower ring strain67. Furthermore, the mild Ru-catalyzed reductive debromination reaction of bromopyrroloindoline 2a, using N,N-diisopropylethylamine and formic acid under white light, also furnished the C3 debrominated product 14 in good yield (Fig. 4c)68. And as a final attempt, the preliminary investigation of the catalytic asymmetric version of this cascade reaction was unsuccessful (for details, see Supplementary Table 1).

Mechanistic investigation

Finally, to investigate the possible intermediacy of certain intermediates in our cascade reaction, several control experiments and the density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted (Fig. 5a–c). For the intermediacy of a spirocyclization product, when unprotected N-H indole 1ag was subjected to Ruijter’s iodospirocyclization conditions39, spiroindoline 15 was formed in 86% yield (as determined by 1H NMR), and it remained stable under our reaction conditions (Fig. 5a). However, when 1ag was directly subjected to our conditions, neither the desired N–CF3-containing pyrroloindoline product nor 15 was observed. Conversely, in the reaction involving N-Ts indole 1f, the desired product 4f was isolated under our conditions, whereas no formation of 4f was observed when applying Ruijter’s conditions followed by our conditions (Fig. 5a). These results clearly rule out the possible intermediacy of a spirocyclization product in our cascade reaction. In addition, the formation of N-trifluoromethylamine 17, detected by 19F NMR but difficult to isolate due to its instability52, in the reaction of a simple aliphatic isocyanide 16 under our standard conditions (Fig. 5b), indicates that the in-situ generated N-trifluoromethylamine or its anion might be a potential nucleophilic intermediate for the subsequent halocyclization reaction. Although both the two localized structures of the α-halo iminium ion A and the halonium ion A’ are presumably possible intermediates for the halocyclization, the fact that substrates with C2 substituents also underwent the reaction smoothly, coupled with DFT calculations on them (for details, see the Supplementary Information on pages 25–27), suggests that this cationic intermediate preferentially adopts the former (Fig. 5c). Based on these results and literature precedent60,61,69, the possible reaction pathways are outlined in Fig. 5d. Initially, the electrophilic bromo activation of TDI 1r with NBS delivers the bromonitrilium ion B. Thereafter, the nucleophilic attack of a fluoride ion on B, followed by halogen exchange reactions, leads to the formation of isocyanide difluoride C, a common intermediate in the synthesis of N–CF3 compounds50–53. In addition, the electrophilic halogenating agent NBS could induce the formation of an electrophilic α-bromo iminium ion A. On the one hand, the isocyanide difluoride moiety within A is further attacked by another fluoride ion to generate the trifluoromethylated amine or its anion D (path a), which undergoes intramolecular nucleophilic addition to furnish the desired N–CF3-containing pyrroloindoline product 2r. Alternatively, the direct intramolecular attack of the nitrogen atom of the isocyanide difluoride moiety on the α-bromo iminium ion may also take place, leading to an annulated iminium species E that is attacked by a fluoride ion to give the same final product (path b).

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a mild and robust cascade oxidative trifluorination and halogenative cyclization reaction of tryptamine-derived isocyanides in the presence of N-halosuccinimide and Et3N·HF. In contrast to the well-established spiroindolines, this reaction afforded an N–CF3-containing pyrroloindoline skeleton. Our work also demonstrates the straightforward access to the N–CF3 moiety from isocyanides under operationally simple conditions. The protocol features mild reaction conditions, broad functional group tolerance, good to excellent yields and high diastereoselectivity. Importantly, the halide functionality within the products enables rapid access to C3-quaternary-substituted N–CF3-containing pyrroloindolines. Given the potential of the products and the synthetic practicality of the protocol, we anticipate the method developed herein will find more valuable applications in medicinal chemistry.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of N–CF3-containing pyrroloindolines

To a stirred solution of NXS (2.5 equiv) in dichloromethane (0.5 mL) in a 10 mL of polyethylene tube under ambient atmosphere, Et3N ∙ HF (62 μL, 12.0 equiv hydrogen fluoride) was added at room temperature, followed by the addition of tryptamine-derived isocyanide 1 (0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 5-10 min until the complete consumption of starting material as confirmed by TLC analysis. The crude product was then purified by wet loading through flash column chromatography on silica gel to afford the desired products.
