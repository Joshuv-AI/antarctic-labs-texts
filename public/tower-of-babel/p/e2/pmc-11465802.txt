Risk factors for severe respiratory syncytial virus-associated respiratory tract infection in a high HIV prevalence setting, South Africa, 2012 – 2018
Authors: Jocelyn Moyes, Stefano Tempia, Sibongile Walaza, Adam L Cohen, Florette Treurnicht, Orienka Hellferscee, Nicole Wolter, Anne von Gottberg, Halima Dawood, Ebrahim Variava, Kathleen Kahn, Shabir A Madhi, Cheryl Cohen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying risk factors for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)–associated severe acute respiratory illness (SARI) will assist with targeting vaccine interventions.MethodsUsing surveillance data from South Africa (2012–2018), we compared the characteristics of individuals with RSV-associated influenza-like illness (ILI) (reference group) to those with RSV-associated SARI to describe factors associated with SARI using a multivariable analysis.ResultsRSV was detected in 6% (483/7792) of ILI cases and 15% (844/5672) of SARI cases. Factors associated with SARI in children included age < 2 months, compared to age 2–4 years (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 54.4; 95% confidence interval (CI) 23.5–125.8), malnutrition (aOR 1.9; 95% CI 1.2–3.2), prematurity (aOR 2.4; 95% CI 1.3–4.6) and living with HIV (LWH) (aOR 22.5; 95% CI 2.9–174.3). In individuals ≥ 5 years, factors associated with SARI included age ≥ 65 years compared to age 5–24 years (aOR 10.7; 95% CI 1.1–107.5), symptom duration ≥ 5 days (aOR 2.7; 95% CI 1.1–6.3), underlying illness (aOR 2.7; 95% CI 1.5–26.1) and LWH (aOR 16.8, 95% CI: 4.8–58.2).ConclusionIndividuals at the extremes of age and those with identified risk factors might benefit most from RSV prevention interventions. Clinical trial numberNot applicable, this is not a clinical trial.Keywords: Respiratory syncytial virus, RSV, Risk factors, Lower respiraptry tract infection, HIV infection

Summary

Comparing individuals with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)-associated influenza-like illness to those hospitalized with RSV-associated illness, living with HIV was a risk factor for severe RSV-associated illness in all age groups. Additionally, malnutrition and prematurity were associated with severe illness in children.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection is responsible for substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly among infants and young children [1]. Identifying specific risk factors for severe illness over all age groups is often challenging due to limitations in study design and lack of age-group specific studies. Identifying specific risk factors for severe illness could assist with identifying target groups for monoclonal antibody therapy or vaccination, as well as guiding cost-effectiveness studies.

The burden of RSV-associated respiratory tract infection (RTI) is high in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) [1]. Globally, an estimated 3.3 million RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) hospitalizations and between 26 300 and 101 400 deaths occur annually in children aged < 5 years. A large proportion of these hospitalizations and deaths occur in low and middle income countries (LMIC) [1]. From a burden study done in our setting the mean annual number of RSV-associated severe illness cases in children aged < 5 years was 96,220 (95% Confidence Interval (CI) 66,470–132,844) [2]. With 82% of these cases (78,571 occurring in children < 1 year. The incidence of RSV-associated illness is higher in older adults and adult with comorbidities, a meta-analysis reported an incidence of 16 cases/1000 person years (95% confidence interval 3.52–73.83) in adults ≥ 65 years [3]. In a study done in our setting documented an incidence of RSV-associated LRTI hospitalisation of 30/100 000 (95% CI 26–34) [4].

In South Africa, young infants, the elderly and people living with HIV (PLWH) and individuals exposed to HIV have an increased risk of hospitalization for RSV [4–6]. However, data on other possible risk factors for severe illness prevalent in LMIC settings such as malnutrition are limited.

We aimed to compare the characteristics of RSV-positive patients with influenza-like illness (ILI) to RSV-positive patients with severe acute respiratory tract infection (SARI), enrolled at two sentinel sites in South Africa prior to the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic between June 2012 and May 2018, to identify factors associated with severe hospitalized illness.

Methods

We made use of a study design previously published by Tempia et al., where cases of RSV-associated ILI (reference group) are compared to cases of RSV-associated SARI [7]. In this design, within the population of those with RSV-associated illness, we used those with ILI as the reference groups to compare the characteristics of these individuals with mild illness to those with SARI. This methodology allows us to identify factors that may predict progression to severe disease, rather than comparing severe illness to healthy controls who do not have the illness. Within this comparison we did take into account confounding factors in the association.

Surveillance

SARI and ILI surveillance

In 2009, South Africa implemented prospective hospital-based sentinel surveillance for SARI and this programme is ongoing in five of the nine provinces in South Africa [8]. In June 2012, prospective clinic-based surveillance for ILI was added to the programme at two public health clinics situated in the referral catchment area of two SARI surveillance sites, namely Edendale Hospital in a peri-urban area of KwaZulu-Natal Province, and the Klerksdorp-Tshepong Hospital Complex, (KTHC) in an urban area of North West Province. Patients enrolled at the two public health clinics (Edendale Gateway and Jouberton Clinics) and at Edendale Hospital and Klerksdorp-Tshepong Hospital Complex between June 2012 and May 2018 were included in the analysis.

Surveillance case definitions

ILI was defined as an outpatient of any age presenting with either temperature > 38 °C or history of fever and cough of duration of ≤ 10 days.

SARI was defined as a hospitalized person (with illness onset within 10 days of admission) who met age-specific clinical inclusion criteria. A case in children aged 2 days to < 3 months included any hospitalized patient with diagnosis of suspected sepsis (as included in the WHO case definition) [9] or physician-diagnosed acute lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) irrespective of signs and symptoms. A case in children aged 3 months to < 5 years included any hospitalized patient with physician-diagnosed LRTI, including bronchitis, bronchiolitis, bronchopneumonia, pneumonia and pleural effusion. Between 2012 and 2014, a case in individuals aged ≥ 5 years included any hospitalized patient presenting with fever (> 38 °C), or history of fever, and cough or sore throat or shortness of breath or difficulty breathing with or without clinical or radiographic findings of pneumonia. From 2015 onwards, the case definition in individuals ≥ 5 years was defined according to World Health Organisation (WHO) recommendations as a patient hospitalized with cough and fever (> 38 °C) or history of fever [10, 11].

Dedicated surveillance staff systematically enrolled patients, following written informed consent (or parent/guardian consent for children < 18 years), between Monday (including admissions from 17h00 on the Sunday) and Friday. The progamme kept logs of patients screened and enrolled throughout the study period. Outcomes data collected included hospital referral for ILI patients and in-hospital death for SARI patients. We excluded ILI patients referred to hospital on the day of enrollment from this analysis, to avoid enrolling individuals as both ILI and SARI.

Laboratory procedures

From 2012–2016, nasopharyngeal aspirates were collected from children < 5 years of age. From 2017–2018, nasopharyngeal (NP) and oropharyngeal (OP) swabs were collected on children aged < 5 years. In persons ≥ 5 years NP and OP swabs were collected throughout the study period. Specimens were placed in viral transport medium, then stored at 4–8 °C. Samples were transported to the National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD) to arrive within 72 h of collection. From 2012–2016, specimens from all age groups were tested for the presence of 10 respiratory viruses (respiratory syncytial virus; influenza A and B viruses; parainfluenza virus types 1, 2 and 3; adenovirus; rhinovirus; human metapneumovirus; and enterovirus) using a multiplex real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay [11]. From June 2016 onwards, testing was limited to influenza and RSV.

Known risk factors for severe RSV illness were considered for inclusion based on factors in published literature either risk factors for LRTI (as for malnutrition) or risk factor specifically for RSV-associated LRTI [12, 13].

Determination of HIV status and definition of HIV-exposure

HIV status was determined from one of the following sources with patient consent: (i) HIV status documented in the patient record, at the time of admission or prior to admission; (ii) in-hospital/clinic testing at the time of admission as per standard testing procedures implemented by the National Department of Health; or (iii) if neither of these were available a dried blood spot was tested at NICD. HIV testing was done by HIV PCR for children under the age of 18 months and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for older children and adults. HIV-exposure was defined as an infant (< 6 months) having negative HIV-PCR on admission and a documented or reported HIV-positive maternal HIV status.

Assessment of malnutrition and prematurity

The weight-for-age Z-score was calculated for children under the age of five years to determine nutritional status (Z-score < -2 considered malnourished). Prematurity was defined as an infant born prior to 37 weeks of estimated gestational age recorded in the Road to Health Card, a record of a child’s birth and vaccination history.

Factors associated with RSV-associated respiratory illness

We considered those with RSV-associated SARI as cases and those with RSV-associated ILI as the reference group. Patients with RSV-associated SARI were compared to patients with RSV-associated ILI by age, underlying medical conditions, and clinical characteristics. For children < 5 years the following underlying conditions were considered both individual and then grouped as a combined variable: heart disease, chronic lung conditions, immune deficiency (other than PLWH), chronic liver disease, renal disease, immunosuppressive therapy, asplenia, diabetes, burns, seizure disorders, spinal injury, and cancers. In individuals ≥ 5 years we added obesity and systemic lupus erythematosus.

We used logistic regression (univariate and multivariable) to assess factors associated with RSV-associated SARI hospitalization. For the multivariable model, we included all variables with p < 0.2 on univariate analysis, and conducted a stepwise backward selection dropping variables with p ≥ 0.05. All models were tested for collinearity. We conducted analyses in age groups < 5 years and ≥ 5 years separately as risk factors may differ among young children and older individuals [7]. We did not include other respiratory viruses in the multivariable model as test was not conducted for the entire study period. To assess the association of pregnancy with RSV-associated SARI we conducted a sub-analysis in women of childbearing age (15 to 49 years). Similarly, we did a sub-analysis of infants aged 0 to 6 months to compare HIV-exposed uninfected infants (HEU) to those who were not exposed to HIV (HIV unexposed uninfected, HUU) to assess the association of HIV exposure as a risk factor for RSV-associated SARI.

Sample size calculation

We applied a two proportion calculation to detect a difference (15%) in detection rate between ILI and SARI cases, with a power of 90% to detect a difference, the sample size in each group was calculated to be 242. With our sample size of 1367 RSV positives we have adequate sample size in all subcategories of risk factor assessment.

Analysis was conducted in Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC.

Results

Study population

Over the study period, 13,600 individuals were enrolled at the two sites. Of these 99% (13,464) had an RSV test result available. The main reasons for non-availability of results was sample leaking on transport. The detection rate of RSV in individuals of all ages was 6% (483/7792) among individuals with ILI and 15% (884/5672) in individuals with SARI.

Children less than 5 years of age

From June 2012 to May 2018, 5786 children under the age of 5 years meeting either the case definition for ILI or SARI were enrolled and had a RSV test result. Of these 48% (2770) were outpatients with ILI and 52% (3016) were hospitalised with SARI. In individuals aged < 5 years the RSV detection rate was 11% (317/2770) for those with ILI and 25% (762/3016) in patients with SARI. (Fig. 1). In patients with SARI, the highest detection rate was in infants aged < 6 months, 36%, (458/1276) and in children with ILI the highest detection rate was in the 6–11 month age group, 14% (73/517) (Fig. 1).

Detection rate of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) among those with outpatient influenza-like illness and inpatient severe acute respiratory illness by age, at two sites in South Africa 2012–2018

HIV-status information was available for 96% (5572/5776) of children aged < 5 years. One percent of individuals were living with HIV (LWH) (32/2675) in individuals with ILI and 8% (239/2897) in those with SARI. Only 17 children LWH had data on antiretroviral treatment (ART) and 47% (8/17) were receiving ART at the time of hospitalisation. The reasons for not receiving ART are not available.

Risk factors for RSV-associated SARI among children aged < 5 years

On multivariable analysis, age < 2 months (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 54.4, 95% Confidence Interval (CI): 23.5–125.8), age 2 months (aOR 12.0, 95% CI: 6.2–23.1), age 3–5 months (aOR 9.6, 95% CI: 5.8–16.0), age 6–11 months (aOR 4.0, 95% CI 2.5–6.4) and age 12–23 months (aOR 3.1, 95% CI: 1.9–5.1) compared to age 24–59 months, malnutrition (aOR 1.9, 95% CI: 1.2–3.2), prematurity (aOR 2.4, 95% CI: 1.3–4.6) and LWH (aOR 22.5, 95% CI: 2.9–174.3) were associated with RSV-associated severe illness (Table 1).

Individuals aged ≥ 5 years

During the study period, 7756 individuals aged ≥ 5 years were enrolled into the ILI or SARI surveillance programme, of these 99% (7678) had a test result for RSV. Of the enrolled individuals, 65% (5022) had ILI and 33% (2656) had SARI. In individuals aged ≥ 5 years, the detection rate was 3% (166/5022) in individuals with ILI and 3% (82/2656) in those with SARI. Age-specific detection rates range from 3 to 4%. (Fig. 1).

HIV status was available for 93% (7228/7756) of enrolled individuals. The HIV prevalence in individuals with RSV-associated ILI was 43% (65/151) and 80% (61/76) in those hospitalised with RSV-associated SARI. Of those with treatment data available 50% (62/125) were receiving ART; ART receipt was 51% (33/65) with ILI and 48% (29/60) with SARI.

Discussion

We drew on surveillance data to describe the factors for severe RSV-associated SARI in a middle-income, high HIV prevalence setting by comparing individuals with hospitalized RSV-associated SARI to individuals with RSV-associated ILI (reference group). Previous studies have described factors associated with severe RSV-associated LRTI or death among hospitalized individuals but without a comparison group [14]. In a review and meta-analysis factors associated with RSV-associated LRTI included: prematurity, low birth weight, male sex, having siblings, maternal smoking, history of atopy, no breastfeeding and crowding [15]. In a case series of RSV-associated deaths in low and middle income countries comorbidities (grouped variable), urban living area and sign of severe diseases (intensive care admission, ventilation, low oxygen saturation) were associated with RSV-associated death [14]. In contrast we have evaluated, these and other factors with a reference group. Importantly, we provide data that describe a significant two-fold increased adjusted odds of RSV-associated severe illness in malnourished children. Other risk factors included age; less than six months, 6–11 months, 12–23 months (as compared to children aged 2– < 5 years); LWH and prematurity. In adults, LWH is strongly associated with severe infection, as is older age, specifically for those aged ≥ 65 years.

Although malnutrition has been described as a risk factor for pneumonia, there are few data on the role of childhood malnutrition as a risk factor for RSV-associated SARI [13]. This may be because many RSV risk factor studies were conducted in high-income countries where the prevalence of malnutrition is low. We report an association with RSV-associated SARI and malnutrition. A similar association was described in children in the Philippines (hazard ratio (HR)3.38 95% CI: 1.63–6.98), but only seen in children whose mothers had > 10 years of education [16]. The increased risk for RSV-associated SARI in malnourished children is likely due to malnutrition-induced immunosuppression [17]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, the meta-odds of premature infants (< 37 weeks gestation) (OR 1.96; 95% CI: 1.44–2.67) having severe RSV-associated RTI are very similar to our estimates (aOR 2.4; 95% CI: 1.3–4.6) [13]. This analysis included published, unpublished data and included studies from high and low-income countries. Although some studies have described the risk of severe RSV illness using gestational ages of < 35 weeks, we did not find an association when applying a gestational age cut-off of < 35 weeks as compared to < 37 weeks [18].

A successful prevention of mother to child transmission programme has led to a dramatic decrease in the prevalence of HIV in young children in South Africa; however, almost a third of children are born HIV-uninfected but exposed to HIV [19]. We have previously described HEU infants to have a greater incidence of hospitalization with RSV-associated SARI compared to HUU infants in our setting (relative risk (RR)) 2.2, 95% CI: 1.4–3.4) [5]. In the current analysis, children exposed to HIV in-utero were at no increased odds of presenting with severe RSV-associated LRTI compared to HUU infants. In the current study, we compared the prevalence of HEU among patients with SARI to those with ILI (rather than the general population as in previous studies). We observed a high prevalence of HEU among infants with RSV-associated ILI (25/59 (42%)), higher than estimates of HIV prevalence in pregnant women in the Northwest Province (Klerksdorp site), 29.3% (95% CI: 26.9%-31.5%), and similar to the prevalence in KwaZulu-Natal Province (Edendale site), 44% (95% CI: 42.5%-46.3%), in 2015 [19]. A possible reason for the high prevalence of HEU among infants with ILI is the case definition we applied for ILI. This case definition included a measure of fever (measured or reported), which may not be representative of all RSV-associated ILI in infants, as some published data suggest that RSV-associated RTI may not present with fever, and thus children with fever may represent a more severe subgroup [20].

Data on the risk of severe RSV-associated LRTI in PLWH are sparse. Several case reports of severe RSV-associated LRTI in adults LWH have been published mostly from the pre-antiretroviral treatment era [21–23]. Although HIV infection has been described as a risk factor for RSV-associated SARI hospitalization in our setting, this study supports LWH as a risk factor for severe RSV-associated SARI in both adults and children [4, 6]. The mechanism for this risk is likely that the immune response to RSV infection requires both cellular and humoral elements and specifically CD4 + and CD8 + T cells that are key in neutralizing RSV infection [24].

Our finding of age ≥ 65 years and any underlying illness (excluding HIV) as independent risk factors for severe RSV among adults is similar to previous studies [24–27]. The increased risk in these groups is likely due to age- or underlying illness-related immune system dysfunction [24].

The increased risk of RSV-associated SARI seen at the Klerksdorp site needs exploring. This urban site is in gold mining area on the temperate inland plateau of South Africa. In contrast, the Edendale site is in a sub-tropical climatic zone. Several studies have explored climatic relationships to RSV seasonality but none have described an association between climate and severity of illness[28–31]. Similarly, studies have explored air pollution with respect to seasonality and epidemics of RSV but we did not have data on air pollution [32]. The HIV prevalence across sites is similar and although working in mines, a possible risk factor for respiratory illness, was significantly associated with SARI on univariate analysis it was not independently associated with RSV-associated SARI so neither explain this association. In the public health system admission to hospital is at the discretion of attending physicians, it is possible that admission criteria vary between the two sites.

Contrary to the increased risk of severe influenza-associated LRTI infection in pregnancy, we did not find that pregnant women have an increased risk of severe RSV. We do report a higher percentage of RSV-associated ILI cases in pregnant women 71% (79/112) as compare to SARI 29% (33/112). Similar to findings to a community based study in Nepal which reports few severe cases of RSV [33]. Few studies have documented the risk of RSV in pregnant women. In a case series (3 cases) in pregnant women with severe RTI, all three patients had underlying lung conditions and one had influenza coinfection (Wheeler). Although the immune system compromise in pregnancy such as the decrease function of CD4 + and CD8 + cells suggest a risk to viral infections such as RSV there are limited data in the published literature.

We described 15 individuals with RSV who died. All the deaths were in individuals with identified risk factors for severe illness. The RSV-Gold network paper describing global aggregated RSV-associated deaths among children aged < 5 years reports that 99% of RSV-associated deaths occur in developing countries. Young age (< 6 months), underlying illnesses and prematurity were present in a high proportion of these deaths [14]. These findings are similar to those in our analysis.

The limitations of our study include the fact that, while we did follow up patients presenting with ILI until admission on the same day or hospitalization at our sentinel sites, it is possible that patients with ILI were hospitalized at locations other than our surveillance sites. Given the low prevalence of some comorbidities and the low detection rates of RSV in some age groups it was not possible to assess the role of each of these on the risk for severe RSV-associated LRTI. Although there is a high prevalence of tuberculosis in our setting, we were not able to test for tuberculosis in the outpatient setting and therefore were not able to assess the role of tuberculosis in RSV-associated LRTI. Our case definition for ILI and SARI included fever for individuals ≥ 5 years of age. In children < 5 years of age, however the presence of fever is not part of the case definition of SARI. Fever is likely a sign of more severe illness among outpatients and this could potentially have reduced our power to detect some risk factors for severe illness, in children.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we describe LWH as an important risk factor for severe RSV-associated illness in children and adults and malnutrition as a risk factor in children. Identifying risk factors may assist with allocation of resources for vaccine or longer acting monoclonal now that these products are becoming available: For example, targeted interventions for premature, malnourished or HIV-infected children to prevent hospitalization with RSV-associated SARI in these groups. Our risk factor approach of considering ILI as a reference group and SARI as cases, which has been used to describe the risk factors for influenza [7], could be used to describe risk factors in other countries conducting surveillance for RSV on an influenza surveillance platform. Additional studies maybe necessary to explore associations not possible in this analysis such as the association between RSV and coinfection with other viruses and bacterial infections (including TB).
