Characterization and quantitation of baculoviral DNA in rAAV vectors produced in Sf9 cells
Authors: Xushan Wang, Andrew Pla, Vedud Purde, Sabine Wenzel, Priyam Raut, Diogo de Oliveira Pessoa, Jorge F Haller, Stuart Nelson, Beverly A Heinz, Sarah M Richer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) vectors are widely used in gene therapy due to their ability to transduce various cell types and tissues, sustained gene expression, and relatively safe profile. However, the production of rAAV vectors using the baculovirus/Sf9 system can result in the mispackaging of the baculoviral (recombinant baculoviral [rBV]) DNA. This study aims to characterize and quantify these rBV DNA impurities in rAAV products. We developed a multiplex digital PCR method to model, characterize, and accurately determine rBV DNA impurities. Our findings indicate that rBV DNA within 5 kb of the inverted terminal repeat (ITR) and the mini-F region has a higher probability of being mispackaged into rAAV particles than other regions. Additionally, using regular PCR plus agarose gel analysis and digital PCR, full-length kanamycin and gentamicin antibiotic genes were detected and quantitated in the rAAV. The study also revealed a strand-selective mispackaging of rBV DNA, with no correlation between the amount of rBV DNA impurity and the vector’s size conflicting with the prevalent belief that smaller vectors will contain more rBV impurities. These results provide insights into the mechanisms of rBV DNA impurity formation and suggest strategies to reduce these impurities, thereby enhancing the safety and efficacy of rAAV-based gene therapies.Keywords: rAAV, gene therapy, baculoviral DNA impurity, residual DNA, multiplex digital PCR, next-generation sequencing, eGFP vectors, RNA-DNA hybrid capture MSD, strand-specific mispackage, full-length antibiotic genes

Introduction

Recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) is becoming the dominant viral vector for gene therapy due to its capacity to transduce various cell types and tissues, sustained gene expression, and relatively safe profile.1 Various rAAV production platforms have been developed for the manufacturing of rAAV viruses, including transient transfection, baculovirus/Sf9 insect cell system, stable producer cell line,2,3 herpes simplex virus (HSV) system,4 and tetracycline-enabled self-silencing adenovirus (TESSA),5 among others. Among these methods, transient transfection and baculovirus/Sf9 insect cell systems are the primary techniques employed for producing clinically approved rAAV products.6 The former primarily involves triple transfection of HEK293 cells,7,8 which poses significant challenges in scaling up production to meet clinical demands, particularly for systemic administration. The latter, baculovirus/Sf9 insect cell system, supports large-scale viral production. This system has been progressing from utilizing three-baculovirus system (three-Bac)9 to two-Bac system10,11,12 and even one-Bac 1.0,13 2.0,14,15 and 3.016 systems. With all these systems, recombinant baculoviral (rBV) backbone and Sf9 DNA can also be packaged into rAAV particles at a low frequency. rBV DNA typically constitutes the majority of DNA impurities present in the rAAV product produced from the baculovirus/Sf9 system.17,18 Quantitative PCR (qPCR) or digital PCR (dPCR) targeting regions such as Autographa californica multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus (AcMNPV) gp64 or AcMNPV DNA polymerase genes is used to detect and quantify these rBV DNA impurities in rAAV products.19 Nevertheless, the packaging of rBV DNA is not entirely random. Recent research shows that sequences near the AAV inverted terminal repeat (ITR) and bacterial origin of DNA replication are significantly more likely to be mispackaged compared to those more distant from the ITRs.18,20 Existing methods fail to accurately quantitate rBV DNA impurity or provide a comprehensive analysis. They are unable to reveal the mechanisms behind occasionally high levels of impurity or offer insights into reducing these impurities. Notably, antibiotic resistance genes such as kanamycin and gentamicin are positioned near the ITRs. This positioning is associated with the commonly used site-specific transposition of the Tn7 transposon into a bacmid, like the Bac-to-Bac expression system. It remains unclear whether full-length versions of these genes are present in rAAV products. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, including single-strand virus sequencing (SSV-Seq)/Illumina, single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing/PacBio, and Oxford Nanopore, have been used to quantify these DNA impurities.16,18,20,21,22,23,24 An orthogonal method is urgently needed to verify the most reliable sequencing technique.25 Such a method is essential not only for quantitating rBV DNA impurities but also for understanding their formation mechanisms, developing strategies to reduce them, and mitigating safety concerns.20 Previous studies suggest that smaller vector sizes are more prone to rBV DNA mispackaging, necessitating further examination with additional vectors.18 In this study, we first developed a multiplex dPCR method for modeling, characterizing, and accurately determining rBV DNA impurities. Our data demonstrated that rBV DNA within 5 kb of the ITR and the mini-F region has a much higher probability of being mispackaged into rAAV particles than other elements. We then confirmed the presence of full-length kanamycin and gentamicin antibiotic genes in the AAV product using dPCR, regular PCR, and agarose gel analysis. Additionally, we found a strand-selective, rather than equal, mispackaging of rBV DNA using an RNA-DNA hybrid capture MSD method. Finally, our findings indicated no correlation between the amount of rBV DNA impurity and the vector’s size.

Results

Development of multiplex dPCR

To accurately quantify rBV DNA impurities in rAAV products, 43 amplicons were designed based on the rBaculovirus-rAAV GFP6.0kb (rBac-rAAV GFP6.0) vector (see Figure 1, with primer and probe sequences listed in Table S3). Twelve amplicons were chosen within the rAAV region, while the remaining 31 were located in the rBV backbone. These amplicons were classified into three categories based on their expected copy numbers: those within the rAAV region should have the highest counts, those distant from the ITRs in the bacmid backbone should have the lowest, and those near the ITRs should be intermediate. The 43 amplicons were further clustered into smaller groups containing 3 to 4 amplicons, each with different dye-labeled probes to facilitate multiplexing. The final layout is depicted in Figure 2A, where order numbers correlate with amplicon locations on the rBac-rAAV GFP6.0 vector. All amplicons were tested using GFP6.0kb rAAV in both single and multiplex formats. As shown in Figure 2B, there was an excellent correlation in copy numbers between these formats, demonstrating no interference between amplicons. To evaluate the variability of multiplex dPCR, eight rAAV products from 13 rAAV vectors were tested three times. A representative example in Figure S1 shows that the relative standard deviation (RSD) percentage for all amplicons was below 20%.

Quantitation of rBV DNA impurity

To measure total rBV DNA in rAAV products, the copy number of each amplicon was plotted against its location on the rBV-rAAV vector (base pairs, bps), as depicted in Figure 3A. Here, the dark blue area represents the rAAV genome region, while the dark red area denotes the rBV backbone vector region. The rBV backbone targets near the ITRs had relatively high copy numbers, which gradually decreased as the distance from the ITR increased. The copy number plateaued around the 5 kb mark. Both the rBV backbone region upstream and downstream of the rAAV genome exhibited similar patterns. On the downstream side, the rBV vector showed low copy numbers at the 5 kb region before increasing again. This area corresponds to the mini-F region of the rBV vector. The area under the curve26 was determined for both the dark blue area (total rAAV genome content) and dark red area (total rBV DNA impurity content). The area under the curve of the dark red region over the entire area under the curve indicated the percentage of the rBV DNA impurities (% rBV DNA impurity) in the rAAV product. Results from testing eight rAAV products three times, shown in Figure 3B, demonstrated a %RSD below 10%, indicating good repeatability. In addition, Figure 3B illustrates the % rBV DNA impurity in all 13 eGFP rAAV products representing a range of vector genome sizes from 3.05 to 6 kb; no correlation with vector size is observed. The GFP4.5kb and GFP4.9kb vectors exhibited a significantly high level of rBV DNA impurity, as indicated in red in Figure 3B. Table 1 shows that copy numbers of amplicons (LS-1-C and RS-1) near the ITRs were significantly higher than those of amplicon LS-12 and RS-14 targeting AcMNPV gp64 and AcMNPV DNA polymerase region of rBV backbone, respectively. This highlights that qPCR or dPCR targeting the gp64 or DNA polymerase regions does not accurately quantify rBV DNA impurities in rAAV products. Tables S1 and S2 indicate that the regions near ITR exhibited significantly higher percentages and fold differences compared to the gp64 and AcMNPV DNA polymerase regions. The GFP and woodchuck hepatitis virus posttranscriptional regulatory element (WPRE) regions exhibited lower folds (Table S2) in the GFP4.5kb and GFP4.9kb vectors due to the high copy number of the gp64 region (Table 1).

To identify the presence of full-length antibiotic genes in the rAAV product, PCR primers were designed to amplify the complete kanamycin and gentamicin genes. Two methods were utilized to quantify and confirm the presence of full-length antibiotic genes in the rAAV products. The first method employed dPCR to amplify and quantify the copy numbers of full-length kanamycin- (855 bp; KanR) and gentamicin (676 bp; GentaR)-resistant genes, as illustrated in Figures 5A and 5B (upper). Dual probes with Fam and Hex dye ensured specificity. Figures 5A and 5B (lower) indicate that full-length KanR and GentaR were 1.58e9 and 1.51e9 copies per mL, respectively, in the 4.9 kb GFP virus. Both probes with dye Fam and Hex showed consistent copy numbers. The full-length KanR and GentaR showed lower copy numbers compared to short amplicons near these regions (Figures 5A and 5B). This might be due to a significant amount of rBV DNA being randomly mispackaged into AAV particles, rather than ITR-mediated packaging in the 4.9 kb virus, and/or heating-induced fragmentation. It was also unable to rule out the potential for reduced amplification efficiency when working with large amplicons. The second method involved standard PCR followed by agarose gel analysis. Figure S3a depicts the location and length of amplicons. Figure S3b confirms that full-length KanR and GentaR were detected in the 4.9 kb GFP virus with the correct size and clear band. The data qualitatively and quantitatively demonstrate the presence of both full-length kanamycin and gentamicin genes in the rAAV product.

Strand-selective rBV DNA packaging near the ITR region

Single-strand DNA packaging into AAV particles occurs from the 3′ end to the 5′ end. If a 3′ ITR serves as the signal mediating the mispackaging of the rBV backbone DNA into the AAV particle, then the minus strand could dominate downstream of the rBV DNA near the ITR region, including the gentamicin gene (Figure 6B, upper). Conversely, on the upstream of the rBV DNA, the plus strand might dominate (Figure 6A, upper). Figure 6B (lower) showed that the minus strand and plus strand of the plasmid DNA spike-in control had similar gentamicin copy numbers. However, the copy number of the minus strand ranged from 73% to 94% within nine rAAV vectors, while the plus strand constituted only 6%–27%. This suggested that the minus strand of the gentamicin gene dominated in the rAAV product. Interestingly, the percentage of the plus strand in the 4.5 and 4.9 kb vectors was the highest among the nine vectors. Figure 6A (lower) showed that the plus strand of the kanamycin gene dominated 79%–94%, whereas the minus strand comprised only 6%–21%. The minus strand exhibited the highest level in the 4.5 and 4.9 kb vectors. The plus strand dominant pattern of kanamycin was reversed for the gentamicin gene. Taken together, these results provide experimental evidence to support the hypothesis that the baculoviral backbone DNA near the ITR was mispackaged into AAV particles using the 3′ ITR as the packaging signal.

Mechanism of high rBV DNA impurity in some rAAV products

The three methods outlined here indicate no correlation between rBV backbone DNA impurity in rAAV products and vector size. Figure 7A directly compares the pattern of the area under the curve analyses of the rBV backbone DNA in the three rAAV products, where the vector size is very similar. The 4.9 kb vector contained the highest rBV DNA impurity, showing elevated levels in the mini-F region. Figure S2 illustrates that the NGS data exhibited a similar phenomenon, where the mini-F concentration was significantly higher in the 4.9 kb vector. Since the rBV backbone region near the ITR appears to use the ITR as the signal for mispackaging, the AAV packaging capacity is around 5 kb. The rBV backbone regions farther from the ITRs could be mispackaged using alternate signal sequences. Figure 7B shows that the residual rBV backbone DNA packaged into rAAV can be divided into two regions—one nearby the rAAV (red area) and one farther from the rAAV (purple area). The red area was 0.172%, and the purple area was 0.783%. Two rBV viruses were used to infect Sf9 cells for AAV production in this study. One rBV contains the rAAV genome (rBV-rAAV), including both ITRs and the gene of interest (GOI), and the other rBV encodes the AAV Rep and Cap genes (rBV-Rep/Cap) and lacks ITRs. Therefore, the results from the red area are expected to be mostly derived from rBV-rAAV, whereas the purple area could originate from both rBV-rAAV and rBV-Rep/Cap. The data shown in Figure 7C revealed a highly significant correlation between the ratio of the purple area to the red area and the total rBV backbone impurity in the rAAV product. Amplicon RS-10 is located in the mini-F region, while RS-1 is near the right ITR. RS-1 should mostly derive from rBV-rAAV, and RS-10 could stem from both rBV-rAAV and rBV-Rep/Cap. Figure 7D illustrates a very strong correlation between the ratio of RS-10 to RS-1 and the rBV backbone impurity.

Discussion

rAAV has become the leading viral vector for gene therapy. However, the rAAV genome within the particle is known to be a heterogeneous population.27 While rAAV particles contain the intact rAAV genome, they may also include DNA impurities such as truncated rAAV genomes and mispackaged DNA from vector plasmid(s), viral vector backbone, and host cell genome present during the production process.20 These truncated genomes and mispackaged DNA do not result in stable, long-term transgene expression and may even cause side effects.18 Accurately quantitating these truncated genomes and mispackaged DNA is one of the most important critical quality attributes. The truncated genomes and mispackaged DNA stem from different mechanisms, requiring varying methods to address them separately. This paper focuses on the mispackaged DNA in rAAV products, referred to as DNA impurities. Given that the vector plasmid, viral vector, and host cells are necessary during production, this mispackaged DNA is an impurity rather than a contaminant. Several production platforms for rAAV manufacturing have been developed, including transient transfection,7,8 the baculovirus/Sf9 insect cell system,9,10,11,12,13,14,15,16 stable producer cell lines,2 HSV,4 and TESSA.5 Among these, HEK293/triple transfection and baculovirus/Sf9 are two primary platforms utilized for rAAV production. HEK293 has limitations in large-scale production, while the baculovirus/Sf9 system is easily scalable. Various methods, including qPCR, dPCR, and NGS techniques such as Illumina, PacBio, and Oxford Nanopore, have been developed to quantify this mispackaged DNA. Mispackaging occurrences are not random; DNA sequences near the ITR or bacterial replication origins are more likely to be included. Reliable methods are needed to accurately quantitate the DNA impurity, understand its mechanisms, and reduce its occurrence. If qPCR or dPCR is designed to target only a few regions of the vector backbone, it cannot measure the amount of mispackaged DNA accurately.18 Illumina, PacBio, and Oxford Nanopore show promise for this purpose, each with advantages and limitations, though there are limited data comparing their reliability. The mispackaged DNA levels vary widely across different production processes, vectors, and quantification methods, ranging from less than 1% to 26%.20,23 This highlights the importance of an independent approach to verify current techniques and emphasizes the necessity of establishing correlations among these distinct orthogonal methods.25

In the SF9/baculovirus system, current data suggest that baculoviral DNA constitutes most of the mispackaged DNA in rAAV products. We developed a multiplex dPCR method using the QIAGEN QIAcuity system targeting multiple regions of the baculoviral vector, especially areas near the ITR. One of our primary objectives was to quantify the rBV impurity during production and elucidate the mechanisms responsible for elevated impurity levels in specific vectors. Therefore, we purified the virus using an affinity column exclusively, without further processing through ion exchange or gradient centrifugation, as these methods may alter the concentration of rBV DNA species within the AAV particles and introduce bias. The rAAV genome exists as a heterogeneous population with varying sizes within AAV particles. Similarly, mispackaged rBV is also expected to display heterogeneity. Upon release from the viral particles, the packaged DNA forms a mixture containing various genome sizes and distinct locations for rBV-AAV. Consequently, mapping the copy number of each amplicon to its nucleotide position on the vector, coupled with area under the curve analysis, enables effective modeling of impurity levels in rBV regions relative to rAAV regions. Our results showed a high correlation with the SSV-Seq/Illumina method, indicating that both can accurately quantitate the rBV backbone DNA in rAAV products. The RNA-DNA hybrid capture method demonstrated that the mispackaged rBV DNA near the ITR regions exhibited strand specificity. The upstream region of the rBV DNA mainly consisted of the plus strand, while the downstream region primarily contained the minus strand near the ITR. As a result, most mispackaged DNA in these regions does not form double-stranded structures after being released from viral particles, whereas most rAAV genomes tend to form double strands rather than single strands. It raises questions about the accuracy of the SMRT/PacBio sequencing method that requires double-strand templates.24 Though Oxford Nanopore is capable of sequencing single-stranded templates, its efficiency for these templates is more than 50 times lower compared to double-stranded templates.22 SMRT/PacBio and Oxford Nanopore sequencing methods also display short-fragment bias, which might profile DNA species in rAAV products but struggle with accurate quantitation. Although the SSV-Seq/Illumina method cannot sequence the full length of packaged DNA, it can still accurately determine percentages of mispackaged DNA in rAAV products. However, the SSV-Seq/Illumina method is costly, requiring expensive equipment and complex data analysis, and has a 1+ day turnaround time, making it unsuitable for routine use. Multiplex dPCR involves initial optimization to establish a set of amplicons but could be developed as a platform method. The vector system remains consistent across projects, only needing amplicon modifications in the GOI region. After development and validation, multiplex dPCR only has 12 reactions, suitable for regular labs. As SSV-Seq/Illumina and multiplex dPCR accurately quantitate the rBV DNA impurity with high correlation, both methods could be used for rAAV product release. Our data show a relationship between the ratio of rBV-rAAV to rBV-Rep/Cap and the rBV DNA impurity and GOI content in rAAV products. Multiplex dPCR does not require expensive instruments; the ratio of RS10 (amplicon in mini-F region) to RS1 (amplicon near the right ITR) with a reference control could monitor the production process, provide timely feedback, improve processes, reduce DNA impurity, and increase GOI genome content in rAAV products.

Previous data indicated a high likelihood of neighboring ITR regions being mispackaged but did not explain the mechanism. Our multiplex dPCR data confirm these findings. Most importantly, our strand-specific probes with the RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD method demonstrate that the mispackaged rBV backbone shows a strand bias—downstream of the rAAV genome with a minus strand and upstream with a plus strand. This suggests that rBV backbone DNA is mispackaged from the 3′ end to the 5′ end in rAAV particles, plateauing at 5 kb, which indicates that the ITR is used as the packaging signal and mediates rBV DNA reverse packaging. The multiplex dPCR data also found that the mini-F region was highly mispackaged in rAAV particles, while other regions of the rBV backbone had much lower levels of mispackaging. The data demonstrated that ITRs and bacterial replication origin were the most significant mispackaging signals. Other regions might be mispackaged due to random mechanisms; even if some specific sequences might lead to favorable mispackaging, the signal might be weak. Contrary to previous findings,18 our data do not show smaller vector sizes having higher baculoviral backbone DNA impurity with 13 vectors tested. Post baculovirus infection of Sf9 cells, the rAAV genome is rescued from the rBV-rAAV to replicate and produce replicating intermediates packaged into pre-formed AAV capsids. The predominant source of packaged AAV vector genomes is these replicating intermediates. If the two ITRs are intact, the replicating intermediates only contain the two ITR-surrounded rAAV genomes. rBV DNA from infections or new synthesis could be used as templates for the reverse packaging, albeit at low levels. Both ITRs are potential packaging signals for rBV DNA for vector sizes less than 4.7 kb. For instance, rBV DNA on the upstream of the rAAV genome can be packaged from both left ITR (nearby ITR) and right ITR (distant ITR). The left ITR-mediated rBV DNA mispackaging should not be impacted by the vector size. Only the right ITR-mediated mispackaging could pass the left ITR and mispackage the rBV DNA in addition to the intended AAV DNA. If possible, it would only occur with a vector size of less than 4.7 kb. We observed that the ratio of the region near ITR (LS-1-C and RS-1) over gp64 (LS-12) was higher with vectors <4.5 kb than with the other vectors (Table S2). However, our data suggest that the DNA region distant from the rAAV genome (purple area in the area under the curve analyses), rather than nearby (red area in the area under the curve analyses, Figures 7B and 7C), determines the difference in rBV DNA impurity levels in rAAV products produced in the same system. Even if the distant ITR could mediate rBV mispackaging, the absolute amount would be very low and would not greatly impact the total DNA impurity. However, if the ITRs have mutations or deletions, particularly around the terminal resolution site (trs) or D sequence region, the rAAV genome cannot be properly rescued and packaged, causing a higher rate of vector backbone replication and mispackaging in rAAV particles.19,28,29,30 Smaller vector sizes may increase vector backbone impurities only if ITRs are impaired. In one instance, a self-complementary rAAV vector with a trs deletion in one ITR displayed up to 26% vector DNA impurity,31 indicating the significant influence of ITR integrity on DNA impurity.

After rAAV administration, only genomes with two ITRs can form circular monomers or concatemers and establish long-term transduction.32,33 Genomes without two ITRs could be rapidly degraded.34 Our data indicate that mispackaged DNA does not contain equal levels of plus and minus strands, at least in the regions flanking the ITRs, with most strands not forming double strands, lacking ITR, or having only one ITR, leading to rapid degradation and minimal safety concerns. Our data indicate that full-length kanamycin and gentamicin were detected in the rAAV product at about 1.5e9 copies per mL, representing 0.025% relative to GFP copies (CORE III, 6.0e12 copies/mL, Table 1). Caution should be exercised, particularly where the antibiotic genes kanamycin and gentamicin reside in the 5 kb region near the rAAV genome. We propose several strategies to reduce mispackaged DNA. First, our data indicate that the ratio of rBV-rAAV to rBV-Rep/cap plays a crucial role in DNA impurity. Optimizing this ratio could increase the rAAV genome and reduce mispackaged DNA. Current data from the HEK293/triple transfection system has validated this possibility.35 Second, mini-F mediates high DNA impurity. The kanamycin and gentamicin genes within the 5 kb region of the rAAV genome show high levels in the rAAV product. These impurities could be reduced by using a homogeneous recombinant system that limits kanamycin, gentamicin, and mini-F DNA sequences in the vectors. Published data have shown that this can limit these genes in rAAV products.36 Third, the 5 kb region near the rAAV genome has the highest chance of mispackaging. It has been suggested to insert well-known, inert stuffer DNA into some rAAV vectors to enhance homogeneity or safety profiles.18 These sequences could be placed just outside the ITRs. While these DNA fragments might inadvertently be packaged into rAAV, they would present fewer potential safety issues. Lastly, secondary structures could impact DNA replication37 and packaging.38 Introducing DNA sequences with certain secondary structures could modify replication or packaging to reduce mispackaging.

It is recommended to limit residual host-cell DNA and reduce size to below approximately 200 bp.39 However, a significant portion of residual DNA is encapsulated within the rAAV particles, rendering it resistant to DNase digestion. Research indicates that mispackaged DNA sizes range from several hundred base pairs to 5 kb. While viral particles containing very small DNA fragments may be reduced during purification, those with larger fragments cannot be easily removed by CsCl gradient ultracentrifugation or ion exchange methods. Our study demonstrated that full-length KanR and GentaR genes, which are much larger than 200 bp, were present in the AAV particles at concentrations reaching 1.5e9 copies/mL. These data underscore the difficulty in residual DNA removal. Furthermore, our investigations revealed that residual baculovirus DNA impurity ranged from 0.2% to 1% in the 13 GFP rAAV products examined, with some published data indicating up to 26% impurity in certain packaging system/vectors.31 Systemic doses require 1e14 viral genome copies (vg)/kg or more, and even a 0.1% impurity could mean high levels of residual DNA impurities. It is imperative to collect additional data spanning various production platforms and purification processes to better characterize such impurities. Moreover, extensive in vitro and in vivo functional studies are required to thoroughly evaluate the safety risks associated with residual DNA.

It is important to note that we have only assessed rBV DNA impurities using the two-Bac system. The mechanism responsible for residual DNA impurity may differ for other expression systems. It is necessary to evaluate each production system and purification process individually to elucidate the mechanisms causing elevated levels of DNA impurities and to identify solutions for reducing them.

Materials and methods

Baculovirus process

AAV9 vectors containing the eGFP gene with various sizes of stuffer DNA were produced using the baculovirus production system. The AAV genome was cloned into a shuttle plasmid, which was then used to generate a recombinant baculovirus through the transformation of DH10Bac-competent cells and transfection of the purified bacmid DNA into Sf9 cells. Sf9 cells were co-infected with the recombinant baculoviruses carrying AAV-eGFP genomes and AAV2rep/AAV9 cap inserts. Three days post infection, the Sf9 cells were harvested and lysed in Sf9 lysis buffer (0.5% V/V TX-100; Sigma-Aldrich, 33 mM Tris-Base, 2 mM MgCl2, and 200 mM NaCl) containing benzonase (50 U/mL; Millipore). Genomic DNA was digested by incubation at 37°C for 1 hour, after which cell debris was removed via centrifugation. Full AAV vector particles in the cleared lysates were purified using AAV9 affinity ligand resin (Thermo Scientific). vg titer was quantified by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) with amplicons in the eGFP and WPRE regions.

dPCR and data analyses

Primers and probes were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies. All sequences are listed in Table S3. Primer stocks were prepared at 200 μM, and probe stocks were prepared at 100 μM in TE buffer. A 12× multiplex primer and probe mixture was made by mixing primers at a concentration of 15 μM and probes at 7.5 μM (final concentration). Each multiplex mixture included primer/probe sets for 3 to 4 amplicons. The following reagents were used: QIACuity Probe Master Mix (QIAGEN, 250102), QIAcuity Nanoplate 8.5 96 well (QIAGEN, 250021), Proteinase K (Promega, MC5008), and DNase I (NEB, M0303L).

The virus was treated with DNase I (34 μL H2O plus 0.1% Pluronic F68, 5 μL of 10× DNase I buffer, 10 μL virus stock, plus 1 μL enzyme) at 37°C for 30 min, then treated with Proteinase K (50 μL DNase I-treated virus sample, 37.5 μL H2O, 10× Proteinase K buffer [1.0% SDS, 10 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 10 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.0], 2.5 μL Proteinase K) at 55°C for 10 min, and lysed at 95°C for an additional 10 min.

The virus lysate was further diluted in PCR dilution buffer (9.8 mL DNA suspension buffer [Teknova, T0223], 100 μL of 10% Pluronic F-68, 100 μL of 10 mg/mL Poly A [Sigma, 10108626001]). The dPCR mixture setup included 50 μL of dPCR master mixture, 60 μL H2O, and 10 μL multiplex primer/probe mixture, sufficient for 8 wells. Transfers of 4 μL of diluted virus and 12 μL of dPCR mixture were made to a new 96-well low DNA-binding plate, mixed well, and then 13 μL was transferred to the QIAcuity Nanoplate and sealed.

The lowest final dilution factors for the amplicons in the rAAV region, the rBV near the ITR region, and areas distant from the ITR were 480,000, 240, and 2,400, respectively, following a 3-fold serial dilution with a total of six doses and two negative controls (NTCs). The dPCR was performed on the QIAcuity Eight with the program: 95°C for 12 min (1 cycle), followed by 40 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 15 s. The amplification and quantification of the full-length kanamycin and gentamicin genes were performed using the following program: an initial denaturation at 95°C for 12 min (1 cycle), followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 2 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min (1 cycle).

For data analysis, thresholds were set above negative partitions and below positive partitions using NTCs as references to determine copies/μL for each well, correcting for dilution factors. Data acceptance criteria included NTCs less than or equal to 1 copies/well, positive partitions greater than 10 copies/well, linearity compatibility checks, and selecting dilution factor corrected points within the linear range where the percentage of the lowest/highest value is ≥ 70% to determine the average concentration of test samples. The average concentration of test samples was calculated and presented as copies/mL.

Area under the curve analysis was conducted by plotting the copy number of each amplicon on the y axis against the middle nucleotide locations of the amplicons within the rBV-rAAV vector on the x axis. The rAAV genome amplicon proximal to the ITR was excluded due to ITR interference. The copy numbers at the junction between the rBV backbone and the rAAV genome were determined as follows: the copy number at the final point of the rBV was set to match the last amplicon in the rBV backbone region, while the initial and final points of the rAAV genome were assigned half of the highest copy number observed among the rAAV amplicons.40

High-throughput RNA-DNA hybrid capture multiplex MSD method

This method has been detailed in another publication.40

Coating U-Plex MSD plate with RNA oligos

First, 4 μL of a 10 μM probe was diluted into 200 μL of diluent 100 buffer. Then, 300 μL of linker was added, mixed thoroughly, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature with gentle shaking for 30 min. The final probe concentration was 80 nM. After this, 200 μL of stop buffer was added, and the incubation continued for another 30 min. All ten probe-linker pairs were combined, mixed well, plated 50 μL per well onto the MSD U-Plex plate, and incubated at room temperature for 1 h.

DNA standard and virus preparation

Plasmid DNA and virus were diluted with PCR diluent (1×TE pH8.0 + 0.01% Tween 80). Then, 50 μL of 1 N NaOH was added to each well of a PCR plate, followed by 100 μL of the diluted DNA or virus. The mixture was pipetted for thorough mixing, incubated at 95°C for 30 min, and then quickly cooled to 4°C for further processing.

Hybridization and capture

The assay plate was washed four times with 500 μL PBST (PBS + 0.1% Tween 20) using a plate washer. Next, 50 μL of 2× hybridization buffer was added to the coated plate, followed by the transfer of 50 μL of the denatured sample. The plate was then sealed, vortexed thoroughly, spun down at 2,000 rpm for 1 min, and incubated at 42°C for 90 min on a microplate vortexer at 600 rpm.

Detection

The assay plate was washed 4 times with 500 μL PBST. It was then incubated with 50 μL of anti-RNA-DNA antibody S9.6 (diluted 1:750 in PBSTEG [PBST + 4mM EDTA pH8.0 + 5% Goat serum]) for 1 h at room temperature. Following incubation, the plate was washed 4 times with 500 μL PBST. Next, it was incubated with 50 μL Sulfo-Tag goat anti-mouse antibody (diluted 1:500 in PBSTEG) for 1 h at room temperature. The plate was washed 4 times with 500 μL PBST, rinsed once with 200 μL PBSTE (PBST + 5mM EDTA pH8.0), and discarded PBSTE. Finally, 100 μL MSD Gold read buffer B was added, and the reading was performed on an MSD QuickPlex SQ.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using MSD Discovery Workbench by fitting it to a standard curve, and the results were then transferred into Excel. Linearity was verified by utilizing only points within the linear range with ≥70% compatibility to determine the average concentration of test samples.

Illumina sequencing and analysis

Sequencing was performed using a protocol similar to the published SSV-Seq 2.0.41,42 DNA was extracted from AAV samples by processing 50 μL of each virus (titers: 5.7E+12 vg/mL – 1.09E+13 vg/mL) through PCR purification columns (QIAGEN, 28104) and quantified with the Qubit ssDNA kit (Thermo Fisher, Q10212).

After DNA extraction, second-strand synthesis was performed using random hexamers. The reaction consisted of 600 ng extracted AAV DNA diluted in water to 28 μL, 5 μL random primer mix (NEB, S1330S), 10 μL dNTP solution mix (NEB, N0447S), 2 μL DNA Polymerase I, Large (Klenow) Fragment (NEB, M0212S), and 5 μL 10× NEB2 buffer (NEB B7002S). The thermal cycler program used was: 4°C for 1 s, 37°C for 1 h with a 0.1°C/sec ramp rate, followed by a 4°C hold.

Library preparation was carried out on 25 μL of prepared AAV DNA samples with the NEB Next Ultra II FS PCR-Free Library Prep Kit (NEB, E7805L) according to the manufacturer’s high-input protocol. These samples were then quantified using the Qubit 1× dsDNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher, Q33231), normalized, denatured, diluted, and loaded onto an Illumina MiniSeq as per the MiniSeq denature and dilute guide, and finally sequenced with 2×150 bp paired-end sequencing using the MiniSeq High Output Reagent Kit (Illumina, FC-420-1003).

Bioinformatics analysis was conducted to determine the percentage of reads aligning to either vector genome, rBV genome, or other potential process-related residual DNAs. BBDuk masked residual DNA reference files for the AAV vector genome sequence. Sequencing reads underwent quality control checks and trimming using fastp software before aligning to the vector genome reference with BBMap. Unmapped reads were extracted and aligned separately to the residual DNA references using BBDuk. The count of reads aligning to each reference file, or remaining unaligned, was used to calculate the percent abundance of each DNA source.
