Health care resource utilization and costs of major bleeding in patients with and without dialysis-dependent end-stage kidney disease: a retrospective study
Authors: Nicholas S. Roetker, Alejandro Victores, Chuanyu Kou, Lori D. Bash, Dena Rosen Ramey, Xuehua Ke, Marc P. Bonaca, James B. Wetmore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMajor bleeding is common in dialysis-dependent end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).ObjectivesTo compare healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) and costs of major bleeding events between dialysis and non-dialysis populations.MethodsWe identified fee-for-service Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥66 years with a first (index) major bleeding event in 2015-2018. Patients with ESKD receiving in-center hemodialysis (HD) and home dialysis from the US Renal Data System were each compared to patients without ESKD from a 20% Medicare sample. HCRU and cost outcomes were compared using model-based standardization, adjusted for age, sex, and race, during the index major bleeding event and a 1-year follow-up period.ResultsPatients receiving in-center HD had index major bleeding hospitalizations that were longer and costlier (adjusted mean differences: 0.7 days [95% CI, 0.6-0.8] and $3.4K [95% CI, $3.2K-$3.7K]) than those without ESKD. During 1-year follow-up, bleeding-related hospitalizations were more common (adjusted rate difference: 37.6 per 100 person-years [95% CI, 35.2-40.1]) and costly (adjusted per-person per-year cost difference: $6.2K [95% CI, $5.8K-$6.7K]) in patients receiving in-center HD than in those without ESKD. Other than blood transfusions, which were more common in home dialysis than in-center HD (adjusted rates per 100 person-years: 255.8 [95% CI, 241.8-269.8] vs 202.1 [95% CI, 199.2-205.0]), HCRU outcomes were generally similar between the dialysis groups.ConclusionPatients receiving dialysis had longer and costlier major bleeding hospitalizations and accrued substantially higher costs after 1 year versus those without ESKD. Readmissions were a key driver of higher HCRU and costs in ESKD.

Introduction

Risk of bleeding increases as kidney function worsens [[1], [2], [3]]. Reasons for this are incompletely understood, but kidney disease is associated with platelet dysfunction, vessel wall and endothelial damage, imbalances of the coagulation cascade [4], and the development of arteriovenous malformations in the gastrointestinal tract [5]. Individuals with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) receiving maintenance hemodialysis (HD) appear to be at much higher risks of gastrointestinal bleeding [2], subarachnoid hemorrhage [3], and subdural hematoma [4] than individuals without ESKD. In addition, factors related to the administration of HD itself contribute to bleeding risk. For example, most patients undergo thrice-weekly cannulation of an arteriovenous access (fistula or graft) with a large-bore needle while receiving systemic anticoagulation with heparin, and long-term platelet activation by the hemodialyzer and associated tubing may also increase bleeding risk [6]. Individuals with kidney disease, including those receiving maintenance dialysis, also have a relatively high prevalence of cardiovascular conditions warranting treatment with antithrombotic medications [7,8], which may in turn further increase bleeding risk.

Kidney disease, particularly ESKD requiring maintenance dialysis, and its associated complications are resource intensive and costly. With progressively worsening chronic kidney disease (CKD), health care resource utilization (HCRU) and costs increase [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. The largest contributor to these costs are inpatient admissions, which increase as CKD progresses [4]. Given the high prevalence of ESKD requiring dialysis [7], HCRU and associated costs are of major importance to patients, clinicians, policymakers, payers, and other stakeholders.

Despite the well-documented risk of bleeding associated with kidney disease, the extent to which bleeding-related HCRU and costs differ between those receiving maintenance dialysis and those without ESKD remains an area of uncertainty. It is plausible that a bleeding event is more resource intensive and costly in a person receiving dialysis than that in one without ESKD, perhaps due to factors that may complicate treatment, prolong hospitalization, or predispose to a rehospitalization following the bleeding event. To investigate this hypothesis, we quantified the difference in HCRU and costs following a bleeding event among fee-for-service (FFS) Medicare beneficiaries receiving maintenance dialysis and beneficiaries without ESKD. We hypothesized that HCRU and costs associated with a bleeding event would be substantially higher among those receiving maintenance dialysis than those among patients without ESKD.

Methods

Data sources and study design

This study used 2014-2019 data for FFS Medicare beneficiaries with and without ESKD from the United States Renal Data System (USRDS) ESKD database and a 20% Medicare random sample, respectively. The USRDS comprises a complete registry of patients with registered ESKD in the United States. Details on these databases are provided in Supplementary Item 1. The databases were used to construct a cohort of patients without ESKD (ie, including those without CKD and those with non–dialysis-dependent CKD) and a cohort of patients with dialysis-dependent ESKD, excluding kidney transplant recipients. The latter was further divided into 2 subcohorts based on receipt of in-center HD or home dialysis (ie, peritoneal dialysis or home HD). Patients included in each cohort were those who experienced a major bleeding event (defined further) in 2015-2018. The index date was defined as the admission date of the first major bleeding event during this period. A baseline period was defined as the 1-year period before the index date. Additional details on the inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in Supplementary Item 1.

A follow-up period of up to 1 year was defined as the time from the index date to the earliest of the following dates: death; kidney transplantation; start of chronic dialysis (in the non-ESKD only); switch between in-center and home dialysis modality (in the ESKD cohort only); disenrolment from Medicare Parts A, B, or D; December 31, 2019; or after 365 days.

The institutional review board at Hennepin Healthcare approved the research protocol (study ID: IRB-FY2022-386) and waived the requirement for informed consent for this retrospective study.

Major bleeding events

This study includes only FFS Medicare beneficiaries who experienced a major bleeding event. To identify these events, we first identified candidate nontraumatic bleeding-related hospitalizations using an adaptation of a claims-based algorithm [9]. The full algorithm is shown in Supplementary Table 1. Major bleeding events were defined as the subset of the candidate bleeding events that were fatal, accompanied by a blood transfusion, or involved a critical site, as has been done in previous claims-based studies [10].

HCRU and cost outcomes

We assessed HCRU and cost outcomes for the index major bleeding event itself and separately, in the 1-year follow-up period. Outcomes related to the index event included the inpatient length of stay (LOS) and total cost, with the latter defined as the total cost paid by Medicare for the index inpatient hospital claim.

Outcomes in the 1-year follow-up period, which excluded the index event, were only assessed among the subset of individuals with a nonfatal index event. Follow-up HCRU outcomes included the following: the frequency of inpatient hospital readmission within 30 days of the index hospitalization discharge; numbers of inpatient hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) admissions and total number of days in hospital (excluding the index hospitalization); and numbers of observation unit, emergency department (ED), outpatient, other institutional, and physician encounters within 1 year of the index date. Definitions for these outcomes are described in Supplementary Table 2.

Cost outcomes in the 1-year follow-up period included Medicare payments for the following claim types: inpatient hospital (excluding costs of the index hospitalization), outpatient, other institutional, physician or laboratory, durable medical equipment, and prescription drugs. Total health care costs were calculated as the sum of the costs for all aforementioned claim types. Costs were also reported separately for outpatient claims related to encounters in the observation unit and ED. All cost outcomes were expressed in 2019 USD after adjusting for inflation [11].

In the follow-up period, we reported both bleeding-related and all-cause HCRU and cost outcomes. We defined bleeding-related HCRU as Medicare Part A or B claims with a diagnosis code from the list in section I of Supplementary Table 1 in any position or Medicare Part D dispensed prescriptions for proton pump inhibitors, which are commonly used in patients at high risk of upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

When defining HCRU and cost outcomes in the ESKD cohorts, all outpatient claims from dialysis facilities were excluded as these claims reflect routine care that is intrinsic and unique to patients receiving maintenance dialysis.

Baseline characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, race, and dual enrollment status in Medicare and Medicaid. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [12], each of the individual CCI conditions [13], and atrial fibrillation were defined using the claims-based algorithms shown in Supplementary Table 3. To characterize background levels of HCRU and cost, we also defined the number of hospital admissions and total, all-cause health care costs (excluding costs related to outpatient dialysis) paid by Medicare Parts A, B, and D in the 1-year baseline period.

Statistical analysis

Bzaseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. LOS and total cost of the index major bleeding event were reported using means, and fatal index events, index ICU admission, and 30-day hospital readmission were each reported using percentages. Outcomes in the 1-year follow-up period were estimated as the sum of the total number of encounters, or costs, divided by the total number of person-years of follow-up. Index event–related HCRU or costs were not included when estimating the follow-up outcomes. Follow-up HCRU outcomes were reported using rates, and cost outcomes were reported as costs per person per year (PPPY) in 2019 USD.

All HCRU and cost outcomes were adjusted for age, sex, and race using model-based standardization [14], unless noted otherwise, as fully described in Supplementary Item 1 and Supplementary Table 4. To allow for direct comparisons of outcomes for each of the ESKD cohorts vs the non-ESKD cohort, we estimated differences in means, percentages, rates, and PPPY costs. Bootstrapping with 1000 replicate samples was performed to obtain 95% CIs.

During the hospital stay, about half of patients in each cohort (non-ESKD: 50.5%; in-center HD: 51.4%; home dialysis: 48.5%) required admission to an ICU. However, the adjusted percentage of in-hospital mortality during the major bleeding event was slightly lower in patients receiving in-center HD (7.8%; adjusted difference, −0.7%; 95% CI, −1.2% to −0.1%) or home dialysis (7.1%; adjusted difference, −1.3%; 95% CI, −3.0% to 0.3%) than in patients without ESKD (8.4%; data not shown).

The unadjusted mean Medicare cost for the index event hospitalization was $16.4K for individuals receiving in-center HD, $15.6K for those receiving home dialysis, and $12.2K for those without ESKD (data not shown). After adjustment for age, sex, and race, the mean index hospitalization cost was $15.6K for patients receiving either in-center HD or home dialysis, which was 28% higher than in patients without ESKD ($12.2K; adjusted difference: $3.4K; 95% CI, $3.2K-$3.7K) (Figure 1B). In a sensitivity analysis that was fully adjusted for age, sex, race, Medicaid (dual) eligibility, comorbid conditions, CCI, number of baseline inpatient hospital admissions, and total baseline health care costs, differences in index event costs between cohorts were only slightly attenuated (eg, 23% higher for in-center HD [$15.0K] vs non-ESKD [$12.2k]; adjusted difference: $2.9K; 95% CI, $2.5K-$3.3K) (Figure 1C).

Thirty-day hospital readmission after the index event

Among individuals with a nonfatal index major bleeding event, the adjusted incidence of all-cause hospital readmission within 30 days of discharge from the index hospital stay was ∼1.6 times as high in patients receiving dialysis (29.6% [in-center HD] and 28.9% [home dialysis]) as in patients without ESKD (18.2%; adjusted differences, 11.4% [95% CI, 10.5%-12.2%] and 10.7% [95% CI, 7.9%-13.4%], respectively) (Figure 2). Inpatient hospitalizations accounted for the largest proportion of both bleeding and all-cause costs (62%-67% and 46-52%, respectively) in patients receiving dialysis (Figure 5). Discussion

Major bleeding is an especially important cause of morbidity, mortality, and HCRU for individuals receiving maintenance dialysis. To quantify and contextualize the HCRU and costs associated with major bleeding in patients with ESKD, we compared these outcomes between patients receiving maintenance dialysis and patients without ESKD using a nationally representative sample of Medicare FFS beneficiaries. We found that, compared with beneficiaries without ESKD, beneficiaries on dialysis had longer and costlier hospitalizations for major bleeding. Among those who survived the index major bleeding event, those on dialysis had higher rates of all-cause and bleeding-related hospital readmissions, as well as higher rates of bleeding-related use of the ICU, the ED, and the observation unit in the ensuing year.

Previous studies have demonstrated that individuals receiving dialysis tend to have longer and costlier hospitalizations, relative to those without ESKD, when hospitalization-requiring bleeds occur. For example, in a study using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS), Lui et al [15] reported that individuals with ESKD who experienced an intracerebral hemorrhage had hospital stays 1.5 times as long as those without ESKD (15 vs 10 days) despite having only trivially higher stroke severity index scores. Similarly, in a study of bleeding peptic ulcers using the NIS, mean LOS was >2 days longer among individuals with ESKD (8.2 days) vs those without (6.0 days) [16]. In 2 studies using the NIS that examined both hospital LOS and the inpatient costs associated with gastrointestinal bleeds, both outcomes were higher among individuals with ESKD. Indeed, costs were fully 50% higher: $54,668 vs $32,869 in 1 study [17] and $48,287 vs $32,141 in another [18].

Several distinct mechanisms likely drive the prolonged hospitalizations and elevated costs observed in the dialysis population. From a physiological standpoint, uremic platelet dysfunction and chronic anemia leave patients with ESKD with less physiological reserve [19], potentially requiring more intensive monitoring and transfusion support during a bleed. Clinically, the management of major bleeding is further complicated by the need to strictly manage fluid balance, often requiring a delicate approach unique to the dialysis population, such as the judicious use of intravenous fluids for resuscitation or blood products (eg, fresh frozen plasma or platelets), which can contribute to volume overload [20]. Furthermore, the logistical necessity of coordinating inpatient dialysis treatments, which are themselves costly and time consuming, introduces administrative complexity that may delay discharge.

Our findings regarding 30-day hospital readmissions were similar to those of studies examining other causes of hospitalization. We found that among individuals receiving dialysis, ∼30% of live discharges following a bleeding hospitalization resulted in an all-cause readmission within 30 days, similar to the percentages reported for cardiovascular-related (34%) [21] or infection-related (30%) [22] hospitalizations. Further, the percentage of the non-ESKD cohort with a 30-day readmission in our study (18%) is similar to the reported percentages of Medicare beneficiaries rehospitalized following a gastrointestinal bleed (16%) [23] or a subarachnoid bleed (17%) [24], contributing to the face validity of our study. Collectively, these findings would suggest that individuals receiving dialysis may be especially vulnerable to the posthospitalization syndrome [25], a condition of generalized risk that results from acute illness and puts discharged patients at increased short-term risk for hospital readmission, at least relative to those without ESKD. Among individuals receiving dialysis, 36% to 38% of the 30-day readmissions were bleeding related, only modestly lower than for the analogous situation for cardiovascular disease (in which 43% of index cardiovascular admissions result in a cardiovascular-related readmission) [21].

As observed with HCRU, health care costs were also much higher in the year following a bleeding event among individuals receiving dialysis. Total (all-cause) costs, adjusted for inflation, were over twice as high among individuals receiving dialysis (≥$107,800 PPPY) than among those without ESKD ($51,600 PPPY); the subset of costs related to bleeding were nearly twice as high (>$20,000 PPPY vs $11,500). This stark cost differential exists even though we specifically excluded the costs of maintenance (outpatient) dialysis. Although there is no reason to suspect that this phenomenon is limited to individuals who experience a bleeding event, our findings, nevertheless, highlight the particularly high costs for treating adverse health outcomes in the dialysis population, many of whom have major comorbid conditions that may be exacerbated in the setting of a major bleed and that themselves may further amplify HCRU and costs.

For patients receiving dialysis, many of whom have cardiovascular comorbidities (eg, atrial fibrillation) warranting antithrombotic therapy, use of antithrombotic medications inherently entails risk of bleeding. Indeed, the optimal choice and dosing of anticoagulants in this population remains controversial, as clinicians must weigh the high risk of thromboembolism against the elevated risk of bleeding complications [[26], [27], [28]]. Thus, there is a need for new antithrombotic medications that can effectively protect against major adverse thrombotic events (eg, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, pulmonary embolism, systemic arterial embolism, deep vein thrombosis, systemic venous thrombosis, and acute limb ischemia), while minimizing the risk of bleeding in at-risk populations, such as those with kidney disease.

Outcomes including LOS, in-hospital mortality, use of the ICU, hospitalization cost, readmission percentage, and most markers of care in the 1-year follow-up were generally similar among the individuals receiving in-center HD and those receiving home dialysis. These findings may appear counterintuitive given the widespread perception that individuals receiving home dialysis are typically healthier and less costly to care for compared with individuals receiving in-center HD [29]. Our observations do not necessarily contradict this concept since they are based on a study cohort that was conditioned on the presence of an index bleeding event. Our findings suggest only that, among individuals on dialysis who experience a major bleeding event, HCRU and cost outcomes generally differ little by dialysis modality.

Additionally, we observed that 1-year rates of blood transfusion events and bleeding-related gastroenterology encounters were higher among individuals receiving home dialysis than among those receiving in-center HD. One potential explanation is that blood transfusions may be more easily avoided among individuals receiving thrice-weekly in-center HD care because their hemoglobin levels can be readily measured (particularly when bleeding is suspected), and likewise, erythropoietin-stimulating agents and intravenous iron can be administered more readily in the in-center HD environment. The ability to monitor laboratory values more closely and to readily adjust the therapies for anemia management among individuals receiving in-center HD may be responsible for these findings.

This study has limitations. Since fatal bleeding was one of the criteria for defining major bleeding, it is not advisable to directly compare rates of index event mortality between the different study groups. We did not have access to clinical information such as decreased hemoglobin levels, timing or acuity of decreases, or number of units of blood transfused; these may have affected the relative severity of bleeds across the selected study groups. For example, any bleeding event requiring a transfusion was classified as a major bleed and thus may have led to the inclusion of less severe bleeding events. This may have disproportionately affected those receiving dialysis, in whom transfusions are more common. This is an observational study, so we cannot rule out the possibility of residual confounding. Our modeling approach, which produced standardized outcome measures for each dialysis cohort, should be interpreted as if these patients had identical distributions of adjusted covariates to the non-ESKD cohort. Although we performed sensitivity analyses that were adjusted for a large variety of baseline characteristics, we defined these under the constraints of an administrative database, which is an imperfect way to assess comorbidity burden. Furthermore, the results from this study may not be generalizable to patients aged ≤65 years, patients with other types of insurance (eg, Medicare Advantage), or those who have received a kidney transplant; additionally, while the use of 2015-2019 data avoids confounding from the COVID-19 pandemic, findings may not reflect recent inflationary changes or practice patterns outside the United States.

In summary, we found that major bleeding events in beneficiaries receiving dialysis, relative to beneficiaries without ESKD, resulted in longer and more costly hospitalizations and were associated with higher rates of all-cause and bleeding-related hospital readmissions. All-cause and bleeding-related HCRU and costs in the 1-year follow-up were also substantially higher in beneficiaries receiving dialysis. Future studies are required to better understand the specific sources of increased bleeding risk in people with kidney disease. Such information is essential to refine bleeding risk stratification for people receiving dialysis, allowing providers and patients to engage in more informed shared decision making when prescribing antithrombotic therapy. Further, the development of therapeutics (eg, antithrombotics) that confer lower bleeding risk are urgently needed for people receiving dialysis and others at increased risk of bleeding.
