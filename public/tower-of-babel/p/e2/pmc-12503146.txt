Visceral Fat and Ossification of the Posterior Longitudinal Ligament
Authors: Tomoya Sato, Tsutomu Endo, Yoshinao Koike, Hideki Sudo, M. Alaa Terkawi, Huohuo Xue, Ryo Fujita, Soya Miura, Ryota Suzuki, Yukitoshi Shimamura, Masahiro Kanayama, Ken Kadoya, Katsuhisa Yamada, Daisuke Ukeba, Misaki Ishii, Norimasa Iwasaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) is relatively common in East Asian populations, with a recently revealed link to obesity. However, evidence linking OPLL with visceral fat obesity, which is prevalent in the Asian population, is insufficient. We aimed to examine the association between visceral fat obesity and the development of OPLL.Methods:In a single-center case-control study, data were collected from 120 Japanese patients diagnosed with OPLL and 91 controls without spinal ligament ossification identified during health screenings. From 2020 to 2023, all participants underwent computed tomography to assess visceral fat content and spinal ligament ossification. OPLL was classified as localized (cervical spine) or diffuse (thoracic/lumbar spine). Multivariable logistic regression was conducted to assess the effect size (odds ratio [OR]) of body mass index on the incidence of OPLL and to compare outcomes between groups with a high and low visceral/subcutaneous fat area (V/S) ratio.Results:The proportion of patients with visceral fat obesity was significantly higher in both the localized and diffuse OPLL groups than in the controls (58.9% vs. 64.1% vs. 25.2%, p < 0.05). Patients with OPLL had a higher rate of comorbid visceral fat obesity than the propensity score–matched controls did (56.8% vs. 18.1%, p < 0.001). The effect of BMI on the development of diffuse OPLL was 2.6-fold greater in the high V/S ratio group (OR, 9.50; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.11 to 42.71) than in the low V/S ratio group (OR, 3.56; 95% CI, 1.51-8.37).Conclusions:Visceral fat obesity was associated with the development of OPLL, particularly diffuse OPLL. The modifying effect of visceral fat accumulation with overweight status was more strongly associated with diffuse OPLL than was that of subcutaneous fat combined with an overweight status.Level of Evidence:Therapeutic Level III. Introduction

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) is a major cause of myelopathy in East Asian populations but is rare in European descent1-3. Localized-type OPLL (in the cervical spine) and diffuse-type OPLL (mainly in the thoracic spine) have different backgrounds4-6. Distinct features of diffuse-type OPLL include extensive ectopic ossification in the spinal ligaments and the coexistence of severe obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥35 kg/m2)4-6. Mendelian randomization analysis in a recent genome-wide association study (GWAS) of Japanese individuals has identified elevated BMI as a probable causative factor in the development of diffuse-type OPLL7. This analytical framework leverages the fact that genetic variants are randomly assigned at conception and unaffected by environmental or behavioral factors, thereby minimizing confounding and enabling robust causal inference. OPLL is also associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and dyslipidemia4-13, supported by US insurance-database analysis14, suggesting that endocrine and metabolic rather than mechanical factors may be involved in pathogenesis15.

Visceral fat obesity increases the risk of stroke, atherosclerosis, and T2D more than subcutaneous fat obesity does16-18. Although the prevalence of obesity in Japan is below the global average19, the age-adjusted mortality rate from cerebrovascular disease (2016) matches that in the United States and Europe20. East Asian populations, even if mildly obese, are more prone to visceral fat accumulation than other ethnic groups (e.g., Caucasians, Africans, Hispanics) and are consequently more susceptible to visceral fat–related diseases21-25. Accordingly, the Japanese Society for the Study of Obesity (JASSO) defines obesity in Japanese people as a BMI of ≥25 kg/m2 with visceral fat accumulation24,25. Given the low visceral fat tolerance in this population, the high OPLL incidence in Japanese individuals may be associated with metabolic and endocrine disorders, including weight gain. A recent study on asymptomatic Japanese individuals showed that a high BMI, modified by visceral fat, may mediate OPLL occurrence26. Based on this finding, we hypothesized that visceral fat accumulation may amplify the risk associated with elevated BMI in the development of OPLL, especially the diffuse type.

In this study, we examined how visceral fat obesity contributes to symptomatic OPLL. We investigated the relationship between visceral and subcutaneous fat accumulation and distinct OPLL subtypes by comparing patients with OPLL with controls without spinal ligament ossification, with a particular focus on the independent contribution of visceral fat accumulation relative to BMI.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

In this case-control study, we included 120 Japanese patients with OPLL (54 men, 66 women), for whom abdominal and whole-spine computed tomography (CT) data were available, and 91 Japanese controls (46 men, 45 women) without spinal ligament ossification (Fig. 1 and supplementary information).

Classification of OPLL Based on Localization

We divided patients with OPLL into 2 groups: 1) localized OPLL (localized to cervical spine; Fig. 2-A and Supplementary Information) and 2) diffuse OPLL (mainly located in thoracic and lumbar spine, but may be associated with cervical spine; Fig. 2-B and Supplementary information).

Measurement of Fat Area Using CT

Abdominal circumference (cm), total fat area (cm2), visceral fat area (cm2), subcutaneous fat area (cm2), and visceral/subcutaneous fat area (V/S) ratio were quantified using noncontrast axial CT images at the level of the umbilicus (Figs. 2-C and 2-D, and supplementary information).

Results

Prevalence of Visceral Fat Obesity in Localized and Diffuse OPLL Groups

The baseline characteristics of patients with OPLL and controls (Supplemental Table 1) were compared, as well as the characteristics of each OPLL group with those of the controls (Table I). The proportions of patients with comorbid hypertension, T2D, and dyslipidemia were significantly higher in the localized and diffuse OPLL groups than in the controls (all p < 0.01). The total fat area was significantly greater in the localized and diffuse OPLL groups than in the controls (p = 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively) and approximately 41.7% higher in the diffuse OPLL group than in the controls (355.9 vs. 251.0 cm2, p < 0.001). The visceral fat area was also significantly greater in both the localized and diffuse OPLL groups than in the controls (167.0 vs. 159.2 vs. 87.8 kg/m2, p < 0.001, respectively). The subcutaneous fat area was significantly greater in the diffuse OPLL group than in the controls (p = 0.02). Moreover, the V/S ratio was significantly higher in the localized and diffuse OPLL groups than in the controls (all p < 0.001).

Analysis of the Matched Cohort

Using propensity score matching, the comorbid rate of visceral fat obesity in the OPLL group was significantly higher than that in the matched controls (56.8% vs. 18.1%, p < 0.001) (Supplemental Table 2).

Effect of High Visceral Fat on Association Between BMI and OPLL

We used multivariable logistic regression to compare the effect size (odds ratio [OR]) of BMI on the development of OPLL against the background of visceral fat versus subcutaneous fat accumulation (Fig. 4). The effect of BMI on the development of OPLL was 2.0-fold greater in the high V/S ratio group (OR, 5.79; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.97 to 17.03) than in the low V/S ratio group (OR, 2.85; 95% CI, 1.41-5.79) and 1.9-fold greater in the high subcutaneous fat group than in the low subcutaneous fat group (OR, 5.42 vs. 2.79). For the development of localized OPLL, BMI had positive effect sizes in the high visceral fat and high subcutaneous fat groups (OR, 2.81 and 2.76, respectively). The effect of BMI on the development of diffuse OPLL was 2.6-fold greater in the high V/S ratio group (OR, 9.50; 95% CI, 2.11-42.71) than in the low V/S ratio group (OR, 3.56; 95% CI, 1.51-8.37). The effect size of BMI in the high V/S ratio group was 5.7-fold greater in the diffuse OPLL group than in the localized OPLL group (OR, 9.50 vs. 1.64).

Discussion

In contrast to a previous study on asymptomatic OPLL, we examined symptomatic patients, enabling a clinically relevant assessment of its association with visceral fat. We found that patients with OPLL had pronounced visceral fat accumulation, with a 2- to 2.5-fold higher prevalence of visceral fat obesity compared with controls. This result remained consistent when patients with OPLL were compared with propensity-matched controls. In addition, we found that visceral fat and BMI have a modifying effect on the development of OPLL, especially diffuse OPLL.

Given that BMI has been identified as a causal risk factor for diffuse-type OPLL via GWAS, our current findings suggest that visceral fat may function as a key modifier within this causal framework. Multivariable logistic regression analysis supported our hypothesis, revealing that the modifying effect of BMI combined with a high V/S ratio on the development of diffuse OPLL was 2.6-fold greater than that observed with a low V/S ratio and 5.7-fold greater than that on localized OPLL. Building upon previous findings in asymptomatic individuals with OPLL26, this study reveals that localized OPLL is also significantly influenced by the combined effect of visceral adiposity and BMI. These findings suggest that both localized OPLL and diffuse OPLL imply a shared mechanism through which visceral adiposity may facilitate OPLL progression. These observations reinforce the involvement of endocrine and metabolic factors linked to visceral fat obesity—beyond BMI-driven mechanical stress—in the pathogenesis of OPLL.

Also of note are the large absolute total fat area, subcutaneous fat area, and high BMI in patients with diffuse OPLL. The mean subcutaneous fat area of the diffuse OPLL group was 25.9% and 20.6% higher than that of the control and the localized OPLL group, respectively. The modifying effect of BMI with a high subcutaneous fat accumulation on the development of diffuse OPLL was 2.4-fold greater than that on the development of localized OPLL; thus, subcutaneous fat obesity may also be a risk factor for the development of OPLL. However, adipokines (including TNF-α and IL-6) produced by adipocytes are secreted from subcutaneous fat although the amount secreted is higher in visceral fat27. Visceral adipose tissue is prone to inflammation because progenitor adipocytes are less likely to differentiate into adipocytes and existing adipocytes are more likely to enlarge. Conversely, existing adipocytes in subcutaneous adipose tissue remain small, which makes insulin resistance less likely to develop28. Moreover, visceral adipose tissue, located within the portal vein system, releases fatty acids and inflammatory cytokines that are transmitted through the liver, contributing to systemic insulin resistance28. Based on these findings, patients with OPLL may be particularly susceptible to the influence of metabolic factors mediated by visceral fat, owing to unique adipose tissue metabolism and anatomic characteristics.

This study provides a new perspective on why OPLL is relatively common in East Asian populations1-3,12,13. Various factors, including genetic factors, lifestyle habits, dietary habits, and sex hormones, are potential risk factors for OPLL1-3,12-14. However, an important physical factor may be the lower tolerance for visceral fat accumulation in Japanese individuals compared with individuals of European descent21-25. Compared with individuals of European descent, Japanese individuals have a higher risk of developing hypertension and T2D, even if their BMI and waist circumference are the same21,29,30. Asian populations have a genetic predisposition to the accumulation of visceral fat and the decline in pancreatic β-cell function31. The World Health Organization recognizes these facts and pointed out that standards appropriate for Asians should be adopted32. A large-scale national health survey, conducted by the Japanese government, revealed obesity rates of 21.7%, 4.1%, and 0.9% for BMI ranges of 25 to 30 kg/m2, 30 to 35 kg/m2, and ≥35 kg/m2, respectively33. These rates are notably lower than those in the United States (30.7% and 41.9% for BMI ranges of 25-30 kg/m2 and ≥30 kg/m2, respectively)34. Patients with severe obesity (BMI ≥35 kg/m2), commonly seen in diffuse-type OPLL, differ significantly from average Japanese individuals (mean BMI, 22-23 kg/m2)33. Therefore, the high prevalence of OPLL in the Japanese population should not be attributed to the proportion of individuals with obesity. Instead, our finding that patients with OPLL accumulate excessive visceral fat indicates that East Asian populations—characterized by a genetic predisposition to visceral fat accumulation and physiologically lower tolerance for its metabolic consequences—may be prone to exposure to metabolic drivers of OPLL even under conditions of mild weight gain.

Among the metabolic factors potentially mediating the influence of visceral fat on OPLL development, leptin warrants particular attention as a molecular mechanism. Leptin, an adipokine mainly secreted by adipose tissue, acts on the hypothalamus to control appetite and metabolism35. It also acts directly on osteoblasts and chondrocytes to promote their proliferation and differentiation36,37. In Japanese Americans without diabetes, leptin levels are positively correlated with the amount of visceral fat accumulation38. Several reports have investigated the link between leptin and OPLL39,40, and leptin receptors have been identified in human OPLL tissue. Experimental studies have shown that leptin, in combination with mechanical stress, enhances osteogenic differentiation of OPLL cells through the MAPK, JAK2-STAT3, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways40. Moving forward, identifying molecular targets that can modulate both metabolic dysfunction and ectopic ossification may provide a new therapeutic strategy for OPLL.

Our study has several limitations. First, because of its cross-sectional design, we were unable to determine whether visceral obesity is a cause or consequence of OPLL. To clarify whether visceral obesity predicts the development of OPLL, obesity intervention trials targeting high-risk groups or prospective longitudinal studies are needed. Second, reduced mobility caused by severe obesity or myelopathy might have contributed to visceral fat accumulation in certain individuals. However, in this study, we observed modifying effects of visceral fat and BMI in patients with OPLL that were similar to those reported in asymptomatic individuals with radiographic evidence of OPLL in a previous study26. Because asymptomatic individuals typically maintain normal levels of physical activity, these findings support the notion that visceral adiposity is unlikely to be merely a secondary consequence of immobility. Third, our results, which are based on a Japanese database, may not be generalizable to other populations. Finally, selection bias might have occurred because we primarily included symptomatic patients who regularly attended the hospital. As such, the study cohort might have included a higher proportion of individuals with more severe symptoms.

Conclusions

We found that visceral fat obesity is associated with the development of OPLL. In this study, we focused on the possibility that the high prevalence of OPLL in Japanese populations may be due to physical characteristics prone to visceral fat accumulation. Particularly, the modifying effect of visceral fat accumulation and being overweight was more strongly associated with the development of diffuse-type OPLL than was the combined effect of subcutaneous fat accumulation and being overweight. Hence, visceral fat may be a modifiable factor with therapeutic significance for the prevention of OPLL.
