Optimization of BP neural network based on Harris Hawk algorithm to predict resistance spot welding quality
Authors: Shuwan Cui, Xuan Zhou, Haijun Xu, Suojun Zhang, Wengang Chen, Feiyang Liu, Xiaozhen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResistance spot welding (RSW), which is still extensively used in the automobile, aerospace, and other equipment production industries, depends critically on the quality of the weld. The size of the welded joint nucleus diameter was used as the output of the spot welding quality monitoring model, with welding current, welding voltage, welding air pressure, and welding time selected as input parameters. This study developed a neural network-based online monitoring model for resistance spot welding. The Harris Hawk Herd Algorithm Optimized BP Neural Network (HHOBP) neural network model, which is the basis for the spot welding quality prediction model, has an R2 coefficient value of up to 0.9981 and may somewhat predict the quality of welded connections. Compared with PSO-BP, GWO-BP, CS-BP, GA-BP, traditional BPNN and support vector machine models, HHOBP has achieved 93.06% ~ 96.15% (MAE), 99.43% ~ 99.81% (MSE), 92.18% ~ 95.46% (RMSE) and 87.54% ~ 96.83% (MAPE) in different error indicators, reflecting excellent modeling capabilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42706-6.Keywords: Resistance Spot Welding, HHOBP Neural Network, Online MonitoringSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Due to its effectiveness, affordability, and adaptability for automation, Resistance Spot Welding (RSW) is a crucial joining technology in sectors such as vehicle production1. Figure 1 illustrates resistance spot welding. However, RSW quality exhibits significant fluctuations due to the process’s inherent nonlinearity, multivariable coupling, and susceptibility to disturbances, e.g., electrode wear, material variations2,3. This instability can lead to defects like undersized nuggets, compromising product reliability4–6. The nugget diameter is a critical indicator of RSW quality4.

Resistance spot welding process diagram2.

Traditional quality assessment methods, often involving destructive testing or manual inspection, are inefficient, costly, and unsuitable for real-time monitoring7,8. Because of this, research has shifted to using data-driven models for realtime prediction together with in-process signals including dynamic resistance, electrode displacement, and force9–16. Acherjee et al. looked into the electrochemical machining (ECM) procedure17. By applying a pollen pollination algorithm (FPA) to optimize the machining settings, the intended process performance was attained. The pollen pollination algorithm’s goal function was a regression equation that was created using experimental data. Finding the ideal set of process parameters for both single and multiple objectives was the goal. Because no parameter needs to be maintained as a constant during the analysis, the experimental findings demonstrate that the FPA can forecast the actual overall parameter changes. FPA is preferred for process improvements and parameter trend prediction because it can discover the global optimum and no parameter remains constant during the optimization process18. For the purpose of to improve the machining settings, Acherjee et al.19 also conducted a study utilizing the ultrasonic machining (USM) technique. To accomplish the desired process response, two potent meta-heuristic algorithms based on population and swarm intelligence inspired by nature were used: Cuckoo Search (CS) and Chicken Swarm Optimization (CSO). It was determined that the CS and CSO algorithms predicted better single- and multi-objective optimization results as compared to the Gravitational Search Algorithm (GSA), Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization Algorithm (PSO), Ant Colony Optimization Algorithm (ACO), and Artificial Bee Colony Algorithm (ABC). The results were identical to those predicted by the Fireworks Algorithm (FWA). In light of their powerful nonlinear fitting capabilities, Backpropagation Neural Networks (BPNNs) have demonstrated potential in this field [20]. Nevertheless, standard BPNNs suffer from drawbacks including sensitivity to initial weights, difficulty in hyperparameter tuning, and a tendency to converge to local minima, limiting their prediction accuracy and robustness21,22.

A parallel and impactful research direction involves establishing process windows or capability spaces to guide parameter selection. For instance, in laser welding, studies have successfully utilized finite element models to correlate process parameters with intermediate performance indicators (IPIs), creating contour maps for rapid process assessment and refinement23. Similarly, comprehensive frameworks integrating multi-physics simulation and sequential modeling have been proposed to define process capability spaces for remote laser welding, enabling robust parameter selection that satisfies multiple key performance indicators (KPIs) while minimizing experimental effort24. These approaches highlight the critical need to translate process models into actionable guidance for practitioners.

Since neural networks and process parameters have been effectively optimized in a variety of industrial domains using meta-heuristic optimization methods (such as GA, PSO, CS, and Support Vector Machines (SVM))25–28, these algorithms have the capacity to handle difficult issues. There is still a significant research gap in the specific context of RSW quality prediction. A revolutionary and potent meta-heuristic algorithm, Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) is renowned for its strong global search capabilities, quick convergence, and effectively balance between exploration and exploitation29–31. Table 1 lists the main differences between HHO and popular metaheuristic methods in neural networks, from which we can see the advantages of HHO algorithm. Notably, the application of HHO to optimize BPNNs for predicting RSW nugget diameter has not yet been explored. Existing RSW prediction models often lack sufficient accuracy and generalization ability to meet the demands of modern high-precision manufacturing.

Methodology

Choosing materials and setting up the experiment

This study includes a variety of metal sheets, including Q235, carbon structural steel; DP590 and DP780, cold rolled duplex steels; DC03 and DC07, cold rolled mild steels; and B1500HS, hot-stamped boron steel. In recent years, hot stamped steels have become increasingly popular among automotive manufacturers because they allow for the construction of lightweight vehicles without compromising passenger safety. In spite of its continuous yielding, low yield tensile ratio, high ductility, and strength, dual-phase (DP) steels are widely used in the automobile industry32. Car roof rails, rear rails, inner body panels, and vibration-damping reinforcement are all made of DP780 steel33. In developing nations like India, where safety concerns and lightweight measures are crucial, the usage of DP780 has grown significantly. Excellent deep-drawing properties are possessed by DC03 steel. Because of its exceptional mechanical qualities, Q235 steel is the preferred material in many industries, such as the automotive, chemical, petroleum, marine, civil engineering, and construction sectors.

In this experiment, B1500HS with plate thicknesses of 1.2 mm and 1.5 mm, DC03, DC07 and Q235 with plate thicknesses of 0.8 mm, 1 mm, 1.2 mm, 1.5 mm, and DP590 and DP780 with plate thicknesses of 1 mm, 1.2 mm, and sizes of 100 mm x 30 mm were used. The range of plate thicknesses used for the studies spans the thicknesses typically utilized in the production of automobiles, from 0.8 mm to 1.5 mm. This range of options can enhance the model’s usefulness and more thoroughly evaluate its predictive capacity. Diversity, representativeness, practicality, and data richness are characteristics of the chosen experimental materials. These features enable them to provide a theoretical foundation and technological support for real-world applications, as well as to thoroughly validate the accuracy and dependability of the Harris Hawk algorithm-optimized BP neural network in forecasting resistance spot weld quality. Nugget diameter measurement

The diameter of the molten metal region created during resistance spot welding is known as the nucleus diameter, and it is a direct indicator of the weld heat input, the nucleus production process, and the strength of the welded connection. Larger kernel diameters usually mean higher weld strength and better weld quality. Compared to other weld quality indicators, the nucleus diameter is easier to measure and quantify. The kernel diameter can be measured directly by metallurgical microscopy, ultrasonic testing, etc. and accurate values can be obtained. It is possible to optimize the welding process and enhance the quality of the welding by examining the link between the kernel diameter and the process parameters. In addition, the kernel diameter is a widely used standard index in resistance spot welding quality assessment, which is clearly defined in many international standards and industry codes. Consequently, 296 samples were created to evaluate the weld quality using the kernel diameter, and the material samples following spot welding trials are displayed in Fig. 6.

Material specimen after spot welding experiment.

The diameter of a material sample’s molten core may be measured using the following method: (i) Choose a representative spot on the weld specimen to sample; this is often the weld’s center or a spot close to it. For a clear view of the nucleus’s cross-section, the sample direction should be perpendicular to the welding direction. (ii) Using a wire cutter, a cross-sectional sample containing the weld joint is taken from the weld specimen. The sample size is large enough to facilitate subsequent sample preparation and observation. (iii) The intercepted sample is placed in an inlay machine and inlaid using epoxy resin. The purpose of the inlay is to protect the edges of the sample for subsequent sanding and polishing. (iv) Use a sander to polish the inlaid sample, gradually transitioning from coarse to fine sandpaper to remove scratches and deformation layers on the sample surface. (v) Use a polishing cloth and polishing solution to polish the sanded sample to obtain a smooth, scratch-free sample surface. (vi) The polished samples were immersed in the etchant by selecting 4% alcohol nitrate solution according to the type of material, where the etching time was 5 s for mild steel (DC03, DC07, Q235) and 10 s for high strength steel (B1500HS, DP590, DP780). The purpose of corrosion is to show the melt core boundary and heat affected zone. The samples were washed with water, dehydrated with alcohol, and blown dry as soon as the corrosion was finished. (vii) The diameter of the melt core was measured using a metallurgical microscope. Place the corroded sample on the carrier stage of the metallurgical microscope and adjust the focal length and light source so that the boundary of the melt core is clearly visible. Measure the diameter of the molten core using a micrometer. The maximum and minimum diameters of the melt core need to be measured and averaged as the final result.

Model and methodology

BP and HHO model

A log sigmoid (logsig) transfer function connects the first layer of the BP model—the input layer, which has four input parameters—to a hidden layer, which has thirteen hidden neurons. The output layer is subsequently linked to the hidden neurons by a linear (purelin) transfer function. Figure 7 depicts the BP model’s structure. The trainlm algorithm is the backpropagation technique employed for the training function. The fusion kernel diameter indication makes up the output layer. The Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (LMA), a nonlinear least squares optimization procedure that finds the parameter that minimizes the error function by applying a quadratic approximation to the error function, is the foundation of the trainlm algorithm. The trainlm technique employs LMA to optimize the error function in BP neural networks, which is often defined as the sum of the squares of the errors of all training samples. It can enhance the model’s capacity for generalization and swiftly and steadily optimize the error function.

Establishment of HHOBP prediction model

Based on the idea of bionics, the HHO method is an optimization methodology with global search capabilities and rapid convergence. In the topology of many-variable problems, HHO is able to strike a decent compromise between exploratory and exploitative inclinations. In BPNNs, the algorithm is applied to the weights updating and threshold adjustment process. During the optimization process, the Harris Hawk (corresponding to the individual in the algorithm) continuously updates its position by searching for the position of its prey (corresponding to the optimal solution). The “prey position” in the HHO may be compared to the weights and thresholds of a BP neural network. By continually searching iteratively for the ideal “prey position,” or the ideal combination of weights and thresholds, the HHO enhances the performance of the BP neural network. By optimizing this technique, the network’s learning speed may be increased while simultaneously improving its prediction accuracy. Create the prediction model for HHOBP. Hyperparameter optimization and experimental setup

The parameters of the HHOBP model are a population size of 20 and a maximum number of iterations of 15. To improve the prediction performance of the BP neural network, this paper uses the HHO to optimize the hyperparameters. The number of buried layer neurons and the learning rate are two of the optimum hyperparameters. Both the number of hidden layer neurons and the learning rate have search ranges of [10, 100] and [0.001, 0.1], respectively. The MSE of the BP neural network on the training set serves as HHO’s objective function.

The specific optimization process is as follows: initialize the HHO population and randomly generate a set of hyper-parameter combinations (number of hidden neurons and learning rate). For each set of hyperparameters, train the BP neural network using 5-fold cross-validation and calculate its average MSE. According to the MSE value, HHO updates the hyperparameter combinations to gradually approximate the optimal solution. The procedure is continued until either the error converges or the maximum number of iterations-15-is achieved. The final BP neural network model is built using the ideal hyperparameter combination that HHO produces.

The population size of 20 and the maximum number of iterations of 15 are the HHOBP model’s parameters in this experiment. Welding quality metrics were predicted using the HHOBP artificial neural network on MATLAB software. The input welding parameters taken into account for the prediction model were welding current, welding voltage, air pressure, and welding duration. R-squared (R2), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), MSE, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) are the metrics utilized to evaluate the effectiveness of the HHOBP model. It is evident that the prediction model’s performance is significantly impacted by the amount of the training dataset. Therefore, the study use 250 datasets for training and 46 datasets for validation and testing of the prediction model.

Convergence and complexity analysis

The validation set is used to assess the BP neural network’s performance after it has been trained using the optimal hyperparameters. The experimental results show that the BPNN optimized by HHO exhibits higher accuracy and stability in the resistance spot welding quality prediction task. Figure 17. illustrates the HHO convergence curve. The Harris Hawk method’s convergence curve shows that the total error is declining, suggesting that the HHO algorithm serves as an optimizer.

HHO convergence curve.

The main factors influencing the computational complexity of the HHO are the population size (N), the number of iterations (T), and the computing cost of the objective function. The population size of HHO is fixed at 20 in this work, and there are 15 iterations. A BP neural network must be trained and its MSE calculated for each set of hyper-parameter combinations. This procedure has a temporal complexity of O(N × T × C), where C is the BP neural network’s training cost. By tweaking the hyperparameters, HHO greatly enhances the prediction performance of BP neural networks despite increasing the processing burden.

Necessity of the HHOBP model for capturing complex process interactions

Although the experimental findings in Sect. "Effect of welding current on the diameter of the molten core" support a roughly linear relationship between specific process parameters and the nugget width in controlled laboratory settings, this oversimplified perspective is insufficient for precise quality prediction. In particular, this perspective is inadequate for actual production situations. The main rationale for using sophisticated models such as the HHOBP is to handle the intricate, nonlinear, and dynamic character of the resistance spot welding procedure.

First, the final nugget quality and the input parameters do not have an independently linear connection. Welding current, welding duration, electrode force, and material characteristics all interact significantly. For example, the electrode force and the consequent contact resistance have a highly nonlinear connection that modulates the influence of increasing welding current on nugget formation. A simple linear or multiple linear regression model would fail to capture these critical interactions effectively.

Second, industrial data often contains noise and variations not present in controlled experiments. Factors such as slight surface contamination, electrode wear, and shunting effects introduce stochasticity that disrupts ideal linear relationships. As demonstrated in Tables 5 and 6, the proposed HHOBP model exhibits remarkable robustness, achieving significantly lower prediction errors (MSE of 0.0010) compared to not only standard BPNN but also other metaheuristic-optimized models and traditional SVM. This superior performance, quantified by error reductions exceeding 90% for most metrics, underscores its capability to model the underlying complex process dynamics beyond what simple empirical models can achieve.

Furthermore, the high coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9981) of the HHOBP model indicates that it accounts for nearly all the variance in the data. This level of explanatory power is unattainable by linear models when applied to a process governed by complex physical phenomena like Joule heating, plastic deformation, and heat dissipation, which are inherently nonlinear.

Therefore, the utility of the HHOBP model is not challenged by the existence of approximate linear trends in isolated bivariate analyses. Instead, its necessity is proven by its demonstrably superior accuracy and robustness in modeling the full, multivariate, and interactive nature of the RSW process, ultimately providing a reliable tool for quality prediction that is essential for modern smart manufacturing.

Furthermore, the genuine value of the proposed advanced model extends beyond the current experimental setup and becomes particularly critical under conditions that reflect real-world industrial challenges. Its capability to capture complex non-linearities, such as the saturation effects near process boundaries illustrated in our process windows, is indispensable for robust optimization and defect avoidance. The model’s architecture also presents a scalable framework ready to incorporate additional, highly informative process variables that are difficult for simpler models to handle. For instance, the integration of dynamic resistance signals or the compensation for progressive electrode wear—both common sources of variation in production—are natural and necessary future extensions of this work. The HHOBP model’s applicability over more straightforward empirical methods is justified by its capacity to learn from such complicated, non-linear data, which guarantees its long-term relevance and value in adaptive and high-precision manufacturing contexts.

Model interpretability: uncovering physical trends through parametric analysis

To address the “black-box” concern and build trust in the HHOBP model’s predictions, this section demonstrates that the model’s internal learning aligns with established physical principles of RSW. This is achieved not by post-hoc interpretation tools, but by systematically analyzing the model’s predictions over the tested parameter ranges, effectively using the model as a high-fidelity digital twin of the process.

The primary relationship in RSW is defined by Joule’s Law (Q ∝ I2Rt), which posits that heat generation, and consequently nugget growth, is most sensitive to WC and welding time (WT). To verify that the HHOBP model has captured this fundamental physics, a leave-one-parameter-out contribution analysis was conducted. The procedure was as follows: (1) The HHOBP model was used to generate predictions for all data points in the test set. (2) The anticipated nugget diameter and each input parameter were correlated using the Pearson correlation coefficient (R). (3) The dominance of each parameter was quantified by calculating the percentage reduction in the R2 value of a simple linear regression between that parameter and the predicted ND when the other parameters were varied, isolating its unique contribution. As unequivocally demonstrated in Table 8, the HHOBP model’s learned relationships are in perfect agreement with the governing physics of RSW. The strong positive correlation for WC and WT confirms that the model has fundamentally learned the principles of Joule heating.

Beyond linear correlations, the model’s ability to capture non-linear interactions and saturation effects was investigated by examining its prediction profiles. For instance, when analyzing the trend between WC and ND while holding WT constant, the model’s predictions show a distinct non-linear saturation trend: the rate of ND increase diminishes at higher current levels. This perfectly mirrors the physical reality where further increases in current yield diminishing returns in nugget growth and eventually lead to expulsion, a key defect mode. A simple linear model would fail to capture this critical process boundary.

Similarly, the model captures the synergistic interaction between WC and WT. At a higher WT, the marginal gain in ND from increasing WC is greater than at a lower WT. This indicates that the model has learned that sufficient time is required to utilize the increased heat input effectively, a nuanced aspect of the process.

In conclusion, this parametric analysis demonstrates that the HHOBP model’s superior accuracy is not an artifact of overfitting but a result of it internalizing the core physical laws of RSW. It correctly hierarchizes parameter importance, captures essential non-linearities, and identifies critical interactions. Therefore, the model produces predictions that are not only accurate but also physically consistent and interpretable, providing a reliable foundation for decision-making in industrial settings.

Summary and future work

This paper proposes an HHOBP model for resistance spot welding nugget diameter prediction by combining the Harris Hawk Optimization (HHO) algorithm with a back propagation neural network (BPNN). In terms of prediction accuracy, stability, and convergence, the experimental findings demonstrate that the model outperforms the comparative model. The excellent performance of HHOBP mainly stems from two inherent advantages of HHO algorithm:

The mechanism for switching policies adaptively. Unlike static optimization systems like particle swarm optimization (PSO) and gray wolf optimization (GWO), HHO may dynamically switch between four round-up tactics (soft/hard enclosure buddy vs. progressive rapid dive) dependent on prey escape energy (E) and random chance (r). This allows HHO to better adapt to the nonlinear response relationship during the optimization process.

The second is the method of coordinated exploration and development. HHO overcomes the drawbacks of conventional gradient descent techniques and single strategy meta-heuristic algorithms by maintaining diversity in the global search through the population collaborative raid strategy and performing fine development locally. This effectively prevents falling into the local optimum in the high-dimensional welding parameter space.

Based on the above mechanism, the HHOBP model significantly improves the prediction performance of BPNN. The specific conclusions are as follows:

Prediction accuracy is significantly improved. Compared with PSO-BP, GWO-BP, CS-BP, GA-BP, traditional BPNN and support vector machine models, HHOBP has achieved 93.06% ~ 96.15% (MAE), 99.43% ~ 99.81% (MSE), 92.18% ~ 95.46% (RMSE) and 87.54% ~ 96.83% (MAPE) in different error indicators, reflecting excellent modeling capabilities.

Enhancement of convergence and stability. HHO’s potent global search capability efficiently optimizes BPNN’s initial weight and threshold, speeds up model convergence, and dramatically lowers BPNN’s susceptibility to local optimal.

Outstanding capacity to simulate intricate nonlinear interactions. The model may accurately describe the complex high-dimensional nonlinear mapping relationship between nugget diameter and welding current, electrode pressure, welding duration, and other parameters.

Although HHOBP shows good performance, there are still the following aspects that deserve further study to improve its practicality and universality:

Enhance model robustness and generalization ability. In the future, regularization or data augmentation techniques can be used, particularly to reduce the danger of overfitting when the sample is small, and the adaptive adjustment mechanism of hyperparameters can be investigated.

Improve computing efficiency to support real-time applications. Distributed computing, network structure pruning, quantization technology and hardware acceleration can be explored to reduce computational complexity.

Extended model validation scope and multi-objective optimization. The generalization capabilities of models should be verified in a wider range of industrial welding scenarios and datasets; At the same time, it can be combined with multi-objective optimization algorithm to realize the common optimization of multiple quality indexes.

Develop real-time prediction and online learning mechanism. In the future, the performance of HHOBP in real-time data streams can be studied, and online update and adaptive mechanisms can be developed to cope with dynamic production environments.

To sum up, HHOBP provides a high-precision and high-stability solution for resistance spot welding quality prediction. Through continuous improvement and exploration in the above directions, the practical application of this model in intelligent manufacturing systems can be further promoted.
