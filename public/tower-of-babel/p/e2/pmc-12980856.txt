Can algorithms come to the rescue of a failing heart? Machine learning, artificial intelligence, and decision-making in cardiogenic shock
Authors: Alice Bottussi, Patrick M. Wieruszewski, Elena Giovanna Bignami, Justyna Swol, Kevin G. Buda, Wisit Cheungpasitporn, Omar Elmadhoun, Jacopo D’Andria Ursoleo
Published: 2026

Cardiogenic shock (CS) remains associated with mortality of 40% to 50% despite advances in contemporary critical care, percutaneous and surgical revascularization, and mechanical circulatory support [1, 2]. This persistent stagnation in outcomes suggests not merely a lack of therapeutic innovation, but a deeper misalignment between the dynamic, rapidly evolving pathophysiology of CS and the predominantly static frameworks used to classify risk and guide treatment, thereby creating an opportunity for data-driven approaches that capture evolving patient trajectories and support real-time clinical decision-making. In this context, the principal value of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) lies not in replacing clinical judgment, but in enabling adaptive, trajectory-aware decision support in a syndrome defined by rapid physiologic change.

Traditional models of classification and care in CS

CS has been classified through several different frameworks based on patient hemodynamic features, shock severity, and mortality risk [3]. At the bedside, patients are typically classified as “dry” or “wet,” reflecting ventricular filling pressures, and “warm” or “cold,” reflecting systemic perfusion, or according to the predominant pattern of ventricular dysfunction (left ventricular, right ventricular, or biventricular) [4]. These phenotypes, derived from clinical examination and supported by echocardiography or invasive hemodynamic assessment when available, can help guide initial therapeutic decisions, including the selection of inotropes, diuretics, and vasopressors.

Despite their clinical utility, current frameworks remain inherently static and often oversimplify a complex and evolving syndrome, compressing a spectrum of physiology into discrete categories without adequately accounting for patient-specific trajectories at the bedside [5].

To address this limitation, severity-based classification systems have been developed to better support risk stratification and escalation of care. Among these, the Society of Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) classification system was introduced to better capture the clinical spectrum of CS severity and facilitate decision-making [6]. Similar frameworks, including the Interagency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (INTERMACS) and United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) systems, are primarily used to inform candidacy for ventricular assist device (VAD) implantation and heart transplantation, respectively [7].

Even with recent efforts to standardize SCAI criteria through defined hemodynamic and biochemical parameters, the intrinsic heterogeneity of CS frequently exceeds the granularity of available classification systems [8]. As a result, current frameworks often fall short in capturing evolving patient trajectories or anticipating abrupt deterioration, a limitation that may contribute to the persistent lack of therapeutic breakthroughs and stagnant mortality in CS [8]. This calls for a shift in our current perspective, moving away from static models towards data-driven approaches capable of integrating dynamic clinical data and supporting more individualized, real-time decision-making [8].

The integration of emerging technologies in healthcare and critical cardiovascular illness

ML and AI may bridge the gap in the development of advanced clinical tools capable of identifying patients in the early phases of CS and dynamically monitoring the frequently evolving disease course [8]. The rapid expansion of AI and ML applications in medicine has already influenced several domains of critical care across a broad range of clinical contexts, including heart failure, sepsis, and acute kidney injury [9–12].

Broadly, AI refers to computational systems emulating cognitive functions, such as learning and problem solving [13]. ML, a subset of AI, focuses on developing models that learn patterns from data to generate predictions or decisions, with performance improving as additional data become available, rather than through explicit rule-based programming [13]. We contend that the recent shift in the approach to CS classification towards its biologically informed molecular mechanisms may likewise be well-suited for the application of AI and ML tools. Stemming from this ground, novel data-driven strategies can play a key role in integrating dynamic models and classifications into clinical care, thus developing patient-specific predictions in the broader context of precision critical care medicine. Indeed, subphenotyping, which introduces subgroups of patients with CS based on different underlying pathobiological mechanisms, may serve the scope to reveal treatable traits such as specific mechanisms of injury, including endothelial, inflammatory, and neurohormonal dysregulation [18]. The integration of biomarker panels (e.g., interleukin, angiopoietin, growth factor pathways, lactate, adrenomedullin, etc.) and their variations in serum concentrations across different temporal trends may further enhance early detection of CS in patients at risk, prediction of end-organ malperfusion, and guidance of therapeutic interventions [19]. AI-driven computational platforms may play a central role in synthesizing these multidimensional data streams, allowing disease trajectories to be modeled and therapeutic responses to be reassessed over time, rather than relying on isolated static measurements.

In this regard, an unsupervised ML approach, specifically the partitioning method k-means clustering, has been applied to clinical and laboratory data to identify three reproducible clinical subphenotypes of CS (non-congested, cardiorenal, and hemo-metabolic). AI-identified subgroups demonstrated distinct clinical profiles and, most importantly, they were associated with different mortality risk, thereby enhancing prognostication in CS populations, potentially prompting different management strategies [20, 21]. Subsequent work by Jentzer et al. employed model-based latent class analysis in a similar cohort, confirming that both k-means clustering and latent class analysis yielded consistent results, assigning patients to analogous sub phenotypes, and supporting the reproducibility of these phenotypic classifications [22]. The ability of AI to rapidly and reliably identify the clinical sub phenotypes is of notable importance and constitutes an essential step in the implementation of AI into precision critical care, guiding targeted therapies with translational potential to improve patient outcomes [23].

Likewise, electronic health records are a particularly rich substrate for both AI and ML applications in CS, given their scale, granularity, and temporal depth. A study by Escobar et al. evaluated the application of a validated ML model to identify patients at risk of clinical deterioration outside the intensive care unit (ICU), demonstrating lower mortality within 30 days after an alert [10]. Such a system may support the development and implementation of personalized treatment strategies informed by data-driven approaches (e.g., ML, large language models, clustering techniques, and other AI-based technologies), facilitating the development of personalized medicine within intensive care, with the goal of improving patient outcomes. In the context of cardiovascular medicine, AI and ML models have shown the ability to analyze high-frequency physiological data to identify early signs of imminent decompensation both in heart failure and CS in ICU and emergency department settings, thereby broadening the window for timely intervention and escalation of therapies prior to overt clinical decline [24].

Furthermore, AI-powered tools have been explored across multiple applications, including the prediction of post-transplant outcomes [25]. However, their performance is often hindered by data scarcity, class imbalance and heterogeneity, especially when AI models are trained on large-scale databases such as the UNOS or the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) registries [25], as these often contain highly variable patient populations, diverse data collection protocols, and non-negligible rates of missing data. To address these limitations, generative AI models, such as generative adversarial networks or variational autoencoders, have emerged as powerful tools for data augmentation and synthetic trajectory generation [26–28]. Another frontier is represented by intelligent clinical agents and multi-agent systems, which can orchestrate multiple predictive and prescriptive models to integrate multimodal data and support decision-making in the context of CS [29]. However, while such models hold potential to mitigate class imbalance and to enhance model training, current generative approaches often lack sufficient integration of patient-specific context and clinical knowledge, with a notable absence of large-scale validation studies [26].

A more promising area of AI development involves the integration of histopathological and molecular data. The CACHE-Grader model, for instance, combines histological features derived from myocardial biopsy and transcriptomic profiles, enabling earlier identification of graft rejection compared with histopathology alone in heart transplant recipients, and therefore broadening the window for timely management. Following both internal and external validation, the model demonstrated statistical non-inferiority to the diagnostic standard, with a sensitivity of 0.85 and a specificity of 0.84, and showed generalizability across external datasets [17].

Similarly, in the domain of mechanical circulatory support, the Impella® platform (Abiomed, Denver, USA) has advanced with the introduction of a new Automated Impella® Controller (AIC) and the SmartAssist technology [30] These systems are presented here as an example of high-density, continuously acquired physiological data, which may be particularly well suited for ML-based risk modeling. Integrating device-derived data with early clinical trends may facilitate the development of predictive models capable of identifying patients likely to achieve native heart recovery and informing decisions regarding durable therapies such as transplantation or long-term VAD implantation [30].

Another innovative concept that has recently emerged in cardiovascular care is the development of a digital twin, a computational representation of an individual patient that integrates a broad range of clinical variables, including the patient’s status and treatments. This is then used to simulate disease evolution, detect abnormalities in real time, and predict trends and patterns using AI algorithms [31]. This model has already been investigated in the setting of temporary mechanical circulatory support, with a study by Jelenc et al. evaluating different potential left-ventricular venting options through a simulation platform developed in Matlab (The MathWorks Inc., MA, USA). In turn, the authors found an inverse linear relationship between venting and left atrial pressure [32]. Several other contributions aimed towards the development of a reliable computational platform to investigate the effects of temporary mechanical circulatory support on cardiac unloading in patients with CS have also been reported [33, 34]. Despite its conceptual appeal, computational latency and model simplification remain important limitations, particularly in rapidly deteriorating patients with CS.

AI-based tools should therefore be viewed strictly as adjuncts rather than substitutes for clinical judgement. Several elements considered in practice may be difficult to replicate within a simulated virtual platform, including septal ventricular interactions, the effects of positive pressure ventilation, and the dynamic influence of vasopressors and inotropes [30]. Additionally, computer-generated data often rely on simplifying assumptions, such as fixed ventricular contractility or constant blood volume, which poorly reflect real-world physiology and may contribute to discordance between simulated and in-human data [35].

A further intrinsic limitation of AI systems lies in their so-called black box nature, whereby outputs are generated through internal processes that are not readily interpretable by clinicians. This lack of transparency may limit clinician trust and willingness to initiate, escalate, or withdraw therapy based solely on algorithm-derived outputs. Explainable AI models have been proposed to improve interpretability and adoption [36]. These approaches can be broadly categorized as inherent or post-hoc explainability, though current methods remain limited in their ability to fully elucidate complex decision-making processes, warranting caution when ML algorithms are applied to bedside care [36].

Similarly, clear regulatory frameworks to define the scope, validation, and clinical applicability of AI-powered systems are warranted. To date, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has authorized approximately 1000 AI-enabled medical devices [37]. The rapid evolution of more complex AI technologies, including large language models, has outpaced existing regulatory paradigms. There is an urgent need for regulatory innovation capable of ensuring safe integration of AI tools into clinical workflows while maintaining rigorous standards for evaluation and oversight [37]. Beyond regulatory constraints, the pragmatic implementation of AI into clinical care also requires the development of models that can be seamlessly integrated into established clinical workflows. In parallel, effective dissemination of information and training is paramount to foster clinicians’ trust and acceptance. Lastly, data interoperability persists as a major barrier, as AI systems must be made compatible with heterogeneous electronic health systems to enable their widespread adoption.

Conclusions and The Way Forward

The development of AI and ML models in CS is gaining increasing attention, with early applications suggesting meaningful potential. Predictive tools capable of anticipating decompensation may help inform bedside decision-making, support medical optimization, guide timely escalation to extracorporeal therapies, and facilitate earlier planning for longer-term care pathways (Fig. 1) [12]. In the near term, AI-enabled models may primarily influence decisions regarding the timing and direction of care, including earlier identification of patients likely to deteriorate despite maximal medical management, more timely escalation to temporary mechanical circulatory support, and recognition of patients in whom de-escalation or avoidance of futile escalation is appropriate. By integrating longitudinal physiologic trends with device-derived and biomarker data, such approaches may also improve discrimination between trajectories of myocardial recovery and progression toward durable VAD implantation or heart transplantation, thereby enabling earlier and more informed pathway planning. The near-term clinical value of AI in CS lies not in algorithmic autonomy, but rather in sharpening situational awareness and aligning clinical decisions with evolving physiology.

Nonetheless, substantial challenges remain. Data privacy, security, interoperability, and heterogeneity continue to limit generalizability, and most ML models have been developed using retrospective datasets with uncertain real-world impact. Prospective studies will be essential to define their role as clinical adjuncts facilitating clinicians’ acceptance, and to validate models seamlessly integrable into established clinical workflows. Ultimately, in CS, the most immediate promise of AI may not lie in autonomous decision-making, but in enhancing earlier cognitive alignment between rapidly evolving physiology and irreversible clinical decisions.
