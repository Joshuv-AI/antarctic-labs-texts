Accumulation of 13C-labeled phosphatidylethanolamine in the termite abdomen revealed by correlative isotope microscopy and mass microscopy
Authors: Rafia Ferdous, Md Muedur Rahman, Takumi Sakamoto, Md Shoriful Islam, Md Maniruzzaman, Md Monirul Islam, Md Foyzur Rahman, Ryo Omagari, Md Al Mamun, Ariful Islam, Shuhei Aramaki, Shoshiro Hirayama, Tomohito Sato, Yutaka Takahashi, Tomoaki Kahyo, Naoya Sakamoto, Hisayoshi Yurimoto, Kenji Matsuura, Mitsutoshi Setou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsotope microscopic imaging and atmospheric pressure matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (AP-MALDI-MSI) provide powerful, complementary approaches for visualizing metabolic dynamics in biological tissues. This study applied these techniques to termite workers fed with 13C-labeled cellulose for 1 week. Termites are classified as eusocial insects because of their colonies’ clear division of labor. The two primary castes in their life cycle are reproductive (king and queen), responsible for reproduction, and non-reproductive (workers and soldiers), who handle tasks such as defense, brood care and foraging. Although various techniques have been developed to detect 13C-labeled biomolecules in samples, it remains unclear whether the iMScopeTM prototype can visualize these molecules with high spatial resolution. Advanced isotope microscopic imaging technique with high spatial resolution (200–300 nm) offered ultra-high-resolution visualization of the relative abundance of the 13C/12C distribution, suggesting precise localization of isotope enrichment in the abdomen. AP-MALDI-MSI performed in the iMScopeTM prototype enabled spatial mapping of 13C-labeled and unlabeled metabolites, such as acetyl-L-carnitine and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), by detecting characteristic mass shifts due to 13C incorporation. The accumulation of PE in the termite abdomen represents an adaptive strategy to optimize nutrient allocation and promote social cohesion, thereby highlighting its potential role in maintaining colony fitness. Our study shows that the iMScopeTM prototype is a novel AP-MALDI-MSI technique to detect 13C-integrated metabolites in the 13C-labeled sample. This study also demonstrated that this technique can detect 13C-integrated PE, which is abundant mainly in termite abdomen.Mini-abstract

Introduction

Isotope microscopic imaging offers the spatial distribution of an element at the nanometer scale. This technique is particularly valuable for tracking the incorporation of stable isotopes (e.g. 13C, 15N) into biological tissues, allowing researchers to study metabolic pathways and nutrient assimilation with exceptional spatial precision [1]. Recent advancements have improved the sensitivity and resolution of isotope imaging, facilitating detailed studies of metabolic processes in various organisms, including insects [2] .

Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) is a powerful analytical technique that enables the label-free visualization of biomolecules—such as lipids, metabolites and proteins—directly from tissue sections [3]. It preserves tissue integrity and allows molecular information to be correlated with histological features [4].

Atmospheric pressure matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-MSI (AP-MALDI-MSI) utilizes a pulsed laser beam to ionize molecules from a matrix-coated tissue surface, enabling the detection, localization and identification of various biomolecules [5, 6]. Shimadzu, Japan, invented the Imaging Mass Microscope (iMScopeTM prototype), an AP-MALDI-MSI equipment with an integrated optical microscope [7]. This technique is beneficial for characterizing plant, animal and human tissues since it enables the analysis of hundreds of molecules without complicated sample preparation [8]. The Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) technique is a powerful analytical tool for the confirmation and quantification of a wide range of molecules, including lipids, proteins (via peptide analysis), small molecules, nucleotides and other metabolites, due to its high sensitivity, ability and specificity to analyze complex biological matrices [9–11].

Termites are the most abundant terrestrial eusocial insects. Unlike the social Hymenoptera, including honeybees and other insects, termites have a development mechanism by hemimetabolous mode with bisexuality and a diploid sex determination system. In the termite life cycle, two major castes are produced. One is a reproductive caste, which includes males (kings) and females (queens), and the other is a non-reproductive caste, which includes workers and soldiers [12, 13]. Division of labor is a prominent characteristic of termites in which few kings and queens generate all the offspring. In contrast, many non-reproductive castes engage in nest building, defense activity and foraging activity in a termite colony. The termites’ special feature is that the kings and queens sustain longer lives, maintaining their sexual capability compared to the other castes [12].

The division of their tasks among the termite castes and anatomical and morphological features are markedly changed during their caste differentiation in the development process. One study reported that the changes in brain morphology and neural diversity among the termite caste underpinned their eusocial roles [14]. Among the termite castes, the worker is considered an altruistic individual who performs important tasks, including foraging, brood care and nest-building. In addition, sometimes, the worker caste shows the reproductive function (neotenic) in lower termites [15]. A recent study demonstrated that the worker caste showed trophallaxis characteristics through the stomodeal mechanism, where the kings and queens are fed an exceptional food known as royal food by the worker caste [16]. Notably, the distribution of compounds is also different between the king’s and queen’s royal food. This special royal food contains some essential nutrients, including phosphatidylinositol (PI) and acetyl-L-carnitine (ALC), and these are prepared by the worker caste after ingestion of foods. Moreover, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) was found in similar abundance in both king’s and queen’s royal food [11]. Previously, PI and ALC were identified by tracking the 13C-labeled cellulose food ingested by the worker castes using the desorption electrospray ionization-MSI (DESI-MSI) technique. Though the iMScopeTM QT has been effectively employed in various applications, including the rapid mapping of drugs and their metabolites in biological tissues, demonstrating its potential for high-resolution spatial analysis, there is no report about its application in 13C-isotope tracking [5, 8]. Other MSI methods have been used for 13C-isotope tracking. For example, 13C-labeled metabolites in plant tissues, including the maize root tips, have been seen using MALDI-MSI [17]. Furthermore, metabolic flux in situ, especially in plant embryos, can be analyzed using procedures that combine 13C-isotope tracking with MSI [18]. Despite various analytical techniques, few offer both isotopic sensitivity and spatial resolution. However, the iMScopeTM prototype’s capability to visualize the 13C-labeled molecules at high spatial resolution is still unknown. This study used advanced isotope microscopic imaging with a high spatial resolution (200–300 nm) and an AP-MALDI-MSI observed in the iMScopeTM prototype to track 13C-isotope and its spatial distribution inside the worker caste body.

Materials and methods

Termite sample collection

In this study, termites were collected from Japanese cedar forests (Kyoto), as described by Tasaki et al. [11]. Specifically, worker caste (Reticulitermes speratus) colonies member was selected and introduced into a glass cell (150 mm × 150 mm) with a 2-mm gap. The cell contained termite culture medium, a mixture of brown rotten pine wood, and cellulose in a 1 : 1 ratio, referred to as mixed sawdust bait.

13C-labeled cellulose feeding of termite workers

Fifteen termite workers randomly chosen from a colony were placed in each well of a 48-well plate, where they were exposed to controlled feeding with 13C-labeled cellulose (13C atoms 97%, IsoLife BV, Netherlands) mixed with an appropriate amount of water. The plates were kept in the dark at room temperature for a week, and the resulting 13C-labeled workers were then collected [11]. In addition, workers from another colony were collected as an unlabeled control group, which had been reared with a termite culture medium devoid of 13C-cellulose. The cellulose was uniformly labeled with 13C-isotope to simplify the tracking and analysis of carbon isotope ratios in the termite body. After the feeding period, termite workers were carefully dissected for microscopy analysis.

Sample preparation for isotope microscopic imaging

Two groups of termite workers (n = 2) were collected from the colonies. One group was labeled with 13C-isotope, and another was unlabeled. Then, the termites were frozen with super cryo-embedding media (Section Lab Co., Ltd, Japan) at −80°C for 1 h. After that, the sample was cryosectioned at 10 µm thickness at −20°C (CM1950, Leica, Germany) and fixed onto hydrophilized silicon wafers (7 × 7 mm, 24 × 24 mm) for isotope microscopic imaging.

Isotope microscopic imaging

To acquire the image of the 13C-isotope and its three-dimensional distribution in the termite worker body, an advanced isotope imaging microscope system was used [2]. The spatial resolution of the measurement was 200 nm in the lateral direction and 10 nm in the depth direction. The area of the measurement was 100 µm for each measurement. The imaging data acquisition time was 20 h. Then, the image of each region was aligned with image processing software. To account for potential analytical artifacts, we note that repetitive 13C signal dots are observed in the same positions across multiple panels. The phenomenon is due to interfering ions (such as 12CH) in the 13C mass spectrum, slightly protruding from the exit slit. The intensity scale of all isotope microscopic images was displayed on an isotope ratio value of 13C/12C (×100) (e.g. 0.0–0.2), with no units. These values reflect relative intensities used for visualization. The scale bars (in micrometers) were included in all panels.

Sample preparation for AP-MALDI-MSI

The unlabeled (n = 2) and 13C-labeled (n = 1) termite workers were frozen with super cryo-embedding media (Section Lab Co. Ltd, Japan) at −80°C for 1 h. After that, the sample was cryosectioned at 10 µm thickness at −20°C on a cryostat microtome (CM1950, Leica, Germany) and mounted on indium tin oxide (ITO) coated glass slides (100 Ω, Matsunami, Osaka, Japan). Before adding the matrix, the termite sections mounted on glass slides were allowed to dry at room temperature using a dry vacuum pump (ULVAC DTC-21) for 15 min. The 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB) matrix (40 mg/ml in 50% methanol) was sprayed onto sample slides using the HTX M5 TM-SprayerTM (HTX M5 Technologies, Chapel Hill, NC, USA) with the following conditions: matrix flow rate of 0.05 ml/min, nozzle temperature of 70°C, gas pressure of 10 psi, velocity of 1250 mm/min and pass number of 24.

AP-MALDI-MSI using iMScopeTM prototype

Mass spectrometry imaging data were acquired using an iMScopeTM prototype (Shimadzu, Japan) with an AP-MALDI source and a time-of-flight mass spectrometer. Standard precautions were used to adjust iMScopeTM settings before measuring unlabeled and 13C-labeled worker termite samples. Termite tissue mass spectra were acquired in three different m/z ranges: 100–400, 350–650 and 600–900. Optimized AP-MALDI conditions were as follows: laser power of 15%, pixel size of 25 μm × 25 μm, laser diameter of 0 μm, repetition number of 1000 Hz, laser shots of 100, detector voltage of 2.1 kV and desolvation line (DL) temperature of 200°C. Lock mass correction was used, utilizing an m/z 155.0345 for positive ion mode to improve mass accuracy. MS Solution (Shimadzu, Japan, version 2.00.00A) software was used for data acquisition.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining and imaging

We stained the same unlabeled and 13C-labeled worker termite sample sections with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to see the microanatomy of the samples performed AP-MALDI-MSI. Before beginning the H&E staining, the samples were briefly pre-washed with 80% acetone (acetone: water (v/v)) to remove the DHB matrix. After 3 min of hematoxylin staining, the sample-containing ITO slides were rinsed with tap water. The slides were then dipped for 1 min into 80% ethanol (ethanol: water (v/v)) and then immersed in eosin for 10–30 s to develop color. The tissue samples were then cleaned with water for 1 min and dehydrated by raising ethanol concentrations (80%, 90%, 100% and 100%). After removing the last traces of water from the samples using xylene, the samples were covered with Pathomount, a mounting medium, to prevent oxidation of the tissues and to secure coverslips. Next, we digitally scanned and imaged the tissue samples stained with H&E using a slide scanner (NanoZoomer S60, Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan) in 40×scanning mode.

LC-MS/MS analysis

For LC-MS/MS analysis, we used the same data performed and partially published by Tasaki et al. [11] to annotate the candidate biomolecule. Lipid analysis was conducted using an UltiMate 3000 HPLC system coupled to a Q-Exactive Orbitrap MS with a heated ESI source. Separation was achieved through a C18 column with a 60-min linear gradient. With a column temperature of 50°C and mobile phase flow rate of 0.3 ml/min, the following analysis condition was used: mobile phase A containing water/acetonitrile/methanol = 2/1/1, 5 mM ammonium formate, 0.1% formic acid and B containing acetonitrile/2-propanol = 1/9, 5 mM ammonium formate, 0.1% formic acid. These mobile phases had linear gradient elution, with B = 20% (0 min)−100% (50 min)–100% (60 min). The MS operated in both positive and negative modes at resolutions of 70 000 (MS) and 17 500 (MS/MS), using data-dependent acquisition over m/z 220–2000. Key parameters included a spray voltage of ±3.5/2.5 kV, 30 eV collision energy and 5.0e4 intensity threshold. In this investigation, we compared our new MSI findings with reanalyzed raw LC-MS/MS data from an updated database to identify additional lipid and confirm their spatial distribution in worker termites. From this database, we confirmed the presence of PE.

Data analysis

Data analysis was carried out using ImageJ2 (Fiji) for image processing and IMAGEREVEALTM MS software (v. 1.30.0.11461, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) for the MSI data analysis. We started MSI data analysis by choosing the unlabeled and 13C-labeled worker termite samples’ total ion current to normalize the data. We kept the other parameters set to default during the investigation, choosing a tolerance/bin size of 5 ppm and a threshold value of 0%. The mass spectra of the samples showed 2996 mass peaks.

Tracking of 13C-cellulose carbon incorporation into metabolites of a termite worker

We used AP-MALDI-MSI to detect candidate biomolecules. First, we confirmed the signal for a known key metabolite, ALC (m/z 204.122, [M+H]+, monoisotopic mass: 203.115), whose importance was suggested in a previous study [11]. Next, we focus on other high-intensity biomolecules. In searching for lipid molecules related to cellulose metabolism among several thousand signals, we detected a prominent peak at m/z 796.529. Based on a database search, this signal was presumptively identified as [M+K]+ adduct of PE (monoisotopic mass: 757.598). Furthermore, in the 13C-labeled sample, a clear signal at m/z 805.652 was observed, indicating the incorporation of 13C-isotopes into this PE. The incorporation of 13C-cellulose carbon ingested by termite workers is found in the candidate compounds derived from cellulose metabolism (Figs. 3 and 4). Using HMDB database searches (https://www.hmdb.ca/spectra/ms/search), two distinct biomolecules could be assigned within 10 ppm mass accuracy.

Detection of different numbers of 13C-isotope incorporated PEs

The AP-MALDI-MSI data revealed that in addition to the primary 13C-integrated PE containing nine 13C atoms (m/z 805.652), several other PE species with fewer 13C atoms were also detected. Specifically, we observed PEs corresponding to the incorporation of one, two, three, four, five, six, seven and eight 13C atoms at m/z 797.603, 798.606, 799.618, 800.621, 801.625, 802.630, 803.645 and 804.641 (right peaks of the doublets), respectively (Fig. 8), as theoretically the +1-isotope peak of 13C = m/z + 1.003. This does not occur naturally in an unlabeled termite. Moreover, the m/z 798.542 is the m/z of phosphatidylcholine (left peak of doublet) [19], which did not demonstrate the incorporation of 13C-isotope by the above m/z.

To illustrate the incorporation of 13C-isotopes into PE, we postulated its biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 9). This pathway demonstrated that 13C-labeled PE species were not limited to those containing nine 13C atoms, but also included variants with lower numbers of 13C-incorporation. These partially labeled PEs suggest a stepwise or incomplete incorporation of 13C-labeled precursors during biosynthesis. The biosynthesis of 13C-labeled PE begins with 13C-labeled cellulose, which is broken down into 13C-labeled glucose. This glucose enters two distinct metabolic routes: the cytidine 5′-diphosphoethanolamine [CDP–ethanolamine] (Kennedy) pathway for synthesizing the head group and the 1,2-diacylglycerol synthesis pathway for generating the fatty acid tails via the glycolytic pathway [20]. During biosynthesis of PE, the head group serves one 13C atom, as glutamate removes two 13C atoms of 3-phospho hydroxy pyruvate to form α-ketoglutarate during 13C-labeled O-phospho-l-serine synthesis (Fig. 9). In de novo fatty acid synthesis, acetyl-coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) supplies two carbons (both 13C-atoms or, mixture of 12C and 13C atoms or, both 12C atoms) at a time during fatty acid elongation [21, 22].

Discussion

This study uses stable isotope tracking to monitor the distribution of 13C-isotope in termite workers. Among the stable isotopes, 13C-isotopes are the most commonly used in bioscience and medicine [23]. The abundance of 13C-isotope is 1%, and the distribution of 13C/12C is 0.011 naturally. The abundance of 13C is considered significant if the ratio exceeds the natural limit [24]. The strategy of 13C-isotope tracking has been reported to elucidate various cellular and molecular phenomena. The implication of 13C tracking is highlighted in several health and disease research fields, including cancer biology research, biomarker discovery, and metabolomics [25, 26]. Using 13C-isotope tracking, quantitative and qualitative metabolic profiling was carried out by measuring the isotopically labeled metabolites in different organisms to identify the physiological and developmental propagation [27]. Our study measured the 13C/12C distribution in the termite worker caste body. We found that the ratio was highly abundant in the abdomen as well as the head of the termite worker caste (Figs. 1 and 2). Higher abundance of 13C/12C distribution in the termite worker abdomen might be an effect of the 13C-labeled food ingestion. This study found a higher abundance of 13C/12C distribution in the abdomen region of the termite worker caste, which contains the 13C-labeled metabolites (Fig. 2).

To explore more details about the 13C-isotope accumulation in the abdomen, we tracked 13C-cellulose carbon incorporation into the candidate metabolites. By using the AP-MALDI-MSI technique, the 13C-integration in ALC and PE was detected, and the signal intensity was higher in the abdomen (Figs. 3, 4 and 7). In termites, particularly the species Hodotermopsis sjostedti, studies have identified the presence of ALC, a specific acylcarnitine variant [28]. This compound has been detected in the gut of worker termites by DESI-MSI and LC-MS/MS. It is also a component of the royal jelly-like secretion provided to termite kings and queens, consistent with the previous report [11]. This metabolite is known to have anti-aging properties and is also an essential factor for the growth and development of the termite life cycle [29].

Phosphatidylethanolamine is one of the most abundant phospholipids in insect membranes [30]. As a building block for cell signaling, anti-inflammatory and proapoptotic chemicals, PE is essential for membrane fusion and curvature, which are components of biological membrane architecture [31]. Although not yet studied in termites specifically, in other insects and eukaryotes, PE biochemistry is linked to autophagy and cellular homeostasis, suggesting a role in tissue maintenance during cellular stress and longevity [32]. Thus, PE is essential for metabolic homeostasis and reproductive physiology in king and queen termites through caste-based nutrient distribution. PE and PI are both synthesized from phosphatidic acid via the CDP–diacylglycerol pathway, yet they serve distinct cellular roles [33]. PI primarily participates in signaling processes, such as phosphoinositide turnover, while PE contributes to membrane structure, lipid metabolism, and can be methylated to form phosphatidylcholine, underscoring its remodeling versatility. Beyond structural functions, PE works synergistically with ALC to maintain mitochondrial integrity and facilitate efficient energy production [34]. This partnership may help sustain the elevated metabolic demands of reproductive castes, reflecting a nutritional and biochemical adaptation that supports termite social hierarchy and division of labor. The distribution of PE was investigated in the Anopheles stephensi mosquito by AP-MALDI-MSI [35]. PE has also been identified as termites’ king and queen royal food component. Still, the 13C-integrated PE was not detected in the termite with the experimental conditions used by Tasaki et al. [11] in DESI-MSI. In this study, we detected the 13C-integrated PE by iMScopeTM prototype comprising AP-MALDI-MSI (Fig. 4). From LC-MS/MS analysis of termite worker, the [M+H]+ adduct of PE (m/z 758.60) was detected instead of the [M+K]+ adduct (Figs. 5 and 6). The m/z 796.529 is considered to be [M+K]+ adduct of PE since [M+K]+ adduct cannot be detected as easily by LC-MS analysis as by mass spectrometry analysis. With AP-MALDI-MSI, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight and nine 13C-isotopes incorporated PEs are detected, demonstrating the role of 13C-labeled precursors during the biosynthesis (Fig. 8). For biosynthesis of PE, CDP–ethanolamine (Kennedy) and 1,2-diacylglycerol synthesis pathways were similar in Drosophila [33, 36]. During the biosynthesis of 13C-integrated PE from 13C-labeled cellulose, nine 13C-isotopes are incorporated, as shown in AP-MALDI-MSI, where one 13C-isotope is assumed to be incorporated in the head group of PEs through CDP–ethanolamine (Kennedy) pathway [36, 37]. The remaining 13C-isotopes are incorporated from the biosynthesis of fatty acid tail groups to form 1,2-diacylglycerol (Fig. 9) [33, 38]. The carbon skeleton of a fatty acid is derived from acetyl-CoA. Generation of cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA and subsequent fatty acid and lipid synthesis from 13C-labeled cellulose results from de novo fatty acid synthesis using fatty acid synthase [39]. The acetyl-CoA containing two 13C-isotopes can be generated from 13C-labeled cellulose via cellulose breakdown and glycolysis pathway [40]. Cancer metabolism research has shown that [1,2-13C] glucose leads to the production of 3-phosphoglycerate containing an odd number of 13C atoms at a rate of 20%. Since 3-phosphoglycerate serves as a precursor for pyruvate, it appears that acetyl-CoA molecules containing an odd number of 13C atoms can be produced, which is the probable cause of an odd number of 13C-integrated PE in AP-MALDI-MSI [41]. AP-MALDI-MSI also demonstrated that 13C-integrated PE was accumulated in the abdomen of the termite worker (Figs. 4 and 7). The termite abdomen is rich in fat body tissue, which contains adipocytes, urocytes and sometimes mycetocytes. Among these, adipocytes are primarily responsible for lipid, glycogen and protein synthesis and storage [42]. These lipid stores are maintained in droplets surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer that includes PE [43]. Given the high metabolic and synthetic activity of adipocytes, PE accumulation in the termite abdomen is expected, as it may act as a reservoir for fatty acids that can be mobilized for energy supply, biosynthetic pathways and colony-level demands [44]. Thus, PE enrichment in the termite abdomen likely reflects its role in nutrient allocation, storage and overall colony fitness.

This study was constrained by the limited availability of 13C-labeled termite samples, which reduced replication and scope, and by minor instrumental artifacts of isotope microscopic imaging reflected in the same-position 13C dots. Isotopic images and spectra (12C, 14N, 13C) could not be acquired simultaneously, necessitating a trade-off between spatial and spectral resolution. While a putative pathway for 13C incorporation into PE was proposed, the exact incorporation sites could not be determined, and other lipid classes were not systematically explored. Future studies using larger sample sets, additional lipid classes and complementary structural approaches will be needed to confirm and extend these findings.

This study successfully visualized the metabolic fate of ingested cellulose in termites at the molecular level through a novel approach of correlative isotope and mass microscopy. Notably, a core technical and biological achievement of this research is the clear visualization, using the iMScopeTM prototype, of the de novo synthesis and abdominal accumulation of 13C-labeled PE. This detection proved difficult in previous studies. These findings elucidated that the iMScopeTM prototype comprising AP-MALDI-MSI could be an ideal option to track 13C-labeled food assimilation in experimental models to understand the fate of 13C-labeled food components.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the iMScope™ prototype, a novel AP-MALDI-MSI technique, enables the detection of 13C-labeled metabolites in 13C-labeled samples. Notably, it successfully identified 13C-integrated PE, which is predominantly localized in the abdomen of termite workers.
