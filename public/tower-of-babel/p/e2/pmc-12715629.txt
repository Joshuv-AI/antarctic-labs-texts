Frontier Orbital Gating of Rectification and Conductance in Tunneling Junctions Comprising Pyridine-Terminated Molecular Wires
Authors: Saurabh Soni, Louise O. H. Hyllested, Marco Carlotti, Sumit Kumar, Jane Kardula, María Camarasa-Gómez, Gemma C. Solomon, Ryan C. Chiechi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This work experimentally investigates a mechanism of rectification in molecular junctions proposed by van Dyck and Ratner, supported by theoretical modeling. The defining feature of the mechanism is the spatial separation of frontier molecular orbitals such that each tracks the two leads independently. We achieve this orbital separation in oligophenyleneethylene molecular wires with electron-rich thiols and electron-poor pyridines at their termini. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations show localization of the frontier molecular orbitals at these termini that increases with the molecular length. Measurements of rectification ratios in molecular ensemble junctions using eutectic Ga–In (EGaIn) top-contacts and Au bottom-contacts reveal a length dependence that is almost completely insensitive to the insertion of a nonconjugated methylene spacer between the thiol anchor and conjugated backbone. Simulations using nonequilibrium Green’s function + DFT methods show that transport is dominated by the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals, which track the EGaIn electrode, leading to rectification. These results validate the approach of creating molecular rectifiers by spatially separating the frontier molecular orbitals and show an approach to modeling their behavior under bias in ensemble junctions.

Introduction

Molecular electronics explores the electronic structure and properties of both ensembles of molecules − and individual molecules, , which are typically contacted by at least two metal electrodes. This investigation often considers the effects of an applied external bias. Over the years, various properties of molecules have been discovered, such as insulators, switches, and rectifiers. ,,− In 1974, Aviram and Ratner proposed a unimolecular rectifier consisting of donor and acceptor fragments separated by a methylene bridge as a prototypical molecular-electronic circuit element. In the intervening decades, numerous molecules in different device geometries have been investigated as potential unimolecular rectifiers operating by the mechanism they proposed. In response, other studies have proposed molecules that defy the donor-bridge-acceptor paradigm, particularly in molecular ensemble junctions (MEJs) ,,, comprising self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) with eutectic Gallium–Indium (EGaIn) as the top electrode. They have suggested thermally assisted hopping-plus-tunnelling (H+T), ,,− the Stark-effect, − and asymmetries created by permanent dipoles − as alternative mechanisms for rectification.

Expanding on their earlier research, van Dyck and Ratner (vDR) reported a rectification mechanism that depends on the tendency of spatially separated frontier molecular levels to track the energies of different electrodes independently. Rather than utilizing donor (i.e., high-lying HOMO) and acceptor (i.e., low-lying LUMO) units, frontier orbitals that are localized on either side of a saturated spacer follow the electrodes in and out of the conduction window at forward and reverse bias, , resulting in asymmetric tunneling charge-transport (rectification). Like most theoretical modelsincluding the original donor-bridge-acceptor modelthe vDR model was developed for single-molecule junctions.

Since MEJs are more stable and thus more usable as next-generation electronic components, it is crucial to understand how these systems behave with regard to rectification. The aforementioned MEJ-based phenomena have been reproduced in several different systems. For instance, molecules based on benzotetrathiafulvalene headgroups were recently reported to rectify depending on the choice of metal substrates, as different frontier levels fall in the conduction window, affecting the H+T mechanism. Furthermore, as the molecular levels participate in electron-transport, temperature-assisted hopping occurs between the metal and the respective energy level, exactly as for the prototypical ferrocene-terminated alkanethiol H+T rectifiers. , Stark-effect-based rectification has been also observed in alkanethiols bearing pyrene and benzo­[a]­pyrene and −COOH head groups. Pure and mixed SAMs of alkanethiols bearing bipyridyl headgroups enabled the observation of the charge-transport transitions from the normal to inverted Marcus regime, , in which the H+T mechanism becomes activationless. , These systems also exhibit a design principle of the vDR mechanism: the bias-induced shifts in the LUMO (on the bipyridyl moiety) and the HOMO (on the thiol anchor) track the EGaIn top electrode and Au bottom electrode, respectively. Bipyridyl-metal complexes terminated by alkanethiols also exhibit control over rectification by changing the alignment and identity of the frontier orbitals with respect to the Fermi level E F by varying the identity of the metal ion. Although all these studies invoke the concept of level-alignment and Fermi pinning at bias, none explicitly follow the vDR mechanism or address the role of orbital resonances. , Finally, these studies also do not address the effect of the two frontier orbitals tracking electrodes independently of each other, which results in rectification.

To investigate the influence of spatially separated frontier orbitals and their interaction with electrodes on molecular rectification, we designed and synthesized a series of highly polarizable p-oligo­(phenylene ethynylene)­pyridine compounds, denoted mOPPyn. Here, n = 1–4 indicates the number of aromatic rings and the prefix m denotes a −CH2– (methylene) bridge between the π system and the thiol anchor, as shown in Figure a. The spatial overlap of the HOMO and the LUMO varies inversely with n, and the highest rectification ratios occur when n = 3, suggesting a correlation between the spatial separation of the frontier orbitals and the magnitude of rectification, as predicted by the vDR mechanism. To control for asymmetric coupling of the HOMO and LUMO to the electrodes, we also synthesized OPPy3 as a control, which is directly conjugated to the thiol anchor. We found that the magnitude of rectification responds to molecular length (n), independent of the coupling to the anchoring thiol and the bottom electrode (m), supporting the assertion of the vDR mechanism that thiols are weakly coupled to the π systems and that interfacial dipoles are more relevant than the degree to which the thiol participates in the π-conjugation.

Results and Discussion

Molecular Design

Asymmetric coupling of a frontier orbital to the leads is not sufficient to induce rectification; , there must (also) be a bias-induced shift (e.g., the Stark-shift and H+T mechanisms) or the recruitment of an additional orbital (e.g., the vDR mechanism). A prerequisite for bias-induced shifts is polarizability, which is often accomplished by inserting a saturated spacer between the π-conjugated fragment (on which the orbital is localized) and the anchoring group. , Similarly, van Dyck and Ratner prescribe a saturated spacer, which both localizes the frontier orbitals on the flanking π-conjugated fragments and decouples them from the opposite electrodes; however, the spacer is not required by the theoretical framework, only that the orbitals which gate transport track the electrodes and are relatively weakly coupled to each other. Further, the model compounds proposed by van Dyck and Ratner (asymmetric oligoenes) would be nontrivial to synthesize, let alone to use for growing SAMs. Thus, we designed a series of molecules that are straightforward to synthesize and grow SAMs from but that still fulfill the requirements of the vDR mechanism. The main requirement is that the frontier molecular orbitals (MOs) track E F, which we will define as pinning, the driving force behind which is polarizability. We designed our molecules without a spacer between the fragments on which the frontier MOs are localized, such that they are fully π-conjugated and the localization of the frontier orbitals is instead controlled by the electronegativities of functional groups. The density functional theory (DFT) orbitals computed at the ωB97M-D4/Def2-TZVP − level in Figure show a clear progression of increasing localization of the orbitals with increasing molecular length. In this design, eq works in our favor since the HOMO will track the Au substrate and the LUMO will track the EGaIn top-contact, such that E F – E MO will depend almost entirely on the interfacial dipoles. The dipole moments of the two anchoring moietiesAu–S and pyridyl-EGaInsimultaneously localize the frontier MOs and lower the frontier MO gap (E g), while the polarizability of the OPE backbone effects pinning. We included a CH2 spacer between the π-conjugated backbone and the thiol anchor to assist the inductive push–pull effect (to localize the frontier MOs) and decrease the tilt angle, but it does not substantially alter the weak coupling between the thiol and the π system that results from poor MO overlap. The spacer also allows us to test that hypothesis and the theory of the vDR mechanism by independently varying the coupling and interfacial dipoles, as discussed below.

Isoplots of molecular orbitals computed at the ωB97M-D4/Def2-TZVP level at ±0.03a.u. show a clear progression of increasing localization of the HOMO (bottom) and LUMO (top) with increasing molecular length.

We chose pyridyl groups as the “pull” in the push–pull because they are structurally analogous to phenyl groups, affecting the frontier MOs without affecting the self-assembly process. Pyridyl groups can act as an anchor for growing SAMs on metallic surfaces, , but several experimental studies ,− have found that molecules containing both thiolate and pyridyl groups preferentially bind to gold surfaces via the thiolate anchoring group. Moreover, Zharnikov and co-workers − and Wöll and co-workers have reported the growth of SAMs of thiolate-anchored, pyridine-terminated π-conjugated molecular wires on metallic surfaces, which they rigorously characterized by (HR)­XPS, NEXAFS, surface IR, and STM. To control for the effects of pyridyl groups, we include OPE3 and mOPE3, which are the same lengths as the mOPPy3 and OPPy3, but which substitute the terminal pyridyl group for a phenyl group. These molecules control for the push–pull effects and provide junctions against which to compare the mOPPyn series, which are unambiguously bound to the electrode through a thiolate anchor.

The operational principle of the mOPPyn MEJs is shown in Figure c,d. At 0 V, E F sits between the frontier orbitals; the work functions (shown above the electrodes in Figure c) are such that the electrodes that are in contact with the ends of the molecules are also closest in energy to the respective frontier MOs, which pin to them (i.e., sit at a fixed E F – E MO). While E g is reduced in a MEJ compared to the gas phase, at zero bias, the orbitals remain separated by E g. As a result of the polarizability of the OPE moieties, the MOs track the electrodes such that at −1 V bias, the LUMO is shifted further apart from the HOMO (Figure d) and E g increases. Since coupling varies proportional to the Lorentzians of HOMO and LUMO (i.e., inversely with E g), the frontier MOs are qualitatively weakly coupled at negative bias. The exact opposite happens when +1 V is applied; E g decreases and, ideally, the frontier MOs become strongly coupled, creating a resonant channel and increasing the transmission probability (Figure e). Thus, the rate of charge-transport through the MEJ is higher at positive bias than at negative bias, and the rectification ratio |R|=|J(+)J(−)|>1 , where J(+) and J(−) are the current densities at positive and negative bias, respectively.

Electrical Characterization

We fabricated MEJs comprising SAMs of the molecular wires shown in Figure a, on template-stripped Au (AuTS, Figure S4) contacted by a cone-shaped non-Newtonian liquid metal alloy, eutectic Ga–In (EGaIn), as the top electrode. An idealized schematic of the AuTS/SAM//Ga2O3/EGaIn MEJ is shown in Figure b, where “/” and “//” denote covalent and noncovalent interfaces, respectively. The AuTS electrode was grounded while external bias was applied to the EGaIn top electrode to acquire tunneling current density vs Voltage (J–V) data. All measurements were performed in a nitrogen-filled flowbox to avoid deleterious environmental effects as described elsewhere. The details of instrument setup and data acquisition methodology are described in the Supporting Information.

The J–V characteristics are shown in Figure . From the log |J| vs V plots in Figure a, it can be seen that the pyridyl-terminated OPPy3 and mOPPy3 are significantly more conductive at positive bias than at negative bias, while OPE3, mOPE3, and OPPy3MeI all yield symmetric J–V curves. This result suggests that the pyridyl groups are necessary to observe rectification, which could be caused by either the pyridyl//EGaIn interface or the electronic structure of the pyridyl-functionalized molecular wires. The methylated derivative OPPy3MeI also exhibits symmetric J–V curves, verifying that free pyridyl (i.e., not phenyl or pyridinium) groups at the SAM//EGaIn interface are necessary for rectification. A previous study of pyridine-terminated oligophenylenes in MEJs showed that the pyridyl//EGaIn interface itself does not cause rectification. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirms the presence of free pyridines for OPPy2–4 and N+ for OPPy3MeI (see Figure S10 and associated discussion for details). Together, these observations suggest that the electronic structure of the molecular wires is responsible for the observed rectification.

Figure b shows the log |J| vs V plots of the mOPPyn series together with mOPE3. The low-bias conductance is comparable across the mOPPyn series, indicating that there is little dependence of conductance on molecular length, which is not the case for OPEn monothiol and dithiol series. , This observation supports the hypothesis that the mOPPyn series fulfills the vDR mechanism criterion that S ≈ 0, meaning that E F – E MO is insensitive to E g, which decreases with increasing conjugation length. Ordinarily, the increase in conjugation length would mitigate the decrease in conductivity that occurs with increasing physical length, but not eliminate it. Table shows that E g decreases by ∼1 eV across the mOPPyn series, which would otherwise lead to such a length-dependent decrease in conductance, leading to a similar tunneling decay coefficient to that observed for OPEs. However, since we do not observe a significant decrease in conductance for mOPPyn, we conclude that the insensitivity of E F – E MO to E g completely mitigates the length-dependent decrease in conductance. Although not common, other S ≈ 0 systems have been reported by observing very small or even negative tunneling decay coefficients, , including polyyne molecular wires with symmetric pyridyl anchoring groups in single-molecular junctions.

Finally, we computed the normalized differential conductance, NDC = (dJ/dV)(J/V) , for all the molecules under study, as shown in Figure e,f. The NDC heatmaps of all of the raw binned data are separately shown in Figure S7 of the Supporting Information. In our previous work, we showed that the presence of peaks in the NDC plots can suggest that the frontier molecular levels come in resonance with the E F level of the metal, increasing the transmission probability of the tunneling charge. Here, it is clear that for OPPy3 and mOPPy3 molecular wires, the NDC curves exhibit a peak at ∼0.6 V. The other molecules in the mOPPy–n series also show a rise in NDC at V → 1, while on the other hand, they all have bowl-shaped NDC for V < 0, same as the control molecules. This suggests the absence of dominance of frontier molecular levels in tunneling conductance for the negative bias range, unlike for the positive bias range for the rectifying molecules. The frontier levels, especially the lowered LUMO because of the electron-withdrawing pyridyl group, come in resonance with the E F of the electrodes. This result is in agreement with the calculated molecular energy levels, as shown in Figure S12, and the transition voltage (V trans) values, as shown in Figure S8 and Table . The V trans + for the pyridine series is smaller than the corresponding V trans –, but specifically for OPPy3 and mOPPy3, the V trans + is very small, suggesting proximity of LUMO level to the E F.

Charge Transport Simulations

To gain further insights into the charge-transport mechanism in the MEJs comprising the pyridine-functionalized OPEn molecules, we performed DFT calculations in conjunction with the nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) formalism for OPPy2, OPPy3, OPPy4, and OPPy5 (details of the calculations are provided in the Supporting Information). The decision to calculate Au–S instead of Au–S–CH2 is based on its simpler geometry and lower degrees of freedom. In contrast, the addition of the −CH2 group increases flexibility and introduces more complex interactions, making it less clear what monolayer structure should be considered (a comparison of the transmission for mOPPy3 and OPPy3 can be found in Section S8 in the Supporting Information). Since our experiments did not reveal a significant difference between these linker groups, we are confident that this choice does not affect the results.

To simulate the experiments, transmission spectra and I–V curves were calculated with biases ranging from −1 to 1 V in a junction consisting of Au – molecule – Aluminum (Al). Al(111) was chosen as the top electrode, since the work function of Al (4.24 eV) is similar to that of EGaIn (4.1–4.2 eV). , The junctions were created following the method of Kang et al., who previously demonstrated that Al(111) can be used as a model for EGaIn due to its similar work function and its lattice constant being comparable to that of Au(111). It is common for calculations of junctions to use Au for both electrodes, but we emphasize the importance of electrode choice, as we observed differences in our calculations depending on the top electrode used. A comparison of the results for the Au and Al top electrodes is provided in Section S8.1.

In order to gain deeper insight into the electronic properties of the system, we analyzed how the transmission spectra respond to different applied biases, revealing a notable shift in the LUMO peak. Figure shows the transmission spectra at −1 V (Figure a) and +1 V (Figure b) for OPPy2, OPPy3, OPPy4, and OPPy5. The orange shading corresponds to the bias window, and the lines indicate the location of the LUMO peak at zero bias. At −1 V, the LUMO peak for all of the molecules is centered around 0.5 eV, shifted up in energy compared to zero bias, as indicated by the arrows. However, at +1 V, the LUMO peaks shift down in energy for OPPy3, OPPy4, and OPPy5. The current is calculated based on the area under the transmission curve within the bias window. As shown in Figure , the peaks shrink with increasing molecular length, suggesting that the current decreases with molecular length. The calculated I–V curves demonstrating this can be seen in Figure S15.

Since the peaks at +1 V shift down in energy and into the bias window, compared to zero bias, more current is observed at +1 V than at −1 V, resulting in rectification, consistent with the experimental results. However, the shortest calculated molecule, OPPy2, seems to behave differently from the others, as the LUMO peak moves up at both +1 and −1 V. We argue that this is caused by a strong interaction with the electrode, evident from both the broader peaks seen for OPPy2 in the transmission and the delocalization of the MO, as shown in Figure . For the longer molecules, the LUMO peak seems to follow the chemical potential of the right electrode with bias. This causes the peak to move up in energy at −1 V and down in energy at +1 V. A similar movement of the LUMO peak can be observed for the bare molecule in an electric field, indicating that it might not solely be pinning to the electrode that causes this shift, but the electric field may somehow enhance or stabilize the effect (for more information about the LUMO peak movement, see Section S8). This behavior is consistent with the vDR mechanism as depicted in Figure c–e, where, i.e., the frontier orbitals are pinned to their respective electrodes and shift independently of each other with bias. To understand the effect of the dipole moment on the observed movement of the LUMO peak, the dipole moments of the molecules were calculated without electrodes. These calculations were performed with two molecules placed in parallel between two plates, making them independent of the electrodes. The dipole moments follow the same trend observed for the LUMO peak: a splitting of the dipoles at +1 V and a gathering of the dipoles at −1 V. The dipole moment and the LUMO peak can be seen in Figure a,b. Figure a illustrates the movement of the LUMO peak with bias. Here, the gray triangles indicate the opening of the bias window with increasing voltage. At −1 V, the LUMO peak for all molecular lengths gathers around 0.5 eV, while at +1 V, the LUMO peaks split, as observed in Figure . Comparing this to Figure b, a similar trend is evident: the dipole moments of the molecules gather at −1 V and spread out at +1 V. This observation illustrates that it is not solely the asymmetry of the electrodes that creates the rectification. ,

The observation of a gathering in both the LUMO peak and the dipole moment at −1 V suggests that the spread of the LUMO across the junction leads to similar transmission characteristics and dipole moments for all molecules in the series. Conversely, at +1 V, the shift of the LUMO toward the pyridyl terminus creates a more pronounced difference in both the dipole moment and the LUMO peak location. This aligns with the molecular design, as we observe that the LUMOs are localized toward the N, pinning to the EGaIn top electrode.

The calculations predict increasing MO localization with length, leading to shifts in the transmission peaks that follow the chemical potential of the electrode and the effect of the electric field under bias. Conductance increases when these peaks shift down in energy at +1 V, whereas conductance decreases at −1 V when the peaks move up in energy, out of the bias window. The shift for mOPPy2/OPPy2 seems to be more affected by the strong coupling to the electrodes than by pinning or the electric field, causing the peak to move up at both biases and thus predicting R ∼ 0. The longer molecules all exhibit greater shifts, and indeed, simulated J/V curves (Figure S16) predict that R increases with length for mOPPyn/OPPyn when n > 2. However, transmission peaks need to be within the bias window to affect R, and as Figures a and show, the positions of the LUMO peaks do exhibit a length dependence that places mOPPy3/OPPy3 closest to E F at +1 V. It is plausible that the peaks could overshoot E F and be pushed outside of the bias window. Indeed, the calculated J/V (Figure S16) curves for the longer molecules show a peak in R around +0.2 V (Figure S16a).

Overall, the DFT calculations are remarkably consistent with the data in Figure , which also show R peaking and leveling off as well as NDC plots showing a transmission peak entering the bias window at V > 0 V. One clear divergence is that calculations predict rectification for mOPPy4, however, it could be that they underestimate the length dependence, and mOPPy4 is already long enough to overshoot E F. Figure f does show a resonance entering the bias window for mOPPy4, but the onset is so close to +1 V that R only starts to increase around +0.75 V. Experimentally, molecular wires eventually undergo a tunneling-to-hopping transition with increasing length, leading to a loss of length dependence in J and R. Unfortunately, we could not access mOPPy5 synthetically (due to decreasing yields and solubility with increasing length) to determine if mOPPy4 is an outlier or if there is indeed a transition in the mechanism, but that is another possible explanation for the divergence between calculations and experiment.

As discussed in the Supporting Information, the raw NDC and transition voltage data support the main theoretical finding qualitatively; the LUMO is pulled toward resonance faster than the HOMO for mOPPy3/OPPy3. This observation can be quantified using a single-level model (SLM) that produces a coupling parameter Γ, an energy offset ε, and a Stark shift γ to describe a single-level transport model. A SLM based on transition voltages developed by Bâldea and validated by Frisbie does exactly that, explaining the slight rectification observed in SAMs of alkanethiols. This Stark shift captures the direction and degree in which the resonance closest to E F shifts with bias as a result of the Stark effect acting on MOs. We have previously used it to explain rectification induced by molecular dipoles and the unusual length dependence of the Seebeck effect in alkanethiols. Conclusions

The vDR model predicts that spatially separated frontier MOs will induce rectification. We have successfully realized this prediction experimentally, but found that only one orbital is necessary to realize rectification. Our molecular rectifiers also did not require a nonconjugated spacer, relying instead on the push–pull effect of conjugated molecular wires terminated with thiols on one end and pyridyl rings on the other. This design resulted in clear rectification, peaking in magnitude for OPPy3 and showing no sensitivity to the insertion of a CH2 spacer between the π-conjugated backbone and the thiol anchor.

We validated the predictions of the vDR mechanism with simulations of molecular junctions that agree remarkably well with the experimental data from SAMs. These results show that theoretical predictions and simulations on molecules can readily translate into practical, device-like platforms incorporating molecular ensembles. They also highlight a simpler synthetic design for vDR rectifiers that does not require a physical spacer to separate the frontier MOs. Looking ahead, the insights gained from this study suggest promising avenues for the development of molecular rectifiers. Continued theoretical and experimental investigations will be essential to refine these concepts and enhance their performance and manufacturability.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

The synthesis of OPE3, OPPy3, mOPPy1, and mOPPy3 has been reported previously. ,, The synthesis of the rest of the molecules is provided in the Supporting Information. Extra-dry toluene and 1,8-diazabicyclo(5.4.0)­undec-7-ene (DBU) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 99.99% Au was obtained from Schöne Edelmetaal B.V., and “Norland 61” optical adhesive was purchased from Norland Products Inc. for preparing AuTS substrates. Uvitron product Intelly ray 600 (UV0832) was used to cure the optical adhesive.

SAM Preparation and EGaIn Measurements

SAMs were prepared using established methodology. 50 μM solutions of the molecules were prepared in extra-dry Toluene under an inert nitrogen atmosphere. 3 mL of this solution was used, and a 100 nm AuTS 1 × 1 cm substrate was immersed overnight. 0.05 mL of 17 mM DBU in extra-dry Toluene was added to the solution for 1.5 h. Following this, the substrates were rinsed with 3 mL of ethanol dropwise. The substrate was dried under nitrogen flow, and EGaIn measurements were performed inside a flowbox with controlled atmosphere (O2 = 1–3%; relative humidity < 10%), the details of which are described in the Supporting Information, same as our previous report.

Density Functional Theory

The calculations were conducted using density functional theory DFT combined with nonequilibrium Green’s Function formalism and the Landauer formula to determine the transmission and conductance. QuantumATK (version S-2021.06-SP1) − was employed for these calculations, utilizing the GGA PBE functional. , A double-ζ basis set and polarization functions on all molecular atoms were used along with periodic boundary conditions. Additionally, noncovalent interactions were accounted for by incorporating the Grimme DFT D3 dispersion correction. Further details regarding the calculations can be found in the Supporting Information.

XPS Measurements

XPS measurements were conducted by using an Escalab 250XI spectrometer from Thermo Fisher Scientific (West Sussex, UK). The instrument operated in constant analyzer energy (CAE) mode with a monochromatic Al–Kα source (hν = 1486.74 eV) and a spot size of 900 μm. Survey scans and narrow scans were acquired at pass energies of 100 and 50 eV, respectively. During data acquisition, the pressure in the measurement chamber was maintained below 1e-9mbar. Further details regarding the analysis of XPS data can be found in the Supporting Information.
