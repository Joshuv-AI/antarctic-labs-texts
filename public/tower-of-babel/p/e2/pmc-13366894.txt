Comparative analysis of pathogen detection methods in spinal infections: a retrospective study of 287 cases
Authors: Qi Dong, Yanchao Wang, Jian Guo, Jingcheng Hu, Meng Zhao, Dageng Huang, Jiuqin Zhou, Lingbo Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aims to systematically evaluate the efficacy and timeliness of various specimens and diagnostic methods used to identify pathogens in spinal infections, thereby informing clinical diagnostic pathways.MethodWe conducted a retrospective examination of patients diagnosed with spinal infections between January 2023 and January 2025. We compared positivity rates and time-to-detection for microbial culture, mNGS, qPCR and RBT/SAT across different types of specimens: blood, muscle/vertebral biopsy, intraoperative specimens, and postoperative drainage.ResultsAmong 287 patients, 213 had confirmed pathogens: non-specific bacteria (42.0%), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (34.7%), and Brucella spp. (21.1%). The overall positivity rate for mNGS was 83.0%; for brucellosis serology (RBT/SAT), the median time to result was 2.0 days. For M. tuberculosis, mNGS yielded a positivity rate of 81.8%. For Brucella, RBT/SAT showed a sensitivity of 91.2% and a median detection time of 3 days. For non-specific bacteria, culture, mNGS, and qPCR performed comparably. The type of specimen significantly influenced the outcomes: blood cultures were relatively rapid but exhibited lower yields (35.7%); ultrasound-guided muscle abscess aspirates demonstrated favorable yield and speed across methods.ConclusionmNGS offers high sensitivity and rapidity; however, traditional culture and qPCR remain vital. Blood and muscle abscess aspirates are useful for rapid diagnosis. Implementing a systematic diagnostic workflow can enhance both the accuracy and timeliness of managing spinal infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13546-6.

Introduction

Spinal infection, an infectious disease characterized by the invasion of pathogens into vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and adjacent tissues, has exhibited an increasing incidence in recent years and poses significant risks of disability and mortality [1]. The early and precise identification of the causative agents is crucial for guiding targeted antimicrobial therapy and enhancing patient prognosis [2]. However, definitive pathogen identification remains a substantial clinical challenge due to several factors, including the prevalent use of antibiotics, the invasive nature of the procedures required to obtain specimens, and the typically low pathogen load in bone tissue samples [3].

Currently, the etiological diagnosis of spinal infections encounters multiple obstacles. Although conventional microbial culture and histopathological examination are the primary diagnostic methods, they are limited by low bacterial detection rates and extended turnaround times, often ranging from several days to weeks, which do not align with the clinical need for rapid and accurate diagnosis and treatment [4]. Recent advancements in molecular diagnostic technologies, such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), have markedly improved the efficiency of pathogen detection, reducing the detection time to within 1–3 days [5, 6]. Nonetheless, qPCR can only detect pathogens with previously known sequences, and mNGS, despite its broad-spectrum capability, is limited by high costs and a significant reliance on reference databases [7]. Additionally, the types of clinically obtainable specimens are varied, including blood, image-guided aspirates (e.g., from muscle abscesses or vertebral biopsies), intraoperative tissues, and postoperative drainage fluids, with pathogen detection rates that differ markedly among these specimen types [8, 9]. Faced with multiple testing methods and diverse specimen types, clinicians often encounter dilemmas in choosing the most appropriate method, lacking an evidence-based framework to guide their decision-making.

Although some studies have explored the diagnostic value of individual methods or single specimen types, there is a dearth of systematic and comprehensive evaluations concerning the diagnostic efficacy and timeliness of various combinations of detection methods and specimen types in the context of spinal infections. To bridge this gap, we conducted a retrospective observational study that included 287 patients with spinal infections. Our study systematically compared the positivity rates and time-to-detection of different etiological diagnostic methods across various clinical specimen types. Additionally, we performed a stratified analysis based on pathogen categories to thoroughly assess their overall diagnostic performance. Our objective is to provide direct evidence for establishing a systematic, evidence-based diagnostic workflow for the etiological diagnosis of spinal infections.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Honghui Hospital (Approval No. 2026-Lunli Yijian-KY-019-01) and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Given that the research involved a retrospective review of medical records, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for obtaining informed consent from patients.

Data collection

A retrospective analysis of clinical data was performed on patients diagnosed with spinal infections and hospitalized in the Department of Orthopedics at Xi’an Honghui Hospital from January 2023 to January 2025. Diagnoses were based on medical histories, laboratory tests, and imaging examinations. Collected data included demographic and epidemiological characteristics such as gender, age, occupation, and place of residence, along with the methods employed for etiological testing and the types of specimens collected for pathogen identification.

Diagnoses adhered to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) criteria and included vertebral osteomyelitis (M46.2, M46.2 × 021, M46.2 × 041, M46.2 × 061), vertebral infection (M46.5 × 091), discitis (M46.401-M46.403, M46.300 × 091), spinal-related abscesses (psoas abscess [M60.008, A18.8000 × 027 + M63], epidural abscess [G06.203], muscle abscess [M60.000 × 093], paravertebral abscess [M79.800 × 081]), spinal abscess (M46.501-M46.504), brucellar spondylitis (A23.9 + M49.1, M49.1), and tuberculous osteomyelitis (A18.009 + M49.0, A18.000 × 081 + M73.8, A18.000 × 049 + M49.0, A18.000 × 064 + M90.0, A18.010 + M49.0*).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (a) patients with a confirmed diagnosis of spinal infection; (b) patients hospitalized for the first time due to spinal infection; and (c) infections caused by bacterial pathogens.

Exclusion criteria included: (a) identification of the causative pathogen prior to hospital admission; (b) incomplete clinical data; (c) administration of antibiotics within one week before admission; (d) absence of definitive pathogen testing; and (e) confirmation of the pathogen as a fungus or parasite.

Laboratory examinations and diagnostic criteria

Laboratory examinations

All laboratory tests were conducted at designated facilities. Routine bacterial cultures, including those from blood, wound exudate, vertebral body aspirates, muscle abscess aspirates, and intervertebral specimens, along with pathological examinations, were performed at the Microbiology Laboratory and the Pathology Laboratory of Xi’an Honghui Hospital, respectively. qPCR, including tests for Xpert MTB/RIF, was also executed in the Microbiology Laboratory of Xi’an Honghui Hospital. The Rose Bengal plate test (RBT) and the standard tube agglutination test (SAT) were administered at Xi’an Eighth Hospital (Xi’an Infectious Disease Center). Positive results were defined by an SAT titer exceeding 1:160 and a positive RBT. mNGS was conducted by Hugobiotech Co., Ltd. using a NextSeq™ 550 sequencer.

Blood samples, which were the initial specimens collected following hospital admission and before the initiation of antibiotic therapy, were cultured in quadruplicate. Wound exudate samples were obtained by thoroughly swabbing purulent material from the wound after disinfecting the surrounding skin. Spinal vertebral aspirates were acquired as bone tissue samples via a CT-guided percutaneous transpedicular approach. Muscle abscess aspirates were collected under ultrasound guidance from the psoas or paraspinal muscles. Intervertebral samples, which included disc material, annulus fibrosus, endplate cartilage, and adjacent bone tissue, were collected during open surgery.

The following criteria were applied to wound exudate swabs to distinguish true infection from superficial colonization or contamination: (1) a positive culture was considered clinically significant only when the same organism was isolated from at least two separate swab specimens collected at different time points, or from a concurrent deep tissue or intraoperative specimen (e.g., biopsy or aspirate); (2) isolates of common skin flora (e.g., coagulase-negative staphylococci, Corynebacterium spp.) were interpreted as contaminants unless they grew in pure culture with moderate-to-heavy growth and were accompanied by histopathological evidence of inflammation (e.g., neutrophilic infiltration). Cases not meeting these criteria were classified as “no pathogen identified” for the swab method.

Diagnostic criteria

The diagnosis of a spinal infection was independently made by two senior attending physicians from both the Spine Surgery and Infectious Diseases departments. The diagnosis was based on a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s clinical presentation, which included symptoms such as back pain, fever, and weight loss; signs such as localized percussion tenderness; laboratory results, for example, leukocyte count, C-reactive protein, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate; and imaging features, for example, bone destruction, epidural abscess, and discitis. Specific pathogen confirmation adhered to predefined criteria: Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection was diagnosed by mNGS and/or PCR, since our laboratory did not meet the biosafety level requirements for mycobacterial culture; Brucella infection was confirmed by a positive RBT/SAT and/or mNGS result; and nonspecific bacterial infections, such as those caused by Staphylococcus aureus, were diagnosed with a positive result from any one of the methods among bacterial culture, PCR, or mNGS. It should be particularly noted that Staphylococcus epidermidis was considered a true pathogen only when isolated from intraoperative deep tissue or blood cultures under aseptic conditions, accompanied by consistent clinical, laboratory, and intraoperative evidence of infection, and when it was the predominant organism in culture.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0) and Microsoft Excel 2021. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables not normally distributed were expressed as the median [interquartile range (IQR)] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. For comparisons among multiple groups, post hoc analyses were performed using Dunn’s test with a Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) and compared using Welch’s t test. Categorical variables are summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For comparisons involving multiple groups, pairwise tests were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. All tests were two-tailed, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Result

Patient baseline characteristics

This study included a total of 287 patients diagnosed with spinal infections. The cohort consisted of 182 males (63.4%) and 105 females (36.6%). The ages ranged from 7 to 87 years, with a median age of 61.0 years (IQR: 53.0–69.0 years) (Fig. 1A). Regarding occupational distribution, farmers were the predominant group, representing 120 patients (41.8%) (Fig. 1B). Geographically, the majority of patients originated from Shaanxi Province, comprising 193 patients (67.2%).

The pathogens in monomicrobial infections demonstrated a significant concentration (Table 1), with M. tuberculosis (MTB) being the most prevalent, identified in 68 cases (31.92% of all confirmed infections). This was followed by Brucella spp. (44 cases, 20.7%) and Staphylococcus aureus (29 cases, 13.6%). Other detected pathogens included Staphylococcus epidermidis (18 cases, 8.5%), Escherichia coli (12 cases, 5.6%), and various others (22 cases, 10.3%) were also detected.

Polymicrobial infections were noted in twenty cases, featuring 17 different combinations of pathogens (Table 1). MTB was frequently involved in these infections, being present in 7 cases, including three co-infections with Staphylococcus aureus. Staphylococcus aureus was also a common participant in polymicrobial infections, involved in 7 cases. Considering both monomicrobial and polymicrobial findings, the overall prevalence of MTB in this cohort was 35.2% (75/213), and that of Staphylococcus aureus was 16.9% (36/213).

Descriptive results of etiological tests in real-world practice

Four etiological tests were used in different patient populations based on clinical suspicion. The descriptive results for each test are presented in Table 2. Among the 67 patients who underwent mNGS, 56 (83.6%) were positive, with a median time from admission to result of 7.0 days (IQR 5.0–11.0). RBT/SAT, which specifically targets Brucella, was performed in 84 patients; 30 (35.7%) were positive, with a median time to result of 2.5 days (IQR 1.0–4.3). Microbial culture was the most frequently ordered test (215 tests), yielding 69 positives (32.1%) and a median time to result of 7.0 days (IQR 4.0–10.0). qPCR was performed in 205 patients, with 98 positives (47.8%) and a median time to result of 9.0 days (IQR 5.0–14.0).

Impact of diagnostic methods on detection rate and time

For spinal tuberculosis, diagnostic positivity rates varied significantly among methods (Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.001). The rate for mNGS was 91.3% and for qPCR was 77.5%, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). Reporting times were not significantly different overall (Kruskal-Wallis H = 0.135, P = 0.713), with 7 days for mNGS and 9.0 days for qPCR. Of note, these turnaround times are system-dependent and may vary across different laboratory settings.

In cases of Brucella infection, both serological tests (RBT/SAT) and mNGS demonstrated significantly higher positivity rates, 91.2% and 78.6% respectively, than conventional culture at 6.3% (P < 0.001), with no significant difference between RBT/SAT and mNGS. Regarding timeliness, the detection time for SAT/RBT [median 3 days (IQR: 1–5 days)] was significantly shorter than that for mNGS [median 8 days (IQR: 4–12 days)] (Mann-Whitney U = 69.5, P = 0.003). Thus, serological testing (RBT/SAT) demonstrated favorable performance for diagnosing brucellosis in this cohort.

For non-specific bacterial infections, the positivity rates for culture, mNGS, and qPCR were 76.47% (65/85), 85.71% (24/28), and 67.74% (42/62) respectively, with no significant difference among them (χ²=3.55, P = 0.169). The median detection times were 7.0, 7.0, and 7.5 days, respectively, also showing no significant differences among the groups (Kruskal-Wallis χ²=0.295, P = 0.863). This suggests comparable diagnostic sensitivity and timeliness among these three methods for such infections.

Impact of specimen type on detection rate and time

For non-specific bacteria primarily detected by culture (Section “Impact of diagnostic methods on detection rate and time”), the positivity rates were: blood 35.7% (15/42), wound exudate 75.0% (6/8), muscle abscess aspirate 63.0% (17/27), vertebral body aspirate 64.3% (9/14), intervertebral specimen 55.0% (22/40), and postoperative wound drainage 66.7% (14/21). These differences were not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.664). The time-to-positivity varied significantly (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.001), being shortest for blood (median 4 days) and longest for wound drainage (median 20.5 days).

The mNGS positivity rates were 76.9% (10/13) for muscle abscess aspirate, 87.5% (28/32) for vertebral body aspirate, and 81.0% (17/21) for intervertebral specimens, with no significant difference (p = 0.650). Overall detection time did not differ significantly (Kruskal-Wallis H = 4.38, P = 0.112), and no specific inter-group differences were observed in post-hoc comparisons, possibly due to the limited sample size.

Given the null detection rate for Brucella by qPCR noted in Section “Impact of diagnostic methods on detection rate and time”, the analysis was confined to MTB and non-specific bacteria. PCR positivity rates varied significantly across specimen types (Fisher’s exact test, p<0.001), with the highest rate in muscle abscess aspirate (82.1%, 23/28), followed by intervertebral specimens (52.3%, 56/107), wound exudate (20.0%, 2/10), vertebral body aspirate (14.8%, 4/27), and postoperative wound drainage (14.3%, 14/98). The time from admission to pathogen identification by qPCR also differed significantly (Kruskal-Wallis test, H = 12.87, p < 0.05), being shortest for Wound Exudate (median 1.0 day) and longest for postoperative wound drainage (median 17.0 days).

Concordance analysis between pathology and molecular/serological results

To evaluate the concordance between granulomatous pathological changes and definitive etiological diagnosis, we compared histopathological findings with molecular/serological test results(Supplementary file S1). Among 99 patients with granulomatous changes on histopathology, 74 (74.7%) were confirmed as spinal tuberculosis by TB-PCR and/or mNGS; 4 (4.0%) were confirmed as brucellosis by mNGS and/or RBT/SAT; the remaining 21 cases (21.2%) tested negative for both tuberculosis and brucellosis, with an undetermined etiology (Table 3).

Discussion

The etiological diagnosis of spinal infections presents a significant challenge in clinical practice; however, the early and rapid identification of the causative pathogen is essential for effective infection management. To address this issue, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 287 patients diagnosed with spinal infections. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the relationship between various etiological detection methods and specimen types to ascertain the optimal diagnostic strategies for different bacterial pathogens.

Based on the findings presented in Table 4, a prioritized diagnostic approach for spinal infections can be established by considering both the detection efficacy of specimen types and their clinical accessibility. Initially, blood culture, RBT/SAT, and T-SPOT (Supplementary Table S1) is recommended as the primary screening test owing to its convenience in sampling and rapid turnaround for results. Subsequently, ultrasound-guided aspiration of muscle abscesses, which is a minimally invasive procedure that yields readily obtainable specimens, demonstrates high positivity rates and short detection times across various testing modalities, thus serving as a crucial secondary diagnostic tool. The third recommended method, percutaneous vertebral body aspirate, although technically complex, provides high detection rates and relatively short turnaround times in both culture and mNGS, marking it as a valuable tertiary option. Lastly, intraoperatively acquired intervertebral specimens, primarily used for histological purposes, do not facilitate direct pathogen identification, require surgical acquisition, and involve prolonged processing times; therefore, they are generally reserved as a supplementary method when other approaches are inconclusive. Furthermore, integrating evidence from Table 5, the optimal diagnostic methods for different pathogens are delineated as follows: MTB is most effectively detected by mNGS and PCR; Brucella species are best identified by the RBT/SAT serological tests, followed by mNGS; and non-specific pyogenic bacteria are optimally detected by mNGS, followed by culture and PCR. Building upon these specimen- and pathogen-specific principles, this study further integrates and proposes a systematic diagnostic workflow for the etiological identification of spinal infections, as depicted in Fig. 2.

This study confirms that mNGS technology achieves a high pathogen detection rate for spinal infections. Specifically, the detection rate for vertebral bone aspirate was 87.5%(Table 5). These findings align with the 77.9% detection rate reported by Li et al. for vertebral biopsy specimens [7]; the rate reported by Shi et al. for surgical samples was 77.17% [8]. Notably, the mNGS detection rate for Brucella was 78.6%, its detection rate for MTB reached 91.3%, highlighting its utility in identifying specific pathogens [10, 11]. Although the mNGS assay typically requires only 24–48 h [12], our study observed the total time from patient admission to definitive pathogen identification by mNGS was not short(median 7.0 days, IQR 4.0–9.0) (Table 5). This is primarily attributed to the time required for preoperative evaluation and biopsy preparation. Despite its high detection rate and efficiency, the substantial cost of mNGS testing remains the primary factor limiting its widespread clinical adoption.

In this study, qPCR (including Xpert MTB/RIF) demonstrated a pathogen detection rate of 77.5% for MTB and 67.7% for non-specific bacteria. The MTB detection rate was 77.5%, compared with 65.1% reported by Karthek et al. [13] and 27.1% by Misra et al. [14], a discrepancy potentially attributable to specimen selection. Notably, the qPCR detection rate for Brucella was 0%, which contrasts markedly with the approximately 93% rates reported by other teams [15, 16]; the reason for this discrepancy remains unclear. Furthermore, qPCR showed a high detection rate of 82.1% for muscle abscess aspirate specimens, with a median detection time of 4.0 h (Table 4). Therefore, for patients with spinal infection accompanied by a paravertebral muscle abscess, percutaneous aspiration followed by rapid qPCR testing represents a viable diagnostic option.

Despite the significant advances in molecular diagnostic techniques, conventional bacterial culture continues to hold an indispensable role in pathogen detection [17]. In this study, which focused on spinal infections caused by pyogenic bacteria, conventional culture of various specimen types yielded positivity rates ranging from 35.7% to 75% (Table 4). However, it is worth noting that among the 15 postoperative spinal infection cases, culture identified the pathogen in 13 cases (86.7%, Supplementary File S2). Although the positivity rate for blood cultures was relatively lower at 35.7%, it is the most readily accessible specimen type and one that is generally well-accepted by patients, and a positive blood culture strongly indicates the presence of bacteremia or even sepsis [18, 19]. This observation is consistent with findings by Zhang et al., which underscore the critical importance of tissue culture in the detection of common pyogenic bacteria [20]. Additionally, culture can compensate for the inability of molecular methods to provide antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, which is essential for the formulation of targeted antimicrobial therapies [4, 5].

This study further confirms the vital role of pathological examination (suggesting granulomatous changes) in diagnosing spinal tuberculosis (MTB) infection, achieving a confirmed positivity rate of 92.8% (64/69, see Supplementary Table S1). However, in addition to Mycobacterium tuberculosis, other pathogens such as Brucella [21], non-tuberculous mycobacteria [22], fungi [23], as well as non-infectious conditions such as sarcoidosis and even syphilis [24], can also cause granulomatous changes. An illustrative case further emphasizes the necessity of a comprehensive diagnostic approach: in a patient presenting with granulomatous inflammation on pathology, negative RBT/SAT but positive for T-SPOT, Brucella was ultimately identified by mNGS, and targeted treatment resulted in a favorable outcome. Therefore, to exclude other granulomatous diseases, all patients are recommended to undergo the following standardized diagnostic workup: (1) mycobacterial culture and Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex PCR; (2) for patients with a history of livestock exposure, serum Brucella agglutination test and/or Brucella-specific PCR/mNGS; (3) special fungal staining (GMS, PAS) and culture; (4) identification of non-tuberculous mycobacteria by 16 S rRNA sequencing; and (5) when necessary, testing of angiotensin-converting enzyme and gallium-67 scanning to evaluate sarcoidosis.

This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, it exclusively included cases of bacterial spinal infections, thereby lacking a sufficient number of cases involving fungal or parasitic agents to allow for a robust statistical analysis. Secondly, due to the retrospective design, indication bias cannot be excluded; for instance, infections with neurological impairment may have been preferentially sampled via surgical biopsy, inflating its relative performance. Thirdly, variations in test accessibility, turnaround time, patients’ economic status, sample types, and clinicians’ ordering preferences led to unequal numbers of patients undergoing each test. Fourthly, our findings are derived from a single tertiary spine center in China and may not be generalizable to populations with different demographic characteristics, comorbidity profiles, microbial epidemiology, or healthcare resource levels. Even within similar epidemiological settings, caution is warranted due to potential differences in laboratory protocols and case mix; external validation in diverse, multicenter cohorts is essential. Finally, the low culture positivity for M. tuberculosis and Brucella likely reflects laboratory limitations: use of solid media without automated liquid systems, inconsistent application of prolonged incubation and special handling for Brucella, and the unavailability of routine culture for these fastidious pathogens even in a tertiary care hospital – all of which may contribute to false-negative results in conventional microbiological examination [25].

Conclusion

The etiological diagnosis of spinal infections critically depends on the choice of diagnostic methods and specimens. While mNGS demonstrated higher detection sensitivity and speed in this cohort, traditional methods such as culture, and qPCR are indispensable. For rapid pathogen detection, preferred specimens include blood cultures and ultrasound-guided muscle abscess aspirates. Based on the analyzed relationships among pathogens, methods, and specimen types, we propose a standardized diagnostic algorithm to assist clinicians in achieving faster and more accurate etiological diagnoses, thereby facilitating timely and targeted therapy.
