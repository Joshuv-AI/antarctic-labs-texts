Spatial analysis of tumor immune microenvironment of TNBC with different neoadjuvant chemotherapy outcomes using multiplex Immunofluorescence
Authors: Inho Park, Su-Jin Shin, Heounjeong Go, Jiwon Ko, Soong June Bae, Sung Gwe Ahn, Joon Jeong, Jee Hung Kim, Yoon Jin Cha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is characterized by aggressive biological behavior and poor prognosis. However, TNBC exhibits higher immunogenicity than other breast cancer subtypes, making it more responsive to immunotherapy. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is the standard treatment for early high-risk TNBC; however, reliable biomarkers for predicting NAC response remain elusive. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) are recognized as predictive markers of NAC response in TNBC, yet discordant cases remain, such as tumors with high TIL levels but poor response. This study aimed to further elucidate the immune environment of TNBC by analyzing TIL, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, and tumor-stroma ratio using pretreatment biopsy tissue slides from 16 patients with TNBC treated with NAC.MethodMultiplexed immunofluorescence for CD8, FOXP3, CD4, CD20, and CK was employed to investigate immune cell (IC) composition and spatial interactions within the tumor immune microenvironment. Cell to cell distance and comparison of subcellular proportion of IC and were analyzed by treatment response, TIL, and PD-L1 status.ResultsSignificant differences were found in IC composition and distribution between patients achieving pathologic complete response (pCR) and those with residual disease (non-pCR). The pCR group exhibited significant enrichment of cluster of differentiation CD8 + IC and CD20 + IC in both tumor and stromal regions, suggesting their critical role in mediating an effective NAC response. In contrast, non-pCR cases showed higher proportions of immunosuppressive CD4 + FOXP3 + IC, particularly in the tumor region. High TIL levels were associated with pronounced B-T cell interactions, as evidenced by the significant clustering of CD20 + and CD8 + ICs near tumor cells, highlighting their cooperative role in antitumor immunity.ConclusionsIn conclusion, our findings suggest that tumoral CD8 + and CD20 + ICs are pivotal determinants of NAC response in TNBC. The enrichment of CD20 + IC under high-TIL conditions underscores the potential role of B-T cell interactions in shaping immune-mediated chemotherapy responses. These insights provide a foundation for leveraging immune-based biomarkers to stratify patients with TNBC and optimize NAC outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02132-4.

Background

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a biologically aggressive breast cancer subtype that accounts for 10–15% of all breast cancers. It is defined by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression, as well as human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER2) amplification [1]. TNBC is a heterogeneous group of disease entities that can be further divided into six subtypes based on molecular features [2]. Compared with other breast cancer subtypes which are generally considered less immunogenic [3], TNBC exhibits higher immunogenicity [4], and has shown promising clinical outcomes with immunotherapy [5, 6].

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is currently the standard treatment for TNBC, even in the early stages [7, 8]. Higher stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) predict increased pathologic complete response (pCR) and superior clinical outcome in patients with TNBC treated with NAC [9, 10]. In the cancer-immunity cycle, various immune cell (IC) components, along with other cellular elements within the tumor microenvironment, initiate and maintain effective antitumor immunity [11]. Moreover, the coordination of diverse B and T lymphocyte populations at different stages of development determines the effectiveness of adaptive immunity and, thus, the survival outcome of patients [12, 13]. High TIL levels in TNBC indicate an inflamed tumor, which is considered a potential target for immune checkpoint inhibitors [14]. CD8 + T cells have been identified as a key component of anti-cancer immunity in many solid cancers, including TNBC [15, 16].

Despite the general trends, not all patients with TNBC with high TIL levels respond positively to NAC, and similarly, not all patients with low TIL levels are non-responsive. To address this discrepancy, our study examined the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in TNBC samples obtained before NAC treatment using multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF). This technique allows for the detailed identification and quantification of various IC types within TIME. In addition, mIHC provides spatial information by mapping each cell, which enables the analysis of spatial distribution of IC and tumor cells (TC), as well as the relative locations of different IC populations [17]. In this study, we categorized IC into several types based on CD markers and FOXP3 expression: CD8 for cytotoxic T cells, CD20 for B cells, CD4 for helper T cells, and CD4 + FOXP3 + IC for T regulatory cells (Treg). We aimed to explore the TIME in treatment-naïve TNBC and its role in NAC response and to elucidate the differences in TIME among patients with similar TIL levels but varying treatment outcomes, as well as those with differing TIL levels but similar treatment responses.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed the electronic medical records of patients with stage II–III early TNBC treated with NAC at Gangnam Severance Hospital between October 2018 and December 2020 (n = 160). Among them, treatment-naïve biopsy samples were available in 16 patients who underwent NAC with anthracycline and/or taxane, followed by curative breast surgery. Clinicopathologic data, including age at diagnosis, histologic grade (HG) based on the Nottingham grading system [18], ER, PR, HER2, clinical subtypes of breast cancer, Ki-67 index, TIL level, pretreatment clinical T and N stages, treatment regimens, and post-treatment pathologic T and N stages, were collected from electronic medical records. Clinical stages were classified according to the seventh edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer guidelines [19]. A pCR was defined as ypT0/is N0.

Histopathologic and Immune Profiling

Histologic evaluation for TILs and tumor-stroma ratio (TSR)

The histological slides of the patients were reviewed by two pathologists (SSJ and YJC). TIL levels were assessed in accordance with guidelines proposed by the International TIL Working Group [20]. All mononuclear cells, including lymphocytes and plasma cells (but excluding polymorphonuclear leukocytes), were counted. Cases with TIL levels >10% were categorized as the high-TIL group, whereas those ≤ 10% were categorized as the low-TIL group based on the median TIL level of the included cases. TSR was defined as the tumor cellularity relative to the surrounding stroma within the overall tumor bed [21, 22]. For the quantification of TSR, assessments were undertaken using scoring percentages, incremented at intervals of 10%.

IHC for clinical subtype.

Nuclear staining values ≥ 1% were considered positive for ER (clone 6F11; dilution 1:200; Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and PR (clone 16; dilution 1:500; Leica Biosystems) [23]. HER2 staining (clone 4B5; dilution 1:5; Ventana Medical System, Oro Valley, AZ, USA) was evaluated according to the 2018 American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Pathologists guidelines [24]. Only samples with a strong circumferential membranous HER2 immunoreactivity (3+) were considered positive, whereas cases with 0 or 1 + HER2 were negative. Equivocal HER2 (2+) cases underwent silver in situ hybridization (SISH) for HER2 gene amplification. This study exclusively included patients with TNBC.

IHC for programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) status

PD-L1 (clone 22C3; dilution, 1:50; Dako, Carpentaria, CA, USA) IHC was performed and evaluated using the combined proportional score (CPS) per the manufacturer’s guidelines and scoring algorithm [25]. The CPS was calculated as the number of PD-L1 staining cells, including TCs, lymphocytes, and macrophages, divided by the total number of viable invasive TCs multiplied by 100. CPS determination was performed at x200 magnification. Scoring excluded regions with edge artifacts, crushing artifacts, or necrosis. CPS ≥ 10 was considered as positive for PD-L1 (22C3).

OPAL mIF and primary data generation

The tyramide signal amplification-based (TSA)-based opal method was used for mIF staining. The 4 μm-thick sectioned slides were baked at 60 ℃ for 1 h, deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in ethanol. After rehydration, the slides were washed with distilled water and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 10 min at room temperature. All multiplexed staining was performed using the Opal 7 Immunology Discovery Kit (OP7DS2001KT; Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). Antigen retrieval was performed using microwave treatment (MWT) in AR buffer. After the MWT, the slides were incubated at room temperature for 15 min and covered with a blocking buffer in a humidified chamber for 10 min at room temperature. The following primary antibodies were used: CD8 (clone 108 M-96; dilution 1:200; Cell Marque, Rocklin, CA, USA), FOXP3 (clone 236 A/E7; dilution 1:50; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CD4 (clone EPR6855; dilution 1:200; Abcam), CK (clone AE1/AE3; dilution 1:500; Novocastra, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK), and CD20 (clone M0755; dilution 1:200; Dako). Secondary antibodies were incubated in a humidified chamber for an hour at room temperature. The TSA visualization was performed using opal fluorophores (Opal 480, Opal 570, Opal 620, Opal 690, and Opal 780). For subsequent antibody staining, MWT was performed using AR buffer to remove the antibody-opal fluorophore complex. Multiplexed staining steps were repeated using TSA visualization. The multiplex staining process was completed with DAPI counterstaining, and the slides were mounted with mounting solution (S3023; Dako). Opal mIF Images of stained slides were acquired with the Vectra Polaris (Akoya Biosciences) whole-slide scanner using a standardized scanning protocol, and processed using digital image analysis software, inForm® (Ver 2.5, Akoya Biosciences). Manual annotation was carried out for region of interest (ROI) for all slides using Phenochart (Ver 1.1, Akoya Biosciences). ROIs were selected in tumor tissue of each case, which were further categorized in “tumor” and “stroma”: “Tumor” area was almost exclusively composed of TC with least intervening stroma. “Stroma” area was exclusive of TC, consisting of a TME, which contained most of the IC. For each ROI, the density of specific cell types expressing CD8, FOXP3, CD4, CD20, and CK per case were quantified by cell counts per square micron. Data table generated from inForm® software was further consolidated using “phenoptr” package of R software (https://www.r-project.org; version 4.3.1). Representative image of paired hematoxyline and eosin-stained image and mIF image is shown in Fig. 1.

Calculation of spatial metrics of TC and IC

Cells expressing CK alone and located in the ROI of the tumor category were defined as TC. Conversely, cells expressing at least one immune marker were defined as IC, irrespective of the CK status. IC counts were separately analyzed within the ROI of the tumor and stroma, as well as across total ROIs. For each ROI category, the numbers and areas of TC and IC were calculated. With the sum of cell area and cell numbers, the density of TC and IC was calculated in the tumor and stroma areas, defined as cell count per mm2. Cells estimated to express multiple markers were excluded, except for CD4 + FOXP3 + cells.

Cell-to-cell distances were calculated using the x- and y-coordinates of each cell’s centroid. To calculate the distance between IC and TC, the closest TC was selected for each IC, and the distance between each IC-TC pair was computed for a density plot. For statistical comparisons, all IC-TC pairs within each case were included, and median (50th percentile) distance per case was used as the representative value for group-level analysis to minimize the influence of potential outliers of disproportionate cell numbers across cases. Given the relatively small sample size, further normalization of pair counts across cases was not performed. Group comparison were conducted using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

For each IC subtype, kernel density estimation was performed using the density() function in R to visualize the distribution of distances to the nearest TC. The Y-axis represents a normalized probability density, where the area under each curve equals 1. The 50th percentile distance – defined as the point at which half of the IC area located closer to the TC – was extracted for each group. These values were used to compare proximity distributions between different TIL or pCR groups. IC were further categorized based on whether their distance to the nearest TC was above or below this median, and statistical significance was assessed using chi-squared tests.

Cells located within 15 μm of each other were considered to be in direct interaction and were further analyzed. For IC in direct contact with each TC, the number of interacting IC, along with the combination and proportion of IC types, was analyzed. For each IC within the same samples, unique IC pairs consisting of two or more cells located within 15 μm of each other were analyzed to determine their proportional distribution.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R software (https://www.r-project.org; version 4.3.1). Continuous variables were compared between the two groups using the t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-squared test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) excluding 1. For comparison of IC subtype proportion between different groups, we calculated the relative proportions of CD20+, CD8+, and CD4 + IC within each sample by dividing each cell type’s density by the total IC density in the respective compartment (tumor or stroma). CD4 + IC were further stratified into FOXP3 + and FOXP3- subsets, and their proportions were calculated relative to total CD4 + IC density. Group-level composition differences were assessed using Pearson’s chi-squared test.

Results

A comparative analysis between the pCR and non-pCR groups revealed no significant differences in age, clinical T and N stages, NG, HG, TIL, TSR, and PD-L1 status. Similarly, the number of fields examined, areas of the ROIs (both stroma and tumor), and TC density showed no notable differences between the two groups. Comprehensive details regarding the NAC regimens administered to these patients are provided in Table 1. Although the pCR group displayed an elevated TIL level compared to the non-pCR group, this difference was not significant.

IC metrics and distribution across the TIL groups

A total of 1,183,644 cells, including 761,976 tumor cells (TC) and 421,668 immune cells (IC), were analyzed, with comprehensive metrics presented in Table 2. The densities of total IC and individual IC subtypes were statistically indistinguishable between the high- and low-TIL groups across stromal and tumor ROIs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, only the CD20 + IC density in the tumor ROI showed a marginally higher trend in the pCR group compared to the non-pCR group (648 ± 872.3/mm2 vs. 122.3 ± 131.1/mm2, p = 0.056). Similarly, the ratios of CD8 + IC/CD4 + FOXP3 + IC and FOXP3-IC/FOXP3 + IC within the CD4 + IC category in the stromal ROI did not differ significantly between groups. Among patients with high TIL levels, the IC densities in the stromal ROI were comparable between the pCR and non-pCR groups, with no significant differences observed in CD8 + IC/CD4 + FOXP3 + IC and CD4 + FOXP3-/CD4 + FOXP3 + IC ratios (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2). However, within the tumor ROI, the non-pCR group exhibited significantly denser infiltration of CD4 + IC compared to the pCR group (169.8 ± 87.3/mm2 vs. 528.5 ± 80.0/mm2, p = 0.029). Specifically, both FOXP3 + IC and FOXP3-IC densities within the CD4 + IC population were significantly higher in the non-pCR group (FOXP3 + IC: 16.5 ± 17.7/mm2 vs. 88.8 ± 45.9/mm2, p = 0.029; FOXP3-IC: 153.3 ± 72.3/mm2 vs. 439.7 ± 148.6/mm2, p = 0.029). Conversely, the pCR group demonstrated a significantly higher FOXP3-IC/FOXP3 + IC ratio within the CD4 + IC category (3.9 ± 1.4 vs. 1.4 ± 0.6, p = 0.029). While the CD8 + IC/CD4 + FOXP3 + IC ratio was higher in the pCR group, this difference did not reach statistical significance (20.7 ± 18.6 vs. 1.1 ± 1.0, p = 0.057). In patients with low TIL levels, the stromal CD8 + IC/CD4 + FOXP3 + IC ratio showed a trend toward being higher in the pCR group (5.9 ± 0.8 vs. 3.2 ± 1.9, p = 0.071). No other significant differences were observed in IC densities or ratios (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2).

IC composition between pCR and non-pCR groups categorized by TIL levels

Significant different IC composition in stroma and tumor ROIs were observed between pCR and non-pCR group. These differences were consistent across all patients, as well as within the high-TIL and low-TIL subgroups. (Table 3; Fig. 2). In the stromal ROI of the pCR group, CD20 + IC and CD8 + IC comprised a significantly larger fraction than that of the non-pCR group (Fig. 2A). In contrast, CD4 + IC were the most dominant components in the non-pCR group (CD20 + IC, 36.7% vs. 16.9%; CD8 + IC, 14.5% vs. 5.9%; CD4 + IC, 48.8% vs. 77.2%; p < 0.001). Within the CD4 + IC category, the FOXP3 + component was slightly more prominent in the pCR group (7.6% vs. 6.9%; p = 0.010); however, it was diminished when considering all IC types (3.7% vs. 5.3%, p < 0.001). In the tumor ROI, CD20 + IC (58.1%) were the predominant component in the pCR group, followed by CD8 + IC (30.7%) and CD4 + IC (11.3%). However, non-pCR group shows contrasting outcome, where CD4 + IC was the largest component (58.9%), trailed by CD20 + IC (29.2%) and CD8 + IC (11.9%). A significantly higher proportion of CD4 + FOXP3 + IC was observed in the non-pCR group than in the pCR group, both within the CD4 + IC category (16.1% vs. 9.4%, p < 0.001) and across all IC types (9.5% vs. 1.1%, p < 0.001).

However, in the low-TIL group, the density of CD4 + FOXP3 + IC did not differ between pCR and non-pCR cases. Instead, CD4 + FOXP3-IC were more densely clustered in pCR cases. (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

In this study, we observed differences in IC composition and spatial distribution in treatment-naïve TNBC samples based on the pCR status and TIL levels. NAC is a standard treatment strategy for patients with TNBC [26], and TIL levels serve as a powerful prognosticator for treatment response and clinical outcomes following NAC [9]. However, some TNBC cases achieve pCR despite low TIL status, whereas others have residual disease even with high TIL status. We observed that cases resulting in pCR typically showed a higher ratio of tumoral CD8 + IC to CD20 + IC, irrespective of TIL status. This implies that the adaptive immune system is activated in response to TC.

In the high-TIL group, CD4 + FOXP3 + IC were significantly enriched in the tumoral area, suggesting that Tregs could appear after a substantial number of TIL are present. Conversely, tumoral CD4 + FOXP3 + IC infiltration in patients with low TIL levels was not significantly different. Ironically, a higher infiltration of these subsets was observed in the stromal ROI of pCR cases. However, considering the extremely low density of CD4 + FOXP3 + IC in the low-TIL group, the statistical power of this observation should be carefully interpreted. In non-pCR cases within the low-TIL group, the insufficient amount of functional antitumor immunity explains the outcomes, as evidenced by the lower fraction of CD8 + IC and CD20 + IC in the stroma and their limited presence in tumor areas.

In a previous study [27], we observed higher CD8 + IC and CD4 + FOXP3 + IC fractions in patients with high-TIL TNBC than those with high-TIL HR + HER2-BC. In this study, predominant CD8 + IC was also observed in pCR cases with low-TIL TNBC, highlighting the importance of cellular immunity in achieving pCR in TNBC. This also explains why enrichment of CD4 + FOXP3 + IC could dampen cellular immunity in non-pCR cases with high-TIL TNBC.

In this study, CD20 + IC constituted an even larger fraction than CD8 + IC. CD20 + IC, otherwise B cells, have been less emphasized than T cells in their antitumor effects so far. B cells are a key component of adaptive immunity involving diverse mechanisms: antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, complement activation, recruitment and organization of other IC, and immunosuppression by regulatory B cells [28, 29] They also constitute tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), which promote an immunotherapy response [30]. In TNBC, B and T cells, especially T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, mediate immunotherapy responses in a mouse model [31]. In our study, the spatial distribution pattern of IC, irrespective of TIL levels, showed clustering of CD8 + IC and CD20 + IC near the TC in pCR cases. The clustering of CD4 + FOXP3 + IC near the TC in non-pCR cases was significant only in the high-TIL group. The distribution of CD4 + FOXP3 + IC did not differ between the pCR and non-pCR groups in the low-TIL group. Instead, CD8 + IC showed a significantly higher density peak and clustering near the TC in pCR cases in the low-TIL group, which also supports the importance of the cellular immunity in treatment response of TNBC.

Interestingly, in the pCR group, the density plots based on different TIL status revealed a similar distribution pattern for CD20 + IC and CD8 + IC. Their plots were largely overlapped, with the distances from TC to the 50th percentile of IC differing by less than 10 μm between high- and low-TIL cases. This observation aligns with a previous study suggesting that a balanced ratio of IC types may be more functionally effective than a large quantity of specific IC types [12]. In contrast, CD4 + IC in the pCR group showed distinct denser clustering near the TC in low-TIL cases. Taken together, this observation suggest that the effective coordination of B and T cells may rely not only on a balanced dynamic among different IC types but also on the presence of a specific amount of CD4 + IC, likely Tfh cells, for the functional efficacy.

Among the IC in direct contact with TC, the CD4 + FOXP3-IC subset was the most prevalent. In the pCR cases, the next largest subset was CD20 + IC, whereas in the non-pCR cases, CD20 + IC and CD4 + FOXP3 + IC were present in similar proportions. This trend was more pronounced in patients with high-TIL TNBC. In the pCR group, the dominant subsets in high-TIL cases were CD4 + FOXP3-IC and CD20 + IC, whereas in low-TIL cases, the predominant subsets were CD4 + FOXP3-IC and CD8 + IC. This observation suggests that B cells may emerge at a later stage in the development of antitumor immunity, following the activation of T-cell components. Consistent with these spatial patterns, case-level analysis further supported the group-level trends (Supplementary Fig. 5). pCR/high-TIL cases generally exhibited shorter IC-TC distances, especially for CD20 + IC and CD8 + IC, whereas non-pCR/low-TIL cases showed relatively longer distances. Although some inter-case variability remained, this overall consistency strengthened the reliability of our spatial findings and suggests that these IC subsets may play distinct roles in shaping treatment response.

In our previous study [27], we hypothesized that the adverse effects observed in patients with high-TIL HR + HER2-BC may stem from a dominant B-cell component, which could potentially be regulatory B cells. However, in this study with TNBC, B-cell predominance was observed in pCR cases with high TIL levels. Additionally, most of the high TIL cases showed PD-L1 positivity. PD1/PD-L1 suppress antitumor immunity and induce immune evasion of TC [32]. However, PD-L1 positivity has been reported to correlate with better prognosis in treatment-naïve TNBC after resection [33]. PD-L1 expression in TC has an inhibitory effect on T-cell expansion and exhausts cytotoxic T-cells [34]. However, PD-L1 also serves as a Tfh cell that is involved in TLS formation and higher-affinity B cell development [12].

In this study, supplementary case-level analyses supported the robustness of our findings, minimizing concerns about potential bias and confirming the consistency of the observed spatial trends. Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, although we analyzed approximately a million cells with sufficient statistical power, the small number of patients and the lack of long-term clinical outcomes after NAC may limit the robustness of our findings. Further investigation of long-term outcomes beyond NAC could provide deeper insights into TIME function. Second, molecular classification of TNBC and genomic analysis were not performed. While we used mIF to classify IC types and analyze their spatial distribution, we were unable to assess the genomic characteristics of the TC itself. This limitation prevents us from identifying specific immune-related features or their influence on TIME. TNBC with high TIL levels might belong to immunomodulatory or basal-like subtypes [2], whereas low-TIL cases may fall under other subtypes, in which TIME might have limited influence on tumor biology or treatment response [35]. Third, we analyzed biopsy samples, which may not represent the entire tumor. We investigated whether different pretreatment TIME leads to varying treatment responses, including all tumor tissue areas within the ROI fields. However, the biopsy samples had an intrinsic limitation, as they might not encompass larger functional immune structures, such as TLS. Fourth, although the combination of pembrolizumab and chemotherapy for NAC, as established by the KEYNOTE 522 [36], is now the standard treatment for early high-risk TNBC, our study did not include samples from this regimen. Instead, our samples were collected prior to the adoption of KEYNOTE 522, which may limit the applicability of our findings to current treatment standards. Finally, we used only a limited number of mIF antibodies. In this study, CXC chemokines and receptors were not included, and PD-L1 expression at the cellular level was technically unavailable, which diminished the in-depth analysis of IC types and their functional states.

Conclusion

Tumoral CD8 + IC and CD20 + IC are important components of the TNBC treatment response. In particular, CD20 + IC are accompanied by a high TIL status, which implies the coordination of B and T cells under high TIL conditions. For further validation, larger datasets with longer clinical outcomes are needed to analyze the integrated gene expression data, functional annotation, and spatial information.
