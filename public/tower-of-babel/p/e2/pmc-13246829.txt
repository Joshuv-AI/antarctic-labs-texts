Investigating the causal role of sleep disorders in anorexia nervosa and obsessive-compulsive disorder: a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Ying Liu, Lixia Chen, Jing Wang, Yuan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether common sleep phenotypes causally influence anorexia nervosa (AN) or obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is unclear; we used Mendelian randomization (MR) to test effects of daytime sleepiness (DS), insomnia, and sleep apnea (SA) on AN and OCD, and the reverse direction.MethodsWe conducted bidirectional two-sample MR using genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of DS (N = 452,071), insomnia (N = 453,379), SA (N = 523,366), AN (N = 72,517), and OCD (N = 33,943), predominantly of European ancestry. Instruments were single-nucleotide polymorphisms meeting genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10⁻8), clumped for linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.001) with mean F > 10; harmonization aligned effect alleles. The primary estimator was inverse-variance weighted (IVW), with weighted median and MR-Egger as sensitivity analyses. Heterogeneity and pleiotropy were evaluated with Cochran’s Q, MR-Egger intercepts, and MR-PRESSO.ResultsGenetic liability to SA was associated with higher risks of AN (IVW OR = 1.47, 95% CI 1.13–1.91, p = 0.004) and OCD (IVW OR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.08–1.37, p = 0.001). DS and insomnia showed no clear associations with either outcome. Reverse analyses provided no evidence that genetic liability to AN or OCD influenced DS, insomnia, or SA. Sensitivity analyses indicated adequate instrument strength (mean F = 15.55–25.75), limited directional pleiotropy, and estimates that were directionally consistent across methods; MR-PRESSO signals in models with AN did not materially alter conclusions.ConclusionsGenetic evidence supports a causal contribution of SA, not DS or insomnia, to AN and OCD, with no evidence for reverse causation. Sleep-disordered breathing may be a modifiable target for prevention or adjunctive treatment; replication in diverse ancestries using objective sleep measures is warranted.Level of evidence III well-designed cohort analytic study.

Introduction

Mental health disorders are among the leading contributors to the global disease burden, profoundly affecting individual well-being while imposing substantial social and economic costs [1]. Among these, anorexia nervosa (AN) and obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) are particularly debilitating due to their chronic, life-threatening nature and high mortality rates [2]. AN is characterized by severe dietary restriction, distorted body image, and an intense fear of weight gain, frequently co-occurring with psychiatric comorbidities such as anxiety, depression, and obsessive–compulsive symptoms [3]. OCD, on the other hand, manifests as persistent obsessions and compulsive behaviors, leading to significant emotional distress and impaired daily functioning [4]. Both conditions cause profound disruptions in social, occupational, and emotional well-being, significantly diminishing quality of life [5–7]. Despite advancements in understanding their pathophysiology, AN and OCD pose substantial therapeutic challenges due to their high rates of treatment resistance and relapse [8, 9]. Their societal impact extends beyond affected individuals, influencing workforce productivity, increasing healthcare costs, and contributing to long-term disability [5, 10, 11]. Thus, elucidating the underlying mechanisms of AN and OCD is crucial for developing more effective treatment strategies and preventive measures.

Sleep disorders including insomnia, sleep apnea(SA), and excessive daytime sleepiness(DS)—have increasingly been recognized as a major public health concern, affecting a substantial proportion of the global population [12]. It is estimated that 10–20% of adults suffer from chronic insomnia, while SA—particularly obstructive sleep apnea—affects up to 4% of middle-aged adults [13]. Beyond their well-documented associations with cardiovascular disease and metabolic disturbances, these sleep disorders have profound implications for mental health, with accumulating evidence highlighting their role as comorbidities in psychiatric conditions such as mood disorders, anxiety, and eating disorders [14].

Emerging research suggests that sleep disorders are highly prevalent among individuals with AN and OCD, potentially exacerbating symptoms and impeding treatment outcomes [15]. In AN, insomnia and excessive DS are frequently observed, and sleep disturbances are thought to worsen emotional dysregulation, a core feature of the disorder [16]. Specifically, insomnia has been implicated in heightened anxiety and cognitive rigidity, thereby reinforcing the cycle of food restriction and distorted body image [17]. Similarly, in OCD, both insomnia and SA are common, with evidence suggesting that these conditions may further impair cognitive and emotional functioning, making it even more challenging for individuals to manage compulsive behaviors [18].

However, the directionality and causality of these associations remain unclear. While numerous observational studies have demonstrated a correlation between sleep disorders and psychiatric conditions, traditional study designs are inherently limited in their ability to establish causality [18]. The cross-sectional nature of most studies and their reliance on regression models make it difficult to discern whether sleep disorders are a symptom of AN and OCD or whether they contribute to the onset and exacerbation of these disorders. This gap in the literature highlights the need for more robust causal research to clarify the role of sleep disorders in the etiology of AN and OCD.

To address these limitations, Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful tool for inferring causal relationships [19]. MR leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables to estimate causal effects, thereby reducing the influence of confounding and reverse causality, which often plague observational studies [20]. This approach provides a novel opportunity to establish causal links that could inform the development of more effective treatments and preventive interventions.

This study aims to investigate the bidirectional causal relationship between sleep disorders and psychiatric conditions, specifically focusing on AN and OCD, using a two-sample MR approach. We examine whether sleep disorders, particularly insomnia and SA, contribute to the risk of AN and OCD, while also assessing the potential reverse causality, where AN and OCD may influence sleep disorders.

By leveraging genetic data, this study seeks to provide robust evidence on the causal interplay between sleep disorders and these psychiatric conditions. If confirmed, these findings could inform more targeted interventions and preventive strategies for at-risk individuals.

Study design

In this study, we applied MR to investigate the potential causal effects of sleep disorders on OCD and AN. Summary-level genetic data were obtained from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [21], including data from the UK Biobank and published meta-analyses. Genetic variants—specifically single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)—served as instrumental variables to proxy for three sleep traits: DS, insomnia, and SA. To ensure valid causal inference, SNPs were selected according to standard MR assumptions: they must be strongly associated with the exposure, unrelated to confounding variables, and influence outcomes only through the exposure pathway [22, 23]. Genetic instrument selection

We used large European-ancestry GWAS for DS, insomnia, and SA. All three datasets report genome-wide significant loci after standard QC and were generated on imputed genome-wide arrays.

DS summary statistics came from a UK Biobank (UKB) GWAS of 452,071 participants aged 40–69 years (baseline 2006–2010), all of European genetic ancestry [24]. DS was assessed with a four-level self-report item (“How likely are you to doze off…?”), analysed as a continuous trait. The primary GWAS used BOLT-LMM with adjustment for age, sex, genotyping array, 10 principal components, and the genetic relatedness matrix; autosomal genome-wide significance was set at 5 × 10⁻⁸. Variant/sample QC included MAF > 0.001, imputation score > 0.3, per-SNP missingness < 10%, and per-sample missingness < 40%; sensitivity analyses further adjusted for BMI and other potential confounders.

We used the UKB insomnia GWAS of 453,379 Europeans[25]. Symptoms were ascertained by self-report (“trouble falling asleep or waking in the night”), analysed in two parallel dichotomies (frequent vs. never/rarely, and any vs. never/rarely). The discovery stage identified 57 loci; effect estimates were robust to additional adjustment for BMI, lifestyle factors, caffeine, depression/stress, and other sleep traits. Replication was shown in HUNT (self-report), Partners Biobank (physician-diagnosed insomnia), and UKB actigraphy subsamples.

SA summary data were taken from the largest European-ancestry meta-GWAS to date (five cohorts: UKB, FinnGen, Partners Biobank, CLSA, AGDS; total N = 523,366; 25,008 cases) [26]. SA cases were defined via ICD-9 327.23/ICD-10 G47.3 or self-reported breathing interruptions; snoring cases were excluded from controls where available. Each cohort performed logistic-regression GWAS adjusted for age, sex, batch, and ancestry PCs; standard QC removed variants with MAF < 0.01 or INFO < 0.6 and non-European ancestry outliers. Study-level results were meta-analysed with METAL using effective-sample-size weighting. The original paper also reported a multi-trait analysis (MTAG) with snoring to boost discovery and BMI-adjusted sensitivity analyses; we relied on the published SA meta-analysis summary statistics for our primary MR.

Ancestry and versions. All exposure GWAS were restricted to European ancestry; DS and insomnia derive from UKB releases analysing baseline 2006–2010 data (2019 publications), and SA is a 2023 multi-cohort meta-analysis.

For both forward and reverse MR analyses, SNPs were harmonized by aligning effect alleles. Instrumental variables were selected based on genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) and linkage disequilibrium threshold (r2 < 0.001, 1000 kb window) [27]. To meet the second core MR assumption—independence from confounders—SNPs potentially associated with outcome-related confounding traits were identified and excluded using the LDassoc tool (https://ldlink.nih.gov). F-statistics were calculated to assess instrument strength [28]. The strength of each genetic instrument was assessed using the F statistic, calculated as: F = [(N − k − 1)/k] × [R2/(1 − R2)], where N is the effective GWAS sample size, k is the number of instruments, and R2 represents the proportion of variance explained by the SNPs. The R2 was estimated using the formula: R2 = 2 × (1 − MAF) × MAF × (β/SD)2[29, 30], in which β is the standardized SNP effect on the exposure, and MAF denotes the minor allele frequency.

Data source for outcomes GWAS datasets

AN GWAS summary statistics were taken from the large meta-analysis by the PGC Eating Disorders Working Group (PGC-ED), comprising 33 datasets with 16,992 AN cases and 55,525 controls of European ancestry drawn from 17 countries [31]. Case definitions reflected lifetime AN diagnoses based on standardized DSM-III-R/DSM-IV or ICD-8/9/10 criteria ascertained via clinical registers, structured interviews, or validated questionnaires; controls were ancestry-matched (screening varied across cohorts). Each cohort underwent the PGC Ricopili pipeline (versions 2017_Oct_11.002 and 2017_Nov_30.003) for QC, imputation to 1000 Genomes Phase 3, and cohort-level GWAS with covariates including ancestry principal components, followed by inverse-variance fixed-effect meta-analysis. The study identified eight genome-wide significant loci and highlighted cross-trait genetic correlations consistent with a metabo-psychiatric component of AN.

We used summary statistics from the largest GWAS meta-analysis of OCS to date, totaling 33,943 individuals of European ancestry across seven population-based cohorts from Sweden, the Netherlands, England, and Canada [32]. OCS were assessed using validated questionnaires (e.g., OCI-R, BOCS, Padua, TOCS). Each cohort applied stringent QC (removal of non-European ancestry outliers by PCA), imputed to HRC or 1000G, and ran mixed-linear-model GWAS with GCTA-fastGWA including sex, age, genotyping batch, and genetic PCs; results were meta-analyzed with METAL. Although no single SNP reached genome-wide significance, the study reported significant SNP-heritability (~ 4%) and strong genetic correlation with clinically diagnosed OCD, supporting the use of OCS as a quantitative proxy.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a bidirectional MR analysis to evaluate the causal associations between sleep disorders (DS, insomnia, and SA) and two psychiatric outcomes (OCD and AN). The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method with a random-effects model was used as the primary analytical approach to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), reflecting the effect of each exposure on binary outcomes.

To assess the robustness of our findings and account for potential horizontal pleiotropy, we performed four sensitivity analyses: the weighted median approach [33], MR-Egger regression [34], MR-PRESSO [35], and contamination mixture methods [36]. The weighted median approach provides valid estimates if at least 50% of the weight comes from valid instruments [33]. MR-Egger regression detects and adjusts for directional pleiotropy by allowing the intercept to differ from zero [34]. MR-PRESSO identifies and removes outlier SNPs, then re-estimates the causal effect to examine the influence of these variants. The contamination mixture method further evaluates the stability of the results in the presence of invalid instruments [36]. Cochrane’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity among SNPs. Instrument strength was evaluated using F-statistics. All MR analyses were two-sided and conducted using the TwoSampleMR [37], MR-PRESSO [35], and Mendelian Randomization [38] packages in R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Forward Mendelian randomization

Across the six exposure–outcome pairs, only SA showed robust positive associations. Using 32 SNPs as instruments for SA, the IVW model indicated higher risks of both outcomes: AN (OR = 1.47, 95% CI 1.13–1.91, p = 0.004) and OCD (OR = 1.22, 1.08–1.37, p = 0.001). The WM estimator produced directionally consistent results (AN: OR = 1.62, 1.17–2.25, p = 0.004; OCD: OR = 1.18, 0.99–1.45, p = 0.062).

For DS, we used 32 SNPs for the DS–AN analysis and 33 SNPs for the DS–OCD analysis. IVW estimates were null (AN: OR = 0.56, 0.22–1.46, p = 0.121; OCD: OR = 1.04, 0.70–1.56, p = 0.840), and WM results were similar (AN: OR = 0.43, 0.15–1.25, p = 0.742; OCD: OR = 0.70, 0.39–1.25, p = 0.227).

For insomnia, 33 SNPs were available for both insomnia–AN and insomnia–OCD. IVW estimates again provided no evidence of effect (AN: OR = 0.78, 0.37–1.61, p = 0.496; OCD: OR = 1.03, 0.74–1.44, p = 0.870), with concordant WM results (AN: OR = 0.88, 0.37–2.07, p = 0.763; OCD: OR = 0.89, 0.57–1.39, p = 0.599). Taken together, the results support a unidirectional pattern whereby genetic liability to SA increases the risks of AN and obsessive–compulsive disorder, whereas genetic liability to AN or OCD does not materially affect DS, insomnia, or SA.

Discussion

In our study, MR analysis revealed a statistically significant causal relationship between SA and both OCD and AN, addressing a critical gap in the existing literature. While previous research has identified associations between sleep disorders and AN and OCD, the directionality of these relationships remained unclear. By employing a two-sample MR approach, we overcame the limitations of confounding and reverse causation inherent in observational studies. These findings advance our understanding of the interplay between sleep disorders and these psychiatric conditions and highlight the need for further investigation into the underlying mechanisms.

Our findings suggest a causal role of SA in the development of AN and OCD, which may be explained by several neurobiological mechanisms. These results align with prior studies reporting a high prevalence of SA in individuals with AN and OCD and suggesting shared pathophysiological pathways [39, 40]. Previous research has demonstrated that SA is associated with cognitive inflexibility, increased compulsive behaviors, and emotional dysregulation, all of which are central to both AN and OCD [41, 42]. Additionally, clinical studies have highlighted that SA exacerbates psychiatric symptoms, including heightened anxiety and obsessive thoughts, further supporting a potential causal link between SA and these disorders [43, 44].

One potential explanation for this causal association is the dysregulation of neurotransmitter systems induced by SA. Chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) and sleep fragmentation, hallmark features of SA, disrupt the dopaminergic, serotonergic, and GABAergic systems in the brain [45–47]. Altered dopamine signaling may impair reward processing, a key feature in AN, while also exacerbating compulsive behaviors in OC [48]. Similarly, dysregulated serotonin levels, which play a central role in mood and anxiety regulation, may contribute to obsessive–compulsive tendencies and cognitive rigidity in AN [48]. Furthermore, reductions in GABAergic inhibitory tone, commonly observed in SA, may lead to increased hyperarousal and intrusive thoughts, further linking the disorder to OCD [46].

Another plausible mechanism is chronic systemic and neuroinflammation triggered by SA. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), are elevated in individuals with SA and have been implicated in neural alterations affecting appetite regulation, cognitive flexibility, and compulsive behaviors [49]. Additionally, microglial activation in response to chronic inflammation may impair neuroplasticity, potentially contributing to rigidity and maladaptive behaviors observed in AN and OCD [50, 51].

The physiological stress response induced by SA also plays a significant role. Repetitive nocturnal arousals and oxygen desaturation activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to sustained elevations in cortisol levels [52]. Chronic HPA axis dysregulation has been associated with heightened anxiety, stress reactivity, and compulsive behaviors, all of which are key components of OCD and AN [40]. Additionally, repeated exposure to physiological stress may sensitize neural circuits involved in fear and anxiety, perpetuating the obsessive–compulsive behaviors and restrictive eating patterns characteristic of these disorders [53].

Furthermore, hypoxia itself provides a direct link between SA and psychiatric morbidity. SA may lead to widespread neural damage, affecting key brain regions such as the prefrontal cortex (PFC), limbic system (amygdala and hippocampus), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and the default mode network (DMN) [54]. Impairments in the PFC weaken impulse control and cognitive flexibility, fostering rigid thought patterns and maladaptive behaviors seen in both OCD and AN. Dysfunction in the amygdala and hippocampus increases emotional reactivity and maladaptive learning, heightening anxiety and reinforcing compulsive behaviors [55]. The ACC, involved in error detection and emotional regulation, may become overactive, amplifying guilt, self-criticism, and obsessionality—features prominent in both AN and OCD. Disruptions in the DMN contribute to excessive rumination, manifesting as intrusive thoughts in OCD and preoccupation with body image in AN [56, 57]. These neurobiological disruptions, along with reduced white matter integrity and impaired functional connectivity, underscore the profound impact of SA on the brain’s regulatory systems [57–59]. Recognizing these effects highlights the critical need for early diagnosis and intervention to mitigate its impact on psychiatric symptoms.

Our study found no clear causal relationship between insomnia, DS, and AN or OCD, contrasting with previous clinical studies that reported associations between these variables. A meta-analysis of six studies, including 111 OCD patients, indicated that insomnia, poor sleep quality, and hyperarousal are common among individuals with OCD [60] Addressing both psychological symptoms and sleep disturbances is crucial for improving treatment outcomes [60]. Similarly, sleep disorders are frequently reported in AN and are linked to anxiety and eating disorder symptoms [61]. Beyond being a consequence of malnutrition, sleep disturbances are integral to AN’s psychopathology, interacting with core symptoms such as anxiety and obsessive preoccupations. The bidirectional relationship suggests that improving sleep quality may help alleviate psychological distress in AN.

The discrepancy between our findings and prior studies may stem from observational study limitations, where insomnia and DS may arise as secondary symptoms of AN and OCD, leading to inflated associations. In contrast, MR minimizes confounding and reverse causation, providing a more robust causal inference. However, the genetic instruments for insomnia and DS may capture only a small variance, limiting statistical power. Additionally, population differences, heterogeneous sleep trait definitions, and environmental factors could obscure causal links.

Furthermore, our reverse MR analysis found no causal effect of AN or OCD on sleep disorders, suggesting that previously observed associations may be driven by shared genetic or environmental factors rather than a direct causal link. It is possible that sleep disturbances exacerbate psychiatric symptoms via stress, inflammation, or neurobiological dysregulation, rather than being primary causal contributors. These findings highlight the need for refining genetic instruments, investigating gene-environment interactions, and exploring indirect pathways to fully understand the relationship between sleep disorders, AN, and OCD.

Conclusion

Clarifying the causal relationship between sleep disorders, AN, and OCD is essential for understanding their underlying mechanisms and developing targeted interventions. This study is the first to apply MR to investigate these associations.

Our findings suggest that sleep disorders contribute to an increased risk of AN and OCD, reinforcing their detrimental effects on mental health. However, we found no evidence supporting a causal relationship between insomnia, daytime sleepiness, or SA and the risk of AN or OCD. These results enhance our understanding of the role of sleep disorders in psychiatric conditions and provide new insights for developing intervention strategies that address sleep as a potential modifiable risk factor in AN and OCD management.

Strengths and limitations

First, to our knowledge this is the first bidirectional MR investigation of sleep phenotypes with AN and OCD. We leveraged very large GWAS datasets and a two-sample design, improving precision and reducing confounding and reverse causation. Second, instrument selection followed strict criteria (genome-wide significance p < 5 × 10⁻⁸, LD clumping r2 < 0.001) and all instrument sets showed adequate strength (mean F > 10). We report instrument counts for every exposure–outcome pair and harmonized alleles using standard pipelines. Third, robustness was evaluated comprehensively (IVW as primary; weighted median and MR-Egger; Cochran’s Q; MR-PRESSO; leave-one-out; Steiger filtering), with broadly consistent findings.

First, genetic instruments explain only a modest proportion of variance in sleep traits, limiting power for small effects and widening uncertainty for null results. Second, phenotype heterogeneity may introduce measurement error: DS and insomnia were self-reported; sleep apnea definitions varied across cohorts; AN case definitions and ascertainment differed; and the OCD outcome was derived from symptom measures using validated scales—each of which may attenuate effects. Third, although exposure and outcome GWAS largely come from distinct consortia, limited sample overlap may exist; any such overlap would bias two-sample MR toward the null but cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, shared genetic architecture with correlated traits (e.g., BMI, depression, smoking) may confound estimates despite sensitivity analyses aimed at detecting horizontal pleiotropy. Fifth, we observed heterogeneity in several AN models, indicating variability across SNP-specific effects. Finally, participants were of European ancestry; generalizability to other populations remains uncertain.

Refinement of instruments (e.g., colocalization, cis-anchored variants), multivariable MR adjusting for BMI and depression, analyses using objective sleep measures (actigraphy/polysomnography), replication in non-European ancestries, and within-family/negative-control designs will further clarify the causal pathways linking sleep phenotypes to AN and OCD.
