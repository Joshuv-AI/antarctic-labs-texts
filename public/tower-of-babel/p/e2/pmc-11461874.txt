TRAF7 determines circadian period through ubiquitination and degradation of DBP
Authors: Shusaku Masuda, Nobuhiro Kurabayashi, Rina Nunokawa, Yuta Otobe, Hiroko Kozuka-Hata, Masaaki Oyama, Yuri Shibata, Jun-ichiro Inoue, Michinori Koebis, Atsu Aiba, Hikari Yoshitane, Yoshitaka Fukada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
D-site binding protein, DBP, is a clock-controlled transcription factor and drives daily rhythms of physiological processes through the regulation of an array of genes harboring a DNA binding motif, D-box. DBP protein levels show a circadian oscillation with an extremely robust peak/trough ratio, but it is elusive how the temporal pattern is regulated by post-translational regulation. In this study, we show that DBP protein levels are down-regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Analysis using 19 dominant-negative forms of E2 enzymes have revealed that UBE2G1 and UBE2T mediate the degradation of DBP. A proteomic analysis of DBP-interacting proteins and database screening have identified Tumor necrosis factor Receptor-Associated Factor 7 (TRAF7), a RING-type E3 ligase, that forms a complex with UBE2G1 and/or UBE2T. Ubiquitination analysis have revealed that TRAF7 enhances K48-linked polyubiquitination of DBP in cultured cells. Overexpression of TRAF7 down-regulates DBP protein level, while knockdown of TRAF7 up-regulates DBP in cultured cells. Knockout of TRAF7 in NIH3T3 cells have revealed that TRAF7 mediates the time-of-the-day-dependent regulation of DBP levels. Furthermore, TRAF7 has a period-shortening effect on the cellular clock. Together, TRAF7 plays an important role in circadian clock oscillation through destabilization of DBP.

Introduction

In mammals, 43% of protein-coding genes show daily expression rhythms with various phases of the circadian cycle1. These diverse oscillations can be generated by combinations of three DNA cis-elements, E-box, D-box and RRE2. A heterodimer of CLOCK and BMAL1 binds to E-box DNA sequences (CACGTG and its related sequences3) to activate transcription of thousands of genes including Per and Cry. Translated PER and CRY proteins then inhibit the E-box-dependent transcription by suppressing CLOCK-BMAL1-mediated transcriptional activation, thereby forming a core feedback loop that oscillates cell-autonomously with a period of approximately 24 h. The core E-box-mediated loop functionally couples with an RRE-mediated loop incorporating Rev-erb and Ror genes and their translated products, REV-ERBs and RORs, that control the Bmal1 expression rhythm. This RRE-mediated Bmal1 transcription rhythm stabilizes the core E-box loop4. In addition to these two DNA cis-elements, D-boxes are also known to contribute to the robust 24-h rhythms of the circadian clock2.

D-box-dependent transcription is activated by D-site binding protein, DBP, and repressed by E4BP4, also known as NFIL3. DBP is a member of proline- and acidic amino acid-rich basic leucine zipper (PAR-bZIP) protein family, and is rhythmically transcribed via E-box in many tissues and in cultured cells. Our ChIP-Seq analysis in the mouse liver previously showed time-of-day-dependent binding of DBP to D-box and the anti-phasic D-box-binding of E4BP45. These reciprocal regulations on an array of D-box-harboring genes drive robust circadian rhythms of gene expression6. Further, we have shown that D-box functions are critical not only for the circadian output but also for the circadian input of the cellular clock5. In cultured cells, E4bp4 was induced by mild acidification of the cultured media, and the acidification caused phase reset of the cellular rhythms, and the acid-induced phase reset was almost completely abolished by ablation of E4bp4 gene5. In addition, the physiological role of D-box-dependent transcription has been studied using gene KO (knockout) strategy. E4bp4-KO mice showed normal behavioral rhythms in the constant dark conditions, as was shown in triple KO mice for Dbp and its paralogs, Tef, thyroid embryonic factor, and Hlf, hepatic leukemia factor (PAR-bZIP TKO mice)7. Nonetheless, daily rhythms in detoxification, free fatty acid metabolism and blood pressure were severely disrupted in the TKO mice8–10, and gene expression profiles were perturbed in the E4bp4-KO mice, indicating a pivotal role of D-box in the circadian output pathways5.

Precise regulation of expression levels (and activity) of PAR-bZIP/E4BP4 proteins is critical for rhythmic transcriptional activity of D-box. In particular, DBP protein levels show a robust circadian oscillation with an extremely high peak/trough ratio like a time-of-the-day-dependent switch5,11. One potential regulatory mechanism for such a robust oscillation is a post-translational modification of DBP protein. Given that protein half-lives range from the order of minutes to months12, rapid turnover of DBP proteins mediated by finely tuned degradation mechanisms may contribute to the temporal pattern of DBP protein, but such mechanisms have not been examined. In this study, we found that DBP protein levels are regulated by ubiquitin-proteasome pathway mediated by E2 enzymes, UBE2G1 and UBE2T, and an E3 ligase TRAF7, Tumor necrosis factor Receptor-Associated Factor 7. Manipulations of TRAF7 levels changed DBP protein amount and the circadian period. We conclude that TRAF7 regulates circadian clock oscillation through destabilization of DBP protein.

Results

DBP protein levels are regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome-dependent degradation

To investigate the mechanism regulating protein levels of DBP, we focused on the protein degradation system. When protein synthesis was inhibited by cycloheximide (CHX) in HEK293T cells overexpressing DBP, we observed a reduction of DBP protein levels (Fig. 1a). We performed multiple experiments and found that the half-life of DBP was in the range of 2–4 h under our experimental condition. Application of MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, attenuated the reduction of DBP protein levels in the presence of CHX (Fig. 1a). Similar results were obtained by monitoring decay of bioluminescence signals derived from DBP-Luciferase (DBP-LUC) fusion protein (Fig. 1b). On the other hand, the degradation rate of Luciferase protein was similar in the presence and absence of MG132 (Fig. 1b). These results suggest that DBP protein is degraded through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Of note, MG132 application did not completely block the DBP reduction (Fig. 1a, b), suggesting contribution of other machinery to DBP degradation. E2 enzymes UBE2T and UBE2G1 are involved in DBP degradation

Protein ubiquitination is mediated by three key enzymes; E1 (a ubiquitin-activating enzyme), E2 (a ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme) and E3 (a ubiquitin-protein ligase). Specifically, the E2–E3 combination is known to determine the specificity of proteins to be ubiquitinated. Having shown the ubiquitin-proteasome-dependent degradation of DBP protein, we next aimed to identify E2 enzyme catalyzing the ubiquitination and degradation of DBP. For this purpose, we screened the effects of E2 dominant negative (DN) mutants, whose active site cysteine is replaced by alanine, on DBP protein levels (Fig. 2a, also see Supplementary Fig. S1). We performed two independent experiments and found that, among nineteen E2 DN mutants13, overexpression of either UBE2T (DN) or UBE2G1 (DN) in HEK293T cells consistently upregulated the expression levels of DBP protein (Fig. 2a and Supplementary S1). The effects of these two E2 enzymes on DBP protein levels were confirmed by further experiments (Fig. 2b). In addition, depletion of UBE2T or UBE2G1 by the shRNA expression independently upregulated DBP protein levels in HEK293 cells (Fig. 2c, d). Simultaneous expression of the shRNAs for UBE2T and UBE2G1 caused additional effect on DBP protein levels (Fig. 2d), suggesting that these E2 enzymes are both involved in the DBP regulatory mechanism. Further, as shown in Fig. 2e, degradation rate of DBP following CHX treatment was decreased by overexpression of UBE2G1 (DN). Proteomic analysis identified TRAF7 as an E3 ligase for DBP

To further investigate the molecular mechanism mediating ubiquitination and degradation of DBP, we next focused on E3 ligases. E3 ligases are known to facilitate ubiquitin transfer from E2 to target substrates, and many E3 ligases are composed of multiple subunits consisting of a substrate receptor (e.g., FBXL3), an adaptor(s) (e.g., SKP1), and a scaffold (e.g., Cullin)14. Among them, a substrate receptor has a substrate recognition domain that confers the specificity of the degradation process. We then aimed to identify a substrate receptor(s) for DBP. For this purpose, Flag-DBP was overexpressed in NIH3T3 cells, and the cells were treated with MG132 for 6 h to prevent proteasome-dependent degradation of Flag-DBP protein. Then Flag-DBP and the associated protein complex were purified by immunoprecipitation with anti-Flag antibody, and the semi-purified proteins were analyzed by shotgun proteomics (Fig. 3a). We found that six lysine residues of DBP (K40, K42, K55, K236, K246, and K292) are ubiquitinated (Fig. 3b), implying that E3 ligase(s) responsible for DBP ubiquitination interacts with DBP in this condition. As expected, we identified six E3 ligases that contain substrate recognition domains (i.e., substrate receptors) (Fig. 3c and Table 1). It should be noted that these proteins were not detected in a control experiment using Flag-Luciferase expressing cells. Among these six E3 ligases, only TRAF7 (tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 7) is indicated to functionally associate with UBE2G1 in STRING database (Fig. 3c). In addition, overexpression of TRAF7 reduced the DBP protein levels (Fig. 4a; see below), while any of the other five E3 ligases did not (Supplementary Fig. S2), highlighting TRAF7 as a strong candidate for E3 ligase catalyzing the UBE2G1-mediated DBP ubiquitination and degradation. Co-immunoprecipitation assay verified that TRAF7 interacts with UBE2G1 (DN) in HEK293T cells (Fig. 3d). Similarly, the interaction between TRAF7 and UBE2T (DN) was also detected (Fig. 3e), although TRAF7-UBE2T interaction is not listed in the STRING database. In addition, we found that TRAF7 was co-immunoprecipitated with DBP (Fig. 3f), supporting the results of our shotgun proteomics. Notably, the interaction was enhanced when we expressed a dominant negative form of TRAF7 (TRAF7 ΔRING) lacking the RING domain required for interaction with E2. This is probably because catalytically inactive TRAF7 continues to interact with DBP without the ubiquitination process. To strengthen our idea that TRAF7 ubiquitinates DBP, we assessed the TRAF7-dependent ubiquitination state of DBP. For this purpose, Flag-DBP, TRAF7, and HA-Ub were co-expressed in HEK293T cells. The cells were treated with MG132, and DBP proteins were immunoprecipitated and subjected to immunoblotting with an anti-HA antibody to detect ubiquitinated DBP. We found that the Ub level was increased when TRAF7 was overexpressed (Fig. 3g), suggesting that TRAF7 mediates the ubiquitination of DBP. Importantly, this increase was blunted when K48R mutant of HA-Ub (K48R) was co-expressed instead of HA-Ub (WT). These data imply that TRAF7 is responsible for generating the K48-linked poly-Ub chain. Together, our results strongly suggest that TRAF7 functions as an E3 ligase for DBP and promotes its ubiquitination.

TRAF7 regulates DBP protein level

TRAF7 was originally identified by a screening for components in the TNFα/NF-κB pathway15, in which TRAF7 promotes ubiquitination of an adapter molecule NEMO, NF-κB essential modulator16, and the tumor suppressor protein p5317. Having shown that TRAF7 forms a complex with UBE2G1/UBE2T and DBP and promotes ubiquitination of DBP, we examined whether TRAF7 regulates DBP protein levels. In a heterologous expression system using HEK293T cells, overexpression of TRAF7 significantly reduced the protein levels of DBP in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4a). In contrast, overexpression of TRAF7 ΔRING resulted in an increase of DBP protein (Fig. 4b). In addition, TRAF7 knockdown by the shRNA expression upregulated the DBP protein level, although the mean difference was slightly above the significant level of 0.05 in shRNA#1-transfected cells (p = 0.089). (Fig. 4c, d). Notably, TRAF7 overexpression had no significant effect on the mRNA level of Dbp (Fig. 4e). Further, MG132-sensitive reduction of DBP protein levels was not observed in TRAF7-knockdown cells (Fig. 4f). In sum, it is most probable that TRAF7 functions as an E3 ligase for DBP and mediates its proteasomal degradation.

To further emphasize the role of TRAF7 as a regulator of DBP protein levels, we sought to assess the impact of TRAF7 ablation on cellular clock function in culture. For this purpose, we generated a TRAF7-KO cell line from NIH3T3 cells using the CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing (Fig. 4g). In the TRAF7-KO cells after synchronization by dexamethasone (Dex) pulse treatment, we confirmed that mRNA levels of Traf7 were constitutively reduced at significantly low levels, whereas Traf7 is constantly expressed at a high level across the day in control NIH3T3 cells (Fig. 4h). In the TRAF7-KO cells, mRNA levels of Dbp were slightly downregulated but still maintained the circadian rhythmicity (Fig. 4h). On the other hand, the peak level of DBP protein was significantly upregulated by TRAF7-KO and more than ~2 fold higher than that in control cells (Fig. 4i). These results imply that TRAF7 could determine the peak level of DBP protein by promoting degradation of DBP. In addition, the effect of UBE2T (DN) and UBE2G1(DN) on DBP protein levels was significantly attenuated in TRAF7-KO cells (Figs. 2b and 4j), suggesting that TRAF7 mediates UBE2T- and UBE2G1-dependent degradation of DBP protein. Together, TRAF7 is most likely to regulate UBE2T/UBE2G1-mediated degradation of DBP, and this degradation system should be a key mechanism underlying the robust temporal regulation of DBP protein levels.

To investigate in vivo roles of TRAF7, we generated TRAF7 deficient mouse. Contrary to our expectations, DBP protein level in the liver of the TRAF7-KO mouse was indistinguishable from that in WT mouse (Supplementary Fig. S3a). It is plausible that a redundant mechanism of E3-dependent DBP degradation may contribute to regulation of DBP protein levels in the liver and/or that the DBP degradation mechanism may be cell type-specific. We then assessed the temporal profiles of protein expression in the TRAF7-KO liver with mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis (Supplementary Data 1). We found that the expression rhythms of several rhythmic proteins were attenuated in the TRAF7 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. S3b), as evidenced by statistical evaluation of circadian rhythmicity, BIO_CYCLE18 and CircaCompare19 (Supplementary Table S1). Remarkably, these included a couple of proteins (ACOT1 and ALAS1/HEM1) whose transcription is known to be regulated via D-box7,10. These results suggest that the activity of DBP is altered in the absence of TRAF7, although we could not rule out the possibility that the indirect effect of TRAF7-KO is involved in the alterations of the temporal profiles of D-box-regulated genes.

The protein levels of DBP show a robust rhythmicity and the circadian protein rhythms should contribute to stabilization and fine-tuning of the molecular clockwork21. Having shown that TRAF7 binds to DBP and regulates its stability and transcriptional activity, we finally asked the impact of TRAF7 on the circadian clock oscillation. It has been known that overexpression or knockdown of DBP cause lengthening or shortening of the circadian period of Rat-1 fibroblasts, respectively22. Consistently, overexpression of TRAF7 causing reduction of DBP levels (Fig. 4a) shortened the period length of the Bmal1-luc rhythms in cultured NIH3T3 cells (Fig. 5b). Also, overexpression of DBP resulted in a decrease of the AUC (area under the curve) of the bioluminescence signal curves of Bmal1-luc rhythms (Fig. 5c), implying attenuation of the rhythmicity of Bmal1-luc reporter. Intriguingly, this attenuation was counteracted by overexpression of TRAF7, as evidenced by an increase in the AUC (Fig. 5c). These effects of TRAF7 overexpression were not observed when TRAF7 was replaced by TRAF7 ΔRING (Fig. 5b, c). Collectively, we conclude that ubiquitinating activity of TRAF7 is crucial for regulation of the circadian period and it contributes to adjustment of the clock oscillation in particular conditions where expression of DBP is upregulated.

Discussion

DBP protein levels highly oscillate in a circadian manner. Its oscillation drives daily variations of many physiological processes, most notably detoxification, free fatty acid metabolism, and blood pressure7–10. While previous studies have mainly focused on the downstream pathways regulated by DBP, it is largely unknown how the expression level of DBP is regulated posttranslationally. In this study, we demonstrated that DBP is degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome system and that E2 enzymes UBE2G1 and UBE2T are responsible for this process. UBE2G1 and UBE2T were identified by a screening that employed dominant negative mutants of well-characterized E2 enzymes (Fig. 2). It is reported that UBE2G1 is required for UV-induced degradation of the replication licensing factor Cdt1, which prevents aberrant replication in S phase of the cell cycle23. On the other hand, UBE2T, also known as FANCT, is associated with Fanconi Anemia, a rare recessive or dominant DNA repair disorder24,25 and it is responsible for monoubiquitination of FANCI-FANCD2 complex that repairs DNA interstrand crosslinks26. To the best of our knowledge, the functional connection between these E2 enzymes and the biological clock has not been examined. The present study highlights the role of these two E2 enzymes, UBE2G1 and UBE2T, as potential modulators of daily oscillations of a variety of DBP-mediated physiological processes.

In this study, we identified TRAF7 as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that regulates the stability of DBP. Ubiquitin has seven lysine residues, and poly-ubiquitin chains can be assembled through linkage to any of the seven residues. In fact, it has been shown that TRAF7 catalyzes different Lys-type ubiquitinations. For example, TRAF7 mediates Lys29-linked polyubiquitination of NEMO16, and this ubiquitination is associated with lysosome-dependent degradation of the target proteins. On the other hand, TRAF7 promotes K48-linked ubiquitination of p5317, followed by proteosome-dependent degradation of ubiquitinated p53. We found that a decrease of DBP protein level after CHX treatment was minimized by application of a proteasome inhibitor but not by lysosome inhibitors (Fig. 1c). In addition, we showed that the increase of Ub levels of DBP upon TRAF7 overexpression was blunted when HA-Ub (K48R) was co-expressed instead of HA-Ub (WT) (Fig. 3g). Collectively, it is most probable that TRAF7 mediates K48-linked polyubiquitination of DBP, thereby leading to its proteasomal degradation.

In TRAF7-KO NIH3T3 cells, the peak of DBP protein level was upregulated, whereas the trough level was similar to that of control cells (Fig. 4i), implying time-of-the-day-specific degradation mechanism of DBP by TRAF7. Interestingly, TRAF7 is unique among TRAF family members in that it has WD40 repeats at its C-terminal region (Fig. 4g). It should be noted that several E3 ligases harboring WD40 domain, such as β-TrCP and FBXW7, recognize a stretch containing phosphorylated amino acid residue(s) termed phospho-degron and promote degradation of the target proteins27,28. In addition, DBP is most probably a multiply phosphorylated protein as evidenced by its broad band shift in immunoblot analysis5. Thus, the time-of-the-day-dependent phosphorylation of DBP should be a potential mechanism that leads to TRAF7-mediated DBP degradation and it should maintain appropriate levels of DBP protein. Further studies on the temporal pattern of DBP phosphorylation at each site and those on the phospho-degron recognized by TRAF7 will unveil the mechanism underlying the time-of-the-day-dependent regulation of DBP protein level. While we demonstrated the role of DBP degradation in terms of cellular clock regulation, it is unclear at which conditions UBE2G1, UBE2T and TRAF7 mediate the degradation of DBP proteins. Clarifying the processes in the future is important to fully understand the physiological role of DBP degradation.

To assess the role of TRAF7 on the clock oscillation, we performed its gain- and loss-of-function experiments. In particular, we found that overexpression of TRAF7 resulted in shortening of the period length of the cellular clock (Fig. 5b). Because TRAF7 promotes proteasomal degradation of DBP protein, it is speculated that DBP protein level remains constantly low in TRAF7-overexpressed cells. This period-shortening effect is very similar to that observed in Dbp-knockdown cells22. Therefore, it is likely that the effect of TRAF7 overexpression on the cellular clock is mediated by down-regulation of DBP protein. This is also supported by our results showing that TRAF7 overexpression counteracts DBP overexpression in terms of the amplitudes of the clock gene expression (Fig. 5c).

In contrast to overexpression of TRAF7, KO of Traf7 gene in NIH3T3 cells had no significant effect on the period length of the cellular clock (Supplementary Fig. S4). In the Traf7 KO cells, a peak level of DBP protein was up-regulated, while its trough level was kept low (Fig. 4i). These results suggest that hyper-accumulation of DBP protein within a restricted time period (i.e., the peak phase of DBP expression) has a marginal effect on the circadian period length. Nonetheless, considering a pivotal role of D-box-dependent transcriptional regulation in the circadian output pathways8–10, daily variations in the activity of several physiological processes may be perturbed by TRAF7 KO. Further analysis of this point using TRAF7 deficient animals will clarify how TRAF7 coordinates the circadian outputs.

In conclusion, our present study provides insights into the regulatory mechanism for D-box-dependent transcription. The identification of UBE2G1-, UBE2T- and TRAF7-dependent degradation of DBP protein carries the hope of deciphering the entire mechanisms underlying the circadian regulation of biological processes, which is crucial for prediction and adaptation to day-night cycles.

Methods

Mice

Mice (C57BL/6 background) were handled in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals at The University of Tokyo. Mice were housed in cages with free access to commercial chow (CLEA Japan) and tap water. The animals were maintained in a light-tight chamber at a constant temperature (23 ± 1 °C) and humidity (55 ± 10%). Only male mice were used in this study, and mice were sacrificed at 2-3 month of age. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Tokyo. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Cell culture

NIH3T3 cells (RIKEN Cell Bank), HEK293T cells (American Type Culture Collection) were maintained at 37 °C under 5% CO2 and 95% air in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (SIGMA, D5796-500ML or Nacalai Tesque, 08459-64) supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 10% FBS.

Plasmids

The coding sequence of Dbp (NM_016974.3) was cloned from mouse liver-derived cDNA and subcloned into pSG5 or Flag/pSG5 as described previously5. The coding sequences of mouse Traf7 (NM_001172114.1) and human Traf7 (NM_032271.3) were cloned from cDNA of NIH3T3 cells and HEK293T cells, respectively. These coding sequences were inserted into pSG5 (Agilent #216201) with 6xMyc-tag (MEQKLISEEDL each). The coding sequences of mouse Dcaf5 (NM_177267.5), Kcmf1 (NM_019715.2) and the sequence from 13090 to 15479 of Ubr4 (NM_001160319.1) were cloned from cDNA of NIH3T3 cells. The coding sequence of Fbxw8 (NM_172721.2) was cloned from MEF cDNA. The expression plasmid for TRIM25 (Flag-TRIM25/pcDNA3) is a kind gift from Dr. Shigetsugu Hatakeyama (Hokkaido University). To monitor the bioluminescence decay of DBP::LUC, cDNA of Dbp was recombined to Luciferase/p3xFLAG_CMV_14 which was described previously29. pEGFP-C1 was purchased from Clontech (#6084-1). Plasmid driving expression of TRAF7 dRING is generated from Myc-TRAF7/pSG5. Whole sequence of Myc-TRAF7/pSG5 plasmid except for the RING domain (aa 92-126) was amplified by PCR and ligated. A series of Flag-E2 (DN)/pRK5 vectors were constructed as previously described13. 3xD-box #1_Luc/pGL3N was constructed in the previous study5. pRL-SV40 was purchased from Promega (#E2231). Short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) were constructed as previously described30. Following sequences are designed by using siDirect (http://sidirect2.rnai.jp/) and inserted into pSilencer3.1-H1 puro (Ambion, AM5768) according to the manufacturer’s instructions: Traf7_sh1, 5’-GCCTT GAAGT CAGAG AAGTG T-3’; Traf7_sh2, 5’-GAAAG CTCTC GGAGA AGATC G-3’; shUBE2G1, 5’-GATGT TGATG CTGCG AAAGA A-3’23; shUBE2T, 5’-GACAT ATCCT CAGAA TTTAA A-3’. A control shRNA was designed as described previously30. See also Supplementary Table S2 for plasmid list.

Antibodies

Anti-FLAG M2 antibody (SIGMA, F3165) and anti-DBP antibody (MBL, PM079; Yoshitane et al., 2019) were used for immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting. Anti-GFP antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-8334), anti-Myc antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-40) and anti-HA antibody (Abcam, ab72479) were used for immunoblotting.

Immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitation was performed as described previously31 with modifications. Transfected cells were lysed for 30 min in ice-chilled lysis buffer [20 mM HEPES-NaOH, 137 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 5%(v/v) glycerol, 0.05% (v/v) Lubrol PX, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 4 μg/mL aprotinin, 4 μg/mL leupeptin, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF); pH 7.8]. The lysate was gently mixed with 40 μL anti-Flag M2 affinity gel (SIGMA, A2220) for 2 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times with wash buffer [20 mM HEPES-NaOH, 137 mM NaCl, 0.05% (v/v) Lubrol PX]. Flag-tagged proteins are eluted by addition of 150 ng/mL Flag peptide in wash buffer and subjected to immunoblotting.

MS spectrometry analysis for DBP-interactome

Proteins purification and shotgun proteomic analysis were performed as previously described29 with modifications. NIH3T3 cells were transfected with expression plasmids for FLAG-DBP or FLAG-Luc (as a control) and cultured for 42 h. Then the cells were treated with 10 µM MG132 for 6 h. The cells were harvested and suspended in lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, 137 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 5% glycerol, 0.05% Lubrol, 1 mM DTT, 4 mg/mL aprotinin, 4 mg/mL leupeptin, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM PMSF; pH7.8) and the cell lysate was incubated with anti-Flag M2 agarose affinity gel (Sigma) at 4 °C for 2 h. The agarose beads were washed once with wash buffer A (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, 137 mM NaCl, 0.05% Lubrol; pH 7.8) and twice with the wash buffer B (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, 137 mM NaCl; pH 7.8). Flag-DBP and its associated proteins were eluted by elution buffer [20 mM HEPES-NaOH, 137 mM NaCl, 150 ng/µL FLAG-peptide (Sigma); pH 7.8] followed by tryptic digestion. Shotgun proteomic analyses were performed by a linear ion trap-orbitrap mass spectrometer (LTQ-Orbitrap Velos, Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with nanoflow LC system (Dina-2A, KYA Technologies).

Preparation of peptides for circadian proteome analysis

Enzymatic digestion of mouse liver proteins was performed according to a phase transfer surfactant (PTS) protocol32 as previously described4. Briefly, mouse livers were collected every 4 h from 26 h after the beginning of the DD condition (expressed as projected CT). The liver blocks frozen in liquid nitrogen were lysed with ice-cold PTS buffer (12 mM sodium deoxycholate [DOC], 12 mM sodium N-lauroylsarcosinate [SLS], 100 mM NH4HCO3) followed by extensive sonication. Proteins (24 µg) in the solution were reduced with final 10 mM DTT at 60 °C for 30 min, and then alkylated by incubation with final 22 mM iodoacetamide [IAA] at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The resultant protein sample was diluted 5 times with 100 mM NH4HCO3 solution and digested with trypsin (Sigma) at 1:100 [wt/wt] by incubation at 37 °C for 18 h in the dark. After the digestion, an equal volume of ethyl acetate was added to the samples, and the mixture was acidified with 0.5% TFA and mixed well to transfer the detergents into the organic phase. After the samples were centrifuged at 15,700 × g for 1 min at room temperature, the aqueous phase containing peptides was collected. The sample was concentrated by a centrifugal evaporator (EYELA) and desalted using Mono-Spin C18 column (GL Sciences). The eluate was dried by the evaporator before LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS-based circadian proteome analysis by DIA method

LC-MS/MS-based circadian proteome analysis was performed as previous described with some modifications4. The dried and desalted peptides were dissolved in distilled water containing 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% TFA. The LC-MS/MS analyses were performed using a MS spectrometer (Q Exactive Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a nano ultra-HPLC system (Dionex Ultimate 3000; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were loaded to the LC–MS/MS system with a trap column (0.3 × 5 mm L-column ODS; Chemicals Evaluation and Research Institute) and a capillary column (0.1 × 150 mm L-column ODS; Chemicals Evaluation and Research Institute) at a flow late of 20 µL/min. The loaded peptides were separated by a gradient using mobile phases A (1% formic acid in distilled water) and B (1% formic acid in acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min (0% to 30% B in 35 min, 30% to 50% B in 5 min, 50%–95%B in 0.1 min, 95% B for 9.8 min, 95%–0% B in 0.1 min, and 0% B for 5 min). The eluted peptides were ionized by electrospray (2.0 kV) and introduced into the MS equipment (positive ion mode, data-dependent MS/MS). For the DIA experiments, the Q Exactive Plus was operated at a full-MS resolution of 70,000 with a full scan range of 500–750 m/z when stated. The full-MS AGC was set to 3e6. Fragment ion scans were recorded at a resolution of 70,000 and maxIT of auto. We used 64 windows of 4-Th scanning from 500 to 750 m/z. The isolated ions were fragmented using HCD with 28% Normalized Collision Energy.

Raw MS data analysis for DIA method

Raw files from DIA comparison experiments were analyzed in DIA-NN 1.8.133 using an in silico DIA-NN predicted spectral library (10,300,991 precursors, allowing for C carbamidomethylation and N-terminal M excision and 1 missed cleavage). The spectral library was generated from a mouse reference database (SwissProt TaID = 10090). The DIA-NN search included the following settings: Protein inference = ‘Genes’, Neural network classifier = ‘Single-pass mode’, Quantification strategy = ‘Robust LC (high precision)’, Cross-run normalization = ‘RT-dependent’, Library Generation = ‘Smart Profiling’ and Speed and RAM usage = ‘Optimal results’. Mass accuracy and MS1 accuracy were set to 0 for automatic inference. ‘No share spectra’, ‘Heuristic protein inference’ and ‘MBR’ were checked. Output data was filterd at 0.01 FDR.

Circadian rhythm detection

Circadian rhythm detection was previously described4. To analyze the circadian rhythmicity, datasets were run through BIO_CYCLE (ver0.9.3)18 obtained from CircadiOmics web portal. The range of the circadian period was set to 20–28 h according to the default settings. Unless otherwise mentioned, rhythmic genes were clarified with three criteria: (1) BIO_CYCLE P-value is less than 0.3, (2) When the expression levels are averaged at each time, the highest expression level (Peak) is 1.5 times higher than the lowest expression level (Trough), and (3) The largest SD value among each time point (SDMAX) is within 1.5 times the deference between the Peak and Trough (Amplitude). In addition, the differences in rhythmicity and mean gene expression were evaluated by the CircaCompare R package (version 0.2.0)19.

CHX chase assay

HEK293T cells were transfected with DBP, 6xMyc-TRAF7 and GFP expression vectors and cultured for 48 h. The transfected cells were then treated with 100 μg/mL cycloheximide (Nakalai tesque, 06741-04) and 25 μM MG132 (CalBioChem, 474790) for time periods specified in the figures and harvested, followed by immunoblotting. For inhibiting lysosomal proteases, 50 μM leupeptin and 10 mM NH4Cl were added. The half-life of protein degradation was calculated using exponential regression.

Degradation assay of DBP-LUC

NIH3T3 cells were transfected with DBP-LUC expression vectors and were selected in 1 mg/ml G418 (Nakalai tesque, 16512-94) for three days. Single colonies were then picked. To monitor the bioluminescence, cells were cultured in the recording medium [phenol-red free DMEM (Sigma Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Equitech Bio, Inc.), 3.5 mg/mL glucose, 25 U/mL penicillin, 25 mg/mL streptomycin, 0.1 mM luciferin, and 10 mM HEPES-NaOH; pH 7.0]. After 48 h, 100 μg/mL cycloheximide was treated. Luciferase activity was recorded at 10 min intervals at 37 °C in air with Dish Type Luminescencer, Kronos (ATTO, AB-2500) or LumiCEC (Churitsu).

Preparation of nuclear fractions of mice liver

Mouse livers were collected every 4 h from 26 h after the beginning of the DD condition (expressed as projected CT). Nuclear proteins were isolated as previously described34. Briefly, the mouse liver (1 g wet weight) was washed with ice-cold phosphatebuffered saline and homogenized on ice with 9 mL of ice-cold buffer A (10 mM HEPES-NaOH, 10 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 4 μg/ mL aprotinin, 4 μg/mL leupeptin, 50 mM NaF, and 1 mM Na3VO4; pH 7.4). The homogenate was centrifuged (700 × g for 5 min), and the resultant precipitate was rinsed twice. The precipitate was resuspended in 2 mL of ice-cold buffer C [20 mM HEPES-NaOH, 400 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 2% (v/v) glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 4 μg/ mL aprotinin, 4 μg/mL leupeptin, 50 mM NaF, and 1 mM Na3VO4; pH 7.8]. After being gently mixed at 4 °C for 1 h, the suspension was centrifuged (18,000 × g for 30 min), and the supernatant was used as the nuclear fraction.

Preparation of nuclear fractions of cells

Transfected cells were lysed with ice-chilled buffer A [10 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.8), 10 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 mM PMSF, 4 µg/mL aprotinin, 4 µg/mL leupeptin, 50 mM NaF, and 1 mM Na3VO4] and then 0.02%[w/v] NP-40 was added. The lysate was centrifuged (5 min, 14,000 rpm), and the precipitate was rinsed by buffer A and centrifuged (5 min, 14,000 rpm). The precipitate was resuspended in SDS-PAGE sample buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl, 2 mM EDTA, 2% SDS, 6% glycerol, 50 mM DTT, 0.02% CBB R-250; pH 6.8] and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min, used as “nuclear fraction”.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

A dual luciferase reporter assay was performed as described previously5 with minor modifications. HEK293T cells were cultured in 24-well plates for 1 day, then 200 ng of Flag-Dbp/pSG5 was transfected in combination with 20 ng of a firefly luciferase expression plasmid harboring 3x D-box sequences (TTACGTAA) as a reporter, and 2 ng of a Renilla luciferase expression plasmid (pRL-SV40) as an internal control using polyethylenimine (Polysciences, #24765). The total amount of transfected DNA (822 ng) was adjusted by adding the empty vector. The transfected cells were harvested 36 h after the transfection and subjected to the dual luciferase assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol with the aid of a fluorescence plate reader (Promega GloMax). Internal control was used to normalize the transfection efficiencies.

Generation of TRAF7 KO cell line

The CRISPR-Cas9 system was used to generate TRAF7 KO cells as described previously4 with modifications. The sequences of sgRNA were designed to target the intron 3 (sg1) and intron 4 (sg2) of Traf7 gene with CRISPRdirect (http://crispr.dbcls.jp/). Generation of TRAF7 KO mouse

Cas9 protein (Guide-it recombinant Cas9 protein, TaKaRa) and sgRNA (Fasmac) were delivered by electroporation to C57BL/6J embryos at the pronuclear stage as described previously4. Briefly, the embryos were washed three times with Opti-MEM I (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 0.1% polyvinylalcohol (PVA) and once with 0.1% PVA-Opti MEM I containing Cas9 protein (100 ng/µL) and sgRNAs (50 ng/µL each). Then the embryos were placed in a line in the gap of an electrode (LF501PT1-10, BEX, Tokyo, Japan) filled with 0.1% PVA-Opti-MEM I (total 5 µL in volume) containing Cas9 protein and sgRNAs, and electroporation was performed using a CUY21EDIT II electroporator (BEX). The sequences of sgRNAs were the same as those used for generation of TRAF7 KO cell line. Note that we confirmed the skipping of Exon4 of the Traf7 gene in all TRAF7 KO mice used by PCR analysis.

Real-time monitoring of rhythmic gene expression

Cellular rhythms were monitored as described previously36 with minor modifications. To monitor the bioluminescence from luciferase, cells were entrained by pulse treatment of 0.1 µM (final concentration) dexamethasone for 30 min, and then cultured in recording media [phenol-red free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (SIGMA, D2902) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 3.5 g/L glucose, 25 U/mL penicillin, 25 µg/mL streptomycin, 0.1 mM luciferin, and 10 mM HEPES-NaOH; pH 7.0]. The bioluminescence signals were continuously recorded for 5–10 days at 37 °C in air with Dish Type Luminescencer, LumiCycle (Actimetrics) and Kronos (ATTO, AB-2500). The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated by summing the absolute Y-axis values of relative bioluminescence rhythms from 24 to 96 h after the dexamethasone treatment. For determination of the circadian period, the raw data of bioluminescence rhythms were detrended by subtracting their 24-h moving averages and were smoothed by their 2-h moving averages. The circadian period was calculated from the slope of a regression line fitted to time points of consecutive peaks and troughs of cellular rhythms by the least-squares method. At least 5 time points of peaks and troughs of cellular rhythms were selected for the fitting.

RNA preparation and RT-qPCR

NIH3T3 or HEK293T cells growing in 3.5 cm culture dish were lysed with 300 µL TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018). Total RNA was prepared with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74104) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. From purified RNA, cDNA was generated by Go Script Reverse Transcriptase (Promega, A5003) with both anchored (dT) 15 primer and random oligo primers or ReverTra Ace qPCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, FSQ-301). The expression levels of genes were examined by using GoTaq Master Mix (Promega, A6010) and StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR Systems (Applied Biosystems). Following primers were used; Traf7_Fw (5’- CCTTT TCGAC CGTCA CTAC-3’), Traf7_Rv (5’- GTCCT GCGAT GCTGT TTG-3’); mDbp_Fw (5’- AATGA CCTTT GAACC TGATC CCGCT-3’); mDbp_Rv (5’- GCTCC AGTAC TTCTC ATCCT TCTGT -3’); mRps29-Fw (5’- TGAAG GCAAG ATGGG TCAC -3’); mRps29-Rv (5’- GCACA TGTTC AGCCC GTATT -3’); hDBP_Fw (5’- CTGAT CTTGC CCTAT CAAGC ATT-3’), hDBP_Rv (5’- CGATG TCTTC GAGGG TCAAA-3’); hACTB_Fw (5’- CACTC TTCCA GCCTT CCTTC-3’), hACTB_Rv (5’- GTACA GGTCT TTGCG GATGT-3’).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel or GraphPad Prism 9. Comparisons of mean differences between groups were based on a Student’s two-tailed t test. For comparison of multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA with a Dunnett/Tukey corrections were applied to avoid false discoveries because of multiple comparisons. Sample sizes are based on related studies29,35,37: All experiments were performed with biological replicates.
