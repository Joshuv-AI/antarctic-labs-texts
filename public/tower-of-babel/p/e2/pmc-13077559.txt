VAMP4/STX8 Mediate the Autophagic Secretion of Mitochondria and Promote TAMs Polarization in HNSCC
Authors: Jingcun Shi, Chun Liu, Xiaoning Wang, Chuwen Li, Zhen Zhang, Ming Yan, Haiyan Guo, Jianjun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnder hypoxic conditions, tumour cells engage in autophagic secretion of mitochondria to sustain energy balance and modulate the tumour microenvironment. However, the exact molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study used a combination of multivesicular bodies membrane proteomics and molecular interaction validation to demonstrate that hypoxia promotes mitophagy‐dependent extracellular vesicles (EVs) secretion in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). This process promotes tumour‐associated macrophages to adopt immunosuppressive phenotypes, reshaping the immune environment. Vesicle‐associated membrane protein 4 (VAMP4) and syntaxin 8 (STX8) are key molecules involved in this secretion. Clinically, VAMP4/STX8 expressions are significantly higher in HNSCC than in normal tissues and correlate with prognosis, indicating their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets. This study revealed the mitophagy‐secretion axis that reshaped the tumour immune landscape, providing a theoretical basis for targeting soluble NSF attachment protein receptor‐dependent EV release to enhance the therapeutic effect of HNSCC immunotherapy.

Introduction

Cellular adaptation to hypoxia represents a fundamental biological process spanning various physiological and pathological contexts, including cancer progression, ischemic diseases, and adaptation to high‐altitude environments (Semenza 2023; Schito and Rey‐Keim 2023). Central to this process is the dynamic regulation of mitochondrial homeostasis, wherein damaged or superfluous mitochondria are selectively degraded via mitophagy (Liu et al. 2023). Under hypoxic conditions, the removal of damaged mitochondria by mitophagy is an important quality control mechanism that maintains cellular homeostasis and functionality (Semenza 2008). Hypoxia‐induced mitophagy functions as a degradation pathway and regulates secretory processes. Emerging evidence suggests that the selective packaging and release of mitochondrial‐derived vesicles or components associated with mitophagy into the extracellular space facilitates intercellular communication and modulates the tumour microenvironment (TME) (Peng et al. 2022; Tan et al. 2022; Liang et al. 2023). Hypoxia‐induced autophagic secretion of mitochondria (ASM) may involve autophagy‐related proteins, Rab GTPases, and soluble NSF attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complexes. Nonetheless, the precise molecular mechanisms that link mitophagy to secretion remain unclear. Understanding how cells coordinate organelle renewal and stress responses under hypoxic conditions and exploring novel strategies for cancer treatment are crucial to clarify these mechanisms.

Membrane transport processes within cells, including vesicle transport, fusion, and secretion, are intricately orchestrated by conserved molecular mechanisms. Among these proteins, SNAREs serve as the primary driver of membrane recognition and fusion. Typically, SNARE forms specific trans‐complexes between vesicles (v‐SNARE) and target membranes (t‐SNARE), facilitating bilayer fusion through a zipper‐like mechanism that overcomes energy barriers (Jahn et al. 2024). Although the core fusion mechanism is well established, the functional diversity of SNARE subsets across various transport pathways remains a central focus. In the endosomal‒lysosomal transport pathway, syntaxin 8 (STX8) and vesicle‐associated membrane protein 4 (VAMP4) are pivotal regulators of the endosomal‒lysosomal transport pathway (Ivanova et al. 2021; Li et al. 2022). STX8 is localized in early and late endosomes and interacts with VAMP4 on recycling vesicles to form a homologous complex essential for endosome maturation, retrograde transport, and lysosomal delivery (Nozawa et al. 2017). The secretion of regulated vesicles on the plasma membrane (PM) depends on syntaxin 4 (STX4), which is the main docking site for the PM (Grassi et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2023). VAMP4, which is traditionally associated with endosomal fusion, is found in secretory vesicles and collaborates with STX4/synaptosome‐associated protein 23 (SNAP23) to perform exocytosis in specific situations. The functional plasticity of VAMP4 is evident by its dual function of connecting endosome transport with extracellular secretion. Despite these advancements, the regulatory mechanism of SNARE proteins in vesicle transport‐secretion remains unclear.

The TME is a complex system that involves malignant cells that manipulate the extracellular matrix and immune components to promote tumour progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy. Tumour‐associated macrophages (TAMs) are principal regulators of immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and tissue remodelling (Cassetta and Pollard 2023). Notably, TAMs can adopt an M2‐polarized phenotype, which is associated with their functional tumour‐promoting activity. This phenotypic transformation is not merely a secondary effect but constitutes a fundamental mechanism of tumour immune evasion (Wang et al. 2024). M2‐polarized TAMs interact with cancer cells through cytokine release, the expression of immune checkpoint ligands, the regulation of metabolism, and the remodelling of various mechanisms. Tumour cells secrete CCL2, CSF‐1, and extracellular vesicle (EV) to recruit monocytes and induce their polarization to the M2 phenotype. Simultaneously, M2 TAMs secrete TGF‐β and interleukin (IL)‐10, which inhibit dendritic cell maturation and T‐cell activation (Qi et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2024; Xiong et al. 2019; Wen et al. 2018). Additionally, by expressing surface ligands such as PD‐L1 and galectin‐9, which interact with inhibitory receptors such as PD‐1 and TIM‐3, on T cells, M2 TAMs induce T‐cell exhaustion and apoptosis. This establishes a ‘vicious cycle’ that sustains tumour growth and diminishes the efficacy of immunotherapy (Huang et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2024). Although there is a connection between the abundance of M2 TAMs and adverse clinical outcomes, how spatial heterogeneity (e.g., perivascular niches and hypoxic niches) regulates the functional specialization of TAMs remains unclear.

This study presents the first comprehensive analysis of the molecular mechanisms through which hypoxia regulates ASM. Our findings reveal that hypoxia concurrently induces damage to mitochondria and lysosomes, leading to mitophagy, which cannot be degraded by lysosomes. The increased expression and interaction of VAMP4 and STX8 facilitate the fusion of mitophagy with multivesicular bodies (MVB), which subsequently merge with the PM to release mitochondrial components. Additionally, our study demonstrated that macrophages are key cellular components that engage in significant communication with tumour epithelial cells. These macrophages undergo polarization through the endocytosis of tumour cell‐derived EV, resulting in the upregulation of immune checkpoint expression and immunosuppressive effects.

Results

Hypoxia Led to Abnormal Mitochondrial Morphology and Function

The cells were subjected to 1% O2 hypoxic conditions for 48 h to study the effect of hypoxia on the mitochondria, followed by immunofluorescence staining for the mitochondrial matrix protein HSP60. 10 µM carbonyl cyanide 3‐chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP), a phosphoric acid oxide uncoupling agent, was applied to the cells for 2 h as a positive control. Mitochondrial morphology revealed that hypoxia induced significant alterations, including reductions in mitochondrial perimeter, branch length, and form factors, among other parameters (Figure 1A,B). Moreover, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that, compared with those in normoxic cells, mitochondria in hypoxia‐stimulated cells were shorter, swelled, and had lower matrix electron density (Figure 1C). JC‐1 was used to evaluate the mitochondrial membrane potential because it accumulated in the mitochondrial matrix and formed red fluorescent aggregated under high‐membrane potential conditions. Conversely, under conditions of low membrane potential, JC‐1 remains a monomer, resulting in green fluorescence. Flow cytometry with JC‐1 staining indicated a notable increase in the JC‐1 monomer‐to‐polymer ratio in hypoxia‐exposed cells, suggesting decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and depolarization (Figure 1D,E).

Additionally, mitoSOX staining, in conjunction with flow cytometry, found that hypoxia markedly elevated mitochondrial superoxide levels (Figure 1F,G). Furthermore, mitochondrial stress assays also suggested that hypoxia significantly impaired mitochondrial function, including basal respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and ATP production (Figure 1H–J). In conclusion, hypoxia affected mitochondrial morphology, reduces the membrane potential, leading to depolarization, and inhibited oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS).

Hypoxia Caused Mitophagy, Which Does Not Involve Fusion With Lysosomes

Upon mitochondrial damage, mitophagy is activated to preserve the healthy mitochondrial population. Spatial transcriptomic profiling of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) specimens corroborated elevated mitophagy activity in tumour cells within hypoxic microenvironments, indicating a potential mechanistic link between hypoxia and mitophagy regulation (Figure 2A). The PINK1/Parkin pathway serves as the principal mechanism by which cells maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby preventing metabolic crises and cell death (Narendra and Youle 2024). In this study, we extracted total protein from SCC25 cells subjected to hypoxic conditions and assessed the status of the PINK1/Parkin pathway by western blot. Normoxia, lactic acid, CCCP, and starvation were used as controls (Figure 2B). During autophagy, LC3BI undergoes lipidation to form LC3BII, which is then integrated into the autophagosome membrane to mark autophagic substrates. The autophagic receptor p62 is sequestered into autophagosomes and subsequently degraded, resulting in a reduction in its level during autophagy. We found that hypoxia‐treated cells presented elevated levels of phosphorylated ubiquitin (p‐Ub) and PINK1, increased LC3BII/LC3BI ratios, and reduced p62 levels, suggesting increased mitophagy activity (Figure 2B).

Additionally, staining for the autophagosome marker protein LC3 and MitoTracker demonstrated that hypoxic stimulation increased LC3 and mitochondrial co‐localization, which also suggested mitophagy (Figure 2C,D). TEM also showed that the hypoxia‐treated autophagosomes contained mitochondria, and the mitophagy count in each cell was significantly greater than that in the normoxia group (Figure 2E,F). Under normal circumstances, lysosomes fuse with autophagosomes after mitophagy, thereby clearing damaged mitochondria (Picca et al. 2023). We observed significant co‐localization of mitochondria and lysosomes in the CCCP treatment group, while this phenomenon was not observed in the hypoxic treatment group after staining with LAMP1 and MitoTracker (Figure 2G).

Hypoxia Disrupted Lysosomal Homeostasis

Our previous research has demonstrated that under hypoxic conditions, the accumulation of HIF‐1α in tumour cells inhibits the expression of the V‐type ATPase ATP6V1A, resulting in lysosomal dysfunction. This dysfunction promotes MVB conversion to the secretory pathway, ultimately enhancing EV release (Wang et al. 2023). Consequently, we hypothesized that the failure of mitophagy to fuse with lysosomes could be attributed to lysosomal dysfunction (Figure 3A). We verified protein expression in HNSCC cell lines after hypoxic treatment and found a non‐significant difference in the expression levels of the lysosomal proteins LAMP1 and CTSD compared with those in the normoxic treatment group (Figure 3B). TEM of the lysosomes in the cells also showed that hypoxia induced lysosomal swelling (Figure 3C).

Given that enlarged compartments are indicative of lysosomal damage (Fernandez‐Mosquera et al. 2019), we used immunofluorescence assays to assess changes in lysosomal diameter. The data indicated that under hypoxic conditions, the lysosomal volume significantly increased (Figure 3D–F). The LysoTracker probe, a fluorescent acid‐sensitive marker for labelling and monitoring acidic organelles was also used to detect the lysosomal acidification. Analysis of the fluorescence intensity proved that the acidic environment within the lysosomal cavity was disrupted (Figure 3G,H). Lysosomal sensor measurements were performed to determine the pH of the lysosomes, which revealed an increase in pH after exposure to hypoxia (Figure 3I). Immunofluorescence assays confirmed that hypoxic stimulation did not significantly alter the co‐localization of TOM70 and LAMP1 (Figure 3J). These findings indicated that hypoxia disrupts the acidic environment within the lysosomal lumen, causing lysosomal swelling without affecting biogenesis. Hypoxia‐induced lysosomal damage impairs mitochondrial clearance.

Hypoxia Promoted the Release of Mitochondrial Components Through EV

Research has demonstrated that EV contain components, including mitochondrial proteins and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) (Cheng et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2024). Rab27 belongs to the Ras superfamily of small GTPases and participates in exocytosis (Fukuda 2013; Izumi 2007). Rab27a localizes to secretory lysosomes and co‐localizes with a subset of CD63‐positive vesicles to regulate exosomal secretion (Elstak et al. 2011; Bahadoran et al. 2001; Wood et al. 2009). To investigate whether mitochondria that have not undergone lysosomal degradation are secreted via EV, we initially performed immunofluorescence co‐staining for the vesicular secretion‐related proteins RAB27A and TOM70. Our results demonstrated a notable increase in the co‐localization of mitochondria and RAB27A after hypoxic treatment (Figures 4A and S1A). Co‐localization of RAB27A with TOM70 suggests exosomal release of mitochondrial proteins into the extracellular space. Additionally, co‐immunofluorescence staining of HSP60 and CD81/CD63 revealed increased co‐localization of HSP60 and MVB, suggesting that hypoxic treatment resulted in the encapsulation of more mitochondrial components into MVB (Figures 4B and S1B). This phenomenon was observed in cells cultured in vitro but has also been confirmed in HNSCC samples. In hypoxic areas with elevated HIF1α expression, there was significant in the co‐localization of mitochondria (HSP60) and MVB (CD81/CD63) (Figures 4C and S1C). Furthermore, we isolated EV secreted from conditioned medium using sequential ultracentrifugation (Qin et al. 2019). The diameter of the EV was approximately 100 nm, and they had a cup‐shaped morphology, as confirmed by TEM (Figure S1D). Western blot analysis further validated the presence of EV markers, including ALIX, TSG101, RAB5, and CD63 (Figure S1E). Additionally, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was used to evaluate the EV concentration and size distribution (Figure S1F–H). Notably, proteomic analysis of the isolated EV revealed significant enrichment of mitochondrial proteins, constituting 11.88% of the total protein type in the EV. Furthermore, hypoxic conditions increased the levels of mitochondrial proteins in EV (Figure 4D–F). DNA components were also detected within the EV (Figure S1I). Western blot and qPCR analyses confirmed the significant upregulation of mitochondrial proteins, including COXIV, ACO2, HSP60, and citrate synthase (CS), as well as mtDNA, including ATP6, COX1, COX2, ND1, ND4, and ND6 (Figure 4G,H). In summary, these findings suggest that hypoxia facilitates the release of mitochondrial proteins and mtDNA via EV.

ATP6V1A Overexpression Led to a Decrease in Mitochondrial Components in EV

Now that hypoxia induces lysosomal dysfunction by inhibiting ATP6V1A expression, we aimed to determine whether restoring lysosomal function could facilitate the degradation of damaged mitochondria. We used lentivirus which overexpressed ATP6V1A (OE‐ATP6V1A) to infect HN6 cells and verified using RT‐qPCR (Figure S2A). Immunofluorescence co‐staining for LAMP1 and HSP60 revealed that ATP6V1A overexpression significantly enhanced the co‐localization of mitochondria (labelled with HSP60) and lysosomes (labelled with LAMP1) in cells subjected to hypoxic conditions (Figure 5A). Following hypoxic stimulation, there was a marked reduction in the co‐localization of RAB27A and TOM70 (Figure S2B). This led to a significant decrease in the number of mitochondrial components encapsulated within MVB, resulting in a notable reduction in the number of mitochondrial proteins and mtDNA within EV (Figures 5B–D and S2C).

Moreover, we also found that in addition to mitochondrial proteins, the autophagy‐related protein LC3B was significantly enriched in the EV in the hypoxic treatment group, underscoring the critical role of mitophagy in the release of mitochondrial components (Figure 5E). The lack of LC3B led to a decreased width‒to‒length ratio and increased the roundness of the mitochondria, even without hypoxia treatment (Figures 5F and S2D). This suggested a reduction in the complexity of the mitochondrial network and an expansion in mitochondrial aggregation. Moreover, following hypoxic treatment of tumour cells deficient in LC3B, there was a marked reduction in the secretion of mitochondrial components (Figure 5G,H). The importance of autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction in ASM is underlined by these results, which highlight the crucial role of lysosomal protection in the degradation of mitochondrial components.

Mitochondrial Components in EV Promoted TAMs M2 Polarization

Mitophagy in tumour cells results in the release of mitochondrial components via EV. Single‐cell sequencing of HNSCC samples was conducted to identify the main recipient cells in the TME. Through the analysis of intercellular communication, it was determined that endothelial cells, M2 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and dendritic cells within the TME exhibit close communicative interactions with tumour epithelial cells. Notably, M1 and M2 macrophages demonstrated the most intimate communication with epithelial cells in tumour (Figure 6A). Subsequent analysis revealed that among the outgoing signalling pathways of tumour epithelial cells, the MK, PLAU, and MIF modules were particularly prominent (Figure 6B). Consequently, we assessed mitophagy in tumour cells using the mitophagy GSEA gene set and examined the relationships between the PLAU and MIF genes and mitophagy activity. The findings indicated that in the cohort with elevated mitophagy scores, MIF and PLAU expression levels were significantly higher than those in the tumour epithelial cells with low mitophagy levels (Figure 6C). This study analysed the overall survival of HNSCC patients by integrating RNA‐seq data with data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. These findings suggested a significant correlation between LC3B levels and patient prognosis, which was influenced by the extent of macrophage infiltration (Figure S3A–C). Using multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining, we proved that there were more CD68+ macrophages in the peritumoral regions compared with CD3+ T cells and CD20+ B cells, and were closer to epithelial cells in HNSCC samples. (Figure 6D). Thus we hypothesized that CD68+ macrophages might engaged in close communication with epithelial cells within the TME and potentially act as recipient cells for EV derived from epithelial cells (Figure 6E). We subsequently isolated EV from the HN6 cell culture medium. Following DID staining, the EV were co‐cultured with THP‐1‐induced macrophages for 12 h. Confocal microscopy displayed that the tumour cell‐derived EV were phagocytosed by the macrophages (Figure 6F). In the co‐culture assay of EV and macrophages, the dosage of vesicles was standardized based on the total protein concentration of EV measured after lysis and the NTA assay(Figure S3D,E). We performed immunofluorescence staining for the M1 marker iNOS and the M2 marker macrophage mannose receptor (MMR) in macrophages co‐cultured with EV to study the effects of EV containing mitochondrial components on macrophage polarization. The results showed that EV containing mitochondrial components facilitated M2 polarization in macrophages but had non significant effect on M1 polarization (Figures 6G and S3F–I). Furthermore, there was notable upregulation of the expression levels of the immune checkpoints PD‐L1 and galectin‐9 on macrophage surface (Figure 6H,I). The secretion of cytokines linked to M2 macrophages, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐10, and TNF‐α, was significantly increased (Figure 6J). Previous research has indicated that mtDNA can activate the cGAS signalling pathway in macrophages, thereby promoting the secretion of various inflammatory factors (Wang et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2023), which consistent with our findings (Figures 6K and S3J). Moreover, treatment of macrophages with the cGAS pathway inhibitor G50 and EV effectively reversed the increased expression levels of MMR proteins (Figure 6L). In addition, LC3B knockdown in tumour cells to inhibit autophagosome maturation reversed the activation of the cGAS pathway (Figure 6M). In conclusion, EV containing mitochondrial components derived from tumour epithelial cells are internalized by macrophages, leading to activation of the cGAS‐STING signalling pathway and promotion of immunosuppressive phenotypes.

Vesicle Transporter Proteins VAMP4 and STX8 Mediated ASM

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying hypoxia‐induced ASM, we isolated MVB from cells subjected to hypoxic conditions and subsequently extracted membrane proteins for proteomic analysis (Figure 7A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the SNARE proteins VAMP4 and STX8, which are involved in vesicle transport, were significantly upregulated in the hypoxia‐treated group (Figure 7B). VAMP4, a member of the V‐SNARE protein family, is predominantly localized in the vesicle membrane and is involved in membrane fusion events during vesicular transport. Conversely, STX8, classified as a T‐SNARE protein, is located primarily on the target membrane and plays a role in membrane recognition and docking during vesicular transport (Koike and Jahn 2019; Jun and Wickner 2019). Using the TCGA database, we found that that VAMP4 and STX8 expression levels were markedly elevated in HNSCC samples compared with normal samples (Figure S4A,B), with high STX8 expression correlated with poor patient prognosis (Figure S4C). Furthermore, Cox regression analysis also hinted that VAMP4 and STX8 expression was associated with patient prognosis, with STX8 potentially serving as an independent prognostic marker for HNSCC (Figure S4D,E). Domain analysis of the two proteins revealed that in addition to SNARE domains, they contained membrane localization sequences and non‐cytoplasmic segments. Consequently, after excising the membrane localization and non‐cytoplasmic sequences, we used AlphaFold 3 to predict the binding structures of the two proteins (ipTM = 0.48, pTM = 0.39). The predicted protein‐binding structures were visualized using PyMOL, which demonstrated that VAMP4 and STX8 interact via multiple amino acids within the SNARE domain (Figure 7C and Table S1). The interaction between VAMP4 and STX8 in tumour cells and the membrane proteins of MVB was corroborated (Figure 7D,E). mIHC staining of HN6 cells with antibodies against VAMP4, STX8, and the MVB marker CD63 displayed significant co‐localization of these proteins in the hypoxic treatment group (Figure 7F). The significant increase in the co‐localization levels of VAMP4, STX8, CD81, and LC3B under hypoxic conditions indicated that the VAMP4 and STX8 interaction may be pivotal in MVB and autophagosome fusion (Figures 7G and S5A). Following prolonged hypoxia treatment, the expression levels of the two SNARE proteins in the cells increased (Figure 7H). Spatial transcriptomic profiling of HNSCC also exhibited concordant upregulation of VAMP4 and STX8 transcripts in tumours, particularly in distinct hypoxic regions (Figure S4F), and further linked their expression to mitophagy activation (Figure S4G).

Following the knockdown of these SNARE proteins using small interfering RNA (siRNA), autophagosomes (labelled with LC3B) containing mitochondrial components (labelled with HSP60) failed to fuse with MVB, leading to a significant reduction in mitochondrial protein and mtDNA components within EV (Figures 7I–K and S5B,C). However, knocking down the expression of VAMP4/STX8 did not affect the secretion of EV (Figure S5D,E). Moreover, several key genes involved in mitophagy, including PINK1, ATG12, BNIP3L, BNIP3, ATG7, and BECN1, have been identified. Cancer hallmark analysis revealed that these genes were associated with the reprogramming of energy metabolism and resistance to apoptosis (Figure S5F). Furthermore, the application of the autophagy inhibitor 3‐MA or transfection with siVAMP4 or siSTX8 significantly increased apoptosis in cells subjected to hypoxic conditions, indicating that mitophagy flux plays a critical role in maintaining cell survival (Figure 7L). Additionally, we engineered truncation mutations for the VAMP4 and STX8 proteins on the basis of their protein domains. Upon deletion of the domain‐containing binding site, the levels of mitochondrial proteins and mtDNA in EV were significantly reduced (Figure 7M–O).

STX4 and SNAP23 Mediated EV Release

The precise role of the SNARE protein in mediating the fusion of MVB and the PM remains incompletely characterized. Research has revealed that STX4 and SNAP23 are involved in the exosomal secretion process in MCF7 breast cancer cells, which primarily facilitates membrane fusion (Liu et al. 2023). Compartment enrichment analysis of the interaction protein network, including VAMP4, STX8, STX4, and SNAP23, revealed the localization of these proteins in various structures, including the SNARE complex and autophagic vesicles (Figure S5G). These proteins were significantly enriched in physiological processes such as SNARE complex assembly and vesicle fusion (Figure S5H). mIHC staining revealed notable co‐localization of CD81, VAMP4, STX4, and SNAP23 in hypoxia‐treated HN6 cells, suggesting that the SNARE proteins STX4, SNAP23, and VAMP4 may play roles in vesicle and PM fusion (Figure 8A). Knockdown of STX4 and SNAP23 expression in tumour cells resulted in a significant reduction in EV secretion without affecting the number of intraluminal vesicles within MVB, indicating their specific involvement in MVB‒PM fusion (Figures 8B–D and S5I,J). Analysis of EV protein and western blot further demonstrated that EV secretion in tumour cells transfected with siSTX4 and siSNAP23 decreased (Figure 8E,F). Knockdown of STX4 and SNAP23 also significantly downregulated the levels of mitochondrial proteins and mtDNA in EV (Figure 8G,H).

Discussion

Although the pivotal role of mitophagy in tumour adaptation and immune evasion is well established, a significant gap in our understanding of its function as a cancer‐promoting mechanism remains. Increasing evidence suggests that tumour cells eliminate dysfunctional mitochondria via mitophagy, thereby preventing reactive oxygen species accumulation and averting cellular energy crises. This process enables tumour cells to adapt to microenvironmental stress, accelerate growth, and facilitate immune escape (Vara‐Perez et al. 2019). However, existing research has focused predominantly on the mechanisms underlying mitophagy and its association with clinical prognosis. The mechanisms underlying the active secretion of mitophagy‐derived components and their regulatory effects on the tumour immune microenvironment, particularly TAMs polarization, have not been systematically explored. This study employed an integrative approach, combining membrane proteomics, molecular interaction validation, and functional experiments, to demonstrate that hypoxia induces mitophagy‐dependent secretion in HNSCC, leading to the release of EV enriched with mitochondrial components. These EV directly induced TAMs polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment. VAMP4 and STX8 are key molecules that mediate secretion processes, offering insights into how tumour cells reprogram the immune microenvironment via ASM and suggesting a potential target to inhibit immune escape in HNSCC (Figure 8I).

Hypoxia is a central feature of various pathological conditions, including solid tumours and ischemic diseases. Reduced oxygen levels initiate adaptive hypoxic responses, with EV facilitating complex intercellular communication (Xi et al. 2021). Clinical evidence links hypoxic tumours to increased risks of recurrence and metastasis, which are correlated with poor prognosis (Jing et al. 2019). A pan‐cancer analysis of the TCGA database identified HNSCC as having the highest hypoxia levels among the 19 malignant tumours (Bhandari et al. 2019). Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier analysis of previous research demonstrated that elevated hypoxia scores and survival outcomes in the TCGA‐HNSCC cohort were significantly correlated (Wang et al. 2023).

Hypoxia induces mitochondrial depolarization, impairs OXPHOS, and reduces ATP production, thereby triggering mitophagy to eliminate damaged mitochondria. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma, PINK1‐mediated mitophagy promotes therapeutic resistance by reprogramming lipid metabolism, whereas its inhibition restores drug sensitivity (Mauro‐Lizcano et al. 2024). Notably, hypoxia drives novel inter‐organelle communication, including mitochondrial engulfment by lysosomes through STX17‐SNAP29‐VAMP7‐mediated membrane fusion, initiating ‘mitochondrial self‐digestion’ (Hao et al. 2023). Mitochondrial quality control (MQC) traditionally involves lysosomal degradation; however, hypoxia disrupts lysosomal function, leading to lysosomal damage and EV release (Wang et al. 2023). Our findings revealed that hypoxia induced lysosomal enlargement and loss of acidity, impairing mitophagy clearance. These mechanisms highlighted hypoxia‐driven remodelling of mitochondrial–lysosomal quality control. However, attempts to reverse hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial damage were not involved, preventing definitive conclusions about the causal relationship between mitochondrial repair and downstream processes. This limitation will be addressed in future studies through targeted experimental designs.

HNRNPK and SAFB interact with LC3 and are secreted via LC3‐enriched EV, underscoring autophagy‐EV cargo crosstalk (Leidal et al. 2020). Dysfunctional mATG8‐PE lipidation disrupts autophagosome formation, prompting mitochondria to generate vesicles through MVB via secretory autophagy, establishing an ‘autophagy‐secretion transition’ (Tan et al. 2022). Lysosomal inhibition (for example, Rab7 deletion or pharmacological blockade) redirects MQC toward the secretion of ubiquitinated mitochondria‐containing EV in cardiomyocytes (Liang et al. 2023). EV‐mediated clearance is increased in aged hearts and Danon disease, linking lysosomal dysfunction to cardiac degeneration. Consistently, mitochondrial components colocalize with secretory proteins (RAB27A and CD81) during lysosomal impairment. Notably, ATP6V1A overexpression enhances mitochondrial‒lysosomal associations, reduces MVB co‐localization, and suppresses mitochondrial secretion.

SNARE proteins mediate intracellular membrane fusion; however, compartment‐specific localization mechanisms remain unclear. Proteomics has revealed the VAMP7‐SNAP47 interaction; SNAP47 is localized to the cytoplasm, ER, and ER‐Golgi intermediate compartment, where it exhibits nucleocytoplasmic shuttling (Leitz et al. 2024). This study revealed that hypoxia upregulated VAMP4 and STX8 expression in MVB membranes, as revealed by mass spectrometry. Moreover, we confirmed the co‐localization of VAMP4/STX8 with MVB (labelled with CD63/CD81) and autophagosomes (labelled with LC3B). Silencing these SNAREs or disrupting their interaction inhibited mitochondrial release, suggesting that the VAMP4‐STX8 interaction increased hypoxia‐driven EV release via MVB‒autophagosome fusion. Although previous studies have implicated STX4/SNAP23/VAMP7 in exosome secretion (Liu et al. 2023), hypoxia did not alter MVB VAMP7 levels in our study. Notably, VAMP4/STX4/SNAP23 colocalized extensively and silencing of STX4/SNAP23 reduced EV release without affecting MVB biogenesis, indicating their role in PM fusion under hypoxia.

DNA‐enriched EV (include genomic DNA and mtDNA) secreted by activated T cells induce dendritic cell activation via cGAS‐STING signalling, enhancing antiviral responses (Torralba et al. 2018). EV‐derived DNA exhibits functional roles in pathologies including malignancies and autoimmune disorders (Malkin and Bratman 2020), with implications in senescence and inflammation. mtDNA within EV from senescent cells boosts immunosuppressive cell activity through cGAS‐STING, facilitating tumorigenesis (Lai et al. 2025). Additionally, mtDNA release and cGAS‐STING activation contribute to hepatic cirrhosis‐associated myopathy (Fan et al. 2025). This study demonstrates that hypoxia‐exposed HNSCC cells release EV enriched with mitochondrial proteins and mtDNA. Co‐culture experiments confirmed these EV activate the cGAS‐STING pathway in THP‐1‐induced macrophages. Our findings suggest that macrophage cGAS‐STING activation is partially attributable to elevated mtDNA content in tumour‐derived EV. EV‐mediated mtDNA transfer is a key mechanism for reshaping the TME. Senescent cells transmit mtDNA to PMN‐MDSCs via EV, activating the cGAS‐STING‐NFκB pathway and PERK phosphorylation to amplify immunosuppression (Lai et al. 2025). In hormone‐resistant breast cancer, CAF‐derived mtDNAhi‐EV are internalized by tumour stem cells, enabling dormant cell survival and drug resistance via OXPHOS restoration (Sansone et al. 2017). Similarly, EV from resistant triple‐negative breast cancer cells deliver mutant mtDNA (e.g., ND4) to sensitive cells, increasing chemoresistance (Abad and Lyakhovich 2022). Our findings demonstrated that hypoxic tumour‐derived EV were enriched with mitochondrial proteins (COXIV, ACO2, HSP60, and CS) and mtDNA (ATP6, COX1/2, and ND1/4/6), implicating EV in the metastatic dissemination of mitochondrial components.

Mitochondria‐derived vesicles (MDVs) constitute a specialized vesicular pathway that selectively transports damaged mitochondrial proteins, lipids, and other cargo to lysosomes for degradation, thus preserving mitochondrial homeostasis. Initial characterizations established MDVs as distinct from classic mitophagy, with roles in oxidative stress response and mitochondrial quality surveillance (Neuspiel et al. 2008; Soubannier et al. 2012). Subsequent investigations have expanded understanding of MDVs in cellular signalling, inflammation, and disease progression, highlighting their integration into broader mitochondrial networks (König and Fielden 2024). MQC operates through interconnected mechanisms, including mitophagy for wholesale organelle elimination, dynamic fission and fusion to segregate dysfunctional segments, ubiquitin‐proteasome‐dependent degradation of outer membrane proteins, and MDVs formation for targeted cargo removal (Sugiura et al. 2014). As one of the ways of MQC, MDVs may serve as one of the main sources of EVs rich in mitochondrial components in this study. However, direct confirmation of MDVs involvement necessitates targeted experiments, such as cargo‐specific labelling and trafficking assays.

Macrophages are central regulators of the TME, and their polarization is controlled by EV. GBM‐derived EV transfer miR‐1246 to induce M2 polarization via TERF2IP/STAT3 signalling while suppressing T cells through CD73‐mediated adenosine accumulation, collectively driving an ‘immune desert’ phenotype (Skouras et al. 2023). Single‐cell RNA sequencing and mIHC validated the macrophage–tumour epithelium crosstalk in the TME in this study. Mitochondria‐enriched EV amplify M2 polarization, upregulate PD‐L1/galectin‐9 checkpoints, and promote cytokine release, thereby reshaping immune dynamics. Therapeutic strategies targeting the EV‒mtDNA axis require consideration of the TME context. Huang's mtDNA‐enhanced dendritic cell vaccine (cNPcancer cell@MVDC) leverages cationic nanoparticles to deliver mtDNA and tumour antigens, activating T/NK cells and normalizing the vasculature to convert ‘cold’ tumours to ‘hot’ tumours (Shang et al. 2024).

Conclusion

Our study provides novel evidence that hypoxia facilitates mitophagy and the release of mitochondrial components in tumour cells via the VAMP4/STX8‐EV release axis, thereby preventing the accumulation of reactive oxygen species and apoptosis. Furthermore, released EV, which contain mitochondrial components, contribute to the immunosuppressive phenotype by interacting with TAMs. These findings underscore the importance of considering hypoxia and mitophagy, particularly SNARE expression levels, in the design of immunotherapy strategies for HNSCC patients. Consequently, further research is warranted to develop innovative targeted therapies that concurrently inhibit SNARE proteins and tumour cells.

Materials and Methods

Patient and Specimen Information

Clinical samples for this study were collected from patients with HNSCC at Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital. The Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital Ethics Committee authorized the study protocol, and informed consent was obtained from all enrolled individuals in accordance with the institutional guidelines.

Cell Culture

The SCC25 and CAL27 cell lines were obtained from ATCC (USA), and the UMD School of Dentistry (USA) provided the HN6 cell line. DMEM (BasalMedia, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat No. 10270106; Gibco, USA) was used to maintain 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 units/mL streptomycin, and 5% CO2 in CAL27 and HN6 cells. In contrast, SCC25 cells were maintained in DMEM/F‐12 (BasalMedia, China) with identical supplements. A ThermoFisher Scientific tri‐gas incubator was used to create hypoxic conditions by combining 1% O2, 5% CO2, and balanced N2. To facilitate attachment, the cells were cultured under normoxic conditions for 12 h before being transferred to a hypoxic incubator for further cultivation. Human monocytic leukemia cells (THP‐1) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA). THP‐1 cells were differentiated into macrophages (M0) by treatment with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA; Cat No. HY‐18739; MedChemExpress, USA) for 48 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh PMA‐free medium for further culture.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as we described before (Wang et al. 2023), in cases where additional markers were to be observed, primary antibodies derived from different species were used. FITC‐phalloidin or TRITC‐phalloidin (Cat No. 40735; 40734; Yeasen, China, 1:200) was used to visualize the cytoskeleton, whereas DAPI (Cat No. 40728; Yeasen, China, 1:1000) was used to stain the Nucleus. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss confocal fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). We chose the ‘Region of Interest’ on the original pictures randomly, and the magnification scale bar was created by ‘Image scaling’ in Zeiss software. The diameter of the LAMP1‐positive lysosomes was determined through proportional conversion on the basis of magnified high‐power field images. Mitochondrial morphology was assessed using the mitochondrial analyser plugin of ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). All co‐localization analyses of fluorescent signals were conducted using ImageJ. The Scatter J plugin (ImageJ) was employed to extract intensity values from individual fluorescence channels and calculate Pearson's coefficient across multiple channels. Table S2 lists the antibodies used in this study.

Lens Electron Microscope

Sample preparation for transmission electron microscopy was performed as we described before (Wang et al. 2023). Lysosomal diameters were calculated from magnified images, whereas the number of mitochondrial autophagosomes per cell was quantified by blinded analysis of >10 cells. For EV, 2.5% glutaraldehyde‐fixed samples (pH 7.4, 4°C) on carbon‐coated copper grids were negatively stained with uranyl acetate and examined under 80‐kV TEM.

Flow Cytometry

The cells were dissociated into single‐cell suspensions and washed with ice‐cold PBS. JC‐1 (Cat No. C2003S; Beyotime, China) and MitoSOX working solutions (Cat No. 40778; Yeasen, China) were prepared following the manufacturer's protocol and incubated with the cells at 37°C for 10‒20 min. For surface staining, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies targeting PD‐L1 and galectin‐9 (Cat No. 13684; 54330; Cell Signalling Technology, USA) diluted in 100 µL of staining buffer for 30‒60 min on ice under light‐protected conditions. Following centrifugation (300 × g, 5 min), the supernatants were discarded, and the cells were resuspended in fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat No. 111‐545‐003; Jackson, USA; 1:200) for 30 min at 4°C. After the washing steps were repeated, the cell pellets were resuspended in 200 µL of PBS supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin and immediately analysed by flow cytometry.

MitoSOX Assay, MitoTracker Assay and LysoTracker Assay

Upon reaching appropriate cell density, culture medium was removed, and cells were stained with respective fluorescent probes. For mitoSOX assay (Cat No. 40778; Yeasen, China), a 5 µM solution was incubated at 37°C for 10 min in the dark, followed by PBS washing and confocal imaging. MitoTracker Red (Cat No. C1035; Beyotime, China) was applied at 1:5000 dilution for 15–30 min at 37°C, after which cells were either imaged directly or fixed for immunofluorescence. LysoTracker Red (Cat No. L7528; Life Technologies, USA) was used at 50 nM in culture medium for 20 min at 37°C, followed by PBS rinsing, glutaraldehyde fixation, DAPI counterstaining (1:1000; Cat No. 40728; Yeasen, China), and confocal analysis. Quantitative assessment of LysoTracker‐positive puncta was performed using ImageJ software, analysing ten random fields per sample. All probes were protected from light during incubation, and imaging was conducted under uniform confocal microscopy settings.

Mitochondrial Stress Assay Using a Seahorse XF Analyzer

Mitochondrial function was evaluated using a Seahorse XF Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, SCC25 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in a Seahorse XF96 plate (Cat No. 103793‐100; Agilent Technologies, USA) and incubated overnight under standard culture conditions. Prior to conducting the assay, the medium was replaced with DMEM (pH 7.4; Cat No. ALS22111; Alicelligent, China) supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM glutamine, and 2 mM pyruvate (Cat No. ALS22121; Alicelligent). This was followed by a 1 h equilibration period at 37°C in an incubator devoid of CO2.

The mitochondrial stress test was executed by sequentially injecting oligomycin at a concentration of 1.5 µM, carbonyl cyanide 4‐(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP) at 0.5 mM, and a combination of rotenone and antimycin A (Cat No. ALS22013; Alicelligent, China) at 0.5 µM. This procedure was designed to evaluate the basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR), proton leakage, maximal respiratory capacity, and non‐mitochondrial OCR. The data obtained were normalized relative to the total protein content, as determined using the BCA assay.

ATP Production Assay

200 µL of lysis buffer was added to each well of a 6‐well plate. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4°C for 5 min, after which the supernatants were collected. The ATP standard solution was diluted to a concentration gradient ranging from 0.01 to 10 µM to construct a standard curve. The ATP detection working solution was prepared according to the instructions provided in the ATP detection kit (Cat. No. S0026; Beyotime). Subsequently, 100 µL of detection working solution was added to each well of a 96‐well plate. The sample was allowed to equilibrate at RT for 3–5 min. Subsequently, 20 µL of either the sample or ATP standard solution was added, and the relative light unit (RLU) value was measured using SpectraMax i3 instrument (Molecular Devices, USA).

Western Blot

SDS lysis buffer was used to lyse EV or cellular samples, and a BCA assay kit (Cat No. 23227; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to quantify protein concentrations. Using the PAGE technique and gradient gels ranging from 4%–20% (Cat No. M42015C; GenScript Bio, China), the proteins were separated and then transferred onto 0.22 µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Cat No. SEQ00010‐N1; Merck Millipore, USA). 5% skim milk was used to block the membranes for 2 h at RT, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies specific to the target proteins. β‐actin, β‐tubulin, or vinculin was used as a loading control for cells, and ALIX or syntenin was used to normalize EV protein levels. Following washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat No. A0208; A0216; Beyotime, China) at RT for 1 h. Immobilon Forte Western HRP substrate (Cat No. P10060; New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) was used to visualize the protein bands, and the Amersham Imager 600 system (GE Healthcare, USA) was used for detection. The intensity values of all protein expression levels from western blot bands were quantified using ImageJ and subsequently normalized to the corresponding loading controls of the control group.

LysoSensor Assay

LysoSensor Yellow/Blue DND‐160 (Cat No. L7545; Invitrogen, USA) was used to determine the lysosomal pH. The LysoSensor assay was preformed according to the manufacturer's protocol. For fluorescence analysis, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS and reconstituted in 100 µL of HBSS. pH was calculated from the logarithmic ratio (X = log(F2/F1)) against a standard curve derived from Y = log(pH). Calibration curves were generated using a modified protocol (DePedro and Urayama 2009), where the cells were treated with pH buffer (3.5–7.5, Cat No. BB‐489113; Bestbio, China) containing 20 µM nigericin (Cat No. HY‐127019; MedChemExpress, USA) post‐staining, followed by fluorescence measurement.

Multiplex Immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

mIHC was performed using tyramide signal amplification (TSA, Cat No. HY‐D1835; HY‐D1833; MedChemExpress, USA; Cat No. RK05902P; ABclonal, China) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Antigen retrieval was repeated between staining cycles using a citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95°C for 5 min to remove primary and secondary antibodies. For adherent cells, antibody clearance was achieved using the mIHC‐specific antibody resolution solution (Cat No. SB‐Y6001; Share‐Bio, China) without heat‐induced antigen retrieval. After the final TSA cycle, the Nucleus were counterstained with 1 µg/mL DAPI (Cat No. 40728; Yeasen, China; 1:1000). Imaging of the sections was performed using the Vectra 3 Automated Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA). The study conducted mIHC detection on paraffin sections from five HNSCC patients.

EV Isolation

EV were isolated according to a protocol described previously (Fukuda 2013). The cells were cultured in medium containing EV‐depleted FBS (SBI, USA). After 48 h of pretreatment, the cell culture supernatant was collected, and the sample was filtered through a 0.22 µm filter (Cat No. SLGP033N; Merck Millipore, USA) after centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 15 min, and 12,000 × g for 45 min. The cell culture supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 120,000 × g for 70 min to obtain a pellet. EV were suspended in PBS for further analysis. EV used in macrophage co‐culture experiments were isolated from HN6 culture supernatants. Specifically, HN6 cells were cultured in exosome‐depleted FBS‐supplemented medium (System Biosciences, USA) under normoxic or hypoxic conditions for 48 h. Following treatment, the culture supernatant was collected for EV isolation. The input volume of EV was standardized based on NTA detection and protein concentration quantification experiments.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

A ZetaView particle tracker (ParticleMetrix, Germany) was used with ZetaView 8.05.02 software to determine the size distribution and particle concentration. Three cycles, 11 cell positions, and 30 frames per second were used in each measurement. Using parameters such as a maximum particle size of 1000, a minimum particle size of 5, and a minimum particle brightness of 20, the analysis is conducted at a shutter speed of 25°C and a shutter setting of 70.

Mass Spectrometry Analysis

The samples were analysed by LC‐MS/MS using timsTOF Pro. The proteins were extracted using 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. The samples were subjected to reduction, alkylation, and trypsin digestion (1:50 enzyme‐to‐substrate ratio) at 37°C overnight. Peptides were separated by reverse‐phase ultrahigh‐performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Acclaim PepMap C18 column. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile (mobile phase B), with a gradient ranging from 3% to 45% over 55 min within a total gradient duration of 60 min.

Peptides were analysed using a TimsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, USA) equipped with a CaptiveSpray nanoelectrospray source at 1.6 kV. Data‐dependent acquisition was performed via parallel accumulation‐serial fragmentation over a mass range of 300–1750 m/z. All other parameters were maintained at their default settings.

The data were processed using PEAKS software (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., USA) against the UniProt Human database (downloaded 1.1.2023) with carbamidomethyl (Cys, fixed), an oxidation variable. Proteins were quantified using label‐free quantitation, and statistical analysis was conducted using Perseus, applying a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The differential expression analysis (DEGs) were subjected to the hypergeometric distribution of R for GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses.

DNA Extraction and Copy Number Calculation in EV

EV were extracted from 2 × 106 cells and resuspended in 100 mL of PBS. To confirm DNA encapsulation within EV, the vesicles were subjected to DNase I (Ambion, USA) treatment at 37°C for 30 min before DNA purification. DNA extraction was performed according to established protocols (Wang et al. 2020). qPCR was used to assess the mtDNA copy number in EV. Table S3 contains all the PCR primers used.

Nuclear DNA, specifically β‐actin, was undetectable after 45 cycles of amplification. The relative expression levels of each mtDNA primer set were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized against the global mean, which is the average Ct value for each primer set. Therefore, the following formula 2− (X—Ct) was used, and the derivation using the 2−ΔΔCt method is due to the inability to obtain a reliable internal control using DNA isolated from EV (Lazo et al. 2021).

Lentivirus Transduction and Stable Strain Screening

Gene overexpression was carried out by synthesizing human ATP6V1A using the pcDNA3.1 lenti‐PGK‐Puro vector. For transfection, the vector was encapsulated in a lentiviral vector. Shanghai Zorin Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (China) was used to construct the vectors and encapsulate the lentivirus. Lentiviral particles containing either OE‐ATP6V1A or empty vectors were used to transfect HNSCC cells. A stable cell line was established by treating the cells with 5 mg/mL puromycin (Cat No. 60209ES; Yeasen, China) for 2 weeks and 72 h post‐transfection.

RNA Extraction and RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, and reverse transcription was conducted using PrimerScript RT reagent following the manufacturer's protocol (Cat No. 639505; Takara, Japan). RT‒qPCR analysis was performed on an ABI StepOne system (Life Technologies, USA) with SYBR Green (Cat No. 11200; Yeasen, China). Relative quantification of the target genes was normalized to that of β‐actin as an internal control. The amplification results were analysed using the formula (2−ΔΔCT).

Small Interfering RNA (siRNA) Transfection

Gene knockdown was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Cat No. L3000015; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded at 50% confluency in 6‐well plates and transfected with 50 nM siRNA (Sangon Biotech, China) or a non‐targeting control. The siRNA transfection reagent complex was incubated in DMEM for 15 min at RT before being added to the cells. After 48–72 h, gene expression was assessed via western blotting or RT‒qPCR to confirm the knockdown efficiency. All the siRNA sequences used are listed in Table S4.

Spatial Transcriptome Sequencing, scRNA‐seq and Analysis

Spatial transcriptome sequencing, scRNA‐seq were performed as we described before (Li et al. 2024). Spatial transcriptomic libraries were constructed using the 10× Visium platform with integrated spatial barcoding followed by PCR amplification. Functional enrichment (q < 0.05) was conducted via g:Profiler. The scRNA‐seq libraries were prepared with the 10× Genomics Next GEM Single‐Cell 3' Reagent Kit v3.1 and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150). DEGs was performed using MAST tests in FindMarkers, applying thresholds of p < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| > 0.58. Cell‒cell communication networks were mapped via CellChat (v1.1.3) and CellPhoneDB (v2.0), and ligand‒receptor interactions were visualized using Igraph and circlize.

Survival Analysis Based on the TCGA Dataset

We acquired STAR‐count RNA‐sequencing data and corresponding clinical annotations for HNSCC from The TCGA database, retaining 504 samples with complete RNA‐seq profiles and clinical records after normalization via log2 (transcripts per million + 1) transformation. Normal tissue expression data were sourced from the TCGA database. Survival disparities were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis with log‐rank tests, whereas univariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to quantify hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Multivariate regression analyses were performed using the forestplot package for visual synthesis of the statistical parameters. All computations were performed using the R software version 4.0.3, adopting a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Prognostic correlations between macrophage infiltration and LC3B expression were further validated using the KM plot pan‐cancer RNA‐seq analysis platform (https://kmplot.com).

EV Fluorescence Labelling

To confirm macrophage internalization of tumour cell‐derived EV, the EV were fluorescently labeled with DiD (Cat No. 127274‐91‐3; Targetmol, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The DiD‐labeled EV were then cocultured with macrophages for 12 h. The cytoskeleton of the macrophages was stained with phalloidin, and the Nucleus were stained with DAPI (Cat No. 40728; Yeasen, China; 1:1000). Images were obtained using a laser confocal microscope.

Multiplexed Flow Cytometry Analysis

ABplex‐100 (ABclonal, China) was used for sample analysis, following the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, standard substances or samples were prepared, and for each assay, 5 µL of microbead suspension was added to each well, followed by 50 µL of either the standard or test sample. The plate was gently shaken and incubated at 37°C for 1 h and then washed once with a magnetic separator. Next, 50 µL of detection antibody solution was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min, followed by an additional wash. 50 µL fluorophore solution was subsequently added, followed by a 15‐min incubation at 37°C in the dark. After 55 µL of wash buffer was added, the fluorescence signals were measured using the ABplex‐100 system. The data were analysed to determine the concentration on the basis of the standard curves.

Endosome Extraction via Discontinuous Gradient Sucrose Centrifugation

The cells were resuspended in 4°C precooled PBS prior to counting. A total of 7 × 107 cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 600 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of ice‐cold HB buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 3 mM imidazole, 1 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors in ultrapure water. Cell lysis was carried out through homogenization with 10–30 strokes or repeated passages through a 23–27G needle 15–20 times. The homogenate was then diluted with HB buffer to a final homogenate‐to‐buffer ratio of 1:0.7 and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and subjected to another round of centrifugation under the same conditions to obtain postnuclear supernatant (PNS).

A discontinuous sucrose gradient with concentrations of 25%, 35%, and 40.6% was prepared in 12 mL ultracentrifuge tubes. The PNS was gently layered on top of the gradient to avoid disturbing the layer boundaries. Gradient centrifugation was performed using a Beckman ultracentrifuge with a swinging bucket rotor at 210,000 × g for 4 h at 4°C, with both acceleration and deceleration set to 1. Fractions of 1 mL were collected from the bottom of the gradient, transitioning from high to low density at a rate of 1 mL/min using a peristaltic pump. Twelve fractions were collected for further analysis. MVB membrane proteins were extracted using the Membrane Protein and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Cat No. P0033; Beyotime, China). The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 4°C and 14,000 × g for 30 min to precipitate the membrane fragments. The supernatant was removed, and 200 µL of membrane protein extraction reagent B was added. The precipitate was vortexed for 5 s for resuspension and then placed on ice for 5 min. This process was repeated twice. Finally, the samples were centrifuged at 4°C and 14,000 × g for 5 min to obtain a membrane protein mixture from the supernatant.

Protein Interaction Prediction and Visualization

Potential interaction partners of VAMP4 and STX8 were predicted via AlphaFold 3. The amino acid sequences of the candidate proteins were submitted to AlphaFold 3 (DeepMind & Isomorphic Labs, UK) for modelling protein‒protein complexes. The interaction interfaces were analysed via PDBePISA (EMBL‐EBI, UK) to calculate both the buried and solvent‐accessible surface areas. The predicted complexes were visualized via PyMOL (Schrödinger, USA) to identify key residues involved in binding, and the combined interface was illustrated via the interfaceresidues.py script.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assay

Cellular proteins were extracted from adherent cultures by enzymatic detachment, followed by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min. Lysis was performed in 1 mL of lysis buffer (Cat No. P70100; New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) supplemented with proteolytic inhibitors and PMSF for 15 min with intermittent mixing. After lysis, the samples were subjected to high‐speed centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, yielding clear supernatants for protein assays. For coimmunoprecipitation, an input fraction (10%) was reserved, while the remainder was divided into duplicates: one was incubated with an IgG (Cat No. 30000‐0‐AP; Proteintech, China) control antibody, and the other was incubated with the specific antibody of interest, both of which were incubated overnight with rotation at 4°C. Prior to incubation with the cell lysates, protein A/G magnetic beads (Cat No. HY‐K0202; MedChemExpress, USA) were pretreated, washed, resuspended in RIPA buffer, and blocked with 5 µg/mL IgG for 2 h at 4°C to prevent nonspecific interactions. Lysate‒bead complexes were allowed to form for 6 h at 4°C and then rigorously washed five times with lysis buffer. After boiling in sample buffer at 100°C for 10 min, the eluted proteins were subjected to western blot analysis with a target‐specific antibody to validate the immunoprecipitation efficiency.

Construction of Protein‐Truncated Plasmids and Transfection

The following constructs were generated to construct the protein‐truncated plasmids: VAMP4(WT), VAMP4(Δ2‐49aa), VAMP4(Δ50‐116aa), STX8(WT), STX8(Δ2‐138aa), and STX8(Δ139‐207aa). Applied Biological Materials Inc. (abm, China) was used to construct the protein‐truncated plasmids. VAMP4 was modified by appending a 3×FLAG tag at the N‐terminus and a 2A‐GFP tag at the C‐terminus. A 3×HA tag was appended to the N‐terminus of STX8, and a 2A‐mCherry tag was attached to the C‐terminus to create the pcDNA3.1(+) vector. Prior to plasmid transfection, the 293T cells reached approximately 70% confluence. For transfection, 2 µg of DNA and 6 µL of ezFect DNA transfection reagent (Cat No. 100—505; Goonie, China) were combined with 100 µL of serum‐free medium, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at RT for 5 min. The two solutions were then combined, mixed evenly, and left to stand at RT for an additional 20 min. Serum‐free medium (800 µL) was thoroughly mixed and added to a 6‐well plate for transfection. After 8 h, the transfection medium was removed and replaced with serum‐containing medium. The transfection efficiency was evaluated 24–72 h later, and subsequent experiments were conducted.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 9.1 (GraphPad Software, USA‌). All the quantitative experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, and the data are reported as the mean ± SD. Group comparisons were performed via Student's t test or the Mann‒Whitney U test for two‐group analyses, whereas one‐way ANOVA was applied for multi‐group evaluations. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was designated as the threshold for statistical significance.
