Long-Term Clinical Course and Progression Patterns of Normal-Tension Glaucoma Following Disc Hemorrhage
Authors: Eun Ji Lee, Hyunjoong Kim, Jungsuk Yoon, Seung Joon Lee, Tae-Woo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to determine the long-term clinical course and patterns of progressive retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thinning and their associated factors in eyes with normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) following disc hemorrhage (DH).MethodsThis longitudinal case series included 227 eyes with NTG followed for ≥10 years after experiencing at least one DH episode. Rates and patterns of global RNFL thinning were assessed using linear and nonlinear regression models. Risk factors for faster RNFL loss and distinct progression patterns were evaluated using random-forest modeling with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis.ResultsMost eyes (93.8%, n = 213) exhibited significant RNFL thinning during follow-up, with 33.0% (n = 75) demonstrating nonlinear progression patterns. The average rate of thinning did not adequately reflect the complexity of progression in eyes with nonlinear progression patterns. Random-forest modeling identified that the key predictors of rapid RNFL thinning were a larger number of additional IOP-lowering medications after first detecting DH, less-severe visual field (VF) damage, and a smaller %IOP reduction. The presence of a decelerating progression pattern (P_dec) was associated with a thicker lamina cribrosa, a larger %IOP reduction, a larger number of additional medications after first detecting DH, and younger age. Subgroup analyses of P_dec eyes showed that earlier stabilization was associated with lower initial IOP, fewer episodes of DH recurrence, and less-severe VF damage.ConclusionsRNFL thinning after DH occurred in diverse patterns, with a substantial proportion of eyes demonstrating late-phase stabilization. Clinical features indicative of smaller mechanical stress were likely key factors in attenuating glaucoma progression following DH.

Disc hemorrhage (DH) is widely recognized as a significant risk factor for glaucoma progression. The underlying mechanisms remain a matter of debate, ranging from mechanical damage of microvessels in the optic nerve head (ONH)1–5 to ischemic microvascular damage,6–8 but DH is consistently associated with structural9,10 and functional10–15 deteriorations in glaucomatous eyes.

Given its prognostic implications, the detection of DH in clinical practice presents a major challenge. Once DH is detected, a key clinical concern is whether the patient is likely to experience further progression, how rapidly deterioration will occur, and what management strategies would be most appropriate for that individual. However, despite extensive studies establishing DH as a significant risk factor for glaucoma progression,13,16–18 there remains a paucity of data on the long-term clinical course of patients with glaucoma following DH. The lack of consensus on the pathogenesis of DH further reduces the ability to make consistent, evidence-based clinical decisions.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the 10-year clinical course of patients with normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) with documented DH. By identifying progression patterns and associated risk factors, we hoped to provide evidence for use in developing individualized treatment strategies that can optimize long-term outcomes for patients with DH.

Methods

This study enrolled patients with NTG who participated in the Investigating Glaucoma Progression Study, which is an ongoing prospective study of patients with glaucoma at the glaucoma clinic of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital. All subjects provided written informed consent for their participation. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Participants

NTG was defined as the presence of an open iridocorneal angle with a baseline untreated IOP of ≤21 millimeters of mercury (mm Hg) and signs of glaucomatous optic nerve damage (i.e. neuroretinal rim thinning or notching, or a retinal nerve fiber layer [RNFL] defect), with a corresponding glaucomatous visual field (VF) defect. A glaucomatous VF defect was defined as a defect that met at least one of the following criteria: (1) outside normal limits on a glaucoma hemifield test, (2) 3 abnormal points with a <5% probability of being normal and one abnormal point with a <1% probability of being normal according to pattern deviation, or (3) a pattern standard deviation (PSD) of probability <5% confirmed on 2 consecutive reliable tests. VF tests were defined as being reliable when the fixation-loss, false-positive, and false-negative rates were <20%, <15%, and <25%, respectively.

To be eligible for inclusion in this study, eyes were required to have at least one documented episode of DH during regular follow-up exceeding 6 months and a subsequent follow-up period of ≥10 years. A DH was defined as an isolated splinter-like or flame-shaped hemorrhage on the optic disc or peripapillary retina. All eyes had been under treatment for more than 6 months before the first documented DH. Eyes were excluded if they had a best-corrected visual acuity worse than 20/40, a spherical equivalent of <−9.0 diopter (D) or >+3.0 D, a cylinder correction of <−3.0 D or >+3.0 D, a history of intraocular surgery with the exception of uneventful cataract surgery, retinal diseases (e.g., diabetic retinopathy, retinal vessel occlusion, or retinoschisis), or neurological diseases (e.g., pituitary tumor). When a subject had two eligible eyes, one was randomly selected for inclusion in the study.

Ophthalmic Examinations

All subjects underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations, which included best-corrected visual acuity assessments, Goldmann applanation tonometry, refraction tests, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, gonioscopy, stereo disc photography, red-free fundus photography (EOS-D60 digital camera; Canon, Tokyo, Japan), measurement of the peripapillary RNFL thickness, enhanced-depth imaging of the ONH using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT; SPECTRALIS; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), and standard automated perimetry (Humphrey Field Analyzer II 750 and 24-2 Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). Other ophthalmic examinations included measurements of the corneal curvature (KR-800; Topcon, Tokyo, Japan), central corneal thickness, and axial length (IOL Master 5; Carl Zeiss Meditec).

The initial IOP was defined as the IOP recorded at data enrollment (i.e. when DH was first detected), and percent IOP reduction was calculated as the difference between this initial IOP and the subsequent mean follow-up IOP. The number of added medications was defined as the difference between the numbers of IOP-lowering medications (active ingredients) being taken at the final follow-up visit and at baseline, while the frequency of medication changes was recorded, with each such change defined as occurring when an IOP-lowering medication was switched, added, or discontinued during the follow-up period.

Circumpapillary RNFL thickness was measured using Spectralis SD-OCT with a 12-degree circular scan protocol centered on the optic disc. In this protocol, the physical diameter of the scan circle is adjusted according to the eye’s axial length and corneal curvature to maintain a constant angular trajectory, thereby correcting for ocular magnification effects. To ensure precise registration and minimize measurement variability across longitudinal visits, the AutoRescan function was utilized to automatically align all subsequent scans to the baseline image. Global RNFL thickness was calculated as the average thickness of all A-scans along this circular scanning path.

Regular follow-up examinations were conducted at 6-month intervals, and included IOP measurement, slit-lamp biomicroscopy with or without stereo disc photography to assess DH recurrence, and OCT measurement of the peripapillary RNFL thickness to calculate the rate of global RNFL thinning. For patients who underwent surgery during the follow-up period, OCT measurements obtained within 6 months postoperatively were excluded from the calculation of RNFL thinning rate, as postoperative inflammation may cause transient RNFL thickening and thereby bias the results. Global RNFL thickness was selected as the primary outcome measure rather than sectoral parameters, as a fixed sectoral analysis would fail to adequately reflect the dynamic and cumulative nature of disease progression in this long-term study.

Investigations of Lamina Cribrosa Morphology and Measurement of Juxtapapillary Choroidal Thickness

Recent attention has focused on the potential role of structural and vascular substrates—particularly the lamina cribrosa (LC) and choroidal thickness (CT)—in influencing susceptibility to glaucomatous damage after DH. The LC is the principal site of glaucomatous axonal injury mediating both mechanical stress and vascular compromise within the ONH.19,20 Alterations in LC architecture may therefore amplify the vulnerability of axons following DH.5,21 Similarly, the choroid provides a critical vascular supply to the ONH and peripapillary region; reduced CT has been linked to impaired perfusion and may exacerbate ischemic stress after DH.22,23 Together, these parameters may help explain interindividual differences in disease progression and provide important prognostic markers in the long-term course of NTG after DH.

The general morphology of the LC was assessed by measuring the LC curvature and thickness on the horizontal scan images of the ONH obtained by enhanced depth-imaging (EDI) OCT (Fig. 1). The corneal curvature of each eye was entered into the Spectralis OCT system prior to scanning to avoid potential magnification errors. A 10 × 15 rectangular volume scan covering the optic disc was acquired using the EDI technique. This protocol consisted of approximately 65 sections (B-scans) separated by 30 to 34 µm, with the slice distance determined automatically by the device. Each B-scan was obtained by averaging 42 frames to maximize signal-to-noise ratio. Only images with a quality score of >15 were included in the analysis.

LCCI was calculated as the ratio of the lamina cribrosa curve depth (LCCD) to the width (W) of the anterior LC surface reference plane {(LCCD/W) × 100}. The methods to obtain the ONH images and those used to evaluate the LC morphology have been described in detail elsewhere.20,24 The LC curvature was evaluated using the LC curvature index (LCCI), defined based on the degree of inflection of the curve representing a section of the LC.25 The LCCI was determined by measuring the width of the anterior LC surface reference plane (W) and then the LC curve depth (LCCD) within the anterior LC surface plane in each B-scan, with LCCI then calculated as (LCCD/W) × 100. Because the curvature was normalized to the LC width, the LCCI is an indicator of the shape of the LC that is independent of the actual size of the ONH. Only the LC within the measured width was considered because the LC was often not clearly visible outside this region. In eyes with LC defects, the LCCI was measured using a presumed anterior LC surface that represented the best fit to the curvature of the remaining part of the LC or by excluding the area of the LC defect. The LCCI was obtained from the three selected B-scan images (midhorizontal, superior midperipheral, and inferior midperipheral regions of the ONH) to calculate the mean LCCI of the eye. The LC thickness (LCT) was assessed in the same three images that were used to measure the LCCI, and was measured as the distance between the anterior and posterior borders at the central three points (100 µm from each point) in the direction perpendicular to the anterior LC surface at the measurement point.26 The measurements made on the three images were used to calculate the mean LCT of the eye.

The manual caliper tool provided in the Heidelberg Eye Explorer software (version 1.10.4) was used to measure the LCCI and LCT. Measurements were made by two observers (authors E.J.L. and J.Y.) who were masked to clinical information. The mean values of the measurements made by these two observers were used in the main analyses.

A focal LC defect was defined as an anterior laminar surface irregularity violating the normal smooth curvilinear U- or W-shaped contour.27 To avoid false-positive results, the defects had to have a diameter of >100 µm and a depth of >30 µm to be considered defects.28 The radial B-scan images were independently reviewed by two observers (authors E.J.L. and J.Y.) who were masked to all other clinical information, and the presence of focal LC defects was determined. The B-scan locations were subsequently compared with the stereo disc photographs to ensure that any identified focal LC defects were not artifacts caused by vascular shadowing. The presence of a focal LC defect was double checked on horizontal B-scan images.

The juxtapapillary choroidal thickness (JPCT) was measured on the same B-scan images used for evaluating the LC morphology (see Fig. 1). The JPCT was evaluated as described in detail elsewhere.20 The area of the choroidal tissue within 500 µm of the border tissue of Elschnig was measured using the built-in drawing tool of the SPECTRALIS viewer software. The JPCT was obtained by dividing the measured choroidal area by 500 µm. The measurements made on the three images were used to calculate the mean JPCT of the eye. Measurements were performed by two masked observers (authors E.J.L. and J.Y.), and the mean of the measurements was used in the analyses.

Evaluation of Glaucomatous Progression Patterns

Progression was defined as a statistically significant negative slope of RNFL thinning in the regression models. Both linear and nonlinear regression patterns were evaluated to classify the nature of RNFL changes over time. Segmented linear regression and quadratic regression fitting methods were used to detect nonlinear trends in RNFL thinning. Quadratic regression was used as a descriptive tool to visualize the overall curvature of RNFL thinning and to systematically classify the trajectories into acceleration or deceleration patterns. Eyes were classified as exhibiting a “nonlinear” pattern only if the quadratic fit was statistically superior to the linear fit, a criterion adopted to minimize the risk of overfitting driven by outliers. In cases where quadratic regression provided a better fit than linear regression, the presence of a statistically significant breakpoint was confirmed using the Davies test. To ensure the robustness of the segmented regression, a minimum of four serial OCT examinations were required for each slope segment (i.e. both before and after the breakpoint). Patients with nonlinear patterns were further divided into acceleration and deceleration groups based on the sign and magnitude of the slope changes across the identified segments, and breakpoint timing.

Statistical Analyses

Differences between groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables along with post hoc tests where appropriate. The rate of RNFL thinning (µm/year) was calculated using linear regression analysis of serial OCT measurements.

Linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations between clinical parameters and the rate of RNFL thinning. Additionally, a nonlinear machine-learning approach using a random-forest regression model was used to capture complex relationships between multiple variables and RNFL thinning rates. Model training and validation were conducted using five-fold cross-validation, with hyperparameters selected via grid search, including the number of candidate features (max_features) and the minimum leaf size (min_samples_leaf). The parameters that minimized the mean absolute percentage error were selected. For regression analyses, the median absolute error was used as the optimization metric, whereas for classification analyses, accuracy was used as the optimization metric. Bootstrap aggregation was used to mitigate overfitting and assess model stability. The final model was trained using the optimal hyperparameters identified during cross-validation. The baseline RNFL thickness was excluded from the model because it was directly related to the outcome variable. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were calculated to assess the relative importance of each variable in the model. This machine-learning analysis was exploratory in nature, aimed at identifying influential factors and underlying patterns using SHAP analysis, rather than producing a validated predictive model for clinical deployment.

Logistic regression and random-forest classification analyses were also performed to identify distinguishing features of the subgroups. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Python (version 3.12; Python Software Foundation), with statistical modeling conducted using the statsmodels package (version 0.13.5). The Random Forest algorithm was implemented using the scikit-learn package (version 1.5.0).

Results

Of the 265 initially eligible eyes that met the inclusion criteria, eyes were subsequently excluded due to poor OCT image quality (n = 12) or insufficient serial data points to support the segmented regression analysis (i.e. fewer than 4 visits before or after the potential breakpoint; n = 26). Consequently, a total of 227 eyes with NTG were finally included in the study. Table 1 lists the clinical characteristics of the participants. The rate of linear RNFL thinning was −0.99 ± 0.60 µm/year (mean ± standard deviation) over the follow-up duration of 11.9 ± 1.7 years. There was excellent interobserver agreement in the measurements of the LCCI, LCT, and JPCT, with intraclass correlation coefficient (95% confidence interval) values of 0.916 (0.892–0.935), 0.952 (0.938–0.963), and 0.957 (0.945–0.967), respectively.

Assessing Risk Factors for Faster RNFL Thinning

Factors associated with the rate of global RNFL thinning were assessed using both linear and nonlinear regression models. The linear regression model showed that faster RNFL thinning was associated with a smaller IOP reduction (β = 0.033, P = 0.036), better VF mean deviation (MD; β = −0.022, P = 0.028), glaucoma filtering surgery (β = −0.330, P = 0.002), and a larger number of added IOP-lowering medications (β = −0.096, P = 0.001), but with low explanatory power (R2 = 0.177; data not presented). The nonlinear random-forest regression model provided substantially better explanatory performance (R2 = 0.445). The SHAP value exceeded 1.5 times the mean of all feature importance values (threshold = 0.032) for the number of additional IOP-lowering medications after first detecting DH, baseline VF MD, and the degree of %IOP reduction, indicating their dominant influence (Fig. 2A). The three most important factors associated with faster RNFL thinning were a larger number of additional IOP-lowering medications after first detecting DH, less-severe VF damage, and a smaller %IOP reduction, followed by larger mean LCCI, glaucoma filtering surgery, and DH recurrence (Fig. 2B).

Classification of Patterns of Progressive RNFL Thinning

Global RNFL thickness did not change significantly over time—indicating no definite signs of progression—in only 14 of the 227 eyes (P_no group). The remaining 213 eyes showed a statistically significant negative linear slope, consistent with progressive RNFL thinning. The pattern was purely linear with no significant nonlinear change in 138 of these eyes, which were classified as the P_lin group. The remaining 75 eyes exhibited a better fit with quadratic (nonlinear) regression, suggesting a nonconstant rate of RNFL loss over time. These nonlinear cases were further categorized into 2 subgroups based on the direction of curvature: 25 eyes with an accelerating pattern of RNFL thinning (P_acc group) and 50 eyes with a decelerating progression pattern (P_dec group). The estimated breakpoints at which the slope became significantly faster or slower were 5.39 ± 1.96 and 5.10 ± 2.26 years in the P_acc and P_dec subgroups, respectively. The clinical characteristics did not differ markedly between the subgroups, with statistically significant differences only found in the baseline VF MD (P = 0.050), baseline VF PSD (P = 0.024), and the rate of RNFL thinning (P < 0.001). We sought to identify the factors that could account for the differences in the pattern of RNFL thinning—particularly those that could mitigate the rate of thinning over time and lead to a stabilizing pattern—by comparing the P_dec group with the combined P_lin and P_acc groups. Although logistic regression analysis did not identify any significant factors associated with this pattern difference (data not presented), the random-forest classification model—supplemented by SHAP analysis—successfully revealed several key features distinguishing the P_dec group (5-fold cross-validation accuracy, 0.77): eyes in the P_dec group were characterized by a larger LCT, larger %IOP reduction during follow-up, larger number of added IOP-lowering medications, and younger age than those in the combined P_lin and P_acc groups (Fig. 4). All of these factors had SHAP values exceeding 1.5 times the mean feature importance threshold (0.014), indicating their strong influence in distinguishing the P_dec group. Given that the estimated breakpoint in the P_dec group was 5.10 ± 2.26 years, this group was divided into 2 subgroups comprising those who stabilized earlier (P_dec_early, n = 26) and later (P_dec_late, n = 24) than the breakpoint. The P_dec_late group had a higher initial IOP (12.2 ± 1.9 vs. 10.9 ± 2.4 mm Hg, P = 0.045), thicker baseline RNFL (85.8 ± 10.8 vs. 77.0 ± 11.6 µm, P = 0.008), and thicker final RNFL (66.6 ± 11.9 vs. 44.1 ± 21.4 µm, P = 0.001). Eyes with recurrent DH were more frequent in the P_dec_late group than in the P_dec_early group (62.5% vs. 23.1%, P = 0.005), and the number of recurrences was also higher (2.5 ± 1.7 vs. 1.3 ± 0.6, P = 0.004; Table 3). The average rate of RNFL thinning did not differ significantly between the groups, but the rate before the breakpoint was significantly faster in the P_dec_early group than in the P_dec_late group (−3.09 ± 1.65 vs. −1.57 ± 0.56 µm/year, P < 0.001; see Table 3). Sensitivity analyses using alternative temporal cutoffs (4.75 and 5.25 years) demonstrated that the principal distinguishing features were highly stable, with both comparative statistics and Random Forest models yielding consistent results across all definitions of the breakpoint (data not shown).

Discussion

This study explored the long-term clinical course of NTG eyes following DH, with a particular focus on the patterns of RNFL thinning and their predictors. Over a mean follow-up of nearly 12 years, the vast majority of eyes (93.8%) demonstrated statistically significant RNFL thinning. Notably, a considerable proportion of eyes exhibited nonlinear progression patterns, suggesting that RNFL thinning after DH does not occur in a constant manner over time.

Regarding the linear RNFL thinning slope during the follow-up period, the machine-learning-based modeling provided about 2.5 times higher predictive power than the conventional regression model (R2 = 0.445 vs. 0.176) and revealed several key factors associated with faster RNFL thinning, including a larger number of added IOP-lowering medications, a higher baseline VF MD, a smaller IOP reduction, a larger mean LCCI, DH recurrence, and glaucoma filtering surgery. Most of these observations are consistent with previous reports, such as faster progression being linked to early-stage disease,20,26,27 insufficient IOP reduction,29,30 and marked LC deformation,26,31 but some findings were less intuitive. Notably, intensifying the IOP-lowering therapy—whether medical or surgical—was not associated with slower RNFL thinning, which may seem paradoxical given the well-established role of elevated IOP in the pathogenesis of glaucoma. A plausible explanation is that treatment was intensified because the eye was already worsening, rather than vice versa. Hence, these variables are better viewed as correlates of ongoing progression rather than independent risk factors. Another explanation is that IOP-independent mechanisms may drive progression in a subset of patients, such that progressive damage continues despite adequate IOP reduction, as reported in an ocular hypertension treatment study,32 or collaborative normal tension glaucoma study.33,34 However, it is important to recognize that glaucoma progression and treatment intensity rarely follow a strictly linear or uniform course, and so relying on a single linear slope across the entire follow-up period may oversimplify the complex, nonlinear dynamics of both disease course and management.

This study found that RNFL thinning occurred not only at variable rates but also in distinct temporal patterns after DH. Nearly one-third of eyes with progressive RNFL thinning showed a nonlinear pattern, some with accelerating but most with decelerating slopes. Whereas risk factors for rapid progression have been widely studied, the decelerating group (P_dec) was of particular interest in this study. Despite initial progression, these eyes showed late-phase stabilization, raising the question of what factors might contribute to such a favorable shift.

Analyses of the Random Forest model characterized eyes in the P_dec group with a larger LCT, a larger number of additional IOP-lowering medications, younger age, and a larger IOP reduction during follow-up relative to the other progression groups. These features may reflect a more resilient ONH and more effective long-term IOP control—conditions that could lead to eventual stabilization. In contrast, the accelerating pattern (P_acc), observed in a minority of eyes, may represent a critical tipping point following the insult associated with DH. This rapid nonlinear deterioration could result from structural decompensation of a mechanically vulnerable LC. The P_dec group was further investigated by subdividing the P_dec group into the P_dec_early and P_dec_late groups based on whether the breakpoint occurred earlier or later than that of the group mean. A lower IOP at first DH detection, fewer episodes of DH recurrence, and less severe VF damage were associated with earlier stabilization.

The Early Manifest Glaucoma Trial did not find IOP-lowering treatment to be beneficial in reducing the presence or frequency of DH, and suggested that an IOP reduction alone is not sufficient to halt glaucoma progression in eyes with DH.14 In contrast, other studies have shown that further lowering of IOP can reduce the likelihood of DH occurrence35 and slow the rate of glaucoma progression.9,36 Our findings reaffirm that effective IOP control is crucial in patients with NTG with DH. The translaminar pressure gradient is expected to be smaller in eyes with a thicker LC, thereby reducing the IOP-induced mechanical stress exerted on the axons.26,37,38 In this context, a large LCT may buffer against mechanical stress. Likewise, younger age and early-stage disease probably signify more robust ONH tissues, contributing to stabilization.

At first glance, it seems contradictory that intensified IOP-lowering therapy was associated with a decelerating pattern of RNFL thinning in the P_dec group, whereas it was simultaneously linked to faster RNFL thinning for a linear slope. Notably, faster overall RNFL loss was associated with treatment intensification and a smaller IOP reduction. This suggests that the eyes in which therapy was intensified but failed to achieve adequate IOP lowering may have continued to progress rapidly. In contrast, eyes that responded well to intensified treatment probably entered a decelerating phase; however, the initial rapid thinning might have resulted in faster overall linear progression (see Fig. 4). This indicates that a single linear slope calculated over the entire follow-up period cannot distinguish between acceleration and deceleration, and underscores the nonlinear nature of glaucoma progression and the variability in treatment intensity over time, neither of which can be fully captured by conventional linear regression approaches.

It could be argued that the RNFL thickness measurement floor acts as a potential confounder for the deceleration pattern observed in the P_dec group. However, several lines of evidence suggest that the floor effect is unlikely to be the primary driver of this pattern. First, the mean final RNFL thickness in both the early (63.9 µm) and late (75.7 µm) deceleration subgroups remained substantially above the typical floor (40–50 µm) reported for Spectralis OCT,39,40 indicating that slope attenuation occurred before the tissue reached the physical measurement limit. Second, the final RNFL thickness in the P_dec group was comparable to that of the P_acc group. The final VF MD in the P_dec group was also comparable to that of other groups. Given that the P_acc group exhibited active, rapid progression at this similar thickness level, this serves as compelling evidence that the “floor” does not inherently preclude the detection of change in this range.

Nevertheless, it is crucial to emphasize that the observation of a decelerating RNFL thinning pattern should not be interpreted as a complete cessation of disease activity or an absence of future risk. In eyes with significant pre-existing damage, the structural reserve is already critically depleted. In such advanced stages, even minimal further loss—which might appear mathematically stable—can lead to profound functional deterioration. Therefore, clinicians should remain vigilant and not assume that a decelerating structural trend guarantees long-term safety, particularly in patients with severe VF loss or recurrent DHs.

Random Forest modeling allowed us to identify complex interactions among risk factors beyond that possible when using traditional regression methods, such as the combined influence of IOP, LC structure, and DH recurrence. Given that glaucoma progression probably results from multiple interdependent processes, this approach may be more clinically relevant in identifying patients at highest risk and guiding tailored treatment strategies. Specifically, these findings offer actionable guidance for the management of NTG eyes with DH—where further IOP reduction is often challenging due to the already low baseline pressure. Standard clinical practice and progression analysis software predominantly rely on linear regression models; however, our results suggest that such linear assumptions may underestimate the velocity of disease progression in structurally vulnerable eyes. Therefore, clinicians should be particularly cautious when these risk factors are present, as relying solely on linear trends may delay necessary therapeutic intensification in patients prone to rapid, nonlinear structural deterioration.

Notwithstanding the strengths of this study, it was also limited by the restricted interpretability and generalizability of the machine-learning models, because their performance depends on the specific dataset used. A second limitation was that despite using objective linear and quadratic regression methods to classify the progression patterns, these approaches may still be influenced by noise in serial RNFL measurements and between-scan variability in OCT. Third, the frequency of DH episodes may have been underestimated due to them being undetected or missed between the scheduled visits. Fourth, treatment modifications during the extended follow-up period were not analyzed in detail. Future prospective studies incorporating continuous treatment data and more-frequent DH monitoring are therefore warranted to confirm and expand upon our findings. Fifth, as this was a retrospective observational study, there was inherent heterogeneity in follow-up intervals and durations. This made it difficult to apply time-dependent modeling to perfectly account for the temporal changes in IOP and the number of medications. Thus, the IOP and medication-related variables should be interpreted with caution, as they may partly reflect the clinician's alertness to disease progression. Sixth, given the transient nature of DHs, which typically resolve within weeks to months, the 6-month follow-up interval might have led to an underestimation of the true frequency of DH episodes. Seventh, the time of OCT scanning was not considered when measuring JPCT. Although the subfoveal CT thickness shows diurnal variation,41–43 little is known about whether the JPCT is also influenced by circadian changes. Therefore, we assume that any impact of diurnal variation would be randomly distributed among the groups. Finally, the requirement for a minimum follow-up of 10 years in a tertiary referral center might have preferentially included more severe yet compliant cases while excluding rapidly progressive or lost-to-follow-up eyes, thereby limiting generalizability and potentially underestimating the most aggressive disease trajectories.

Conclusions

This study from a tertiary referral center has revealed considerable heterogeneity in the long-term clinical course of NTG eyes following DH. Whereas most eyes showed significant progression after DH, a substantial proportion exhibited a decelerating rate of RNFL thinning, which was probably associated with a thicker LC, intensification of IOP-lowering treatment, younger age, and larger IOP reduction following DH detection. Notably, earlier stabilization was more likely to occur in eyes with a lower IOP at the time when DH was first detected, absence of DH recurrence, and less-severe VF damage.
