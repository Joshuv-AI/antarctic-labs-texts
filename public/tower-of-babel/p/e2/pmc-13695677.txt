Dissecting the shared genetic architecture between testosterone traits and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder
Authors: Wen Lu, Xiaoyan He, Pu Lei, Yixin Liu, Xianyan Zhan, Qingyan Ma, Bin Yan, Xiancang Ma, Jian Yang, Yuan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder with sex differences, possibly linked to testosterone; however, the relationship remains unclear. This study aimed to clarify the genetic correlation and polygenic overlap between ADHD and testosterone traits, identify shared genomic loci, and investigate the underlying biological mechanisms through functional annotation. Genomic data on ADHD and three testosterone traits (total testosterone [TT], bioavailable testosterone [BT], and sex hormone-binding globulin [SHBG]) were obtained from publicly accessible genome-wide association studies. Employing the MiXeR bivariate causal mixture model, we quantified the polygenic overlap between ADHD and these testosterone traits. Subsequently, we applied the conjunctional false discovery rate (conjFDR) method to identify genomic loci and performed functional annotation with the Functional Mapping and Annotation tool to aid biological interpretation. Using MiXeR, we found negative correlations between TT and ADHD, and SHBG and ADHD, but a positive correlation between BT and ADHD. Over one-third of testosterone-associated variants were predicted to affect ADHD. Using the conjFDR approach, we identified 22–51 genomic loci shared between testosterone traits and ADHD, including MCM9 and MANBA. Functional enrichment analysis highlighted the predominant involvement of these mapped genes in signal transduction pathways, synapses, cell differentiation, and neurogenesis. In conclusion, we reported a substantial polygenic overlap between ADHD and testosterone traits, identified multiple shared genomic loci implicating common biological mechanisms, and highlighted the association of glutamatergic synapses and neurogenesis with ADHD and testosterone levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-026-03076-6.Keywords: Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, Testosterone, Sex hormone-binding globulin, Genetic overlap, MANBA, MCM9

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common neurodevelopmental condition that affects 2.5–5.3% of school-aged children and adolescents worldwide [1]. Its core symptoms include inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, although their manifestations and progression vary among individuals [2, 3]. The pathogenesis is still unclear. ADHD is currently considered to have a high degree of heritability, involving common and rare genetic variations [4]. The latest meta-analysis of GWASs on ADHD identified 27 genome-wide significant risk loci, implicating 76 potential risk genes with notable enrichment for expression during early brain development [5]. In general, ADHD genetic risk showed associations with multiple brain-specific neuronal sub-types, including midbrain dopaminergic neurons [5]. In neurobiology, different animal models have revealed changes of synaptic structure and function in ADHD, which may be related to hyperactive and impulsive behaviors [6–8]. Current research focuses on the dysfunction of dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems, especially those in the prefrontal cortex-striatum circuit [9, 10]. However, new evidence suggests that the glutamatergic system is impaired during development, resulting in decreased synaptic signal intensity [11].

Sex differences in ADHD prevalence are well documented, with male-to-female ratios of 4:1 in clinical studies and 2.4:1 in the general population [12, 13]. This can be explained by the fact that boys often exhibit externalizing symptoms (such as hyperactivity/impulsivity and conduct problems), while girls are more prone to internalizing symptoms (such as inattention and mood problems) [14–16]. Destructive externalizing behaviors are more likely to attract the attention of parents, teachers and doctors, but covert internalizing behaviors are often ignored [17, 18]. Thus, the current diagnostic criteria are more suitable for boys, while girls are more likely to be missed or misdiagnosed [19, 20]. Alongside these sociocultural and methodological influences, biological factors may also contribute to the observed sex differences in ADHD by shaping brain development [21–23]. Among these biological factors, sex hormones, particularly testosterone, play a critical role in mediating sex-specific brain development and maturation in humans [24–27]. Testosterone profoundly affects the brain structure and function by regulating sexually dimorphic neurodevelopment, neural progenitor cell dynamics, and neuroprotective pathways [28–30]. Excessive testosterone exposure may alter the neurodevelopmental trajectory through interactions with genetic and environmental factors, thereby increasing the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders [31–34]. The main forms of testosterone detected clinically are total testosterone (TT) and free testosterone (FT), which are used for the diagnosis of androgen-related diseases [35]. TT is the most commonly used and easily accessible indicator, including FT and testosterone combined with sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) or albumin [36]. Bioavailable testosterone (BT) refers to the testosterone part in the blood that can be utilized by tissues and exert androgen activity, including FT and testosterone that is weakly bound to albumin [37]. Evidence suggests that testosterone contributes to ADHD development. In boys with ADHD, testosterone levels show notable changes, including a substantial increase in FT and BT, and a decrease in SHBG; methylphenidate (MPH) treatment improved ADHD symptoms and partially restored testosterone levels to normal [38]. Prenatal testosterone exposure may be associated with a higher risk for ADHD in offspring, with the association appearing stronger in girls [39]. These findings suggest a possible link between testosterone and ADHD. However, the magnitude of the shared genetic components and common underlying causes remains unclear.

Advances in high-quality GWASs and statistical methods enable researchers to examine genetic correlations and polygenic enrichment patterns across traits. Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSR) is commonly employed to assess genome-wide genetic correlations between two phenotypes [40], while the bivariate causal mixture model (MiXeR) quantifies shared and unique genetic variants by analyzing effect-direction mixtures [41]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the genetic correlations and polygenic overlap between ADHD and three testosterone traits — TT, BT, and SHBG, identify common genomic loci, and investigate their potential biological functions.

Methods

Data acquisition

The flowchart of the current study is depicted in Fig. 1. Genetic data for ADHD were obtained from an extensive GWAS comprising 38,691 cases and 186,843 controls performed by the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (Table S1) [42]. To perform sex-stratified analyses, we incorporated an earlier GWAS of ADHD with 19,099 cases (26% females) and 34,194 controls (38% females), as sex-specific data were not provided in the latest large-sample GWAS [43]. Data on TT, BT, and SHBG were derived from the most recent GWAS, which included up to 425,097 participants aged 40–69 years old from the UK Biobank [44]. Sex-specific datasets were also extracted with male samples varying from 178,782 to 194,453 and female samples varying from 188,507 to 230,454 for each trait (Table S1). Genetic association analysis was adjusted for genotyping chip, baseline age, and ten genetically inferred principal components. Only the data of European participants were used. Ethical approval was obtained from the respective ethics committees for all initial GWASs, and all participants provided informed consent.

Analysis of genetic correlation and polygenic overlap

LDSR was employed to investigate genetic correlations between ADHD and testosterone traits using default settings [40]. Firstly, GWAS summary statistics for both traits were converted to standardized format and harmonized with a given single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) list to ensure the alleles in our summary statistics match those in the data used to estimate LD scores. Secondly, genetic correlations between pairwise traits were estimated with the 1000 Genomes Project European panel using the LDSR software. Since LDSR cannot fully account for mixed effect directions among shared genetic variants, we further adopted the MiXeR method to measure polygenic overlap extending beyond genetic correlations [41]. Briefly, data were first converted to standardized format as the LDSR analysis, and then a univariate Gaussian mixture model was built to identify causal variants specific to each trait with the 1000 Genomes Project European data as a reference panel. Finally, a bivariate mixture model was applied to determine the count of shared variants between pairwise traits [41]. Venn diagrams were generated to display the MiXeR results, highlighting distinct and overlapping polygenic elements between traits. Conditional Q–Q plots were created to visually depict polygenic enrichment and trait overlap. Significant cross-trait enrichment was indicated by progressive leftward deviations from the null line, with the degree of deviation corresponding to increasing association significance for the secondary phenotype.

Identification of shared genomic loci

We next employed the conditional and conjunctional false discovery rate (cond/conjFDR) approach, which uses an empirical Bayes statistical framework to enhance the discovery of SNPs associated with each trait (condFDR) and to identify SNPs jointly associated with two traits (conjFDR) [45]. To achieve this, the method first computed the condFDR value of each SNP for ADHD conditional on testosterone traits and vice versa, and then calculated the conjFDR value for each SNP as the maximum of the two condFDR values. The pleioFDR software was applied to estimate such values using the same converted format datasets with LDSR and MiXeR. We adopted a conjFDR threshold of < 0.05 to define a statistically significant SNP jointly associated with ADHD and testosterone traits. Lead SNPs were further selected based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) of r2 < 0.1 within 250 kb, using the Functional mapping and annotation (FUMA) tool and the European reference panel of the 1000 Genomes Project [46]. Novel loci were identified by cross-referencing candidate SNPs against the NHGRI-EBI Catalog of human GWASs. The extended major histocompatibility complex (chr6:25119106–33854733) was excluded to minimize the inflation effects resulting from LD patterns.

Functional annotation and gene-set enrichment analysis

Annotation, mapping, and functional assessment of the SNPs were conducted using FUMA v1.3.6a [47]. The Variant Annotation Tool was used to annotate SNPs, and their potential deleterious effects were evaluated using Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD) scores [48, 49]. Positional mapping was performed for gene annotation with the maximum distance from SNPs to genes less than 10kb [47]. MAGMA was performed to identify significant genes and gene sets. Gene based P-value was computed for protein-coding genes by mapping SNPs to genes if SNPs were located within the genes. Gene set P-value was computed using the gene-based P-value for 4,728 curated gene sets and 6,166 Gene Ontology (GO) terms obtained from MsigDB v5.2. Enriched biological pathways or gene sets in GO, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome Pathway databases were reported with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Results

Genetic correlation and polygenic overlap between ADHD and testosterone traits

LDSR analysis showed that ADHD was negatively associated with TT (rg = -0.099, P = 4.6 × 10− 5; Table S2) and SHBG (rg = -0.158, P = 1.0 × 10− 13). The genetic correlation between ADHD and BT was not significant (rg = 0.058, P = 0.071); however, MiXeR analysis suggested a positive correlation (rg = 0.108, SE < 1 × 10− 4). MiXeR showed consistent genetic correlations with ADHD, TT, and SHBG (Table S2). As shown in Fig. 2A–C, all three testosterone traits demonstrated extensive polygenic overlap with ADHD. Over 7.7k genetic variants had an effect on ADHD, reflecting its huge polygenicity. By contrast, the number of variants influencing TT (0.6k), BT (0.9k), and SHBG (0.8k) was much smaller (Table S2). Among these variants, more than 0.2k were shared between TT and ADHD, accounting for 36% of those affecting TT. BT and SHBG showed variant overlaps of 0.5k (52%) and 0.7k (80%) with ADHD, respectively. All Akaike information criterion (AIC) values were positive, suggesting all three models of MiXeR analysis were well fitted (Table S2). Conditional Q–Q plots revealed progressive leftward shifts for testosterone traits in relation to ADHD (Fig. 2D–I), indicating strong cross-trait enrichment between ADHD and testosterone traits. Sex-stratified analyses revealed consistent directions of genetic correlations between ADHD and testosterone traits across males and females, but the MiXeR analysis revealed that the genetic correlations of all three testosterone traits with ADHD were relatively higher and closer to the main results in females than in males (Table S2; Fig. S1-3).

Genomic loci shared between ADHD and testosterone traits

Leveraging cross-trait polygenic enrichments, we detected 28, 22, and 51 genomic loci jointly associated (conjFDR < 0.05) with ADHD and TT, BT, and SHBG, respectively (Fig. 3; Table S3–5). Among the top lead SNPs with conjFDR < 0.01, 7/8, 5/7, and 4/8 were novel, including the MANBA locus (Table 1). Notably, 75% of the lead SNPs in the ADHD-TT shared loci exhibited opposing effects on these traits, consistent with the negative genetic correlations observed in LDSR and MiXeR analyses. For BT and SHBG, the majority (86%) and a smaller proportion (38%) of lead SNPs, respectively, displayed effect directions aligning with MiXeR-derived genetic correlations.

Functional annotation of shared genomic loci

Gene annotation analysis indicated that a considerable proportion (67.9–86.4%) of overlapping genomic loci were located in intronic or intergenic genomic regions (Table S3–5). Certain loci overlap between ADHD and multiple testosterone traits, including MANBA, which was associated with ADHD, TT, and SHBG (Fig. S4; Table S3–5). Additionally, variants linked to ADHD and all testosterone traits were mapped to specific genes, such as MCM9, RP11-81K2.1, STAG1, and C9orf64. CADD annotation revealed several loci with high deleteriousness scores (CADD > 12.37) common to both ADHD and testosterone traits. For example, rs35917528, located in the exonic region of MCM9, was identified as a highly deleterious locus (CADD = 14.07) shared between ADHD and all three testosterone traits.

Gene-set analysis of shared genomic loci

Gene set analysis was performed using MAGMA-identified genes (Table S6–8). We reported pathways that reached nominal statistical significance at P < 0.05 in Fig. 4. Based on this exploratory and relatively loose threshold, genes shared between ADHD and TT were mainly enriched in pathways involving signal transduction and synapses, particularly glutamatergic synapses, similar to the pattern observed in SHBG. Additionally, three GO-Biological Process pathways, including regulation of cell differentiation, neurogenesis, and neuron generation, were identified as playing roles in the shared regulatory mechanisms linking BT and ADHD (Table S9–11).

Discussion

This study investigated the polygenic overlap between ADHD and testosterone levels. Using the LDSR and MiXeR, we observed significant negative genetic correlations between ADHD, TT, and SHBG. MiXeR showed a positive genetic correlation between ADHD and BT. Sex-stratified analyses revealed broadly consistent results across males and females, albeit with certain differences in some correlation coefficients. Substantial polygenic overlaps were found between ADHD and the three testosterone traits, with approximately 36–80% of variants influencing testosterone traits having an effect on ADHD. The conjFDR method identified 22–51 loci that were jointly associated with ADHD and testosterone traits. Functional annotation suggested that the common biological mechanisms are primarily involved in pathways related to synaptic signal transmission and neurogenesis.

The negative correlation between ADHD and SHBG and the positive correlation between ADHD and BT identified in this study are consistent with some previous studies. In boys with ADHD, one study reported a significant increase in BT and a decrease in SHBG [38], while another study showed that SHBG levels were negatively correlated with behavior symptoms [50]. Notably, an ongoing controversy in the literature exists regarding the association between testosterone traits and ADHD. Some researchers believe that testosterone contributes to the etiology of ADHD and have provided evidence that prenatal androgen exposure is related to a higher prevalence of ADHD [39, 51]. Evidence on the association between postnatal testosterone exposure and ADHD is limited. A study suggested that women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), a disorder characterized by hyperandrogenism, exhibit more ADHD symptoms [52]. However, other studies reached contrasting conclusions. A cross-sectional study of 148 prepubertal children with ADHD found no substantial differences in FT or SHBG levels between individuals with ADHD and controls [50]. A recent Mendelian randomization (MR) study suggested no causal association between BT and ADHD [53]. The emergence of this controversy may be influenced by various factors such as changes of testosterone levels with age [54], circadian variation in testosterone levels [55], time windows of testosterone exposure, and forms of testosterone measured. Other confounding factors, such as body mass index, medication, diseases and comorbidities, and living environment, may also contribute to this inconsistency. By leveraging the extended genetic overlap analyses, our study provides strong support for the association between testosterone traits and ADHD and offers novel insights into the pathogenesis of ADHD.

Our study found that MANBA is a shared gene locus among ADHD, TT, and SHBG. MANBA encodes β-mannosidase, a lysosomal enzyme involved in the hydrolysis of mannose residues in glycoprotein metabolism [56]. In the core structure of N-glycoprotein, N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) forms a β-1,4-mannoside bond with mannose. The cleavage by β-mannosidase contributes to glycoprotein degradation and metabolite recycling [57]. Oral administration of GlcNAc could drive the formation and repair of myelin in mice [58]. In patients with multiple sclerosis, serum GlcNAc levels negatively correlated with imaging and microstructural measures of demyelination [58]. Mutations in MANBA cause β-mannosidosis, characterized by mental retardation and hearing loss, in Central European Roma [59]. An MR study linked higher plasma MANBA levels to lower ADHD risk [60]. We infer that MANBA mutations may contribute to ADHD by affecting myelin development. Neuroimaging studies have shown changes in myelin-related indicators in specific brain regions of ADHD patients, and these changes are related to clinical symptoms [61–63]. Animal models have revealed the molecular mechanisms (such as down-regulated Dusp15 gene expression) and environmental effects (such as early adversity) of myelin changes in ADHD, and emphasized the key role of myelin plasticity in cognition and behavior [62, 64, 65]. However, no direct evidence links MANBA to testosterone levels. Glycoprotein metabolism may affect testosterone regulation. In patients with PCOS, α-1-acid glycoprotein (AGP) correlated positively with TT levels and negatively with SHBG [66]. Another study confirmed the association between sex and AGP N-glycosylation in a general population [67]. Therefore, we speculate that MANBA may affect testosterone synthesis and metabolism through its role in glycoprotein metabolism, which needs further exploration.

Additionally, we found that the MCM9 locus was associated with ADHD and all three testosterone-related traits. MCM9 encodes a protein involved in DNA replication and damage repair. It forms a complex with MCM8 to recruit downstream repair factors (such as RAD51) to the damage site. Functionally intact MCM9 is essential for effective DNA repair, and its deletion or mutation can impair this process [68]. A study found that MCM9 mutations impaired DNA repair during spermatogenesis, resulting in human Sertoli cell-only syndrome [69]. In patients with primary ovarian insufficiency, a large number of MCM9 variants were found [70, 71]. These findings highlight MCM9’s protective role in the reproductive system. Since DNA repair is involved in many biological processes, MCM9 may have a role in ADHD and testosterone levels. However, this association has not been reported, highlighting areas for future research.

Gene set enrichment analysis revealed shared biological pathways between ADHD and testosterone traits. The overlapping mechanisms involve signal transduction, synapses (especially glutamatergic synapses), cell differentiation, and neurogenesis. Signal transduction and cell differentiation are basic pathways in biological processes. Therefore, it is not surprising that they were identified as common pathways in ADHD and testosterone. In this study, we focused on the role of glutamatergic synapses and neurogenesis in ADHD and testosterone levels. Evidence of different levels suggests that glutamatergic synaptic dysfunction is a key mechanism of ADHD. In an ADHD rat model, α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor (AMPAR)-mediated excitatory synaptic transmission in the hippocampus was weakened, and immunogold labeling showed a decrease in the density of AMPAR subunits GluA1 and GluA2/3, which might be related to the attention deficit of ADHD [72]. A clinical study found that glutamate levels in the anterior cingulate cortex of ADHD patients increased and were positively associated with hyperactive and impulsive symptoms of ADHD [73]. A systematic review showed that in children with ADHD, glutamate levels in fronto-cerebellar circuit and amygdala were significantly reduced after MPH treatment, which suggested that the efficacy of MPH in children was partially related to the regulation of glutamatergic neurotransmission in specific brain regions [74]. The evidence for associations between testosterone and synapses mainly comes from animal models, especially those of aging or specific diseases [75, 76]. Testosterone might exert neuroprotective effects by increasing synaptic density, promoting dendritic spine transformation, and regulating synaptic protein expression, which is partly related to androgen receptor-dependent signaling pathways [75–81]. However, the role of testosterone in the glutamatergic system may be limited and needs to be confirmed in future studies [82]. It is necessary to study the effect of testosterone on synaptic function in juvenile or adult animals, as well as the relationship between this effect and ADHD.

Animal models have also provided evidence of neurogenesis defects in ADHD. In the spontaneously hypertensive rat model, the neurogenesis process was disturbed, manifested as decreased proliferation or delayed migration of neuronal progenitors, which was associated with hyperactivity and impulsivity [83, 84]. Environmental factors may induce dysregulation of neurogenesis-related gene expression, which in turn affects the proliferation of glial cells and neurons, leading to abnormal brain development [85, 86].Studies also show that testosterone can modulate neurogenesis by increasing the proliferation and survival of neural progenitor cells (especially excitatory neuronal progenitors) and regulating the HDAC/mTOR signaling pathway, which can partially explain sex differences in brain development and some neurodevelopmental disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder [30, 33, 87, 88]. Therefore, we hypothesize that testosterone may be involved in the pathogenesis of ADHD by regulating neurogenesis, which can be a direction for future research.

This study has a few limitations. First, we presented the LDSR and pathway enrichment results using uncorrected nominal P values at the threshold of 0.05 rather than a stricter threshold for multiple comparisons. Accordingly, the reported findings should be interpreted with caution and replicated in larger datasets. Second, we performed sex-stratified analyses which to some extent supported our conclusions. However, we were unable to further discuss the differences between males and females because the sex-stratified data were derived from an additional small sample study, and the results might not be entirely reliable. Third, the variants identified in the discovery GWASs explain only a modest proportion of the total phenotypic variance for each trait, suggesting that a substantial fraction of genetic risk remains undetected. Also, sex chromosomes were excluded from the analysis, resulting in a certain degree of information loss. Fourth, the neuroendocrine environment is dynamic across life stages [54], but in our study, all testosterone traits were measured in adults, which might have a certain impact on the results. Therefore, replication in independent cohorts is essential to validate our findings. Finally, the newly identified genetic markers and pathways require additional research to assess their relevance in understanding the etiology of ADHD and developing therapeutic interventions.

In conclusion, this study found significant negative genetic correlations between TT, SHBG, and ADHD, and a positive genetic correlation between BT and ADHD. These results reveal extensive genetic overlap, with many testosterone-associated variants predicted to influence ADHD. Functional annotation suggests that the genetic link between testosterone and ADHD may involve biological processes related to synaptic function and neurogenesis, which provides a mechanistic hypothesis for future research. Our study provides new insights into the pathogenesis of ADHD and potential targets for its treatment.
