Human Pluripotent Stem Cell-Derived Macrophages Modify Development of Human Kidney Organoids: A Preliminary Study
Authors: Filipa M. Lopes, Ioannis Bantounas, Alexandra Sarov, Adrian S. Woolf, Susan J. Kimber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHuman foetal kidneys contain macrophages, innate immune cells postulated to enhance their development. Macrophages have also been implicated in the pathobiology of human kidney malformations and Wilms tumour. Human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived kidney organoids contain nephrons but lack macrophages.MethodsWe hypothesised that combining hPSC-derived macrophages with hPSC-derived kidney precursors would modify nephrogenesis. Macrophages were added in numbers of 1%, 5%, or 20% relative to a constant number of nephrogenic cells.ResultsAs assessed by CD68 immunostaining, no macrophages were detected in organoids derived from MAN13 hPSCs without added hPSC-derived macrophages. Composite organoids contained macrophages between tubules, mimicking native human foetal kidneys. Quantification of macrophages in histology sections positively correlated with numbers of macrophages added. Assessed by CD31/PECAM-1 and CD68 co-immunostaining, macrophages were scattered through organoids, with some near endothelia. Added macrophages, however, did not alter the extent of CD31/PECAM-1 immunostaining. Macrophages added at 5% were associated with increases in the percentage cross-sectional areas occupied by immunostaining for the glomerular markers synaptopodin and nephrin. Higher numbers (20%) of macrophages disrupted organoid growth.ConclusionThis preliminary study demonstrates that it is technically feasible to populate hPSC-derived nephron organoids with hPSC-derived maturing macrophages. The study also suggests that, depending on their quantity, added macrophages may have beneficial or harmful effects on nephron organoids. These insights support the proposition that macrophages play roles in normal and abnormal development of human kidneys. Further studies are needed to discover the molecular mechanisms of these effects and to replicate findings using other hPSC lines.

Introduction

The human kidney is essential for life, excreting waste products by filtration of blood in glomeruli followed by the modification of ultrafiltrate by tubules. Severe kidney failure, requiring dialysis or transplantation, affects around five million people globally [1]. Congenital defects of the kidney and urinary tract are among the most prevalent types of human malformations [2], and they are the commonest cause of severe kidney failure in young children [3]. Furthermore, Wilms tumour, or nephroblastoma, is the commonest solid organ malignancy in children and it arises through abnormal kidney development [4].

The embryonic metanephros is the precursor of the mature mammalian kidney [5, 6], and in humans it arises at 5 weeks of gestation [7]. Its two epithelial lineages are derived from intermediate mesoderm. The metanephric mesenchyme differentiates into nephron epithelia comprising glomerular podocytes and Bowman capsule, and proximal and distal tubules, while the ureteric bud undergoes serial branching to form collecting ducts, each fusing with a distal tubule. Layers of nephrons are generated between 7 and 34 weeks of human gestation [7]. Native mammalian kidneys also contain vessels [8, 9]. Blood endothelia are derived from a combination of angiogenic invasion from outside the metanephros combined with in situ vasculogenesis from metanephric mesenchyme [8]. Blood vessels populate interstitial spaces between metanephric tubules and invade tufts of glomerular podocytes to form capillary loops, essential to supply blood for filtration [7, 8, 10].

Tissue resident macrophages promote development and function of a range of organs [11]. Developing murine metanephric kidneys contain macrophages [12–15]. In animal experimental models, various roles have been attributed to macrophages including clearance of apoptotic cells [12, 13] and enhancing vessel morphogenesis [15]. Developing human kidneys also contain macrophages [16–19], but their possible roles there are unclear, in large part because of the challenges of undertaking functional studies on human foetal tissues.

Organoids derived from pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) are being used to model normal human kidney development [20–24] and early onset genetic diseases [25, 26]. Human PSC (hPSC)-derived nephron organoids are rich in glomerular podocytes and tubules, reflecting their in vitro derivation from intermediate mesoderm-like precursors, as occurs during native embryogenesis [27, 28]. These organoids also contain endothelia between tubules [24], but during ex vivo organoid culture they rarely invade glomeruli to form capillary loops, as will occur once implanted in vivo [22, 29]. hPSC-derived nephron organoids do not contain macrophages or express sets of genes that characterise these innate immune cells [23, 26, 30]. Indeed, the lack of macrophages is expected given that during normal development kidney macrophages originate from haematopoietic precursor cells residing within yolk sac and liver [31, 32], lineages not generated in nephron organoid culture.

The generation of macrophages from hPSCs has been described [33]. We hypothesised that combining hPSC-derived maturing macrophages with hPSC-derived nephron precursors would modify kidney development in organoid cultures. Here, we explored the possible effects of adding different numbers of macrophages harvested at earlier or later stages of their in vitro maturation to hPSC-nephron organoids.

Methods

Human Tissues

Foetal kidneys were fixed, processed, and paraffin sections prepared as described [24, 34].

Human PSCs

MAN13, a human embryonic SC line that we have extensively used to generate nephron organoids [22, 24, 26], was grown on 6-well plates coated with 5 mg/mL recombinant human vitronectin (Life Technologies, A14700) in TeSR1 (STEMCELL Technologies, 85850). Cells were passaged with 0.5 mm EDTA solution (pH 8) (Invitrogen, 15575-038) and replated in TeSR1 containing RevitaCell Supplement (×100; Thermo Fisher, A2644501) for 24 h.

Lentiviral Reporter Gene Transduction

MAN13 PSCs used to generate macrophages were transduced with a reporter gene to facilitate their tracking within living organoids. In previous lentiviral transduction experiments using this hPSC line, the elongation factor 1 α (EF1α) promoter was found to drive strong and widespread expression of reporter genes and luciferase throughout the nephron organoid differentiation protocol [22, 24]. Here, lentiviral vectors transducing the enhanced green fluorescence protein (EGFP) reporter gene driven by the EF1α promoter were generated as follows. HEK-293T cells in 15 cm dishes at 50% confluency were transfected by calcium phosphate precipitation with 10 μg pRRL.sin.cppt.EF1α-EGFP-WPRE [22], 10 μg pMDLg-pRRE, 3.4 μg pMD2.G, and 2 μg pRSV-Rev per dish. Cell media was collected over 2 days and centrifuged at 6,000 g overnight, the pellet resuspended in PBS, and this suspension centrifuged in a SW40-Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter Ltd, High Wycombe, UK) at 50,000 g for 90 min. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, at 1:2,000 of the original total volume of cell media that had been collected. The viral vector titre was calculated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) detecting EGFP fluorescence in HEK-293T cells transduced with serial dilutions of the vector, then determining the percentage of EGFP-positive cells. When MAN13 hPSCs were transduced at a multiplicity of infection of 5, EGFP expression approached 100%, so no cloning of cells was undertaken for this study.

Production of Macrophages from hPSCs

The method was adapted from a published protocol [33]. One well in a six-well plate of confluent MAN13 cells was fed with fresh TeSR1 medium supplemented with 50 ng/mL BMP4 (R&D, 314-BP-010), 50 ng/mL VEGF (R&D, 293-VE-010), and 20 ng/mL SCF (Thermo Fisher, PHC2115). Cells were passaged using the StemPro EZPassage tool (Thermo Fisher, 23181010), and embryoid bodies (EBs) were generated in suspension plates (Grenier, 657970) for 4 days, supplemented with cytokines on day two. EBs were collected and transferred to six-well plates (10-15 EBs per well) coated with 0.1% w/v porcine gelatin (Sigma, G9136). EBs were fed with X-VIVO15 (Lonza, BE02-060F) medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL CSF1 (BioLegend, 574806), 25 ng/ml1 IL-3 (PeproTech, 200-03-10), 2 mm Glutamax (Gibco, 35050061), 0.05 mm β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, 31350-010), and this medium was replaced every 3 to 4 days. After 2 to 3 weeks, EBs started to shed non-adherent haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) into suspension. These cells were harvested from the supernatant and plated into six-well plates in X-VIVO15 media supplemented with 100 ng/mL CSF1 (BioLegend, 574806), 2 mm Glutamax (Gibco, catalogue number 35050061), and maintained for up to 11 days during which period they underwent macrophage differentiation.

Differentiation of PSCs into Human Nephron Organoids

PSCs were induced to form organoids over 25 days with a 7-day 2D phase followed by an 18-day 3D phase, as described [22, 24, 26]. MAN13 cells were plated into a well of a six-well plate coated with 5 μg/mL recombinant human Vitronectin (Life Technologies) and fed with TeSR1 (STEMCELL Technologies, 85850) medium supplemented with RevitaCell Supplement (×100; Thermo Fisher, A2644501). Differentiation was initiated the following day by replacing this with STEMdiff APEL2 (STEMCELL Technologies, 05270) supplemented with 1% (v/v) PFHM-II Protein-Free Hybridoma Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11370882), containing 8 μm CHIR-99021 (Tocris, 4423). APEL2 with PFMH-II is hereafter referred to as “basal media.” On day 4 of the 2D protocol, cells were fed with basal medium plus 200 ng/mL FGF-9 (PeproTech, 100-23) and 1 μg/mL heparin (Sigma-Aldrich, H3149-25KU). Medium was replenished every day until day 7 of the 2D phase when cells were dissociated into a single-cell suspension by TrypLE (Gibco, 12604-021) for 1–2 min at 37°C followed by centrifugation at 400 g and then they were resuspended in basal medium. The suspension was aliquoted as 2 × 105 cells in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 6,000–7,000 rpm for 2 min. Pellets, comprising nephron organoid precursor cells, were transferred onto Millicell culture inserts (0.4 μm pore size; Millipore, PICM03050), with three organoid pellets placed on each insert, and they were pulsed with basal medium plus 5 μm CHIR-99021 for 1 h. Next, the medium was replaced with APEL2 supplemented with 200 ng/mL FGF9 and 1 μg/mL heparin (Sigma-Aldrich, H3149). From this point onwards, medium was replaced every 2 days. On day 12 of the 3D organoid phase, basal medium without additives was added until day 18, the end of the culture period.

Incorporation of hPSC-Derived Macrophages into Kidney Organoids

hPSC-derived macrophages were harvested at days 6 or 9 of the final stage of their differentiation protocol, and they were mixed with 2 × 105 nephron precursor cells from day 7 of the 2D differentiation phase. Most of the experiments used day 6 or day 9 macrophage cells that we also designate as “earlier” or “later” stage maturing macrophages. Macrophages were added at one of three cell numbers: 2,000, 10,000, or 40,000 cells, hereafter simply called “1%,” “5%,” or “20%,” in comparison with the constant number of nephron precursor cells used to generate each organoid. EGFP expression was visualised under an inverted epifluorescence microscope (Leica) in composite organoids over the next 18 days in 3D culture.

Immunocytochemistry

PSCs, EBs, and maturating macrophages were fixed with 4% PFA flowed by PBS washes. Blocking was undertaken with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich, 9048-46-8) with 10% serum from the species in which the secondary antibody was raised. Primary antibodies (Table 1) were incubated overnight at 4°C. Fluorescent secondary antibodies were incubated during 45–60 min at room temperature followed by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) nuclear staining and PBS washes. Images were acquired on an Olympus IX-71 inverted fluorescence microscope and captured using a QImaging Retiga-SRV camera [26].

Flow Cytometry

For FACS, cells were harvested and, to prevent non-specific binding, were blocked in 5% foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher, 10500064) for 30 min. Following the blocking step, cells were washed with PBS containing 2% FBS followed by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Primary antibodies were diluted in wash buffer (PBS + 2% FBS) and incubated for 30 min. Following primary incubation, samples were topped up with wash buffer and centrifuged, and the supernatant was removed. Secondary antibodies were added at a 1:2,500 dilution and incubated for 30 min on ice. Cells were washed again with wash buffer and centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. DAPI was incubated for 3 min followed by another wash. The final cell pellets were resuspended in 400 µL of wash buffer and briefly vortexed prior to FACS acquisition. Flow cytometric acquisition was performed using FACSDiva software, recording a minimum of 10,000 events per sample. Analysis was done using FlowJo v10.10.1.

Immunostaining of Histology Sections of Organoids and Native Human Foetal Kidneys

Organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 µm. Organoid and native human kidney sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, and slides were boiled in a microwave in 10 mm sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Sigma-Aldrich, C9999). After cooling to room temperature, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 0.3% hydrogen peroxide (Sigma-Aldrich, H1009) in PBS for 10 min. Sections were permeabilised using 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min and blocked using 1% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich 05470) with 10% serum from the species in which the secondary antibody was raised. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody diluted in 1% BSA in PBS. Primary antibodies were omitted for negative controls. Slides were incubated with biotin-conjugated species-specific secondary antibodies with 1% BSA at room temperature for 2 h. Following PBS washes, they were incubated in avidin-biotin enzyme complex (Vector Laboratories, VECTASTAIN Elite ABC Reagent, PK-6100) for 1 h at room temperature. Peroxidase activity was detected with SIGMAFAST 3,3 diaminobenzidine peroxidase substrate solution (Sigma-Aldrich, D4293), and sections were counterstained with haematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich, HH516). Sections were dehydrated and mounted with DPX mounting medium (Fisher Chemical, D/5330/05). Brightfield images were acquired on a 3D Histech Pannoramic 250 microscope slide scanner using a ×20 objective (Zeiss), and selected images were captured using the Case Viewer software (3D Histech). For immunofluorescence on paraffin sections, tissues were dewaxed and rehydrated as described above. Blocking was undertaken with 1% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, 9048-46-8) and 10% serum from the species in which the secondary antibody was raised. Primary and Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies are listed in Table 1. To block autofluorescence, Vector® TrueVIEW® kit (Vector, SP-8500) was used following user guide recommendations. Nuclei were stained with DAPI and mounted with mounting medium from the same kit.

Quantification of Cells and Tissues within Organoids

To quantify cell types within organoids, histology sections through the mid-transverse diameter of the organoid were analysed. To quantify numbers of macrophages, discrete cells which immunostained for CD68 were counted across the whole area. To quantify the “mass” of glomerular tissues, histology sections were immunostained for synaptopodin or nephrin and the proportion of the whole cross-sectional area occupied by stained tissues was calculated. To quantify endothelia, histology sections were immunostained for CD31/platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1), and the proportion of the total area occupied by positive staining was calculated. Similar analyses were undertaken for collagen-1. In all these analyses, immunostained areas were measured using ImageJ and expressed as a proportion of the total cross-sectional area. To estimate the proportions of glomeruli containing CD31/PECAM-1 immunostaining, we randomly selected 20 glomeruli per organoid taken from each of the four quadrants of their mid-transverse diameters. A glomerulus was designated as positive for vessels if it contained at least three CD31/PECAM-1 positive isolated cells, or an overt blood vessel, or both features. Statistical analyses were undertaken using GraphPad Prism 10.2.2. Results were expressed as mean ± SEMs for parametric data, or medians and interquartile ranges for non-parametric data sets. Parametric data sets were analysed using one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons followed by post hoc t tests. Non-parametric data were analysed by Kruskal-Wallis tests followed by Mann-Whitney U tests.

Results

Differentiating MAN13 hPSCs to Macrophages

hPSCs were transduced with lentiviral vector carrying a GFP expression construct. To differentiate them to macrophages, cells were fed with a series of different media containing specific growth factors (Fig. 1a), using a published protocol [33]. hPSCs were differentiated through an EB stage during which HSCs emerged in suspension. These shed cells were harvested and subsequently matured as adherent cells over a further 9 to 11 days in vitro to generate macrophages. Figure 1b shows phase contrast images of this sequence, and Figure 1c depicts the sequence visualised under fluorescence showing extensive GFP expression. Cells were investigated using immunocytochemistry, with antibodies against HSC and macrophage markers (Fig. 2) [16, 17, 33, 35]. PSCs were negative for macrophage markers. Throughout the final 11 days of the protocol, the majority of cells appeared positive for the HSC marker CD45; for CD74 and CD81, markers of resident kidney macrophages [35]; and for CD68, as found in human foetal kidneys [16, 17]. At the end of the protocol, they were also positive for 25f9. No significant background signal was detected in these immunohistochemistry experiments (online suppl. Fig. 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552689). FACS analyses, performed only on the final day, suggested that 51–89% of cells were positive for the various macrophage markers (online suppl. Fig. 2).

Detection of Macrophages within Nephron Organoids

MAN13 PSCs were differentiated into organoids over a 25-day protocol (Fig. 3a). Nephron precursor cells were generated over 7 days in 2D culture (Fig. 3b), and they were dissociated and pelleted onto tissue culture inserts. Over the next 18 days, each pellet was differentiated from an amorphous cell aggregate to a structure with internal patterning. In organoids where nephron precursor cells were mixed with macrophages, these appearances were grossly preserved, as assessed by phase contrast microscopy (Fig. 3c–e). Using fluorescence microscopy of these composite living organoids (Fig. 3f–h), positive signals for the EGFP reporter were detected over the whole of the 18-day 3D culture, suggesting that viable macrophages were present throughout this period. As nephrogenesis proceeds within organoids, the tissue becomes thicker so that visualisation of macrophages within cultures becomes technically limited. As expected, live imaging of nephron organoids grown without added macrophages showed no significant fluorescence (online suppl. Fig. 3).

However, in histology sections of organoids supplemented with macrophages, we detected no fluorescence in the GFP emission wavelength (online suppl. Fig. 4). Thus, the EGFP fluorescence was not maintained through processing involving paraformaldehyde fixation, dehydration in ethanol, and paraffin-embedding. A similar effect has been attributed to denaturation of the fluorescent protein by ethanol [36]. Accordingly, we elected to use CD68 immunostaining to detect macrophages in histology sections. Indeed, we identified macrophages using CD68 immunostaining within tissue sections of organoids harvested at the end of the 3D culture period (Fig. 4). In sections of human foetal kidneys (Fig. 4a), macrophages were detected in the stromal/interstitial compartment between tubules and glomeruli. Nephron organoids also contained tubules and glomeruli (Fig. 4b, c). In those generated without added hPSC-derived macrophages, no CD68-positive cells were detected (Fig. 4b), whereas macrophages were detected in between tubules and around glomeruli within composite organoids (Fig. 4c). Next, we quantified macrophages within organoids at the end of the culture period, comparing numbers when 1%, 5%, or 20% of macrophages were incorporated from earlier (day 6) to later (day 9) in the final phase of the in vitro differentiation protocol. There was a positive correlation of numbers between macrophages counted within organoid sections and numbers added (Fig. 4d, e).

Analyses of Endothelia within Organoids

Histology sections of composite organoids were co-immunostained for CD31/PECAM-1 and CD68. Endothelial cells were detected either as individual cells or in apparent vessel-like patterns. Macrophages were detected and scattered across the histology section, sometimes in proximity to endothelial cells (Fig. 5a). In brightfield imaging, CD31/PECAM-1-positive cells, sometimes taking the form of a vessel-like structure with an apparent lumen, were prominent in interstitial spaces of nephron organoids generated either without (Fig. 5b) or with (Fig. 5c) added macrophages. We then quantified endothelia in organoids using two approaches. Firstly, the proportional area occupied by endothelia in cross sections of organoids was measured. In organoids without added macrophages, around 1% of the total area was occupied by endothelia and this value did not change significantly in organoids generated with 1%, 5%, or 20% day 6 (early stage) macrophages (Fig. 5d). In similar experiments where day 9 (later stage) macrophages were used, there appeared to be a concentration-dependent effect, with 1% added macrophages reducing the total endothelial area compared with 5% added macrophages (Fig. 5e). Secondly, we assessed whether glomeruli contained endothelia. In all conditions studied, most glomeruli imaged were negative (Fig. 5f) but rare glomerular tufts contained CD31/PECAM-1-positive cells manifesting as vessel-like structures or as isolated positive cells (Fig. 5g). Quantification showed no significant change in proportions of positive glomeruli in organoids with added macrophages (Fig. 5h, i). Although a trend to increased numbers was noted when early-stage macrophages were added, this did not attain statistical significance.

Analyses of Glomeruli within Organoids

Next, we assessed the “mass” of glomeruli by quantifying the percentage area of tissue sections occupied by cells immunostained for synaptopodin (Fig. 6a–f). Low-power views show that synaptopodin-positive clusters of developing glomeruli are limited to the periphery of the organoid. Podocyte tufts were positive in both organoids generated without or with added macrophages. In addition, the Bowman capsule was also positive for synaptopodin, as reported in native infant human kidneys, with loss of Bowman capsule staining only occurring later in childhood [37]. Indeed, it is known that glomeruli forming within hPSC-derived nephron organoids are immature [22]. In experiments where early-stage (day 6) macrophages were added, there was a statistically significant increase in glomerular area, by approximately three-fold, when nephrons were generated with 5% or 20% added macrophages. By contrast, when organoids were generated with more mature (day 9) macrophages, 1% added macrophages were found to have reduced the glomerular area compared with 5% added macrophages. Immunostaining for nephrin, another glomerular marker, was then undertaken to compare organoids generated without added macrophages with those grown with 5% added macrophages (Fig. 6g–j). Adding macrophages was associated with an increased proportion of histology area occupied by nephrin immunostaining.

High Numbers of Macrophages Inhibit Organoid Growth

Transverse paraffin sections taken at the mid-height of each organoid were imaged (Fig. 7a, b), and their areas quantified (Fig. 7c, d). In experiments where 1% or 5% day 6 or day 9 maturing macrophages were added to nephron precursors, the final organoid areas were not significantly different from organoids generated without macrophages. In contrast, using 20% of either earlier or later maturing macrophages, organoid sizes were significantly less than without macrophages. Moreover, addition of 20% day 9 maturing macrophages was associated with disruption of tissue morphology within the medulla of organoids manifesting as prominent stromal-like and immature blastema-like cells (Fig. 7e–j). Such atypical tissue was not seen in organoids generated with lower numbers of either early or late stage maturing macrophages. The organoids with 20% macrophages appeared to immunostain prominently for collagen 1, a fibrosis marker (online suppl. Fig. 5).

Discussion

Macrophages have been detected in the healthy developing human metanephric kidney [13, 19], but their possible roles are unknown. To test the hypothesis that macrophages modulate human kidney development, we harnessed hPSC technology, combining hPSC-derived macrophages with hPSC-derived kidney precursors. The current experiments demonstrate that it is technically feasible to populate hPSC-derived nephron organoids with hPSC-derived maturing macrophages. This platform provided the opportunity to explore the possible effects of macrophages on early human kidney development in a controlled in vitro setting. At the end of the 18-day 3D culture period, macrophages, assessed by CD68 immunostaining, were not detected in organoids generated without added macrophages, supporting RNA sequencing analyses of hPSC-derived kidney organoids from several laboratories [23, 26, 30, 38]. In contrast, organoids in which hPSC-derived macrophages had been added contained CD68-positive cells in interstitial spaces, as did human foetal kidneys. These observations are consistent with the fact that nephron organoids are generated via intermediate mesoderm-like precursor cells, as occurs in vivo [28], whereas in vivo macrophages populate the metanephros after being generated by other, distant haematopoietic cells [31, 32]. Quantification of macrophages within histology sections at the end of the organoid culture period showed a positive correlation with the number of macrophages added. This, together with the detection of EGFP-expressing cells within living composite organoids, confirmed that added macrophages survive in the organoid environment and thus the robustness of our composite nephron/macrophage organoid model.

The proximity of macrophages to endothelia in organoids from the current experiments resembled the pattern reported in the mouse metanephric kidney studies [15]. This, together with murine observations that macrophages enhance vessel patterning in the murine metanephric explants [15], prompted us to explore possible vascular effects of adding macrophages in composite human nephron organoids. Added macrophages, however, neither increased overall CD31/PECAM-1-positive endothelial area nor endothelial invasion into glomeruli, which generally remained avascular. While our results do not exclude more nuanced effects on vessels within organoids, such as modifying vascular anastomoses [15] or specific patterns of vessels, our observation shows that macrophages are not advantageous for the generation and/or maintenance of vessels within interstitial spaces of human nephron organoids. In vivo, the formation of capillary loops within glomeruli is driven by an angiogenic growth factor gradient originating in glomerular endothelia [10]. In nephron organoid culture, this gradient appears absent or insufficient [21, 22, 39], although it can be restored in human glomeruli that continue to differentiate after subcutaneous implantation of hPSC-derived kidney progenitors into SCID-Beige mice [22]. After implantation, developing kidney organoids become perfused by the host’s blood supply and the human glomeruli acquire capillaries [22], also observed after implantation of organoids under the kidney capsule [29]. Nephron organoids subjected to flow in culture also show enhanced vascularisation [40], and in future this system could be used to study the effects of added macrophages. In other contexts, macrophages have been implicated in enhancing lymphatic differentiation, either by virtue of secreting growth factors or through macrophage/lymphatic transdifferentiation [41], with both cell types expressing the hyaluronan receptor 1, LYVE-1 [9]. In vivo, foetal kidneys contain lymphatic vessels [9], and it remains possible that resident macrophages are required for their differentiation.

We found that the addition of maturing macrophages at a dose of 5% was associated with an increase in the proportional area occupied by glomeruli, as assessed by immunostaining for synaptopodin and nephrin. This suggests that macrophages may favour glomerular maturation. In future, a comprehensive assessment of molecular maturation of glomerular and tubular cell populations in the presence of added macrophages could be undertaken by single-cell RNA sequencing [26]. While the mechanism underlying this effect requires further study, one likely possibility is that macrophages release growth factors that either directly or indirectly enhance glomerular growth. A previous study reported that addition of CSF-1, a macrophage trophic factor, enhanced the growth of rodent metanephric explants, increasing nephron numbers [14]. While the mechanism was unclear, it was postulated to be linked to an observed upregulation of the gene triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 [14]. Conversely, in another study of explanted rodent foetal kidneys, addition of tumour necrosis factor α, an inflammatory cytokine, increased macrophage numbers and impaired nephron formation [13].

We found that the size of organoids, as measured by the mid-section area, was not changed by the addition of low (1%) or medium (5%) numbers of added macrophages. In contrast, the addition of a high number (20%) of macrophages, either from earlier or later in their in vitro differentiation from hPSCs, significantly reduced the area of organoids compared to those without added macrophages. Importantly, in all conditions, the starting number of nephron precursor cells was the same. While the numbers of macrophages used here cannot easily be equated with numbers resident in native metanephric kidneys, likely to be less than a few percent of all cells, the results suggest that macrophages may have harmful effects on growth when present in high numbers. This was emphasised by the additional observation that adding 20% of day 9 maturing macrophages was also associated with disruption in the centre of the organoid, with prominent stromal-like and immature blastema-like cells that were not apparent in other conditions. These aberrant cells at least superficially resemble the immature and disorganised tissues found in certain diseases of human kidney development. This includes causing dysplastic kidney malformations [16], where kidney contains incompletely differentiated tubules. Moreover, malformed human kidneys contain foci of macrophages and T lymphocytes, and tumour necrosis factor α is detected in the urine of human foetuses with malformed kidneys [16]. It is now appreciated that some cases of dysplastic kidneys are caused by mutations of genes involved in nephrogenesis, and aspects of the in vivo phenotype can be modelled in nephron organoids generated from mutant hPSCs [26]. In future, it may be informative to assess whether addition of hPSC-derived macrophages modifies the abnormal phenotype in such mutant organoids. Macrophages are also prominent in tissue sections of Wilms tumour or nephroblastoma [42–44]. Here, M2 macrophages have been associated with a poor prognosis, partly through enhancing epithelial to mesenchymal transition of tumour cells, a prelude to metastasis [44].

The current experiments can be compared with other studies that considered potential interactions of immune cells and kidney organoids. One such reported the indirect co-culture of human kidney organoids with human peripheral blood monocytes or PSC-derived macrophages [45]. Native monocytes or the PSC-derived macrophages each decreased CHIR99021-induced apoptosis in kidney precursors at the initiation of the 3D organoid protocol [45]. Moreover, native monocyte co-cultures promoted expression of transcripts associated with nephron differentiation, including that encoding the glomerular marker, nephrin [45]. These observations complement our current work in which hPSC-derived macrophages incorporated into nephron organoids enhanced the percentage glomerular area. In another study [46], co-culture of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells with hPSC-derived kidney organoids induced fibrosis within the composite tissues. In this experiment, however, a variety of native inflammatory cell types were employed, rather than hPSC-derived early differentiating macrophages as described in the current study.

One important caveat to our study is that, as assessed by FACS analyses, most but not all the added cells expressed macrophage immunocytochemical markers. In future studies, the hPSC-derived macrophages could first be sorted to 100% purity, and the effects of adding a purer macrophage population might be more marked. In general, our results show that addition of macrophages from either day 6 or day 9 in differentiation produced rather similar effects including the numbers of macrophages in organoids; the lack of effect on endothelia; the increase in glomerular area assessed by nephrin staining; and the inhibition of organoid growth when added in high numbers. This may reflect the fact that several key macrophage markers are expressed, as assessed by immunocytochemistry, throughout the final phase of the differentiation protocol.

In summary, this preliminary study suggests that, depending on their quantity, macrophages have beneficial or harmful effects on organoids. This supports the proposition that macrophages play roles in normal and abnormal development of human kidneys. Further studies are needed to discover the molecular mechanisms behind these effects and to replicate findings using other hPSC lines.
