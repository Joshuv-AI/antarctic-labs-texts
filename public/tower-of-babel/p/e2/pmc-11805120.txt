Role of Cone-Beam Computed Tomography in Assessment of Lateral Wall Thickness of Maxillary Sinus in Posterior Edentulous Maxilla
Authors: Debanti Giri, F. Lalrawngbawli, Tapan K. Giri, Debabrata Biswas, Saurav Banerjee, Tridib N. Banerjee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:To use Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) to evaluate the lateral wall thickness (LWT) of the maxillary sinus in the posterior edentulous maxilla, as well as the effect of ridge height (RH), edentulous region, type of edentulism (Complete and partial), age, and gender, for the placement of dental implants.Materials and Methods:To conduct this cross-sectional study, A CBCT scan of 60 patients of different age groups was taken for analysis. Each parameter was evaluated at the edentulous sites in the premolar and molar regions due to missing one or more posterior teeth at four levels from sinus floor 3, 7, 10, and 15 mm with residual RH less than 10 mm. Data were analyzed using Friedman’s ANOVA and one-way ANOVA. Bootstrapped linear regression was performed to assess the influence of various variables on LWT.Results:LWT increased from 3 mm to 15 mm in all teeth except the first premolar region, with significant differences (P < 0.01). LWT was generally lower in premolars and higher in molars. Age, gender, and RH did not predict LWT (P > 0.05), However, the type of edentulism significantly predicted LWT. (P = 0.005).Conclusion:CBCT scans are recommended before sinus surgeries due to anatomical variations.

INTRODUCTION

In the field of implant dentistry, addressing the challenges presented by the posterior edentulous region in the upper jaw requires specialized techniques. These include only grafting to widen the bone, sinus grafts to increase bone height and modified surgical approaches to accommodate lower bone density areas.[1] Over time, maxillary sinus grafting has gained popularity for its effectiveness in addressing reduced vertical bone height. The maxillary posterior ridge, unlike the atrophic mandible, tends to narrow and shift toward the palate with bone loss. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) has become a valuable tool, offering detailed 3D images with lower radiation exposure, and aiding in presurgical assessment, especially in evaluating sinus dimensions.[2] The study in question aimed to investigate the lateral wall thickness of the maxillary sinus in the posterior upper jaw, considering factors such as ridge height, type of edentulism, age, and gender, to inform optimal implant placement using CBCT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study, approved by the Ethics Committee of Dr. R. Ahmed Dental College and Hospital, Kolkata, comprised sixty CBCT scans. Participants provided informed consent, meeting specific inclusion criteria: maxillary sinus between premolars and molars due to missing teeth, residual ridge height <10 mm, visible maxillary sinus extending ≥15 mm from the sinus floor, and presence of adjacent or opposing teeth for ridge delineation. Exclusions included unclear ridge heights, sinus pathology, and ambiguous ridge borders. Demographics (age, gender) were recorded. Imaging followed optimal protocols for clarity. Measurements were taken in sagittal, axial, and coronal sections. Reference points included adjacent or opposing dentition for partial edentulism and the palatine canal for complete edentulism. The following measurements were performed in the coronal section using the toolkit available from the software:

Statistical analysis

After confirming nonnormality with Shapiro-Wilk’s test, nonparametric tests were conducted. Friedman’s ANOVA and Kruskal Wallis test compared LWT and RH among teeth groups and edentulous sites. Bootstrap linear regression assessed predictors of LWT including age, gender, type of edentulism, and RH. Significance was set at P ≤ 0.05.

RESULTS

The patients had a mean age of 62.6 ± 8.2 years (range: 25-86 years) and a median age of 62.5 years. Males constituted 63.3%, while females were 36.7%, with 36.7% being completely edentulous and 63.3% partially edentulous. Table 1 and Figure 1 illustrate the LWT across all teeth at various cross-sections. LWT generally increased from the 3 mm to 15 mm sections, with significant differences observed in all teeth (P < 0.01) except for PM1 (P = 0.11). At the 3 mm, 7 mm, and 10 mm levels, premolars exhibited lower LWT values compared with molars, but intragroup comparisons showed no significant differences (P > 0.05). However, at the 15 mm section, an increasing trend in LWT from P1 to M2 was evident, with significant differences between molar and premolar regions (P = 0.004). RH decreased from PM1 to M2, although without statistical significance (P = 0.12). Linear regression analysis revealed that age, gender, and RH did not significantly influence LWT, except for the type of edentulism, where partially dentulous patients exhibited higher LWT than completely edentulous ones.

DISCUSSION

Understanding sinus anatomy is vital for precise surgical procedures such as sinus lifts, where preoperative radiological tests play a pivotal role. Authors emphasize the value of CBCT scans in diagnosing and planning treatments, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and treatment efficacy.[3] This study’s main focus was LWT at depths of 3, 7, 10, and 15 mm from the sinus floor in various edentulous regions (PM1, PM2, M1, M2), chosen to minimize vascular risks often present 15-20 mm above the sinus floor. Lateral wall osteotomy typically ranges from 3 mm to 15 mm above the sinus floor. Although most arteries are >15 mm from the crest, situations of significant alveolar ridge resorption may pose closer proximity risks, highlighting the importance of thorough preoperative assessments.[4]

In this study, in the premolar region no significant differences were found between the LWT at any of the levels for PM1, the findings of which correspond to Kiakojori et al.[5] However, Yang et al.[6] found that LWT values decrease from 3 mm to 15 mm levels, the reason may be attributed to the differences in measuring techniques or differences in the ethnicity. In the PM2 region, a detectable difference was noted between the LWT at 3 mm and 15 mm levels, aligning with the results of Kiakojori et al.[5]

In the molar regions, similar to the premolar region, LWT increased from 3 mm level to 15 mm, conforming to the finding of Kiakojori et al.[5] and Monje et al.[7] Intergroup comparisons revealed that the LWT was irregular concerning the 3 mm and 7 mm levels. However, in the 10 mm and 15 mm levels, there was a gradual increase in LWT from the PM1 region to the M1 region and a slight dip in the LWT in the M2 region. The present finding is in contrast to previous studies by Urban et al.[8] Yang et al.,[6] Danesh-Sani et al.[9] and Neiva et al.,[10] have found that the LWT gradually increases from the 2nd premolar to 2nd molar, where the 2nd molar had the greatest thickness. The lateral wall of the maxillary sinus in dentate skulls is thick at the PM1 region, thins at the posterior region reaches its thinnest at the interproximal region between the first premolar and M1 region and then thickens at the M1 region.[9]

In this study, a notable disparity emerged only at the 15 mm level, where LWT in PM1 was markedly lower compared with M1 and M2 regions, consistent with earlier research.[6] The osteotomy typically concludes at this 15 mm mark. Infraorbital and posterior superior alveolar artery anastomoses within sinus walls supply tissues; damage risks include bleeding and membrane perforation, hindering visibility and potentially causing Schneiderian membrane perforation.[11] Age, gender, and RH did not affect LWT, echoing prior findings. However, partially edentulous subjects exhibited significantly higher LWT than completely edentulous ones, aligning with earlier studies.[57] These outcomes are in line with Yang et al.[612] Conversely, Khajehahmadi et al.[12] found no correlation between maxillary sinus LWT and edentulism status.

The second outcome variable in this study was the RH, which showed a gradual decrease from the PM1 region to the M2 region, with no statistical difference between the teeth.

The RH did not seem to influence LWT significantly, although a negligible correlation was found. The results of Monje et al.[7]; and Kiakojori et al.,[5] indicated a positive association between RH and age across all regions This discrepancy could be explained by variations in the measurement technique or by an insufficient sample size.

Although this study demonstrates strengths, notably in evaluating background variables’ association with LWT, it has limitations. Uneven sample distribution led to inconsistent findings. Failure to consider edentulism duration and tooth loss causes may limit its significance. Future research could explore additional anatomical variables for a more comprehensive understanding.

CONCLUSION

Within this study’s confines, it’s evident that maxillary sinus LWT increases from 3 to 15 mm from the floor. Edentulism type influences LWT, whereas age, gender, and RH do not. CBCT scans are recommended before sinus lift surgeries to assess maxillary sinus anatomy changes.
