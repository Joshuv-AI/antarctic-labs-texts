Forecasting residential aged care expenditure with learning algorithm and demographic simulation
Authors: Hengzhe Zhao, Jinhui Zhang, Han Lin Shang, Evelyn Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Australia, residential aged care accounts for a significant share of government expenditure, with financial sustainability and changing population profiles emerging as critical concerns. This study utilizes a longitudinal dataset and develops a statistical simulation model to project the profiles of individuals entering residential aged care, the number of admissions, and the associated government spending. Our approach integrates ensemble learning methods, including eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), with the Hamilton–Perry demographic projection method to provide precise predictions of residents’ length of stay. This integration enables more reliable forecasts of the future residential aged care landscape. The analysis was conducted at the state level to capture regional differences. We examined both demographic features, such as age, marital status, and gender, and medical features, including dementia status and care needs as measured by the Aged Care Funding Instrument (ACFI). We find that XGBoost outperforms previously used survival models for predicting residents’ length of stay. Our simulation model projects that both the number of residents and aggregate government expenditure in residential aged care will continue increasing until 2041. The model also provides groundwork for future research by offering micro-level insights into usage patterns and financial sustainability challenges.Keywords: Residential aged care, Length of stay, Landscape simulation

Introduction

Over the past three decades, Australia’s population aged 65 years and over has increased by almost fourfold, with the share of the total population rising from 8.3% in 1954, to 16% in 2020. This ageing trend is expected to continue until 2066 [1]. Several factors have contributed to this demographic shift, including increases in life expectancy and declines in the fertility rate following the baby boomer era. As a result, the aged care sector faces significant challenges with higher demand for services, labour shortages, and greater fiscal pressure due to a higher dependency ratio.

In the financial year 2021–22, [2] it is estimated that more than Australian dollars (A$) 25.1 billion were allocated to aged care services, representing 1.62% of GDP and 3.83% of total government spending. Specifically, most of the expenditure for aged care services was allocated to residential aged care (RAC) services (59.2% or ∼A$14.9 billion per year) with a smaller proportion allocated to home care and support services (33%), and other care services (7.8%). This is important as RAC provides a mix of daily living support and medical treatment for older people and plays a crucial role in the aged care system, resulting in significant expenditure on the health system in recent decades. A previous study estimated that spending on RAC services in 2021–22 was 12% higher compared to 2017–18, and the average annualized cost per resident had increased to A$85,891 in 2022–23 from A$71,420 in 2014–15 [3].

The rising demand for costly RAC presents a significant challenge for public budget allocation. Several studies have been conducted to estimate RAC expenditure; however, few studies have estimated the implication of rising demand for RAC on government expenditure. The most commonly used methodology is cohort–based, which generally involves three steps [4, 5]. First, RAC residents are divided into different age cohorts, and the number of people in each cohort for a future year is estimated by assuming a constant survival rate from one cohort in the past to the adjacent cohort in the future. The term “survival rate” is used because, in elder cohorts, the mortality rate compared to fertility rate and immigration is dominant in population change. Second, for a chosen year as the base year, we estimate the aged care expenditure per capita in the base year for each cohort. Third, the total expenditure on aged care is estimated by aggregating steps one and two, adjusting for the expected prolonged life expectancy, and expansion of morbidity. Such a methodology requires less data; however, more assumptions need to be made, which may affect the precision of forecasting when some assumptions are inappropriate.

While traditional cohort-based approaches offer computational simplicity, they carry significant limitations. First, cohort-based methods rely on aggregate age-cohort averages, thereby overlooking substantial within-cohort heterogeneity in sociodemographic and medical characteristics that critically influence both length of stay (LOS) and care costs [6, 7]. Second, traditional econometric models typically assume constant per-capita expenditure or apply deterministic growth rates, failing to capture the dynamic evolution of resident profiles and care needs over time. Third, existing microsimulation models, while granular, often lack the integration of machine learning techniques that can capture complex, nonlinear relationships between resident characteristics and care utilization patterns.

Our study addresses the knowledge gap and advances the aged care forecasting literature by offering three methodological innovations. First, we employ an episode-based simulation framework that explicitly models individual-level admission profiles, preserving the full heterogeneity in resident characteristics rather than collapsing into aggregate cohorts. This micro-level approach enables more precise forecasting by accounting for variations in age, gender, marital status, dementia diagnosis, and clinical care needs as measured by the Aged Care Funding Instrument (ACFI). Second, we linked demographic simulation via the Hamilton–Perry method with machine learning-based LOS prediction, ensuring that expenditure projections reflect both population dynamics and individual-level care trajectories. This integration addresses a key knowledge gap in the literature as most existing cohort-based models project population ageing but do not incorporate the changing composition of residential aged care facilities (RACF) admissions in terms of their clinical complexity. Third, we compare the predictive performance of XGBoost against traditional survival analysis approaches (Cox proportional hazards, accelerated failure time (AFT) models), demonstrating that when data are sufficiently large, flexible machine learning methods can achieve superior predictive accuracy.

Our contributions position our work as a methodological advance over existing approaches by combining the demographic rigor of cohort-component models with the predictive power of machine learning and the granularity of microsimulation. Consequently, our findings are more adaptive and data-driven forecasting framework capable of supporting policy scenario analysis and regional-level budget planning.

In this work, we consider an episode-based methodology for estimating RAC expenditure, which includes simulating prospective admissions in RACFs with pseudo profiles. The simulation preserves the relationship between the admission of RACF and the entire population structure. Then, utilizing tuned machine learning models that are trained on the past RACF episode data, we derive the expected LOS and daily RAC expenditure of future residency according to the profiles of each resident and aggregate this information to estimate the total expenditure. The maximum daily expenditure in RACF is regulated by the Australian government and the estimation is approached through the use of the ACFI.

Previous literature shows that people in the same age and gender cohort are versatile in sociodemographic and medical characteristics that significantly affect LOS and care expenditure per capita in RACFs and analyzed the heterogeneity of LOS within a survival analysis framework [6, 7]. In this work, given a sufficiently large sample size, we extend the comparison by incorporating XGBoost, a machine learning model. XGBoost is evaluated alongside survival models for predictive accuracy. The results indicate that although XGBoost uses fewer censored observations, it still outperforms the survival models in predictive performance.

This paper follows the strand and provides the implications for budget distribution from a quantitative perspective beginning with the estimation of the RAC expenditure. Specifically, this study utilized a range of properly tuned machine learning models trained on episode data in RAC and evaluated the methodology to estimate RAC expenditure.

In the following sections, we start by introducing the data sets that were used for our forecast. Next, we introduce our forecasting methodology, where we emphasize the specific statistical problem at each stage of the forecasting, and explain our model selection. In the findings section, we list the main results from model training and projection. We will then discuss the insights provided by our model in three key areas: the availability of places in RACFs, the demographics of residents in RACFs, and the projected expenditure on RACFs, and policy recommendations.

Data

We used three data sets for our estimation: the RACF administrative data, demographic distribution data, and subsidy scheme data. We use administrative data from RACFs in Australia, provided by the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) and sourced from the National Aged Care Data Clearinghouse.

This data set is requested for the Aged Care Assessment program and a Commonwealth–funded home–based aged care research project. Data privacy and confidentiality were ensured to fulfil the requirements of the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare Act of 1987. The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the AIHW, but restrictions apply to their availability. These data were used with permission for the current research and are therefore not publicly available. Data are available from the AIHW on a reasonable request.

We have 2021 and 2016 Australian Bureau of Statistics population census data for population counts, stratified by age, gender, and place of residence at the state level, aligned with the RACF admission data. In reality, only a percentage of the amount being subsidized is adjusted based on means-tested results, whereas the rest is known as private contributions. Our work focuses on the impact of demographic change on RAC expenditure, rather than on residents’ financial profiles, although in reality, both factors jointly influence RAC expenditure.

Methodology

Our methodology consists of three steps. First, we fit an XGBoost model for LOS in RACFs, using residents’ socioeconomic and medical characteristics as predictors. This allows us to quantify the relationship between LOS and individual profiles. Second, we simulate admissions from 2021 to our projection end year, 2041, and predict LOS for these simulated individuals using the fitted XGBoost model. Third, we combine real and simulated admissions into a single data set, which is then expanded based on each resident’s LOS. This expansion yields a comprehensive resident-level data set that represents the RACF population over time. We use this data set to analyze industry trends and forecast total RAC expenditure.

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [10]. All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

Length of stay regression

We exclude age groups below 65 years from our dataset, since the minimum age for admission to RAC is 65. Entries with an age group below 65 years imply the use of RAC for disability care and Indigenous aged care (with a minimum entry age of 50). In contrast, our dataset does not contain an Indigenous identifier. In our data, 16% of the individuals have had multiple admissions. Residents can be discharged from the RACF and return later if needed. In most cases, residents are transferred to the hospital and will return shortly. Hence, in our study, we aggregate LOS for each admission episode and each unique individual, even when there are multiple entries.

We then exclude the top 5% of extreme LOS observations as outliers following the previous work by [6, 7]. In Fig. 1, we plot the distribution of LOS in different admission years, distinguishing between residents who have been discharged from RACF and those who remain in care. The former group consists of uncensored residents, for whom a complete LOS dataset is available, while the latter group consists of censored residents, whose LOS is still ongoing at the time of data collection. For each admission year, two density curves are shown, one for each group representing their marginal LOS distributions. These are not intended to compare group sizes, but rather to visualize the shape and spread of LOS within each group.

Marginal distributions of LOS for all and censored admissions, by admission year, with censoring percentages for each year, are indicated to the right of the density curves

Among uncensored residents, the LOS distribution approximately follows a log-normal shape, with increasingly longer tails for earlier admission years. In contrast, the LOS for censored residents appears closer to a normal distribution, with decreasing variance as the admission year recedes. On the right side of Fig. 1, we report the percentage of censoring for each year. For example, in 2020, 61% of residents remained in care at the time of data collection in 2021, implying a censoring rate of 61%.

Two previous studies on the regression analysis of LOS and resident characteristics: [6] and [7] employed survival models, namely Cox proportional hazards (CoxPH) and AFT models, respectively. These approaches were designed to leverage the partial information contained in the censored observations within a survival analysis framework. Due to a sufficiently large uncensored dataset (representing at least 70% of observations), we extend this methodology by incorporating the predictive power of tree-based eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [11]. To compare the performance of our three shortlisted models: two survival models and XGBoost, we design a unified estimation and evaluation workflow that maximizes the predictive potential of each model while ensuring comparability. First, we randomly sampled 20% of the uncensored observations from the admission dataset to form the test dataset. The remaining 80% constitutes the training dataset.

All three models are estimated using the full training dataset, which includes both censored and uncensored observations. All models are then used to predict the LOS for the testing dataset, which contains only uncensored cases. In survival models, the predicted LOS is the expected survival time, calculated by integrating the estimated survival curve. Restricting the test dataset to uncensored observations ensures that error metrics are comparable across the three models on a common evaluation set, although it does not fully capture the survival models’ advantage in handling censored observations; we discuss the implications of this design choice in the Limitations subsection. The performance of the LOS model is evaluated by the mean squared error (MSE) and the mean absolute error (MAE). The MSE is defined as , where is the actual LOS and is the predicted LOS for observation i, and n is the number of test observations. The MAE is defined as . MSE penalizes larger prediction errors more heavily due to the squared term, making it sensitive to outliers, while MAE provides a more interpretable average deviation measured in days. Lower MSE and MAE imply better performance.

Admission simulation

Rather than projecting future admissions to the RACF and their characteristics directly from historical admission data, we simulate the broader population from which admissions are drawn. This approach addresses a key limitation of relying solely on admission data: it overlooks the demographic dynamics of the underlying population. As noted by [12], Australia’s fertility rate remained low during the 1930 s and 1940 s, rose sharply in the 1950 s and 1960 s with the baby boomer generation, and declined again in the 1980 s and 1990s. These fluctuations shape the population’s age structure over time and, in turn, influence future RACF demand. Admission-based projections alone fail to account for these demographic shifts, which are essential for anticipating future trends in aged care usage.

In our work, the regional effect plays a crucial role in shaping both LOS and the aggregated subsidy and has great potential for informing targeted policy-making. The Hamilton–Perry method [13] is well suited to forecast regional populations because it implicitly accounts for immigration, fertility, and other common demographic trends [5, 14]. This method requires population data by gender and age group from the two most recent consecutive censuses.

In our study, immigration has minimal impact on the state-level demographic simulation in Australia [15]; moreover, the impact of immigration on the usage of RACF is mitigated by strict health examination [16], and means the evaluation of skilled migration, which takes the dominant share of migration [17]. The use of means-tested RACF subsidies is negligible for immigrants. The fertility rate is excluded because we forecast for only a short period, and the youngest possible admission in the last year of forecasting would be middle-aged by the beginning of our forecasting period.

The next step is to compute the number of admissions to RACFs from the population, stratified by age, sex, state, and admission year, yielding the admission ratio. In our work, we use log-linear regression to forecast future admission ratios and replace zero values in the existing admission ratios with an arbitrarily small number of . The choice of log-linear regression is driven by ease of interpretation, and the use of the log-transformed ratio rather than the plain admission ratio yields a more pessimistic projection, improving our work’s adaptability to future policy changes.

To satisfy the input requirements of our LOS model, we need to further match the simulated ACFI level, marital status, spoken language, and dementia diagnosis to those simulated admissions by summarizing past trends. Since all features are categorical, we use a multinomial logistic regression model for classification. The multinomial logistic regression extends the binary logistic regression to handle response variables with more than two categories.

The features are matched with a sample category drawn from the output distribution. Instead of matching simultaneously, this is done sequentially to improve accuracy by collecting as much information as possible between the matched and matching features. We predict LOS for admissions using the best LOS models from the shortlist. For those with censored observations, we updated their LOS with the predicted LOS. Then we have the complete simulated admission data. We merge this with the original admission data to have complete admission data from 2008 to 2041.

Simulation period and data characteristics: Our simulation extends from 2021 to 2041, yielding 21 years of projected data compared to 13 years of historical observations (2008–2020). This longer projection period is deliberate and serves two purposes. First, it captures the full impact of the baby boomer cohort (born 1946–1964) as they age into RACF eligibility, a demographic transition critical for long-term expenditure planning. Second, it provides a policy-relevant 20-year forecast horizon from the present, enabling scenario analysis for budget allocation and service capacity planning. The simulation preserves the distributional characteristics of historical admissions by sampling resident attributes (ACFI scores, marital status, language, dementia diagnosis) from the empirical distribution within the same State/Sex/AgeGroup strata.

We calculated admission rates using pooled data from 2016 to 2020, defined as the total admissions divided by the total population across this five-year period for each State/Sex/AgeGroup stratum. This pooled approach differs from averaging annual rates and inherently captures the declining trend observed during 2017–2020 (59,359 admissions in 2017 declining to 54,207 in 2020). This decline likely reflects multiple factors, including the expansion of Home Care Packages (HCP), which provided community-based alternatives to residential care, and the COVID-19 pandemic’s impact on RACF admissions in 2020.

Notably, this overall decline masks substantial heterogeneity across age cohorts, which is discussed further in the Results section. Age-stratified analysis reveals that older cohorts experienced disproportionately larger declines: the 85–89 age group declined by 16.0% (from 16,594 to 13,934 admissions), while younger cohorts (65–79 years) declined only 2.7% on average. The 70–74 cohort actually increased by 3.2%. This pattern contradicts conventional elasticity predictions—younger cohorts, who might substitute residential care with home-based alternatives more readily, showed greater resilience in admissions. The sharper decline among the oldest cohorts likely reflects multiple pandemic-related factors: elevated COVID-19 mortality among older RACF residents, heightened family reluctance to admit during the pandemic peak, delayed urgent admissions, and disruptions to the hospital-to-RACF transition pathway, which disproportionately affect the frail elderly. This age-differential response underscores that the 2017–2020 decline was not solely driven by policy-induced substitution toward home care, but also by exogenous shocks that most severely affected the most vulnerable populations.

Uncertainty quantification

To quantify forecast uncertainty and provide interpretable bounds for our expenditure projections, we use a Monte Carlo simulation. Long-term forecasts inherently carry uncertainty from multiple sources: stochastic variation in individual LOS, sampling variability in attribute assignment, and projection uncertainty in admission rates. Rather than presenting point estimates alone, we generate 95% prediction intervals to convey the precision of our projections.

Our Monte Carlo procedure involves running the full simulation pipeline 200 times with different random seeds. In each iteration, the deterministic components (population forecasts, average admission rates) remain fixed, while stochastic elements vary: (1) the sampling of resident attributes from empirical distributions and (2) the random component added to LOS predictions. This lognormal error specification was selected based on residual diagnostics showing variance increasing with predicted LOS (variance ratio = 3.76 across prediction quintiles), indicating heteroscedastic errors that scale proportionally with LOS magnitude rather than remaining constant.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our expenditure projections to key modeling assumptions, we conduct a sensitivity analysis varying admission rates. Admission rates are a critical driver of total expenditure, reflecting both demographic demand and policy factors (e.g., home care availability and eligibility criteria). Our baseline scenario uses the average 2016–2020 admission rates by State/Sex/AgeGroup strata. We test two alternative scenarios: a low scenario with baseline rates reduced by 10%, and a high scenario with rates increased by 10%. These bounds represent plausible variation reflecting potential policy shifts (e.g., expanded home care reducing RACF admissions, or tightened eligibility increasing them).

For each scenario, we re-run the full simulation pipeline: demographic forecasting, admission projection, attribute sampling, and LOS prediction, while holding all other parameters constant. This allows us to isolate the impact of admission rate assumptions on projected admissions, resident days, and expenditures. By comparing baseline projections against the low and high scenarios, we quantify the sensitivity of our results to this parameter and provide policymakers with ranges reflecting alternative demand trajectories.

Since the exact admission date is not available in our dataset, we assume that all admissions occur on the 183rd day of the admission year. By knowing each resident’s assumed start date and their length of stay, we convert admission data into longitudinal resident-level data, enabling us to capture the evolving profile of RACF residents across calendar years.

To compute government expenditure on RACF services per resident, we link these simulated resident records to the ACFI subsidy schedule. The latest version of the ACFI schedule was released in July 2022, just before its replacement by the Australian National Aged Care Classification (AN-ACC) system in October 2022. A comprehensive list of the ACFI and AN-ACC subsidy schedules from 2020 to 2024 is available in [9]. For illustration, the most recent ACFI schedule is shown in Table 1.

To calculate the total expenditure per resident, we sum the daily subsidy amounts for each calendar year the resident spends in an RACF. For any given year, residents are assigned the average subsidy amount from the ACFI schedules available that year, based on their respective ACFI care levels. For years without an official schedule, subsidy values are linearly interpolated. Since ACFI subsidies are influenced by inflation and government budget allocations, we assume an annual growth rate of 1.2% to estimate subsidies for missing years, both retrospectively and prospectively. This growth rate is derived as the geometric mean of the annual changes observed in the published subsidy schedules.

Uncertainty quantification and sensitivity analysis

To provide interval estimates reflecting projection uncertainty, we conducted 200 Monte Carlo simulations with stochastic variation in LOS predictions and in attribute sampling. Following the methodology described in the Methods section, we conducted residual diagnostics on the XGBoost model to select an appropriate error distribution for our simulation. Figure 4 presents the residual diagnostics that empirically justify our choice of a lognormal error model. Panels (D) and (E) demonstrate that the residuals on the log scale are approximately normally distributed, while panels (C) and (F) reveal the characteristic funnel shape of heteroscedasticity in natural scale residuals (where the variance ratio is 3.76). This empirical evidence supports the use of a lognormal (multiplicative) error specification in our Monte Carlo procedure, ensuring that uncertainty scales proportionally with predicted residency duration.

Demographic trends

Discussion

In our projection, we observed significant trends in the demographic profile of RACF residents. Most notably, the centenarian cohort (100+) is expanding very rapidly, projected to grow from 0.7% to 15.5% of admissions by 2041. This shift toward an older resident profile is accompanied by a slight downward trend in the mean LOS, mirroring a “compression of morbidity” where residents enter care at increasingly advanced ages and with higher levels of clinical complexity [6]. This is consistent with recent empirical evidence from the Australian context, which shows a steady decline in estimated LOS since 2017 [6, 7]. Although the decrease in mean LOS is relatively small, it highlights that future RACFs will serve a population with distinct care needs, including a higher prevalence of multi-morbidity, greater cognitive impairment, and increased frailty.

These demographic shifts have critical implications for sector sustainability. Traditional care models optimized for the 85–89 age group may become inadequate, requiring RACFs to invest in specialized geriatric care training, palliative care capacity, and end–of–life support infrastructure. Furthermore, as the oldest populations are highly vulnerable to exogenous shocks, evidenced by the disproportionate decline in 85–89 age group admissions during 2017–2020, future pandemic preparedness and home care substitution programs must account for age-differential impacts to avoid unintended reductions in access for the most vulnerable.

In Australia, accommodation can be paid upfront as a lump sum, known as a refundable accommodation deposit (RAD), or at regular intervals through daily accommodation payments (DAPs). Our model suggests that the rising number of residents implies balance sheet expansion for RAC providers, as debt will be raised through RADs. This introduces further complications for the sector from the perspective of RACF liquidity [18]. Future studies on RACF liquidity may use our model to predict future residents and their LOS. Aggregating these projections with residents’ RADs/DAPs preferences would assist in evaluating individual RACFs’ or the entire sector’s financial positions. The results can also facilitate expansion planning, budgeting, and urban planning. This represents only one potential research application that our model can support. From a consumer perspective, future research should examine retirees’ preferences for RACFs, accounting for their sociodemographic, financial, and medical characteristics. Such an investigation is essential for advancing more person-centred models of care, especially given the limited attention this topic has received to date. Furthermore, our model can contribute to policymaking in the field of RAC by forecasting the future landscape of both the RAC and home care sectors with minor modifications.

In the bigger picture, Australian retirement income has three pillars: the age pension, superannuation, and social welfare. There is a shift of focus from public to private welfare through the introduction of superannuation, for which the tax concession is expected to exceed social expenditure on the age pension by 2040 [19]. Retirement income is closely integrated with healthcare subsidies through the Medicare levy and related measures, but not with aged care. Integrating the social insurance system with aged care services is a viable option to mitigate financial pressures in the sector and improve the adaptability of budget distribution [20].

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, regarding the treatment of censored observations in LOS modeling, our XGBoost model was trained exclusively on uncensored cases—residents who had been discharged from RACFs by the time of data collection. While this approach leverages the superior predictive flexibility of machine learning models, it does not explicitly exploit the partial information contained in censored observations (i.e., residents still in care). This design choice may introduce bias, as censored cases tend to be longer-stay residents. To gauge the plausible magnitude of this bias, we compare descriptive statistics across the two groups in our cleaned analysis dataset (after the year-specific 5th–95th percentile trimming). Uncensored residents (n = 653,610) have a mean observed LOS of 639.9 days (median 439), whereas censored residents (n = 112,111) have a mean duration-so-far of 741.0 days (median 623) at the time of data collection. The duration-so-far for censored cases is a lower bound on their eventual LOS, so the ∼ 101-day excess over the uncensored mean understates the true gap. This comparison supports the concern that training only on uncensored observations could lead to predictions that underestimate average LOS and, by extension, total expenditure. We note that survival models such as Cox proportional hazards and AFT models can incorporate censored data; however, our empirical evaluation demonstrated that XGBoost still achieved superior out-of-sample predictive accuracy on uncensored test cases. Future methodological work could explore survival-based machine learning extensions (e.g., XGBoost-AFT) to more fully leverage censored observations while maintaining predictive performance.

Second, our methodology relies on several simplifying assumptions, the relaxation of which could affect the results. We assume all admissions occur on day 183 (July 2) of the admission year, due to the lack of exact admission dates in our data. While this assumption facilitates the conversion of admission data into longitudinal resident-level records, alternative temporal distributions (e.g., uniform distribution across the year) could yield different expenditure trajectories. Additionally, we apply an assumed annual subsidy growth rate of 1.2% to estimate subsidies in years without published schedules. This rate reflects past inflationary trends and budget allocations but may not capture future policy shifts or economic conditions.

Third, although the assumptions underlying the Hamilton-Perry method remain adequate for this framework, it cannot account for policy changes. For example, although the Home Care Package was introduced during the study period in 2008 [3, 21], our study was unable to incorporate the impact of residents’ financial profiles on the use of residential aged care due to data limitations. Therefore the analyses was based on the assumption that all individuals pay the full cost of care services. The data set used in this study includes location information at the state and territory level. Future research could further explain the heterogeneity in RACF usage by incorporating finer regional divisions and accounting for remoteness. Furthermore, questions concerning the role of individual behavior and covariate dynamics over time, such as how residents’ preferences and care needs evolve, merit investigation but are beyond the scope of this study and should be addressed in future research.

Conclusion

This study makes a methodological contribution to aged-care expenditure forecasting by integrating demographic simulation, machine learning, and individual-level episode modeling into a unified framework. Unlike traditional cohort-based approaches that rely on aggregate averages and deterministic growth assumptions, our episode-based methodology preserves individual-level heterogeneity in resident characteristics, enabling more precise and adaptive projections. By linking the Hamilton–Perry demographic method with machine learning–based LOS prediction (XGBoost), we ensure that expenditure forecasts properly reflect both population aging dynamics and the evolving clinical complexity of RACF admissions. Our empirical findings demonstrate that flexible machine learning models can outperform traditional survival models in predictive accuracy when uncensored data are sufficiently large, offering a data-driven alternative for policy scenario analysis.

Our model provides insights into three key areas: the availability of places in RACFs, the demographics of RACF residents, and projected expenditure on RACFs. We showed that our model can be used in ways that benefit stakeholders, including the government, RACFs, and residents. First, the operation of RACFs can benefit from prior knowledge of residents’ expected LOS. Taking into account the profiles of incoming residents and the expected LOS, our model provides guidance on how to make RACFs more adaptable by adjusting the mix of room types, medical equipment, and the set of physician skills to the evolving needs of users. Second, for the government, our model provides a reliable projection of expenditures, which can be used for budget allocation and for stress-testing proposed policies. This application can be further improved by zooming in on specific postcodes or regions. Third, this methodology can also be used to analyze the home care industry, for which geographical location is also critical. In so doing, our model lays the ground for future research in the RAC sector through micro-level insights into usage patterns and sustainability challenges, while offering a more rigorous methodological foundation for aged-care forecasting than existing cohort-based or microsimulation approaches.
