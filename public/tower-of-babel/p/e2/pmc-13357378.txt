Mitochondrial dynamics imbalance in hepatocellular carcinoma: from molecular mechanisms to new strategies for targeted therapy
Authors: Fuxiang Chen, Chunyang Ma, Xitong Bo, Yilong Zhou, Suqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a malignancy with high global mortality, exhibits a close association with mitochondrial dynamic imbalance. Mitochondria maintain cellular homeostasis through a dynamic equilibrium of fission and fusion, and dysregulation of this process can trigger metabolic reprogramming, oxidative stress, and dysregulation of multiple modes of cell death, ultimately driving HCC progression. This review systematically elucidates the molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets associated with mitochondrial dynamic imbalance in HCC. It begins by analyzing the structure and functional characteristics of mitochondria, outlining the universal impact of their fission-fusion regulatory network on cancer biology. Subsequently, it focuses on HCC-specific pathological mechanisms, revealing how mitochondrial dynamic imbalance reshapes the tumor microenvironment via the Warburg effect, lipotoxicity, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst. A key emphasis is placed on the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in regulating multiple modes of cell death, including apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy, and the emerging mechanism of cuproptosis. The link between cuproptosis and mitochondrial copper accumulation, lipoylated protein aggregation, and metabolic collapse provides a novel perspective for HCC therapy. Finally, the review critically evaluates therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial dynamics, discussing the translational potential of DRP1 inhibitors (e.g., Mdivi-1), mitophagy activators (e.g., rapamycin), and copper chelators (e.g., tetrathiomolybdate). Future directions, such as mitochondrial-targeted nanodelivery systems and multi-target combination therapies, are also explored. This comprehensive review aims to provide a theoretical foundation and innovative insights for understanding the pathogenesis and advancing precision therapy in HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide with high incidence and mortality rates (1). Although its mortality has declined in recent years, the overall disease burden remains substantial. Studies have shown that the development and progression of HCC are closely associated with imbalances in mitochondrial dynamics. Mitochondrial dynamics, regulated by the balance between fusion and fission, plays a critical role in maintaining cellular energy homeostasis and metabolic equilibrium, and is deeply involved in HCC initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance (2). In HCC cells, mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins, such as pro-fusion proteins MFN2 and OPA1, and pro-fission proteins DRP1 and FIS1 are often dysregulated, leading to disruption of the mitochondrial network (3). This imbalance is also intricately linked to metabolic reprogramming in HCC: enhanced fission promotes glycolysis, while dominant fusion favors oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Such metabolic plasticity provides a foundation for tumor adaptation to hypoxic microenvironments (4).

As multifunctional double-membrane organelles, mitochondria are central to cellular metabolism, energy production, signal transduction, and apoptosis (5). Mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated in various diseases, including cancer (2). Dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics is a common feature in multiple tumors, including HCC, and in HCC it is primarily characterized by enhanced mitochondrial fission and impaired fusion. This review aims to summarize alterations in mitochondrial dynamics in HCC, elucidate the mechanisms through which mitochondrial dysfunction influences hepatocarcinogenesis and metastasis, and explore potential therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial dynamics, thereby providing a comprehensive perspective on the role of mitochondria in HCC.

Mitochondrial origin, structure, and mtDNA dysfunction in HCC

According to the endosymbiotic theory, mitochondria evolved from α-proteobacteria engulfed by an archaeal host, eventually specializing into energy-producing organelles (6), mitochondrial biogenesis involves replication of mtDNA, transcription of nuclear and mtDNA genes, and assembly of OXPHOS complexes (7). In HCC, dysregulated mitochondrial biogenesis and OXPHOS assembly are early hallmarks of metabolic reprogramming, directly supporting malignant proliferation and therapy resistance.

Structurally, mitochondria are double-membrane-bound organelles consisting of the outer membrane (OMM), inner membrane (IMM), intermembrane space, and matrix (8,9). The OMM is highly permeable, facilitating exchange via VDAC, porins, and the TOM complex, and also participates in fatty acid elongation and other reactions (10). The IMM is protein-rich and folds into cristae to increase surface area and enhance OXPHOS capacity; cristae stability is critical, as its dysfunction is linked to Parkinson’s disease (11,12). Notably, HCC cells frequently exhibit fragmented cristae morphology and reduced IMM density, further compromising respiratory chain efficiency and promoting a glycolysis-dominant phenotype (Warburg effect).

In addition to these structural features, mitochondria possess their own genome, mtDNA, a small circular molecule independent of nuclear DNA, encoding respiratory chain subunits, tRNAs, and rRNAs (13). Lacking histone protection, having limited repair capacity, and residing near the ROS-generating electron transport chain (ETC), mtDNA is highly susceptible to mutations and copy number reduction (14). These abnormalities impair the assembly or stability of ETC complexes I, III, IV, and ATP synthase, leading to electron leakage and excessive ROS production (15). In the HCC microenvironment, elevated ROS further drives genomic instability, HIF-α stabilization, and metastatic signaling, while the resulting oxidative damage to mitochondrial lipids, proteins, and mtDNA itself forms a self-amplifying vicious cycle that sustains tumor progression (16).

Mitochondrial dynamics

Mitochondrial dynamics maintain the homeostasis of the mitochondrial network through fission, fusion, and mitophagy (17).

Regarding mitochondrial fission, this process is primarily mediated by the GTPase DRP1 (18). In HCC, DRP1 is frequently overexpressed, leading to mitochondrial fragmentation (19). Excessive division promotes proliferation, while moderate division may inhibit metastasis, mainly including three aspects: energy stress, cell cycle arrest, and distribution disorder.

To begin with, moderate fission causes a mild drop in mitochondrial OXPHOS efficiency. This lowers ATP production and raises the AMP/ATP ratio, which in turn activates AMPK. Once activated, AMPK puts the brakes on anabolic metabolism by suppressing mTORC1 and boosting the p53/p21 axis, ultimately holding cells at the G1/S checkpoint (20,21).

Another key mechanism is that moderate fission can dial down cyclin D1/E and CDK4/6 while ramping up p21/p27. At the same time, it curbs the activity of pro-proliferative kinases like Akt and ERK. Together, these changes prevent the retinoblastoma protein from being phosphorylated and stop E2F from being released, so cells never make it into S phase (22,23).

Then there is the issue of disrupted distribution and quality control. When the rhythm or extent of fission gets out of balance, mitochondria cannot be evenly divided between daughter cells after mitosis. This interferes with PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, triggers mtDNA damage, and leads to the release of apoptotic factors (24). The net result is a lasting hit to the proliferative capacity of daughter cells. All these mechanisms work together, forming the molecular foundation for how moderate fission puts a lid on cell proliferation.

We also found that fragmented mitochondria induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, cause oxidative damage to nuclear DNA (e.g., accumulation of 8-oxoguanine), and increase the mutation risk of tumor suppressor genes such as TP53 and PTEN, thereby promoting HCC development (25). A key mechanism underlying enhanced fission involves phosphorylation of DRP1 at Ser616 (26). The deubiquitinase USP1 stabilizes cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) by preventing NEDD4L-mediated degradation, which in turn promotes DRP1-Ser616 phosphorylation and exacerbates mitochondrial fragmentation (27). Notably, USP1 is highly expressed in HCC tissues and significantly associated with poor prognosis (28). Additionally, signals derived from ETC dysfunction, including elevated ROS, energy stress, and disrupted calcium signaling, further drive excessive fission: ROS oxidatively modifies and activates DRP1, recruiting it from the cytoplasm to the outer mitochondrial membrane (29), while energy stress and calcium dysregulation activate DRP1 via phosphorylation through kinase pathways such as ERK and CaMKII (30).

In contrast to fission, mitochondrial fusion works synergistically to maintain the morphological and functional balance of the mitochondrial network, with the outer mitochondrial membrane fusion protein MFN2 acting as a tumor suppressor in HCC (31). In hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related HCC, the hepatitis B virus X protein (HBx) inhibits the transcriptional activity and nuclear translocation of p53, attenuating p53-mediated transcriptional activation of MFN2 and resulting in its downregulation (32). Reduced MFN2 expression contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic reprogramming, resistance to cell death, and enhanced invasiveness, all of which drive HCC progression. Moreover, ETC dysfunction-associated energy stress, ROS accumulation, and AMPK pathway activation can further impair MFN2 function through transcriptional downregulation or oxidative damage, disrupting fusion and exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction (33).

Complementing fission and fusion, mitophagy is the selective autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria, a process critical for mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction. The outer mitochondrial membrane protein FUNDC1 serves as a receptor that mediates mitophagy in a ubiquitin-independent manner and participates in the regulation of mitochondrial dynamics (34,35). Under physiological conditions, FUNDC1 directly binds to the autophagy-related protein LC3, targeting damaged mitochondria to autophagosomes for degradation (36). In the HCC microenvironment, hypoxia can activate FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy, which may contribute to tumor cell adaptation and survival (37).

Mitochondrial dysfunction in HCC

Cancer is a multifactorial disease characterized by extensive alterations at genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolic levels (38). Mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly perturbations in mitochondrial dynamics (excessive fission and impaired fusion) and aberrant metabolic reprogramming, serves as a key driver in the initiation and progression of HCC (39), a hallmark that is increasingly recognized to share certain mechanistic or metabolic commonalities with other malignancies. Below, we focus on the core mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction in HCC.

Mitochondrial dynamics, orchestrated by fission and fusion events, are critical for maintaining mitochondrial network homeostasis. In HCC, dysregulation of this balance is a prominent feature, primarily driven by abnormal activation of fission machinery and suppressed fusion capacity. Mitochondrial fission is predominantly mediated by DRP1, whose overexpression and stabilization are frequently observed in HCC. Unlike USP1, which indirectly regulates the functional activity of DRP1 by activating upstream kinases, USP3 directly maintains the DRP1 protein itself, preventing its degradation. The deubiquitinating enzyme ubiquitin-specific protease 3 (USP3) contributes to DRP1 accumulation by cleaving K48-linked polyubiquitin chains, thereby inhibiting proteasomal degradation of DRP1 in tumor cells (37,40). Additionally, oncogenic signaling pathways, such as EGFR or KRAS mutations that activate downstream ERK or PI3K/AKT cascades, promote DRP1 transcription; ERK activation further enhances DRP1 translocation to the OMM and fission activity via phosphorylation (41,42). Post-translational modifications (e.g., phosphorylation, ubiquitination, S-nitrosylation) also fine-tune DRP1 function (43), with DRP1 hyperphosphorylation in HCC driving excessive mitochondrial fission and fragmentation (44). This fragmentation disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential, impairs OXPHOS efficiency, reduces ATP production, and elevates ROS generation, collectively fostering cancer cell proliferation, migration, chemoresistance, and tumor progression through autophagy activation (45,46). These two different deubiquitinase regulatory mechanisms play an important pathological role in the malignant progression of HCC.

In contrast to fission hyperactivation, mitochondrial fusion is impaired in HCC. Fusion is mediated by MFN1/2 (localized to the OMM) and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1, localized to the IMM), with MFN1/2 expression significantly downregulated in HCC (47). Promoter hypermethylation of fusion protein genes inhibits transcription factor binding, thereby suppressing gene expression (48). The tumor suppressor p53, a key transcriptional regulator of MFN2, is often destabilized and degraded in HCC due to overexpression of E3 ubiquitin ligases MDM2/MDMX (e.g., via gene amplification or oncogenic signaling from EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathways), leading to loss of p53-mediated pro-fusion activity (49-51).

Imbalance between excessive fission and impaired fusion constitutes a central mechanism driving metabolic reprogramming in HCC, such as the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis) (52). DRP1-mediated fragmentation and ROS accumulation stabilize hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), which upregulates glycolytic enzymes (e.g., hexokinase 2, HK2; lactate dehydrogenase A, LDHA). Concurrently, reduced MFN1/2/OPA1 expression compromises mitochondrial network integrity, forcing cells to rely on glycolysis for energy production and promoting proliferation (53). Mitochondrial dynamics also bidirectionally regulate fatty acid oxidation: DRP1-mediated fission enhances this process (e.g., by increasing carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1/2, CPT1/2, activity and lipid droplet contact), while MFN1/2-mediated fusion inhibits it (54,55). Metabolites such as acetyl-CoA and ceramide further feedback-regulate dynamin-related protein function, forming a complex regulatory loop. Beyond metabolic reprogramming, dysfunctional mitochondrial ETC induce electron leakage and excessive ROS production (56). High ROS levels directly damage nuclear and mtDNA, causing mutations and breaks that activate oncogenes or inactivate tumor suppressors; they also weaken intracellular antioxidant defense systems, exacerbating oxidative stress and DNA damage to promote tumorigenesis (57,58). Mitochondrial dysfunction additionally drives aberrant activation of oncogenic signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K/Akt), dysregulation of Bcl-2 family proteins, disrupted ER-mitochondria calcium crosstalk, and activation of calcium-dependent enzymes, all of which enhance tumor cell growth and survival (59-62) (like Figure 1) (drew by Adobe Illustrator software).

Impact of mitochondrial dynamical imbalance on HCC

Mitochondrial dynamical imbalance primarily manifested as DRP1-driven excessive fission and dysfunction of fusion proteins profoundly reshapes the fate of HCC cells. This imbalance leads to mitochondrial fragmentation, disrupts normal structure and function, induces metabolic reprogramming (e.g., enhanced glycolysis), exacerbates genomic instability, and causes significant dysregulation of key cell death pathways. These dysfunctions not only provide cancer cells with sustained proliferative energy and adaptability to harsh microenvironments but also profoundly affect the susceptibility to multiple programmed cell death modalities (including apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy, and cuproptosis). Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a tightly regulated form of programmed cell death essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis, serving to eliminate damaged, infected, or mutated cells and reduce energy consumption (57). Defective apoptosis is a key mechanism in the development and progression of malignant tumors, including breast, lung, colorectal, and HCCs (58).

Apoptosis is primarily executed through two core pathways. The intrinsic (mitochondrial) apoptotic pathway is triggered by intracellular stress signals (e.g., DNA damage, oxidative stress). Activated pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins translocate to the OMM, increasing its permeability and releasing apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c (Cyt C) into the cytoplasm (63). Cytosolic Cyt C binds to Apaf-1 and, in the presence of ATP/dATP, forms the apoptosome, which recruits and activates procaspase-9. This in turn cleaves and activates downstream effector caspases (e.g., caspase-3, -6, -7), inducing apoptosis (73,74). The extrinsic (death receptor) pathway is initiated by binding of ligands (e.g., FasL, TNFα) to cell surface death receptors (e.g., Fas/CD95, TNFR1, DR3/4/5) (75). Receptor-ligand binding induces formation of the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), activating caspase-8. Activated caspase-8 can directly activate downstream effector caspases (e.g., caspase-3, -6, -7) to execute apoptosis (76,77). Dysregulation of this pathway is closely associated with tumorigenesis and autoimmune diseases.

Mitochondrial dysfunction (e.g., fragmentation due to dynamical imbalance) significantly interferes with apoptosis. Fragmented mitochondria exhibit reduced OXPHOS efficiency and insufficient ATP synthesis, this energy deficiency affects ATP-dependent apoptotic processes and calcium pump function (78). ATP depletion also impairs ER calcium pump activity, leading to cytosolic calcium overload and ER calcium depletion. Disrupted calcium homeostasis further damages mitochondrial function and may aberrantly regulate apoptosis-related proteins (e.g., Bcl-2 family) (79). Imbalance in Bcl-2 family proteins can cause premature or excessive cytochrome c release. Abnormal opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) or persistent dynamical imbalance (e.g., excessive fission) may also interfere with cytochrome c release by altering mitochondrial membrane stability and morphology. Furthermore, inefficient ETCs in fragmented mitochondria lead to electron leakage and ROS accumulation, further damaging the mitochondrial membrane, promoting increased permeability, and facilitating cytochrome c release.

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, regulated cell death characterized by excessive accumulation of lipid peroxides. Its hallmarks include intracellular iron accumulation, glutathione (GSH) depletion, and inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) (64). Mitochondrial dynamical imbalance particularly mitochondrial fragmentation due to DRP1-mediated excessive fission, it is a key factor influencing ferroptosis susceptibility in HCC cells, primarily by disrupting iron metabolic homeostasis and promoting lipid peroxidation (80).

Metabolically, mitochondrial fragmentation impairs the regulation of mitochondrial iron homeostasis. Abnormal expression or activity of iron transport proteins [e.g., MFN2, transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1), divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1)] on fragmented mitochondria leads to increased iron uptake and/or decreased iron release. Abnormally elevated intracellular free iron (Fe2+) catalyzes the Fenton reaction, generating abundant ROS, particularly highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Excessive ROS directly attack cell membranes and mitochondrial membranes rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), initiating chain reactions of lipid peroxidation that drive ferroptosis (81).

In terms of lipid metabolism, mitochondria are the primary site for fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) (82). Mitochondrial fragmentation may downregulate or compromise the function of carnitine/organic cation transporter 2 (OCTN2), thereby restricting the import of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix (83). This functional impairment is not caused by direct physical damage from fragmented mitochondria, but instead arises from a multimechanistic cascade initiated by ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial fragmentation. First, lipid peroxidation products derived from fragmented mitochondrial membranes can covalently modify OCTN2 or disrupt the lipid microenvironment of the plasma membrane that is indispensable for its physiological function (84). Second, mitochondrial stress induced by fragmentation upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which in turn suppress OCTN2 transcription through NF-κB and STAT3 signaling pathways (85). Collectively, these sequential mechanisms converge to attenuate OCTN2 activity, limiting mitochondrial uptake of long-chain fatty acids and further aggravating metabolic dysregulation during ferroptosis. Key enzyme activities (e.g., carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A, CPT1A) are reduced in fragmented mitochondria, hindering the conversion of fatty acids to acylcarnitines and their subsequent oxidation (86,87). Abnormal activity of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) can also affect CPT1A activity and FAO progression via malonyl-CoA levels. Inhibition of FAO leads to accumulation of long-chain fatty acids (especially PUFAs) in the cytoplasm, providing substrates for lipid peroxidation (82).

Regarding phospholipid remodeling and peroxidation susceptibility, long-chain fatty acyl-CoA synthetase 4 (ACSL4) is a key driver of ferroptosis. It preferentially utilizes PUFAs such as arachidonic acid (AA) and adrenic acid (AdA), catalyzing their conversion to fatty acyl-CoA esters (88). These PUFA-CoA esters are incorporated into phospholipids (particularly phosphatidylethanolamine, PE), increasing the PUFA content of membrane phospholipids (65,89). Additionally, PUFA accumulation due to FAO inhibition provides more substrates for ACSL4, promoting the synthesis of PUFA-containing phospholipids. Meanwhile, excess ROS produced by fragmented mitochondria readily attack these PUFA-rich membrane lipids, initiating lipid peroxidation (65). This alteration in membrane lipid composition significantly increases cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis.

Thus, in HCC, mitochondrial dynamical imbalance promotes ferroptosis through synergistic mechanisms: directly disrupting iron homeostasis and catalyzing ROS generation on one hand, and inhibiting FAO leading to PUFA accumulation while increasing membrane lipid peroxidation susceptibility via ACSL4-mediated phospholipid remodeling on the other. These mechanisms collectively cause uncontrollable accumulation of lipid peroxides, ultimately triggering ferroptosis in HCC cells.

Pyroptosis

Pyroptosis is an inflammatory form of programmed cell death executed by gasdermin proteins (e.g., GSDMD), characterized by plasma membrane pore formation, cell swelling and rupture, and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-18) (66). In HCC, mitochondrial dysfunction significantly promotes pyroptosis and influences disease progression by shaping the immune microenvironment (67). Dysfunctional mitochondria release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including mtROS, mtDNA, and cardiolipin, which can directly activate inflammasomes such as NLRP3. Activated inflammasomes cleave caspase-1, which then processes GSDMD, its N-terminal fragments form pores in the plasma membrane, leading to cell lysis and release of mature IL-1β/IL-18, directly executing pyroptosis (67). Mitochondrial dynamical imbalance-induced fragmentation exacerbates mtROS production (90). Excessive mtROS not only directly damages cellular components but also activates pro-inflammatory transcription factors like NF-κB, upregulating the expression of inflammasome components (e.g., NLRP3, pro-IL-1β) and GSDMD, forming a positive feedback loop for pyroptosis (68). Therefore, mitochondrial dysfunction (particularly through DAMP release and the fragmentation-driven mtROS/NF-κB axis) is a key driver of pyroptosis in HCC cells.

Mitochondrial dysfunction-induced pyroptosis can exert dual effects on the tumor immune microenvironment (91). On one hand, pyroptosis driven by mitochondrial damage releases large amounts of IL-1β/IL-18, which can recruit M2-type tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), inhibit CD8+ T cell function, and upregulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) to promote invasion and metastasis (67,92). On the other hand, pyroptosis may also exert anti-tumor immune effects. Mitochondrial DAMPs (e.g., HMGB1, ATP) released by pyroptotic cells can activate dendritic cells (DCs), enhancing antigen presentation and T cell activation (93). The shift toward an anti-tumor direction is not spontaneous, but requires specific interventions: combined immune checkpoint blockade or activating caspase 3-mediated GSDME cleavage through drugs like oxaliplatin (94), converting it from a pro-tumor mode to pyroptosis-mediated immune activation. In addition, directly neutralizing IL-1β or depleting MDSCs can also reverse immunosuppression (95). In summary, the pro-tumor effects of pyroptosis dominate in HCC, while anti-tumor effects require external interventions to be realized.

Autophagy

The interaction between mitochondrial dysfunction and autophagy is a core aspect of cellular stress response, exhibiting complex bidirectional regulation in HCC (96,97). Mitochondrial abnormalities (e.g., reduced ATP synthesis, excessive ROS production, collapse of membrane potential, and disrupted calcium homeostasis) dynamically regulate autophagic activity through multi-layered signaling networks (98,99).

Mitochondrial dynamics imbalance, particularly DRP1-mediated excessive fission, is a key trigger of autophagy initiation in HCC. On one hand, excessive mitochondrial fragmentation impairs OXPHOS, leading to ATP depletion. This activates AMPK, which directly inhibits mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), which is a central nutrient/energy sensor that suppresses autophagy (100,101). The inactivation of mTORC1 relieves its inhibitory effect on the ULK1 complex, thereby promoting autophagosome formation (102). On the other hand, aberrant activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in HCC drives DRP1 hyperphosphorylation at serine 616 (S616) and reduced inhibitory phosphorylation at serine 637 (S637), exacerbating mitochondrial fission and fragmentation (103). This morphological disruption further amplifies ROS production, which activates transcription factors such as HIF-1α and NF-κB to upregulate autophagy-related gene expression (69). For damaged mitochondria, a decrease in membrane potential induces the accumulation of PINK1 on the OMM, recruiting Parkin for ubiquitination of mitochondrial proteins. These labeled mitochondria are then targeted to autophagosomes via adaptor proteins (e.g., p62/SQSTM1 binding to LC3) for selective clearance (mitophagy) (104-106).

Notably, severe mitochondrial dysfunction can paradoxically impair autophagic flux. Profound ATP depletion hinders the activation of the class III PI3K complex (essential for autophagosome membrane elongation) and lysosomal proton pump function (critical for degradation) (107). Excessive ROS also oxidatively modifies key autophagy proteins (e.g., ATG4, Beclin-1), disrupting their function and blocking autophagy progression (70); additionally, mitochondrial dysfunction may reduce lysosomal membrane stability or enzymatic activity, inhibiting autophagosome-lysosome fusion (108).

Mitochondrial dynamics-driven autophagy exerts a dual role in HCC progression. Moderate autophagy (including mitophagy) clears dysfunctional mitochondria, maintains cellular homeostasis, and provides energy/biosynthetic precursors (e.g., amino acids, fatty acids) for HCC cells under metabolic stress (109). In contrast, autophagic dysregulation promotes tumorigenesis: autophagy deficiency leads to accumulation of damaged mitochondria, exacerbating ROS production, genomic instability, and pro-inflammatory signaling (110); excessive autophagy enhances HCC cell survival under nutrient deprivation or chemotherapeutic stress by sustaining metabolic support (109).

Collectively, mitochondrial dynamics imbalance (e.g., DRP1-mediated excessive fission) is a pivotal regulator of autophagy in HCC, with the crosstalk between these two processes shaping tumor cell fate. Targeting key mediators of mitophagy (e.g., PINK1/Parkin) or modulating autophagic flux (e.g., lysosomal function regulators) represents a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC.

Cuproptosis

Copper (Cu) is an essential cofactor for mitochondrial respiration and antioxidant defense (111). In HCC, the hypoxic microenvironment resulting from mitochondrial dynamical imbalance upregulates the copper transporter CTR1 via HIF-1α, promoting copper influx. Simultaneously, the copper efflux pump ATP7B is inactivated due to promoter hypermethylation or mutation, collectively leading to abnormal mitochondrial copper accumulation and triggering cuproptosis (71). Abnormally accumulated Cu2+ is adsorbed into the matrix by the mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced to Cu+ within mitochondria. Cu+ specifically binds to the lipoylation sites of DLAT, a key enzyme in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, inducing conformational changes and abnormal oligomerization. Oligomerized DLAT causes misfolding of the mitochondrial lipoylated protein network (including iron-sulfur cluster proteins), forming insoluble aggregates and disrupting metabolic function (72). Concurrently, Cu+ can also generate ROS explosively via the Fenton reaction, directly oxidizing and damaging mitochondrial DNA and membrane phospholipids (112). ROS also activate BAX/BAK channels, inducing mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) and releasing pro-apoptotic factors such as Cyt C (113). Although cuproptosis, like ferroptosis, is a metal ion-induced cell death, it differs in several aspects, Ferroptosis is driven by lipid peroxidation (Fe2+-catalyzed PUFA peroxidation), whereas cuproptosis is triggered by Cu+ directly binding DLAT, inducing proteotoxic stress (115). Morphologically, ferroptosis is characterized by mitochondrial shrinkage, cristae disappearance, and plasma membrane rupture, while cuproptosis features significant mitochondrial swelling and disintegration of cristae structures, with the plasma membrane remaining intact (116).

As a novel form of regulated cell death, the specific mechanisms of cuproptosis are still not fully understood. Although research on cuproptosis in HCC is still in its early stages, targeting mitochondrial copper homeostasis remains a potential new strategy for HCC treatment.

Therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial dysfunction in HCC

Currently, sorafenib and lenvatinib are the standard first-line targeted therapies for advanced HCC; however, their clinical efficacy is frequently limited by primary or acquired resistance (117,118), which is closely linked to adaptive changes in mitochondrial metabolism and dynamics. In this section, we first elaborate on the therapeutic responses and resistance mechanisms of sorafenib and lenvatinib related to mitochondrial dysfunction, then deeply discuss the therapeutic effects, mechanisms, limitations, and clinical prospects of mitochondrial-targeted agents including Mdivi-1, rapamycin, and tetrathiomolybdate, and finally summarize the challenges and future directions of this therapeutic field.

First-line targeted therapies for HCC and their correlation with mitochondrial dysfunction

Sorafenib, a multi-kinase inhibitor targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), and Raf kinases, was the first targeted drug approved for advanced HCC, and lenvatinib, another multi-kinase inhibitor with higher potency against VEGFRs, has become a first-line alternative due to its comparable efficacy and better tolerability in some patient populations (117,118). Both drugs exert antitumor effects partially by disrupting mitochondrial function in sensitive HCC cells, while adaptive changes in mitochondrial dynamics and metabolism are key drivers of drug resistance.

In sensitive HCC cells, exposure to sorafenib or lenvatinib induces severe mitochondrial stress, characterized by reduced OXPHOS activity, excessive ROS production, and cytochrome c release from the mitochondrial intermembrane space into the cytoplasm, which ultimately triggers caspase-dependent apoptosis (119). However, HCC cells often develop resistance to these drugs through adaptive remodeling of mitochondrial behavior, among which the dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics, the balance between DRP1-mediated fission and MFN2/OPA1-mediated fusion, plays a pivotal role.

Resistant HCC cells typically exhibit excessive mitochondrial fission, which not only maintains a hybrid metabolic phenotype (combining aerobic glycolysis and OXPHOS) to meet the energy demand of tumor growth but also activates cytoprotective mitophagy to clear damaged mitochondria, thereby escaping apoptosis (120,121). Specifically, in sorafenib-resistant HCC cells, increased DRP1 phosphorylation at Ser616 and upregulated expression of fission-related proteins (e.g., fission protein 1, FIS1) have been observed, accompanied by a metabolic shift toward aerobic glycolysis while retaining sufficient OXPHOS activity to maintain ATP levels (122). For lenvatinib resistance, higher matrix stiffness in the tumor microenvironment upregulates FIS1 expression (123), triggering fission-dependent mitophagy, and FAM111B-mediated degradation of MFN2 impairs mitochondrial fusion, further tilting the dynamics balance toward fragmentation (124). Collectively, adaptive alterations in mitochondrial dynamics and their coupled metabolic reprogramming constitute the core mechanism driving HCC resistance to sorafenib and lenvatinib, highlighting the potential of targeting mitochondrial dysfunction to overcome drug resistance.

Mitochondrial-targeted therapeutic agents for HCC

Given the critical role of mitochondrial dysfunction in HCC progression and drug resistance, targeting mitochondrial-related pathways has emerged as an important complementary research direction for HCC treatment. The main therapeutic strategies include regulating mitochondrial dynamics, modulating mitochondrial quality control (e.g., autophagy), and alleviating mitochondrial toxicity, with representative agents including the DRP1 inhibitor Mdivi-1, the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin, and the copper chelator tetrathiomolybdate. These agents have shown promising antitumor effects in preclinical studies, but their clinical translation still faces multiple challenges.

Mdivi-1: a DRP1 inhibitor targeting mitochondrial fission

Mdivi-1 is a cell-permeable quinazolinone derivative that specifically inhibits the GTPase activity of DRP1, modulates its phosphorylation status and mitochondrial localization, blocks excessive mitochondrial fission, maintains the integrity of the mitochondrial network, and thereby suppresses HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (125). Unlike non-specific mitochondrial inhibitors, Mdivi-1 exerts targeted effects on mitochondrial fission by directly binding to DRP1, which makes it a potential candidate for reversing mitochondrial dysfunction-related drug resistance in HCC.

In preclinical models of HCC, Mdivi-1 primarily exerts its antitumor effects through combination therapy, which is considered a feasible approach to overcome the limitations of single-agent treatment. When combined with sorafenib, Mdivi-1 can effectively overcome sorafenib resistance in HCC cells with high ZEB1 expression by inhibiting DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission, thereby synergistically inducing apoptosis and suppressing tumor growth (126). In addition, combination of Mdivi-1 with chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy enhances the antitumor activity of CAR-T cells by regulating CD39 expression on tumor cells, which improves the tumor immune microenvironment (127). Mechanistically, Mdivi-1 reduces OXPHOS activity by inhibiting mitochondrial fission, thereby weakening the stemness of liver cancer stem cells (LCSCs), which is a key subpopulation responsible for tumor recurrence and drug resistance (128).

However, the clinical translation of Mdivi-1 is hindered by its off-target effects and neurotoxicity. To address these issues, a hyaluronic acid-modified poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticle delivery system has been developed, which achieves tumor-specific targeting in tumor-bearing mice by recognizing CD44 receptors overexpressed on HCC cells. This delivery system not only reduces the tumor volume significantly but also improves the tumor immune microenvironment by reducing ROS production and inhibiting inflammatory responses, while minimizing systemic toxicity (128). It should be noted that high doses of Mdivi-1 (≥50 µM) may impair mitochondrial complex I function, leading to reduced ATP synthesis and ROS bursts, which poses a risk of neurotoxicity; therefore, the therapeutic window of Mdivi-1 requires precise control in clinical application (129). Currently, there is still a lack of human clinical data on Mdivi-1, and its safety and effectiveness need to be further verified in well-designed clinical trials.

Rapamycin: an mTOR inhibitor regulating mitochondrial quality control

Rapamycin is a macrolide compound that exerts its biological effects by binding to the intracellular receptor FK506-binding protein 12 (FKBP12), forming a complex that inhibits the activity of mTORC1. As a key regulator of cell metabolism, proliferation, and autophagy, mTORC1 suppression by rapamycin relieves its inhibitory effect on autophagy, promotes the clearance of damaged mitochondria through mitophagy, and thereby inhibits tumor growth (130,131). Given that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is widely activated in HCC, rapamycin and its analogs (e.g., everolimus, temsirolimus) have been considered potential therapeutic agents for HCC (132).

However, the clinical translation of mTOR inhibitors in HCC has been extremely limited. Clinical trials have shown that everolimus did not improve the overall survival (OS) of patients with advanced HCC who had previously received sorafenib treatment (7.6 vs. 7.3 months, P>0.05), and the combination of temsirolimus with sorafenib also failed to reach the predefined efficacy threshold (133). The root causes of these clinical failures are multifaceted: first, mTOR inhibition-induced feedback activation of autophagy provides an escape route for tumor cells, as excessive autophagy can promote tumor cell survival under nutrient-deprived conditions (134); second, cytokines [e.g., IL-10, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)] and immunosuppressive cells (e.g., regulatory T cells, TAMs) in the tumor microenvironment reduce the sensitivity of HCC cells to mTOR inhibitors (135); third, selective inhibition of mTORC1 leads to feedback activation of the AKT pathway, which counteracts the antitumor effect of mTOR inhibitors (135).

Currently, the only clearly beneficial scenario for mTOR inhibitors in HCC management is post-liver transplant immunosuppression. Studies have shown that rapamycin-based immunosuppressive regimens can reduce the risk of HCC recurrence in patients who have undergone liver transplantation for HCC, which may be related to its inhibitory effect on LCSCs and tumor angiogenesis (136). Future breakthroughs in the clinical application of mTOR inhibitors for HCC will rely on three key aspects: biomarker-driven patient selection to identify subgroups that may benefit from treatment, combination with autophagy inhibitors or immune checkpoint inhibitors to enhance antitumor efficacy, and the development of multi-target drugs that can simultaneously block the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and its compensatory pathways.

Tetrathiomolybdate: a copper chelator alleviating mitochondrial copper toxicity

Copper is an essential trace element involved in mitochondrial respiration and redox regulation, but excessive copper accumulation in mitochondria can induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage, which is closely associated with HCC progression. Tetrathiomolybdate is a copper chelator that can bind to serum copper (forming albumin-copper-thiomolybdate complexes) and tissue-deposited copper, block cellular copper uptake, promote copper excretion, and thereby alleviate mitochondrial copper toxicity (137). In addition to its role in copper chelation, tetrathiomolybdate also exerts anti-angiogenic effects by inhibiting copper-dependent angiogenesis factors (e.g., VEGFR), which further enhances its antitumor potential in HCC.

However, the clinical application of tetrathiomolybdate is limited by its potential toxicity. In patients with Wilson’s disease (a genetic disorder characterized by copper accumulation), long-term use of tetrathiomolybdate may cause dose-dependent hematologic toxicity (e.g., anemia, leukopenia), gastrointestinal reactions (e.g., nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), and transient elevation of liver enzymes (138) (as shown in Table 2). These toxic effects need to be closely monitored in clinical trials, and the optimal dose and treatment duration should be determined to balance efficacy and safety. Currently, there are few preclinical studies on tetrathiomolybdate in HCC, and its antitumor efficacy and mechanism in HCC need to be further explored.

Challenges and future directions

Current therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial dysfunction in HCC face three core challenges: first, the mechanistic complexity of mitochondrial dysfunction in HCC, which involves multiple pathways (e.g., mitochondrial dynamics, metabolism, autophagy) and crosstalk with the tumor microenvironment, making it difficult to achieve effective treatment with single-target agents; second, insufficient drug delivery efficiency, as most mitochondrial-targeted agents have poor solubility and tissue selectivity, leading to low concentration in tumor cells and high systemic toxicity; third, potential drug toxicity, as mitochondria are essential organelles for normal cell function, and non-specific targeting may cause damage to normal tissues.

Future research directions may focus on the following aspects: first, developing precision delivery systems (e.g., mitochondrial-targeted nanocarriers, ligand-modified nanoparticles) to improve the selectivity and bioavailability of drugs, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity; second, designing rational combination therapies (e.g., DRP1 inhibitors combined with antioxidants, mTOR inhibitors combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors) to circumvent single-agent toxicity and overcome drug resistance; third, developing novel compounds (e.g., highly selective DRP1 allosteric inhibitors, cuproptosis inducers) that target mitochondrial dysfunction more specifically, with higher efficacy and lower toxicity; fourth, identifying reliable biomarkers to predict the therapeutic response of mitochondrial-targeted agents, realizing personalized treatment for HCC patients.

Conclusions

In summary, this review confirms that mitochondrial dynamical imbalance serves as a central driver in the pathogenesis and progression of HCC. Mechanistically, excessive mitochondrial fission mediated by DRP1 and defective mitochondrial fusion regulated by MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 collectively induce mitochondrial fragmentation, which in turn triggers metabolic reprogramming (including enhanced Warburg effect and lipotoxicity accumulation) and oxidative stress characterized by ROS bursts. Additionally, this review underscores that mitochondrial dysfunction modulates multiple programmed cell death pathways in HCC cells, including apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy, and cuproptosis, highlighting the significant potential of precise mitochondrial function regulation as a breakthrough strategy for HCC treatment.

Based on the aforementioned mechanisms, future research should prioritize three unique and complementary directions: first, the development of precision tools for regulating mitochondrial dynamics, such as mitochondria-specific CRISPR systems, to repair mtDNA mutations or modulate nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes (e.g., SIRT3-mediated DRP1 deacetylation); second, the integration of spatial multi-omics technologies to dissect the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment; and third, the establishment of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven dynamic metabolic models to predict treatment efficacy and optimize clinical protocols.
