Biological control of citrus rust mite Phyllocoptruta oleivora by three bacterial species
Authors: Mahmoud M. Al-Azzazy, Saleh S. Alhewairini, Medhat Rehan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The citrus rust mite, Phyllocoptruta oleivora (Acari: Eriophyidae) is a major pest of citrus in most humid regions of the world due to the cost of control and damage caused to fruits. The adverse effects on non-target organisms, the environment and increasing resistance associated with chemical acaricides have increased the need for biological control alternatives. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effect of three bacterial species Streptomyces thinghirensis strain HM3, Streptomyces tricolor strain HM10 and Bacillus subtilis E5 on mortality rate of P. oleivora under laboratory and field conditions. Additionally, we investigated their adverse consequences on the predatory mite, Amblyseius swirskii Athias-Henriot (Acari: Phytoseiidae). The greatest efficiency rate on P. oleivora was noted with S. thinghirensis strain HM3 sprays. Four days after treatment, mite mortality was 89.26 and 84.47% under lab and field conditions, respectively, followed by S. tricolor strain HM10, which showed miticidal activity against mobile stages of P. oleivora. Six days after treatment, mite mortality reached 84.90 and 83.66% under lab and field conditions, respectively. Furthermore, the death rates were noticeably lower (72.60% and 71.25%) using B. subtilis E5 under lab and field conditions, one week after treatment. The predatory mite, A. swirskii, displayed higher capacity for tolerance than P. oleivora to the three bacterial species. The corrected mortality percentages were 17.21, 15.30 and 13.10% under lab conditions and 15.89, 13.41 and 11.76% under field conditions after one week of exposure with S. thinghirensis HM3, S. tricolor HM10 and B. subtilis E5 respectively. S. thinghirensis and S. tricolor exhibited ovicidal activity against laid eggs of P. oleivora under laboratory conditions. Where suppressed hatching of 65.52, 63.26% of laid eggs of P. oleivora, respectively. While B. subtilis E had no impact on the eggs. Exploring the genome of S. thinghirensis HM3 revealed a potential biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) related to the antibiotic actinomycin D production. This pathway contained more than 29 gene classified as regulators, transporters and functioning genes. Besides, possible 7 and 8 proteases and protease inhibitors were identified inside the genome of HM3 and HM10, respectively. This study presents a potential novel approach for biocontrol of citrus rust mite, P. oleivora, highlighting their applicability in P. oleivora population management.

Introduction

The citrus rust mite (CRM), Phyllocoptruta oleivora (Ashmead) (Acari: Eriophyidae) is a cosmopolitan key pest of citrus1. CRM feeds on leaves, green branches and fruit of all citrus species and varieties, penetrating the epidermal cells and resulting in the death of cells and fruit rind russeting resulting in loss in yield and fruit quality making them unfit for export. CRM inflicts dangerous economic damage and can decrease yield by 75–100% in addition to nearly 25% reduction in fruit volume if not treated2–4. In Saudi Arabia, P. oleivora damages all citrus species and cultivars in most growing regions5. The intense use of synthetic pesticides to manage mite pests resulted in increasing resistance among these pests, destruction of natural enemies, abatement of biodiversity, presence of pesticide residues in crops, diminution of beneficial microbes and negative impacts on the environment and human health6–9. These challenges severely limit the sustainable agriculture and farming systems and primarily necessitate the development of non-chemical practices of pest control. For that, farmers are seeking more sustainable solutions, mainly focusing on biocontrol5,10. Biocontrol is a promising substitute and a large variety of microbial biocontrol agents that have been improved in recent years for the controlling of phytophagous mites and insects. Numerous techniques are employed by bacterial biocontrol agents to keep plants from pests and diseases. These include inducing systemic resistance of plants, parasitizing, production of antibiotics, toxins, enzymes and competition for nutrients. Additionally, they are less toxic, highly selective and their pesticidal efficacy is caused by the toxin production or by microbe itself11–13. Pathogenic bacteria serve as a significant source of biological control agents, comprising numerous effective strains. Bacterial bio-pesticides are inexpensive and the most prevalent form of microbial pesticides, and as insecticides, they are also specialized to some pests14. Furthermore, many species of bacteria are opportunistic pathogens that can exist in the environment, which could turn pathogenic if circumstances are favorable15. In Saudi Arabia, various studies were performed on the biocontrol of Tetranychus urticae Koch, by three bacterial preparations of Bacillus subtilis, Acinetobacter sp and Bacillus qassimus under greenhouse and laboratory conditions5. Nevertheless, no research to date has investigated the potential of S. thinghirensis strain HM3, S. tricolor strain HM10 and B. subtilis E5 on phytophagous mites. Streptomyces thinghirensis, essentially found in samples of local soil in Saudi Arabia, has been investigated because of its capacity to promote the plant growth through Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), siderophore production, phosphate solubilization and biological control the phytopathogens. These bacteria have gained attention for their effectiveness in biocontrol, displaying a range of activities that suppress pathogenic microorganisms. Their arsenal of natural, biologically derived compounds positions them as formidable alternative contenders to synthetic pesticides, offering a more sustainable and eco-friendly approach to crop protection13,16–18. Also, serving as potent bioinoculants that safeguard plants from infectious diseases19. In addition, Streptomyces spp. provides plants with essential minerals such as copper, phosphorus, sulfur and iron, further bolstering plant health and resilience against diseases20,21. Furthermore, Streptomyces species emerge as viable biological alternatives to chemical disease management strategies, heralding a new era in sustainable agriculture where the reliance on chemical pesticides is greatly reduced. Besides, Streptomyces produce diverse nematocidal compounds that profoundly affect nematode life cycles, significantly curtailing the damage of these pests inflicted on plant and crop health22,23. However, no previous work examined its acaricidal potential against phytophagous mites.

The current study was dedicated to assessing the effect of three bacterial species: S. thinghirensis strain HM3, S. tricolor strain HM10 and B. subtilis E5 against citrus rust mite (P. oleivora, one of the key pests of citrus), and give particular attention to one of its natural enemies, the predatory mite A. swirskii under laboratory and field conditions. Furthermore, Streptomyces spp. is competent at secreting antibiotic, lytic enzymes, including proteases and chitinases, which contribute significantly to controlling plant parasitic nematode populations and degrading their eggshells22,24.

Results

Effect of three bacterial species on the mobile stages and eggs of P. oleivora

Four days after spraying, mite mortality reached 89.26 and 84.47% under lab and field conditions, respectively. The second strain that was superior was S. tricolor strain HM10, which showed miticidal activity against mobile stages of P. oleivora. Six days after treatment, mite mortality reached 84.90 and 83.66% under lab and field conditions, respectively. Furthermore, the death rates were noticeably lower (72.60% and 71.25%) using B. subtilis E5 under lab and field conditions, when applied for seven days treatment (Tables 1 and 2). Diagnostic characteristics considered as general features of bacterial infection were documented in the current investigation: discontinuation of feeding, incapable of moving, deterioration of the external skin, low rate of excretion after feeding stop, discharge of diarrhoea-like faeces, noticeable variations in color (dark gray color tinged with black) and cadavers turning light black due to the bacterial infection.

The impact of three bacterial preparations on the predatory mite, Amblyseius swirskii

Statistical analysis showed that the decrease percentages of A. swirskii populations were not significantly different among the three treatments (P < 0.001). Evaluation of the three bacterial species on P. oleivora egg hatchability

The untreated control showed an exponential increase in the hatchability of eggs, culminating at 96.87% at the end of the observation period by day 5, surpassing the hatch rates reported in the treated groups (Table 4). In contrast, S. thinghirensis strain HM3 and S. tricolor strain HM10 showed effectively inhibited egg hatchability, reaching 34.48% and 36.74% of laid eggs by P. oleivora, respectively at the end of the incubation period, demonstrating suppression of hatching when treated with these strains. While B. subtilis E5 had no impact on the eggs. Specifically, the initial hatchability rate for this strain was recorded at 25%, on the second day of incubation. This increase persisted, reaching 94.28% by the end of the incubation period. The egg hatchability rates in the control group and the group treated with B. subtilis strain showed similarity, but they significantly differed from the hatchability rates at the other strains. Discussion

The target pest, Phyllocoptruta oleivora, is one of the major mite pests causing severe loss to citrus crops, deteriorating both quality as well as production3,25. Conventional chemical control approaches are often unsuccessful and have detrimental effects on public health. Their repeated use overtime may also result in problems of pesticide resistance and the destruction of natural enemies7,9. Microorganisms create valuable natural products that contain bioactive compounds and natural antibiotics that can be used in various applications. These substances enable them to interact and endure harsh environments26. Microorganisms, such as bacterial pathogens and antagonistic fungi, provide a much safer, more sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative to the commercially available chemical pesticides27,28. Streptomyces and Bacillus bacteria are widely acknowledged as the most noteworthy organisms for the manufacture of natural and bioactive materials29–32.

Several studies revealed that some bacterial species have pesticidal impacts against mite pests and different diseases in a wide range of crops5,33,34. In the present study, we employed three bacterial species S. thinghirensis strain HM3, S. tricolor strain HM10 and B. subtilis E5 to explore their miticidal potential, in addition, their side effects on the predatory mite, A. swirskii. Our study revealed the miticidal efficacy of all three bacterial species against P. oleivora under lab and field conditions. Nonetheless, the highest efficacy on mobile stages of mites was documented in S. thinghirensis strain HM3, followed by S. tricolor strain HM10, and B. subtilis. Similar findings were attained by Al-Azzazy et al.28, who mentioned that the direct application of Acinetobacter sp., Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus qassimus on spider mite, T. urticae, caused a mite mortality of 84.11%, 72.22% and 70.74%, respectively under lab conditions. The findings of Azlay et al.35 are also comparable to the current findings, in implying that Streptomyces violascens has high virulence potential on root-knot nematodes Meloidogyne spp. Bacterial pathogens invade their hosts mostly through the mouth parts and alimentary tract. In other cases, they enter through integument, which impairs the exoskeleton’s essential function in the pests, leading to lethal36,37. Additionally, Bacterial infections possibly classified as toxaemia, or septicaemia, when bacteria multiply in the hemocoel, producing toxins that may cause paralysis of the alimentary tract, resulting in the death of the mite38. In the current investigation, numerous of these factors may have played a role in the mortality of the citrus rust mite, P. oleivora. In a different investigation, Wilson et al.39 stated that bacterial hemolysins are external toxins that target blood cell membranes, causing cell tears and body distension. These hemolysins might also be a factor in the pathogenicity of S. thinghirensis HM3, S. tricolor HM10 and B. subtilis E5 against P. oleivora, particularly given the fast mortality monitored.

In comparison to other ecosystem interventions, the safety and environmental influence of the pathogenic bacteria should be evaluated considering the danger to natural enemies40. The three bacterial species revealed selective toxicity towards the undesired phytophagous mites while saving predatory mites. Practically, less than 20 percent of A. swirskii were killed by the three bacterial species, while S. thinghirensis strain HM3 caused more than 89.26% death in the citrus rust mite population. Higher activity of the three bacterial species towards citrus rust mite, P. oleivora can be clarified by the capability of bacteria to produce multi-metabolites and/or penetrate their thin body wall, inter-skeletal membranes, or through body openings. Once inside, it can affect several biosites causing eventual death. In the case of predatory mites, A. swirskii the exoskeleton is thicker, ventral and dorsal shields41 which might inhibit bacteria from penetrating the bodily tissues beneath them. Also, A. swirskii feeds only on alive individuals who most probably were not exposed to bacteria spray droplets. However, there is no conclusive evidence that the predator obtains the endosymbionts from its prey40. Furthermore, the bodyweight of A. swirskii is about 40 times higher than that of P. oleivora ones that creates weight difference selectivity. All the reasons above interlaced together to enhance the selectivity of the three bacterial species towards P. oleivora over A. swirskii. These findings generally concur with the research conducted by another authors (i.e. Hess and Hoy42, Al-Azzazy et al.28).

Liu et al.43 identified the actinomycin biosynthesis gene cluster (BGC) from the mangrove-derived actinomycete Streptomyces costaricanus SCSIO ZS0073. The cluster contained a set of four genes that direct the synthesis of the 4-methyl-3-hydroxy-anthranilic acid (4-MHA) precursor. Moreover, three non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) produce the 4-MHA pentapeptide semi-lactone, which upon dimerization, imparts final actinomycin D. This cluster located in a 39.8 kb and involved 25 open reading frames (ORFs) include four positive regulatory genes. Osman et al.44 isolated more than sixty-two fungal isolates and among them, four isolates potent control activities against Tetranychus urticae Koch (Acari: Tetranychidae). The secondary metabolites revealed a variety of compounds assigned to mellamide, nodulisporic acid, ochratoxin C, and 7-Oxocurvularin.

Our study showed that S. thinghirensis and S. tricolor significantly inhibit egg hatching under lab conditions. These effects are consistent with other results, against root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne sp.), including those of the Streptomyces hydrogenans strain DH1 that totally inhibits egg hatching45. Additionally, S. rubrogriseus HDZ-9-47 was reported to cause high juvenile mortality and halve egg hatching46. In the current study, we can suggest that the high death rate caused by S. thinghirensis strain HM3, S. tricolor strain HM10 and B. subtilis could probably offer solutions for decrease pesticide use and mites’ resistance to acaricides.

Materials and methods

Culture conditions

Three strains were selected for the current research, two identified strains (Streptomyces thinghirensis HM3 (MN527231), and Streptomyces tricolor HM10 (MN527236))31, in addition to new isolated strain E5. These strains previously showed good activity against many pathogenic fungi and bacteria, reflecting ability to secret many antibacterial, antifungal and antibiotic compounds. The desired strains HM3 and E5 were grown in Nutrient Broth (NB) whereas HM10 strain grown in glucose soyabean meal broth (GSB) containing (g/l) 10 g soybean meal, 10 g glucose, 1 g CaCO3 and 10 g NaCl with pH adjusted to 7.047. The grown inoculated cultures for 72 h at 30 ℃ and 150 rpm, were subjected to filtration with Whatman Filter Paper Grade1 to remove cells. The resulted filtrates were applied against Phyllocoptruta oleivora in Lab and field.

Laboratory trials-residual effects on mobile stages and eggs of P. oleivora

Naturally infested orange fruits were collected from the experimental station of Qassim University, Saudi Arabia. fruits were infected with P. oleivora. Pre-treatment counts all mite’s mobile individuals and laid eggs were recorded before treatment. Orange fruits were directly sprayed with 50 ml of the bacterial preparations of S. thinghirensis strain HM3, S. tricolor strain HM10 and strain E5 utilizing a hand pump sprayer. In the control group, only tap water was sprayed. Orange fruits were arranged on soaked cotton wool (0.7 cm thick) in Petri dishes (15 mm in diameter × 2 mm high). To prevent mites from crawling out, a ring of Vaseline was placed around the lower part of the fruit. To keep the cotton moist, 5 ml of water was added daily to the Petri dishes. Transparent plastic boxes (45 × 305 × 15 cm) were used to hold the Petri dishes and placed in an incubator at 25 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% RH and a photoperiod of12 h light (12L)/12 h dark (12D). Dead mites 24 h following transfer were eliminated and excluded from statistical analysis. Orange fruits were examined daily, and the number of dead mites were noted during seven days. The hatching of laid eggs was recorded in case of only eriophyid mite, P. oleivora, where hatched protonymphs s of P. oleivora were counted every day until the end of hatching in untreated control and hatched protonymphs were removed daily. The presence of bacteria was confirmed by examining mite cadavers under a stereomicroscope (SD30, Olympus, Japan).

Laboratory trials-direct effects on predatory mite, A. swirskii

Colonies of predatory mite, A. swirskii were established with individuals collected from unsprayed orange orchards in Al- Qassim region (26.3489° N, 43.7668° E), in northcentral Saudi Arabia. To acquire egg cohorts of the same age, sixty gravid females of A. swirskii taken from the stock colony were placed in the rearing arenas with about 800 spider mites, T. urticae as prey. These mite rearing arenas were maintained at 55 ± 5% RH and 28 ± 2 °C. After one day, eggs laid by A. swirskii were transferred individually to new rearing arenas, and the newly hatched larvae were supplied with spider mite, T. urticae as prey; suitable numbers of prey nymphs (27 T. urticea individuals) were added daily to the rearing arenas48. Thirty identically aged adult females of A. swirskii were moved to a bean leaf disc including the midrib (6 cm. in diameter), placed face down on cotton kept saturated with distilled water in a Petri dish (10 mm in diameter × 2 mm high), the margins of the leaf discs were covered with moist cotton wool, to prevent mite escape and sprayed with 20 ml of the bacterial preparations of S. thinghirensis strain HM3, S. tricolor strain HM10 and B. subtilis using small pressure spray bottle. For each bacterial species, three replicates were carried out and 25 adult females of A. swirskii were considered per replicate. Mortality among females was recorded 72 h following spraying. Control groups were sprayed with tap water only. Bacteria incident was checked by stereo microscope inspection of A. swirskii cadavers.

Field trial-effects on P. oleivora and A. swirskii

This study was conducted at the unsprayed orange orchards in Al- Qassim region (26.3489° N, 43.7668° E), in northcentral Saudi Arabia. Four groups, each consisting of three orange shrubs (Citrus sinensis L.) (4-year-old) of similar size, vigor and shape with a history of P. oleivora infestations were selected for the study. The experimental research design comprised complete randomized individual trees (replicates). Three replicates were employed for each bacterial species and untreated control. Three different species of bacteria, S. thinghirensis strain HM3, S. tricolor strain HM10 and B. subtilis E5 were freshly prepared from the stock culture (one liter each) and sprayed directly after filtration (900 ml from each strain culture) on orange trees (approximately 300 ml for each tree) using a one-liter volume hand atomizer for testing all mobile stages of eriophyid mite, P. oleivora and compared with the control, as well as their side effects on the predatory mite, A. swirskii. The untreated control was sprayed with well water. Ten orange fruits in each tree were picked at random from all directions, kept in polyethylene bags and inspected under the stereoscopic microscope in the laboratory. For each replicate, a pre-spray count was performed to determine the initial density and distribution of P. oleivora and predatory mite A. swirskii. Observations were made for 1, 3 days and 7 days of post-treatment.

Strain identification and phylogenetic tree

DNA was extracted from strain E5 by the method of Cook and Meyers49. PCR amplified the 16S rRNA region by DreamTaq PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific) using the general primers for bacterial identification (27F and 1492R). PCR product was purified and sequenced (Capillary Electrophoresis Sequencing, Macrogen, South Korea), subsequently, assembled and deposited in GenBank under the accession number PV017724.

The 16S rRNA sequences of S. thinghirensis HM3, Streptomyces tricolor HM10 and Bacillus subtilis E5 were aligned utilizing ClustalW50. The phylogenetic tree was inferred from the sequences retrieved from the BLAST search in NCBI database by MEGA X51, with the following parameters, Tamura-Nei model, Maximum Likelihood and Bootstrap method.

Genome analysis and identification of putative biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs)

Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were identified by submitting S. thinghirensis strain HM3 (GenBank: JAKFGP000000000.1) and Streptomyces tricolor HM10 (GenBank: JAJREA000000000.1) genomes to antiSMASH bacterial version (antiSMASH 7.0, accessed on 9 March 2025)52. Besides, PRISM (PRediction Informatics for Secondary Metabolomes)54 applied to identify unknown secondary metabolite biosynthesis gene clusters in addition to their predicted chemical structures53. Pfam database was implemented as a popular resource for analyzing the genomes for protein families and domains54.

Conclusions

The current investigation is the first study into acaricidal properties of three bacterial species isolated from unexploited ecosystems against citrus rust mite. The current study showed that S. thinghirensis strain HM3 and S. tricolor strain HM10 are efficient in controlling citrus rust mite, P. oleivora populations under field and lab conditions, whereas B. subtilis produces moderate control. Therefore, pathogenic bacteria can be used as a potential microbial control on P. oleivora in a program of integrated pest management (IPM) to control P. oleivora in the orange orchards, offering a viable alternative to conventional synthetic acaricides. Nevertheless, besides their ability to kill mite and inhibitory effects on the eggs hatching in the present study, the variety of bioactive substances produced by Streptomyces spp. includes substances capable of enhancing plant growth55. Moreover, complementary research should be conducted to evaluate the potential of these bacterial species in mite pests’ management programs in orchards as an approach to meeting the increasing demand for nonchemical control strategies.
