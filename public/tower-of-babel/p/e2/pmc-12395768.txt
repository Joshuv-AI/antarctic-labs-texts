Pulse wave-driven machine learning for the non-invasive assessment of coronary artery calcification in patients with end-stage renal disease undergoing hemodialysis
Authors: Yanxin Wang, Lin Yang, Ziwei Li, Xinyu Zhang, Hongyang Zhao, Man Ji, Dongmei Hao, Jie Yang, Chong Wang, Ying Li, Guangfei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery calcification (CAC) represents a major cardiovascular risk in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing hemodialysis. Given that radial artery pulse waveforms can reflect vascular status, this study aimed to evaluate their utility in the non-invasive assessment of CAC severity.Methods58 patients with ESRD undergoing hemodialysis were enrolled. CAC severity was assessed using low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) and classified into four groups based on Agatston scores: no calcification (0), mild (1–100), moderate (101–400), and severe (> 400). Radial artery pulse waveforms were recorded before, hourly during, and after hemodialysis. Key features were extracted based on morphological differences among groups. Statistical inter-group comparisons and intra-group trend analyses were performed. A gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) model was trained to classify CAC severity using waveform features.ResultsClear morphological differences were observed among the four CAC groups. The non-calcified group showed a distinct main wave followed by identifiable tidal waves. With increasing CAC severity, the tidal waves became progressively attenuated and less distinguishable, resulting in a smoother overall waveform, particularly in the severe calcification group. Pulse waveform features exhibited significant variation across CAC groups and over the hemodialysis process, including parameters related to waveform morphology, descending limb, complexity and distribution, mean value, and full-process stereoscopic pulse wave features. The GBDT model demonstrated robust and consistent performance, with an average accuracy of 84.1% and a macro-AUC of 0.962 in fivefold cross-validation, and comparable results (83.9% accuracy, 0.961 macro-AUC) on the independent test set. Notably, the model performed particularly well in identifying Severe Calcification cases.ConclusionsRadial artery pulse wave analysis may serve as a non-invasive adjunct for assessing CAC severity in patients with ESRD undergoing hemodialysis.Keywords: Pulse wave, Coronary artery calcification, End-stage renal disease, Machine learning, Gradient boosting decision tree

Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) is the final stage of several chronic kidney diseases (CKD) and is characterised by severe and irreversible impairment of kidney function [1, 2], resulting in the loss of the ability to remove metabolic waste from the body [3]. As a result, patients are forced to rely on long-term hemodialysis to sustain life. This condition not only drastically reduces quality of life, but is also associated with a variety of complications that significantly increase the risk of mortality [4–6]. Among these, vascular calcification stands out as a common comorbidity of chronic kidney disease and has emerged as a central factor in the development of cardiovascular disease in patients with ESRD [7, 8]. The mechanism is closely linked to a variety of pathological changes triggered by declining renal function. Dysregulation of phosphorus metabolism [9, 10], secondary renal hyperparathyroidism [10, 11], and a chronic inflammatory state [12] collectively drive the progression of vascular calcification and myocardial fibrosis, thereby increasing the incidence of cardiovascular disease. In addition, impaired renal function triggers a cascade of pathological changes, such as abnormalities in lipid metabolism [13, 14], chronic vitamin D deficiency [15, 16], endothelial dysfunction [17], and increased arterial stiffness [18–20], leading to vascular calcification. The accumulation of metabolic wastes and toxins in renal failure exerts toxic effects on the vasculature [21, 22], while imbalances in various vascular active factors, such as increased oxidative stress and reduced nitric oxide bioavailability [23, 24], indirectly promote vascular calcification. Furthermore, hemodynamic instability during hemodialysis has been demonstrated to exacerbate the risk of calcification. Recent studies have also suggested that alterations in body composition, such as reduced muscle mass and increased fat mass, may be associated with elevated cardiometabolic risks in hemodialysis patients, indirectly contributing to vascular dysfunction [25]. Coronary artery calcification (CAC), characterised by the deposition of calcium salts in the arterial walls of the coronary arteries, is a primary area of research interest in the field of vascular calcification. The Agatston score is widely regarded as the clinical gold standard for evaluating the extent of CAC [26]. Higher CAC scores have been shown to be significantly associated with the progression of ESRD and the worsening of renal prognosis [27]. When CAC scores exceed 400, the risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease-related hospitalisation in patients with ESRD is substantially higher [28, 29]. Although the detection of CAC primarily relies on imaging methods, these techniques pose issues related to radiation exposure and high costs [30, 31], and global guidelines do not recommend routine screening using coronary CT [32]. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to identify a non-invasive, convenient, and cost-effective assessment method.

In recent years, pulse wave analysis has been widely recognized as a promising non-invasive tool for cardiovascular risk assessment, and machine learning techniques have been increasingly adopted in biomedical research [33, 34]. For instance, Bialonczyk et al. demonstrated that machine learning-based analysis of brachial or radial artery pulse wave features could effectively identify CKD patients at high risk for CAC [35]. Lyu et al. reported that radial pulse waveform features, analyzed using machine learning models, enabled accurate discrimination among healthy individuals, hypertensive patients, and those with coronary artery disease (CAD), thereby supporting the clinical utility of pulse wave characteristics in CAD detection [36]. Furthermore, Maruhashi et al. found that the upstroke time (UT) of the ankle pulse wave outperformed traditional hemodynamic parameters in detecting CAD [37]. Zhang et al. further explored the use of both linear time-domain and nonlinear multiscale entropy features of pulse waves for assessing coronary lesions [38]. Ejiri et al. confirmed that various pulse wave velocity (PWV) indices—particularly carotid-femoral PWV (cfPWV)—are strongly associated with the presence and severity of CAC [39]. Additionally, Jin et al. proposed that cfPWV can be estimated from single-point radial pulse waveforms, providing a simplified approach for CAC-related PWV measurement [40]. These studies collectively demonstrate a meaningful association between pulse wave parameters and CAD or CAC, underscoring the potential of pulse wave analysis in cardiovascular risk evaluation. Despite recent advances, several limitations hinder the clinical applicability of previous pulse wave-based CAC studies. Many have focused on parameters such as PWV, which require multi-site signal acquisition and distance measurements—challenging to implement in bedside or large-scale screening settings. Moreover, complex signal transformations and the use of less accessible anatomical sites, such as the brachial artery or ankle, further reduce interpretability and routine usability. Notably, most studies relied on binary classification, failing to stratify CAC severity, which is essential for personalized risk assessment.

As demonstrated in previous research, pulse wave parameters are crucial indicators for evaluating the physiological and pathological states of the cardiovascular system [41–44]. These parameters are widely applied in the field of cardiovascular health assessment. The morphological analysis of pulse waves, encompassing the total pulse area and the position, shape, intensity of characteristic points such as the main wave, tidal wave, and dicrotic wave, exhibits a close correlation with cardiovascular status [41, 45–48]. Specifically, the amplitude and position of the wave are primarily determined by vascular elasticity, peripheral vascular resistance, heart rate, and left ventricular function [49, 50]. Evidence also suggests that increased arterial wave reflections negatively impact ventricular afterload and coronary perfusion, with its pathological effects well-documented [51–53].

In this context, this study aims to comprehensively explore the pulse wave characteristics associated with varying degrees of CAC in patients with ESRD undergoing hemodialysis by extracting multiple pulse wave parameters. We systematically analyze the variations in these pulse wave features across different levels of coronary calcification and the changes in pulse wave dynamics during the hemodialysis process. Moreover, we utilize a Gradient Boosted Decision Tree (GBDT) machine learning approach to develop a predictive model based on these pulse wave feature parameters.

The primary objective of this study is to provide a rapid, non-invasive method for assessing the severity of CAC in hemodialysis patients. This initiative is expected to aid in the early identification of cardiovascular risks in ESRD patients, enhance the capacity for early warnings about the dangers of CAC, and provide robust evidence to support clinical auxiliary diagnosis and intervention strategies. This comprehensive approach aims to improve the management and outcomes of cardiovascular health in this vulnerable population.

Results of GBDT model

The performance of the GBDT model was evaluated using both fivefold cross-validation and an independent test set. Across the five folds, the model achieved a mean accuracy of 0.841 ± 0.039, macro-average recall of 0.825 ± 0.059, macro-average precision of 0.843 ± 0.056, and macro-average F1-score of 0.828 ± 0.048. The mean macro-average AUC was 0.962 ± 0.024, while Cohen’s kappa and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) were 0.777 and 0.779, respectively, indicating stable classification performance across different validation splits (Table 3). On the independent test set, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.839, macro-average recall of 0.847, macro-average precision of 0.836, and macro-average F1-score of 0.831. The macro-average AUC was 0.961, with Cohen’s kappa and MCC values of 0.776 and 0.782, respectively (Table 4). The confusion matrix (Fig. 6A) illustrates the model’s classification capability across the four CAC severity categories, while the ROC curves (Fig. 6B) show class-wise AUCs of 0.98 (No), 0.92 (Mild), 0.95 (Moderate), and 0.99 (Severe), confirming the model’s discriminative power.

Discussion

In this study, patients with ESRD were classified into four distinct CAC groups based on LDCT imaging calcification scores. The differences in pulse wave characteristics during hemodialysis were analyzed, and a GBDT model was developed to classify CAC severity using pulse wave features. The results indicate significant differences among patient groups in the waveform morphology parameters, descending limb parameters, waveform complexity and distribution parameters, waveform mean value parameters, and full-process stereoscopic pulse wave parameters. Furthermore, the GBDT model demonstrated stable and high performance in classifying CAC severity among ESRD patients, as evidenced by consistent results from both fivefold cross-validation and independent test set evaluation. The cross-validation results yielded a mean accuracy of 0.841 (± 0.039) and a macro-average AUC of 0.962 (± 0.024), while the independent test set showed comparable metrics (accuracy = 0.839, macro-AUC = 0.961). These results suggest that the severity of CAC in ESRD patients exerts distinguishable effects on the cardiovascular system, which can be effectively captured by radial artery pulse wave characteristics.

During hemodialysis, both hemodynamic stress, including coronary flow reserve (CFR), and electrolyte imbalances contribute to the progression of CAC [9, 54]. CAC contributes to increased arterial stiffness, impaired myocardial perfusion, and cardiac load imbalance, which significantly deteriorate hemodynamic stability. As a hemodynamic indicator, pulse wave characteristics exhibit a strong association with the severity of CAC. In comparison to the other groups, patients in the No Calcification group demonstrated enhanced vascular elasticity, enabling their arterial walls to swiftly revert to their original state following the closure of the aortic valve [55, 56]. This superior elastic recoil property resulted in a pronounced pulse wave peak at the aortic valve closure point, whilst concomitantly augmenting the arterial wall's attenuation of the pulse wave. The vessels in the No Calcification group exhibited enhanced compliance, characterised by reduced pulse wave velocity (PWV), enabling more agile adaptation to blood flow fluctuations and thereby preserving haemodynamic stability [57, 58]. This was evident in the pulse wave characteristics, which demonstrated increased complexity, a more pronounced and earlier tidal wave, a leftward shift in the NB, and an increase in PB. Consequently, higher SampleEn, Kurtosis, and Skewness values were observed, indicating more pronounced pulse waveform asymmetry, steeper distributions, and enhanced pulse waveform complexity. Notably, throughout the hemodialysis process, the waveform parameters in the No Calcification group exhibited no significant alterations, indicative of their robust vascular adaptability.

As coronary calcification progresses, cardiovascular function progressively deteriorates, with calcified plaques leading to reduced fractional flow reserve (FFR) and impaired ejection function [59]. Severe Calcification patients exhibit compromised vascular function and hemodynamic abnormalities, characterized by increased arterial stiffness and diminished elastic recoil [60, 61]. Consequently, the main wave duration is prolonged, with the main wave peak appearing later, while the tidal wave becomes progressively elevated and merges with the main wave. Sometimes, the tidal wave may exceed the main wave, causing the main wave to become less distinct, leading to a single-peaked, rounded waveform, which is associated with reduced regularity of the pulse wave in severe calcification patients.

For patients with ESRD, regular hemodialysis treatment, conducted three times a week for four hours each session, is not only a crucial life-sustaining alternative therapy but also a necessary foundation for their long-term survival. During the hemodialysis process, patients typically experience a progressive decline in circulating blood volume. The patients with CAC in ESRD, however, the significantly reduced vascular wall compliance results in marked differences in their physiological responses to volume changes. It is noteworthy that these physiological response differences may be often indirectly observable through changes in the arterial pulse wave.

Although previous studies have primarily focused on using biochemical markers [62–64] (such as serum phosphate and the fibrinogen) and imaging techniques [65, 66] (including CT and echocardiography) to assess coronary calcification in patients with ESRD, there has been limited research systematically analyzing the vascular condition of patients before, during, and after hemodialysis. Additionally, studies that comprehensively evaluate the degree of coronary calcification based on pulse wave feature parameters are also relatively scarce.

Our study, in contrast, systematically measured pulse waves at hourly intervals throughout the entire hemodialysis process, from pre-hemodialysis to post-hemodialysis, extracting pulse wave parameters from multiple angles. This allowed for a comprehensive analysis of the systematic impact of different degrees of CAC on the pulse wave characteristics in hemodialysis patients. Furthermore, a GBDT model was developed based on pulse wave parameters to classify the severity of calcification. The results further indicate that pulse wave parameters could serve as a novel method for assessing CAC in patients with ESRD.

This study proposes a simple and effective method for the assessment of CAC, with promising clinical application prospects. The core innovation lies in the use of radial artery pulse waveform analysis—a technique that is convenient, noninvasive, low-cost, and requires minimal patient cooperation—making it particularly suitable for the vulnerable ESRD population, who often experience frailty, malnutrition, and reduced quality of life due to long-term dialysis treatment [67]. This approach allows clinicians to rapidly acquire pulse wave data during routine examinations without the need for complex or invasive equipment. By analyzing key waveform features such as the amplitude, slope, and mean values of the main and tidal waves, clinicians can intuitively observe waveform changes and preliminarily determine their association with CAC severity. Furthermore, by incorporating these pulse wave characteristics into a machine learning model, the study enables automated risk stratification and rapid grading of CAC, providing an efficient screening tool and clinical decision support. Based on the predicted risk category, the model can recommend whether further imaging examinations (such as CT or coronary angiography) are warranted, thus facilitating early identification and timely intervention for CAC and reducing the risk of adverse cardiovascular events caused by diagnostic delays. Additionally, this pulse wave-based approach is sensitive to hemodynamic fluctuations caused by volume changes during hemodialysis, making it useful for dynamically monitoring cardiovascular status in real time. This assists clinicians in adjusting treatment strategies promptly, thereby improving the individualised management and therapeutic precision for ESRD patients with CAC.

Despite proposing a pulse wave analysis approach for assessing CAC in hemodialysis-dependent ESRD patients, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size was limited, as only a small number of long-term hemodialysis patients from a single center were included, resulting in data imbalance across groups. Future research should expand the cohort and optimize group allocation. Second, although pulse wave data from multiple acquisition time points were included, their individual contributions were not evaluated separately. Future studies should investigate the predictive significance of pulse wave features at each time point to determine the optimal timing for CAC evaluation. Third, this study did not include key clinical variables such as calcium-phosphorus metabolism, inflammatory markers, or nutritional status, thus limiting the model’s comprehensiveness. Future efforts should integrate pulse wave parameters with relevant laboratory indices and clinical data to develop more comprehensive and mechanism-based predictive models.

Materials and methods

Patients

A retrospective study was conducted on patients with ESRD who underwent hemodialysis at the Hemodialysis Center of the Department of Nephrology at Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University, from February 2023 to August 2023. The inclusion criteria for the study participants were as follows: Patients had a diagnosis of ESRD, with a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) lower than 15 ml/(min·1.73 m2). They had received hemodialysis for a minimum of 3 months and were 18 years old or older. The exclusion criteria were as follows: Patients were excluded if they had any of the following conditions: chronic atrial fibrillation or arrhythmias, a myocardial infarction within 1 month prior to the start of the study, congestive heart failure, or acute or malignant diseases. The final study population included 58 patients with ESRD undergoing hemodialysis.

Pulse wave collection

Prior to data acquisition, all participants were instructed to remain seated and motionless for five minutes to stabilize their hemodynamic status. In the hemodialysis room, pulse wave signals were collected from the radial artery of the non-fistula arm in patients. A strain gauge pressure sensor was used to capture the waveform, secured in place over the radial artery using an elastic band to ensure signal stability and minimize motion artifacts. Signal acquisition was performed using the PowerLab data acquisition system (AD Instruments Pty. Ltd., Power Lab 8/35, Bella Vista NSW 2153, Australia). The data channel was configured with a digital low-pass filter set at 50 Hz, and a sampling frequency of 1 kHz. Acquired pulse waveforms were displayed and recorded in real time using LabChart software. During each standard hemodialysis session, pulse wave signals were recorded at Pre-HD, 1 h-HD, 2 h-HD, and 3 h-HD, as well as Post-HD. At each time point, a continuous 5-min recording was obtained to ensure sufficient waveform data for feature extraction and subsequent analysis.

Low-dose computed tomography for CAC assessment

This study employed low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) to evaluate CAC within the study population. CAC scores (CACS) were determined using the Agatston scoring method [68]. To extract single pulse waveforms for morphological analysis, the original radial artery pulse signals were preprocessed using MATLAB R2023a. As illustrated in Fig. 7C, a pulse wave is typically comprised of three main peaks: point A denotes the main wave peak, point B represents the tidal wave peak, and point C indicates the dicrotic wave peak. Pulse waves are comprised of two limbs: an ascending limb and a descending limb. The ascending limb signifies the sudden expansion of the artery during ventricular systole, whereas the descending limb indicates ventricular diastole. In this study, seven primary pulse wave characteristics were extracted, including waveform morphology parameters, descending limb parameters, waveform complexity and distribution parameters, waveform mean value parameters, and full-process stereoscopic pulse wave parameters.

The waveform morphology parameters include NA, NB, NC, PA, PB, PC, where NA represents the main wave peak position, NB indicates the tidal wave peak position, NC denotes the dicrotic wave peak position, PA represents the main wave peak amplitude, and PB indicates the tidal wave peak amplitude, PC represents the dicrotic wave peak amplitude, as shown in Fig. 7C.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of the characteristic parameters were performed using SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0, IBM, Inc.). The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess non-parametric differences in pulse wave parameters across calcification groups at the same time points (between-group comparisons), as well as for evaluating changes over time within each group (within-group comparisons). When the overall test indicated statistical significance (p < 0.05), post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Dunn–Bonferroni method to adjust for multiple testing. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model construction

GBDT model was developed to classify CAC into four severity categories in patients with ESRD. The model was based on 290 samples comprising pulse wave features and their corresponding acquisition time points (Pre-HD, 1 h-HD, 2 h-HD, 3 h-HD and Post-HD). All features were standardised using Z-score normalisation prior to modelling. Model development was conducted in two stages to ensure robustness and generalisability. In the first stage, fivefold stratified cross-validation combined with grid search was used to optimise the model's hyperparameters. During each fold, the training subset was resampled using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to address class imbalance. The optimal hyperparameter combination was selected by maximising the macro-average F1-score, which provides a balanced evaluation across all classes in the presence of class imbalance. Evaluation metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, macro-average area under the ROC curve (AUC), Cohen’s kappa, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) were calculated across folds to assess performance stability. In the second stage, an independent evaluation was performed. The entire dataset was randomly partitioned into training and test sets using stratified sampling with a 7:3 ratio. SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training data to prevent information leakage. The GBDT model with optimal parameters was retrained on the resampled training set and evaluated on the independent test set. Performance metrics were computed using the same criteria, and model outputs were visualised using a confusion matrix and multi-class receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. This two-stage modelling strategy enabled both internal validation and external testing, thereby enhancing the credibility and robustness of the proposed classification model.

Conclusions

This study categorized ESRD hemodialysis patients into four groups according to the degree of CAC, focusing on analyzing the difference of pulse wave parameters at the same hemodialysis time point and the change of pulse wave with the hemodialysis process. GBDT model was then used to assess the degree of CAC by pulse wave parameters. Significant differences in pulse wave parameters were observed among hemodialysis patients with varying degrees of calcification, and the GBDT model performed well in assessing CAC. This study highlights the potential application of pulse wave characteristics in CAC evaluation and confirms the feasibility of non-invasive assessment, providing an important reference for the future development of non-invasive CAC detection technologies.
