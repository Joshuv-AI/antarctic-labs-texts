Stepwise targeting hyaluronic acid-based polymeric prodrug for acute kidney injury and prevents its progression to chronic kidney disease
Authors: Li-Bin Zhou, Xuan-Ze Peng, Hui-Min Long, Jing-Bo Hu, Jian-Ting Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is characterized by a sudden decline in kidney function, often due to ischemia-reperfusion or nephrotoxic drugs. A key factor in AKI development is mitochondrial dysfunction, which disrupts energy and oxygen supply, increases ROS generation, and triggers inflammation. Addressing AKI through mitochondrial targeting remains challenging. Melatonin (MLT), a hormone secreted by the pineal gland, shows promise in AKI treatment. To enhance site-specific antioxidative efficacy, triphenylphosphonium-hyaluronic acid (TPP-HA), targeting CD44 receptors and mitochondria, was synthesized and linked to MLT via a cleavable linker, creating TPP-HA-TK-MLT (THTM). THTM improved MLT targeting in renal tubular epithelial cells through CD44 receptors and enhanced mitochondrial distribution via TPP. This effectively suppressed mitochondrial ROS under pathological conditions, ameliorated mitochondrial permeability and swelling, and increased resistance to stimuli such as ischemia-reperfusion injury or nephrotoxic drugs, reducing cell apoptosis. Moreover, THTM significantly reduced the progression of folic acid-induced kidney injury to chronic kidney disease, demonstrating its potential to lower the risk of chronic kidney disease.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Renal tubular epithelial cells, Mitochondria, Melatonin, Ischemia-reperfusion, Cisplatin

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI), a disorder marked by an abrupt decline in renal function, affects over 5000 individuals per million annually and results in more than 1.7 million deaths globally each year [1]. AKI manifests clinically as a rapid decline in the kidney's ability to excrete waste, characterized by reduced urine output and increased retention of products from nitrogen metabolism [2]. In many instances, reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can interact with lipids, nucleic acids, and proteins to induce oxidative stress and inflammation, play a significant role in the development of AKI. Normally, small quantities of ROS are produced during oxidative metabolism in healthy kidneys and are well-tolerated without adverse effects. However, excessive ROS production, either by renal-infiltrating cells or through endogenous sources, can lead to kidney damage and ultimately AKI [3]. With only renal dialysis and transplantation available as specific treatments, targeting ROS has become a primary focus in AKI prevention strategies. The antioxidant N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) has previously been reported to effectively prevent contrast-induced AKI [4,5]. Subsequent research aimed at validating the efficacy of NAC in AKI prevention has been conducted, yet it has left medical professionals unsure about the drug's preventive benefits.

Recent advancements in nanotechnology have facilitated the development of innovative therapies for diseases associated with ROS, utilizing a range of functional nanomaterials including carbon, ceria, platinum, and redox polymers [[6], [7], [8]]. Nonetheless, due to their substantial size, most of these nanoparticles are indiscriminately absorbed by the mononuclear phagocyte system (such as the liver and spleen) and are not readily taken up by the kidneys because of the glomerular filtration barrier. Ultra-small nanoparticles such as quantum dots, and gold nanoparticles, which have hydrodynamic diameters below the renal filtration threshold, can traverse the glomerulus and are excreted within a specified timeframe. However, these nanoparticles generally do not possess antioxidant properties. Nagasaki and colleagues developed pH-responsive nanoparticles that encapsulate nitroxide radicals, functioning as effective ROS scavengers and providing significant alleviation of AKI [9]. Despite reducing the side effects of nitroxide radicals by confining them within the core of the nanostructures, unintended release of these radicals in non-targeted areas might harm healthy tissues or organs. Furthermore, the overall biocompatibility of these nanoparticles, which contain potentially harmful derivatives, remains a critical issue. Thus, the thoughtful creation and development of new antioxidative nanomaterials that are both highly renal-accumulative and low in toxicity are crucial for effectively scavenging ROS and preventing AKI.

Polymeric prodrug is a distinctive formulation wherein therapeutic drugs are covalently linked to water-soluble polymeric materials, thereby profoundly altering the physicochemical properties and in vivo behavior of the parent drug [10,11]. Compared to free drugs, polymeric prodrugs can significantly prolong drug circulation time, enhance tissue distribution and accumulation, and improve bioavailability. The design of such drugs generally adheres to several principles: 1) Chemical bonds should exhibit sufficient stability in the extracellular environment to prolong drug activity in vivo. 2) Once inside cells, bonds (unstable under conditions such as acidity, alkalinity, or enzymatic action) should swiftly degrade, releasing intact drug molecules. 3) Polymer carriers should exhibit adequate stability in the bloodstream circulation. Leveraging the aforementioned characteristics of polymeric prodrugs, drug is conjugated with hydrophilic polymeric carrier materials to enhance drug half-life and distribution, thereby achieving active targeting delivery to renal tubular epithelial cells.

Previous studies have indicated that the adhesion factor CD44 is either absent or minimally expressed in normal renal tissues. However, in certain proliferative and inflammatory renal diseases such as mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, and renal ischemia-reperfusion injury, abnormal overexpression of the CD44 protein has been observed [12,13]. The aberrant expression of CD44 protein is closely associated with mesangial cell activation and proliferation, as well as the infiltration of inflammatory cells in renal glomeruli. Experimental findings suggested that under the influence of its ligand hyaluronic acid (HA), CD44 promoted the secretion of inflammatory mediators such as Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1) by renal tubular epithelial cells, indicating the potential involvement of CD44 expression in the inflammatory response of renal tubulointerstitium. HA is one of the natural ligands specific to the adhesion factor CD44, possessing good water solubility, numerous hydroxyl, and carboxyl groups, thus providing a promising avenue for the development of renal tubular epithelial cell-targeted prodrugs [[14], [15], [16]]. Building upon this foundation, further modifications were made to polymeric prodrugs to incorporate mitochondria-targeting moieties. Triphenyl phosphine (TPP) stands as one of the most successful mitochondria-targeting molecules in current research. With its three phenyl rings, TPP increases molecular surface area, forming a delocalized positive charge that effectively penetrates the hydrophobic membrane of mitochondria [17]. Under the mediation of TPP, melatonin, actively transported into renal tubular epithelial cells, is further transported to mitochondria to exert its role in scavenging excessive ROS, and reverse the progression of AKI.

Building upon the aforementioned research background, this study first conjugated hyaluronic acid with the mitochondria-targeting moiety triphenyl phosphine (TPP), creating a carrier material (TPP-HA) with sequential targeting capabilities for CD44 and mitochondria. Subsequently, TPP-HA was linked to melatonin via a thioketal bond to form TPP-HA-TK-MLT polymeric prodrug (THTM), enhancing the distribution of melatonin within renal tubular epithelial cell mitochondria and facilitating its effective release under ROS stimulation. This approach enables the efficient elimination and suppression of ROS within pathological mitochondrial environments, improving mitochondrial permeability and swelling while enhancing cellular resistance to oxidative stress and cytotoxic stimuli such as nephrotoxic drugs, ultimately enhancing cell viability.

Materials and methods

Materials

Melatonin (MLT) was purchased from Energy Chemical (China). Hyaluronic acid (HA, molecular weight: ∼4000 Da) was purchased from Shandong Focusfreda Biotech (China). (4-Carboxybutyl)triphenylphosphonium bromide (TPP), indocyanine green (ICG), and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) was purchased from aladdin (China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical (USA). MitoTracker™ Deep Red FM (M22426) was purchased from Thermo (USA). CCK-8 assay kit (C0041), Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (C2008S), CM-H2DCFDA (S0035M), MitoSOX Red (S0061M), Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1062L), pCMV-AT1.03 (D2604) and TUNEL apoptosis assay kit (C1090) were purchased from Beyotime (China). Anti-CD44 (15675-1-AP, 30854-1-AP), anti-KIM-1 (30948-1-AP) and anti-α-SMA (14395-1-AP) antibodies were purchased from Proteintech (China). TGF-β1 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (EK0515) was purchased from BOSTER Biological Technology. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and malondialdehyde (MDA) kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. ATP content assay kit (BC0300) was purchased from Solarbio Life Science.

Human renal tubule epithelial cell lines (HK-2) were procured from Pricella Life Science&Technology Co., Ltd (China), and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin) 37 °C in 5 % CO2.

Synthesis of TPP-HA-TK-MLT (THTM)

Synthesis of TPP-HA

Firstly, (4-carboxybutyl)triphenylphosphonium bromide (TPP, 443 mg, 1.0 mmol) was refluxed with 2 mL thionyl chloride (SOCl2) in 20 mL dichloromethane (DCM) to make an acid chloride. After removal of the resulting SOCl2, it was reacted to hyaluronic acid (HA, 400 mg, 0.1 mmol) with N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA, 155 mg, 1.2 mmol) as the base. The resulting mixture was purified by dialysis (MWCO, 3.5 kDa) against deionized water for 48 h, followed by lyophilization to give TPP-HA as a white solid. The structure was characterized by 1H NMR spectrum in D2O.

Synthesis of TK-MLT

To a mixture of melatonine (MLT, 696 mg, 3.0 mmol) and triphosgene (297 mg, 1.0 mmol) in 15 mL anhydrous DCM was added DIPEA (426 mg, 3.3 mmol) dropwise under argon atmosphere at ice bath. The reaction was warmed to room temperature and stirred for 5 h. After the consumption of MLT, the mixture was concentrated under reduced pressure and redissolved in dry DCM. Then a mixture of TK (982 mg, 5.0 mmol) and DIPEA (775 mg, 6.0 mmol) were added to the above mixture and stirred for another 24 h. The reaction was quenched with 0.1 M HCl and washed with water 3 times, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and evaporated. After purified on a silica gel column, 0.64 g yellow oil was obtained as product TK-MLT, yield 47 %. 1H NMR (400 MHz, Chloroform-d) δ 8.07 (s, 1H), 7.44 (s, 1H), 7.05–6.92 (m, 2H), 5.96 (s, 1H), 4.58 (t, J = 6.6 Hz, 2H), 3.89 (s, 3H), 3.82–3.75 (m, 3H), 3.60 (q, J = 6.4 Hz, 2H), 3.09 (t, J = 6.7 Hz, 2H), 2.89 (dt, J = 10.3, 6.2 Hz, 5H), 1.99 (s, 3H), 1.67 (s, 7H). HR-MS (ESI): m/z C21H30N2O5S2, [M+H]+ calcd 455.1669, found 455.1659.

Synthesis and characterization of HA-TK-MLT (HTM) and TPP-HA-TK-MLT (THTM)

Compound HA-TK-MLT (HTM) was synthesized by esterification reaction between HA acid and TK-MLT alcohol. Briefly, a mixture of HA (200 mg, 0.05 mmol), EDCI (121 mg, 0.63 mmol), and HOBt (400 mg, 0.1 mmol) in 20 mL DMSO/H2O (4/1, v/v) solution was stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Then a solution of TK-MLT (282 mg, 0.62 mmol) in DMSO was added and stirred at 50 °C for 48 h. The resulting mixture was purified by dialysis (MWCO, 2.5 kDa) against deionized water for 48 h, followed by lyophilization to give HTM as a white solid.

The synthesis of TPP-HA-TK-MLT (THTM) was similar as compound HTM, simply using TPP-HA to react with TK-MLT at the same condition. The structures were confirmed by the representative peaks and the substitution ratio were calculated by the integration of them in 1H NMR spectra. The molecular weights of TPP-HA and THTM were measured by GPC. The syntheses of control compound TPP-HA-Hep-MLT (THHM) and corresponding intermediates were similar as compound THTM and characterized by 1H NMR and HR-MS.

MLT release profile of THTM

In vitro release of MLT from THTM was investigated by incubating THTM in 1 mL release medium with H2O2 (10 mM) at 37 °C. At designed time intervals, the mixture was detected by HPLC to monitor the cleavage of THTM with a C18 column. THHM was treated with the same protocol as control. The product was analyzed on a Hypersil GOLD™ C18 column at 20 °C with the elution method (acetonitrile/water, 9/1, containing 0.1 ‰ formic acid, 0.3 ml/min). The column temperature and detection wavelength were at 20 °C and 210 nm, respectively, with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, and an injection volume of 5 μL. HPLC-MS (Thermo Scientific™ Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer) was used for evaluating released components of THTM after incubation with H2O2 for 48 h.

Cell viability

HK-2 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate and cultured in a CO2 incubator for 24 h. Once the cells adhered sufficiently to the surface, different concentrations of THTM prepared in culture medium were added, and the cells were further incubated for 48 h. After removal of the supernatant, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. The culture plate was gently shaken to ensure even distribution of the reagent. Subsequently, the plate was incubated in the incubator for 1 h. After the incubation period, the optical density (OD) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific™ Multiskan FC), and the cell viabilities was calculated.

The effects of THTM on the viability of HK-2 cells after incubated with H2O2 or cisplatin was assessed using a CCK-8 assay [18]. HK-2 cells were incubated with H2O2 (200 μM, 2 h) or cisplatin (10 μM, 12 h), and then treated with THTM for another 24 h. After removal of the supernatant, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. The OD of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the cell viabilities was calculated.

Cellular uptake

To study the cellular uptake and intracellular distribution of THTM using HK-2 cells, we seeded HK-2 cells at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in a 12-well plate and used H2O2 (200 μM, 2 h) to induce oxidative stress damage in the cells. FITC fluorescence probe was used to label THTM (THTM(F)), and THTM(F) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. Following incubation, the medium was discarded, and the cells were fixed with 4 % formaldehyde and stained with DAPI. Fluorescence distribution of THTM(F) within the cells was observed using a fluorescence inverted microscope, and the fluorescence intensity of THTM(F) in the images were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ. In addition, the cellular uptake of THTM(F) in HK-2 cells were examined by flow cytometer. The cellular uptake of THTM(F) in cisplatin-induced HK-2 cells were also examined by fluorescence imaging and flow cytometer.

Co-localization of THTM and CD44 receptors

Immunofluorescence and colocalization analysis were performed to investigates whether THTM enters cells via the CD44 receptor [19,20]. Initially, HK-2 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in confocal dishes and incubated overnight. Cells were then pretreated with either H2O2 (200 μM) or PBS for 2 h. The medium was subsequently replaced with THTM(F)-containing media, and the incubation continued for an additional 6 h. After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and cells were washed twice with PBS. The cells were then fixed with methanol at −20 °C for 10 min. Anti-CD44 antibodies were labeled on HK-2 cells using immunofluorescence (diluted at 1:5000). Images of the green fluorescence signal from THTM(F) and the red fluorescence signal from the CD44 receptor were captured using a confocal laser microscope and analyzed for colocalization [21].

Co-localization of THTM and mitochondria

Immunofluorescence and colocalization analysis were performed to investigates the mitochondrial targeting capability of THTM [22,23]. HK-2 cells are incubated overnight in confocal culture dishes at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. Following this, pretreatment with H2O2 (200 μM) or PBS for 2 h, after which the culture media are replaced with THTM(F)-containing or HTM(F)-containing media, and incubation was continued for an additional 6 h. The solution was then removed, and the plate was washed twice with PBS. Mitochondria are labeled using the Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos kit (EX = 579 nm, EM = 599 nm). A laser confocal microscope is used to capture the green fluorescence signal of THTM(F) and the red fluorescence signal of the mitochondria, followed by co-localization analysis.

Annexin V-FITC/PI analysis

HK-2 cells were seeded at a 6-well plate and cultured overnight. Then, cells were incubated with H2O2 (200 μM, 2 h) or cisplatin (10 μM, 12 h), and then treated with THTM for another 24 h, PBS, MLT, HTM and THHM as controls. After incubation, the cells were harvested by digestion with trypsin without EDTA and collected. The collected cell pellets were washed three times with PBS and then stained according to the instructions of the Annexin V-FITC/PI staining kit [24]. Following staining, flow cytometry analysis was performed, and data were statistically analyzed and graphed using the Flowjo software.

Mitochondrial ROS

The changes of mitochondrial ROS in oxidative stress damaged HK-2 cells were examined by MitoSOX Red. HK-2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in a 12-well plate and cultured in a CO2 incubator. Cells were incubated with H2O2 (200 μM, 2 h) or cisplatin (10 μM, 12 h), and then treated with THTM for another 24 h, PBS, MLT, HTM and THHM as controls. After removal of the supernatant, 1 mL of MitoSOX Red staining solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Intracellular MitoSOX Red fluorescence signals were observed using a fluorescence inverted microscope [25].

Mitochondrial membrane potential

HK-2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in a 12-well plate and cultured in a CO2 incubator. After that, cells were incubated with H2O2 (200 μM, 2 h) or cisplatin (10 μM, 12 h), and then treated with THTM for another 24 h, PBS, MLT, HTM and THHM as controls. Then, cells were incubated with Rhodamine 123 staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After stained with Hoechst 33342, the intracellular Rhodamine 123 fluorescence signals were observed using a fluorescence inverted microscope.

ATP release

HK-2 cells were transfected with 0.5 μg of pCMV-AT1.03 using Lipofectamine 3000 [26]. After that, cells were incubated with H2O2 (200 μM, 2 h), and then treated with THTM for another 24 h, PBS, MLT, HTM and THHM as controls. The intracellular ATP contents were observed using a fluorescence inverted microscope.

SOD and MDA

Cells or tissue samples were harvested and homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer appropriate for SOD and MDA assays. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12 000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatants, which were then stored at −80 °C until analysis. SOD and MDA were measured using a commercial assay kit, following the manufacturer's instructions [27]. The results for SOD activity and MDA content were normalized to the protein concentration of each sample, determined by a BCA assay.

TUNEL

DNA disintegration was identified via a TUNEL assay. After treatment, cell samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde at room temperature, followed by a 5-min PBS rinse. They were subsequently permeated with Triton X-100 solution for 2 min on ice. 50 μL of TUNEL detection solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. After stained with DAPI, the samples were observed using a fluorescence inverted microscope.

Animal

Balb/c mice were acquired from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animal procedures were performed following the ARRIVE guidelines and the standards set by the US National Institutes of Health for the ethical care and use of laboratory animals. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines set by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Ningbo University (NBU20220163).

AKI animal model

IR-AKI mice were established as stated in previous documentation [28,29]. In brief, Balb/c mice were subjected to fasting for a duration of 12 h. Subsequently, under anesthesia, the mice were positioned on a homeothermic plate (at 37 ± 2 °C) to ensure the body temperature remained stable, while both kidneys were exposed through a midline abdominal incision. The bilateral renal arteries were quickly isolated and clamped with non-invasive clamps to provoke ischemia. Successful blocking of blood flow was noticeable as the kidneys transitioned gradually to a uniform, soil-gray color. Post a 40-min ischemic period, the clamps were removed from the arteries to resume blood flow. This led to an immediate recovery of the kidneys' initial light reddish-brown color within a short span, signifying successful reperfusion. Cis-AKI mice were established via a single intraperitoneal injection of cisplatin at 15 mg/kg [30].

Biodistribution

The biodistribution of THTM in IR-AKI or Cis-AKI mice was examined under was imaged through living imaging (PerkinElmer, IVIS Lumina). IR-AKI or Cis-AKI mice were intravenously administrated with 1.0 mg/mL of THTM(F), healthy mice as control. At 6 h post-injection, the main tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested and imaged by living imaging. In addition, the distribution of THTM(F) in kidney tissues of IR-AKI or Cis-AKI mice was detected at 2, 6, 12 and 24 h post-injection. Then, whether THTM(F) was distributed in kidney tubules was detected. At 6 h post-injection, the kidney tissues were harvested, fixed, sectioned and stained with DAPI for observation using an inverted fluorescent microscope.

Pharmacokinetics

Balb/c mice (8–10 weeks old, 25–30 g) were fasted overnight with free access to water prior to experimentation. Animals were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 3) and administered MLT (10 mg/kg) or THTM (equivalent MLT dose: 10 mg/kg) via tail vein injection. Blood samples (∼100 μL) were collected from the retro-orbital plexus at predetermined intervals (0.25, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h) into heparinized tubes. Plasma was separated via centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Drug concentrations in plasma were measured using a validated HPLC method (C18 column, 5 μm, 100 Å, 150 × 4.6 mm) following protein precipitation and filtration (0.22 μm). Pharmacokinetic parameters, including half-life (t1/2) and area under the concentration-time curve (AUC0-∞), were calculated using non-compartmental analysis, with statistical comparisons made between formulations.

Assessment of renal function

The changes in renal function were assessed via determining serum creatinine (CRE) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels. Serum samples were measured by using an automated analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Renal histopathology

In order to evaluate the morphological alterations in the kidneys of the AKI mice, kidney tissues were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The renal tissues were preserved in 10 % formalin for a period exceeding 24 h. Afterwards, these kidney tissues were encased in paraffin and segmented into sections of 5 μm. Following the H&E staining, the sections were photographed with a light microscope.

In situ ROS detection

In this study, mice were first anesthetized using pentobarbital sodium, and CM-H2DCFDA was administered into the renal circulation according to established protocols [31]. Forty-five minutes following the injection, the kidneys were harvested and preserved in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Subsequently, the tissue underwent dehydration in 20 % sucrose for 12 h, after which it was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cryosectioning was performed using a cryostat set to −20 °C, producing 5 μm thick sections. These sections were then mounted on adhesive slides and left to air-dry for 5 min at ambient temperature before further analysis. Finally, after the PBS washes, the slides were sealed and subsequently visualized under an inverted fluorescent microscope.

In vivo safety evaluation

To explore the biological safety of THTM in vivo, the mice were given THTM via intravenous injection, PBS as control. Following 1, 15 and 30 days from the administration, Blood samples were collected for analysis, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE). After killed by cervical dislocation, the main organs including lung, spleen, kidney, heart and liver were excised. The organs were then H&E-stained for further examination to ascertain whether THTM reciprocated any morphological changes on these crucial organs.

Statistical analysis

Data from the experiments were processed using GraphPad Prism 8 software, with outcomes displayed as the mean ± SD. The statistical differences between any two groups were established using the Student's t-test. Any result yielding a P-value less than 0.05 was deemed significant.

Results and discussion

Characterization of THTM

We first designed a mitochondria-targeted hyaluronic acid-based carrier TPP-HA. The synthesis of TPP-HA was illustrated in Fig. S1. Briefly, TPP was first reacted with thionyl chloride to obtain TPP acid chloride, followed by a nucleophilic substitution with HA to get TPP-HA carrier. Its chemical structure and substitution ratio were determined by 1H NMR spectrum in D2O (Fig. S4). The characteristic signals between 7.52 and 7.72 ppm were attributed to the aromatic protons, and the triple peaks at 2.16 ppm belonged to the methylene group on TPP. Meanwhile, the single peak at 1.86 ppm was assigned to acetyl group in HA, suggesting the successfully synthesis of TPP-HA, and the substitution ratio of TPP in TPP-HA was calculated to be about 4/1 (TPP/HA, mol/mol).

Subsequently, three types of MLT-based prodrugs were synthesized, including ROS-sensitive prodrugs of HA-TK-MLT (HTM), TPP-HA-TK-MLT (THTM) and ROS-insensitive prodrug of TPP-HA-Hep-MLT (THHM) (Fig. S1). The structures of small molecules of TK-MLT and Hep-MLT were confirmed by NMR and HR-MS (Fig. S6–9). The successful conjugation of MLT onto the backbones of HA via TK or 1,7-heptanediol (Hep) linker was confirmed by the appearance of peaks at ∼1.8 ppm (attributed to the methyl protons in MLT), or peaks at ∼1.95 ppm (attributed to acetyl group in HA) in HTM, THTM or THHM spectra (Fig. S10–12). The substitution ratio of MLT was calculated to be about 5.0/1 in both HTM and THTM, and 3.7/1 in THHM (MLT/HA, mol/mol). And the MLT contents were calculated to be 14.2 % for THTM and 12.1 % for THHM, respectively. The molecular weight changes of THTM and TPP-HA were further measured by GPC (Fig. S13).

ROS-responsive cleavage ability of THTM

The TK linker in THTM is expected to be cleaved by ROS, thereby achieving an on-demand drug release. H2O2 as a typical and relatively stable ROS stimulator was used to investigate the ROS responsibility of THTM, where the ROS-insensitive prodrug of THHM was used as a control. HPLC was used to monitor the cleavage of TK and release of MLT from THTM in the presence of 10 mM H2O2. As shown in Fig. 1A, THTM exhibited a peak at a retention time (Rt) of 22.4 min. Along with time, this peak decreased gradually and a new peak at Rt = 10.0 min and 13.6 min appeared after 1 h and increased gradually. The HPLC trace of pure MLT (Rt = 10.0 min) and TPP-HA (Rt = 13.6 min) suggested the decomposition of THTM after exposed to H2O2 (Fig. 1B). We further evaluated the component with Rt = 10.0 min by HPLC-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) (Fig. 1C). The abundant ions at a mass/charge (m/z) ratio of 233.1285 stood for the singly protonated, singly charged ion [MMLT + H]+, which further demonstrated the release of MLT. Based on these results, we proposed the activation mechanism of prodrug THTM in the presence of H2O2 (Fig. 1D).

Characterization of THTM. (A) HPLC traces of THTM after incubation with H2O2 at different time. (B) HPLC trace of pure MLT and TPP-HA. (C) LC-MS of THTM in the presence of H2O2. (D) The proposed activation mechanism of THTM in the presence of H2O2. (E) The ROS-responsive drug release behavior of THTM in the presence of H2O2. (F) The turbidity changes of THTM in the presence of H2O2. Data are presented as means ± SD, n = 3.

The ROS-responsive drug release behavior of THTM was quantitatively assessed under oxidative conditions (10 mM H2O2) using dialysis membrane methodology. THTM exhibited characteristic oxidative stress-triggered release kinetics, with 28.87 % MLT liberation occurring within the initial 4 h (phase I), followed by sustained release reaching 83.16 % cumulative delivery at 24 h (phase II). Complete drug release (98.2 %) was achieved by 48 h, demonstrating efficient thioketal linker cleavage under ROS-rich microenvironment. In stark contrast, the non-responsive control formulation THHM (lacking ROS-sensitive linkage) showed minimal MLT release (15.0 % at 48 h), confirming the critical role of the ROS-labile thioketal moiety in mediating targeted drug liberation.

The ROS-responsive release characteristics were evaluated through solution turbidity analysis in PBS and DMEM/F12 media (Fig. 1F). Experimental groups comprised: (1,5) free MLT; (2,6) THHM; (4,8) THTM with 10 mM H2O2 treatment, alongside H2O2-free THTM controls (3,7). Upon oxidative stimulation, THTM-treated solutions exhibited immediate turbidity development, indicative of rapid MLT precipitation through ROS-mediated carrier decomposition. This response demonstrated concentration-dependent kinetics, with complete phase separation achieved within 12 h post-H2O2 addition. In contrast, THHM formulations maintained optical clarity throughout the 12-h observation period, confirming the essential role of the thioketal linker in enabling oxidative stress-triggered payload release.

CD44 receptor and mitochondria targeting activity of THTM

Immunofluorescence was used to detect the expression of the CD44 receptor in renal tubular epithelial cells after induction by H2O2 or cisplatin. The expression levels of the CD44 receptor were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software, with the results shown in Fig. S14. Following H2O2 stimulation, the expression of CD44 on the surface of HK-2 cells was significantly increased compared to normal cells. Similarly, CD44 receptor expression was markedly elevated in HK-2 cells after cisplatin induction (Fig. S14). These findings suggested that CD44 receptor expression significantly increased after induction by H2O2 or cisplatin, making it a promising target for CD44 receptor-mediated drug delivery strategies.

Before conducting the cellular targeting study of THTM, the biocompatibility of THTM with HK-2 cells was first evaluated. The results indicated that HK-2 cells exhibited minimal cytotoxicity after being incubated with THTM at concentrations ranging from 0 to 500 μg/mL for 24 h (Fig. S15). In addition, upon incubation of HK-2 cells with various concentrations of THTM (ranging from 0 to 500 μg/mL) for a period of 24 h, no distinct abnormalities were observed in the cellular morphology (Fig. S16). All the cells in each treatment group retained their normal shape and no morphological alterations, such as cell shrinkage, fragmentation, or dissolution, were detected. These findings suggested that within the tested concentration range, THTM did not induce any significant changes in the morphology of HK-2 cells, thereby further substantiating the superior biocompatibility of THTM in this cell line.

To evaluate the targeting ability of THTM in vitro, a H2O2-induced HK-2 cell model was established. The internalization of THTM by H2O2-induced HK-2 cells was assessed by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy using FITC-labeled THTM(F). Compared to the limited distribution of THTM(F) in normal HK-2 cells, the distribution of THTM(F), THHM(F), and HTM(F) was significantly enhanced in H2O2-induced HK-2 cells. This increased internalization was closely associated with CD44 receptor-mediated active transport in oxidative stress-damaged HK-2 cells (Fig. 2A and B). Furthermore, neither the conjugation of TPP nor the modification of the responsive linker had any noticeable effect on the internalization of THTM(F). These observations showed that the targeting mechanism was primarily driven by oxidative stress conditions and the active transport mediated by the CD44 receptor. In order to further substantiate these findings, flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 2C) was conducted, confirming the increased internalization of THTM(F) in H2O2-induced HK-2 cells, where higher fluorescence intensity was observed in H2O2-induced cells compared to normal HK-2 cells. This result was consistent with the fluorescence imaging results, further supporting the hypothesis that oxidative stress enhanced THTM internalization. In addition, the internalization of THTM(F) was also investigated in cisplatin-induced HK-2 cells, providing further insight into its targeting ability under different stress conditions. Similar to the H2O2-induced model, fluorescence imaging (Fig. 2D and E) and flow cytometry (Fig. 2F) demonstrated the increased cellular uptake of THTM(F) in cisplatin-induced cells. To functionally validate the essential role of CD44, competitive blockade assays with free HA were performed. Remarkably, free HA incubation markedly reduced THTM(F) uptake in both H2O2-induced (Fig. 2A–C) and cisplatin-induced HK-2 cells (Fig. 2D–F), confirming that CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis is indispensable for THTM active targeting.

Building on this functional evidence, fluorescence imaging techniques were employed to explore the co-localization of CD44 receptors with THTM(F) to visualize the receptor-mediated internalization process. This was done to determine whether the entry of THTM into cells was associated with CD44 receptor-mediated active transport. Co-localization analysis of the fluorescence data was conducted using ImageJ software. The Pearson correlation coefficient, which is commonly used in fluorescence co-localization analysis, ranges from 1 to −1, where 1 indicates perfect positive correlation (both entities appear together), −1 indicates perfect negative correlation (the entities never appear together), and 0 indicates no correlation. Fluorescence colocalization analysis confirmed CD44 receptor-mediated cellular uptake of THTM(F) (Fig. 3A). Quantitative evaluation demonstrated significant spatial correlation between green fluorescent THTM(F) and red-labeled CD44 receptors, with Pearson coefficients of 0.66 (H2O2-induced) and 0.74 (cisplatin-induced) - representing 2.64-fold and 3.36-fold increases compared to non-targeted FITC controls (0.25 and 0.22). This precise colocalization pattern substantiated the critical role of CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis in THTM cellular internalization, while the minimal FITC-CD44 association excluded nonspecific binding artifacts.

Building on the understanding of the cellular internalization process, the study next explored the mitochondria-targeting capabilities of the THTM conjugate by leveraging TPP, a lipophilic cationic moiety known to facilitate mitochondrial accumulation. The lipophilic cationic mitochondria-targeting moiety TPP leverages the membrane potential difference between the outer and inner mitochondrial membranes to spontaneously accumulate in large quantities within mitochondria. Drug delivery systems, facilitated by TPP conjugation, can thereby target mitochondria. Upon co-incubation with cells, the mitochondria-specific red fluorescence probe MitoTracker DeepRed binds specifically to mitochondria, emitting red fluorescence upon laser excitation to pinpoint mitochondrial localization. Hence, in this study, THTM was labeled with FITC, and mitochondrial localization was assessed through fluorescence signal colocalization, evaluating the mitochondria-targeting functionality of TPP-modified THTM(F). Mitochondrial co-localization analysis validated TPP-enhanced targeting specificity (Fig. 3B). Quantitative imaging revealed superior spatial correlation between THTM(F) (green) and mitochondrial probes (red), demonstrating Pearson coefficients of 0.80 (H2O2-induced) and 0.74 (cisplatin-induced) - representing 1.38-fold and 1.32-fold increases versus non-TPP modified HTM(F) controls (0.58 and 0.56). The enhanced colocalization confirmed TPP-mediated mitochondrial accumulation, while HTM(F)'s limited mitochondrial association highlighted the essential role of triphenylphosphonium in overcoming mitochondrial membrane barriers. DAPI counterstaining (blue) confirmed nuclear exclusion of both conjugates, excluding nonspecific nuclear interactions.

Kidney-targeting distribution of THTM

Immunohistochemical staining analysis revealed that the expression of CD44 receptors was significantly upregulated in the renal tissues of mice in the IR-AKI and Cis-AKI model groups, characterized by specific brown-positive staining in the membranes of renal tubular epithelial cells and interstitial regions (Fig. 4A). The aberrantly high expression of CD44 receptors in pathological renal tissues may be related to the mediation of inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue repair mechanisms. Studies have indicated that CD44 can activate the NF-κB pathway, promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors such as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), and exacerbating the inflammatory response in the renal interstitium [32,33]. The pathological-specific expression pattern of CD44 in this study not only validates its potential as a biomarker for renal injury but also directly supports the rational design of a CD44-targeted THTM. By utilizing the dynamic upregulation of CD44 receptors in disease states, THTM may achieve precise drug accumulation in damaged kidneys, providing a new strategy to overcome the off-target effects of traditional antioxidant therapies.

To further explore the potential of CD44-targeted delivery systems in renal injury, we next examined the biodistribution of THTM(F) in the IR-AKI and Cis-AKI mouse models. The biodistribution of THTM(F) was evaluated in IR-AKI and Cis-AKI mice models using in vivo imaging system. At 6 h post-injection, major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested and imaged. In vivo imaging (Fig. 4B and C) demonstrated that THTM(F) primarily accumulated in the kidneys of both IR-AKI and Cis-AKI mice, with notable fluorescence observed in the target organs. Minimal fluorescence was detected in heart, spleen and lung, indicating a preferential distribution to the kidneys. Healthy control mice showed significantly lower fluorescence in the kidneys compared to the IR-AKI and Cis-AKI mice. The distribution of THTM(F) in kidney tissues of IR-AKI mice was further examined at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection. Imaging results (Fig. 4D and E) revealed that THTM(F) was initially present throughout the kidney tissues and showed sustained accumulation over time. The intensity of fluorescence in the kidney tissues was highest at 6 h and remained detectable at 12 and 24 h, indicating prolonged retention. Similar distribution of THTM(F) in the kidneys was observed in Cis-AKI mice (Fig. 4D and E).

Fluorescence microscopic analysis (Fig. 4F) of renal tissue sections from IR-AKI and Cis-AKI mice, counterstained with DAPI, revealed distinct distribution patterns of FITC-labeled THTM (THTM(F)). Strong green fluorescence signals were predominantly localized to renal tubules. The tubular-specific distribution of THTM(F) aligns with the pathologically amplified CD44 expression pattern observed in injured renal epithelia. This spatial targeting precision likely stems from dual mechanisms: (1) CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis facilitated by HA-TPP conjugation, and (2) enhanced vascular permeability in damaged nephrons. Such compartmentalized accumulation ensures maximal drug availability at injury epicenters. The absence of glomerular retention further supports the design rationale of size-controlled nanoparticles (<10 nm) to bypass filtration barriers. These findings collectively validate the translational potential of CD44-directed nanotherapeutics for site-specific AKI intervention.

The pharmacokinetics of THTM was investigated in healthy mice. THTM or MLT were injected intravenously at a MLT dose of 10 mg/kg, and their t1/2 and AUC0-∞ were calculated using non-compartmental analysis model (Fig. S17 and Table 1). The pharmacokinetic evaluation revealed distinct disposition characteristics between THTM and free MLT. THTM exhibited significantly prolonged systemic exposure, demonstrated by a 4.57-fold increase in AUC0-∞ (542.486 ± 195.835 ng h/mL vs. 118.679 ± 19.521 ng h/mL for MLT; ∗P < 0.05), and an extended 5.04-fold extended elimination half-life (t1/2, 11.868 ± 1.498 h vs. 2.355 ± 1.136 h for MLT; ∗P < 0.05). The significantly enhanced metabolic stability conferred by the thioketal linker—effectively shielding MLT from rapid enzymatic degradation and renal clearance—validates our design rationale of leveraging ROS-responsive chemistry for drug protection. This protective effect directly enables 4.57-fold greater systemic exposure, suggesting preferential accumulation in pathological tissues. Thus, the thioketal-based platform not only prolongs drug circulation but also potentiates passive targeting to disease sites. Critically, the extended elimination half-life implies reduced dosing frequency for sustained therapeutic efficacy—a pivotal translational advantage. These pharmacokinetic properties, synergized with our release data demonstrating ROS-triggered MLT liberation, establish THTM as a dual-functional nanoplatform that integrates: (1) Circulation longevity for optimized biodistribution; (2) Microenvironment-responsive activation for site-specific drug release at pathological sites.

THTM ameliorated the damaged tubular epithelial cells

To evaluate the protective effects of THTM on cell viability under oxidative stress, a CCK-8 assay was performed on H2O2-treated HK-2 cells. The results, as shown in Fig. 5A and B, demonstrated that THTM significantly improved cell viability compared to the untreated control group. Notably, THTM exhibited superior protective effects on cell viability when compared to other compounds tested, including MLT, THHM, and HTM. This suggested that THTM had a more potent effect in counteracting H2O2-induced oxidative stress in renal tubular epithelial cells. In addition to its protective role under oxidative stress, THTM also demonstrated a significant ability to improve cell viability in cells treated with cisplatin, a well-known nephrotoxic agent. As shown in Fig. S18, cisplatin treatment resulted in a marked decrease in HK-2 cell viability. However, THTM treatment effectively counteracted this reduction, leading to a significant recovery in cell viability compared to the cisplatin-only treated cells. This finding further supported the potential of THTM as a therapeutic agent for preventing nephrotoxic damage.

To investigate the effect of THTM on mitochondrial ROS production under oxidative stress, MitoSOX staining was performed in H2O2-treated HK-2 cells. The fluorescence intensity of MitoSOX, which specifically detects mitochondrial superoxide, was measured to assess mitochondrial ROS levels. The results, as shown in Fig. 5C and D, indicated a significant increase in mitochondrial ROS production in H2O2-treated cells compared to the control group. However, treatment with THTM resulted in a notable reduction in MitoSOX fluorescence intensity, indicating a decrease in mitochondrial ROS production. This protective effect was found to be superior to that of MLT, THHM, and HTM, suggesting that THTM was particularly effective in mitigating mitochondrial oxidative stress. In addition to its effects on H2O2-induced oxidative stress, the impact of THTM on mitochondrial ROS production was also evaluated in cisplatin-treated HK-2 cells. Cisplatin treatment resulted in a significant increase in mitochondrial ROS production, as indicated by elevated MitoSOX fluorescence (Fig. S19). However, THTM treatment effectively reduced the intensity of MitoSOX staining, indicating that THTM could similarly protect against cisplatin-induced mitochondrial oxidative damage. This further supported the potential of THTM as a broad-spectrum agent for mitigating mitochondrial oxidative stress in different renal injury models.

Annexin V/PI flow cytometry was performed to quantify apoptotic cell populations in HK-2 cells. As shown in Fig. 5E and F, H2O2 treatment led to a significant increase in the proportion of apoptotic cells, including both early and late apoptotic populations, compared to the untreated control group. In contrast, treatment with THTM effectively reduced the percentage of apoptotic cells, indicating that THTM significantly protects HK-2 cells from H2O2-induced apoptosis. Notably, THTM demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing apoptosis compared to other compounds, such as MLT, THHM, and HTM, highlighting its more potent anti-apoptotic effects under oxidative stress. In addition to its effects on H2O2-induced apoptosis, the impact of THTM on cisplatin-induced cell apoptosis was also investigated. Cisplatin treatment resulted in a significant increase in apoptotic cells, as indicated by a higher percentage of Annexin V-positive cells (Fig. S20). However, treatment with THTM significantly reduced the proportion of apoptotic cells in cisplatin-treated HK-2 cells, demonstrating its protective effect against cisplatin-induced apoptosis. TUNEL/DAPI fluorescence dual staining was performed to detect apoptotic cells in HK-2 cells. As shown in Fig. 5G, H2O2 treatment resulted in a significant increase in TUNEL-positive cells, indicating enhanced DNA fragmentation and apoptosis. However, treatment with THTM effectively reduced the number of TUNEL-positive cells, suggesting that THTM significantly protects HK-2 cells from H2O2-induced apoptosis. The reduction in the TUNEL-positive signals were markedly greater in the THTM-treated group compared to the other compounds tested, including MLT, THHM, and HTM, demonstrating that THTM exhibits superior anti-apoptotic efficacy in this model of oxidative stress. In addition, THTM showed similar anti-apoptotic efficacy in cisplatin-induced HK-2 cells (Fig. S21).

To mechanistically unravel THTM's anti-apoptotic effects, we probed the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway. H2O2-induced injury triggered Bax activation and cytochrome C translocation from mitochondria to cytosol. As shown in Fig. 5H–J, while free MLT partially suppressed these changes, THTM treatment exerted significantly stronger protective effects: (1) Bax expression was reduced versus H2O2 group (∗∗P < 0.01) and MLT group (∗∗∗P < 0.001); (2) cytosolic cytochrome C release significantly decreased versus H2O2 group (∗∗∗P < 0.001) and MLT group (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), confirming effective inhibition of mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening. These data conclusively establish that THTM's mitochondrial-targeted delivery specifically blocks the Bax/cytochrome C axis, directly linking its subcellular precision targeting to apoptosis suppression. Taken together, these findings confirm that THTM achieves targeted mitochondrial accumulation via CD44/TPP dual recognition, where ROS-triggered MLT release effectively scavenges excessive oxidants, thereby attenuating apoptosis in renal epithelial cells (Fig. 5K).

Mitochondrial functional integrity was quantitatively assessed through Rhodamine 123 staining to evaluate permeability transition pore (MPTP) dynamics - a pivotal event in oxidative stress-mediated organelle failure. As shown in Fig. 6A and B, H2O2 exposure (200 μM, 2 h) and cisplatin treatment (10 μM, 12 h, Fig. S22) induced significant reductions in Rhodamine 123 fluorescence intensity versus controls, respectively, indicative of severe MPTP opening and membrane potential (ΔΨm) collapse. THTM treatment significantly attenuated these effects, confirming THTM's capacity to stabilize mitochondrial ultrastructure and prevent apoptosome activation. Mitochondrial bioenergetic function was evaluated through quantitative ATP imaging using the pCMV-AT1.03 biosensor. As shown in Fig. 6C, H2O2-challenged cells exhibited obvious reduction in ATP-associated fluorescence intensity versus untreated controls, indicating severe oxidative phosphorylation impairment. THTM treatment restored ATP levels to baseline, outperforming MLT, HTM and THHM.

THTM ameliorates kidney injury in AKI mice

Having demonstrated the potent anti-apoptotic and antioxidant effects of THTM in HK-2 cells under oxidative stress, we next sought to evaluate its therapeutic potential in an in vivo model of AKI. The promising results observed at the cellular level, where THTM effectively scavenged excessive oxidants, preserved mitochondrial function, and reduced apoptosis, encouraged us to investigate its protective effects in a more complex in vivo system. Given the critical role of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathophysiology of AKI, we hypothesized that THTM could provide substantial protection against kidney injury in animal models, similar to its effects in vitro. To explore this hypothesis, we utilized two well-established AKI models: ischemia-reperfusion injury (IR-AKI) and cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity (Cis-AKI), both of which are known to induce severe kidney damage via mechanisms involving oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. In this part of the study, we aimed to assess whether the protective effects of THTM observed at the cellular level could translate into improved renal function, reduced tissue damage, and attenuated oxidative stress in these animal models.

IR-AKI mice were intravenously injected with THTM, followed by collecting the serum and kidneys 24 h later for further evaluation (Fig. 7A). The 7-day survival outcomes demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy of THTM in IR-AKI mice. While sham-operated controls exhibited 100 % survival, IR-AKI mice showed severe mortality with only 50 % survival. Treatment with THTM completely rescued survival rates to sham levels (100 %), outperforming both free MLT (83.3 %) and THHM (66.7 %) (Fig. 7B). The renal function was assessed via measuring serum creatinine (CRE) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels. As shown in Fig. 7C and D, ischemia-reperfusion led to a significant increase in both CRE and BUN levels, indicating severe kidney dysfunction. Treatment with THTM significantly reduced CRE and BUN levels, demonstrating its ability to improve renal function in this model. In contrast, treatment with MLT, HTM and THHM had a more modest effect, with lesser reductions in CRE and BUN. Next, we evaluated oxidative stress in the kidneys by measuring SOD activity and MDA levels. Fig. 7E and F shows that ischemia-reperfusion resulted in significantly decreased SOD activity and increased MDA levels, reflecting elevated oxidative stress. THTM treatment markedly reversed these changes, significantly increasing SOD activity and decreasing MDA levels, thereby reducing oxidative stress in the renal tissue. On the other hand, MLT, HTM and THHM treatment showed only partial improvement in SOD and MDA levels.

THTM ameliorates renal fibrosis in AKI to CKD

Therapeutic evaluation in folic acid-induced chronic kidney disease (CKD) demonstrated THTM's superior efficacy in attenuating renal fibrosis and functional decline (Fig. 9A). THTM treatment (days 15/17/19/21) ameliorated serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, outperforming MLT, HTM, and THHM (Fig. 9B and C). Oxidative stress modulation was evidenced by restored SOD activity and reduced MDA content, reflecting effective redox homeostasis restoration (Fig. 9D and E). Histopathological analysis revealed less collagen deposition (Fig. 9F) and α-SMA-positive signals (Fig. 9G), confirming attenuated fibrotic progression. These findings correlated with significant suppression of TGF-β1 secretion, indicating disruption of profibrotic signaling (Fig. 9H and S23). The coordinated improvement in functional parameters (CRE/BUN), oxidative markers (SOD/MDA), and fibrotic indices (collagen/α-SMA/TGF-β1) substantiates THTM's dual mechanism: (1) CD44/TPP-mediated mitochondrial targeting mitigates oxidative injury at the injury epicenter, and (2) sustained MLT release blocks TGF-β-driven myofibroblast activation. This multimodal action effectively interrupts the AKI-CKD transition by concurrently addressing both initial oxidative damage and subsequent fibrotic remodeling, positioning THTM as a promising therapeutic strategy against chronic renal deterioration.

The biosafety of THTM

To evaluate whether THTM induced hemolysis in red blood cells, we co-incubated varying concentrations of THTM with red blood cells and measured the absorbance to determine the hemolysis rate. As shown in Fig. S24, increasing the concentration of THTM from 10 μg/mL to 200 μg/mL did not result in red blood cell rupture, with hemolysis rates remaining below 5 % across all tested concentrations. The biocompatibility of THTM was a crucial parameter for in vivo drug delivery. These findings indicated that THTM did not cause hemolysis in red blood cells within the dosage concentration range.

The biochemical data (Fig. S25) showed that the levels of AST, ALT, BUN, and CRE in THTM-treated mice were comparable to those in the PBS control group at all time points. Specifically, ALT and AST levels, indicative of liver function, remained within normal ranges, as did BUN and CRE levels, which reflect kidney function. No significant deviations were observed, suggesting that THTM does not adversely affect liver or kidney function under the tested conditions. H&E staining results (Fig. S26) revealed no significant morphological alterations in the organs of THTM-treated mice compared to the PBS control group. The histological structures of the lung, spleen, kidney, heart, and liver appeared normal, with no signs of inflammation, necrosis, or other pathological changes. This indicated that THTM did not induce significant tissue damage or adverse effects on the examined organs.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates the therapeutic superiority of the mitochondria-nanoregulator THTM in addressing the critical challenges of AKI management and its progression to CKD. By integrating CD44 receptor-mediated renal targeting with TPP-driven mitochondrial accumulation, THTM achieves spatiotemporally controlled delivery of MLT to injury sites, resolving the dual challenges of systemic off-target effects and insufficient organelle-level antioxidant delivery. Key mechanistic insights reveal that THTM restores mitochondrial homeostasis through three synergistic actions: (1) scavenging pathological ROS via ROS-responsive MLT release, (2) stabilizing membrane potential (ΔΨm) to prevent permeability transition pore opening, and (3) enhancing ATP synthesis by reactivating oxidative phosphorylation.

Notably, THTM interrupts the AKI-CKD transition by suppressing TGF-β1-driven fibrotic signaling and collagen deposition, outperforming free MLT and non-targeted analogs. Pharmacokinetic profiling confirms THTM's enhanced bioavailability and renal retention, while safety evaluations validate its hemocompatibility and absence of hepatorenal toxicity. The dual-targeting design—exploiting CD44 upregulation in injured epithelia and mitochondrial dysfunction as pathological hallmarks—represents a paradigm shift in targeted therapy strategies for nephroprotection. These findings position THTM as a clinically translatable candidate, offering a unified solution for acute injury mitigation and chronic progression blockade through precision organelle rehabilitation.

While THTM demonstrates superior efficacy in preclinical AKI/CKD models, two key considerations merit attention for clinical translation: First, the heterogeneity of CD44 expression in human AKI subtypes (e.g., sepsis-induced vs. ischemia-induced injury) may impact targeting precision, necessitating patient stratification strategies based on biomarker profiling. Second, the multi-step synthesis of THTM (requiring sequential TPP conjugation, TK-MLT synthesis, and polymer-drug coupling) presents scalability challenges for GMP-compatible production. Future studies should explore continuous-flow reactors and simplified conjugation chemistries (e.g., click reactions) to enhance manufacturability without compromising therapeutic payload. To address these limitations, future work will: (1) Conduct head-to-head efficacy comparisons against MitoQ/SkQ1 in standardized AKI models; (2) Establish CD44 expression signatures in human renal biopsies across AKI etiologies; (3) Develop continuous-flow microreactor platforms for scalable TPP-HA conjugate synthesis.
