Myopericytoma of the Knee: Report of a Rare Case and Literature Review
Authors: Yun Kyoung Shin, Yeo Jin Kim, Je Ryung Gil, Hyun Jung Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myopericytoma is a rare pericytic (perivascular) soft tissue tumor. Its occurrence in the knee is exceptionally uncommon, with only 14 cases reported to date. Although it is generally benign, malignant transformation has been reported. As it has only recently been recognized as a distinct pathological entity, it may be overlooked in the differential diagnosis of soft tissue tumors. This case report describes the medial knee, highlights its clinical and imaging findings. Awareness of this rare tumor in atypical locations is essential for timely diagnosis, appropriate treatment, and structured follow-up.

INTRODUCTION

Myopericytoma is a rare benign soft tissue neoplasm of perivascular origin, first recognized as a distinct pathological entity in 1998 and classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as part of the pericytic (perivascular) tumor group (12). Histologically, it shows concentric proliferation of myoid spindle cells around small vessels and overlaps with glomus tumors and smooth muscle neoplasms (12). Myopericytoma belongs to a spectrum including myofibroma, angioleiomyoma, and glomus tumor, making immunohistochemistry crucial for differential diagnosis (3). In this recently characterized entity, myopericytoma, radiologic imaging helps identify malignancy and prevent delayed diagnosis. Owing to its rarity, particularly in the knee, only 14 cases of myopericytoma have been reported to date, to the best of our knowledge (234567). Its rarity often leads to delayed diagnosis. In this report, we present a case of myopericytoma involving the knee, describing clinical features, imaging findings, and surgical treatment.

CASE REPORT

A 62-year-old male presented with a gradually enlarging soft mass on the medial aspect of the left knee, progressing over four years. He reported intermittent stabbing pain that is more pronounced at night, with a pain score of 6 on a Numeric Rating Scale (0–10), which corresponds to a moderate-to-severe intensity of pain. His medical history included diabetes mellitus, with no trauma history. Physical examination revealed a well-demarcated, movable 2-cm mass tender on palpation. Plain radiographs showed a bulging soft tissue contour (Fig. 1A). Contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a well-defined subcutaneous mass measuring 2.1 × 1.5 × 1.3 cm, isointense on T1-weighted images and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences, surrounded by a thin dark rim. The lesion abutted the medial patellar retinaculum without perilesional edema and exhibited vivid peripheral enhancement (Fig. 1B, C). Differential diagnoses included nerve sheath tumor and nodular fasciitis, although other superficial soft tissue tumors could not be excluded. Surgery was planned to relieve the patient’s anxiety and to allow histopathological examination. Under spinal anesthesia, an orthopedic surgeon excised the mass. Histopathology confirmed myopericytoma, showing well-circumscribed spindle cell proliferation with hemangiopericytoma-like branching vessels and concentric perivascular arrangement. The tumor cells tested positive for smooth muscle actin and negative for desmin and CD34 (Fig. 1D, E). Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and nine months post-surgery, the patient remains well, without recurrence or complications. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Committee (IRB No. 2025-09-009).

DISCUSSION

Myopericytoma is a rare soft tissue neoplasm. In a large cross-sectional study of 623 foot and ankle soft tissue masses, Tay et al. (8) reported a myopericytoma incidence rate of only 0.8% over a decade. Its apparent rarity reflects not only the uncommon nature of the entity itself but also its historical classification under hemangiopericytoma, with recognition as a separate pathological entity occurring only in recent decades. Consequently, available information remains limited, and published case reports are relatively scarce. In particular, involvement of the knee is particularly rare: Eight cases have been documented according to Rodrigues’s report in 2020 (3), and to the best of our knowledge, only six additional cases have since been reported—one in 2021 (7), three in 2022 (2), one in 2024 (6), and one in 2025 (5)—bringing the total to fourteen. These 14 reported cases are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. As previously described, myopericytoma is a slow-growing tumor that follows a benign course in most cases (3); notably, in our review of the 14 reported knee cases, with follow-up periods ranging from 1 (2) to 144 months in the previously reported knee cases (see Supplementary Table 1 and its second reference, Dray et al.), no malignant transformation or metastasis was observed. Only one case demonstrated local recurrence, reported by Sakuda et al. (6), in which a patellar myopericytoma initially treated with curettage recurred after 18 months of follow-up, necessitating patellectomy; thereafter, no further recurrence was observed during an additional 3 years of follow-up. Patients usually present with a long-standing, gradually enlarging, well-circumscribed mass, which is generally painless (4). The tumor is typically observed in the extremities, with the lower extremities more frequently affected in young patients (9). Associations with trauma and immunocompromised states such as HIV infection have been anecdotally reported in myopericytoma cases (310); however, none of the 14 reported knee cases mentioned any such associations. Radiologically, myopericytomas are typically well-defined and hypervascular soft-tissue masses (9). On MRI, previously reported cases have commonly shown a low-to-isointense signal on T1-weighted imaging and high signal intensity on T2-weighted or fluid-sensitive sequences, typically with homogeneous internal features (2, 479). Although nonspecific, these findings are consistent across most reported cases. Some lesions may demonstrate calcification or internal hemorrhage, resulting in heterogeneous signals (9); however, such features were absent in our case and in all previously reported knee lesions.

Our case also demonstrated T1 isointensity and vivid post-contrast enhancement, in line with prior reports, while the concentric T2 appearance with a relatively darker central portion was less commonly described. Given the subcutaneous location, small size (approximately 2 cm), T1 isointensity, and T2 hyperintensity, nerve sheath tumor and nodular fasciitis were initially considered. However, myopericytoma shares substantial imaging findings that overlap with these entities, and no definitive radiologic feature can reliably distinguish it from other superficial soft-tissue tumors. Consequently, histopathological confirmation remains essential. As a recently recognized and exceedingly rare soft tissue tumor, myopericytoma may still be unfamiliar to many clinicians and radiologists, which can cause it to be overlooked in the differential diagnosis. Although most cases follow a benign course with a favorable prognosis, local recurrence has been reported, particularly after incomplete excision (4). Importantly, a small subset of tumors may undergo malignant transformation or metastasis (7). Awareness of myopericytoma’s diagnostic entity, characteristic features, and radiologic appearance is therefore essential. Familiarity with its distinctive features allows earlier consideration during image interpretation, thereby preventing diagnostic delay and ultimately contributing to timely treatment and improved patient care.
