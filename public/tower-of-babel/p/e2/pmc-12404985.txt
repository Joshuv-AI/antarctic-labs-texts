Octodon degus: a natural animal model of aging and Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Michael J Hurley, B Maximiliano Garduño, Robert M J Deacon, Todd C Holmes, Xiangmin Xu, Patricia Cogram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study of aging and age-related diseases has traditionally focused on short-lived laboratory species such as mice. The common degu (Octodon degus) is a long-lived rodent species native to Chile that naturally develops neuropathology and behavioral deficits characteristic of Alzheimer’s disease together with other comorbidities associated with aging. These characteristics make the degu a useful model to gain insights into the interaction of aging and neurodegeneration.Subject terms: Alzheimer's disease, Scientific community

The Octodon degus

The common degu (Octodon degus) is a small, diurnal rodent native to the semi-arid shrublands and rocky outcrops of central Chile, where it forms complex social colonies and constructs extensive burrow systems. This species is well-adapted to the Mediterranean climate of the Chilean valleys and has evolved behaviors such as group foraging and cooperative burrow maintenance, which contribute to its survival in this challenging environment.

In captivity, degu can live 10–14 years, which is exceptionally long for a rodent of their size. Whereas, in the wild their lifespan is shorter (typically 2–5 years) due to predation and environmental pressures. Their extended lifespan under controlled conditions makes them particularly valuable for longitudinal studies of aging, as they naturally progress through developmental, adult and aged stages in a timeframe that allows the observation of gradual, age-associated cognitive decline.

From an evolutionary perspective, the degu belongs to the order Hystricomorpha, sharing closer ancestry with chinchillas, guinea pigs and naked mole rats compared with murid rodents, such as mice and rats. This phylogenetic distance is substantial because most laboratory mouse and rat strains, while commonly used in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) research, are short-lived and often rely on transgenic models of familial AD. In contrast, degu are non-transgenic, long-lived rodents that naturally develop Alzheimer’s-like pathologies with age, including amyloid-β (Aβ) plaque accumulation and tau hyperphosphorylation1,2. The degu’s combination of natural aging, extended lifespan and evolutionary distinctiveness makes it a valuable model for studying age-related cognitive decline and neurodegeneration. Compared to murid rodents, which rarely live long enough to develop late-onset pathologies, degu exhibit sporadic and multifactorial features of human AD, making them more suited for translational research (Fig. 1). Large and long-lived species such as cats, dogs and even dolphins might have natural Alzheimer’s-like pathology and could make better disease models than mice3. However, there are ethical, financial and practical reasons why such species are not routinely used. Husbandry costs expressed as per diem are higher for larger animals compared to mice. Non-murine rodent animal models, such as the degu, are gaining increasing attention as natural animal models of aging and AD.

Currently, most degu research is concentrated in Chile, where the species is native. However, colonies have been established in several other countries for comparative biology, behavioral neuroscience and aging studies. While the exact number of colonies worldwide is difficult to estimate, most of the established colonies outside Chile originated from a limited pool of animals exported during the 1960s and 1970s, before the enactment of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) in 1975 that restricted the distribution of plants and animals. Consequently, there is a relatively narrow genetic pool in these populations4. The genetic bottleneck in these longstanding degu colonies likely contributes to differences in the phenotypic expression of aging and cognitive decline observed between wild-caught and laboratory-bred degu. Most of these colonies are concentrated in North America, Europe and Japan, with few established outside these regions, likely due to the logistical and regulatory challenges of importing and maintaining exotic rodent species. Despite these limitations, the degu remains a promising model for aging and neurodegenerative research due to its naturally occurring Alzheimer’s-like pathology1,2.

Strategic colony management and breeding practices can improve the narrow gene pool. The introduction of wild-caught degu obtained under appropriate ethical and legal permits in Chile, combined with crossbreeding among established international colonies, can help restore genetic diversity. Advances in genomic screening now allow for the selection of breeding pairs to maximize heterozygosity. These measures would enhance the genetic variability of laboratory populations, thereby improving the translational relevance of Alzheimer’s-like phenotypes observed across studies. Establishing a degu colony is generally straightforward, provided that the facilities are equipped to meet the species-specific environmental and husbandry needs5.

Research resources for the degu have progressed considerably in recent years. The availability of an initially complete genome assembly (OctDeg1.0) and growing transcriptomic and proteomic datasets provides a robust molecular foundation for contemporary studies. Due to the high sequence homology between degu and human proteins associated with AD, such as amyloid-β (97% sequence identity in Aβ1–42), tau (MAPT, ~89% identity) and synaptic markers like synaptophysin and PSD95 (>90% identity), many commercially available antibodies exhibit cross-reactivity with degu samples2. This compatibility enables the application of well-established human research protocols, substantially reducing the need for custom reagents and enhancing the model’s cost-effectiveness. Furthermore, the close structural similarity of these AD-related proteins increases the translational relevance of degu-based findings.

Degu are highly social, adaptable and resilient rodents that thrive in ventilated rack systems similar to those used for conventional laboratory rodents. Their relatively long lifespan provides a distinct advantage for longitudinal studies but also necessitates a long-term institutional commitment to colony maintenance. Our collaborative team, consisting of labs in Chile (University of Chile) and the US (University of California Irvine), has developed standardized husbandry and breeding protocols that have proven highly effective for maintaining healthy and productive degu colonies5.

Degu exhibit a natural aging process that is highly comparable to that of humans, including age-associated cognitive decline, spontaneous development of Aβ plaques and tau pathology, and metabolic dysfunctions such as type 2 diabetes6. Importantly, degu also show behavioral changes in burrowing behavior consistent with impairments of activities of daily living in people with AD, making them a valuable animal model of AD7. Degu live a semi-fossorial lifestyle, naturally engaging in burrowing, an activity integral to their daily life. This ethologically relevant behavior has proven to be a useful metric for assessing cognitive decline in the degu, because digging reflects a hippocampal-dependent activity of daily living. This key behavioral domain is vulnerable to deterioration in aging and neurodegenerative conditions. In 2006, Deacon and colleagues first demonstrated that reductions in burrowing behavior can serve as a sensitive and early indicator of hippocampal and prefrontal cortex dysfunction in rodents7. A decline in burrowing behavior correlates with age-associated cognitive deficits and Alzheimer-like neuropathology, including amyloid-β and tau accumulation. As a naturalistic and low-stress assay, the burrowing test offers a reproducible and translationally relevant behavioral tool for monitoring cognitive decline in degu. The burrowing test measures a hippocampal-dependent activity of daily living, in which animals instinctively remove food pellets or bedding from a tube over a defined period. A decline in burrowing behavior reflects impairments in hippocampal and prefrontal cortex function, both of which are affected early in AD. This test therefore provides a naturalistic and translational measure of cognitive and functional decline in aging and AD models. As part of a specialized battery of behavioral tests developed for the degu, the burrowing paradigm provides sensitive and translational endpoints for AD research. Degu possess the cognitive capacity to perform episodic-like memory tasks, including novel object recognition (what), object location (where), and temporal order recognition (when), allowing early detection of memory decline that mirrors prodromal AD in humans8,9. By combining episodic memory and activity of daily living measures, this behavioral toolkit links cognitive and functional decline directly to neuropathology, offering robust and clinically relevant readouts for evaluating therapeutic interventions in a natural aging model2,10.

In our studies2,10, burrowing behavior was assessed in 4–6 year-old degu because this age represents the transitional period between middle and late adulthood in degu. In laboratory settings, degu typically live 10–14 years, with individuals over 6–8 years of age considered aged or geriatric11. The 4- to 5.5-year window corresponds to the early senior stage in humans, roughly equivalent to ages 55 to 70, when the earliest signs of cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s-like neuropathology begin to appear in the sporadic form of the disorder12. This period is therefore ideal for detecting subtle preclinical and early-stage behavioral and neuropathological changes while avoiding the confounding effects of terminal age-related neurodegeneration. Degu with Aβ accumulation have been shown to perform poorly in burrowing tests, thus providing a link between the neuropathological and behavioral defects found in AD2,6. This result parallels findings in other models, where hippocampal damage disrupts species-specific behaviors. Remarkably, degu are among the few non-traditional laboratory species in which hippocampal place cells have been electrophysiologically identified13. Their presence in the degu highlights the cognitive and neural complexity of this species and supports their use in studying spatial memory decline in aging and neurodegeneration.

The degu characteristics associated with Alzheimer’s Disease

A key point of contention in the literature has been the overgeneralization that all degu develop Alzheimer’s-like pathology as they age8,9,14. While some aged degu exhibit molecular hallmarks of AD, including Aβ plaques, neurofibrillary tangles (associated with tau protein) and Apoe mutations2,9,10, others do not show these markers, mirroring the variability observed in humans. The presence of these Alzheimer-like traits in naturally aging animals allows researchers to study the interplay of genetics, environment and longevity in disease evolution and to investigate the emergence of Alzheimer-like pathology in a more holistic context, revealing how systemic factors might contribute to the cognitive decline observed in individuals with AD.

One fascinating observation in the degu that may explain the varying susceptibility to Alzheimer’s-like pathology in this model is the status of their Apoe gene. Apoe is a critical gene associated with the risk and progression of AD in humans, with three isoforms (ε2, ε3, and ε4) conveying varying degrees of risk of developing the disease. Most rodents, by contrast, only have a single Apoe4-like variant, making them less representative of the human genetic landscape. The degu APOE protein has over 70% homology with human APOE; however, the discovery of two Apoe variants in the protein’s hinge region suggested a unique evolutionary divergence that may influence the progression of AD10. These variants could modulate the structural conformation of APOE’s lipid-binding properties and interaction with Aβ, thereby affecting the onset or progression of AD-like pathology observed in aged degu. This natural genetic variability further enhances the model’s value for studying the interplay between APOE function and neurodegeneration in a mammalian system without genetic manipulation. Currently, ongoing studies are focusing on characterizing the functional impact of these naturally occurring APOE variants in degu, as they have been found in some but not all individuals within outbred colonies. This variability presents a unique opportunity to investigate genotype–phenotype correlations in a naturalistic context. Understanding how these mutations influence amyloid processing, lipid metabolism and neuroinflammatory responses will be critical for determining their role in modulating susceptibility to late-onset spontaneous Alzheimer-like pathology in this species. These findings may also increase our understanding of human risk and resilience mechanisms.

The degu model serves as a valuable alternative to transgenic mouse models by recapitulating the sporadic and multifactorial nature of AD. Degu naturally develop key AD pathologies, including Aβ accumulation and tau hyperphosphorylation, during aging, enabling investigation of gene–environment interactions and variability relevant to late-onset human AD. When used alongside transgenic mice in mechanistic studies, the degu offers a behaviorally rich and physiologically relevant platform for validating therapeutic interventions in a context that closely parallels human disease progression.

Genetic diversity plays a critical role in determining the utility of animal populations as disease models. A whole-genome study comparing outbred and inbred degu colonies revealed stark differences between outbred colonies and inbred colonies that exhibited more than 11 million genetic variants and only 2 million variants, respectively10. The reduction in genetic diversity among highly inbred populations likely contributed to the failure of earlier studies in the US and Europe to observe Alzheimer-like pathology markers in degu15,16. The degu colonies used for the American and European studies seem to have originated from a very small colony of 20 founder animals that was established at MIT in 19644. Notably, these highly inbred colonies may lack the Apoe variants, which may be critical for the development of Alzheimer-like pathology. The importance of maintaining genetically diverse, outbred populations cannot be overstated, particularly for studying diseases like AD that are influenced by complex genetic and environmental interactions. We posit that recognizing and embracing genetic variability is critical to exploring the genetic and environmental factors that confer resilience or vulnerability to neurodegeneration, just as in humans.

Summary

It is important to consider the value of naturally aging animal models as complementary to transgenic mice, which primarily represent familial forms of AD through genetically deterministic mechanisms. Most human cases of AD are sporadic and are influenced by aging and lifestyle factors rather than single-gene mutations. By studying animals that develop Alzheimer-like pathology naturally, researchers can explore pathways and environmental interactions that may be overlooked in transgenic models. These animals could provide valuable context for understanding the broader biological processes underlying aging and neurodegeneration, thereby complementing and enhancing findings from traditional models17. By opening this discussion, we invite the scientific community to further consider the role of long-lived, naturally aging animals in AD research to understand spontaneous and sporadic disease development in genetically diverse populations.

The degu model has offered unique insights into the sporadic and age-related forms of the disease. However, to fully realize its translational potential, several critical steps must be taken to bridge the gap between basic research and clinical applications. Ongoing efforts to fully annotate the degu genome and identify genetic variants associated with cognitive decline and resilience are essential. Understanding the impact of genetic background on AD susceptibility will improve the predictive value of preclinical studies. There are future directions that will make degu research even more valuable. These include ethologically guided environmental enrichment in degu colonies, behavioral monitoring of complex social interactions between individuals, automated longitudinal behavioral assessments that minimize degu stress and concomitant artifactual behaviors, and improved imaging techniques, such as MRI/PET and miniscope neural activity recordings, coupled with peripheral biomarker characterization. Standardization and refinement of such methods will provide powerful tools for early diagnosis and treatment evaluation in degu longitudinal studies. Furthermore, conducting proof-of-concept studies with small molecules, biologics and gene therapies targeting AD-related pathways will further validate the model’s utility for drug development. Establishing international biobanks for degu tissue, cell lines and genetic material will also facilitate collaborative research.

A multidisciplinary approach is therefore required to enhance the degu’s translational potential by integrating genomics, behavioral science, neuroimaging and biomarker discovery. These efforts will help position the degu as a powerful natural model for AD and aging, providing important insights into the molecular and physiological mechanisms of neurodegeneration. While transgenic models remain invaluable, broadening our approach to include species that naturally develop age-related diseases could lead to a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving aging and AD. Such models may ultimately help bridge the gap between preclinical research and human clinical applications.
