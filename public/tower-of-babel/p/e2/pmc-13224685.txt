Causal relationship between GM, immune cells and esophageal adenocarcinoma: a mediation analysis based on MR
Authors: Wenbo He, Mingjun Gao, Qinglin Ren, Mengmeng Wang, Siding Zhou, Qingwen Liu, Kai Chu, Yannv Qin, Yiwei Fan, Hui Zou, Yaqian Cui, Yusheng Shu, Xiaolin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundModulating the immune system represents a pivotal mechanism through which the gut microbiome (GM) exerts influence over disease progression. However, Further investigation is necessary to ascertain the causal role of GM in Esophageal Adenocarcinoma (EAC) and the potential for immune cell mediation.MethodsInitially, a two-stage, two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using the Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method was performed to assess the causal effects of GM conditions on EAC, while exploring the potential mediating role of immune cells in the GM-EAC association.ResultsA MR analysis identified 5 types of GM and 9 types of GM metabolic pathway abundances with potential causal relationships to EAC. Furthermore, 26 immune cell characteristics(ICTs) were identified as closely associated with EAC. Mediation MR analysis revealed that ICTs negatively influenced the relationship between Rothia and EAC.ConclusionOur MR and mediation analyses suggest that specific gut microbes, microbial pathway abundances, and peripheral immune traits are associated with EAC susceptibility. Immune mediation was modest (-6–15%), implying predominantly direct microbiota effects with secondary immune modulation, and providing hypotheses for mechanistic validation and potential microbiome/immune intervention targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-03984-3.

Introduction

Cancers originating from the esophagus represent a significant global health concern [1]. In 2022, esophageal cancer is projected to account for approximately 511,054 new cases and over 445,391 deaths worldwide, ranking as the eleventh most commonly diagnosed cancer and the seventh leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1, 2]. Esophageal cancer is histologically classified into squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma, each of which has different etiology, pathology, tumour location, treatment and prognosis [3]. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is most prevalent in Eastern Europe and Asia, while esophageal adenocarcinoma(EAC) is more common in North America and Western Europe. Tobacco use is a moderate risk factor for esophageal adenocarcinoma, with the associated risk remaining unchanged even years after cessation [4, 5]. In contrast, a high body mass index (BMI) is recognized as the most significant risk factor for EAC [6, 7]. It is therefore incumbent upon researchers worldwide to work together to address the challenges of investigating the impact of various risk factors on the pathogenesis of EAC and to develop novel, safe and effective treatments for EAC.

In recent years, numerous studies have reinforced the notion that the GM, often referred to as the “second genome of humans,” plays a crucial role in human health [8, 9]. GM performs lots of vital functions, including the production of vitamins, metabolism of dietary compounds, prevention of intestinal pathogen proliferation, and protection against systemic infiltration [10–12]. Alterations in the GM can initiate disease onset and significantly impact the development and progression of various cancers, including EAC [13].

The primary mechanisms through which the GM influences disease development are the regulation of metabolism, endocrine function, inflammation, and the immune cell system [14, 15]. An increasing body of research has confirmed the intricate and profound relationship between the immune system and cancer [16, 17]. Normally, immune cells stop tumours growing and kill them. Sometimes though, they can even help them to spread [18]. In individuals with a healthy immune system, an increase in FOXP3⁺T cells and a decrease in CD8⁺T cells are associated with a heightened risk of developing breast, colorectal, and lung cancers [19]. However, the definitive link between systemic immunity and cancer remains insufficiently evidenced.

MR is an emerging statistical technique employed to investigate causal relationships and assess the connection between risk factors (exposures) and diseases (outcomes) [20]. This method imitates randomised trials and reduces the effect of other variables in observational studies. MR is a highly valuable tool for investigating causal relationships between risk factors and clinical diseases, given that it allows for the examination of the impact of genetic variation, which is randomly assigned at the moment of conception [21]. This study aims to employ MR methods to examine the relationship between EAC and GM-related diseases and exposures, as well as to assess whether systemic immune cells mediate this process. A comprehensive two-sample MR analysis will be conducted to evaluate the mediating effect of immune cells.

Materials and methods

Study design

A two-step approach was utilized to determine the causal relationship between GM types and their metabolic pathway abundances in relation to EAC, and to assess whether ICTs mediate this relationship. Firstly, two-sample MR analysis (primarily the IVW method) was employed to identify potential causal effects of various GM types and GM metabolic pathway abundances on EAC. In parallel, the same procedure was used to analyse the ICTs. Finally, a second round of two-sample MR analysis was conducted on the filtered GM and its metabolic pathway abundances, as well as the selected ICTs, with effect sizes and mediation proportions computed. Additionally, we ensured that the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for exposure and outcome were derived from distinct data sources, thereby eliminating any potential overlap. Data sources

The summary statistical data for GM at the genus level were derived from an European ancestry study that utilized shotgun metagenomic sequencing of fecal samples from 7,738 individuals in the Dutch Microbiome Project [22]. These data were used to associate imputed genotypes with variations in microbial taxonomic abundances and microbial metabolic pathway abundances, where pathway abundances were defined as microbial metabolic pathway abundances inferred from shotgun metagenomic sequencing data. Summary-level data for EAC were obtained from the GWAS Catalogue (accession: EBI-a-GCST003739) [23]. The data for 731 ICTs (Ebi-a-GCST0001391 to Ebi-a-GCST0002121) were sourced from the GWAS Catalog [24]. Six primary types of immune cells exist: B cells, CD4⁺T cells, CD8⁺T cells, dendritic cells, TBNK cells, and regulatory T cells. Additionally, the study encompassed 731 ICTs, including absolute cell (AC) counts (n = 118), relative cell (RC) counts (n = 192), median fluorescence intensities (MFI) indicative of surface antigen levels (n = 389), and morphological parameters (MP) (n = 32). All participants in this study were from Europe.

Genetic instrumental variables (IVs) selection

The filtering criterion for SNPs as instrumental variables (IVs) for EAC, GM and ICTs was set to p < 1e×10⁻⁵, in line with previous studies [25]. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed using an r² threshold of 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window, retaining a single sentinel SNP for each LD block, such that all selected instrumental variables exhibited pairwise r² < 0.001. The F-statistic was used to screen SNPs. It is calculated using the formula: F = r²/(1 -r²) * (N - K − 1)/K, where N is the sample size, K is the number of SNPs, R² represents the proportion of variance explained by the SNPs, MAF is the minor allele frequency, and b is the effect size [27]. The F-statistic was calculated by dividing β by the square of the standard error, with a cut-off value of 10 [28].

Statistical analysis

The two-sample MR analysis was conducted using the “TwoSampleMR,” “VariantAnnotation,” and “ieugwasr” packages. Among five methods (“MR Egger“ [30], “Weighted median” [31] “Inverse variance weighted (IVW)” [32], “Simple mode“ [33] and “Weighted mode“ [30]) in MR analysis, the IVW method was the primary approach for causal estimation because of its precision and robustness. A p-value of less than 0.05 indicated a significant association [33]between exposure and outcome. P values from all MR analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) approach [34]. Cochran’s Q statistic, derived from the IVW and MR-Egger methods, was used to assess the degree of heterogeneity. To identify instances of pleiotropy, the MR-Egger intercept test and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier method (MR-PRESSO) were employed using the “MR-PRESSO” package. MR-PRESSO was utilized to address horizontal pleiotropy and assess stage differences [30]. A leave-one-out analysis was performed to assess the influence of outlying genetic variants.

To minimize the potential for reverse causality from the outcome to the exposure, we performed reverse-direction Mendelian randomization (reverse MR) analyses. A null (non-significant) association in the reverse MR was interpreted as supportive evidence for the hypothesized forward causal direction.

For comparative purposes, we also report results from the IVW method [32]. The indirect effect of GM on EAC risk through a potential mediator was assessed using the “product of coefficients” method, and the standard error was calculated using the delta method [35].

We implemented a two-step MR framework to assess whether the effect of GM (X) on EAC (Y) is mediated through ICTs (M). Mediation (indirect) effects were calculated using the product-of-coefficients approach on the log-odds scale. In the first step, genetic variants robustly associated with GM was used as instrumental variables (IVs) to estimate the causal effect of GM on ICTs (β_{XM}). In the second step, genetic variants associated with ICTs were used as IVs to estimate the causal effect of ICTs on EAC (β_{MY}). To ensure consistent effect directions across datasets, we harmonized summary statistics so that effect estimates corresponded to the same effect allele.The mediated (indirect) effect was calculated using the product-of-coefficients approach: β_{indirect} = β_{XM} × β_{MY}. The standard error of the indirect effect was derived via the delta method, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were constructed. The direct effect was defined as β_{direct} = β_{total} − β_{indirect}, where β_{total} denotes the total effect of X on Y estimated via MR using X-associated instruments. When β_{total} was non-zero and directionally consistent, the proportion mediated was computed as β_{indirect} / β_{total}. To account for multiple testing across all exposure–mediator–outcome combinations, we applied appropriate corrections.

Results

Total effect of GM on EAC

Following a comprehensive screening process, a total of 17,717 SNPs were selected, corresponding to 207 microbial taxa and 205 pathway abundances representing the genetic makeup and metabolic functions of the GM. All SNPs displayed F-statistics above the threshold of 10, indicating the absence of weak instrument bias. Under the assumptions of Mendelian randomization, the MR analysis using the IVW method provided evidence of potential causal effects of EAC on 1 microbial family, 2 generas, 2 species of GM, and 10 metabolic pathway abundances. However, one GM–derived metabolic pathway reached nominal significance in the reverse MR analysis (p < 0.05); therefore, we excluded this finding from downstream analyses and interpretation to mitigate potential bias from reverse causality.

The final analysis identified statistically significant positive associations between EAC risk and one bacterial family, one genus, and three GM metabolic pathway abundances. Specifically, Micrococcaceae (Actinobacteria) was associated with increased EAC risk (OR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.08–1.59; p = 0.007), and a comparable association was observed for Rothia (Actinobacteria) (OR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.08–1.59; p = 0.007). With respect to functional profiles, the superpathway of L-aspartate and L-asparagine biosynthesis (ASPASN.PWY) exhibited an elevated odds ratio (OR = 1.45; 95% CI: 1.23–1.89; p = 0.005), and the TCA cycle VI (obligate autotrophs) pathway (PWY.5913) likewise demonstrated a positive association (OR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.15–1.89; p = 0.002). In addition, purine ribonucleosides degradation (PWY0.1296) was associated with increased EAC risk (OR = 1.37; 95% CI: 1.03–1.82; p = 0.027), as was pyridoxal 5′-phosphate biosynthesis I (PYRIDOXSYN.PW) (OR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.02–1.55; p = 0.030).

Conversely, the analysis indicated inverse associations between EAC risk and one genus, two GM species, and six GM metabolic pathway abundances. At the taxonomic level, Eubacterium ramulus (Eubacteriaceae) was associated with a reduced risk of EAC (OR = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.68–0.99; p = 0.041), as was Alistipes putredinis (Bacteroidetes) (OR = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.45–0.89; p = 0.009). In addition, the genus Prevotella (Bacteroidales) demonstrated a protective association (OR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54–0.95; p = 0.019).

With respect to microbial functional potential, pyruvate fermentation to acetone (PWY.6588) was inversely associated with EAC risk (OR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.55–1.29; p = 0.017), as was adenosine ribonucleotides de novo biosynthesis (PWY.7219) (OR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.62–0.98; p = 0.030). Similarly, the superpathway of phosphopantothenate biosynthesis I (PANTO.PWY) exhibited a negative association (OR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.60–0.96; p = 0.020). Further significant inverse associations were observed for ADP-L-glycero-beta-D-manno-heptose biosynthesis (PWY0.1241) (OR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.65–1.00; p = 0.049), the NAD salvage pathway I (PYRIDNUCSAL.PWY) (OR = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.69–1.00; p = 0.049), and tRNA processing (PWY0.1479) (OR = 0.72; 95% CI: 0.57–0.90; p = 0.004). Notably, sensitivity diagnostics provided no evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy, as neither the heterogeneity test nor the pleiotropy test yielded statistically significant results (both p > 0.05)(Figs. 2 and 3).

Effect of the characteristics of immune cells on EAC

Following the Mendelian randomization analysis, a total of 26 ICTs were identified as being potentially causally associated with EAC out of the 731 ICTs tested, primarily based on IVW estimates. Across B-cell–related phenotypes (n = 7), several traits were associated with an increased risk of EAC, including IgD⁺CD38br %lymphocyte (OR : 1.123; 95% CI: 1.019–1.237; p-value : 0.019), CD19 on IgD⁺(OR : 1.163; 95% CI: 1.035–1.308; p-value : 0.012), CD24 on CD24⁺CD27⁺(OR : 1.098; 95% CI: 1.004–1.201; p-value : 0.042), CD24 on sw mem (OR : 1.089; 95% CI: 1.002–1.183; p-value : 0.044), and CD24 on transitional (OR : 1.136; 95% CI: 1.006–1.283; p-value : 0.040). In contrast, CD19 on IgD⁻ CD38⁻ (OR : 0.887; 95% CI: 0.795–0.989; p-value : 0.032) and IgD on IgD⁺CD24⁺(OR : 0.836; 95% CI: 0.739–0.946; p-value : 0.004) were inversely associated with EAC.T-cell–related phenotypes constituted the largest group (n = 13) and displayed both risk-increasing and risk-decreasing associations. Genetically predicted increases in T/B (OR: 1.127; 95% CI: 1.020–1.245; p-value :0.019), T cell % leukocyte (OR : 1.191; 95% CI: 1.039–1.366; p-value : 0.012), CD28⁻ DN (CD4⁻CD8⁻) %DN (OR : 1.147; 95% CI: 1.033–1.273; p-value : 0.010), HVEM on naive CD8br (OR : 1.081; 95% CI: 1.009–1.158; p-value : 0.027), and CD45RA on TD CD8br (OR : 1.258; 95% CI: 1.055–1.499; p-value : 0.010) were positively associated with EAC. In contrast, CM CD4⁺AC (OR : 0.872; 95% CI: 0.778–0.977; p-value : 0.019), EM DN (CD4⁻CD8⁻) AC (OR : 0.868; 95% CI: 0.767–0.982; p-value = 0.025), Lymphocyte AC (OR = 0.820; 95% CI: 0.700–0.959; p-value : 0.013), CD28⁺DN (CD4⁻CD8⁻) % DN (OR : 0.872; 95% CI: 0.785–0.968; p-value : 0.010), CD3 on secreting Treg (OR : 0.925; 95% CI: 0.860–0.994; p-value : 0.033), CD3 on CD39⁺CD4⁺(OR : 0.933; 95% CI: 0.877–0.993; p-value : 0.030), CD127 on CD45RA⁻ CD4 not Treg (OR : 0.859; 95% CI: 0.743–0.992; p-value : 0.038), and CD4 on naive CD4⁺(OR : 0.895; 95% CI: 0.808–0.992; p-value : 0.034) were inversely associated with EAC.

Impact of GM on the characteristics of immune cells

Subsequently, we performed a secondary MR-based meta-analysis focusing on the subset of gut microbial features (taxa and metabolic pathway abundances) and ICTs that had shown statistical evidence of association with EAC in the preceding screening step. In this analysis, GM features were treated as exposures and ICTs as outcomes. Overall, 21 GM–immune trait pairs reached nominal significance (all P < 0.05), and seven associations remained strongly significant (P < 0.01), indicating non-random, directionally consistent links between specific microbial signatures and host immune phenotypes (Fig. 5).

Among bacterial taxa, Rothia (family Micrococcaceae) demonstrated both positive and inverse associations with immune traits. (Because Rothia and the Micrococcaceae family were instrumented using the same SNP(s), we only discuss the findings for Rothia in the following sections.) Notably, Rothia showed a robust inverse association with CD40 on monocytes (OR = 0.766; 95% CI: 0.650–0.902; P = 0.001), while exhibiting positive associations with CD3 on CD39⁺CD4⁺(OR = 1.243; 95% CI: 1.041–1.485; P = 0.016), CD4 on naive CD4⁺(OR = 1.231; 95% CI: 1.029–1.473; P = 0.023), and CD3 on secreting Treg (OR = 1.224; 95% CI: 1.020–1.469; P = 0.030). In addition, Prevotella (family Prevotellaceae) was inversely associated with CD19 on IgD⁻ CD38⁻ (OR = 0.771; 95% CI: 0.617–0.963; P = 0.022), whereas it was positively associated with SSC-A on plasmacytoid DC (OR = 1.318; 95% CI: 1.030–1.686; P = 0.028). For Eubacterium ramulus, a significant inverse association was observed with Im MDSC %CD33dim HLA DR⁻ CD66b⁻ (OR = 0.758; 95% CI: 0.633–0.908; P = 0.003), together with positive associations with IgD on IgD⁺CD24⁺(OR = 1.172; 95% CI: 1.023–1.344; P = 0.022) and CD4 on naive CD4⁺(OR = 1.180; 95% CI: 1.012–1.376; P = 0.035).

To clarify whether immune traits mediate the GM–EAC relationship, we performed mediation analysis on the significant triplets listed. Reverse MR analysis

All of the above findings were evaluated using reverse MR. Results showing evidence suggestive of reverse causality were excluded, thereby reducing the likelihood of bidirectional causal relationships.

Discussion

Over the past decade, the advancement of metagenomic sequencing technology has made it possible to fully quantify the microbial communities within the gut. An increasing number of studies have investigated how these microbiomes contribute to the mechanisms of cancer development, with a particular focus on etiology and molecular mechanisms. There is now a consensus among researchers that GM play a pivotal role in linking immune responses to cancer [36, 37]. The relationship between GM and the development of various cancers, including gastric cancer [38], colorectal cancer [39], breast cancer [40], and lung cancer [41], has been elucidated by numerous studies. The majority of current research on the microbiota composition in oesophageal cancer is concentrated on the analysis of esophageal and oral microbiota [39, 42], the relationship between EAC and GM is also gaining significant attention from scholars worldwide [43].

This study makes use of the most recent available dataset on the gut microbiome metagenome, together with a dataset on ICTs, to conduct MR analysis with data from patients with EAC [22, 24]. Several species within the phylum Bacteroidetes (Alistipes putredinis) were identified as having a potential inverse causal relationship with the development of EAC. This suggests that they may have protective effects against EAC. Prevotella, which is associated with a healthy plant-based diet, is usually considered a ‘beneficial’ bacterium in the human body. A study has suggested that the genus Prevotella may benefit glucose homeostasis and host metabolism, playing a vital role in preserving GM stability [44], a nother study has demonstrated that an increase in the abundance of tissue-associated Prevotella may enhance the survival rate of colorectal cancer patients [45]. Mechanistically, the risk-increasing pathway abundances appear to converge on a tumor-promoting microenvironment at the interface of immunity, metabolism, and nutrient substrate availability. Purine nucleoside degradation may reshape nucleoside metabolites such as inosine, which has been reported to modulate T-cell responses and influence immune checkpoint blockade efficacy, implying a pronounced context dependence; variations in epithelial barrier permeability and baseline immune tone may plausibly alter the direction and magnitude of its effects [46]. An enhanced capacity for aspartate/asparagine biosynthesis may reflect tumor-associated demands for amino acid supply. Aspartate can be growth-limiting in certain malignancies, and microbiota-mediated “consumption” or remodeling of the intestinal amino acid pool—including asparagine—has been shown to modify tumor responses to dietary asparagine, suggesting that microbial amino acid flux could condition nutrient availability and downstream tumor biology [47]. The positive association of TCA cycle–related pathway abundances may indicate microenvironmental shifts (e.g., redox balance and oxygen availability) during upper gastrointestinal tumorigenesis that select for taxa favoring more respiratory metabolism. Supporting this interpretation, metagenomic studies in esophageal and gastroesophageal junction cancers have reported enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation or TCA-associated functions [48]. Interpretation of the pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP; vitamin B6) biosynthesis pathway warrants caution. Although higher host vitamin B6 status is frequently linked to reduced cancer risk in epidemiologic studies, microbial “biosynthetic potential” does not necessarily translate into host bioavailable PLP, and may instead represent a surrogate of microbial community configurations enriched for one-carbon and/or amino acid metabolic programs [49]. Among the protective signals, the vitamin B5/CoA axis is biologically plausible, as it may enhance antitumor immune surveillance and improve PD-(L)1 responses [50]. The links involving NAD salvage synthesis and tRNA processing likely capture broader microbial growth/repair and redox programs; thus, pathway-level MR findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating evidence of microbiota–host metabolic crosstalk rather than direct mechanistic proof [49].

Our MR analyses revealed nominal associations between multiple peripheral blood IMTs and EAC risk. Given that OAC/EAC often develops in the context of chronic mucosal injury and inflammation, with a heterogeneous and frequently immunosuppressive microenvironment, immune differentiation states and co-regulatory signaling may be particularly relevant to susceptibility [51]. Among T-cell–related IMTs, higher risk was associated with increased T cell % of leukocytes, a higher T/B ratio, a greater proportion of CD28⁻ double-negative (DN) T cells, higher HVEM (TNFRSF14) expression on naive CD8 cells, and higher CD45RA expression on terminally differentiated CD8 cells. Collectively, these patterns are compatible with immune imbalance and a shift toward immunosenescence/terminal differentiation: CD28 loss is a canonical marker of T-cell senescence and has been linked to reduced proliferative reserve and altered effector programs in cancer contexts [52], while CD45RA re-expression is often used to indicate TEMRA-like states after chronic antigen exposure [53]. The HVEM–BTLA axis is a key co-inhibitory pathway; genetically predicted HVEM elevation may reflect a stronger inhibitory “set point,” although tissue specificity and directionality require confirmation [54]. Conversely, protective IMTs (e.g., higher CM CD4, naive CD4, and higher CD127/IL-7R on non-Treg memory CD4) align with better immune homeostasis and renewal capacity. B-cell findings were heterogeneous: several IgD⁺/CD24-related traits tracked with higher risk, whereas CD19 on IgD⁻CD38⁻ cells and IgD levels on IgD⁺CD24⁺cells were protective. Prior literature suggests tumor-infiltrating B cells/plasma cells can support antitumor immunity, yet immunosuppressive B-cell programs may be pro-tumorigenic in specific contexts [55].

Beyond adaptive immunity, the pDC signal provides a plausible innate-immunity clue [56]. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) are major producers of type I interferons (IFN-I) and can contribute to antitumor immune surveillance through innate sensing and subsequent immune priming; however, within established tumors, pDCs are often functionally reprogrammed with reduced IFN-I output and may promote immune escape in tolerogenic contexts. In our study, the pDC-related trait (SSC-A on plasmacytoid DC) showed a protective direction, suggesting that genetically predicted peripheral pDC status may reflect a more intact systemic IFN-I–related immune “set point,” rather than the locally impaired pDC phenotype commonly observed in tumor tissues. Given the susceptibility of dendritic cells to immunosuppressive conditioning, we interpret this association as hypothesis-generating evidence for systemic innate immune tone rather than direct proof of pDC function within the EAC microenvironment [57]. Myeloid findings were mixed, consistent with the marked heterogeneity of monocytes and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). MDSCs are widely implicated in immune evasion by suppressing effector T cells (e.g., via ARG1, iNOS/NO, and ROS) and reinforcing broader immunosuppressive networks; increased circulating MDSCs have been reported in esophageal cancer and linked to adverse clinical features [58]. In our results, higher Im MDSC %CD33^dim HLA-DR⁻ CD66b⁻ and higher CD62L⁻ monocyte %monocytes were associated with a protective direction, whereas higher CD11b on Mo-MDSCs and higher monocyte CD40 were associated with increased risk. A cautious interpretation is that proportion-based metrics may not directly index functional suppressive capacity, whereas surface molecules such as CD11b may better reflect adhesion/migration and recruitment programs that facilitate trafficking of suppressive myeloid cells to relevant tissues and promote immune escape [59]. Elevated monocyte CD40 may indicate a more pro-inflammatory activation state, potentially aligning with the chronic inflammatory milieu of Barrett’s esophagus; however, compartment specificity and the true biological directionality require further validation [60].

Several peripheral blood IMTs may partially mediate or counterbalance the association between the GM and EAC. The pDC trait SSC-A aligned with the protective total effect and showed a relatively larger mediation proportion (~ 14%), implicating an innate immune “set point” as a contributor to protection. In conclusion, by integrating Mendelian randomization with proposed mechanistic insights, we have shifted from identifying associative immune characteristics to hypothesizing their functional roles within a microbiome-immune-EAC axis. This framework not only enhances our comprehension of EAC immunobiology but also pinpoints specific, testable pathway abundances for developing novel immunotherapeutic and microbiome-modulating strategies.

The present study has a number of advantages, which are outlined below. First, the most recent datasets were used in a two-sample Mendelian Randomization analysis, which revealed not only the potential causal relationships between ICTs and GM in EAC, but also the potential causal pathway abundances linking GM metabolism to EAC. Furthermore, we employed the reverse MR analysis method to verify our MR results, thereby ensuring their robustness. Ultimately, through Mendelian mediation analysis, we demonstrated the mediating role of immunological traits in the causal relationships between GM, its metabolic pathway abundances, and EAC. These findings are crucial for advancing our understanding of EAC pathogenesis and for identifying potential targets for immunotherapy.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. First, and most critically, the genetic instruments and outcome data used in this study were derived exclusively from individuals of European ancestry. This fundamental characteristic of the summary-level data severely limits the generalizability of our findings. EAC demonstrates striking geographic and racial disparities in incidence, with rates markedly higher in Western, predominantly European-ancestry populations compared to Asian and other ethnic groups. Therefore, our findings represent a population-specific snapshot and must be validated in multi-ancestral cohorts to determine their universal applicability. Secondly, although rigorous steps were taken to identify and account for outlier instrumental variables, there remains a possibility of horizontal pleiotropy effects. Thirdly, it is crucial to acknowledge that MR analysis is hypothesis-driven, and experimental or clinical research. Therefore, our results require confirmation through experimental models such as investigating the effect of Rothia supplementation in EAC animal models and clinical studies involving long-term, targeted interventions on the gut microbiome and subsequent monitoring of immune traits and EAC risk.

Conclusion

Leveraging recent large-scale GWAS data on gut metagenomics and peripheral immune cell traits, this study applied Mendelian randomization and mediation analyses to provide genetic evidence that specific gut microbial taxa, microbiome-derived metabolic pathway abundances, and peripheral immune phenotypes may jointly shape susceptibility to EAC. Several microbial signals (e.g., Alistipes putredinis and Prevotellaceae/Prevotella-related features) showed protective directions, whereas risk-associated pathway abundances primarily implicated metabolic programs linked to immune–metabolic interactions and nutrient substrate availability. Multiple peripheral ICTs—particularly those consistent with immune imbalance and immunosenescence—exhibited nominal associations with EAC risk and contributed limited mediation of microbiota–EAC relationships. However, the overall mediation proportion was modest (~ 6–15%), indicating that microbiota-associated effects are largely direct, with immune traits acting mainly as secondary modulators. Collectively, these findings propose a testable “microbiota–immunity–metabolism–EAC” framework and offer hypotheses for downstream mechanistic studies and the identification of microbiome- or immune-oriented intervention targets.
