Relationship between daytime napping duration and pan-cancer risk: a prospective cohort study based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)
Authors: Guoqing Li, Tianyu Gan, Zelong Qian, Yiqin Zhou, Hui Zhou, Yibo Wu, Ling Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, cancer has become a hot topic in the field of health management, and its etiology may be related to a variety of factors. Current research suggests that daytime napping duration is associated with variety of diseases, but few studies have explored the relationship between daytime napping duration and the risk of pan-cancer. Therefore, we based our study on the CHARLS database and Mendelian Randomization (MR) to investigate the relationship between daytime napping duration and pan-cancer risk.MethodsThis study is based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and Mendelian Randomization (MR). We first used Mendelian Randomization to explore the causal relationship between daytime napping and pan-cancer. Cox proportional regression was used to investigate the relationship between daytime napping duration and the risk of pan-cancer. The RCS curve is used to display the non-linear relationship between daytime napping duration and pan-cancer risk.ResultsAmong 6 166 Chinese participants, compared with those who never napped, the overall cancer risk decreased by 31.0% (HR = 0.690), 33.9% (HR = 0.661), and 41.4% (HR = 0.586) for individuals who napped 1–30 min, 31–60 min, and over 60 min, respectively. Restricted cubic splines indicated a linear inverse association between nap duration and cancer risk (P for non-linear = 0.552), and sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results.ConclusionLonger daytime napping duration was associated with a lower overall cancer risk. After adjusting for multiple confounders, people who napping over 60 min remained linked to an approximately 40% reduction in cancer risk. Our study found an inverse association between daytime napping duration and cancer risk, but larger sample sizes and objective sleep measurements are still needed for validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01126-4.Keywords: Daytime napping duration, Cancer incidence, CHARLS, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

In recent years, cancer has become a hot topic in the field of health management [1, 2], and its etiology may be related to a variety of factors [3]. Cancer is a major global disease burden. Each year, tens of millions of people worldwide are diagnosed with cancer, and more than half of these patients eventually die from the disease. In many countries, cancer is the second most common cause of death after cardiovascular diseases. With significant improvements in the treatment and prevention of cardiovascular diseases, cancer has already or will soon become the leading cause of death in many parts of the world. Since the elderly are most susceptible to cancer, and the aging of the population continues in many countries, cancer will remain a major health issue globally [1].

The brain plays a crucial role in controlling the body’s circadian rhythms, and there is evidence that habitual short sleepers have lower serum melatonin levels than long sleepers [4]. Melatonin is synthesized and secreted by the pineal gland in the brain and plays an important role in controlling the body’s circadian rhythms [5]. Sleep duration is associated with various health outcomes [6, 7], including cardiovascular disease, and cancer mortality (U-shaped) [8–11]. Therefore, sleep duration may affect melatonin levels by determining the length of light exposure, thereby influencing human health.

Previous studies have shown that daytime napping duration is associated with a variety of diseases. A meta-analysis of 11 prospective cohort studies encompassing 151,588 participants showed that, compared with non-nappers, a daily nap ≥ 60 min was significantly associated with higher risks of cardiovascular disease (RR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.22–2.71) and all-cause mortality (RR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.11–1.45). This relationship followed a J-shaped dose-response curve, whereas naps lasting < 60 min carried no significant risk [12]. Moreover, a UK population-based cohort study found a J-shaped association between nap duration and the risk of type 2 diabetes, with longer naps (>60 min) linked to an increased risk [13]. The Dongfeng-Tongji cohort study in China (n = 19,370) showed that individuals napping >90 min had a 25% higher risk of incident coronary heart disease compared with those napping 1–30 min (HR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.05–1.49), and this risk was further amplified when combined with ≥ 10 h of nocturnal sleep [14]. Furthermore, a dose-response analysis of eleven cohort studies demonstrated a linear, positive relationship between nap duration and all-cause mortality : the longer the nap, the higher the mortality risk, and this association was independent of nocturnal sleep duration [12]. In short, habitual daytime napping duration is associated with various metabolic abnormalities and is closely linked to overall human health. Therefore, daytime napping may, through the aforementioned factors, directly or indirectly influence the initiation and progression of cancer.

Many studies currently indicate that long nighttime sleep can reduce the risk of developing cancer [15–17]. However, there are currently few studies that investigate the relationship between daytime napping duration and the risk of pan-cancer. Therefore, we conducted a prospective cohort study based on the CHARLS database and MR to explore the relationship between daytime napping duration and the risk of pan-cancer among the Chinese population.

Materials and methods

Study population

We first employed Mendelian randomization to investigate the causal relationship between daytime napping and pan-cancer risk. The exposure data, labeled “Nap during day” (ukb-a-12), were obtained from Neale Lab and comprised 337,074 individuals. The outcome data for overall cancer (ieu-b-4964) were sourced from the UK Biobank, encompassing 422,659 participants.

Secondly, we used the CHARLS database to conduct a prospective cohort study examining the relationship between daytime napping and overall cancer risk. The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a population-based longitudinal survey targeting individuals in China, encompassing demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle behavior, and health-related information. The national baseline survey of this study began in 2011, involving 17,708 respondents, and is conducted biennially [18].

Some of the relevant data in this study were self-reported by the participants. In CHARLS database, nighttime sleep duration was determined by the questionnaire: “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night)? (This may be shorter than the number of hours you spend in bed.) “Daytime napping duration was determined by the questionnaire: “During the past month, how long did you take a nap after lunch?” The diagnosis of cancer was determined by the questionnaire: “Have you been diagnosed with ‘Cancer or malignant tumor (excluding minor skin cancers)’ by a doctor?” To ensure the reliability of cancer diagnoses, participants were asked to confirm the accuracy of the previous records in subsequent follow-ups.The details of the inclusion process are shown in Fig. 1. The CHARLS study has been approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). Each participant has signed an informed consent form.

Flow diagram for participants included in the study

Covariables

The variables considered in this study include age, gender, height, weight, waist circumference, educational background, smoking, alcohol consumption, marital status, residence location is rural or urban, whether suffering from hypertension, whether suffering from diabetes, whether suffering from coronary heart disease, whether suffering from emotional, nervous or psychiatric problems and nighttime sleep duration. We categorized participants into eight groups based on their nap duration, as follows: (1) Never takes a nap (2) Naps for 1–30 min (3) Naps for 31–60 min (4) Naps over 60 min.

Statistical analysis

Mendelian randomization

We selected genetic variants (SNPs) strongly associated with the exposure (P < 5 × 10⁻³⁴), unrelated to the outcome (P > 5 × 10⁻⁸), and independent of confounders as instrumental variables from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). And We used PhenoScanner to exclude potential SNPS that could influence the outcome. Causal effects of the exposure on the outcome were estimated using five models: MR-Egger, simple median, weighted mode, weighted median, and inverse-variance-weighted (multiplicative random effects). Sensitivity analyses and pleiotropy tests were conducted with MR-Egger and leave-one-out analyses to ensure robust, unbiased results.

Prospective cohort study

In the descriptive analysis, the two control groups determined by cancer status were assessed using weighted t-tests (for continuous variables) or weighted chi-square tests (for categorical data). For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used, and the results were expressed as the median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical parameters are expressed as proportions, and continuous variables are expressed as means and standard deviations. A weighted multivariate Cox proportional-hazards regression model was used to study the association between daytime napping duration and pan-cancer risk, expressing HR values and 95% confidence intervals.

In the analysis, we established three models: Model 1 is the unadjusted crude model, Model 2 is adjusted for age, gender, waist circumference, height, weight, smoking, alcohol consumption, educational background, marital status, place of residence and nighttime sleep duration. Model 3 adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease and whether suffering from emotional, nervous or psychiatric problems based on Model 2. We used the Never takes a nap group as the reference to further assess the differences in pan-cancer risk for different daytime napping duration. RCS curve was used to explore the nonlinear relationship between daytime napping duration and pan-cancer risk. All analyses were performed using stats (version = 26.0) software and R version 4.3.2. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Mendelian randomization

After linkage-disequilibrium pruning (r² < 0.1, window size = 1 Mb), we employed five Mendelian randomization methods-MR-Egger, simple median, weighted mode, weighted median, and inverse-variance weighted (multiplicative random-effects)-to investigate the causal relationship between daytime napping duration and overall cancer risk. The results indicate that, except for MR-Egger, the remaining four approaches all support a positive causal association. All results are presented in Fig. 2.

Results of the five mendelian randomization analyses

To assess the robustness of the Mendelian randomization estimates, we performed a suite of sensitivity analyses. Cochran’s Q test produced a P-value of 1, indicating no detectable heterogeneity among the instrumental SNPs. The MR-Egger intercept test yielded a P-value of 0.74, providing no evidence of directional pleiotropy. The scatter plot of MR results and the leave-one-out plot (based on the 30 SNPs with the strongest effect sizes) are presented in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively. The MR scatter plot and leave-one-out plot indicate that the causal estimates remain robust.

Association between daytime napping duration and pan-cancer risk

The relationship between daytime napping duration and pan-cancer can be seen in Table 2. Our study results indicate that people who never takes a nap have a higher risk of pan-cancer, while naps over 60 min show the lowest risk association, and this result remains significant in all models. In model 3, compared to participants who never takes a nap, the risk of cancer for individuals who Naps for 1–30 min decreased by 31.0% (HR = 0.690, 95%CI : 0.686–0.693). In the same, compared to participants who never takes a nap, the risk of cancer for individuals who Naps over 60 min decreased by 41.4% (HR = 0.586, 95%CI : 0.583–0.590). Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the validity of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by re-running the statistical analyses after excluding all patients with hypertension, coronary heart disease, diabetes, or any emotional or psychiatric disorders. The results showed that cancer risk gradually decreased as daytime napping duration increased (HR = 0.993, 95%CI : 0.993–0.993, p < 0,001).

Discussion

We conducted mendelian randomization and prospective cohort study to explore the relationship between daytime napping duration patterns and pan-cancer. According to our research, as daytime nappping duration increases, pan-cancer risk gradually decreases.

An increasing number of epidemiological studies have shown an association between short sleep duration and cancer [19–21]. A cross-sectional study conducted in China indicated that short sleep duration is significantly correlated with cancer. A categorical meta-analysis revealed that short sleep duration increases the risk of cancer among Asians [20]. Recent studies have explored the relationship between trajectories of sleep duration [22] and health outcomes such as hypertension [23], diabetes, dementia [24], cardiovascular events [25], and all-cause mortality [26], also suggesting that napping can influence cancer development from multiple aspects.

Daytime napping may be associated with cancer development through several mechanisms. Studies have shown that short sleep duration is related to reduced levels of melatonin, which has the function of inhibiting the occurrence and growth of cancer [27]. Since daytime napping duration may affect the overall pattern of melatonin secretion, this could be one mechanism by which napping is associated with the risk of cancer. Additionally, melatonin may affect the development of cancer by regulating the production of sex hormones through various mechanisms related to the estrogen signaling pathway [20]. Furthermore, sleep deprivation may impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to recognize and eliminate tumor cells [27–29]. Short sleep duration may disrupt metabolic pathways associated with obesity, which could increase the risk and progression of various types of cancer [30, 31]. Nap duration may also affect metabolic pathways, especially when it impacts the quality of nighttime sleep. Short sleep duration may be a manifestation of chronic stress, which plays an important role in the occurrence and progression of cancer [32, 33].

Different mechanisms contribute differently to the development of various types of cancer, which may lead to different risks of cancer associated with different nap duration. For breast cancer, daytime napping may influence the circadian rhythm, thereby altering melatonin secretion and consequently affecting sex-hormone levels [34]. For gastric cancer, daytime napping may disrupt melatonin secretion, thereby weakening the body’s antioxidant capacity, DNA-repair efficiency, and anti-inflammatory responses in the gastric mucosa, making it more vulnerable to ROS- and Helicobacter pylori–related damage [35, 36]. Daytime napping may influence the development of liver cancer by affecting blood glucose levels [37]. In summary, napping may influence the development of specific cancers through multiple or distinct mechanisms. However, research on the specific molecular pathways through which napping affects cancer development remains limited and warrants further exploration.

This study focuses specifically on patients’ daytime napping duration. Although both daytime napping and general daytime sleep occur outside the nocturnal window, their mechanistic impacts on cancer may diverge: the former can be viewed as a circadian-friendly “energy patch”, whereas the latter-by misaligning circadian timing and lowering melatonin-may act as a “carcinogenic loophole.” A midday nap typically occurs between 12:00 and 14:00, during the “post-prandial dip” in alertness. At this time both core body temperature and cortisol are at their natural nadir, parasympathetic tone predominates, and sleep readily enters stage N2 with minimal slow-wave sleep. Consequently, a brief nap (≤ 30 min) scarcely disturbs the nocturnal melatonin peak and does not appreciably delay the master circadian phase. In contrast, daytime catch-up sleep” taken in the late afternoon or after night shifts often overlaps with the rising phase of melatonin or the major nocturnal sleep period, making circadian misalignment, suppressed melatonin secretion, and phase delay far more likely [38–40]. A brief post-prandial nap taken during the peak of insulin and GLP-1 release improves hepatic insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance while suppressing ROS production. By contrast, catch-up sleep in the late afternoon or evening is often compounded by late-night snacking, caffeine intake, and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), leading to cycles of intermittent hypoxia–reoxygenation, insulin resistance, and an IL-6/TNF-α inflammatory cascade that accelerates the progression from NAFLD through fibrosis to hepatocellular carcinoma and also promotes gastric cancer [41–43]. Future studies should adopt a multimodal design that integrates continuous actigraphy, melatonin metabolites, and clock-gene expression to disentangle the independent and interactive effects of “when to sleep” and “how long to sleep” on cancer risk, thereby providing an evidence base for precision sleep interventions.

We observed that the effect size reported by the CHARLS cohort is unusually large: a > 60-minute siesta is associated with a 41% reduction in overall cancer risk, which stands out as excessively prominent when compared with established lifestyle risk factors. For reference, the pooled relative risk of smoking ranges from 1.9 to 2.5, while regular exercise or obesity avoidance typically results in only a 10–25% risk reduction. Such a substantial protective effect should be interpreted with caution, and there are likely three types of biases underlying it: (1) Residual confounding related to health-seeking behaviors; (2) Social desirability bias in recalling “beneficial siestas” and self-report bias in cancer outcomes; (3) Reverse causality, whereby subclinical tumors or systemic inflammation lead to daytime sleepiness and prolonged siestas. (4) Limited sample size. Before objective sleep measurement via devices, repeated exposure assessment, and the use of lagged analysis to rule out preclinical diseases, the point estimate of this study may be overestimated and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than causal evidence.

However, our study also has some acknowledged limitations. Firstly, our assessment of sleep parameters was based on self-reported information rather than objective measurements, which could lead to measurement errors. Information about daytime napping was limited to naps after lunch, and other potential daytime naps, such as those of shift workers, may have been overlooked. Secondly, information on other aspects of sleep, such as participants’ family history, medication use, dietary intake, occupation, socioeconomic status, or other behavioral habits, all of which could introduce bias into the results.Thirdly, cancer diagnoses were self-reported based on physician diagnoses, which could introduce a certain degree of bias. Fourthly, the sample size for the relationship between daytime napping duration and cancers were limited, and a larger sample size is needed to ensure the reliability of the results. Although we adopted a pan-cancer endpoint to maximize statistical power and to reflect overall cancer risk, we recognize that this approach may conceal site-specific effects. Moreover, the CHARLS cohort and the UK Biobank MR-analytic datasets differ in ancestry, cultural napping habits, exposure definition (post-lunch vs. any daytime nap) and outcome ascertainment; the two lines of evidence are therefore not directly comparable. Both sets of results are merely complementary to our findings and neither can be used to corroborate the other. Lastly, the study population mainly consisted of Chinese middle-aged and older adults, among whom the proportion of nappers is relatively high. Therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to other age groups and cultures. Due to numerous limitations, our study also cannot demonstrate a causal relationship.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides new evidence for the relationship between daytime napping duration and pan-cancer incidence. Based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database and Mendelian randomization analyses, this study found an inverse association between daytime napping duration and overall cancer risk: the longer the nap, the lower the risk of cancer. Compared with individuals who never nap, those who nap for more than 60 min experienced an approximately 41% reduction in cancer risk. However, due to the numerous limitations of this study, more accurate sleep-measurement tools and larger samples are needed in the future to further explore the relationship between nap duration and overall cancer risk.
