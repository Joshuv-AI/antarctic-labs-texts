Determinants of Drinking Water Filtration Decisions in Puerto Rican Households: A Logistic Regression Analysis of User Perceptions
Authors: Manner Thurlby Atimboro, Farah Mneimneh, Cristina Poleacovschi, Ivis Garcia, Robbyn Anand, Marcus Nartey, Toby N. T. Nelson, Beatrise Berzina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Puerto Rico has been severely impacted by climate-related issues and natural hazards, which have damaged water infrastructure and compromised overall water quality. This has led residents to mistrust water from municipal utility suppliers. As a result, Puerto Ricans have adopted alternative drinking sources and filtration methods, including reliance on bottled water and home water filtration methods. Although there is a good understanding that many residents mistrust tap water, there is limited understanding regarding whether and how users rely on home water filtration methods. We collected survey data from 506 residents in Puerto Rico in 2024. Logistic regression analysis shows that users typically filter their water due to a combination of demographic factors. For instance, income was a significant predictor (p-value <0.001), along with concerns about water quality, color, the perceived presence of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), overall dissatisfaction with their water quality, and perceived health risks associated with consuming tap water. This is the first study in Puerto Rico on household filtration and extends our knowledge about specific factors that influence public trust in water quality, ultimately supporting future evidence-based approaches to enhancing trust in tap water.

Introduction

The Puerto Rico Aqueduct and Sewer Authority (PRASA) is Puerto Rico’s main public water utility, serving approximately 97% of the population. The remaining 3% of the population is served by decentralized non-PRASA supplies such as bottled water, wells, and springs. One major event that severely disrupted both PRASA and non-PRASA water systems was Hurricane Maria. The hurricane damaged numerous PRASA-operated water treatment plants, leaving over 1 million residents without reliable access to clean water. Furthermore, several malfunctions and operational errors in the PRASA water system contributed to widespread disruptions in water service. This resulted from the filtration system at one facility being compromised by storm damage, leading to inconsistent chlorination of unfiltered water before distribution. Another facility system suffered electrical damage, leading to improper backwashing of its filters. Meanwhile, a third facility’s system intermittently supplied water without adequate chlorination. Finally, a fourth facility temporarily switched from chlorinated groundwater to surface water as a result of a power outage, which resulted in a tragic case of leptospirosis.

Non-PRASA systems serve approximately 3% of Puerto Rico’s population. These systems are small, unregulated, and often at risk of not meeting EPA drinking water quality standards. Despite these known challenges, residents exhibit a higher degree of trust in these systems because non-PRASA systems are community-managed. Non-PRASA aqueducts were also affected by Hurricane Maria, with one in five remaining nonoperational months after the storm. The damage to the water systems during Hurricane Maria amplified the existing drinking water crisis on the island. Prior to the hurricane, over 70% of the water supply violated the Safe Drinking Water Act’s testing requirements for lead and copper levels. , This series of shocks and stresses has affected users’ behaviors. Users have increasingly expressed persistent concerns about water quality, including visible contamination such as discolored water and sediment, unpleasant odors and tastes, and reports of microbial and chemical pollutants. As a result, users rely on alternative water sources such as bottled and well water, even though it comes at a higher cost and effort. Bottled water often requires repeated purchases and the physical transport and storage of heavy containers. Well water may involve traveling to off-site or communal wells, manually hauling water over varying distances, and maintaining the pumping equipment. Another approach to address tap water quality concerns includes home water filtration methods, also known as point-of-use (POU) devices, that remove impurities from tap water at the location where it is consumed. POU devices range from units that attach directly to existing taps to units that are portable jugs and employ various treatment methods, including activated carbon filters, reverse osmosis (RO) systems, ultraviolet (UV) disinfection systems, and ceramic filters. Users buy home water filtration devices to improve not only water quality but also the organoleptic properties, categorized as color, odor, sediment, and taste.

Despite their potential benefits, the use of home filtration devices comes with a cost burden related to the upfront device costs and operational costs, particularly for older adults or low-income households. For example, the inclusion of maintenance costs substantially increases the share of income devoted to water expenses, particularly for the bottom 20% of the income distribution in regions such as Puerto Rico. While previous literature has not evaluated these costs, our review of online resources shows that a Brita filter costs approximately $30, and an RO system costs around $200. These costs are similar on average across the US, suggesting that for a region like PR, the cost would be a burden to residents, considering their income distribution. For these low-income households, reliance on alternative sources can mean paying the highest price for water, which often pushes their total water expenditure ratio above the 3% international affordability standard. There is also a cognitive and physical burden related to maintaining the device, as users often replace the filter cartridge on their own. If filters are not changed regularly, there is a concern that they may even worsen the water quality. Although the use of home filtration devices is expected to be widespread in Puerto Rico due to distrust in tap water, this phenomenon and its drivers have not been documented before. Existing studies outside Puerto Rico show that users filter their water due to distrust in tap water , and organoleptic concerns, including bad smells, cloudy water, or bad taste, regardless of whether the water meets safety standards. , Collective beliefs about water quality and demographic characteristics such as income, education, household size, and geographic location also play a role. However, these findings largely reflect contexts with different institutional and cultural dynamics, raising questions about how similar drivers operate in Puerto Rico, where water quality concerns are compounded by repeated infrastructure failures and climate-related disruptions. Water filtration is also more prone in locations where water source contamination and natural hazards are more frequent. Despite these studies, the understanding of the level of home filtration in Puerto Rico and its antecedents is limited. Addressing this gap will provide an understanding of the level of distrust in tap water and bring awareness to the demand for water filtration in Puerto Rico for policymakers. We hypothesize that the main factors driving the use of home filtration include distrust in tap water, concerns about taste, odor, and appearance, demographic characteristics, and experiences with water system disruptions such as Hurricane Maria.

Methods

Sample Selection and Sample Size

A survey was conducted across Puerto Rico using a stratified random sampling method. Municipalities were first categorized as urban or rural based on U.S. Census classifications, and random samples of households were then selected within each stratum using address-based listings from municipal and postal records. Within each selected household, one adult respondent was randomly chosen to participate. This approach was chosen to ensure the broad representativeness of households across urban and rural regions, increasing the validity and generalizability of the results. The sampling process was initiated in early June 2024. Participants were contacted via email or phone call, depending on regional accessibility. All participants voluntarily consented to participate in the study. A total of 506 responses (n = 506) were collected from participants across various regions of Puerto Rico. About 50% (252) of respondents depended on bottled water, which exempted them from the further question of water filtration since bottled water is considered potable and therefore does not require further treatment. In addition to thematic content, the survey captured demographic characteristics to contextualize the analysis (see results in Table ).

Survey Administration

The Center for Survey Statistics and Methodology (CSSM) at the Iowa State University (ISU) Statistical Laboratory, which specializes in managing research that includes survey design, implementation, and data collection, was responsible for the national survey. CSSM hired a third-party vendor, Dynata, to administer the survey. The study followed all guidelines based on the Iowa State University Institutional Review Board Protocol (#24-015-00).

Survey Construct

The survey included 165 items and was administered between June and July 2024. It addressed three key thematic areas: (1) Current drinking water sources, quality, and concerns; (2) drinking water supply challenges; (3) presence of contaminants and current purification systems (see Figure ). Independent variables include perceptions and experiences related to water quality. More precisely, to evaluate the organoleptic characteristics of tap water that may affect filtration decisions, respondents were asked about the taste, smell, and appearance of their water from the following sources: PRASA, Community Aqueducts, cisterns, wells, rivers, oceans, or other sources. Additionally, the survey explored respondents’ awareness and perceived presence of common drinking water contaminants (see Appendix A), as well as their satisfaction with the water source and concern about health risks from specific contaminants. Respondents also reported whether they had tested their drinking water quality. These items aimed to assess whether knowledge or concern about specific contaminants influenced the likelihood of adopting water filtration practices. Finally, the models account for demographics and socioeconomic factors that could influence drinking water filtration decisions. Age was coded as a continuous measure. Gender was coded as being dichotomous. Finally, the average annual income is included as a continuous measure.

Statistical Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed by using Binary Logistic Regression (BLR) in STATA18. The use of BLR in STATA18 for data analysis was specifically chosen due to its suitability for modeling a binary dependent variable, which in this study pertains to the decision to filter drinking water (filter/not filter). This methodological choice offered a robust analytical approach that did not necessitate the more stringent distributional assumptions required by alternative methods such as discriminant analysis, particularly given that the analysis focused on 254 respondents for filtration decisions. Although other statistical techniques were considered, BLR is a commonly employed and well-validated statistical technique in public health and behavioral studies that investigates binary outcomes related to water perceptions and decisions. This broad application establishes a strong precedent for its use in this research. Initially, descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were conducted for key variables, including organoleptic perceptions, contaminant concerns, satisfaction with the water source, and demographic factors, to explore their relationships with water filtration decisions. Subsequently, logistic regression was utilized to assess the predictive power of these variables on the likelihood of filtering drinking water. This method was selected due to its appropriateness for modeling a binary dependent variable. A significance threshold of 0.05 was applied throughout the analyses. Respondents receiving water from PRASA were more likely to filter their water (β = 0.485, p = 0.039), suggesting that reliance on the public water utility is associated with risk mitigation practices (filtration). This implies that if users believe the utility is unreliable, they may be more proactive in mitigating risks themselves through POU filtration methods. On the other hand, if users trust the utility to handle problems, such as ensuring clean water during or after a flood, users may not feel the need to take their own precautions, like treating their own water or conserving water.

One of the most statistically significant predictors was income, which displayed a t-value of 3.97 and a p-value < 0.001, indicating a highly significant association. However, the coefficient for income was reported as 0.000. This coefficient value results from rounding, not from a lack of association. Given that income was likely measured in small units (e.g., dollars), the effect of a one-unit increase (i.e., $1) on the probability of adoption is extremely small, so small that it is rounded to zero in the output. Nevertheless, the high t-value and low p-value confirm that the variable contributes meaningfully to the model. This suggests that individuals with higher income are significantly more likely to use filtration, but the magnitude of the effect becomes meaningful only when considered across larger changes in income (e.g., thousands of dollars). Meanwhile, age showed a positive, marginally significant relationship with filtration (β = 0.021, p = 0.068), indicating that older individuals were slightly more likely to adopt filtration.

The perceived water discoloration (color) was positively associated with filtration at a 10% significance level (β = 0.630, p = 0.053), and reported concerns about PFAS contamination were significantly associated with the filtration decision (β = 0.738, p = 0.028). Other variables, including gender, taste, odor, and various reported contaminants (e.g., microplastics, E. coli, lead, solvents), did not exhibit significant associations. The constant term was negative and significant (β = −3.388, p < 0.001), indicating a low baseline likelihood of filtration when all predictors are at zero. These findings suggest that both demographic factors and specific water quality concerns, particularly regarding PFAS and visual indicators, play a role in shaping household water filtration decisions.

This study presents one of the pioneering works examining factors influencing people’s decision to filter tap water in Puerto Rico. We describe a multidimensional framework that reveals how visible cues, invisible threats, institutional trust, and demographic factors influence water treatment behaviors. Study findings demonstrate that filtration adoption is driven by a complex interplay of organoleptic perceptions, contamination fears, and socioeconomic capabilities rather than simple dissatisfaction with water service.

The most documented factor in the literature related to distrust in tap water is organoleptic properties. , Assessing organoleptic properties in practice is more complex, with users using one to multiple properties to assess water quality. However, color is generally found to be the more salient organoleptic property that influences users’ distrust in water across numerous studies. ,− This study shows that concerns about color do more than cause distrust in tap water; it influences users’ behaviors by prompting them to filter their tap water before drinking. Users think of clear water as pure and safe, while discolored or cloudy water can evoke immediate concerns about contamination. Color is a highly visible cue to users, but discoloration does not always indicate health risk. Although users’ organoleptic concerns can provide initial cues about the water quality, accurate assessments of any safety risks require additional testing and expertise. Information about how discoloration can be a cue of both healthy and unhealthy water needs to be included as part of water literacy campaigns in Puerto Rico. The two other organoleptic properties (smell and taste) did not have an effect on users’ filtration decisions, although the two properties affected Puerto Ricans’ distrust in tap water. The findings confirm that smell and taste are milder forms of dissatisfaction with water and are not sufficient to alter water consumption behaviors. Although this study’s survey data cannot provide an explanation for how organoleptic properties impact filtration, one explanation for why smell and taste are less salient in determining filtration is due to users being more aware and used to chlorine and water treatment chemicals, which impact taste and smell.

The study also proposes that perceptions about contaminants that are invisible to the users influence filtration decisions, although water utilities will communicate these contaminants as part of consumer confidence reports or required water boil alerts. The significance of PFAS concerns (β = 0.738, p = 0.028) but not that of E. coli (β = −0.252, p = 0.479) reveals fundamental differences in how residents perceive and respond to these contamination types. PFAS significance reflects residents’ fear of invisible, persistent “forever chemicals” that standard treatment cannot remove, creating lasting anxiety about unknown exposure. Unlike E. coli, PFAS contamination is undetectable without sophisticated testingit has no taste, smell, or colormaking household filtration feel like the only protective option against this invisible threat. The persistence of PFAS in both the environment and human body, coupled with its links to cancer and various systemic health effects, creates a chronic fear that drives permanent behavioral change through filtration adoption.

In contrast, E. coli’s nonsignificance (and negative coefficient) suggests residents view bacterial contamination as a manageable, episodic risk adequately addressed through existing protocols. When E. coli is detected, utilities issue immediate boil water advisories, providing clear, temporary actions that residents can take. Once the advisory is lifted, the threat passes. This acute, visible response system may reduce filtration adoption among households aware of E. coli as they rely on boiling during emergencies rather than investing in permanent filtration. The negative coefficient implies that knowledge of E. coli might be associated with lower filtration rates, possibly because residents trust that chlorination and utility monitoring adequately manage bacterial risks.

Generally, since Hurricane Maria, public awareness has grown regarding these contaminants, with respondents indicating that they knew E. coli and PFAS were present in their water. This may be attributed to the growing recognition of their impacts by the public and the increasing focus on PFAS and E. coli in water studies. E. coli is a type of bacteria with strains that can cause severe gastrointestinal illness, including diarrhea, particularly in vulnerable populations like children and older adults. However, its immediate, treatable nature contrasts sharply with PFAS’s long-term, untreatable presence. The severity of the associated risks of these two contaminants and how popular they are may have influenced how highly knowledgeable users are of them. In essence, while knowledge of both contaminants being present in water contributes to health concerns and reduces trust in the water source, only PFASwith its invisibility, persistence, and lack of standard treatment optionsdrives the adoption of filtration methods. The severity and public recognition of PFAS as an unmanageable threat, versus E. coli as a manageable one, explain why only PFAS concerns translate into long-term filtration behavior, making it a crucial step for many residents’ peace of mind and control over their drinking water quality.

Moreover, satisfaction with the water source was not significant in the models. One possible explanation is that the nature of this survey item was too broad or subjective to capture the specific concerns that prompt filtration. For instance, an individual may report general satisfaction with water service, such as consistent supply or pressure, while still harboring doubts about safety and quality, which can independently motivate filtration. , This disconnect suggests that satisfaction alone may not reflect the nuanced factors that drive household water filtration decisions, such as sensory discomfort and health concerns. Additionally, existing research shows that trust, rather than satisfaction, is often a more reliable predictor of consumer behavior in the context of water use. Users may express neutral or even positive satisfaction scores while still lacking confidence in the safety of their water, particularly when there’s a history of service interruptions or contamination events. , Another possible reason is the limited communication between water providers and consumers, especially regarding water quality reports and contaminant risks. While utilities are required to distribute consumer confidence reports, inconsistencies in how this information is delivered and interpreted may prevent it from meaningfully shaping satisfaction or filtering behavior. While satisfaction captures a general sense of contentment, it may not accurately reflect the more immediate trigger, such as taste, color, odor, or safety fear, that leads individuals to filter tap water.

Finally, regression results show that older individuals were slightly more likely to implement water filtration technologies. This aligns with emerging studies of post-Hurricane Maria communities in Puerto Rico, which consistently identify age as a key factor shaping water-related risk perceptions and health vulnerability. For instance, EPA-funded research involving over 500 interviews across multiple municipalities revealed that older adults frequently expressed mistrust in tap water, often stemming from their experiences during water-service disruptions caused by natural disasters, and that age significantly influenced older adults’ risk perception and behavior related to water safety (objective 2). Similarly, the 2024 study of older adults surviving Hurricane Maria found that the choice of drinking water source, including reliance on municipal or bottled water, was closely associated with older adults’ psychological resilience, further linking age, water source preference, and resilience in the Puerto Rican context. Together, these findings provide empirical support for interpreting the positive age coefficient in your model as reflective of older adults’ greater propensity for water filtration adoption, driven by increased risk perception and mistrust following infrastructure failures.

Conclusions

This study examined how water organoleptic properties, perception of contamination, satisfaction with water services, and demographic characteristics influence household-level decisions to filter drinking water in Puerto Rico. While public trust in water systems was frequently discussed in the literature, our findings suggest that, although conceptually important, trust was not a statistically significant predictor of filtration behavior in this study. Instead, factors such as color and the presence of PFAS and E. coli contaminants emerged as more direct motivators of filtration decisions, underscoring the importance of visible, sensory indicators in shaping user behavior.

Demographic variations also influenced filtration choices with income levels playing a notable role. Individuals with higher incomes may interpret and take action on water quality risks more critically due to their ability to afford filtration systems.

The identified factors that significantly impact filtration decisions could provide data that will help water utilities better understand the situation on the ground, such as the public’s perception of water aesthetics (like color). This model can provide municipal utility providers, specifically PRASA, with a real-time understanding of public sentiments to ensure a more user-centered approach to addressing issues of water quality. Considering the complexity of PRASA’s governance and fiscal structure, like the loss of approximately 65% of the water produced due to leaks, targeted policy interventions based on public perception are essential. Policymakers in Puerto Rico, including PRASA and the Department of Health, can institutionalize consistent and transparent assessments of community perceptions of water quality, access to filtration resources, and trust in public water systems. PRASA could also integrate these systematic perception assessments into its existing Consumer Confidence Report (CCR) program. PRASA is federally required to produce CCRs but has previously underutilized or failed to share water quality testing results consistently with the public, contributing to a lack of trust. The ability of PRASA to address factors flagged by the public, such as the color of the water, will contribute to restoring public trust, particularly since aesthetic concerns drive user adoption of home filtration systems from this work’s findings. Perception data could be used to target resources, such as the provision of temporary filtration systems, to the most vulnerable households during water. Given that low-income families in Puerto Rico currently bear the financial burden of home filtration, PRASA and the Department of Health can leverage the significant federal funding available, including the $455 million anticipated from the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act for drinking water improvements and the $6.5 billion federally funded capital improvement program. So PRASA can tap into EPA programs that subsidize filtration for disadvantaged communities, while long-term infrastructure fixes are underway, addressing the equity argument. To address the long history of governance failures that have eroded community trust, PRASA should consider establishing community water quality advisory boards as a specific governance mechanism. These boards would create formal channels for continuous community feedback that is currently missing.

Most manufacturers of water filtration technologies design their systems with aesthetic and economic considerations in mind, while regulations ensure contaminant removal to meet safety standards. In addition to making ongoing improvements, manufacturers can consider the needs of the user when designing these technologies. Addressing concerns such as color could improve user adoption.

Limitations

The study provided insights into the factors influencing water filtration decisions among Puerto Rican residents; however, it is important to acknowledge some limitations. This study is part of a 165-item survey in which we see the potential for fatigue among respondents due to the length of the questions. The length of the survey potentially affected their attention and the quality of response but cannot be guaranteed since the survey completion time was not tracked. Subsequent studies should consider much shorter surveys, focusing on filtration decision factors, to ensure the effectiveness of the approach and the quality of the responses.

The logistic regression analysis has provided valuable insights into the relationship between filtration and various factors. However, data were collected during a single time frame and within the specific context of Puerto Rico which has perpetually faced water quality challenges.

Among the 506 total survey respondents, 70% were women, and within the filtration group (n = 254), the ratio was just about the same with 71% women. Future studies could be improved by ensuring gender balance, considering the region’s population is approximately 53% female (about 17% more women participated in the survey than men).

The current set of factors may not represent all of the possible water filtration influence factors that a more expansive model could (e.g., cultural practices, historical exposure, or geographic difference). Also, the questions regarding contaminants and concerns for health risks may not have been uniformly understood by all participants considering their varied educational backgrounds. We also acknowledge that there may have been a social desirability bias on the part of respondents, which could have led them to exaggerate behaviors perceived as favorable or underreport those viewed negatively. For instance, claiming to filter tap water or minimizing reliance on bottled water due to growing environmental awareness when one does not.

Despite the above limitations, the outcome of this study has shed new light on the underexplored issue of why people choose to filter their water and provides valuable insights that can lead efforts to address the issue of water quality and build trust. We hope future research will explore different demographics and contexts and use strategies to minimize these limitations.
