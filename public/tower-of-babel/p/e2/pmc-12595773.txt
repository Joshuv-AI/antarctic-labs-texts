Chinese physicians’ experiences with end-of-life communication and hospice care transition: a qualitative study with content analysis
Authors: Jie Zhong, Wenjuan Zhao, Xiaobin Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMainland China’s end-of-life care system remained underdeveloped marked by underutilization of hospice care services. Physicians-initiated end-of-life communication is likely to result in immediate enrollment in hospice care. However, there is a lack of empirical evidence on how physicians in China undertake end-of-life communication in advanced cancer. This qualitative study aimed to explore physicians’ experiences on discussing end-of-life matters and facilitating hospice care transition within families of patients with advanced cancer.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted. Using the purpose sampling, physicians were recruited from 8 hospitals in Shanghai, China. Physicians were eligible if they had experiences in treating patients with advanced cancer and were ineligible if they did not directly participate in end-of-life discussions with patients/families. Semi-structured interviews were conducted from August 2022 to February 2023. Qualitative data were analyzed by content analysis approach.ResultsAmong 18 physicians, three major themes emerged regarding barriers to effective end-of-life communication at physician, family, and system levels: (1) Physician avoidance in disclosing terminal prognosis: Prognostic uncertainty, Skill deficiency, Emotional burden, Concerns about therapeutic hope, and Systematic avoidance; (2) Family priority in end-of-life decision-making: Complex family dynamics, Cultural stigma, and Unstructured family coping; (3) Systematic fragmentation in hospice care integration: Limited awareness and understanding, Ineffective referral mechanisms, and Insufficient leadership support.ConclusionEnd-of-life communication in Shanghai is hindered by physician challenges, cultural norms favoring family decisions, and systemic gaps. Targeted training for non-hospice physicians, balanced approaches to patient autonomy and family roles, and strengthened referral/reimbursement policies are needed to improve hospice transitions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01924-x.Keywords: Physicians, Palliative care, Hospice care, Neoplasms

Introduction

Palliative and hospice care are crucial parts of person-centered care to meet a person’s physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines palliative care as a systematic approach provided to patients with life-threatening illnesses and their families, aimed at improving their quality of life and ability to cope with crises [1]. Cancer is the leading cause of death globally, with an estimated 9.6 million cancer deaths worldwide in 2018 and China accounting for 30% of global cancer deaths [2]. Recommended by the American Cancer Association, palliative care should be offered at the diagnosis of cancer, provided at the same time as part of cancer treatment, and continued after treatment is complete [3]. Hospice care is a subtype of palliative care that specifically focuses on end-of-life stage for patients with a prognosis of 6 months or less [4]. The goal is to help patients pass away comfortably, peacefully, and with dignity, while also providing support services to their families.

Insufficient shared decision-making and end-of-life communication hinder hospice care enrollment and quality of death [5]. Hospice care is often seen as “giving up,” despite evidence that patients and families report feelings of hope, relief, and improved quality of life due to the care provided [6]. Evidence shows that conversation about hospice care initiated by physicians is more likely to result in immediate enrollment in hospice care compared to those initiated by patients/families or non-physician providers [7, 8]. Patients and families desire to learn about hospice care from physicians at the most appropriate timing in their disease trajectory. Therefore, effective end-of-life communication on disclosing terminal prognosis and facilitating hospice care transition has a profound impact on preparing patients and families for the impending death. Benefits include reduced isolation, enhanced family concordance on care goals, higher satisfaction, and lower decision-making burden. It also correlates with better treatment outcomes and reduced end-of-life costs. Conversely, poor communication exacerbates psychological distress, depression, caregiving burdens, and interpersonal strain.

Mainland China’s end-of-life care system remains underdeveloped, and the quality of death remains relatively low. The Economist Intelligence Unit’s Quality of Death Index ranked mainland China 53 out of 80 countries in 2021, while Hong Kong and Taiwan ranking 9th and 3rd respectively [9]. Due to the underutilization of hospice care in China, most end-of-life patients do not receive sufficient management for pain and other physical, psychological, and spiritual issues [10]. Recognizing this gap, the Chinese government has initiated hospice care integration into health systems. A pilot project on hospice care was launched in 5 cities (including Shanghai) in 2017 and expanded to majority of provinces in 2023. Over the past five years, China has conducted various forms of training activities nationwide, cultivating multidisciplinary professionals (such as doctors, nurses, social workers, and volunteers) in hospice and palliative care [11]. However, current palliative care services remain predominantly physician led. Nurses often function in a primarily auxiliary capacity, while the roles of social workers and psychological counselors are frequently undervalued or underutilized. Shanghai is a pioneer in developing and implementing an early-stage, community-based hospice care service system [12, 13]. The community-based model provided early screening, positive evaluation, physiological support, psychological support, and social support to hospice care patients, though service implementation varies significantly across centers. However, the referral to community-based hospice care remains low. In 2024, Shanghai has a total of 261 hospice care facilities, spread across the city’s 248 community health centers and 13 general hospitals. The city currently has 1,224 inpatient hospice beds and provides over 1,800 home hospice beds annually [14]. Most patients with advanced cancer are predominantly treated in secondary and tertiary hospitals. Consequently, transition at the end-of-life stage requires a shift from hospital-based curative care to community-based comfort care. The lack of initiative among non-hospice care physicians to initiate end-of-life conversations could result in insufficient patient referrals from hospitals to community settings. Previous survey in Shanghai showed that 78.2% cancer patients did not receive introduction to hospice care from their physicians [15]. Despite this, there is currently a lack of empirical research on how physicians in China undertake end-of-life discussions in patients with life-threatening illnesses including advanced cancer [16]. Accordingly, this study aimed to explore physicians’ experiences on discussing end-of-life matters and facilitating hospice care transition within families of patients with advanced cancer in Shanghai, China. Our qualitative findings might provide potential strategies to support effective end-of-life communication and hospice care enrollment initiated by physicians.

Methods

Study design

This study used a descriptive qualitative design with content analysis to explore the lived experiences of experiences on end-of-life communication and hospice care transition. Content analysis was conducted to capture contextualized narratives from physicians and generate themes and codes based on representative narratives.

Study settings and subjects

We recruited physicians via purposive sampling from 3 tertiary and 5 secondary hospitals across urban (Xuhui, Jing’an, Putuo) and rural (Fengxian, Baoshan) districts of Shanghai (August 2022-February 2023). Eligible physicians were: (1) practicing clinicians treating diagnosed advanced cancer patients (distant metastasis); (2) formal hospital staff; and (3) voluntarily participating. We excluded those without direct end-of-life discussion experience or unlicensed trainees. Maximum variation in gender, age, years of experience, and professional title was considered to ensure diversity in data collection until data saturation was achieved. Except for the oncology-related specialty, we also extended to other specialties such as integrated traditional Chinese and Western medicine, in which physicians have the chance to interact with advanced cancer patients. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling. Eligible participants were approached by two researchers (WZ and XL) and invited to join the study. Written consent was obtained from all participating physicians.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews, initially developed by literature review and expert consultation. The original interview guide was pilot tested with two physicians to assess question clarity, flow, and its ability to elicit comprehensive responses. Based on this feedback, the formal guide was refined from a broader outline to include more specific prompts and concrete examples (Appendix 1). The finalized interview guideline included 2 closed questions (e.g., “Would you refer patients in need of hospice care to relevant hospitals?”) and 10 open-end questions (e.g., “In your opinion, what are the difficulties that hinder your discussions with patients and their families about hospice care?”). All interviews were conducted by the researcher (XL), an experienced female associate professor in hospice and palliative care. The purpose, significance, and methodology of the study were explained in detail. Participants provided basic information and consented to recorded interviews, which took place in a private, comfortable setting. Each interview lasted 30–45 min, focusing on end-of-life communication practices. Non-verbal cues such as tone, intonation, gestures, and facial expressions were also recorded in field notes. Data collection concluded when no new themes emerged.

Data analysis

Within 24 h after the interviews, the audio recordings were transcribed into text, and interview notes were promptly organized. The interview records of the participants were sequentially numbered from D1 to D18, and separate documents were created for each. Content analysis was applied for data analysis and NVIVO8.0 software was used. The specific analysis process was as follows: (1) The researcher repeatedly and carefully read the interview materials to familiarize themselves with the overall content until a sense of immersion in the data was achieved; (2) Each sentence was analyzed, and significant expressions were annotated for open coding; (3) Similar or related codes were compared and categorized, gradually forming themes and categories; (4) The themes, categories, and codes were defined, and representative examples were extracted from the interview materials [17]. To enhance the analytic rigor and mitigate subjective bias, we employed investigator triangulation through a systematic coding process. Specifically, our research team first developed a preliminary codebook based on interview guideline and initial reading of the transcripts. Then two coders (JZ and WZ) coded transcripts dependently during which they met after coding every 3 transcripts to compare their applications of the codes and refine the codebook by adding or revising codes and themes. While there were remaining discrepancies after each meeting, the entire research team gathered to reach consensus. While all coding processes were completed in Chinese, the codes and themes were translated into English by JZ and verified by WZ. We defined saturation as the point at which no new substantive themes or concepts were identified in the data, and the properties of existing themes were sufficiently developed. For retrospective assessment of data saturation, we reviewed the entire dataset to confirm that no new codes or themes were present in the later interviews.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Fudan University (Approval: YL-20230602-22). Written informed consent was obtained prior to the interview. Participants were informed that they could refuse to answer any questions or withdraw from the study at any time. Participant confidentiality was ensured by de-identifying interview transcripts, and only research members had access to the data. We offered each participant a gift worth 100 RMB after the interview for the appreciation of their time and efforts.

Results

Semi-structured qualitative interviews were conducted with 18 physicians. Their demographic information is presented in Table 1 and their current practices on end-of-life communication are summarized in Table 2. Their age ranged from 29 to 50 years old and half of them were female. Most of them (66.7%) reported having 1 to 10 times end-of-life communication with patients or families on a weekly basis. Most of them initiated end-of-life communication when the patient can no longer tolerate anti-tumor treatment (83.3%) or estimated survival rate is less than 3 months (83.3%). All participants heard of hospice care but less than half of them (7/18) received hospice care training. The duration of interviews ranged from 20 to 55 min with the average duration of 36 min. Through the systematic coding process, the team reached a consensus on the final thematic structure and data saturation was achieved without new codes or themes in later interviews. Three major themes emerged regarding the barriers to effective end-of-life communication at physician, family, and system levels: (1) Physician avoidance in disclosing terminal prognosis; (2) Family priority in end-of-life decision-making; (3) Systematic fragmentation in hospice care integration. Themes and codes

Physician avoidance in disclosing terminal prognosis

Despite recognizing the significance of effective end-of-life discussions - particularly in aligning patient-family wishes with end-of-life care and preparing them psychologically for the impending death - physicians expressed avoidance in initiating end-of-life conversation on terminal prognosis due to prognostic uncertainty, skill deficiency, emotional burden, concerns about therapeutic hope, and protective medical practice.

Prognostic uncertainty

Physicians in our interviews expressed their reluctancy to proactively initiate end-of-life discussions, which could be manifested by the tendency to delay end-of-life communication until the patient could no longer undergo anti-tumor treatment or was estimated with an expected survival period of less than three months. Participants reported variability in determining the timing for initiating end-of-life communication, with thresholds ranging from imminent death (hours to days), short-term survival (weeks), intermediate survival (one to two months), and longer-term transition points (less than three months). However, the lack of consensus on specific survival thresholds (days vs. weeks vs. months) reflected inherent challenges in prognostic certainty.

D2: “If the patient is likely to pass away within three months… you need to inform them that the condition is very serious, with an average survival period of less than three months.”

D18: “If it’s truly the terminal stage, you might estimate that the patient has only one or two months left, or even less if there are widespread metastases. This is just a rough assessment.”

D4: “When all indicators are poor, and survival is measured in weeks, or when the patient could pass away at any moment, we need to bring up end-of-life arrangements and discussions.”

Skill deficiency

Physicians identified significant challenges in disclosing terminal prognosis due to skill deficiency on discussing end-of-life issues. Despite frequent family requests to withhold information, as disease progressed and symptoms intensified, patients often began experiencing distress and requiring opportunities to engage in advance care planning. Discussing end-of-life issues, particularly disclosing terminal prognosis for the first time, requires advanced communication strategies. The participants described disclosing the information bit by bit like “squeezing toothpaste” depending on the family’s understanding of the disease and acceptance of death.

D4: “When the patient reacts emotionally, you need experience and skill to guide them through it. This is the most difficult part of the communication process.”

D15: “It’s not that my communication skills are excellent; it entirely depends on the family’s understanding of the disease, their acceptance of the illness, and their acceptance of death.”

Emotional burden

Physicians described end-of-life communication as profoundly “emotionally draining”. Daily disclosing terminal condition often generated feelings of frustration, contrasting with the sense of professional fulfillment derived from curative treatment. This emotional burden could be manifested in three ways: pre-disclose anxiety (concerns about potential patient distress or emotional outbursts), in-the-moment tension (acute stress during the actual delivery of difficult news), and post-disclosure impact (lingering emotional affect that could reduce clinical focus and impair subsequent task performance).

D17: “The patient or family might already be under a lot of stress, and after the conversation, they might break down emotionally. Many family members cry in front of you. This can affect your mood and efficiency in your next tasks. When communicating with other patients or families, you might worry about causing them distress. Your own mood can influence the next family you talk to, so you have to be extra careful and cautious.”

Concerns about therapeutic hope

Physicians expressed their concerns about the potential negative psychological consequences of end-of-life communication as “a huge blow”. Several participants highlighted those patients receiving this information often experience profound psychological distress (e.g., anxiety, resistance, loss of hope, diminished self-efficacy, or even suicide), which could lead to non-compliance and refusal of treatments. To maintain therapeutic hope, some physicians in our interviews advocated for delayed end-of-life communication to enhance patient/family understanding and adaptation to the disease progression. On the contrary, some participants advocated that informed patients would have better compliance with treatment and less psychological distress. Patients need to have the opportunity to seek further evaluation and make decisions about their own care plan.

D5: “Patients in the advanced stages are under a lot of psychological pressure. If I, as a doctor, ask about their wishes, it might scare them. They might feel that their time is limited, which could increase their psychological burden.”

D1: “If the patient is already in a bad mood upon admission - most patients in cancer hospitals are - and I suddenly tell them everything, it could be a huge blow. They might lose confidence and refuse treatment, which is not good.”

D11: “The patient needs to know, or they may need to seek further evaluation at a tertiary hospital. The patient must be informed, otherwise their compliance may decrease, and they may develop emotional issues.”

Systematic avoidance

At the legal level, physicians often experience role ambiguity or adopt defensive non-disclosure practices to avoid potential medical disputes with families. Non-hospice care physicians may lack awareness that end-of-life communication falls within their professional responsibilities, frequently perceiving it as the exclusive domain of hospice specialists. Adhering to China’s regulations, which mandates patient disclosure but permits family-mediated communication when direct disclosure is deemed inappropriate, physicians often recognize families as the primary medical decision-makers for end-of-life care. This protective approach illustrates how physicians navigate the complex interplay of legal obligations, cultural expectations, and clinical judgment in end-of-life communication in China.

D1: “We don’t know whether the law requires us to tell the patient or not. But if we tell the patient now, the family might immediately file a complaint.”

Family priority in end-of-life decision-making

Within Chinese cultural and social contexts, family-oriented decision making heavily influenced end-of-life practices, including complex family dynamics, cultural stigma, and unstructured family coping.

Complex family dynamics

Physicians acknowledge that the family’s wishes must be respected, and the physicians need to balance the end-of-life needs of both the patient and the family. During the patient’s treatment, immediate family members (typically adult children) were usually the ones involved in decision-making. They were most familiar with the patient’s treatment process, condition, and psychological state. However, they were sometimes pressured by extended family members who viewed giving up resuscitation as unfilial, making it difficult to initiate end-of-life discussions even when the patient is in the terminal stage. When the family insisted on aggressive treatment, initiating hospice care transitions became particularly challenging.

D16: “The immediate family is usually calm and rational. They know the patient has seen many specialists and is in the terminal stage, unable to eat and suffering greatly. Some even ask for relief from suffering. But when distant relatives return and see the situation, they often pressure the family to pursue extreme treatments, believing that giving up is unfilial. The pressure from relatives, friends, and neighbors can be overwhelming.”

D12: “First, I assess the patient’s general condition and diagnosis. If it’s an acute cancer and the patient is elderly, we usually explain that the prognosis is poor. Treatment decisions are based on the family’s wishes. Some families may directly state that they do not wish for aggressive treatment and only want to reduce the patient’s suffering.”

Cultural stigma

Many Chinese families view death negatively due to cultural taboos, making them reluctant to discuss end-of-life matters. As a result, some participants avoid terms like “hospice care” or “palliative care.” In Chinese tradition, directly addressing death is highly taboo. Families often inquired about disease progression, treatment costs, chances of recovery, the implications of continuing or stopping treatment, whether to disclose the diagnosis to the patient, and next steps. However, when communicating with families, participants deliberately avoided broaching end-of-life topics, as raising the possibility of a patient’s impending death could deeply distress them.

D1: “Patients are often very taboo about this. Telling them outright that they don’t have much time left can be a huge shock, especially for their children.”

D15: “Many patients themselves are unwilling to accept death. They insist on staying in the hospital, even if their family has already come to terms with the situation. The patient might be stubborn, saying, ‘I want to stay here, even if I have to undergo another round of chemotherapy or radiation.’”.

Unstructured family coping

Some families may cope relatively better due to the prolonged disease course, the patient’s advanced age, sufficient time for psychological adjustment, and a more comprehensive understanding of the patient’s experiences. On the contrary, when patients are younger or have experienced a short, rapidly progressing illness, their families often lack sufficient psychological preparation for impending death. Many still cling to hope for a cure, making it difficult to accept the reality of the terminal condition. In such cases, discussions about end-of-life matters were often met with resistance or denial, requiring physicians to invest significant time in helping families come to terms with the patient’s condition. Conversely, families with stronger coping mechanisms and better psychological preparation were more likely to acknowledge the patient’s terminal prognosis and the inevitability of death, facilitating smoother communication.

D15: “When a patient has been ill for a long time or has visited many hospitals, and each hospital has told them the same thing, they tend to accept it. However, if the illness has progressed quickly or the patient is young, the family’s acceptance is often lower. Sometimes, with families who cope well, I can clarify everything in less than five minutes, and they fully accept it. But with families who struggle to accept the situation, it can take me over half an hour, or even an hour, to explain everything by the patient’s bedside.”

Systematic fragmentation in hospice care integration

While physicians acknowledged hospice care’s benefits - such as preserving dignity through longer stays, improved environments, and flexible family visits, its adoption remained low due to systematic barriers in limited awareness and understanding, ineffective referral mechanisms, and insufficient leadership support.

Limited awareness and understanding

Without proper public education, many patients and families resisted hospice care due to public misconceptions. Long-term hospitalized patients were often reluctant to transition to community health centers, driven by deep trust in tertiary hospitals and fears of being “abandoned.” Further, without proper hospice care training, some physicians might perceive hospice care as merely “waiting for death,” failing to recognize its value in improving end-of-life quality. They felt that recommending hospice care conflicted with their role as a healer, compounded by limited exposure to its principles in their clinical practice. This lack of understanding led to avoidance in discussing hospice options with patients and families. Notably, nearly half of participants were unaware of community-based hospice care services and their specific roles in hospice care transitions.

D11: “As doctors, we focus on saving lives…But letting someone die peacefully doesn’t seem like what a doctor should do. It feels contrary to our original purpose in studying medicine. Some doctors actively avoid this topic, and many still refuse to discuss it, viewing hospice care as simply waiting for death. There’s a lot of misunderstanding and ignorance about hospice care…”.

Ineffective referral mechanisms

When discussing hospice care with patients, physicians often struggled to offer clear guidance on the hospice care services available or the transfer pathways. Most participants voiced a need for a formalized referral system, yet none of the eight institutions represented had established structured collaborations with community-based hospice care systems. Some participants indicated that establishing a formal referral system between tertiary hospitals and community health centers would benefit patients by preserving their dignity in death and allowing for extended hospice stays. For families, it would make visiting easier and reduce caregiving pressure. For physicians, it would facilitate communication with community health centers, mitigate legal risks, optimize time management, and relieve bed shortages. However, designing and implementing such a system presents challenges in the additional workload for tertiary hospitals to provide ongoing guidance to community centers, staffing and resource allocation concerns in community centers, and the necessity to establish clear admission and discharge criteria.

D3: “We could say, ‘We have a patient who will be discharged soon and needs hospice care. Could you assess them before discharge to determine their condition? If they have less than two weeks left, do you have beds available?’ Or we could create a networked system to see which hospitals have available beds and coordinate with them.”

D9: “Under the first-visit responsibility system, many issues arise, such as costs and departmental evaluations. Public hospitals are now evaluated based on mortality rates, making referrals difficult. In a few years, Shanghai’s healthcare system may undergo reforms, such as implementing DIP or DCP (diagnosis-related group payment systems). If my department is labeled as interventional medicine, we might only be able to admit interventional surgery patients, and similar cases might no longer be accepted due to insurance restrictions.”

Insufficient leadership support

Physicians frequently expressed concerns about the limited capacity of community-based hospice services, which could undermine their initiative to facilitate end-of-life conversation on hospice care transition with patients and families. Hospice care often generates lower economic returns compared to intensive, curative care, leading to reduced institutional prioritization. Prolonged bed occupancy by hospice care patients would reduce turnover rates, complex insurance reimbursement issues, and impact institutional performance evaluations based on mortality rates. Additionally, underdeveloped community healthcare systems face severe physician shortages, leaving hospice care understaffed and functionally constrained.

D15: “Ultimately, the problem is that these patients occupy beds for too long, which is the key issue. Only when bed occupancy is no longer a metric for evaluating hospital performance will real change begin. Issues like insurance reimbursement and other financial systems also play a role.”

Discussion

Main findings

This qualitative study contributed to the existing literature by providing the perspectives of Chinese physicians in discussing end-of-life matters and facilitating hospice care transition within families of patients with advanced cancer. Our qualitative findings aligned with the existing evidence from the perspectives of Chinese patients on the tendency of being reluctant to discuss end-of-life care and leaving families to be decision makers rather than patients themselves. Additionally, our findings expanded on physicians’ experiences on initiating community-based hospice care transitions during their clinical practice in Shanghai. Patients with advanced cancer indicated that talking about end-of-life matters was emotionally burdensome and useless to their treatment [18]. Our interviewed physicians resonated that daily end-of-life communication was emotionally draining, and they were concerned about the negative impact on therapeutic hope in patients. The avoidance of having end-of-life communication might result in low quality of death in patients, because of the unknown situation of their disease prognosis and the missing opportunity of advance care planning about their own goal of care [19]. On the contrary, proactive initiation by physicians and active engagement by patients in end-of-life communication could contribute to care plans aligning with the willingness and values of patients [20]. Our qualitative findings not only identified the challenges perceived by physicians but also offered valuable insights for improving these practices.

Physicians often expect families to maintain rational and objective perspectives regarding the patient’s disease prognosis. However, if ambiguous information is provided which defers the difficult task of comprehensive disclosure, patients and families might fail to develop a realistic understanding of the disease prognosis [21]. As a result, when hospice care transition becomes medically appropriate, many families retain unrealistic treatment expectations due to fragmented communication. Physicians often overestimate the harms of truth-telling to patients, contributing to emotional burden in daily end-of-life communication. There is no evidence supporting that concealing the truth helps patients feel better, on the contrary, research underscores high levels of anxiety and depression among patients who are not correctly informed with respect to their disease prognosis [22]. A survey in Southeast Asian reported that 62.1% of palliative care patients wanted full disclosure of information and 20.7% of patients felt dissatisfied with the information provided by physicians [23]. The lack of truth-telling results in passive engagement and even exclusion of patients in their own care planning [24]. Therefore, cultivating some extent of transparency to patients could alleviate physician emotional burden and empower patients to express their end-of-life preferences.

Physicians acknowledged family priority in end-of-life decision-making indicated by complex family dynamics, cultural stigma, and unstructured family coping. Aligning with previous findings in Chinese families [16, 18], family-oriented decision-making, compounded by death taboos, was one of the critical challenges experienced by Chinese physicians to initiate end-of-life communication. In our qualitative interviews, Chinese physicians indicated that they had to balance the needs between patients and families. In contexts of Chinese cultural and social norms, families (typically adult children) not only deal with the loss of their loved one but act as the primary decision maker for their loved one’s care plans. Putting emphasis on collectivism, adult children are expected to make the final decisions on their parents’ treatments [23, 25]. Consequently, joint end-of-life decisions are made between physicians and families without engagement of patients themselves. On the other hand, “giving up” life-saving treatment may be regarded as lack of filial piety, a major traditional norm in Chinese families. Adult children might suffer due to the struggle between their own filial attitudes (e.g., prolonging parental life at all costs) and filial behaviors (e.g., consenting to comfort care aligning with parental wills) during the end-of-life stage of their parents’ lives [26]. Not only do they hesitate to inform their parents about terminal prognosis, but they also often object healthcare professionals’ opinions to convey them such bad news [27].

Clinical implications

Our qualitative findings suggested that standardized communication tools such as advance directive and serious illness conversation guide could provide structured frame to end-of-life communication. Poorly delivered prognostic information can disorient patients and families, exacerbate psychological distress, and contribute to the underutilization of hospice care, and compromise the quality of death [28]. Additionally, to increase the willingness and readiness to participate in end-of-life conversations, physicians need to understand the most fundamental and profound fear of death in patients and families. By encouraging patients to share life narratives and values [29, 30], physicians can reframe end-of-life communication as an opportunity to honor self-identify as death is a natural part of life.

Our qualitative findings suggested that non-hospice care physicians - who often serve as gatekeepers to hospice care services - demonstrated a high variety of understanding on hospice care indications and benefits. Despite the increased support from the Chinese government for hospice care, a lack of public education makes physicians unaware of the benefits of hospice care and patients/families regard the transition to hospice care as abandonment [31]. The China’s hospice care training model with local characteristics is still under exploration. Research recommended that targeted training for Chinese healthcare providers should focus on psychological and spiritual care, the management of pain and other symptoms, and traditional Chinese medicine [12]. Additionally, hospice care institutions need to provide clear clarifications of their service elements and charging standards to facilitate medical reimbursement. Lastly, local leadership support is necessary to provide financial and policy support to the implementation of hospice care. For example, standardized transitions between curative care settings (hospitals) and comfort-focused care settings (hospice care programs) with clear reimbursement policies could eliminate the barriers of initiating end-of-life conversation about hospice care transition between non-hospice care physicians and patients.

Limitations

There are several limitations to be considered. The qualitative study was conducted in Shanghai to explore the challenges in discussing end-of-life matters and facilitating hospice care transitions experienced by physicians. Our exploratory qualitative findings informed the significance of integrating hospice care services, therefore, more evidence from less developed regions will be needed to identify future venues for multi-center or quantitative studies. Second, we primarily focused on non-hospice care physicians about their experiences in hospice care transition, while the explanation from hospice care physicians and the training they received have not been integrated and fully explored. Lastly, we recruited the sample size of 18 physicians from different specialty (mainly oncology) and achieved thematic saturation without emerging codes after discussion. However, theoretical saturation [32] may require a larger sample size to fully understand the complex phenomenon of end-of-life communication in contexts of Chinese cultural and social norms.

Conclusion

This qualitative study aimed to explore the perceived challenges by physicians in end-of-life communication with families of patients with advanced cancer. Semi-structured interviews with 18 physicians revealed challenges at physician, family, and system levels. To address these challenges, standardized communication tools could provide structured frame to end-of-life communication. Culturally sensitive training programs should also be developed to help physicians navigate family dynamics while respecting patient preferences. Additionally, clearer hospice referral pathways and reimbursement policies would encourage physician-initiated transitions. By implementing these strategies, China’s healthcare system can better support patients, families, and physicians in making informed, compassionate end-of-life care decisions.
