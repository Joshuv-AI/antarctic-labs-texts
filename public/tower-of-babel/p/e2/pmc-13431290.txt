Mixture diffusion model for multimodal antibody design
Authors: Vasanth Durvasula, Tiara Natasha Binte Sayuti, Jagath C Rajapakse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibody design requires modeling complementarity-determining region (CDR) loops that are highly flexible and adopt diverse conformations to achieve high-affinity antigen binding. Current diffusion-based generative models almost universally adopt unimodal distributions to parameterize sequence–structure transitions, which produce smooth conformations but constrain generation to a single conformational mode. This limitation impedes the exploration of alternative high-affinity binding conformations, particularly for challenging targets where exceptional binders may exist in low-probability regions of the conformational space. To address this, we introduce the mixture diffusion model for multimodal antibody design, a denoising diffusion probabilistic model that uses mixture density parameterizations for both positional and rotational updates. Through experiments on antibody–antigen complexes from the Structural Antibody Database (SAbDab), we find that increasing the number of mixture components improves model performance by capturing distinct canonical-like backbone conformations of CDRs. Our model achieves competitive amino acid recovery and binding-affinity-related metrics while maintaining physically consistent backbones. Through our results, we establish mixture-based diffusion modeling as a practical path toward discovering high-quality antibody conformations that remain inaccessible to conventional single-mode diffusion frameworks.Keywords: antibody design, conformational diversity, diffusion models, Gaussian mixtures, sequence–structure co-design

Introduction

Antibodies are widely exploited as therapeutic and diagnostic molecules because their highly diverse repertoires enable selective recognition and neutralization of a broad spectrum of disease-associated antigens, including viral particles and tumor-associated proteins [1, 2]. As a result, antibodies constitute one of the fastest-growing classes of biologic drugs [3]. Binding specificity is primarily determined by six complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), among which CDR-H3 exhibits the greatest sequence and structural diversity [4, 5]. Large-scale structural analyses of antibody variable regions, curated in resources such as in the Structural Antibody Database (SAbDab) [6], show that CDR loops do not adopt a single fixed geometry but instead cluster into canonical structures [7–11]. This structured yet multimodal conformational landscape underlies high-affinity binding while posing significant challenges for computational antibody design [12].

Traditional in silico design approaches, including Rosetta-based protocols [13–15] and atomistic molecular dynamics simulations [16, 17], can in principle capture this conformational diversity by sampling thermodynamic ensembles on rugged, high-dimensional energy landscapes. Molecular dynamics studies of CDR-H3, e.g. reveal broad loop ensembles whose population shifts correlate with binding properties and emphasize the functional relevance of sparsely populated conformers [18, 19]. However, these physics-based methods are computationally demanding, sensitive to force-field and sampling choices, and difficult to scale to high-throughput antibody design pipelines [4].

Recent progress in generative modeling has provided scalable alternatives to physics-based sampling. Diffusion probabilistic models corrupt data with noise and learn a parameterized reverse-time denoising process to recover the data distribution, enabling flexible modeling of complex, high-dimensional spaces [20, 21]. Flow-based methods offer a complementary formulation in which generation is defined by a deterministic transport field that maps a simple prior to the data distribution through an ordinary differential equation [22, 23]. Flow matching and rectified flow further improve sampling efficiency by directly matching velocity fields along interpolation paths [22, 23]. Together, diffusion and flow-based models form the foundation of many recent advances in scientific generative modeling, including molecular and structural applications [24, 25].

Within structural biology, generative models have evolved from sequence-only representations to geometric architectures that explicitly model 3D structure using equivariant neural networks [26–28]. These approaches enable conditional generation and improved structural realism, which are particularly important for macromolecules with flexible regions. More recent work emphasizes multistate and ensemble-aware generation, highlighting the importance of sampling multiple functional conformations rather than predicting a single dominant structure [25, 29, 30].

Antibody design presents additional challenges due to the extreme structural diversity of CDR loops, particularly CDR-H3 [4, 11]. AI-assisted pipelines increasingly integrate learned structure prediction with physics-grounded scoring to accelerate design. For example, IsAb2.0 combines AlphaFold-Multimer with the FlexddG energy model to automate antibody–antigen complex construction and in silico affinity optimization, with experimental validation in a nanobody design case study [31]. Diffusion-based methods such as DiffAb jointly model CDR sequence and structure in an equivariant framework [32], while RFdiffusion extends diffusion-based backbone generation to epitope-conditioned binder and antibody-like scaffold design, enabling the generation of full-length antibody backbones under structural constraints [33]. Guided and constrained variants further incorporate evolutionary, geometric, or physical information to improve realism and affinity [34–36]. In parallel, large antibody and protein language models capture repertoire-scale sequence statistics and support sequence completion, optimization, and affinity maturation [37–40]. Across these systems, generative modeling has substantially improved sequence recovery and structural realism [24, 25, 41].

Despite these successes, most existing generative models adopt unimodal parameterizations at the denoising or decoding step. Diffusion-based methods such as DiffAb [32] and flow-matching approaches such as FlowDesign [42] typically parameterize a single mean update for Cartesian coordinates and backbone orientations at each step, either as a single Gaussian kernel in the reverse diffusion process or as a single drift field in continuous-time flows. When the underlying conditional distribution exhibits multiple conformational modes, such unimodal parameterizations approximate the conditional average of the target distribution, a well-known consequence of unimodal regression objectives [43]. As a result, mode-specific loop conformations occupying shallow side minima in the CDR free energy landscape may be de-emphasized relative to dominant basins, in line with ensemble and population shift behavior reported for flexible antibody CDR loops [18, 19].

Throughout this study, we distinguish three related but different notions of multimodality. A ‘mixture component’ refers to one of the position-head or orientation-head Gaussians predicted at a given residue and timestep, i.e. a model-internal object inside the denoising distribution; the model is therefore multimodal at the per-residue level. A ‘structural mode’, equivalently a structural basin, refers to an evaluation-level CDR-loop backbone cluster obtained by agglomerative clustering of Kabsch-aligned C coordinates using a Å RMSD cutoff; the ‘near-native basin’ denotes structural modes within Å RMSD of the native loop. A ‘canonical-like structure’ refers to an analogous backbone family computed from native training-set CDR loops of the same CDR type and length. We summarize generated structural modes using cluster count and Shannon entropy, and we compare generated loops with canonical-like structures to quantify empirical backbone-family coverage. Our sampling strategy (Materials and methods Section) enforces a single mixture component per loop at each timestep, so per-complex multimodality arises across independent sampling runs rather than within a single decoded loop.

Mixture-based parameterizations provide a natural way to represent the multimodal distribution of CDR loops. In sequence space, finite mixture models have been shown to capture antibody repertoire statistics and support diverse and interpretable sequence generation and humanization [44]. In continuous spaces, Gaussian mixture extensions of diffusion and flow-based models increase the expressiveness of the reverse transition kernel and better approximate non-Gaussian, multimodal dynamics, particularly in few-step regimes. Moreover, related advances in protein generative modeling explicitly target multistate or multimodal sequence–structure ensembles, emphasizing the value of sampling multiple functional states rather than a single dominant conformation [29, 30].

General mixture-based diffusion methods support this view. Gaussian Mixture Solvers (GMS) [45] fits a Gaussian mixture reverse transition kernel at sampling time via generalized method of moments, and GMFlow [46] trains a network to predict a Gaussian mixture over flow velocities, demonstrating that mixture parameterizations meaningfully expand the expressiveness of generative transitions. Both, however, are developed for Euclidean image-space settings with spatially factorized mixtures. To our knowledge, mixture-parameterized reverse transitions have not been incorporated into antibody sequence–structure diffusion, where positional and orientational updates are coupled on , nor systematically evaluated for their ability to recover canonical CDR structures and rare, high-quality binders.

To address this gap, we introduce the mixture diffusion model for multimodal antibody design (MDMAD), a mixture-based denoising diffusion framework that explicitly captures the multimodal conformational complexity of CDR loops. MDMAD parameterizes both positional and orientational denoising distributions as Gaussian mixtures within a unified diffusion model, operating in residue-local frames for Cartesian updates and on for rotational updates. This formulation enables the representation and sampling of multiple plausible structural hypotheses per residue and timestep while maintaining training stability and computational efficiency.

Results

Overview of evaluation

Our goal is not only to improve average sequence recovery or structural accuracy, but to model the inherently multimodal conformational landscapes of antibody CDR loops. Accordingly, while we report standard aggregate metrics such as amino acid recovery (AAR), C root mean square deviation (RMSD), and binding improvement rate (IMP), we emphasize that these quantities alone do not fully capture the quality of a generative antibody design model. In particular, RMSD averaged over samples conflates faithful recovery of a dominant structural mode with controlled exploration of multiple plausible backbone conformations.

We therefore complement aggregate benchmarks with analyses that explicitly probe structural coverage and diversity. These include measurements of multimodal conformational recovery via clustering, canonical-like backbone family coverage relative to empirical distributions, and joint structure–binding landscapes across generated samples.

Benchmark datasets

We compare our method against previous baselines, RAbD [14], DiffAb [32], DIFFFORCE [36], and AbMEGD [35]. RAbD is a Rosetta-based, energy-driven framework that performs CDR grafting, sequence design, and Monte Carlo sampling with energy minimization to optimize antibody–antigen interfaces. DiffAb is a diffusion-based generative model that jointly denoises CDR sequences, backbone coordinates, and residue orientations conditioned on the antigen and antibody framework. DIFFFORCE extends DiffAb by incorporating gradients from a differentiable force field into the reverse diffusion process, biasing sampling toward lower-energy conformations. AbMEGD is an end-to-end equivariant graph diffusion model that integrates atomic-level geometry and residue-level representations to capture multiscale sequence–structure interactions.

For a fair cross-method comparison, we reproduce the baselines DiffAb and AbMEGD in a unified pipeline. Both methods are reproduced on the same SAbDab snapshot released with DiffAb, using a uniform training recipe of 200 000 iterations at a batch size of 16, and all generated samples are passed through the same OpenMM and Rosetta relaxation protocols before metric computation. RAbD values are taken from the DiffAb benchmark comparison, where RAbD was reported under the same benchmark setting used for DiffAb. DIFFFORCE values are taken from the original DIFFFORCE paper, as the implementation was not publicly available for retraining within our unified setup; these DIFFFORCE values are also the values reported in the AbMEGD comparison table. Reproduced AbMEGD numbers should be interpreted as a controlled re-evaluation under our unified benchmark setting, rather than as a direct reproduction of the original AbMEGD experimental setting. In our experiments, AbMEGD was trained on the DiffAb-released SAbDab snapshot (2022) for 200} 000 iterations at a batch size of 16 under the same training recipe used for all reproduced baselines, whereas the original AbMEGD report used a more recent 2024 SAbDab snapshot. We retain the reproduced AbMEGD values because they provide a controlled comparison under the same benchmark protocol used for MDMAD and DiffAb, which is the basis of the fairness claim.

The AAR values reported in our ablation tables (Tables 6–8) are higher than those in DiffAb’s original report [32]. This gap is not due to differences in test split or evaluation metric, both of which we keep identical to the released DiffAb benchmark. Rather, Table 8 attributes the gap to a methodological improvement that is applied uniformly across all our MDMAD variants, which is to replace DiffAb’s heuristic categorical reverse update with the Bayes-consistent analytic posterior under the same forward uniform-mixing corruption. We therefore include a DiffAb + analytic posterior entry in Table 1 as the appropriate point of comparison for unimodal-equivalent MDMAD variants, and we report DiffAb’s original method that is reproduced as well to preserve the literature value. Numbers for all MDMAD variants in Tables 6–8 are computed from our own runs under this analytic-posterior pipeline.

The dataset consists of antibody–antigen complexes with X-ray resolution Å, annotated with Chothia numbering and standard CDR definitions. Complexes missing the target CDR or with loop lengths exceeding 30 residues are excluded. Test complexes are selected using MMseqs2 clustering at 50% CDR–H3 identity, holding out five antigen clusters for evaluation. The 50% CDR-H3 clustering ensures that no test CDR-H3 falls within 50% sequence identity of any training CDR-H3, and the 19 held-out complexes form five entire clusters, with all remaining clusters used for training [32]. These held-out clusters include structurally diverse antigens such as SARS-CoV-2, Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), and influenza, providing antigen-level diversity under the standard DiffAb benchmark protocol. Following DiffAb, clustering is performed on CDR-H3 because H3 is the most sequence- and structure-diverse loop and is the dominant determinant of antigen-specific recognition. Consequently, the split provides the strongest test of generalization for H3. For non-H3 CDRs, which are more structurally conserved, the benchmark should be interpreted as a standardized cross-method evaluation under the released DiffAb protocol rather than as a strict unseen-loop-family generalization test. Antigen sequences, framework regions, and non-H3 CDRs are not explicitly de-duplicated under this protocol, but they enter only as conditioning context; the target CDR loop is the sole region masked and regenerated, and antibodies from different CDR-H3 clusters typically engage shared antigens at distinct epitopes or poses, so antigen overlap does not by itself imply interface leakage. This does not affect the fairness of our comparisons, because MDMAD, DiffAb, DiffAb + analytic posterior, and reproduced AbMEGD are evaluated on the same held-out complexes using the same relaxation and metric-computation pipeline.

Overall sequence, structure, and binding performance

We evaluate sequence fidelity, structural accuracy, and binding improvement using AAR, C RMSD, and IMP. AAR measures the fraction of CDR positions whose generated residue matches the native sequence, RMSD is computed between generated and native CDR C coordinates, and IMP is the fraction of generated designs with improved Rosetta binding energy relative to the native complex, i.e. , where .

We compare MDMAD against prior sequence–structure co-design methods on the standard DiffAb benchmark described in the above Benchmark datasets Subsection. For each CDR type, we report AAR, IMP, and C RMSD averaged over samples per complex, after applying the same OpenMM and Rosetta relaxation protocol to MDMAD and all baselines. Unless stated otherwise, MDMAD refers to the , configuration. Full OpenMM and Rosetta settings are provided in Supplementary methods, Section F.

Table 1 shows that MDMAD’s main advantage under global sampling is amino acid recovery. MDMAD obtains the highest AAR on five of six CDR types, with L3 being the only exception, where it is second to DiffAb + analytic posterior. This suggests that mixture-based sequence–structure denoising improves sequence recovery beyond the categorical posterior correction alone.

The IMP and RMSD measures on the structures are more mixed. MDMAD achieves the highest IMP on H3 and the second-highest IMP on H1, but it does not consistently outperform all baselines on the remaining CDRs. In particular, RAbD remains strong on several light-chain IMP and RMSD metrics, while DiffAb + analytic posterior often gives lower RMSD.

MDMAD generally has higher average RMSD than the strongest RMSD baselines under global sampling. This is intended through global mixture-based decoding, which samples multiple plausible backbone conformations instead of concentrating all samples near a single native-like structural mode. Accordingly, average RMSD should be interpreted together with diversity-sensitive metrics such as cluster count, cluster entropy, canonical-like structure coverage, and best-of- RMSD, which are analyzed in the following sections.

Mixture-based generation expands structural coverage

Aggregate metrics such as AAR and RMSD summarize average sequence and structural accuracy, but they do not characterize how generated structures are distributed across the conformational space. We therefore examine structural coverage using three complementary analyses: multimodal conformational coverage, which measures whether multiple distinct backbone conformations are recovered for the same target CDR; canonical-like backbone family coverage, which quantifies how many empirical backbone families are represented among generated loops; and the joint structural–binding landscape, which relates structural deviation from the native loop to changes in binding energy. Detailed definitions and algorithms for clustering, canonical-like structure assignment, and distributional metrics are provided in Supplementary methods, Section E.

Dissecting the contributions of mixture modeling

Effect of mixture size on performance

We vary the number of mixture components in MDMAD to assess the effect of mixture capacity under global sampling. Table 6 shows that increasing mixture size improves AAR for most heavy-chain CDRs, with clear gains for H1, H2, and H3 when moving from the unimodal model to . For light-chain CDRs, AAR is less sensitive to mixture size and performs better in lower mixture sizes.

The effect on IMP and average RMSD is less uniform. IMP improves for H1 and H3 at intermediate mixture size and for L3 at the largest mixture size, but no single variant is best across all CDRs. Similarly, larger mixtures can increase average RMSD under global sampling because they sample a broader set of conformations rather than concentrating only near the native structure.

Larger mixtures produce more structural clusters and higher cluster entropy (Fig. 4A and C). At the same time, best-of- RMSD remains broadly comparable across mixture sizes (Fig. 4D), indicating that the increased structural diversity does not eliminate near-native sampled conformations. The canonical-like hit rate also increases monotonically with mixture size for all six CDR types (Fig. 5D). Since hit rate measures the fraction of generated loops assigned to observed canonical-like backbone families, this increase indicates that added mixture capacity expands coverage of biologically observed backbone modes rather than simply dispersing samples into non-canonical conformations.

MAP sampling achieves the highest amino-acid recovery for most CDRs, with pronounced gains for H1 and H2 and comparable performance on H3. Per-residue sampling, which independently samples mixture components at each residue, achieves strong binding improvement for several CDRs, particularly L1, L2, and L3, but does not enforce component consistency across the generated loop. Global sampling produces the strongest binding improvement for heavy-chain CDRs, particularly H1 and H3, while expectation sampling consistently ranks between the two. Light-chain CDRs show less uniform behavior: MAP and expectation perform best for L1 and L2, whereas global sampling becomes competitive for L3. Sampling strategy also affects structural accuracy. Expectation sampling yields the lowest RMSD across CDRs, MAP produces the highest, and global sampling lies in between. This ordering is consistent with the fact that expectation sampling averages over mixture components and produces smoother structures, whereas MAP and per-residue strategies make residue-wise component choices that can reduce loop-level geometric coherence. Global sampling avoids this issue by sharing one component choice across residues, thereby promoting loop-level component consistency.

Sampler choice therefore controls a clear balance between sequence recovery, binding improvement, and structural consistency. We therefore use global sampling as the default strategy because it preserves stochastic exploration across independent samples while avoiding incoherent residue-wise component switching within a single generated CDR loop.

From DiffAb to MDMAD: component-wise ablation

To attribute gains over DiffAb to specific design choices, we start from the released DiffAb implementation and progressively introduce our modifications up to the unimodal MDMAD of configuration (Table 8). Throughout, we keep the encoder, noise schedule, and evaluation protocol fixed. The ablation isolates three changes: (i) categorical posterior, (ii) local-frame position targets, and (iii) likelihood-based continuous heads.

Discussion and conclusion

Antibody CDR loops are structurally multimodal. Even at fixed length and context, native structures populate multiple well separated backbone basins, and these alternatives can influence antigen recognition. Most diffusion based antibody sequence-structure co-design models parameterize reverse time denoising with unimodal updates in both position and rotation. This favors smooth refinement but can collapse uncertainty into a single dominant trajectory.

Our results show that mixture parameterized denoising is a practical way to represent this multimodality within CDR conformations. Relative to unimodal baselines, MDMAD increases conformational coverage with a higher cluster entropy, more distinct structural clusters, and broader empirical canonical-like structure coverage. It also maintains strong sequence recovery and competitive interaction scores under the same evaluation protocol. Global mixture selection increases average RMSD by encouraging exploration, but best of performance remains comparable. This indicates that multimodal sampling expands the reachable design space without losing access to near native solutions.

Through our model and experiments, we find that explicitly modeling multimodal reverse transitions is a useful inductive bias for antibody design. Mixtures provide a structured mechanism for sampling alternative loop geometries that better reflect the discrete heterogeneity observed in the SAbDab, rather than implicitly averaging across modes.

Nonetheless, this study has limitations. Our evaluation relies on structural and energetic proxies such as AAR, RMSD, BoN RMSD, and Rosetta-based interface energies, which do not replace experimental validation. In particular, Rosetta-derived values are computational proxies and do not substitute for experimental affinity measurements. IMP results should therefore be interpreted as relative comparisons under a fixed scoring protocol rather than absolute affinity predictions. In addition, the mixture-size ablations should be interpreted as evidence that mixture modeling improves conformational coverage and canonical-like structure recovery, rather than as definitive evidence that mixture parameterization is necessary or uniformly improves all conventional metrics. We also use fixed mixture parameterizations across CDR types, which may not be ideal for highly variable loops such as H3. Finally, the model is trained without explicit binding-aware supervision. Improved binders emerge from the learned distribution and sampling strategy rather than direct optimization.

We introduced MDMAD, a mixture based diffusion model for antibody sequence structure co-design that augments denoising transitions with Gaussian mixtures in residue local Cartesian coordinates and on . By combining Bayes consistent categorical training with likelihood based continuous heads, MDMAD better captures the multimodal CDR landscape and systematically broadens structural coverage. More broadly, mixture parameterized diffusion offers a general template for generative modeling in settings where discrete structural states coexist and unimodal transitions impose an averaging bias.

Future work includes incorporating adaptive mixture parameterizations, additional structural or physical constraints during generation and validating mixture predicted alternatives in experimental or higher fidelity computational pipelines.
