Ferrostatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, mitigates all-trans-retinal-induced retinal pigment epithelium degeneration in mice
Authors: Xiaoqing Shen, Yuling Chen, Beiting He, Ruitong Xi, Jingmeng Chen, Yalin Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisruption of the retinoid (visual) cycle causes all-trans-retinal (atRAL) to accumulate in photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), contributing to Stargardt disease type 1 (STGD1) and dry age-related macular degeneration (dAMD). Yet, the mechanisms underlying atRAL-induced RPE degeneration are not well understood. Here, we explored whether ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death marked by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, mediated RPE damage by atRAL.MethodsCell and animal models of STGD1 and dAMD were established using ARPE-19 cells loaded with atRAL and Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice subjected to light exposure. Cell viability and morphology, mitochondrial morphology, ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis-related gene and protein expression were assessed using MTS assay, inverted and confocal microscopy, fluorescence staining, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and Western blotting, respectively. The conditions of mouse retina were evaluated by electroretinography (ERG), optical coherence tomography (OCT), fundus imaging, and immunofluorescence labeling.ResultsatRAL triggered ferroptosis of ARPE-19 cells through Fe2+ accumulation, ROS generation, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial damage, and abnormal expression of typical ferroptosis-related genes and proteins. These effects were capable of being alleviated by treatment with ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a potent ferroptosis inhibitor. Moreover, ferroptosis was clearly observed in the RPE of light-exposed Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice that showed rapid and excessive accumulation of atRAL in the retina. Notably, intraperitoneal administration of Fer-1 effectively mitigated the degeneration of the RPE and photoreceptors and significantly ameliorated retinal function in Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice following light exposure.ConclusionsFerroptosis plays a critical role in RPE damage by atRAL. Inhibition of ferroptosis by Fer-1 may enhance RPE cell survival and prevent subsequent photoreceptor degeneration in STGD1 and dAMD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07195-7.Keywords: Ferroptosis, Retinal pigment epithelium, Age-related macular degeneration, Stargardt disease, Lipid peroxidation

Background

Defects in the retinoid (visual) cycle, a series of biochemical reactions essential for vision that involve the continuous generation of 11-cis-retinal and clearance of all-trans-retinal (atRAL), have been implicated in retinal degeneration [1, 2]. 11-cis-retinal is the chromophore of rod and cone pigments, and its continuous regeneration is crucial for vision [3]. atRAL, a critical intermediate of the retinoid (visual) cycle, is continuously isomerized into its 11-cis form to support vision [3]. Deficiencies in ATP-binding cassette transporter 4 (Abca4), which encodes a protein that shuttles atRAL from the disk lumen to the cytosol of photoreceptor outer segments, and retinol dehydrogenase 8 (Rdh8), which encodes an enzyme that reduces atRAL in the cytosol of rod and cone outer segments, cause the accumulation of atRAL and its condensation products [4]. This accumulation results in severe retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)/photoreceptor dystrophy, which is closely associated with Stargardt disease type 1 (STGD1) and dry age-related macular degeneration (dAMD) [5–8]. Exposure to bright light will lead to rapid accumulation of atRAL in the retina and accelerate the RPE/photoreceptor degeneration.

RPE cell damage occurs early in the pathogenesis of STGD1 and dAMD [9]. Moreover, emerging evidence indicates that atRAL accumulation is an important cause of RPE degeneration in these two ocular diseases [4]. Previous studies have demonstrated that atRAL induces apoptosis in ARPE-19 cells through mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, or endoplasmic reticulum stress [10–13]. Additionally, the activation of c-Jun N-terminal kinase by atRAL is reported to elicit RPE apoptosis through the induction of caspase-3 activation and DNA damage [10]. Moreover, atRAL triggers pyroptosis and apoptosis of ARPE-19 cells by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome [14].

Ferroptosis is a non-apoptotic form of cell death characterized by iron-dependent membrane lipid peroxidation [15]. Iron accumulation, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and overwhelming lipid peroxidation are the hallmarks of ferroptosis [16]. Disruption of iron homeostasis, particularly the degradation of ferritin, releases excess free ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) [17]. This Fe²⁺ then reacts with hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in the Fenton reaction, generating highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH), a potent form of ROS [18]. ROS attack polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in membrane phospholipids, producing lipid hydroperoxides (LOOHs) [19]. Ferroptosis ensues when these lethal lipid peroxides exceed the cell’s clearance capacity, a process in which the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis plays a crucial role [20]. Fer-1, a synthetic radical-trapping antioxidant, is a potent inhibitor of ferroptosis [21]. However, it remains unclear whether atRAL incites ferroptosis in RPE cells. In this study, we sought to elucidate the role of ferroptosis in atRAL-induced RPE atrophy.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J wild-type mice were purchased from the Xiamen University Laboratory Animal Center. The generation and characterization of Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice on a C57BL/6J genetic background, as well as the procedures for establishing the light-induced retinal degeneration model using these mice, have been previously described [22, 23]. Male and female mice were randomly assigned to the experimental groups. C57BL/6J and Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice aged 4 weeks were dark-adapted for 48 h. Subsequently, they were exposed to 10,000 lx LED light for 1 h and then housed in darkness for 5 days. Control C57BL/6J and Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice were normally maintained for 5 days in the dark. Alternatively, four-week-old Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice were dark-adapted for 48 h, and then received intraperitoneal injections of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (vehicle) or ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) at a dose of 4 mg/kg. An hour after injection, the pupils of the mice were dilated using tropicamide, after which the mice were exposed to 10,000 lx of white light emitting diode (LED) light for 1 h. Following light exposure, the mice were kept in complete darkness for 5 days, during which they continued to receive daily administration of DMSO or Fer-1 throughout the recovery period. Control Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice were intraperitoneally injected with DMSO or Fer-1, without light exposure. Optical coherence tomography (OCT), electroretinography (ERG) and fundus imaging analyses of these mice were performed with blinded investigators.

Cell culture and treatment

The human retinal pigment epithelial cell line ARPE-19 (CRL-2302, ATCC) was purchased from the FuDan IBS Cell Center (Shanghai, China). The culture conditions for ARPE-19 cells have been described in our previous studies [23]. For treatment, ARPE-19 cells were pretreated with 20 µM Fer-1 for 2 h, followed by exposure to 15 µM atRAL for 6 h.

Cell viability and morphology

The MTS assay for evaluating cytotoxicity was performed as previously reported [22, 23]. ARPE-19 cells were pretreated with Fer-1 at concentrations of 10, 20, or 30 µM for 2 h, after which they were incubated with or without 15 µM atRAL for an additional 6 h. Cell morphology was examined by a Leica DMi8 inverted microscope (Wetzlar, Germany).

Fluorescence staining

ARPE-19 cells were treated with 15 µM atRAL for 6 h, with or without a 2-hour pretreatment with 20 µM Fer-1. Next, the cells were incubated with Hoechst 33,342 (10 µM) at 37 °C for 30 min, along with one of the following fluorescent probes: MitoTracker® Red CMXRos (500 nM), Rhodamine-123 (10 µg/ml), FerroOrange (1 µM), H2DCFDA (5 µM), MitoSOX™ (500 nM), C11-BODIPY 581/591 (10 µM), and Liperfluo (10 µM). Finally, the cells were washed three times with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) and immediately imaged using an FV4000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. Mouse posterior eyecups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 min, with the RPE layer oriented upward. The RPE/choroid flat mounts were then blocked in PBS containing 5% goat serum and 1% Triton X-100 for 2 h at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the tissues were incubated with anti-ZO-1 antibody (1:200) or anti-4-HNE antibody (1:100) overnight at 4 °C. Following three PBS washes, the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with ABflo®594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200) and DAPI (1:200). Finally, the stained flat mounts were mounted using VECTASHIELD® Antifade Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories, H-1700) and imaged using the FV4000 confocal laser scanning microscope.

Flow cytometry

ARPE-19 cells were treated with 15 µM atRAL for 6 h, with or without a 2-hour pretreatment with 20 µM Fer-1. Next, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min with one of the following fluorescent probes: MitoSOX™ (500 nM), H2DCFDA (5 µM), or Liperfluo (10 µM). The cells were then washed with PBS, harvested by trypsinization, and collected by centrifugation. Cell pellets were washed twice with PBS, resuspended in pre-chilled PBS, and passed through a 40 μm nylon mesh cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Flow cytometry was performed using a Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer (USA), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

qRT-PCR was performed as previously described [24]. The primer sequences used for amplification are listed in Table 1.

Western blotting

Western blotting was carried our as previously reported [22]. The unprocessed original images of western blot results were provided in the Supplementary Information: Figure S1.

Fundus imaging and OCT

Fundus imaging and OCT were conducted as previously described [23]. Total retinal thickness was measured manually using the built-in measurement tools of the accompanying software. The optic disc (optic nerve head) served as the central reference point. Measurements were taken starting 250 μm to either side of the optic disc, and additional points were measured at 125 μm intervals. This approach enabled a quantitative assessment of total retinal thickness across the scanned region.

ERG

Full-field ERG was performed to assess retinal function in mice. The mice were dark-adapted overnight, and anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation under dim red light. After their pupils were dilated with tropicamide, full-field ERG recordings were obtained using an animal ERG system (Diagnosys LLC, Lowell, MA, USA). Representative ERG waveforms were recorded at a stimulus intensity of 10 cd·s/m², and the amplitudes of the a-wave and b-wave at this intensity were quantified for statistical analysis. Additionally, stimulus-response curves were generated by plotting the a- and b-wave amplitudes across a range of flash intensities to evaluate retinal responsiveness.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Ferroptosis causes atRAL-induced damage in ARPE-19 cells

With reference to previously published cytotoxicity data, ARPE-19 cells were treated with 15 µM atRAL for 6 h in this study [14]. MTS assay results indicated that atRAL treatment significantly reduced ARPE-19 cell viability, while Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, mitigated this effect in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). Based on the maximum protective effect threshold, 20 µM Fer-1 was chosen as the treatment concentration for subsequent experiments. Likewise, Fer-1 significantly alleviated atRAL-induced morphological changes, including cell rounding, shrinkage, and cytoplasmic rupture (Fig. 1B).

B − E ARPE-19 cells were pretreated with 20 µM Fer-1 for 2 h, and then exposed or unexposed for 6 h to 15 µM atRAL. (B) Cellular morphology. Scale bar: 20 μm. (C) Mitochondrial morphology was visualized by confocal microscopy after cell staining with MitoTracker® Red CMXRos. Scale bar: 10 μm. Mitochondria play a crucial role in the regulation of ferroptosis [25]. Confocal microscopy using the MitoTracker® Red CMXRos probe revealed that atRAL triggered severe mitochondrial fragmentation and abnormal aggregation in the perinuclear region of ARPE-19 cells, in contrast to the filamentous network observed in controls (Fig. 1C). These morphological abnormalities were clearly ameliorated by Fer-1 (Fig. 1C). Moreover, a significant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) in atRAL-loaded ARPE-19 cells was visualized by Rhodamine-123 fluorescence staining, but it was dramatically prevented by Fer-1 (Fig. 1D). The cystine/glutamate antiporter, solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), also known as xCT, imports cystine for glutathione biosynthesis. This process is crucial because glutathione serves as a cofactor for glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), an enzyme that converts toxic phospholipid peroxides (L-OOH) into benign lipid alcohols (L-OH) [26]. Ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) is a component of ferritin. Ferritinophagy degrades FTH1 to release Fe2+, triggering ferroptosis [27]. Western blot analysis demonstrated that atRAL markedly suppressed the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins SLC7A11, GPX4 and FTH1 in ARPE-19 cells, which were effectively mitigated by Fer-1 (Fig. 1E). These findings suggest that ferroptosis involves atRAL-induced RPE cell damage.

atRAL evokes iron dysregulation in ARPE-19 cells

Excess iron can contribute to ferroptosis by catalyzing the Fenton reaction, or by serving as the active core of oxidoreductases, such as arachidonic acid lipoxygenases (ALOXs) and cytochrome P450 enzymes [28]. As expected, confocal microscopy using FerroOrange staining revealed that atRAL significantly increased intracellular Fe²⁺ levels in ARPE-19 cells, an effect that was markedly attenuated by Fer-1 (Fig. 2A).

atRAL triggers iron dysregulation in ARPE-19 cells. ARPE-19 cells were pretreated for 2 h with 20 µM Fer-1, and then incubated for 6 h with or without 15 µM atRAL. (A) Confocal micrographs of Fe2+ stained with FerroOrange. Quantification of Fe2+ fluorescence intensity (n = 3). Scale bar: 10 μm. (B) qRT-PCR profiling of iron homeostasis-related genes (n = 3)

Iron homeostasis is regulated by balancing iron transport, uptake, storage, export, and utilization [29]. Cellular iron uptake is primarily mediated by transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1), which binds the transferrin-Fe³⁺ complex and undergoes endocytosis. Fe³⁺ is reduced to Fe²⁺ by the six transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate (STEAP) family, such as STEAP3, and is then released into the cytosol through divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1). Fe²⁺ entering the cytosolic iron pool is distributed with the assistance of the poly(rC)-binding protein (PCBP) family. Excess free iron is stored in ferritin, a protein complex composed of FTH1 and light-chain ferritin (FTL). Ferritin degradation is mediated by nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4). Cellular iron export is mainly mediated by the Fe²⁺ transporter ferroportin (FPN), which is coupled with ferroxidases such as ceruloplasmin (CP) and hephaestin (HEPH). These ferroxidases oxidize Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺, allowing the released iron to be loaded onto transferrin [30]. qRT-PCR analysis of ARPE-19 cells exposed to atRAL revealed significant transcriptional changes in iron homeostasis regulators (Fig. 2B). The mRNA levels of iron acquisition components (TF, TFRC, STEAP3, DMT1), iron storage modulators (NCOA4, FTH1, FTL, PCBP1, PCBP2), and redox-associated transporters (CP, HEPH) were upregulated. In contrast, the mRNA levels of FPN, the sole iron exporter, were significantly downregulated. However, treatment with Fer-1 normalized expression changes of these genes (Fig. 2B). These results indicate that atRAL disrupts iron homeostasis in RPE cells, leading to Fe²⁺ accumulation and ferroptosis induction.

atRAL triggers ROS production in ARPE-19 cells

ROS is a characteristic feature of ferroptosis [31]. The mitochondrial electron transport chain is the primary source of ROS, with superoxide anions (O₂•⁻) being the dominant mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) species [32]. Pathological accumulation of ROS renders cells susceptible to ferroptosis by promoting lipid peroxidation [33]. Intracellular and mitochondrial ROS levels were assessed using H2DCFDA and MitoSOX™ Red staining, respectively. The cell-permeable H2DCFDA probe undergoes ROS-dependent oxidation to form fluorescent dichlorofluorescein (DCF), providing a quantitative measure of total intracellular ROS. MitoSOX™ Red, a fluorogenic dye that selectively localizes to mitochondria, was used to visualize mitochondria-specific superoxide levels. Upon oxidation by superoxide radicals, it exhibits red fluorescence. Confocal microscopy and flow cytometry analyses revealed significant increases in both cytoplasmic and mitochondrial ROS levels in ARPE-19 cells following atRAL exposure (Fig. 3A–D). These effects were markedly suppressed by Fer-1 treatment (Fig. 3A–D). Collectively, these observations imply that ROS production induced by atRAL contributes to RPE cell ferroptosis.

atRAL incites lipid peroxidation in ARPE-19 cells

The execution of ferroptosis involves the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-containing cell membrane phospholipids, leading to the formation of lipid hydroperoxides (LOOHs) [15]. The BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 probe specifically labels the membranes of living cells. During oxidation, the fluorescence emission spectrum shifts from red to green, and the green/red fluorescence ratio serves as a quantitative measure of LOOH accumulation. The Liperfluo fluorescent probe emits strong green fluorescence in areas with high lipid peroxide accumulation, such as cell membranes. Its signal intensity correlates positively with LOOH levels. Confocal microscopy and flow cytometry analyses demonstrated that atRAL markedly exacerbated lipid peroxidation in ARPE-19 cells, which was significantly reduced by Fer-1 (Fig. 4A–C). These data suggest that atRAL induces RPE cell ferroptosis through driving lipid peroxidation of membrane phospholipids.

Light-induced retinal degeneration implicates RPE ferroptosis in Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice

Prior studies have confirmed the presence of atRAL in the RPE of both Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice and humans [10]. Moreover, it is established that bright light exposure results in rapid accumulation of atRAL in the retina and accelerates RPE degeneration in Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice [5]. In the current experimental paradigm, 2-day dark-adapted C57BL/6J and Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice were exposed to 10,000 lx LED light for 1 h, followed by 5 days of dark rearing. Retinal structure and function were then evaluated 5 days post-illumination. Full-field ERG revealed significant reductions in a- and b-wave amplitudes in light-exposed Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice (Fig. 5A–C). OCT imaging showed a substantial decrease in total retinal thickness of Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice subjected to light exposure (Fig. 5D). Funduscopic analysis identified pronounced pigmentary disturbances in the RPE layer of Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice under photic stress (Fig. 5E). Whole-mount zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) immunofluorescence staining revealed that light exposure disrupted tight junctions in the RPE of Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice, indicating compromised barrier integrity (Fig. 5F). However, control and light-exposed C57BL/6J mice exhibited no functional or structural deficits in the retina (Fig. 5A–F). Immunofluorescence staining of RPE flat mounts with 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), a marker of lipid peroxidation [34], showed a significant increase in the levels of lipid peroxidation in Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice subjected to light illumination (Fig. 5G). The results of Western blotting disclosed a remarkable decline in protein levels of ferroptosis-associated proteins FTH1 and GPX4 in the RPE/choroid of Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice after exposure to light (Fig. 5H). This effect was not observed in control or light-exposed C57BL/6J mice (Fig. 5H). These findings suggest that light-induced retinal degeneration in Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice is at least partly associated with RPE ferroptosis.

Repression of RPE ferroptosis by Fer-1 mitigates light-induced retinal degeneration in Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice

Four-week-old Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice were dark-adapted for 48 h and then intraperitoneally administered either DMSO or Fer-1 (4 mg/kg). One hour after the injection, the mice were exposed to 10,000 lx of white LED light for 1 h. Subsequently, they were kept in darkness and received daily injections for 5 days. ERG analysis revealed that Fer-1 administration effectively attenuated the light-induced reduction of a- and b-wave amplitudes in Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice (Fig. 6A–C). The results of OCT imaging showed that Fer-1 treatment visibly alleviated retinal structural degeneration and significantly restored retinal thickness (Fig. 6D). Fundus imaging indicated that RPE pigmentary alterations were distinctly prevented by Fer-1 (Fig. 6E). Additionally, intraperitoneal Fer-1 treatment effectively preserved RPE integrity in light-exposed Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice (Fig. 6F). Moreover, the levels of lipid peroxidation were significantly reduced in the RPE of Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice following light exposure when Fer-1 was administered intraperitoneally (Fig. 6G). As expected, Western blot analysis demonstrated that intraperitoneal administration of Fer-1 clearly restored the expression of ferroptosis regulatory proteins FTH1 and GPX4 in the RPE/choroid of Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice after light exposure (Fig. 6H). These data suggest that inhibiting RPE ferroptosis preserves retinal structure and function in light-exposed Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice.

Discussion

Currently, there remains a critical lack of effective treatments for both STGD1 and dAMD, two major forms of progressive retinal degeneration characterized by the gradual functional loss of RPE and photoreceptor cells. This ultimately leads to irreversible central vision loss [35, 36]. dAMD accounts for approximately 85 − 90% of AMD cases and is the most prevalent retinal degenerative disease in the elderly [35]. By contrast, STGD1 is the most common autosomal recessive macular dystrophy caused by mutations in the Abca4 gene [37]. A central pathological hallmark shared by both diseases is the progressive loss of RPE cells, which is closely related to the accumulation of atRAL [5, 38]. Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice, which have impaired atRAL transport and metabolic pathways, exhibit pathological accumulation of atRAL and mimic the cardinal features of both STGD1 and dAMD [4, 39]. The physiological process by which RPE cells phagocytose photoreceptor outer segments explains why atRAL can accumulate in the RPE [40]. Indeed, we have previously corroborated the presence of atRAL in the RPE/choroid of both Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice and human donor eyes [10].

Though both apoptosis and pyroptosis are observed in RPE cells from models of STGD1 and dry AMD, necroptosis is not detected [10]. Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [41], has emerged as a key pathogenic mechanism in neurodegenerative diseases and ischemic conditions [42, 43]. However, its role in RPE cell damage and the progression of STGD1 and dAMD has not been systematically investigated. In this study, we provided in vitro and in vivo evidence that ferroptosis was a critical downstream consequence of atRAL overload in RPE cells.

In cultured ARPE-19 cells, exposure to atRAL reduced cell viability (Fig. 1A) and induced hallmark features of ferroptosis, including plasma membrane rupture (Fig. 1B), mitochondrial fragmentation (Fig. 1C), and ΔΨm loss (Fig. 1D). These effects resembled those of the classical ferroptosis inducer, erastin [44, 45]. Ferroptosis is typically characterized by the intracellular overload of redox-active Fe²⁺, which catalyzes lipid peroxidation through Fenton chemistry [15]. Retinal iron accumulation has been documented in aged individuals and patients with dAMD in past studies [30]. In the present study, it was found that atRAL could perturb the expression of iron homeostasis-related genes, resulting in iron overload in RPE cells (Fig. 2A, B). ROS drive lipid peroxidation of PUFAs, which in turn leads to irreversible membrane rupture and subsequent ferroptotic cell death [15]. We indeed observed a marked increase in the levels of total and mitochondrial ROS as well as lipid peroxidation in ARPE-19 cells following atRAL treatment (Figs. 3 and 4). GPX4 acts as a central suppressor of lipid peroxidation, and its expression is promoted by SLC7A11-mediated cystine uptake and glutathione synthesis [15]. RPE-localized GPX4 is essential for maintaining the health of the RPE and outer retina, and its knockout recapitulates the phenotypes of dry AMD [46]. By contrast, FTH1 limits iron-mediated oxidative damage by sequestering labile Fe²⁺ into non-reactive Fe³⁺ storage forms [47]. Indeed, the expression of GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 was significantly downregulated in atRAL-loaded cells (Fig. 1E). Fer-1 treatment alleviated mitochondrial damage, normalized the expression of iron metabolism-related genes, reduced intracellular Fe²⁺ levels, and suppressed ROS production and lipid peroxidation in RPE cells exposed to atRAL, ultimately rescuing cell viability (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Earlier work has demonstrated that adult mice lacking GPX4 specifically in the RPE develop pathological hallmarks of geographic atrophy in age-related macular degeneration [48]. While our results implicate ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of both STGD1 and dAMD, genetic interventions such as GPX4 overexpression are still required to substantiate the therapeutic potential of ferroptosis inhibition.

Iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation have been documented in the aging retina [30], and intravitreal iron injection recapitulates oxidative stress and key features of geographic atrophy [49]. Our animal data reinforced the causal link between ferroptosis and AMD pathogenesis, underscoring this cell-death pathway as both a mechanistic driver and a promising therapeutic target. From a translational standpoint, established pharmacologic tools, including the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1, the iron chelator deferoxamine, and the lipid-peroxidation inhibitor liproxstatin-1 [50], already provide a solid foundation for intervention. Yet realizing clinical benefit will require concerted efforts in drug optimization, targeted ocular delivery, and rigorous clinical evaluation before ferroptosis-targeted therapies can be advanced for patients with STGD1 and dAMD.

In lines with the results from cell-based assays, Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice suffering from light exposure, which exhibit rapid and excess accumulation of atRAL in the retina, showed significantly elevated levels of lipid peroxidation in the RPE and impairments in retinal structure and function (Fig. 5A − F), as well as a clear decrease in protein levels of FTH1 and GPX4 in the RPE/choroid (Fig. 5G, H). We have previously shown that photoreceptor ferroptosis in light-exposed Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice can be relieved by intraperitoneal administration of Fer-1, which effectively rescues the structural alterations of neural retina [51]. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that Fer-1 may also act directly on photoreceptors. The RPE plays a central role in maintaining photoreceptor structure and function by supplying metabolic and nutritional support, recycling visual pigments, and phagocytosing shed outer segments [52]. The current investigation further evidenced that intraperitoneally injected Fer-1 dramatically inhibited ferroptotic cell death in the RPE of Abca4−/−Rdh8−/− mice following exposure to light, thereby preventing retinal function decline and ameliorating the structural degeneration of both the RPE and photoreceptors (Fig. 6). These lines of evidence suggest that ferroptosis is a common cause of RPE and photoreceptor atrophy induced by atRAL. Given that an important consequence of RPE cell death is photoreceptor damage [10–13], repressing RPE ferroptosis may help to better mitigate retinal degeneration in STGD1 and dAMD.
