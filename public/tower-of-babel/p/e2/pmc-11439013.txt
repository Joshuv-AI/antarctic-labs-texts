Mathematical modeling insights into improving CAR T cell therapy for solid tumors with bystander effects
Authors: Erdi Kara, Trachette L. Jackson, Chartese Jones, Rockford Sison, Reginald L. McGee II
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As an adoptive cellular therapy, Chimeric Antigen Receptor T cell (CAR T cell) therapy has shown remarkable success in hematological malignancies but only limited efficacy against solid tumors. Compared with blood cancers, solid tumors present a series of challenges that ultimately combine to neutralize the function of CAR T cells. These challenges include, but are not limited to, antigen heterogeneity - variability in the expression of the antigen on tumor cells, as well as trafficking and infiltration into the solid tumor tissue. A critical question for solving the heterogeneity problem is whether CAR T therapy induces bystander effects, such as antigen spreading. Antigen spreading occurs when CAR T cells activate other endogenous antitumor CD8 T cells against antigens that were not originally targeted. In this work, we develop a mathematical model of CAR T cell therapy for solid tumors that considers both antigen heterogeneity and bystander effects. Our model is based on in vivo treatment data that includes a mixture of target antigen-positive and target antigen-negative tumor cells. We use our model to simulate large cohorts of virtual patients to better understand the relationship involving bystander killing. We also investigate several strategies for enhancing bystander effects, thus increasing CAR T cell therapy’s overall efficacy for solid tumors.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapies are one of the most innovative cancer treatment modalities available today and have radically altered the treatment landscape for multiple malignancies1. Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) are engineered fusion proteins that allow T cells to recognize specific antigens expressed on cancer cells2. By removing immune cells, manipulating their genes, and implanting them back into the body, scientists have created a way to “teach” our immune system to identify and destroy cancer cells. This procedure got its first two FDA approvals in 20173. Since then, four more therapies have been approved2,4. These therapies have demonstrated repeated success in handling hematological tumors, including certain leukemias, lymphomas, and most recently, myeloma5. Given the promising clinical responses for several hematological malignancies, there is a vested interest in examining the applicability to solid tumors. Unfortunately, CAR T cell therapy has not generated even close to the same level of success in the fight against solid tumors due to several challenges that are often interconnected6. Some difficulties in applying CAR T cell therapy to solid tumors are tumor antigen heterogeneity, trafficking and infiltration into tumor tissue, and immunosuppressive tumor micro-environment7. Of these, antigen heterogeneity - variability in the expression of the target antigen on tumor cells is particularly challenging8. The ideal tumor-associated antigen (TAA) would be expressed on all tumor cells because CARs can only attack cells having the target antigen. Therefore, antigen heterogeneity impairs the detection of cancer cells by CAR T cells, and therapeutic success is unlikely unless almost all the tumor cells express the TAA9.

A critical question for solving the heterogeneity problem is whether CAR T cell therapy induces “bystander effects”10,11. The term bystander effect refers to the phenomenon where CAR T cells not only target cancer cells expressing a specific antigen but also stimulate cytotoxic activity against nearby cancer cells that lack the targeted antigen expression. In the same context, bystander cells refer to immune cells triggered by CAR T cells playing a role in killing tumor cells. These bystander cells can include CD8 T cells, innate immune cells (such as macrophages, neutrophils, or natural killer (NK) cells), or even antigen-presenting cells (APCs) involved in antigen cross-presentation. The bystander effect allows CAR T cells to exert their therapeutic impact beyond the targeted antigen-expressing cells, contributing to eradicating heterogeneous cancer cell populations commonly found in solid tumors. Two types of bystander effects are indirect tumor cell killing and antigen spreading. Indirect killing could result from the activation of macrophages and/or natural killer cells by cytokines released after CAR engagement. Antigen spreading occurs when CAR T cells induce the activation of other endogenous antitumor CD8 T cells against other antigens that were not initially targeted11. It usually occurs following initial therapy-mediated tumor destruction, which leads to the release of secondary tumor antigens. The extent to which this occurs will be critical in setting the thresholds of antigen expression that will be used to define eligible candidates.

Experimentally evaluating the bystander effect in CAR T cell therapy presents several challenges. Firstly, relevant experimental models are required to accurately assess immunologic bystander effects. While many preclinical studies have utilized immunodeficient mouse models with human tumors, evaluating the bystander effect necessitates intact immune systems in mouse models. An intact immune system is essential to assess the extent to which CAR T cells can effectively cure target antigen-positive and target antigen-negative tumor types and thus define the presence and impact of bystander effects. Secondly, defining and quantifying bystander effects can be technically challenging. Precise characterization of antigen expression profiles on tumor cells, designing well-defined mixtures of antigen-positive and antigen-negative tumor cell populations, and accurately measuring the impact of CAR T cells on these populations require sophisticated techniques and methodologies12.

Mathematical models have been increasingly used to study CAR T cell therapy13–15. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no mathematical modeling investigation of bystander effects of CAR T cells in solid tumors. In this paper, we build a mathematical model based on an in vivo experiment12 that includes a mixture of target antigen-positive and target antigen-negative tumor cells. Our model investigates the interaction of four distinct cell populations: target antigen-positive tumor cells, target antigen-negative tumor cells, CAR T cells, and bystander cells. Our results indicate that augmenting the number of CAR T cells reaching the tumor site does not enhance the therapeutic outcome, even in cases of high antigen expressivity, suggesting the need for bystander effect. However, our virtual patient analysis reveals that enhancing the cytotoxic capability of bystander cells can generate significantly improved outcomes.

The plan for the paper is as follows. In “Methods”, we describe the mathematical model designed to investigate antigen heterogeneity and bystander effects, and explain the key steps of parameter estimation using in vivo data provided in12. In “Results”, we develop a heterogeneous virtual patient population that reflects the variability in the experimental data to simulate different treatment scenarios to better understand bystander effects on therapeutic outcomes. Finally, in “Discussion”, we explore the potential extension of our model to address various scenarios regarding the bystander phenomenon.

Virtual patient analysis predicts tumor response patterns

In this section, we create a virtual patient (VP) cohort to accommodate the variability inherent in the model parameters and the actual data, enabling us to assess how this variability impacts treatment outcomes. It is important to note that our virtual cohort represents pre-clinical scenarios using mouse models.

Utilizing these virtual “patients” allows us to conduct statistical analysis and draw conclusions on the effectiveness of various treatments or treatment combinations. We apply the treatment regimen described above to each VP in the cohort and identify any patterns or trends that may contribute to successful therapeutic outcomes.

To generate virtual patients (VPs), we begin by considering the parameters r1 (day−1), r2 (day−1), and K1 (mm3), and generate values for each parameter such that they fall within a 0.5 relative error band of each data point with respect to the control data for the 100%, 90%, and 75% cases. By imposing these acceptance criteria, we aim to obtain a set of parameters, r1, r2, and K1, that capture the dynamics in the experimental data. In total, we generated 14,000 such values using this methodology, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Enhancing bystander cell cytotoxicity increases survival rate

Now that we have a large virtual patient cohort containing responders, partial responders, and non-responders, we can investigate which parameters have different distributions across these three groups. One way to achieve this task is to use the Kruskal-Wallis (also known as H test) test, a non-parametric test used to determine if there are statistically significant differences between two or more groups of an independent variables’ impact on a dependent variable. Being non-parametric means the method makes no assumptions about the underlying distribution. Since our data regarding virtual patients do not appear to follow a specific distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis test is an appropriate candidate for our analysis. In addition, we utilize the η2 (eta squared) statistic to measure the effect size, thereby quantifying the degree to which the groups differ19. According to Cohen’s guidelines, an η2 value smaller than 0.06 represents a small effect, between 0.06 and 0.14 indicates a medium effect, and 0.14 or higher suggests a large effect20. For all subsequent statistical analyses, we employ a significance threshold of p = 0.05. It is important to note that while the Kruskal-Wallis test can detect differences across the distributions, it remains agnostic to the shapes of these distributions. While the test primarily assesses median differences under the assumption of identical distribution shapes, it remains applicable when shapes differ. In such cases, a significant result implies that at least one group’s distribution differs from the others in some way, which could be due to differences in medians, variances, or shapes21. After the Kruskal-Wallis test indicates significant differences among the groups for certain parameters, it is a common practice to employ a post-hoc test such as Dunn’s test or Mann–Whitney U test to perform pairwise comparisons between groups. In this work, we employ Dunn’s test. This additional analysis allows us to identify which specific group pairs exhibit significant differences. For more details about non-parametric tests, the reader can refer to ref. 22.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a mathematical model to investigate the bystander effect in CAR T cell therapy for solid tumors. Our model exhibits a remarkably accurate fit to the unconventional data obtained from the in vivo experiment in ref. 12. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first mathematical investigation specifically focused on the bystander effect in CAR T cell treatment of solid tumors. The findings of our study are consistent with key observations made by ref. 12. In their study, the authors observed that the augmented persistence of administered CAR T cells did not influence the bystander effect. Instead, they provided evidence demonstrating that the presence of endogenous CD8 T cells was a necessary factor for the occurrence of the bystander effect. Our results indicate that augmenting the number of CAR T cells reaching the tumor site does not enhance the therapeutic outcome, even in cases of high antigen expressivity, suggesting an insufficient or absent bystander effect. However, our model further reveals that enhancing the cytotoxic capability of bystander cells yields significantly improved outcomes, as revealed through virtual patient analysis. In ref. 24 it was also observed that the bystander effect played a role on patient outcomes. They observed that a negative relapse index, which includes the bystander effect, determined whether or not and when an antigen-negative relapse would occur.

It is important to highlight that our model incorporates components from well-established works cited in the model development section. In particular, our modeling approach aligns with a similar philosophy with18, which explores the impact of CAR T cell therapy in the presence of various chemotherapy regimens, primarily for hematologic malignancies. The key variables in this context are tumor cells, CAR T cells, effector T cells, and chemotherapy drugs. Our work breaks down the tumor population based on whether CAR T cells can target them. While our model was applied to solid tumors, mixing models have been applied to hematological cancers as well.25 examined “how antigen density affects cellular mechanisms underlying heterogeneous responses” in several hematological cancers. We did not explicitly model drug infusion in our model due to the the nature of the bystander experiment discussed in ref. 12. The authors therein did not administer a high dose of CTX since the goal was to investigate the effect of low-dose CTX on bystander effect and determine if this dose could enhance the anti-tumor immune response without causing anti-tumor effects or lymphodepletion, which essentially invalidates the observability of bystander effect.

Researchers are developing novel ways to overcome the challenge of antigen heterogeneity associated with CAR T cell therapy, including testing approaches for enhancing bystander killing. For example12, showed that pretreatment with cyclophosphamide before CAR T cell administration initiated bystander effects mediated by endogenous CD8 T cells and resulted in cures of tumors with heterogeneous antigen expression. Another mechanism currently being explored is bystander killing mediated by the FasL-Fas pathway26. The recent discovery that small molecule therapies can inhibit certain regulators of Fas signaling and can increase the efficacy of bystander killing is showing promise and helping to prevent patient relapse26.

Due to the limited amounts of data available for parameter estimation, synthesizing data from various sources is often necessary. In fact, for our model, the only viable option to inform parameter choices was to use data from mouse models, human patients, various CAR-T cell types, and different cancer types, both solid and hematological. We acknowledge that using kinetic parameter values fit using experimental data from mice12 and kinetic values from human patients18 for other parameters could limit the predictive scope of the model. To control for unintended consequences due to this data integration, the values from human kinetics are only used for parameters determined to be insensitive during Sobol sensitivity analysis. We also conducted a virtual patient analysis, which allowed us to explore the range of all parameters to avoid being constrained by limited data. Given the good agreement with available experimental data, our model offers a foundation for further improvements and provides valuable insights into existing theories or the development of new hypotheses regarding this phenomenon. It is important to note that the study of bystander phenomena remains an active research area with ongoing advancements. Building upon the existing research, below we outline several potential research questions that can be explored within the same modeling framework. Interested readers can refer to ref. 12 for a more comprehensive discussion. Finally, we note that the existence of models such as ours can support the collection of more experimental data. Model-based design of experiments is a field where existing mathematical models can be leveraged to suggest future experiments that can aid in subsequent model development and refinement27,28.

It is clear that the bystander effect requires the presence of CD8 positive T cells. However, it is not clear exactly by which mechanism those effector T cells are activated. In this sense, one can investigate the role of CD4 helper T cells or regulatory T cells(Tregs). In the context of the data we use, the latter is of special importance. Tregs are a specialized subset of T cells that play a crucial role in immune regulation and maintaining immune tolerance. In the context of cancer immunotherapy, Tregs can have a suppressive effect on the immune response against tumors29. This suppression occurs through various mechanisms, including the inhibition of anti-tumor responses by endogenous CD8 T cells. On the other hand, CTX has been shown to have the ability to reduce the population or function of Tregs30,31. By reducing the number of Tregs, CTX can potentially alleviate their suppressive effects on immune responses, particularly those directed against tumors. This pathway can be explored via mathematical modeling by incorporating Tregs and CTX into the existing framework.

Cytokines produced by CAR T cells are another direction that can be explored. Upon activation, CAR T cells can release cytokines such as interferon gamma (IFNg) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-a)32,33. These cytokines have the potential to directly kill tumor cells34. Additionally, the released cytokines can activate the components of innate immune system, including macrophages, neutrophils, or natural killer (NK) cells. Once activated, these immune cells can induce tumor destruction through mechanisms such as phagocytosis. Although a fully coupled modeling scenario is possible, the terms related to cytokines and innate immune system can be potentially decoupled from the other model variables and directly linked to CAR T dynamics since this pathway is primarily triggered by CAR T activation.

Exploring the concept of cross-presentation, also known as T cell cross priming, could also be a promising line of investigation. This refers to the process by which APCs, particularly dendritic cells (DCs), present exogenous antigens(antigens that originate from outside the body) to CD8 T cells10. DCs are immune cells that are crucial for orchestrating immune responses. They have a pivotal role in determining whether CD8 T cells, which are important for anti-tumor immune responses, will mount an immune response against tumor antigens or exhibit tolerance. DCs can present antigens from engulfed or captured tumor cells to CD8 T cells leading to the activation of CD8 T cells, enabling them to recognize and target tumor cells. Thus, a CAR T cell therapy combined with a strong activation of DCs could potentially amplify the bystander effects, where the activated CD8 T cells also target neighboring tumor cells that do not express the specific antigen recognized by CAR T cell. Reader can refer to ref. 35 for similar experimental study. Similar to previous modeling scenario, DC population, in addition to agents activating DCs, can be linked to bystander cells to create a similar framework.

So far, our emphasis regarding the bystander dynamics has been based on ODE modeling. However, one can also consider partial differential equation (PDE) modeling to account for spatial dynamics as well. In fact, PDE modeling can be useful for several reasons. Firstly, bystander effect often involves interactions and diffusion of signaling molecules, cytokines, and other mediators within the tumor micro-environment. Thus, PDE models can capture the spatial distribution and diffusion processes, allowing for a better representation of the complex dynamics occurring in the tumor. Moreover, as outlined in the introduction, solid tumors are characterized by spatial and cellular heterogeneity, with different regions exhibiting distinct characteristics. PDE models can incorporate spatial variations in cell density, antigen expression, and other factors relevant to the bystander effect. For example, in the context of our study, distribution of antigen-positive and negative tumor cells withing the solid tumor bulk would likely have a major impact on the efficacy of the CAR T cell treatment as well as emergence of bystander effects. However, it is widely recognized that obtaining spatial data is often more challenging than acquiring temporal data in biological and clinical setting. This challenge becomes particularly pronounced when investigating the bystander effect, as outlined in the introduction, due to the complexities involved in setting up and conducting experiments in spatially resolved systems.

In Eq. (1), the killing of the antigen-positive tumor population is modeled through two ratio-dependent cell-kill terms denoted by DB (day−1) and DC (day−1). Those terms were originally proposed by de Pillis et al.36 to capture the interaction between tumor cells and immunological components, including CD8-positive T cells and natural killer cells. Therein, they discovered that tumor cell lysis by tumor-specific CD8-T cells is better modeled by using a term that is a function of the ratio of T cells to tumor cells rather than a usual linear cell-kill term. We also tested linear cell kill and confirmed that it does not capture the dynamics of our data; thus, we adopted the ratio-dependent functional form for cell kill for our model.

Ratio-dependent killing terms are given in Eq. (5). The parameter l (dimensionless) describes the degree of dependence of the killing rate on the ratio of CAR T cells or bystander cells, while the parameter s (dimensionless) characterizes the steepness of killing of the corresponding terms. The parameters dB (day−1) and dC (day−1) represent the maximum killing rate that can be achieved when the concentrations of bystander and CAR T cells are at their respective saturation levels. In Eq. (1), CAR T cells selectively recognize and target the antigen-positive tumor cell population, leading to their death. Experimental Studies and Data

We calibrate our model using data described in ref. 12. To test the hypothesis that CAR T cells induce significant bystander effects, Klampatsa et al. created an immunocompetent mouse model of malignant mesothelioma (MM) in which established tumors generated from murine AE17 MM cells that uniformly expressed the CAR target antigen, mesothelin, could be cured. Specifically, in their experiment, 2 × 106 murine AE17 MM cells, lentivirally transduced to express human mesothelin (AE17om), were seeded into the right flanks of B6 mice, and the tumor population reached approximately 50 mm3 by day three post-injection. The CAR T dosing schema they used to cure tumors with 100% expression of the target antigen consisted of two doses of 107 transduced M11 CAR T cells each, given intravenously on day 4 and day 6 post-tumor inoculation. They repeated all experiments at least twice, n = 8 mice per group, and recorded the tumor volume at several time points post-injection. Their results showed that M11 CAR T cells did not affect tumors negative for the target antigen.

Next, they mixed tumor cells that expressed the target antigen with varying percentages to assess whether the treatment schema could eliminate tumors that did not universally express the target antigen. They found that M11 CAR T cells could not cure 50%, 75%, or 90% mesothelin-positive tumors, indicating the lack of bystander effects. As defined in the introduction, bystander effects refer to the phenomenon where CAR T cells are able to eliminate tumor cells that do not express the target antigen via a variety of mechanisms, including Fas-FasL death receptor engagement and endogenous CD8 T activation11. However, they found that administering low-dose (100 mg/kg) cyclophosphamide (CTX) to the mice one day before treatment elicited bystander effects and cured tumors with varying antigen expression. We used this data to calibrate some of our model parameters sequentially. We used the free online tool Plot Digitizer Huwaldt39 to extract the data provided in ref. 12.

In our work, we employed MATLAB41 for parameter fitting and the numerical solution of the ODE system. Specifically, we leveraged the “fmincon” function from MATLAB’s Optimization Toolbox. fmincon finds the constrained minimum of a scalar function of several variables starting at an initial estimate and is a gradient-based method that is designed to work on problems where the objective and constraint functions are both continuous and have continuous derivatives. In our work, we define the objective function as the root mean square error between the total tumor size predicted by the numerical solution of the ODE system and given by the actual data.

We numerically solve the ODE system using MATLAB’s built-in ode45 function, designed for non-stiff differential equations. The ode45 function employs a 4th-order explicit Runge-Kutta method with a Dormand-Prince pair. For our implementation, we configured the step size to be Δt = 0.05 days and set the time range to span [3, 30] days. Note that injection of CAR T cell, vC(t), is a discontinuous function as its value abruptly changes from 0 to a positive number. It is well known that discontinuous source terms pose a serious challenge to ODE solvers. To tackle this issue, we solve the ODE piecewise between CAR T cell injection days. Essentially, we break our integration interval into sections that don’t contain any discontinuities and solve on each section separately. This process essentially involves resetting initial conditions for Tpos, Tneg, C, and B for each new interval based on the solution from the previous interval.
