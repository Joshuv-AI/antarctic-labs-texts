Interpreting Serial AI-ECG Changes During Heart Failure Therapy
Authors: Damiano Venturiello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: acute heart failure, aortic valve, atrial fibrillation, cardiac resynchronization therapy, coronary artery bypass, electrocardiogram

Artificial intelligence–enabled electrocardiography (AI-ECG) has expanded the role of the standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) beyond conventional pattern recognition. Deep learning models have demonstrated the ability to extract signatures associated with left ventricular dysfunction, abnormal filling pressures, cardiomyopathic phenotypes, and future heart failure (HF) risk, transforming the ECG from a descriptive test into a potential digital biomarker of cardiovascular health.1, 2, 3 Yet despite these advances, most AI-ECG applications remain fundamentally cross-sectional, providing a probability estimate of disease at a single point in time.

In the case series by Yoo et al,4 the authors explore an emerging and clinically relevant question: can AI-ECG be used not only to detect disease, but also to follow disease over time? Through 4 illustrative HF cases involving surgical coronary revascularization, aortic valve replacement with cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), percutaneous coronary intervention, and rhythm control for atrial fibrillation (AF), the authors describe serial trajectories of artificial intelligence (AI)-derived left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction scores during treatment. Their observations suggest that AI-ECG outputs may change in parallel with conventional markers of disease severity, while also highlighting important limitations in interpreting these changes.

From Detection to Tracking

The central contribution of this report is conceptual rather than technological.

Historically, AI-ECG studies have focused on diagnostic performance. The primary question has been whether an algorithm can identify an underlying abnormality such as reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) or elevated filling pressures. The cases presented by Yoo et al4 in the issue of JACC: Case Reports address a different question: can serial AI-ECG assessment provide meaningful information regarding disease evolution?

This distinction is particularly relevant in HF. Contemporary management relies on longitudinal assessment of symptoms, biomarkers, imaging findings, arrhythmia burden, and treatment response. Although echocardiography remains the cornerstone of structural and functional evaluation, repeated imaging is resource-intensive and often performed intermittently. In contrast, ECGs are inexpensive, widely available, and increasingly integrated into digital health platforms.

Recent reports in cardiac amyloidosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy have suggested that AI-ECG outputs may change alongside treatment response and disease progression.5,6 The observations reported by Yoo et al4 extend this concept to HF and raise the possibility that AI-ECG may eventually evolve from a screening tool into a dynamic biomarker of disease status.

Interpreting a Changing AI-ECG Score

The most educational aspect of this report may be the discordant cases.

In one patient, substantial recovery in LVEF following aortic valve replacement and CRT was accompanied by persistently elevated AI-derived LVSD scores. In another, AI-ECG trajectories varied considerably across transitions between AF and sinus rhythm before normalizing after successful rhythm control. These observations highlight an important principle: AI-ECG algorithms do not directly measure ventricular function. They analyze electrical signals associated with ventricular dysfunction. Consequently, a changing AI-ECG score may reflect more than 1 biological process.

For clinicians interpreting serial AI-ECG results, 3 questions should always be considered.

Has ventricular function changed?

Changes in LVEF, filling pressures, chamber remodeling, and HF status may influence AI-ECG outputs.

Has cardiac rhythm changed?

AF fundamentally alters ECG morphology and may affect algorithm performance independently of ventricular function.7

Has conduction or pacing status changed?

CRT, ventricular pacing, QRS narrowing, and conduction remodeling can substantially modify the electrical substrate analyzed by AI models.8

This framework has practical implications. A worsening AI-ECG score should not automatically be interpreted as worsening ventricular function. Likewise, an improving score may reflect restoration of sinus rhythm or electrical remodeling rather than true myocardial recovery.

Therefore, serial AI-ECG trajectories should always be interpreted within the broader clinical and electrophysiological context.

Toward Clinically Interpretable AI Biomarkers

The report by Yoo et al4 also highlights a broader challenge facing cardiovascular AI: interpretability.

Because the LVSD and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction algorithms were originally developed and validated against echocardiographic definitions of systolic (LVEF ≤40%) and diastolic dysfunction (E/e′ >15), some degree of concordance with LVEF and E/e′ is expected.9,10 The more important question is whether serial AI-ECG trajectories provide clinically meaningful information beyond the variables used for model development.

Future studies should move beyond simple correlation with imaging findings and evaluate whether serial AI-ECG changes predict worsening HF, hospitalization, treatment response, or mortality. Equally important will be the incorporation of rhythm-specific analyses and dedicated assessment of pacing and conduction-related effects. Understanding the determinants of score variability may ultimately prove as important as improving algorithm accuracy itself.

Conclusions

Yoo et al4 should be congratulated for exploring an underinvestigated dimension of AI-ECG application. Their report shifts the discussion from disease detection to disease tracking and highlights both the promise and the challenges of longitudinal AI-ECG assessment.

For practicing clinicians, the take-home message is straightforward: a changing AI-ECG score should never be interpreted in isolation. Ventricular function, rhythm status, and pacing or conduction changes must all be considered before clinical conclusions are drawn.

As AI-ECG moves from diagnostic classification toward longitudinal monitoring, understanding what a changing AI-ECG score truly represents may become one of the most important questions in cardiovascular AI.
