Associations Between Sports-Based After-School Activities, Cognitive Ability, Physical Health, Problematic Internet Use, Psychosocial Health, and Health-Related Behaviors Among Indonesian Adolescents
Authors: Rizky Nurulfa, Aija Klavina, Csaba Melczer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to compare cognitive ability, physical (motor proficiency and fitness), and psychosocial (depressive symptoms and quality of life) outcomes between adolescents participating in extracurricular sports (ES) and non-sports (NS) and to examine predictors of problematic Internet use (PIU).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 433 Indonesian adolescents (ES=239, 49.0% males; NS=194, 52.1% males). Standardized measures included the Problematic and Risky Internet Use Screening Scale for PIU, Raven’s Standard Progressive Matrices for cognitive ability, Bruininks–Oseretsky Test-2 for motor proficiency, the Indonesian Student Physical Fitness Test, the Youth Quality of Life–Short Form for quality of life, and the Patient Health Questionnaire–Adolescent Version for depression. Group differences were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney test. Spearman’s correlations were used to assess associations between variables, and linear regression was used to identify predictors of PIU.ResultsES adolescents showed significantly higher cognitive ability, motor proficiency, physical fitness, and quality of life and lower PIU and depressive symptoms than the NS groups (all p<0.001). PIU positively correlated with depression and negatively correlated with motor proficiency, physical fitness, cognitive ability, and quality of life. The regression analyses identified depression as a strong positive predictor of PIU, whereas cognitive ability, motor proficiency, physical fitness, and quality of life were significant negative predictors, explaining the substantial variance in PIU.ConclusionAdolescents participating in ES demonstrated higher cognitive, physical (motor proficiency and fitness), and psychosocial outcomes (depressive symptoms and quality of life) than NS participants. Depression was positively associated with PIU, whereas motor proficiency, fitness, cognitive ability, and quality of life were inversely associated with PIU.

INTRODUCTION

Adolescence is a formative period marked by rapid physical, cognitive, and psychosocial development, during which lifestyle behaviors and environmental contexts greatly influence health outcomes [1]. In contemporary societies, adolescent development increasingly occurs within a highly digitalized environment marked by widespread internet access, mobile technologies, and changing after-school activity patterns. Although digital technologies provide opportunities for learning, communication, and social participation, they also introduce potential risks when Internet engagement becomes excessive or poorly regulated. Adolescents may be particularly vulnerable to these risks, as their executive functioning and self-regulatory capacities are still developing [2].

One major concern in this field is problematic Internet use (PIU), which is defined as excessive, uncontrolled, and maladaptive patterns of online behavior that interfere with daily functioning [3]. Increasing evidence indicates that PIU among adolescents is associated with negative outcomes across multiple domains, including mental health, academic performance, physical health, and social functioning. Previous research has shown that PIU is linked to higher levels of depressive symptoms, emotional distress, and reduced life satisfaction [4,5].

Beyond psychosocial functioning, adolescents’ functional capacities, including cognitive ability, motor proficiency, and physical fitness, play important roles in shaping developmental outcomes. Cognitive ability, broadly defined as the capacity for reasoning, learning, and problem solving, is a key predictor of academic achievement and life success, and [6,7] physical competence is also essential during adolescence. Motor proficiency, which involves coordinated fine and gross motor skills, supports engagement in daily and structured activities [8,9]. Similarly, physical fitness, including cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, and flexibility, contributes to physical health and reduces the risk of chronic disease [10]. Research has also demonstrated positive associations between physical fitness, motor competence, and cognitive processes such as executive function, attention, and self-regulation [11-13].

Psychosocial health indicators such as depression and quality of life (QoL) are closely related to adolescent well-being and behavioral patterns. Depression is a significant mental health concern among young people, including those in Indonesia, where national surveys have indicated that a considerable proportion of adolescents experience psychological difficulties within a given year [14,15]. Depressive symptoms may include persistently low mood, irritability, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and impaired concentration [16]. In contrast, QoL reflects adolescents’ perceived well-being across multiple domains, including physical health, emotional functioning, social relationships, and environmental satisfaction [17]. Specific items commonly assessed include perceived physical health, energy levels, emotional well-being, social support from groups and family, satisfaction with school and leisure activities, and perceived safety in the home and community environments. Higher QoL is generally associated with regular physical activity, participation in structured sports, and social engagement, all of which promote both physical and psychosocial well-being [18]. Together, these psychosocial indicators provide a broader perspective on adolescents’ mental and social functioning.

Participation in extracurricular activities, particularly after-school programs, is an important environmental factor that influences adolescent development. Extracurricular activities are structured voluntary programs outside the formal school curriculum that contribute to social, cognitive, and emotional growth [19]. Sports-based extracurricular activities provide structured opportunities for physical activity, teamwork, and social interaction, which may enhance physical competence, psychological resilience, and positive behavioral regulation [20]. In contrast, non-sports extracurricular activities, such as academic clubs or arts programs, may provide valuable developmental experiences but can also involve more sedentary engagement or increased screen exposure [21,22]. Some studies suggest that adolescents who engage primarily in sedentary after-school activities may be more vulnerable to PIU because of extended screen time or solitary study habits [23].

The Indonesian context provides a particularly relevant setting in which to examine these relationships. Indonesia is one of the fastest-growing digital societies globally, with Internet penetration rising from 64.8% in 2018 to over 77% in 2022 [24]. Adolescents represent a highly active digital demographic group, with more than 75% of individuals aged 13–18 years reporting daily Internet use [25]. Recent national and regional studies have reported concerning levels of PIU among Indonesian adolescents, with prevalence estimates ranging from 19% to 40% depending on the population studied [14,15]. Despite these trends, limited research has examined how structured after-school activities simultaneously relate to adolescents’ cognitive ability, physical health, psychosocial well-being, and PIU within the Indonesian context. However, limited research has examined how structured afterschool activities interact with adolescents’ cognitive ability, physical health, psychosocial well-being, and PIU within this context.

This study aimed to compare cognitive ability, physical health (motor proficiency and fitness), PIU, psychosocial health, and health-related behaviors between sports and non-sports groups, and to identify significant predictors of PIU. This study hypothesized that higher motor proficiency, cognitive ability, and physical fitness are associated with lower PIU, while better QoL and lower depression symptoms act as protective factors. Adolescents in extracurricular sports (ES) programs are expected to show lower PIU, higher motor proficiency, a better QoL, and fewer depressive symptoms than those in non-sports (NS) programs.

METHODS

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional analytical design to examine the associations between PIU and a range of functional, psychosocial, and health-related lifestyle factors among Indonesian adolescents. Functional variables included cognitive ability, motor proficiency, and physical fitness, and psychosocial variables included QoL and depressive symptoms. Health-related lifestyle behaviors included physical activity, screen time, sleep patterns, and eating behaviors. Comparisons were made between students who participated in ES activities and those enrolled in NS programs. Data were collected between September 2025 and January 2026. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians, and assent was obtained from the students. Self-report questionnaires were administered in a classroom setting under the supervision of a researcher. This measurement was conducted in the school gym during semester break. The tests were conducted over three days: on the first day, a physical fitness test was administered following completion of the QoL questionnaire; on the second day, cognitive ability tests, the Problematic and Risky Internet Use Screening Scale (PRIUSS), and health-related lifestyle behavior questionnaires were administered; and on the third day, a motor proficiency test was conducted following the completion of the Depressive Symptoms Questionnaire. Prior to data collection, the teachers provided the students with practice during one learning session.

Participants

A total of 433 participants (50.3% males and 49.6% females) were recruited from two residential areas (urban and rural): Jakarta (DKI Jakarta Province) and Bogor Regency (West Java Province). Participants were eligible if they were 12–16 years old, enrolled in general education, and had written informed consent from a parent or guardian. Adolescents with neurological, cognitive, or physical impairments, medical conditions restricting physical activity, or incomplete questionnaire responses were excluded from the study. Participants were then classified into two groups based on their participation in ES. The ES group included adolescents who participated in organized sports at least once per week for a minimum of six months. Adolescents who did not meet these criteria were classified into the NS group. This grouping allowed comparisons of cognitive, physical, psychosocial, and lifestyle outcomes between ES and NS adolescents (Fig. 1).

Of the 453 participants initially enrolled, 20 were excluded due to missing data across the study variables through list-wise deletion, resulting in a final analytical sample of 433 participants with complete data. Missing data across variables ranged from 0% to 4.4%. Given the relatively low proportion of missing data, list-wise deletion was considered an appropriate method and was unlikely to substantially bias the results [26].

Instruments

Validated and standardized instruments were used to assess adolescents’ psychosocial and functional characteristics as well as health-related lifestyle behaviors. Psychosocial variables included PIU, QoL, and depressive symptoms. Functional variables included cognitive ability, motor proficiency, and physical fitness. Health-related lifestyle behaviors included physical activity, screen time, sleep duration and difficulty, and eating behavior. As no fully standardized Indonesian versions of several instruments were available, the questionnaires were translated and culturally adapted following a forward–backward translation procedure. The translated versions were subsequently pilot tested to evaluate their clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness for Indonesian adolescents before data collection.

Problematic Internet use

PIU was measured using a standardized PRIUSS, consisting of 18 items risk-based screening scale for PIU with questions organized into three subscales: 1) social impairment (six items), 2) emotional impairment (five items), and 3) risky/impulsive internet use (seven items) [27]. Adolescents who scored 26 points or more were classified as being at high risk, while those who scored between 15 and 25 points were classified as being at moderate risk for PIU. The PRIUSS response selections use a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, with the following responses: “never”=0, “rarely”=1, “sometimes”= 2, “often”=3, and “very often”=4 [28,29]. Data were collected using an online application and paper printouts.

Cognitive ability

Cognitive ability was assessed using Raven’s Standard Progressive Matrices (RSPM), a widely used nonverbal measure of abstract reasoning and general intelligence. The test requires participants to identify the missing element that completes visual pattern sequences of increasing difficulty. Each correct response receives 1 point and incorrect responses receive 0 points, resulting in total scores ranging from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating stronger cognitive ability. Raw scores can be interpreted as follows: 0–20 (below average), 21–30 (low average), 31–40 (average), 41–50 (high average), and 51–60 (superior). The RSPM has been validated across cultures and age groups, including adolescents in Southeast Asia, and it is suitable for cross-cultural research because it minimizes language and educational biases [30]. The RSPM generally requires approximately 30–45 min for completion.

Motor proficiency

Motor proficiency was measured using the Bruininks–Oseretsky Test of Motor Proficiency, Second Edition (BOT-2). Motor proficiency was assessed using four subtests of the BOT-2: 1) bilateral coordination (7 items, score range=0–24 points), 2) balance (9 items, score range=0–37 points), 3) running and speed (5 items, score range=0–52 points), and 4) strength (5 items, score range=0–42 points) [29]. Administering these four subtests took approximately 15–20 min. The raw score for each item is converted into a point score according to the BOT-2 scoring system to produce a measurable performance metric. The point scores for each item are summed to obtain a subtest score, which is then converted into a standardized score based on age and gender using the BOT-2 conversion table for statistical analysis. These scores were combined to obtain the total motor proficiency score [29,31].

Physical fitness

Physical fitness was assessed using the Indonesian Student Physical Fitness Test (Tes Kebugaran Pelajar Nusantara [TKPN]), the official Indonesian student fitness test developed by the Ministry of Youth and Sport. The TKPN battery includes four components: the PACER test to assess cardiorespiratory endurance, the V-sit reach test to evaluate flexibility, the squat thrust test to measure muscular endurance and agility, and the sit-up test to assess abdominal muscular strength and endurance. All components were administered according to standardized protocols. Performance scores were converted into composite fitness scores based on age- and gender-specific norms, with higher scores indicating a better physical fit. TKPN has demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability for assessing student fitness in Indonesia. The cumulative point scores from the four subtests were calculated and subsequently transformed into scale scores, standard scores, and age- and gender-referenced equivalents. The TKPN requires approximately 45–60 min to administer depending on the participant’s aerobic endurance. Based on the scale scores and normative data, participants’ performance was categorized into five levels: excellent (5), good (4), average (3), poor (2), and very poor (1) reliability alpha Cronbach 0.75 [32].

Quality of life

The Youth Quality of Life–Short Form (YQOL-SF) is a 15-item self-administered instrument that assesses multiple dimensions of adolescent QoL. Items are rated on an 11-point scale (0=“not at all” to 10=“completely”) and transformed to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better perceived QoL [33]. The overall score is the average of all transformed items. The YQOL-SF demonstrates strong reliability and validity, including high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.77– 0.96) and acceptable test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.74–0.85), and typically requires about 10 min to complete [34].

Depressive symptoms

The Patient Health Questionnaire–Adolescent Version (PHQ-A) is a brief self-report screening instrument used to assess depressive symptoms in adolescents aged approximately 11–17 years, adapted from the adult PHQ-9 to reflect adolescent experiences and language. The instrument consists of nine items, each corresponding to the core symptoms of major depressive disorder according to the DSM-5 criteria, such as depressed mood, sleep disturbances, appetite changes, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and thoughts of self-harm [35-37]. The questionnaire typically requires approximately 5–10 min to complete, depending on the respondent’s reading level. Adolescents report the frequency of symptoms over the past two weeks using a 4-point Likert scale: 0=“not at all,” 1=“several days,” 2=“more than half the days,” and 3=“nearly every day,” resulting in total scores ranging from 0 to 27 [38,39]. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. Research has demonstrated that the PHQ-A has good reliability and validity in adolescent populations, with strong sensitivity and specificity for detecting depression in school and clinical settings [39,40].

Health lifestyle behaviors

Healthy lifestyle behaviors were assessed across four domains: physical activity, information technology (IT) use, eating habits, and sleep behaviors [41]. Adolescents reported the number of days and minutes spent in outdoor and indoor activities outside school over the past week, with responses dichotomized into weekly versus daily activities according to the World Health Organization guidelines (2020) [42]. IT use, including watching TV, playing games, chatting, emailing, and online messaging, was reported separately for weekdays and weekends, following the Health Behavior in Schoolaged Children scoring system with a cutoff of three hours per day to facilitate international comparisons [43-45]. Eating habits were assessed using five items addressing the frequency of three daily meals, fruit and vegetable consumption, avoidance of processed foods, soda intake, and limiting sweets. Sleep duration were assessed by measuring sleep duration on weekdays and weekends, as well as difficulties in falling asleep. Typically, the questioner requires about 5–10 min to complete.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Latvian Academy of Sports Education (No. S/51813, 20 January, 2024) and the Ethics Committee of Universitas Negeri Jakarta, Indonesia (No. 104/UN39.14. PT.01.05/1/2026). All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) were calculated, and data were examined for normality, linearity, and homogeneity of variance. The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that most study variables significantly deviated from a normal distribution across extracurricular participation; therefore, non-parametric statistical procedures were considered appropriate for subsequent analyses. A Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to compare the functional outcomes of PIU and physical, cognitive, and psychosocial outcomes (QoL and depressive symptoms) between ES and NS groups. Spearman correlations were conducted separately for ES and NS groups to assess the associations among the study variables. The correlation coefficients were interpreted as small (0.10–0.29), medium (0.30–0.49), and large (0.50–0.69), very large (0.70– 0.89), and nearly perfect (≥0.90) [46]. Linear regression analyses were performed to identify significant predictors of PIU, with demographic variables entered in the first block, healthrelated behaviors in the second block, and cognitive and motor variables in the third block at a significance level of p< 0.05. Hierarchical multiple regression analyses were performed separately for the ES and NS groups to identify variables associated with PIU. Predictors were entered into four blocks: 1) cognitive ability; 2) motor proficiency and physical fitness; 3) QoL and depressive symptoms; and 4) lifestyle and activity-related variables. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance statistics. Variables related to weekday and weekend sleep duration were excluded because of severe multicollinearity (VIF >30). All the remaining predictors showed acceptable multicollinearity (VIF <10; tolerance >0.10) [47].

RESULTS

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the participants and the descriptive results of the psychosocial and functional outcomes. A total of 433 adolescents were included in the study: 194 in the NS group and 239 in the ES group. In terms of gender distribution, 101 males (52.1%) were in the NS group, and 117 males (49.0%) were in the ES group. The ES group had a significantly lower body mass index (BMI) compared with the NS group (p=0.018).

Students in the ES group demonstrated significantly lower PIU and lower depression levels, while showing markedly higher cognitive ability, motor proficiency, fitness performance, and QoL (all p<0.001) than the NS group. Notably, the mean PRIUSS score in the NS group (25.42) was close to the established high-risk cutoff (≥26), indicating that a substantial proportion of NS participants approached clinically relevant levels of PIU. The highest difference between the two groups was observed in QoL (r=0.70, p<0.001).

In terms of lifestyle behaviors, adolescents in the ES group engaged in more physical activity than those in the NS group. They spent 27.93 min/day more on outdoor activity, 38.41 min/day more on indoor activity, and participated in physical activity 1.51 times/week more frequently (p<0.001). In contrast, screen time was lower among ES participants, by 1.37 hours on weekends and 3.35 hours on weekdays (p< 0.001). Sleep behavior also differed between the groups. Adolescents in the ES group reported longer sleep duration and fewer sleep difficulties, sleeping 2.35 hours longer on weekdays and 2.06 hours longer on weekends compared with the NS group (p<0.001). The outputs of the subgroup analyses are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

As presented in Table 2 Spearman’s correlation analyses conducted separately for the NS and ES groups showed that PIU was significantly associated with all study variables (mostly p<0.001; eating three meals per day in the ES group: p=0.006). Across both groups, functional outcomes only demonstrated consistently strong negative correlations with PIU, with coefficients ranging from -0.95 to -0.79. These associations included cognitive ability, motor proficiency, physical fitness, QoL, outdoor activity, and sleep duration, indicating that a higher PIU was strongly related to poorer functional performance and reduced well-being. For psychosocial outcomes, PIU showed strong positive correlations with depressive symptoms (0.73 to 0.81) and strong negative correlations with QoL (-0.94 to -0.91).

For health-related lifestyle behaviors, screen time variables demonstrated strong positive correlations, particularly with weekday study-related screen time and weekend leisure screen time (0.85 to 0.95). Physical activity indicators, including outdoor activity and weekly physical activity time, showed moderate-to-strong negative correlations with PIU (-0.91 to -0.33). Sleep duration on weekdays and weekends showed strong negative correlations (-0.91 to -0.83), whereas sleep difficulty showed strong positive correlations (0.73 to 0.75). Dietary behaviors demonstrated weak-to-moderate correlations, with fruit consumption and avoidance of processed foods negatively correlated with PIU, and soda consumption positively correlated with PIU.

Tables 3 and 4 presents the results of hierarchical multiple regression analyses examining the predictors of PIU among adolescents in the NS and ES groups. In the NS group, cognitive ability significantly predicted PIU in Model 1, explaining 67.9% of the variance. The inclusion of motor proficiency and physical fitness in Model 2 significantly improved the model fit, followed by further significant increases after adding psychosocial variables (QoL and depression) in Model 3 and lifestyle behaviors in Model 4. The final model explained 95.3% of the variance in PIU (adjusted R2=0.950, p<0.001). Depression was the strongest positive predictor of PIU, whereas cognitive ability, motor proficiency, fitness, QoL, and physical activity were significant negative predictors. Sleep difficulty and indoor activity showed small positive associations, whereas dietary behavior was not a significant predictor. A similar pattern was observed in the ES group. Cognitive ability explained 87.1% of the variance in PIU in Model 1. The subsequent inclusion of motor proficiency, fitness, psychosocial variables, and lifestyle behaviors significantly improved the model fit at each step. The final model also explained 95.3% of the variance in PIU (adjusted R2=0.951, p<0.001). Lower cognitive ability, motor proficiency, fitness, QoL, and physical activity were associated with higher PIU, whereas depressive symptoms remained a significant positive predictor. Sleep difficulty and dietary variables were not significant predictors in the ES group. The high proportion of explained variance observed in both models likely reflects the strong interrelatedness of adolescent cognitive, behavioral, and psychosocial functioning domains, as well as the shared variance among predictors. Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable levels overall, although some predictors showed elevated VIF values (up to approximately 10), and sleep duration variables were excluded owing to severe multicollinearity (VIF >30) to ensure model stability and interpretability.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the associations between ES participation and cognitive, physical (motor proficiency and fitness), and QoL, depression, and PIU, among Indonesian adolescents. Comparisons between adolescents engaged in ES and NS programs revealed notable differences across multiple health-related, cognitive, and lifestyle domains. Additionally, significant predictors of PIU were identified, highlighting the complex interplay between cognitive, physical, psychosocial, and lifestyle factors.

A notable finding of this study is that the mean PIU score in the NS group approached the established high-risk cutoff score of 26. Based on the PRIUSS interpretation guidelines, the NS group’s average fell within the upper range of moderate-risk PIU and was close to the clinically meaningful levels of problematic Internet behavior. By contrast, the ES group demonstrated substantially lower PIU scores, falling within the lower moderate-risk range. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies reporting that excessive internet use among adolescents is frequently associated with poor psychological well-being, emotional distress, reduced social functioning, and lower life satisfaction [2,48]. In contrast, adolescents participating in ES demonstrated lower PIU levels, supporting the notion that structured physical activities may serve as protective contexts that promote self-regulation, social engagement, and healthier lifestyle patterns [20,49]. However, because the present study used a cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of directional or causal effects.

Functional outcomes: cognitive ability and motor proficiency and physical fitness

Adolescents participating in ES demonstrated substantially higher cognitive performance compared with those in the NS group. Based on the RSPM classification, the NS group’s mean score reflected average cognitive ability, whereas the ES group’s mean score fell within the high-average range. These findings align with previous literature indicating that participation in organized sports and regular physical activity are associated with executive functioning, attentional control, working memory, and processing speed [50-52]. These results extend evidence from general physical activity studies, which sometimes show only modest cognitive improvements [53] by emphasizing the additional cognitive stimulation provided by organized, socially interactive sports.

Similarly, motor proficiency was significantly higher among adolescents who engaged in ES. Motor proficiency encompasses coordination, agility, balance, strength, and fine and gross motor controls. These abilities are regularly engaged in organized sports participation. Motor proficiency may reflect repeated engagement in coordinated physical activities that require neuromuscular control and proprioceptive awareness [54,55]. Improved motor competence is associated with greater confidence in their physical abilities, which may encourage adolescents to participate in a broad range of physical activities. Higher motor proficiency may be associated with continued physical activity engagement during adolescence and adulthood.

In addition to cognitive and motor advantages, adolescents also demonstrate higher levels of physical fitness. Physical fitness, which is assessed using the TKPN, reflects several components of health-related fitness, including cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, flexibility, and speed or agility. Adolescents who participate in organized sports are typically exposed to training that includes aerobic, anaerobic, and strength-related activities. Such training is associated with physiological adaptations, including improved oxygen uptake, cardiovascular efficiency, and muscular strength [49,56-58].

Higher levels of physical fitness during adolescence are widely recognized as important indicators of current and future health statuses. Research has suggested that adolescents with higher cardiorespiratory fitness have a lower risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic disorders later in life [59,60]. Participation in structured sports may be associated with more favorable long-term health profiles. In addition, resistance- and strength-based activities commonly incorporated into youth sports programs can enhance musculoskeletal development and reduce the risk of injury [61]. Previous research has also shown that adolescents who engage in organized sports tend to exhibit greater muscular strength, aerobic capacity, and physical competence than their peers who participate only in unstructured physical activity [10,62].

Psychosocial outcomes

Adolescents participating in ES reported a substantially higher QoL and fewer depressive symptoms than those in the NS group. On the PHQ-A, the NS group’s mean score reflected mild depressive symptoms, whereas the ES group demonstrated minimal depressive symptom levels. Similarly, the YQOL-SF scores indicated substantially better perceived well-being and psychosocial functioning among ES adolescents. These findings are consistent with previous literature suggesting that organized sports participation is associated with greater social connectedness, emotional resilience, selfesteem, and perceived competence [63-65].

In contrast, NS adolescents display higher depressive tendencies and lower QoL, suggesting that a lack of structured engagement may increase their vulnerability to psychosocial difficulties. These findings emphasize the potential of structured physical activity to provide adolescents with supportive peer networks, opportunities for achievement, and adaptive coping skills [66-68]. Regression analyses further confirmed that depressive symptoms were a robust predictor of higher PIU, underscoring the interplay between emotional well-being and behavioral regulation in adolescence.

Associations between PIU and cognitive, physical, and behavioral variables

Correlation analyses indicated strong associations between PIU and multiple cognitive, physical, psychosocial, and behavioral variables. Higher PIU was associated with lower cognitive ability, motor proficiency, physical fitness, and QoL, as well as higher depressive symptoms, greater screen exposure, and poorer sleep patterns.

The relatively high correlations observed among several psychosocial and behavioral variables may partly reflect conceptual overlap between constructs assessing related domains of adolescent functioning as well as shared method variance resulting from the use of self-report measures administered within a single assessment context [69].

The regression analyses revealed that depressive symptoms were significant predictors of PIU. Adolescents experiencing emotional distress may use online environments as a coping strategy associated with higher levels of Internet use. These findings highlight the interconnected nature of emotional well-being and behavioral regulation during adolescence. Consistent with previous research, adolescents involved in ES demonstrated lower levels of PIU than did those in the NS group [29,70,71], which was associated with reduced cognitive ability, motor proficiency, physical fitness, and QoL, whereas higher levels of PIU were associated with increased depressive symptoms, sleep difficulties, and screen time. Interestingly, the correlations between PIU and several functional variables were stronger among ES participants, suggesting that higher engagement in structured physical activity may reinforce protective mechanisms against excessive digital behavior.

Healthy lifestyle behaviors

The present study also found that adolescents engaged in ES exhibited healthier lifestyle behaviors than their NS peers did. ES participants reported greater engagement in both outdoor and indoor physical activities, higher weekly physical activity frequency, longer sleep duration, and shorter leisure screen time.

These patterns likely reflect the structured nature of organized sports programs that encourage regular activities and establish daily routines. Participation in sports often requires scheduled training sessions, competitions, and team commitments, which may limit the time available for sedentary activities, such as prolonged screen use. Similarly, previous studies have reported that adolescents involved in ES tend to demonstrate higher levels of daily physical activity and lower sedentary behavior [49].

Daily behavioral routines appeared to be strongly related to PIU. Adolescents who maintained regular physical activity patterns and structured schedules demonstrated lower levels of PIU. Structured sports participation is associated with structured daily routines and lower levels of unregulated screen-based activities, thereby reducing opportunities for excessive digital engagement [72,73].

Sleep behavior also differed significantly between groups. ES adolescents reported longer sleep duration on both weekdays and weekends as well as fewer sleep difficulties than NS adolescents. The relatively long weekend sleep duration observed in the ES group should be interpreted with caution. One possible explanation is compensatory “catch-up sleep,” a common pattern among adolescents who accumulate sleep debt during structured school days and extracurricular commitments [74,75]. This behavior may be more pronounced in ES participants owing to greater physical demands and more tightly scheduled weekdays. Previous research has shown that adolescents often extend their sleep duration on weekends to compensate for insufficient weekday sleep, although this pattern may also be associated with irregular sleep–wake cycles and adverse health outcomes [76,77]. Adequate sleep is essential for cognitive function, emotional regulation, and physical recovery [78]. Previous studies have shown that regular physical activity can improve sleep quality, increase sleep duration, and regulate circadian rhythms, which may partially explain the sleep advantages observed in sports participants [79,80]. In adolescents, participation in organized sports has been associated with healthier sleep patterns and reduced sleep disturbances, suggesting that structured physical activity is associated with better sleep-related outcomes and indicators of overall well-being [81,82].

Interestingly, dietary behaviors such as fruit consumption and avoidance of processed foods were not significantly associated with PIU. This finding suggests that, although nutrition remains an important component of overall health, behavioral factors such as physical activity, sleep patterns, and screen time management may play a more direct role in influencing PIU [83,84].

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study provides a comprehensive view of adolescent development by integrating cognitive, motor, physical, psychosocial, and lifestyle factors to better understand PIU. Using a relatively large and diverse sample, adolescents involved in ES and NS programs highlighted the potential benefits of organized sports beyond general activity. Correlation and regression analyses offer a multidimensional perspective on how cognitive ability, motor proficiency, physical fitness, QoL, depressive symptoms, and lifestyle behaviors are related to PIU.

This study had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and reverse causation cannot be ruled out; adolescents with lower PIU and better overall functioning may be more likely to engage in and sustain participation in sports activities. Second, reliance on selfreported measures may have introduced reporting bias and shared method variance. Third, although school location (urban vs. rural) was included as a contextual demographic indicator, direct measures of socioeconomic status were not collected, limiting our ability to fully account for potential socioeconomic confounders. Additionally, the differences observed between the groups (e.g., BMI and other characteristics) may reflect underlying selection effects rather than the impact of sports participation itself.

Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to clarify causal pathways and disentangle the selection effects from the true intervention impacts. The inclusion of objective measures (e.g., accelerometers for physical activity, device-based screen tracking, and sleep monitoring) enhances measurement validity. Incorporating comprehensive socioeconomic indicators and examining potential moderating mechanisms such as self-regulation, social support, and environmental influences would provide a deeper understanding of how structured sports participation may be associated with reduced PIU. Despite these limitations, the findings indicate that participation in organized sports is associated with more favorable cognitive, psychosocial, and behavioral profiles along with lower levels of PIU.

CONCLUSION

In summary, ES participation is associated with enhanced cognitive, motor, physical, and psychosocial functioning among adolescents as well as lower PIU. Depression emerged as a significant risk factor of PIU, whereas cognitive ability, motor proficiency, fitness, and overall well-being were inversely associated with PIU. These findings underscore the multifaceted benefits of organized sports and highlight the importance of integrating structured physical activity into adolescent health-promotion strategies. Addressing multiple domains of adolescent development simultaneously may provide the most effective approach for reducing maladaptive behaviors such as PIU.
