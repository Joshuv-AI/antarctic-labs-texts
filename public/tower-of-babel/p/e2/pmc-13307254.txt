Long‐Term Exposure to Air Pollution and Incidence of Parkinson's Disease: A Danish Nationwide Administrative Cohort Study
Authors: Thomas Cole‐Hunter, Sonja Antic, Youn‐Hee Lim, Jiawei Zhang, Laust Hvas Mortensen, Kees de Hoogh, Steffen Loft, George Maria Napolitano, Stéphane Tuffier, Marie Laura Bergmann, Bert Brunekreef, Gerard Hoek, Beate Ritz, Zorana Jovanovic Andersen, Rina So
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong‐term exposure to air pollution has been linked to Parkinson's disease (PD) incidence, yet evidence is mixed, partly because of challenges with PD diagnosis and definition. We examined this association in a nationwide administrative cohort.MethodsWe followed 3,280,190 Danish residents ≥30 years old from January 1, 2000 until December 31, 2018 for PD incidence, defined as either first hospital contact for primary PD or redeemed prescription of PD medication, as recorded in the Danish National Patient Registry or Prescription Registry, respectively. We assigned annual mean air pollution exposure concentrations at baseline residential address using the hybrid land‐use regression model (fine particulate matter [PM2.5], nitrogen dioxide [NO2], ozone [warm‐season, O3w], black carbon [BC]) rendered at 0.1 × 0.1 km. We used Cox proportional hazard models adjusting for age, sex, individual‐level, and area‐level socioeconomic factors.ResultsDuring a mean (standard deviation) follow‐up of 15.7 (5.6) years, 36,665 participants developed PD. Median (interquartile range [IQR]) exposure levels of PM2.5, NO2, O3w, and BC were 12.4 (2.0), 20.2 (7.9), 80.2 (4.3) μg/m3, and 1.01 (0.4) × 10−5/m, respectively. Hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) for associations between air pollutants (per IQR) and PD incidence were: 1.05 (1.03, 1.07) for PM2.5; 1.03 (1.01, 1.05) for NO2; 0.98 (0.97, 1.00) for O3w; and 1.04 (1.02, 1.06) for BC.ConclusionsIn a representative nationwide cohort, we find that long‐term exposure to air pollution is associated with PD incidence. This unique study, with access to incidence data from administrative health registers, provides new evidence supporting air pollution as a PD risk factor. © 2026 The Author(s). Air pollution has previously been linked to adverse effects on the brain and central nervous system, with long‐term exposure leading to neurodegenerative diseases, including dementia, Alzheimer's disease, and Parkinson's disease (PD). 1 Biological mechanisms for air pollution, especially fine particulate matter (PM2.5), include systemic inflammation, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress, 2 and major risk factors for the neurodegenerative processes of PD are age, being male, and pesticide exposures. 3 PD is the second most prevalent progressive neurodegenerative disease (after Alzheimer's), with a prevalence ranging from 1 to 2 of 1000 individuals, and this number has been increasing to 10 of 1000 in individuals over 60 years old worldwide. 4 Globally, PD is estimated to affect nearly 9 million individuals, with an increase of approximately 150% from 1990 to 2019. 5 This will increase the already large cost burden on society because of caring for PD patients, with PD bearing the highest per‐person cost among all brain disorders. 6

Epidemiological findings concerning links between air pollution and PD are, however, still mixed (Supplementary Table S1). One major explanation for the mixed findings may be differences in methods for assessing outcomes (Supplementary Table S1), with a combination of medical record, medication, or neurologist‐based PD diagnosis. Definitions solely based on hospital records are usually more specific, but lack sensitivity because they do not include milder PD cases. Definitions adding medication have higher sensitivity, but may include less specific parkinsonisms (eg, secondary PD). 14 , 17 A previous study in Denmark added medication prescription information alongside hospital diagnoses to address potential underestimation of prevalent or incident cases of PD, and the study found that adding filled PD prescriptions to the hospital‐diagnosis‐only definition increased the number of PD prevalence cases by several fold, highlighting the importance of balancing sensitivity and specificity. 6

In this study, we examine the association between long‐term exposure to air pollution and incidence of PD, using unique Danish nationwide health registers to define PD incidence combining hospital contact diagnosis data with PD (dopaminergic) medication prescription data.

Subjects and Methods

Study Population

We used the Danish nationwide administrative cohort of 3,323,612 residents in Denmark 30 years or older at baseline on January 1, 2000, which was previously also used in the ELAPSE project. 18 , 19 From Statistics Denmark, we extracted data including sex, age, country of origin, household income, and highest attained level of education, occupational, and marital status of each individuals as of 1999. Additionally, we gathered data on mean household income and unemployment rates at the parish level as of 2001. The region for each individual was determined using their residential address in 2000. We ended follow‐up on December 31, 2018.

Incidence Outcome Definition

We defined PD incidence as either the first hospital contact (outpatient, inpatient, or emergency room admission) for primary PD or the first redeemed prescription of PD‐specific medication, whichever came first, as recorded in the Danish National Patient Registry and Prescription Registry, respectively. 20 , 21

We searched for the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐10 code G20 (primary PD) for hospital admissions from the year 1994 onward, and the ICD‐8 code 342 (corresponding to ICD‐10's G20) for admissions before the year 1994. A primary diagnosis of PD only was used as it is considered to be more reliable and accurate (increasing specificity) compared with supplementary diagnoses of PD, when the patient is hospitalized for a different disorder to primary PD. This consideration is based on a previous finding that 65% of patients with a primary PD diagnosis were from a neurologic department compared to only 7% when PD was a supplementary diagnosis. 20

Furthermore, we searched for the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code N04B02 and N04B03 for levodopa medication redemption (≥1 prescription) from the year 1995 onward, when prescription registration started in Denmark. 21 The supplementary PD medications were also extracted from the prescription registry database, including amantadin, cabergolin, entacapone, pergolid, pramipexole, rasagiline, ropinirol, and selegilin, some of which (largely pramipexole and ropinirol) had, since circa 2010, been indicated for treatment also of non‐PD ailments such as restless leg syndrome. Therefore, we conservatively considered these supplementary medications as indicating prevalence or incidence of PD only if prescribed before levodopa medication or hospitalization for PD (Supplementary Figure S1). Individuals with hospital admission for PD or medication prescription for levodopa before the cohort baseline (January 1, 2000) were excluded from analyses as prevalent cases (Supplementary Figure S1).

Air Pollution Exposure Assignment

We estimated annual average air pollutant (PM2.5, nitrogen dioxide [NO2], black carbon [BC], and ozone [O3]) exposure concentrations to participant residences at December 31, 1999 using the spatial European‐wide hybrid land‐use regression (LUR models for the year 2010 from ELAPSE rendered at 0.1 × 0.1 km grids. 22 We assigned O3 as a maximum running 8‐hour average in the boreal warm season (O3w, April–September). Details on these methods have been published previously. 11 In brief, the LUR models incorporated data from the European Environment Agency AirBase routine monitoring of PM2.5 and NO2 in 2010 as well as filter reflectance measurements of PM2.5 absorbance for BC from the European Study of Cohorts for Air Pollution Effects (ESCAPE) project during 2009 and 2010. The predictors for the LUR models included land cover and road length, data from satellite, and dispersion models. The year 2010 was chosen for modelling because it offered the most comprehensive monitoring data for PM2.5 across Europe.

For the sensitivity analysis, we used annually varying exposures accounting for residential mobility, based on back‐ and forward‐extrapolation of the 2010 LUR models, using data from the Danish Eulerian Hemispheric Model (DEHM) (details in Supplementary Text S1).

Statistical Analysis

The methodology in this study closely followed the framework by the ELAPSE project. 23 Our main analysis used Cox proportional hazard models with age as the underlying time scale. The follow‐up started on January 1, 2000 and ended at the date of event, with censoring at the first occurrence of either the date of death, emigration, disappeared from records or end of follow‐up (December 31, 2018). Components of the three models, considering the third as our main model, are:

Model (M) 1: accounted for age (underlying time scale), sex (strata), region (strata), and parish (cluster);

(M2): M1 + adjustment for household income in deciles, occupational status, immigrant status, marital status, and educational level;

(M3): M2 + adjustment for parish‐level mean income and percent unemployment (year 2001).

We assessed the shape of concentration‐response curves by replacing the linear term of each pollutant with a natural cubic spline term with two degrees of freedoms (df), but removed the cluster term for parish to reduce computation time (with the expectation of only increased confidence intervals [CI] by including this term).

We investigated effect modification for baseline characteristics: (1) individual factors, including age (by tertiles ≤43.9/43.9–58.4/≥58.4 years), sex (male/female), household income (quintiles), occupational status (employed/unemployed), and educational level (below/above college level); and (2) for exploratory purposes, existence of co‐morbidities before baseline, including stroke, ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, depression, and diabetes mellitus.

Sensitivity analyses investigated (1) robustness to co‐pollutant adjustment; (2) proportional hazards violations; 24 (3) unmeasured behavioral confounding (via indirect adjustment 25 using related data from the Danish National Health Survey [DNS] in years 2010 and 2013 26 ); and (4) alternative exposure metrics of back‐ and forward‐extrapolating exposure estimates, early‐case exclusion, and alternative PD definitions (details in Supplementary Text S2).

Results

PD Incidence

From 3,323,612 participants of the Danish nationwide administrative cohort, we excluded a total of 31,593 participants because of missing covariate (region [n = 241], parish and parish‐level socioeconomic status [SES] [n = 28,729], and individual‐level SES [n = 2623]) information required for our main model (Supplementary Figure S2). We further excluded 11,829 participants because of prevalent PD. Characteristics of included versus total population were equivalent, whereas those excluded because of prevalent PD were older in age and more often receiving pension and being widowed (Supplementary Table S2).

Among 3,280,190 participants with complete information during a follow‐up period of 15.7 years, 36,665 developed PD. Incidence of PD was defined by first PD hospital admission in 5866 (16.0%) participants and by first PD medication prescription in 29,748 (81.0%) participants, whereas 1051 (2.9%) participants had same‐day PD admission and prescription. Prevalent cases were excluded from the analyses and detailed numbers can be checked in Supplementary Figure S1 and its note.

Those with PD compared to without PD were more likely to be: older (62.6 years); male (55.1%); married/partnered (67.0%); and non‐immigrants (95.7%) (Table 1). The average age of PD diagnosis was 77.7 years.

Air Pollution Exposure

Annual mean concentrations of PM2.5, NO2, and O3w all exceeded the World Health Organization (WHO) 2021 Air Quality Guidelines, 27 at 12.4 ± 1.5 μg/m3, 20.2 ± 7.9 μg/m3, and 80.2 ± 4.3 μg/m3, respectively (Table 2). They fall, however, below the European Union (EU) air quality standards 28 of 25 μg/m3, 40 μg/m3, and 120 μg/m3. The annual mean concentration of BC, without WHO guidelines or EU standards, was 1.0 ± 0.4 × 10−5/m (Table 2). Moderately positive correlations were observed for PM2.5 and NO2 (0.64) and BC (0.69), whereas PM2.5, NO2, and BC were moderately negatively correlated with O3w (−0.55 to −0.72, respectively) (Table 2).

Sensitivity Analyses

Results were robust across a wide range of sensitivity analyses. Associations persisted after co‐pollutant adjustment (Supplementary Table S4), indirect adjustment for body mass index (BMI) and smoking (Supplementary Table S5). The proportional hazards assumption was violated for education, household income, marital status, and occupational status; however, when these variables were included as strata, effect estimates remained consistent (Supplementary Table S6). Estimates remained positive when using back‐extrapolated estimates at baseline and extrapolated time‐varying exposures (Supplementary Table S7 and S8), after excluding cases in the first 1 to 2 years of follow‐up (Supplementary Table S9). Under alternative PD case definitions, associations were stronger when restricting to hospital‐based cases, whereas in prescription‐only definitions, only PM2.5 remained significantly associated with PD incidence (Supplementary Table S10).

Discussion

In our representative nationwide administrative cohort study of over 3 million individuals with 36,665 incident PD cases, we detected statistically significant positive associations with long‐term exposure to air pollutants PM2.5, BC, and NO2. O3w was null or negatively associated with incident PD, even in two‐pollutant adjusted models, a pattern that has been seen previously in our previous Danish study on cause‐specific mortality, 19 and most likely reflects that our modelled O3w is a secondary pollutant and typically inversely correlated with all primary (and largely traffic‐related) pollutants including PM2.5, BC, and NO2 as can be seen in our data.

Studies are inconsistent on which source or component of air pollution is most relevant for PD. Our previous study in a pooled European cohort suggested potassium, a component of PM2.5 and a marker of biomass burning, to be most strongly associated with PD mortality. 11 In New York State, organic matter and nitrate (marker of traffic in urban areas) as components of PM2.5 were seen to be most strongly associated with aggravation of PD (measured as first hospitalization). 29

Stronger associations were observed for hospital‐based PD cases (Supplementary Table S10), likely because of capturing more clinically confirmed cases. Prescription‐based definitions, although capturing more mild cases, may be affected by misclassification (eg, off‐label use, symptomatic treatment). Combining both sources increases case numbers and coverage of both severe and mild cases, but may dilute associations because of heterogeneity in case definitions. These differences highlight that case ascertainment strategies can influence effect estimates and should be considered when comparing studies.

Biologically, the mechanism through which air pollution, especially PM2.5 but also NO2, affects the central nervous system and thereby increases risk for central nervous system disease including PD is through systemic inflammation, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress. 2 Epidemiologically, a link between air pollution exposure and incident PD has been investigated in 20 previous studies among populations in Europe, North America, China, South Korea, and Taiwan (Supplementary Table S1). The evidence coming from these studies is still equivocal, with some showing no or weak associations. However, some convincing evidence has been reported for the pollutants PM2.5 and NO2. For example, several large population‐based studies and ours reported strong positive associations, including a study from South Korea, showing elevated risk of NO2 exposure, 8 a study from China reporting 41% increased risk per IQR of 8.2 μg/m3 in PM2.5, 30 a large United States Medicare cohort presenting a 13% increased risk per 5 μg/m3 increase of PM2.5, 10 and two studies from Canada reporting increased risk of 9% 31 and 4% 32 per IQR of 1.6 and 9.8 μg/m3, respectively (see Supplementary Table S1 for details).

Concerning NO2, a previous Danish case–control study among the National Hospital Register reported, when we use 5 μg/m3 to calculate the HR for the comparison, a HR of 1.16 (95% CI, 1.05–1.28), 13 while we found a HR of 1.01 (95% CI, 1.00–1.02), which could be attributable to the fact that the current study, unlike that previous study, did not use medical record reviews removing false positives and, therefore, non‐differential misclassification (biasing toward the null). Elsewhere, the strong associations have been seen in South Korea 9 and the Netherlands, 12 although both compared the 4th to the 1st quartile of NO2 exposure concentration rather than using a continuous IQR as we did, which makes comparison challenging.

BC has been less frequently studied with respect to PD, with only two studies to date that are showing conflicting results. 7 , 31 The Canadian study 31 reported a smaller HR per IQR increase in BC concentration, which while not significant, is similar in magnitude to our HR. On the other hand, the Finnish study 7 observed no association with PD per an IQR increase in BC concentration, which is not directly comparable to our reflectance measurements of PM2.5 absorbance that did result in a 4% increased risk. In addition to the differences in BC measurement methods, the discrepancy of results between the Finnish study and our own may reflect variation in the dominant emission sources, such as residential heating versus road transport (supported by the similar observed NO2 effects in both studies). Moreover, the exposure assignment itself was less spatially refined and, therefore, potentially misclassified in the Finnish study, at 1 × 1 km as compared to ours at 0.1 × 0.1 km. Our study is, therefore, the first to show a statistically significant association of BC with PD incidence. Although the two aforementioned studies also use rigorous PD case definition, ours draws from a nationwide registry, which powers our ability to find statistical significance.

Concerning O3, we saw a weak negative or null association with PD, which corroborates findings from some previous studies. 9 , 33 , 34 Other studies have found a suggestion of a positive association 15 , 32 , 35 with incident PD. The Canadian study reported the strongest effect for O3, with an increased risk of 9% per IQR increase in O3 concentration. 35 Besides a relatively low O3 exposure setting in the current study, discrepancy between studies may be because of the nature of some modelling methods (a LUR model in Denmark and a chemical transport model in Canada) such that O3 results are a mirror image of the NO2 results, which is a strong marker of road traffic and a precursor to O3. In the Canadian study, O3 was moderately positively correlated with PM2.5 and weakly, but also positively correlated with NO2.

In comparison with existing cohort studies of PD, reported PD incidence rates vary widely across countries and study populations, including our observed PD incidence rate of 71.2 per 100,000 person‐years and previous cohort studies (26–298 per 100,000 person‐years) (Supplementary Table S11). However, these incidence estimates are not directly comparable, because they were derived using different population structures and mostly reported as crude rates. Our previous study on long‐term exposure to air pollution and mortality from PD, among the ELAPSE (European) pooled cohort, suggested similar results to the current study. 11 Using the same air pollution exposure models as the current study, we reported positive associations for PM2.5, NO2, and BC with PD mortality (and negative association for O3w). Effect sizes for all pollutants were larger, but so were the CI, which may be explained by the inherent larger heterogeneity between regions and populations included in the trans‐European pooled cohort study compared to the current Danish nationwide cohort.

In our sensitivity analyses, when including NO2 in two‐pollutant models with PM2.5 and BC, estimates were seen to strengthen, suggesting some importance of pollutant source, such as it being from combustion (eg, biomass burning, traffic tailpipe) emissions as seen previously. 11 Two‐pollutant models have been performed in four of the 19 related studies: two found enhanced effect estimates, 34 , 35 while two found attenuated effect estimates 11 , 12 (Supplementary Table S1). Moreover, indirectly adjusting our models for BMI and smoking status did not affect our findings, as with our previous pooled cohort study where we included BMI and smoking status directly in the models. In the DNS, the ancillary population used for the indirect adjustment, we did not observe a statistically significant association between smoking and PD, unlike the inverse association that has previously been suggested. 36 The lack of the expected inverse association may, therefore, partly reflect limitations in smoking data in our ancillary population, but could also be consistent with the reverse causation hypothesis, 36 whereby individuals with prodromal PD are more likely to stop smoking as dopaminergic changes reduce the reinforcing effects of nicotine.

Our findings suggest effect modification for NO2 and BC by age, with older individuals (≥43.9 years old at baseline) only showing an association with PD incidence. The effect of age may be related to early‐onset PD being largely genetically determined, whereas environmental factors including air pollution contribute far more to late‐onset risk of PD, with longer‐term air pollution exposure over the life course, 37 which has been suggested previously in South Korea. 9 Our findings also suggest effect modification by occupational status (for PM2.5), but also household income (for NO2 and BC). In particular, somewhat stronger associations were observed in the pension group in our further analyses with detailed categories of occupational status (Supplementary Table S12). This may reflect greater susceptibility among individuals retiring early or on disability pensions because of comorbidities or early PD symptoms, or that employed individuals have work‐related exposures and, therefore, poorer exposure assessment by home‐address modelling only (compared to unemployed). Perhaps more interesting, is that our effect estimates are higher for NO2 and BC among higher income households, and with that older age, which in Denmark reflects inner‐city residents most affected by road traffic emissions. Finally, in exploratory analysis, we did not find effect modification by any co‐morbidity existing before baseline (stroke, ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, depression, diabetes mellitus), which to our knowledge has not been studied elsewhere.

Only a few studies have examined sex‐specific susceptibility to air pollution in relation to PD incidence, and findings have been mixed. Although some studies reported higher risks in women, 16 , 38 others found no differences 13 , 39 or even lower risks compared with men. 15 Our results suggest a higher risk among women, which may be partly explained by differences in exposure patterns (eg, occupation) 40 as well as physiological factors such as hormonal status, body and lung size, particle deposition, and inflammatory responses, which have been suggested to differ by sex and could modify the impact of PM2.5. 41 , 42 Further sex‐stratified analyses in future studies will help clarify these potential mechanisms.

In terms of study strengths, we used a two‐tier approach in addressing the diagnostic challenge for register‐based PD research, using both primary PD hospital admission and PD medication prescription fulfilment in combination. The issue of PD misdiagnosis, or studies including non‐primary diagnoses of PD, has been discussed recently. 17 , 43 As shown previously in Denmark, 15% of hospitalized PD patients, after rigorous review by medical (movement disorder) specialists, had an invalid diagnosis of idiopathic PD. 13 Although such validation efforts are not practical for national registry cohorts, future research could attempt this for sub‐samples.

Our study limitations include relying on a spatial exposure model for the year 2010, assuming participants did not change address during follow‐up (~1/3 of participants have moved since baseline). This approach was chosen because of the lack of sufficient monitoring stations in earlier years, which made it challenging to construct fine‐scale models for pollutants. A study by Gulliver et al 44 conducted sensitivity analysis in which they developed separate LUR models—one for NO2 in 2000 and another for PM2.5 in 2003. In Denmark, predictions from the 2010 model correlated strongly with the 2000 NO2 model (R 2 > 84%) and moderately with the 2013 PM2.5 model (R 2 = 49%). 22 These results imply that spatial variation in air pollution, particularly for NO2, tends to remain relatively stable over time, which justifies the use of 2010 exposure estimates in our analysis. Furthermore, additional sensitivity analyses that accounted for large‐scale temporal pollution trends and residential mobility still showed positive associations (Tables S7 and S8). The time‐varying approach captured temporal declines in air pollution during follow‐up (Supplementary Figure S3), with which sensitivity analyses provided consistent associations, suggesting robustness of the main findings. Although exposure levels of air pollution were assigned individually based on their residential address using high‐resolution (0.1 × 0.1 km) models, this approach still assume equal exposure levels among residents within the same grid and does not account for individual mobility. However, residential and time–activity‐adjusted exposure estimates have been shown to be highly correlated, resulting in similar health effect estimates. 45 Another limitation is the lack of information on some potential covariates of interest such as behavior‐related factors including BMI and smoking status. We were able to compensate for the lack of these risk factors by introducing an indirect adjustment based on DNS data in sensitivity analyses, and we did not see adjustment of smoking status (nor BMI) change the effect of air pollution on PD (SupplementaryTable S5). However, it is still worth noting that this method is dependent on how representative the ancillary dataset (DNS 2010) is of our main study population. Although the demographic and socioeconomic distribution in DNS 2010 is similar to that of the main cohort (eg, age, sex, region), its participants tend to have higher education and income levels (Supplementary Table S13). As a result, the relationship between lifestyle factors and air pollution in our main study population may differ from that observed in DNS 2010, potentially leading to bias in the indirect adjustment results. Therefore, future studies may need to explore these and other co‐contributing factors. Moreover, the LUR prediction model for different air pollutants inherently produces exposure estimates that are highly correlated using the same or very similar predictive covariates. In addition, although traffic‐related co‐exposure of noise has been suggested as important to consider alongside air pollution as a risk factor for PD development, 12 this area of research requires further study to ascertain an independent action by, or biological mechanism through environmental noise. We lacked detailed occupational data, and therefore, could not adjust for exposures such as farming (pesticides), welding, or metal/chemical industry work (heavy metals), which might be associated with both higher air pollution levels and increased PD risk. 3 , 46 , 47 Residual confounding by occupation, therefore, cannot be ruled out. We also lacked molecular or biomarker data (eg, inflammatory, oxidative stress, or neurodegenerative markers) to directly investigate potential biological mediation pathways between air pollution and PD. Future studies integrating molecular data may help clarify underlying mechanisms. Last, because analyses were based on complete cases, potential bias may arise if the missingness mechanism was not completely at random. However, as the proportion of missing data was minimal (~1%), the potential impact on the study findings is expected to be limited.

For most previous PD mortality studies, PD is more likely the contributing rather than the primary cause of death. 11 In addition, the observed association is at least partly because of the also important and established association between air pollution and cardiopulmonary disease. Besides PD, air pollution (primarily PM2.5) is associated with a range of major cardiorespiratory diseases, diabetes mellitus, lung cancer, dementia, as well as a wide range of previously unassociated diseases. 48 Therefore, work to reach the WHO Air Quality Guidelines, even in countries of low pollutant concentrations such as Denmark, is crucial to alleviate the large public health burden attributed to air pollution.

Conclusion

We observed a positive association between long‐term exposure to air pollution (PM2.5, NO2, and BC) exposure and PD incidence, in a nationwide Danish cohort with unique data on hospital contact and PD medication prescription information. This large study provides robust new evidence in support of air pollution as a risk factor for PD.
