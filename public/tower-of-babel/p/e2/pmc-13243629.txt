Unraveling the genetic architecture of non-Huntington chorea: a biobank-scale study of rare variants and repeat expansions
Authors: Fulya Akçimen, Monica Diez-Fairen, Ignacio Alvarez, Victor Puente, Spencer Grant, Jorge Hernandez-Vara, Marzieh Khani, Mariateresa Buongiorno, Félix Javier Jiménez-Jiménez, José A G Agúndez, Miquel Aguilar, Esther Cubo, Jesus Perez, Javier Pagonabarraga, Núria Caballol, Asuncion Avila, Jinhui Ding, Elena García-Martín, Hortensia Alonso-Navarro, Yaroslau Compta, Carlos Cruchaga, Katrin Beyer, J Raphael Gibbs, Andrew Singleton, Sara Bandres-Ciga, Pau Pastor, Spanish Study Group for Genetics of Chorea members
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChorea can arise from genetic, metabolic, pharmacologic, and autoimmune causes. In clinical practice, however, non-genetic causes are rare. The most common genetic cause is a CAG repeat expansion in HTT, leading to Huntington’s disease (HD). Beyond HD, systematic studies have been lacking and many individuals with non-HD chorea remain without a molecular diagnosis. We conducted whole-exome and genome sequencing analysis on 190 non-HD chorea cases, leveraging data from the All of Us Research Program (n = 134), UK Biobank (n = 26), and a clinically ascertained multicenter Spanish cohort recruited by the Spanish Study Group for Genetics of Chorea (SSGGC) (n = 30). Variant calling was performed without pre-filtering based on a disease or gene list, and variants were clinically contextualized using OMIM, ClinVar, and in silico predictions. We identified thirteen protein-altering variants, including six previously described as pathogenic or likely pathogenic. Notably, we identified a pathogenic JPH3 expansion in a patient of Black race and c9orf72 expansions in individuals of European and South Asian ancestry. These findings explained 23% of cases in the SSGGC, 12% in UK Biobank, and 4% in All of Us. Our results broaden the genetic architecture of non-HD chorea and highlight the value of multi-ancestry genomic approaches for rare movement disorders.Subject terms: Diseases, Genetics, Neurology

Introduction

Chorea is a hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized by involuntary, irregular, purposeless, nonrhythmic, abrupt, and unsustained movements that can flow from one body part to another1. These movements are unpredictable in direction, timing, and distribution. Family history can provide valuable clues to a genetic cause, as a growing number of genes have been implicated in chorea as part of various neurological syndromes.

Patients with chorea can be categorized based on family history, age at onset (AAO), disease course, and topographical distribution. Additional clinical features such as polyneuropathy, ataxia, cutaneous or scleral telangiectasias, liver pathology, or MRI findings2 can help in the diagnosis of chorea. The causes of chorea are heterogeneous and can arise from various etiologies, including structural, genetic, metabolic, pharmacologic, and autoimmune. However, in clinical practice, non-genetic causes are rare, and early-onset or a positive family history often suggests an underlying genetic origin.

The most common hereditary cause of chorea is Huntington’s disease (HD), an autosomal dominant disorder associated with an expansion of 36 or more CAG repeats in the HTT gene3. However, other genes can also lead to phenotypes resembling HD. Among adult-onset choreas, spinocerebellar ataxia 17 (SCA17), dentatorubral–pallidoluysian atrophy (DRPLA), and c9orf72 expansions have been suggested as a relatively frequent cause of non-HD chorea, the latter often presenting with prominent cognitive decline4,5. Another locus associated with late-onset chorea, particularly in African ancestry populations, is a repeat expansion variant in JPH3, which causes Huntington’s disease-like 2 (HDL2), a condition that closely resembles HD in its clinical presentation6. In addition, more than 22 genes have been described in patients exhibiting choreic movements7,8. Protein-altering variants in the GNAO1, NKX2-1, ANO3, ADCY5, ATP8A2, GRIA2, HPCA, SLC20A2, and other genes are associated with early-onset forms of chorea, while variants in genes linked to mitochondrial protein-associated neurodegeneration (MPAN) often present in conjunction with other hyperkinetic movement disorders8,9. Infrequently, chorea can be associated with other SCA loci10,11.

Despite recent advancements, the majority of patients diagnosed with non-HD chorea remain undiagnosed12,13. Although these cases account for a relatively small proportion of patients, establishing a genetic diagnosis is crucial for informed genetic counseling and provides valuable insights into disease trajectory and management. Notably, since the genetic causes of patients with chorea are heterogeneous, no large-scale studies have investigated the genetic landscape of non-HD chorea using advanced sequencing techniques such as whole-exome sequencing (WES) or whole-genome sequencing (WGS). Therefore, this study aims to explore the genetic basis of “orphan” non-HD choreas through a multicenter, multi-ancestry, unbiased biobank-scale approach, leveraging whole-exome sequencing of clinically based patients in the Spanish Study Group for Genetics of Chorea cohort and data from the All of Us Research Program and the UK Biobank14,15.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics of 190 chorea cases are summarized in Table 1. Cohort specific pipelines are summarized in Supplementary Data 1. Of the 30 participants from the SSGGC, 29 were self-reported Caucasian, and one individual was of self-reported Black race. Detailed clinical characteristics of the SSGGC dataset are presented in Supplementary Data 2. Most patients had late-onset chorea (AAO > 50), with 17% reporting a family history of chorea. Chorea was generalized in 80% of the patients, while the remaining individuals exhibited appendicular or facial choreic movements. Thirty percent of the patients had a family history of other neurological conditions, primarily Parkinsonism and or dementia. In addition, 46% of patients presented with other neurological symptoms apart from chorea. Cognitive impairment, predominantly frontal or subcortical, was observed in 24% of patients, while 13% had ataxia, and fewer had dystonia, myoclonus, or tics.

Genotype-phenotype correlation

We identified thirteen rare (MAF < 0.01) protein-altering variants with a minimum CADD score of 12.37 in genes previously implicated in the chorea phenotype. Of these, six were previously classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, three were novel, and four had an uncertain clinical significance (Table 2). Two of these variants were in genes previously described in chorea or choreoathetosis—characterized by a combination of chorea and slower, writhing movements (NKX2-1 and PRRT2), while nine were in genes associated with movement disorders in which chorea could be variably present among the reported clinical features (ADCY5, ANO3, GCDH, GBA1, LRRK2, VPS16, ATP7B, and PUM1). Potential disease-associated protein altering variants in the chorea-associated genes

Age ranges were provided for the carriers in the All of Us dataset to protect participant privacy.

A novel heterozygous p.Ile228Ser variant (14:36517801:A:C, rs1299048749) in NKX2-1 was identified in a female Caucasian case from the SSGGC (Patient 3). Her cranial MRI showed small vessel ischemic lesions. While NKX2-1 variants were typically reported in infancy or early childhood16–18, this individual had a later AAO of 62 years. Consistent with the clinical characteristics of NKX2-1-associated chorea, the patient presented hypothyroidism16,17, dystonia16,19, and gait impairment17. Her son, who had a diagnosis of chorea with obsessive symptoms at the age of 10, was found to be wild-type for this variant. While several variants in NKX2-1 have been associated with chorea or choreoathetosis, the p.Ile228Ser variant has not been previously reported as disease-causing.

We identified a pathogenic frameshift PRRT2 variant, p.Arg217ProfsTer8, (16:29813694:G:GC, rs587778771), associated with paroxysmal choreoathetosis. This variant was previously reported in paroxysmal choreoathetosis and episodic kinesigenic dyskinesia in individuals from diverse populations, including Han Chinese, Japanese, and European origins20,21. The carrier was a female case of European ancestry from the UK Biobank dataset with an AAO of 36.

A heterozygous missense variant, p.Ser223Pro (3:123447879:A:G, rs1374607949), of uncertain clinical significance in ADCY5 was identified in a late-onset female case of African ancestry from the All of Us dataset. Her clinical presentation included dystonia, trigger finger (right ring finger), mononeuritis, Bell’s palsy, cerebellar ataxia, and multiple neurological and systemic symptoms. These included constipation, delirium, disorientation, dysarthria, anarthria, hemiplegia, and hemiparesis following cerebral infarction, sleep disturbances (insomnia and somnolence), major depressive disorder, orthostatic hypotension, oral phase dysphagia, and facial muscle weakness as a sequela of stroke. While previous ADCY5-phenotype associations have primarily been reported in childhood-onset cases22,23, only a limited number of adult- and late-onset cases have been documented24,25.

Two pathogenic heterozygous GCDH variants, p.Arg161Trp (19:12893629:C:T, rs1173575355) and p.Val400Met (19:12897818:G:A, rs121434372), were identified in a male patient from the SSGGC (Patient 10), with an AAO of 6. Homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in GCDH are associated with autosomal recessive glutaric aciduria type 1, a neurometabolic condition frequently presenting with chorea or choreoathetosis26. The presence of two heterozygous pathogenic variants raises the possibility of compound heterozygosity and supports GCDH-related etiology in this early-onset case. The patient also exhibited partial epileptic seizures in childhood (with more than 20 seizure-free years), as well as mild psychomotor delay, mild myoclonic movements, imbalance, and clumsiness in sports.

We also identified known variants in GBA1 and LRRK2, as well as a novel variant in ANO3, which are associated with Parkinson’s disease (PD), Parkinsonism, and dystonia, respectively, conditions in which chorea is frequently reported27–29. The GBA1 missense p.Asp448His variant (1:155235727:C:G, rs1064651) was detected in a male case from the SSGGC (Patient 17), with an AAO of 76 and a disease duration of nine years. The p.Asp448His variant is classified as severe in the GBA1-PD Browser30. The patient presented with slower progressive chorea accompanied by mild subcortical dementia and gait ataxia. In addition, the missense p.Arg1325Gln variant (2:40308481:G:A, rs72546338) in the LRRK2 gene was identified in a female case of African ancestry from the All of Us dataset, who developed symptoms in her seventies. Given that neither individual exhibited Parkinsonism, we cannot exclude the possibility that these findings are incidental rather than directly related to the chorea phenotype. Additionally, we identified a novel heterozygous ANO3 variant (11:26639241:C:T, p.Pro714Leu) in a female patient exhibiting abnormal neuroimaging findings indicative of excessive iron deposition. Brain MRI revealed signal alterations in gradient and iron sequences affecting the basal ganglia, substantia nigra, red nucleus, and cerebellar dentate nuclei, suggesting iron deposition beyond what is expected for her age (Fig. 1), resembling Neurodegeneration with Brain Iron Accumulation (NBIA)-like features31. ANO3 variants have been previously associated with early-onset dystonia and chorea32–34, with only two reported cases of ANO3 pathogenic variants linked to late-onset chorea23,35. To our knowledge, this is the first description of an ANO3 variant associated with distinctive neuroimaging abnormalities.

Study workflow.

We leveraged short-read whole-exome and genome sequencing analysis on 30 patients from a Spanish multicenter, clinically ascertained cohort, 134 non-HD chorea patients from the All of Us Research Program dataset, and 26 non-HD chorea patients from the UK Biobank. We focused on finding rare protein-altering variants and short tandem repeat expansions.

Two heterozygous missense VPS16 variants (p.Ala206Val, 20:2860850:C:T, rs201833647 and p.Arg474His, 20:2863343:G:A, rs141226767) were identified in two early-onset female individuals from the UK Biobank dataset, with AAOs of 10 and 3 years, respectively. Notably, the individual with the p.Ala206Val variant was also diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease in her late 70 s, suggesting that this is not a neurodegenerative trajectory but probably a neurodevelopmental defect. VPS16 variants were linked to only early-onset isolated dystonia36,37 until recently, Monfrini et al. reported that carriers can exhibit additional clinical features such as choreoathetosis and myoclonus38. Consistent with this, both individuals in our study exhibited early-onset chorea, providing further evidence of a broadened phenotypic spectrum linked to VPS16.

Additional variants in other neurodegenerative disease-associated genes were identified in late-onset cases with atypical presentations. A homozygous pathogenic ATP7B variant, p.Met645Arg (13:51961849:A:C, rs121907998) was identified in a late-onset female case from the SSGGC dataset. She developed symptoms at age 81, presenting with subcortical dementia, gait disorder, falls, and ataxia, with a slow disease progression. MRI findings revealed mild pallidal calcifications. No other potential compound heterozygous variants were identified in ATP7B. This variant, in both heterozygous and homozygous states, has been previously reported as a common cause of Wilson’s disease, is characterized by hepatic dysfunction and neurological signs, including chorea, particularly in East Asian, Middle Eastern-South Asian, and South American populations39. Interestingly, homozygous carriers were reported to exhibit less severe clinical manifestations, and a significant proportion of homozygous carriers were diagnosed after age 40 compared to heterozygous carriers (83% vs. 18%)40, aligning with the mild and slowly progressive disease course observed in Patient 1.

Two PUM1 variants of uncertain clinical significance were identified: p.Ser247Arg (1:30995202:T:G, rs1293403314) in a Caucasian female presenting with cognitive impairment from the SSGGC dataset and p.Ile727Thr (1:30964817:A:G, rs750056686) in an individual of American ancestry from the All of Us dataset. Both patients exhibited a late AAO.

Disease-associated repeat expansions implicated in related neurological diseases that could explain the chorea phenotype were ruled out in most patients. HTT (HD) and TBP (SCA17) expansions were excluded among all the participants from the SSGGC and All of Us cohorts. Of note, no fully penetrant pathogenic ([CAG/CAA]n > 48) or reduced penetrance (48 > [CAG/CAA]n > 41) TBP repeat expansions were detected in either cohort.

The c9orf72-GGGGCC repeat expansion was ruled out in 80% of the SSGGC patients (n = 30), while the ATN1-CAG repeat expansion, which causes dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy, was excluded in eleven patients (37%). Additionally, some of the common hereditary spinocerebellar ataxia repeat expansions, including ATXN1, ATXN2, ATXN3, ATXN7, and PPP2R2B loci, were ruled out in seven patients (23%). For a detailed list of the candidate short-tandem repeats, please refer to Supplementary Data 2.

Further in silico analysis identified a heterozygous repeat expansion (CAGn=43) in the JPH3 gene in a male of Black or African race from the SSGGC cohort with a familial history of chorea. He developed symptoms at age 45, presenting with dementia, moderate subcortical dementia, and polyneuropathy. Additionally, c9orf72 repeat expansions were found in two individuals from the All of Us dataset. One of the individuals was a male of South Asian genetic ancestry with a family history of dystonia and AAO in his 20 s. His EHRs documented torsion dystonia, lack of coordination, and sleep disturbances. The second carrier was a female individual of European ancestry with a late AAO (Table 3). The ExpansionHunter software estimated the hexanucleotide repeat expansion lengths in c9orf72 as 170 and 451 repeat units, respectively. As this estimation was based on a short-read sequencing approach, the actual repeat lengths are likely to be longer, and precise sizing requires confirmation through Southern blotting. Visualization of the reads carrying wild-type and expanded repeats is presented in Supplementary Figs. 1–3.

Discussion

We investigated the genetic basis of “orphan” non-HD chorea by using WES and WGS data from the multicentric SSGGC initiative, the All of Us Research Program, and the UK Biobank. Our analysis identified 13 protein-altering variants and three short tandem repeat expansions; two c9orf72 and one JPH3. Together, these accounted for 23% of cases in the SSGGC cohort, 4% in the All of Us cohort, and 12% in the UK Biobank cohort. This indicates a higher diagnostic yield in the clinically ascertained SSGGC cohort than in the biobank cohorts. Our findings suggest novel genotype-phenotype associations. Among these, a heterozygous missense variant in PUM1 (p.Ile727Thr) was identified in one patient of American ancestry. In gnomAD v4.1, the allele frequency of this variant was highest in the Admixed American population (1.33 × 10−4), compared with Europeans (1.70 × 10−6) and the global population (9.91 × 10−6). In the All of Us dataset, the allele frequency was 2.58 × 10−4 in individuals of Latin American ancestry and 2.0 × 10−6 in Europeans. This finding raises the possibility that p.Ile727Thr is enriched in specific populations. It also highlights the importance of ancestry-aware interpretation in non-HD chorea. Taken together, these results expand the genetic spectrum of non-HD chorea and highlight the importance of diverse, multi-ancestry cohorts for interpreting rare variation.

Two individuals in the SSGGC cohort who were carrying HTT alleles in the intermediate CAG repeat size range (17/24 and 23/27, both at the lower end of the intermediate spectrum), were also carriers of ANO3 and GBA1 rare variants. Recent studies suggest that intermediate alleles may increase the risk of several neurodegenerative disorders41, but more data are needed to determine whether intermediate alleles exert measurable biological effects. Given the very small number of carriers and the heterogeneous genetic backgrounds of these cases, we did not attempt to conclude clinical contribution or potential modifier effects.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. One key limitation is the reliance on an in silico method to detect potential repeat expansions in cases where polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was not performed. As a result, some potentially impactful variants, including other structural deletions or insertions, may have been overlooked. Additionally, the use of different data sources (WES vs. WGS) and varying quality control pipelines across cohorts may introduce technical variability, limiting the consistency and comparability of variant detection. In silico prediction methods are also prone to false positives, particularly in repetitive or low-complexity regions, underscoring the need for orthogonal validation such as PCR-based assays. Future studies incorporating long-read sequencing could offer a more comprehensive assessment of repeat expansions and other structural variants, thereby enhancing our understanding of the genetic underpinnings of chorea.

Another important limitation relates to differences in phenotyping and population assignment across cohorts. The SSGGC cohort was clinically ascertained with a detailed neurological evaluation, whereas case identification in the All of Us and UK Biobank cohorts relied on EHR-derived diagnostic codes without specialist confirmation. This imbalance limits the depth of phenotype-genotype correlations and may affect the overall cohesiveness of the study. Differences in age at onset and disease duration observed across biobank cohorts likely reflect heterogeneity in EHR depth and ascertainment, which may limit cross-cohort comparisons for these phenotypes. In addition, genetic ancestry information was not uniformly available across datasets. SSGGC participants only had self-reported race data, whereas ancestry inference in the All of Us and UK Biobank cohorts was performed using different tools and reference panels, further complicating cross-cohort comparisons and potentially introducing variability in ancestry assignments. This discrepancy in population classification methods reduces the ability to compare findings across cohorts directly. Another relevant consideration is the potential presence of other causes of chorea. Although protein-altering genetic etiologies were uniformly assessed in the SSGGC cohort via WES, screening for short tandem repeat expansions was not fully comprehensive across loci. For the biobank-derived cohorts, only short-read WGS data were available, and DNA could not be accessed for orthogonal PCR confirmation. While PCR or long-read sequencing remain the gold standard, recent work has shown that short-read WGS can detect clinically relevant repeat expansions with high sensitivity and specificity, supporting its use when PCR confirmation is not feasible42. Long-read sequencing is now being deployed in both biobanks and will enable comprehensive validation and refinement of diagnostic yield in future studies.

Additionally, very rare non-genetic causes of chorea, such as autoimmune or paraneoplastic disorders, that are most often associated with specific autoantibodies like IgLON5 and CV2/CRMP5, may have been overlooked. However, the clinical course of the SSGGC patients included in this study did not align with the typical presentation of such autoimmune or paraneoplastic causes of chorea. Notably, in one family, a heterozygous NKX2-1 p.Ile228Ser variant was identified in a woman with adult-onset chorea accompanied by hypothyroidism, dystonia, and gait impairment, a constellation strongly consistent with NKX2-1-related chorea. Her son, who had childhood-onset tic-like hyperkinesia with obsessive symptoms, tested wild-type for this variant. Rather than indicating a lack of genotype–phenotype concordance, this scenario likely reflects both the sporadic occurrence of NKX2-1 mutations and the phenotypic and nosological heterogeneity inherent to pediatric hyperkinetic presentations. Childhood tics and obsessive behaviors are not typical features of NKX2-1-associated chorea, making a phenocopy explanation more plausible than reduced penetrance in this case. Furthermore, most of the candidate variants identified in our study are singletons, observed in only one individual, warranting caution in interpreting their pathogenicity. Among these, only the ATP7B variant has been functionally characterized43, whereas the effects of the others remain unclear. Future studies leveraging larger datasets or functional assays will be crucial to validate these findings and clarify their potential role in disease.

Overall, ongoing collaborations between researchers and clinicians will be essential to translating the genetic insights from our study into meaningful clinical applications. Despite the limitations, our work provides valuable contributions to understanding the genetic basis of chorea and highlights the need for further research in diverse populations. By incorporating larger datasets and utilizing advanced sequencing technologies, future studies will be able to refine our findings, enhance the accuracy of genetic diagnoses, and expand the genetic landscape of chorea. These efforts will enhance the precision of genetic diagnoses, ultimately paving the way for more effective and personalized treatments for patients with chorea and related disorders.

Methods

Participants and study design

This study includes 190 chorea cases, sourced from the Spanish Study Group for Genetics of Chorea (SSGGC), the All of Us Research Program, and the UK Biobank. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the cohorts included in the study. Figure 2 shows a summary of our workflow, which we explain in further detail below.

MRI axial T2-weighted images from the ANO3 p.Pro714Leu variant carrier show extensive iron deposit at the substantia nigra (A) and basal ganglia (B), which is also reflected in the axial FLAIR sequence (C).

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The SSGGC is an initiative dedicated to investigating the genetic causes of non-HD choreas in Spain. Researchers in the SSGGC were invited to collect DNA samples from patients meeting the clinical criteria for chorea. Written informed consent was obtained from all SSGGC participants, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Mútua de Terrassa (project name: EXHONUCO, project ID: ID EO/1882). The origin of candidates was individuals with undiagnosed chorea syndromes referred to the specialized Movement Disorder clinics within the SSGGC.

The selection criteria for non-HD chorea patients included in this study were as follows: (1) patients with involuntary hyperkinetic movements characterized by purposeless, spontaneous, and irregular motions that may flow from one body part to another, ranging in severity from mild restlessness to a continuous stream of disabling, violent movements; chorea was distinguished from other hyperkinetic movement disorders according to established clinical guidelines44; (2) patients with either focal, axial, and/or appendicular chorea. Although additional neurological features such as cognitive decline, Parkinsonism, ataxia, polyneuropathy, and other movement disorders can coexist, chorea was identified as the prominent neurological sign from the disease onset.

Exclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) based on clinical, laboratory, and neuroimaging data, cases are attributable to the following causes of chorea were excluded: autoimmune and paraneoplastic choreiform syndromes (Sydenham’s chorea, systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, chorea gravidarum, Behçet’s disease); infectious causes; structural lesions of the basal ganglia (ischemic or hemorrhagic infarcts, neoplasms, abscesses, demyelinating lesions, central pontine/extrapontine myelinolysis, cavernoma); metabolic, endocrine, and toxic causes (nonketotic hyperglycemia in diabetes mellitus, hypoglycemia, hypo/hypernatremia, hypocalcemia, hypoparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, liver failure, including chronic hepatocerebral degeneration, kidney failure, carbon monoxide, manganese, mercury, thallium, organophosphates, vitamin B12 deficiency, Wernicke’s encephalopathy); drug-induced chorea (mainly dopamine receptor antagonists and L-dopa, anticholinergics, antiepileptic drugs, calcium channel blockers, lithium, tricyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, anti-malarial drugs, steroids, oral contraceptives, antihistamines, psychostimulants [methylphenidate, amphetamines, pemoline, cocaine], baclofen, digoxin, cyclosporine, theophylline, buphrenorphine, hydromorphone and other opiates, ceftriaxone, memantine); and (2) patients with HTT-CAG repeat expansion (CAGn) equal or > 36.

The All of Us cohort comprises participants recruited from across the United States as part of a nationwide precision medicine initiative. In the All of Us dataset, individuals with documented chorea (SNOMEDCode: 271700006) in their electronic health records (EHRs) and for whom WGS data were available were included in the case cohort. Individuals with an HD diagnosis in their EHRs were not included45. The All of Us Institutional Review Board (IRB) reviews the protocol, informed consent, and other materials for All of Us participants. In the UK Biobank dataset, individuals with chorea were defined by the UK Biobank field ID 131274, accessed in April 2025, excluding individuals with an HD diagnosis.

Genetically inferred ancestries for All of Us participants with WGS data in release 8 were provided as part of the data resources45. In the UK Biobank, genetic ancestry was determined using a custom ancestry prediction pipeline implemented in the GenoTools package (https://github.com/dvitale199/GenoTools)46. For SSGGC participants, genetic ancestry estimation was not feasible; therefore, only self-reported race information was available.

Genotyping of disease-associated repeat expansions

In the SSGGC cohort, the lengths of disease-associated repeat expansions in HTT, spinocerebellar ataxia genes (ATXN1, ATXN2, ATXN3, ATXN7, PPP2R2B, TBP), and ATN1 were analyzed using PCR-based Sanger sequencing or fragment analysis. The presence of the c9orf72 expansion was also examined as follows: DNA was extracted by standard procedures using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Repeat-primed PCR was carried out as described by Renton and colleagues47. Briefly, for each sample, 150 ng of genomic DNA was used in 15 ml PCR reactions containing 7.5 ml of FastStart PCR Master Mix (Roche Applied Science, Penzberg, Germany) and 0.18 mM 7-deaza-dGTP (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), 13% Q-Solution (QIAGEN), 7% DMSO (Merck Millipore, Burlington, Ma, USA), 0.9 mM MgCl2 (QIAGEN), 0.7 mM reverse primer, 1.4 mM 6FAM-fluorescent labeled forward primer, and 1.4 mM anchor primer. Amplification reactions were performed using a touchdown cycling program as described previously47. PCR products were analyzed on a SeqStudio genetic analyzer, and the results were obtained with the GeneMapper software48.

In the SSGGC, the All of Us and UK Biobank datasets, further in silico analysis of disease-associated repeat expansions was conducted using ExpansionHunter v5 and its published variant catalog file, which includes the respective genomic regions49,50. The REViewer package v0.2.751 was used to visualize the expanded motifs.

Sequencing procedures

We performed WES on individuals from the SSGGC cohort using the SIDT xGen Exome Whole Exome Research Panel v1.0 with the custom Spike-In baits kit. Samples were sequenced on a NovaSeq6000 with paired-end reads, with a mean depth of coverage of 50x. Alignment and variant calling were performed using the Broad Institute’s functional equivalence pipeline, which follows the Genome Analysis Toolkit Best Practices52.

WGS on individuals from the All of Us dataset was performed by the genome centers funded by the All of Us Research Program14,53. All genome centers followed the same sequencing protocols, which involved PCR-free 150 bp paired-end libraries sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequencing data were processed using DRAGEN v3.4.12 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), with alignment performed to the GRCh38 reference genome54. Individuals with a mean coverage of less than 30x were excluded from further analysis. Further details on quality control can be found at https://support.researchallofus.org/hc/en-us/articles/29390274413716-All-of-Us-Genomic-Quality-Report. Genetic ancestry was estimated by projecting principal components derived from high-quality variants onto reference panels from the HGDP and 1000 Genomes Project as previously described45 (Supplementary Data 1).

WGS on individuals from the UK Biobank was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, with a mean depth of coverage of 34x, and analyzed using DRAGEN v.3.7.8 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Alignment was performed against the GRCh38 reference genome. Further details on quality control can be found at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=187. Genetic ancestry was determined using a custom ancestry prediction pipeline implemented in the GenoTools package46.

Assessment of relatedness

Kinship coefficients within the SSGGC and UK Biobank were estimated using the KING software (v2.2.8) to identify potentially related individuals, following the guidelines provided in the KING manual (available at https://www.kingrelatedness.com/manual.shtml). In the All of Us dataset, relatedness had been previously assessed using the Hail pc-relate function45.

Annotation and variant prioritization

Functional annotation of the identified variants was done using ANNOVAR55, incorporating relevant datasets such as Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner (REVEL)56, AlphaMissense57, PrimateAI58, and Mendelian Clinically Applicable Pathogenicity (M-CAP)59 and the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) v4.160. The clinical significance of candidate variants was cross-checked on the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/). Protein-altering or splicing variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 0.01 in the gnomAD and a minimum Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) PHRED-scaled score of 12.37. This PHRED-scaled score is predicted to be pathogenic by Amendola et al.61 and falls within the top 5–6% most deleterious variants in the genome, according to Kircher et al.62. Importantly, variant interpretation was not based on phenotype-based pre-filtering. Instead, all retained variants were mapped to OMIM gene–disease associations (OMIM genemap2, downloaded September 2024), and associated disease phenotypes were subsequently reviewed through PubMed to determine whether chorea or related hyperkinetic movement disorders have been reported. This two-step approach allowed for unbiased variant inclusion followed by clinical contextualization, rather than restricting the analysis to a predefined list of chorea-related genes.

The OMIM gene2map2 file, obtained from omim.org in September 2024, was used for genotype-phenotype correlations. We specifically reviewed variants in genes with an OMIM association by cross-referencing their associated phenotypes in PubMed to identify any potential links to chorea or dystonia. Our search was based on the list of all remaining variants in genes that may show neurological manifestations.
