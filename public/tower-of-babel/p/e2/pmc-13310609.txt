Grade III zone 9 blunt aortic injury in an 8-year-old boy: Novel intervention and case review
Authors: Mario Matabele, Connor Grayzel, Doran Mix, Derek Wakeman, Michael Stoner, Mohammad Rachad Wehbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blunt aortic injury is rare in the pediatric population and usually results from high-energy trauma such as motor vehicle collisions. We report an 8-year-old boy with polytrauma who sustained a grade III infrarenal blunt aortic injury after a motor vehicle collision and underwent endovascular aortic repair. Pediatric endovascular management presents unique challenges, including small vessel size, future somatic aortic growth, and uncertainty regarding long-term stent durability. Data describing endovascular repair of infrarenal blunt aortic injury in children remain limited. This case highlights important technical considerations and supports endovascular repair as a viable alternative to open repair in select pediatric patients.

Pediatric aortic injuries are rare, typically result from blunt trauma, and carry high morbidity and mortality.1 Historically, open surgical repair or nonoperative management were the predominant treatments, but endovascular approaches have gained increasing use.2 Endovascular repair for thoracic aortic injury has been associated with lower mortality compared with open or nonoperative management in adults, although data for pediatric abdominal aortic injury remain limited.2,3 One report described successful endovascular repair of abdominal aortic trauma in a pediatric patient, highlighting challenges related to small vessel size and limited guidelines.4 This case report adds to the limited literature supporting endovascular aortic repair in children.

Case report

An otherwise healthy 8-year-old boy presented as a level I trauma activation after a high-speed motor vehicle collision. He was a restrained rear-seat passenger found unresponsive at the scene. On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable but obtunded (Glasgow Coma Scale score, 7) and intubated. Secondary survey revealed a seatbelt sign and absent pedal pulses bilaterally. Computed tomography (CT) scans demonstrated C2-C3 distraction injury, T1 cord rupture with consequent paraplegia, grade III infrarenal aortic injury, traumatic abdominal wall hernia, and free intraperitoneal fluid (Fig 1, A and B). Pediatric surgery performed a negative laparotomy, plastic surgery reconstructed the abdominal wall, and vascular surgery repaired the infrarenal aortic injury. Preincision assessment confirmed absent dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial signals with preserved femoral signals bilaterally. The right common femoral artery was exposed through a longitudinal groin incision and accessed with a micropuncture technique. An 8F sheath was placed over a 0.035-inch wire and heparin administered; the sheath size was chosen to accommodate intravascular ultrasound examination if needed.

Completion angiography demonstrated exclusion of the injury without extravasation and preserved flow through the renal and iliac arteries (Fig 2, B). Pedal signals remained triphasic postoperatively and rectal aspirin was initiated on postoperative day 1. CT angiography on postoperative day 2 demonstrated a widely patent stent without endoleak. The proximal inferior mesenteric artery was covered but distal perfusion was maintained via collateral flow. Surveillance imaging with ultrasound examination and a CT scan is planned at 1 year. Written informed consent for publication of this case report and associated images was obtained from the patient's parent/legal guardian.

Discussion

Zone 9 blunt aortic injury is exceedingly rare in children, and most commonly results from blunt abdominal trauma during a motor vehicle collision.1,5,6 In properly restrained children, seatbelts distribute force over the anterior superior iliac spines.7 A more cranially positioned belt can compress the lower abdomen during rapid deceleration, producing the triad of hollow-viscus, aortoiliac, and spinal injuries known as seatbelt syndrome.

The initial management of zone 9 blunt aortic injury requires rapid assessment, stabilization, and determination of operative need based on hemodynamic status. Our patient was hemodynamically stable and underwent CT angiography,8 which delineated the aortoiliac anatomy, identified major vessel involvement, and evaluated for end-organ ischemia. Although open repair has traditionally been standard in pediatric patients, endovascular repair is feasible in select cases. In this patient, hollow viscus perforation and possible bladder injury increased the risk of graft infection with open repair. Femoral artery cutdown was selected rather than percutaneous access because of the small vessel caliber and the risk of arterial vasospasm in pediatric patients.

Pediatric endovascular repair remains limited by device availability and uncertain long-term durability. The pediatric aorta undergoes substantial growth, with the mean abdominal aortic diameter nearly doubling from approximately 8.8 mm in early childhood to 16.5 mm during adolescence9 and increasing by roughly 0.1 to 0.3 mm annually thereafter.10 This patient had an abdominal aorta measuring 7 mm.

Endovascular stent placement in pediatric patients presents challenges in long-term management, and limited literature guides postoperative surveillance and reintervention. Evidence from thoracic aortic trauma supports the expanding role of endovascular therapy in younger patients. A study of nearly 1700 patients aged <20 years found that 18% underwent thoracic endovascular aortic repair for blunt thoracic aortic injury; <2% underwent open repair. Thoracic endovascular aortic repair was associated with lower mortality despite increased risks of stroke and acute respiratory distress syndrome.11 Grubbs et al12 similarly reported lower mortality and higher rates of discharge to home among patients undergoing endovascular repair after thoracic aortic trauma.

Well-powered pediatric blunt abdominal aortic injury outcome studies are lacking, with evidence largely limited to case reports. Papazoglou et al13 reported a 9-year-old with infrarenal blunt aortic injury after a motor vehicle collision who underwent endovascular placement of two self-expanding stents following an initial attempt at conservative management. Another describes an 8-year-old who developed an infrarenal aortic pseudoaneurysm and underwent emergent endovascular aortic repair with an 11 mm × 5 cm VIABAHN (W. L. Gore & Associates) covered stent as damage control.14 Together with the present case, these reports demonstrate successful endovascular management of traumatic infrarenal aortic injury in children.

The Gore VBX stent (W. L. Gore & Associates) received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration in 201715 and consists of closed-cell stainless-steel rings connected primarily through expanded polytetrafluoroethylene covering, rather than metallic interconnectors.16 Several architectural features support its use in the pediatric aorta.

The single connecting strut between diamond-shaped cells allows a predictable fracture pattern that occurs more uniformly than open-cell platforms such as the LifeStream (Becton Dickinson) and iCast (Cook Medical) systems. Bench testing by Mejia et al17 demonstrated that the Gore VBX stent tolerated dilation to 20 to 22 mm before fabric failure. Fracture occurred primarily along the connecting strut, suggesting that progressive dilation may produce controlled linear fracture rather than irregular deformation. Similarly, in children with congenital heart disease, serial high-pressure dilation fractured pulmonary artery stents synchronously.18 These findings suggest that the predictable fracture characteristics of the VBX platform may allow controlled serial dilation in growing patients. The radial recoil characteristics of the VBX platform are also favorable for managing blunt aortic injury where immediate luminal stability is essential. Mejia et al17 reported recoil of <3.4% in nominal and overdilated configurations compared with approximately 8.5% recoil for the iCast platform.

Progressive dilation may lead to stent shortening (napkin ring phenomenon). Mejia et al17 and Blais et al16 reported shortening of ≤38% with serial dilation and approximately 9% per millimeter of overdilation. In this case, the injury involved a short infrarenal segment with adequate landing zones, making controlled shortening less clinically significant. Preservation of expanded polytetrafluoroethylene lining integrity is also essential for graft stability. Blais et al16 demonstrated that disruption of the stent covering typically occurs during or after stent fracture, emphasizing the importance of maintaining graft integrity to ensure exclusion of the injured segment.

This case highlights the technical challenges of endovascular repair in pediatric vessels, particularly graft sizing in growing arteries. Emerging devices capable of gradual expansion as the vessel enlarges may help to address these limitations. Our management plan includes annual surveillance with abdominal ultrasound examinations, ankle-brachial indices, and/or CT angiography to monitor dilation of the zone 9 8L VBX stent as the proximal aorta enlarges. When these segments approach approximately 16 mm—likely between ages 13 and 17 years—we will avoid further dilation and plan for stent explantation with in-line abdominal aortic reconstruction using a Dacron graft.

Conclusions

This case highlights the feasibility, technical challenges, and long-term considerations of endovascular repair of aortic injury following blunt abdominal trauma with the 8L VBX platform.
