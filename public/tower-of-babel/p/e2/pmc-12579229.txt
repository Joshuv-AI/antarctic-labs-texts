Induction of salinity tolerance in maize (Zea Mays L.) by exogenous application of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid
Authors: Hira Fatima, Ameer Khan, Muhammad Nadeem, Ahlam Khalofah, Toqeer Abbas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present research study was conducted at the Department of Botany, University of Sargodha, Pakistan to evaluate the performance of two maize varieties under various salinity levels and mitigate the drastic impacts of salinity under the influence of combined and single foliar applications of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid. Ten seeds of each variety were placed in a pot of each replicate of every treatment. Seeds of two maize varieties were collected from the research institute “Maize and Millet Institute Yousafwala, Sahiwal”. Various attributes of maize varieties, including morphology, physiology, biochemical and nutritional properties, at both the vegetative and reproductive growth stages were evaluated under salt stress and with applications of growth promoters, i.e., ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid. The Sahiwal-2002 performed better than the Akbar in terms of all the parameters under consideration. These findings confirmed that the Sahiwal-2002 has greater salt tolerance potential than Akbar does. Salinity stress drastically reduced the growth and yield of both maize varieties by decreasing their physiological performance and biochemical contents. Therefore, various applications of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid have been applied as foliar sprays to mitigate the negative effects of salinity on maize varieties at the vegetative and reproductive growth stages. Among all the treatments, at the vegetative stage, foliar application of a combination of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid (120 ppm and 110 ppm) ameliorated the negative effects of salinity stress on maize crops and can be used to increase the vegetative growth and plant density of maize varieties with improved and stabilized nutritional contents. The sodium level increased with increasing salinity. At the reproductive stage, foliar application of a combination of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid (240 ppm and 220 ppm) ameliorates the negative effects of salinity stress on maize crops and can be used to increase the growth and yield of maize varieties with improved and stabilized nutritional contents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22174-0.Keywords: Maize, Morphology, Ascorbic acid, Salt stress, Gibberellic acid, ProlineSubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Maize is a crucial grain crop for humans worldwide, but its yield is influenced by a variety of ecological stresses1. Corn production is sensitive to abiotic issues, dehydration, heat, salt concentration, aqueous soil, wind, and cold on a large scale2. Maize responds differently to salinity according to its growth stage and period of salt exposure3,4. Salinity reduces development and lowers the yield of corn crops5. Salinity drastically damages the membranes of various photosynthetic and accessory pigments, including carotenoids, chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b6. Both sodium and chloride are the main ions involved in diverse physiological processes7.

Salinization poses a significant environmental challenge in today’s era. Soils with an electrical conductivity exceeding 4 dS m−1 (equivalent to 40 mM sodium chloride) at 25 °C in the root zone, and 15% exchangeable sodium content, are classified as saline soils. The excessive accumulation of salts in these soils constitutes a major abiotic stress factor worldwide, severely impacting agricultural productivity and ecosystem health8. Under saline conditions, physiological and biochemical processes in plants undergo significant alterations, ultimately leading to reduced plant growth and productivity9. Saline conditions disrupt physiological responses in plants, altering the ion balance, stomatal behavior, and photosynthetic capacity, ultimately leading to decreased plant growth10. Ionic stress and osmotic stress can occur in plants because of the salinity of the soil. In the first phase plants suffer from osmotic stress which results in physiological drought. In the second phase, sodium and chloride ions enter the plant body and disturb cell organelles11.

Salt stress decreased shoot and root lengths, fresh and dry weights, chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, and antioxidant enzyme activity, while increased oxidative damage and Na+ ion accumulation in maize plants. Nonetheless, GA3 treatment boosted maize growth, decreased oxidative stress, and increased antioxidant enzyme activity and K+ ion concentrations during salt stress12. Exogenous applications of plant growth regulators and antioxidants have emerged as promising approaches to mitigate the adverse effects of salinity stress on crops. Several scientists have used a variety of strategies to lessen the negative effects of salinity on plants. Various compounds, such as ascorbic acid (AsA), salicylic acid (SA), and trehalose, are used for resistance in plants during stress circumstances13.

The harmful impacts of reactive oxygen species on vegetation development can be alleviated by various modifications, such as germination-promoting ions, amino groups, and antioxidant chemicals14. Exogenous application of osmoprotectants and antioxidants such as ascorbic acid is one of the most effective methods for mitigating the effects of salinity. Foliar application of ascorbic acid (AsA) can efficiently control antioxidative metabolism in crops. The external application of AsA can be accomplished by sprinkling, pre-treatment of seeds and application of rooting medium15. GA3 also has the potential to increase the performance of crops under normal conditions. Foliarly sprayed GA3 also lowers the intensity of Na+, so in this study, the performance of ascorbic acid and gibbererelic acid was evaluated in maize plants with and without salt stress as well as with and without different foliar sprays16.

Total soluble proteins are the most metabolically active proteins known in plants, whereas sodium toxicity reduces protein content. The reduction in the amount of total soluble proteins may be due to a decrease in the amount of protein synthesized or an increase in protein hydrolysis under salinity stress17. Ionic imbalance occurs in plants facing salt stress; thus to maintain this ionic imbalance plants try to synthesize these proteins18. A decrease in protein concentration enhances the production of total free amino acids because of the breakdown of proteins into amino acids under stress conditions19.

Soil salinization is a major threat to global food security, particularly key crops like maize. The goal of this research was to determine the efficiency of individual and combined foliar sprays of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid in reducing the deleterious effects of salt stress on maize during the vegetative and reproductive stages. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the individual and combined effects of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid on maize growth, physiological processes, and nutritional quality under saline conditions at the vegetative and reproductive stages, as well as to determine the most effective treatment for alleviating salinity stress in maize, resulting in increased growth, yield, and nutritional quality. By exploring this novel approach, this research aims to contribute to the development of strategies to improve maize productivity and resilience under saline conditions.

Materials and methods

Seed collection and experimental design

Seeds of two maize varieties, Sahiwal-2002 (tolerant) and Akbar (sensitive), were acquired from the Maize and Millets Research Institute in Yusafwala, Sahiwal. The experiment was performed in plastic pots with two NaCl salt concentrations, i.e., 55 and 110 mM (Fig. 1A). After 2 weeks of seed germination (Fig. 1B), foliar spraying of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid in separate and combined forms was performed on the plants. The concentration of ascorbic acid was maintained at 120 and 240 ppm, and the concentration of gibberellic acid was maintained at 110 and 220 ppm. A combination of these two foliar sprays was also used to determine its effects on maize.

(A–D) Effect of foliar applications of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid on Maize (Zea mays L.), plastic pots (A), after 2 weeks of seed germination (B), Akbar (left), Sahiwal-2002 (right) (C), vegetative stage (D), of maize varieties at vegetative and reproductive stage under saline and non-saline conditions.

Sowing soil and replicates

Pots were filled with 20 kg of clay loam soil, and seeds of both maize varieties (Akbar and Sahiwal-2002) were sown in plastic pots under various treatments (0, 55 and 110 mM) of sodium chloride. Pots were arranged in a CRD design in all replicates of each treatment for both varieties. After seedling emergence, various concentrations of ascorbic acid (0, 120 and 240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (0, 110 and 220 ppm) were sprayed on the foliage of the seedlings separately and in combination to evaluate their effects on various attributes of the maize crop (Fig. 1A–D).

Growth parameters

The measurements were conducted to assess plant development throughout both the vegetative and mature stages. A measuring tape was used to measure the plant’s length in centimeters from the base to the top. Fresh weight was determined in grams using an electronic balance or weighing scale after the plants were thoroughly uprooted and washed. The dry weight of the plants was measured in grams after they had been oven dried at 70 °C until totally dry. The mean values for each parameter were derived by averaging the measurements from all of the plants in each treatment, resulting in a thorough evaluation of plant growth and development under various conditions.

Photosynthetic pigment analysis

A spectrophotometer was used to measure chlorophyll a, b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid (Arnon’s technique20. Fresh leaves weighing 0.2 g were chopped and preserved in 80% acetone at 4 °C for 24 hours under darkness. The extract was then spun at 1,000,000 rpm for 5 mins using a centrifuge machine. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 663, 645, and 480 nm with a spectrophotometer (Hitachi-220, Japan).

Ascorbic acid (AsA)

The method described by Mukherjee and Choudhuri21 was used to determine ascorbic acid. 0.25 g of leaf sample was crushed and mixed with 5 mL of 6% trichloroacetic acid. Each sample was given 6 mL of a solution containing 4 mL of extract and 2 mL of a 2% acidic dinitrophenyl hydrazine solution (2 mL), followed by a drop of thiourea solution in 70% ethanol before being heated in a water bath. After 15 mins, cool the reaction liquid to room temperature before adding 5 mL of 80% diluted H2SO4 to halt the reaction. The absorbance at 530 nm was detected. A standard curve was used to calculate the amount of ascorbic acid in each sample.

Proline contents

The proline content of maize was measured using Bates’ technique22. Leaves (0.5 g) were coarsely grated with 10 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid and filtered through Whatman filter paper (WFP, No. 2). This filtrate was mixed with 2 mL of acid ninhydrin solution (prepared by dissolving 1.25 g of ninhydrin in 20 mL of 6 molar orthophosphoric acid and 30 mL of glacial acetic acid) and 2 mL of glacial acetic acid. This mixture was cooked in a water bath at 100 °C for an hour. The liquid was then cooled in an ice bath before adding 10 mL of toluene to form a chromophore. Air was circulated continuously for 1–2 mins to separate the aqueous phase from the chromophore. This isolated coloured phase was allowed to emerge at room temperature for 2–3 mins. The absorbance was recorded at 520 nm via a spectrophotometer. In the experiment, we used toluene to read a blank sample.

Determination of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity

The method described by Cakmak23 was utilized to determine ascorbate peroxidase activity. The reaction mixture was created by combining 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.6), 0.1 mM Na-EDTA, 12 mM H2O2, 0.25 mM ascorbic acid, and the sample extract. At 290 nm, the absorbance of ascorbic acid decreased (extinction coefficient of 2.8 mM cm−1) in a 1 mL reaction mixture.

Total soluble protein (TSP)

Leaf samples were taken from each treatment and instantly frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen materials were pulverized into a fine powder with a mortar and pestle before being extracted in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 1 mM EDTA and 1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) buffer. 100 mg of powdered leaf tissue was combined with 1 mL of extraction buffer and vortexed for 10 seconds. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 mins at 4 °C.

Free amino acids (FAAs)

After buffer extraction, mix 80% (v/v) ethanol and 100 mg of powdered leaf tissue with 1 mL of extraction buffer and vortex for 10 seconds. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 mins at 4 °C. The supernatant was utilized to estimate amino acids using the ninhydrin assay24. Glycine was employed as a standard.

Statistical analysis

For analysis of variance (ANOVA) along with least significant difference we used Statistix-8.1. All the recorded data were statistically analyzed via the statistical software Statistix-8.1. Fisher’s analysis of variance test was used to determine the variation between varieties and applied treatments of salt stress and foliar sprays23. The results were subjected to randomized complete block design (RCBD). Graphs were prepared via Microsoft Excel 2010.

Chlorophyll-a contents

The Chlorophyll-a content was high in both maize varieties under non-saline conditions, but an increase in salinity significantly decreased the level of chlorophyll-a content in both maize varieties without any exogenous spray (Fig. 3A). The highest value (0.809 mg g−1 F.Wt.) of chlorophyll-a was observed in Sahiwal-2002 at non-saline conditions with foliar sprays of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) in combination. The lowest value of chlorophyll-a (0.09 mg g−1 F.Wt.) was observed in Sahiwal-2002 at 55 mM NaCl stress and under foliar spray of ascorbic acid (120 ppm).

(A–C) Effect of foliar applications of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid on, chlorophyll-a (A), chlorophyll-b (B), and total chlorophyll (C), of maize varieties (Akbar and Shaiwal-2002) at vegetative and reproductive stage under saline and non-saline conditions.

Chlorophyll-b contents

The chlorophyll-b content in both maize varieties significantly decreased in response to increasing salinity, in the absence of exogenous applications. Conversely, a notable increase in chlorophyll-b content was observed when a combination of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) was applied as a foliar spray to both varieties. Notably, compared with Akbar, Sahiwal-2002 demonstrated a greater capacity to maintain the chlorophyll-b content under both non-saline and saline conditions (Fig. 3B).

Total chlorophyll contents

The total chlorophyll content in both maize varieties significantly decreased in response to increasing salinity, in the absence of exogenous applications. However, compared with Akbar, Sahiwal-2002 presented a greater capacity to maintain the total chlorophyll under saline conditions. Furthermore, the foliar application of a combination of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) resulted in enhanced total chlorophyll concentration in Sahiwal-2002 relative to Akbar, under non-saline conditions, as well as at 55 mM and 110 mM NaCl, the highest salinity level tested (Fig. 3C). Under stress conditions, foliar application of ascorbic acid (120 ppm) and gibberellic acid (110 ppm) mitigated the negative effects of salt while increasing carotenoid content in the leaves of both maize varieties during the vegetative stage.

Ascorbic acid (AsA)

Under non-saline conditions, the ascorbic acid content was low in both varieties, but increased significantly with increasing salinity in the absence of exogenous spray. Akbar presented a greater ascorbic acid content than Sahiwal-2002 under saline conditions. The maximum increase in ascorbic acid content was observed when a combination of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) was applied under non-saline conditions, with Akbar showing a greater response. Under saline conditions (55 mM NaCl), the combined application of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) increased the ascorbic acid content more in Akbar than in Sahiwal-2002. At 110 mM NaCl, gibberellic acid (110 ppm) increased the ascorbic acid content in Akbar, whereas the combination of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) increased the ascorbic acid content in Sahiwal-2002 (Fig. 4A). The highest ascorbic acid content (142.56 µmol g−1 F. Wt.) was observed in Akbar at 110 mM NaCl stress with foliar spraying of gibberellic acid (110 ppm), whereas the lowest value (48.60 µmol g−1 F. Wt.) was recorded in Sahiwal-2002 without exogenous spraying (Table 4).

Proline contents

The proline content was low without salt stress but increasing the in salinity significantly increased the proline content in both maize varieties without any exogenous spray. Moreover, Akbar performed better with respect to proline content than Sahiwal-2002 did under saline conditions. The maximum increase in proline content was observed when ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) were applied in combination, while Akbar performed better than Sahiwal-2002 with respect to proline contents under non-saline conditions (Table 4).

Compared to the other treatments, the foliar spray of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) increased proline content in Akbar more than the Sahiwal-2002 at salinity level of 55 mM. The combined effect of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) sprays increased proline content in both varieties at the greatest salinity level, 110 mM NaCl (Fig. 4B).

The proline highest value (193.11 µg g−1 F.Wt.), was detected in Akbar under 110 mM NaCl stress and under foliar spraying of both ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) in combination. The lowest value of proline (43.19 µg g−1 F. Wt.) was observed in Sahiwal-2002 under non-saline conditions without any exogenous spray.

Ascorbate peroxidase activity

The ascorbate peroxidase contents were low without stress, but increasing the salinity significantly increased the ascorbate peroxidase contents in both maize varieties without any exogenous spray. Moreover, Akbar performed better in terms of ascorbate peroxidase content than Sahiwal-2002 did under saline conditions.

The maximum increase in ascorbate peroxidase activity was observed when ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) were sprayed as a combined effect in Sahiwal-2002 under non-saline conditions whereas the combined effect of ascorbic acid (120 ppm) and gibberellic acid (110 ppm) spray resulted in the maximum increase in ascorbate peroxidase activity in Akbar under non-saline conditions. The maximum increase in ascorbate peroxidase activity was observed when ascorbic acid (120 ppm) and gibberellic acid (110 ppm) were applied in combination, while Akbar performed better than Sahiwal-2002 at 55 mM and 110 mM NaCl (Fig. 4C).

The ascorbate peroxidase highest value (5.05 unit g−1 F.Wt.), activity was detected in Akbar under 110 mM NaCl stress and under foliar spraying of both ascorbic acid (120 ppm) and gibberellic acid (110 ppm) in combination. The lowest value of ascorbate peroxidases (0.64 unit g−1 F.Wt.) was observed in the Sahiwal-2002 under non-saline conditions without any exogenous spray.

Total soluble protein contents

Under non-saline conditions, both maize varieties had substantial total soluble protein content; however, increasing salinity drastically reduced total soluble protein content in both maize varieties without any exogenous spray. Furthermore, the Sahiwal-2002 had higher total soluble protein content than the Akbar under saline conditions.

The combined foliar treatment of ascorbic acid (120 ppm) and gibberellic acid (110 ppm) resulted in the greatest increase in total soluble protein content, particularly in the Sahiwal-2002 in non-saline conditions. Akbar had the highest total soluble protein content when treated with a combination of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) under non-saline conditions. At a salinity of 55 mM, foliar treatment of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) caused with a larger increase in total soluble protein content in Akbar than in Sahiwal-2002 (Fig. 5A).

Total free amino acid contents

Under non-saline conditions, both maize varieties had low total free amino acid content, but increasing salinity dramatically increased total free amino acid content in both maize varieties without the use of an exogenous spray. Furthermore, Akbar outperformed Sahiwal-2002 in terms of total free amino acid content when treated with saline.

In Sahiwal-2002, the combination of ascorbic acid (120 ppm) and gibberellic acid (110 ppm) resulted in the greatest increase in total free amino acids, whereas in Akbar, the combination of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) resulted in an increase in total free amino acids under non-saline conditions. The application of ascorbic acid (120 ppm) as a foliar spray increased total free amino acid content in Sahiwal-2002, whereas the use of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) increased total free amino acid content at a salinity level of 55 mM in Akbar. In Sahiwal-2002, the combined effect of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) increased the total free amino acid content, whereas in Akbar, the combination of ascorbic acid (120 ppm) and gibberellic acid (110 ppm) increased the total free amino acid content at 110 mM NaCl (Fig. 5B).

Sodium contents

The sodium content in two maize cultivars (Sahiwal-2002 and Akbar) increased significantly with increasing salinity. Akbar accumulated higher sodium levels than Sahiwal-2002 did under saline conditions. The highest sodium content (47 mg g−1 DW) was detected in Akbar at 110 mM NaCl stress with foliar application of 220 ppm gibberellic acid. In contrast, the lowest sodium content (26.21 mg g−1 DW) occurred in Akbar under non-saline conditions with 240 ppm ascorbic acid as an exogenous spray. The combined foliar application of 120 ppm ascorbic acid and 110 ppm gibberellic acid mitigated salinity stress and modulated the sodium content in both cultivars at the reproductive stage (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

Growth parameters

The combined foliar spray of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) increased growth metrics while mitigating salinity’s unfavorable effects. Under stress conditions, the combination foliar application of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) reduced salinity’s detrimental effects and increased plant height and fresh weight in both maize varieties at the vegetative stage. The dry weight of both maize types improved at the vegetative stage under stress circumstances as a result of the combination foliar application of ascorbic acid (120 ppm) and gibberellic acid (110 ppm), which alleviated the detrimental effects of salt and raised plant dry weight.

Salt stress inhibits shoot development and biomass production by accelerating leaf abscission and reducing internode growth, leaf initiation, and expansion due to a considerable reduction in the rate of cell elongation and the number of elongating cells25,26. This variation in the growth of both maize varieties in response to salt stress is mainly due to variations in the levels of endogenous growth hormones and the ability to avoid or tolerate the negative impacts of salt stress27,28. Compared with salt-sensitive hybrids, salt-tolerant maize hybrids have greater potential to maintain turgor in cells and continue their growth under salt stress26.

Photosynthetic pigments

In the current study, average concentrations of all photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll-a, chlorophyll-b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids) were higher in the plants of both maize varieties grown under non-saline conditions (control), but salt stress (55 mM and 110 mM NaCl) significantly reduced concentrations of all these photosynthetic pigments in both varieties at the vegetative and reproductive stages. Veloso et al.29, reported a linear decline in the concentrations of all these photosynthetic pigments in response to salt stress. This reduction in photosynthetic pigments is due to the disintegration of the cellular membrane, elevated level of electrolyte leakage, and reduced uptake of Mg ions in response to sodium toxicity30,31.

A foliar spray of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) combined with gibberellic acid (220 ppm) reduced the deleterious effects of salt stress on pigment concentrations in maize cultivars at both growth stages. Iqbal et al.32 found that foliar application of gibberellic acid reduced the negative effects of salt stress on maize plant pigment concentrations, dramatically increasing pigments, quantum yield, and photosynthetic rate. In the current study, the improved physiological performance of maize varieties grown under saline conditions with foliar application of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid could be attributed to the balanced uptake of ions and the appropriate pH within plants, which prepared the plants for pigment accumulation. Similar findings were also reported in previous studies of sunflower33, soybean34, and maize28,35,36.

Ascorbic acid contents

The current study found that the total concentration of ascorbic acid in Sahiwal-2002 was significantly higher than in Akbar; however, salt stress (55 mM and 110 mM, NaCl) reduced the level of ascorbic acid in both maize varieties during the vegetative and reproductive growth stages, respectively. The low quantity of ascorbic acid in the leaves of both maize varieties could be attributable to the high level of ROS because it serves as a critical substrate in the detoxification of these reactive oxygen species37.

Exogenous foliar application of ascorbic acid (120 ppm) combined with gibberellic acid (110 ppm) mitigated salt stress (55 mM NaCl) by increasing the endogenous ascorbic acid content in maize varieties. Increasing the concentration of both ameliorants (240 ppm + 220 ppm) was more effective at alleviating 110 mM NaCl stress, likely because of increased substrate availability for ROS scavenging. Similar findings were reported by Sheoran et al.38. Similarly, a previous study suggested that exogenous foliar application of gibberellic acid helps to combat salinity stress in sugar beet39 and maize12 by increasing the endogenous level of ascorbic acid at the seedling and reproductive growth stages.

Proline contents

Proline is a key global nonenzymatic antioxidant that not only scavenges ROS but also modulates a variety of fundamental pathways in plants, reducing the detrimental effects of both biotic and abiotic stressors. The total concentration of proline in this study increased under salt stress (55 mM and 110 mM NaCl) in both maize types during the vegetative growth stage. The current study also found that exogenous foliar application of ascorbic acid in combination with gibberellic acid (240 ppm + 220 ppm) reduced the negative effects of salt stress (55 mM and 110 mM NaCl) by increasing the endogenous concentration of osmoprotectants (proline contents) in both maize varieties during the vegetative stage. An increased concentration of both ameliorants was found to be effective with increasing levels of salt stress. Similar findings were reported by Hasanuzzaman et al.40 in Brassica campestris and Noreen et al.28 in Hordeum vulgare for salt stress amelioration via the exogenous application of ascorbic acid.

Ascorbate peroxidase activity

Peroxidases are heme-containing glycoproteins that are involved in a variety of physiological activities, including lignin and ethylene production, as well as the defence mechanism against abiotic and biotic stress41. The current study found that foliar application of ascorbic acid and giberellic acid (240 ppm and 220 ppm) was the most effective at reducing the effects of salt stress on the accumulation and activity of stress-responsive enzymes in both maize varieties during the vegetative phase. This class of enzymes catalyzes the dehydrogenation of diverse groups of phenolic and endiolic substrates by scavenging H2O2 and protects plant cells from various types of injurious and destructive effects of reactive oxygen species42. Our findings are in accordance with the findings of Meloni et al.43,44.

Total soluble proteins and free amino acids

Proteins and amino acids are nitrogen-containing organic compounds that play vital roles in the primary growth and development of plants. They help to alleviate the drastic impacts of abiotic stress, including salinity. These are readily available sources of nitrogen during high-level salt stress, when plants are unable to take up nitrogen from the soil43,44. Previous studies revealed that the levels of soluble proteins and free amino acids suddenly increase in plants under stress30,45, but the quantitative and qualitative patterns of these organic compounds change in plants with increasing stress duration and severity46,47.

Under non-saline conditions, the Sahiwal-2002 variety has a higher total soluble protein content (mg per g of leaf tissue) than the Akbar variety. At the vegetative growth stage, both maize cultivars were exposed to 55 mM and 110 mM NaCl, which lowered their total soluble protein content. The decrease in soluble protein levels in the leaf tissues of both maize varieties in response to salt stress could be attributed to their breakdown into basic elements such as carbon and nitrogen for growth and the basic phenomena required for plant survival under stress conditions48,49. Similar trends for free amino acid and soluble protein contents were reported in maize and other plants under stress18,50,51.

The current investigation further confirmed that, as compared to the salt-sensitive variety (Akbar), the salt-tolerant variety (Sahiwal-2002) had lower total soluble protein and total free amino acid levels. These findings are consistent with those of de Lacerda et al.52 and El-Esawi et al.53 for sorghum, cashew, and maize, respectively. The deleterious effects of salt stress on maize plants of both kinds were minimised by foliar application of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid.

High concentrations of sodium chloride promote the production of antioxidants and their related enzyme activities in maize Naz et al.54. The increased activity of various stresses related to biochemicals with significant increases in the concentrations of various biochemical components has been reported in the leaves of salt-stressed maize hybrids, where they act as ROS scavengers, in the membrane stabilization system, in homeostasis and in other important physiological processes. This increase is more prominent in salt-tolerant genotypes/hybrids and cultivars than in sensitive ones55,56.

Conclusion

The exogenous application of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid mitigated the adverse effects of salinity on maize plants by increasing growth parameters, photosynthetic pigments, ascorbic acid content, proline content, and antioxidant enzyme activity. The combined foliar application of ascorbic acid (240 ppm) and gibberellic acid (220 ppm) was most effective at alleviating salt stress, particularly at 110 mM NaCl. These findings suggest that the exogenous application of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid can be a useful strategy for improving salt tolerance in maize. Future studies should investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of ascorbic acid and gibberellic acid on salt tolerance in maize. Additionally, field trials should be conducted to validate the efficacy of this approach under natural conditions.
