Fast and accurate identification of emerging viral reassortment from genome sequences
Authors: Dehan Cai, Jiayu Shang, Cheng Peng, Herui Liao, Mang Shi, Yanni Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSegmented virus genomes, such as those of influenza A viruses (IAVs), consist of multiple segments. This structure enables the generation of novel strains through reassortment, where segments from different strains combine, greatly increasing the genetic diversity of segmented viruses. Reassortment can confer new biological properties to viruses, such as increased transmission rates, as evidenced by several influenza pandemics throughout history. Consequently, it is crucial to monitor reassortment events, particularly emerging ones. The vast availability of viral genome data provides an opportunity to identify emerging reassortment events. However, traditional methods often struggle with scalability due to high computational costs. This study introduces VReassort, a tool for fast and accurate detection of emerging reassortant strains using genome sequences. VReassort leverages deep learning models and phylogenetic-tree-derived features to detect reassortments between segment pairs. Experiments on simulated and real data demonstrate VReassort’s superior performance (F1-score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $> $\end{document} 0.8) across diverse scenarios. Remarkably, VReassort analyzed ~1000 IAV strains in under 2 min, achieving speeds over 100 times faster than the benchmark tool. Applying VReassort to large-scale IAV data (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $> $\end{document}8000 strains) uncovered intriguing reassortment patterns, while its application to rotavirus data confirmed the feasibility of extending VReassort to other segmented viruses.

Introduction

While many RNA viruses have continuous genomes, segmented RNA viruses, such as influenza virus and rotavirus, have genomes divided into multiple distinct segments [1, 2]. The well-known influenza A virus (IAV), for example, has a genome composed of eight segments that encode up to 11 proteins [3]. Of these, six segments encode internal proteins that are crucial for replication and virulence, while the remaining two segments encode the surface glycoproteins hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), which mediate viral entry into and release from host cells [4, 5]. Based on the highly divergent HA and NA segments, IAVs are classified into >120 antigenic subtypes (e.g. H1N1 and H3N2) and continue to evolve [6]. Furthermore, IAVs infect a wide range of species, including humans, birds, and swine [7]. Even among strains of the same subtype, variations in biological properties can affect their ability to infect different hosts [8].

Due to the segmented genome structure, the IAVs possess the capacity to generate new viral strains through a special mechanism called reassortment [9, 10]. When multiple segmented strains infect the same host cell, genome segments from different strains can be packaged into a single virus particle, resulting in the emergence of new viral strains with significant genetic changes [11]. Such reassortant strains, which carry genetic material from distinct parental strains, have more chances to exhibit enhanced fitness, increased virulence, and even the ability to cross the species barrier [12, 13], thereby raising the risk of potential pandemics. Historically, reassortant IAV strains have been responsible for several flu pandemics over the past century [14–16]. For example, the 2009 swine flu pandemic was caused by the emergence of a novel H1N1 strain. Investigations revealed that this strain resulted from a combination of a prior triple reassortment event involving bird, swine, and human IAV strains, along with an Eurasian swine IAV strain [17, 18].

Given the potential of reassortment to produce viral strains with enhanced properties, understanding and monitoring IAV reassortments are critical for predicting and preventing future outbreaks or pandemics caused by novel IAV strains. Some studies have explored the segment packing mechanism of IAVs and identified key packing signals [19], advancing our understanding of the reassortment process. However, the complex nature of reassortment remains incompletely understood. Many researchers have conducted experiments in lab to investigate the reassortment patterns of specific IAV subtypes or lineages [20–22], providing valuable insights into the reassortment process. But such experiments are both time-consuming and labor-intensive. Studying reassortment through genome sequencing data offers an effective alternative [23, 24]. With advancements in sequencing technologies, an unprecedented number of IAV genomes have become available in public databases (e.g. NCBI and GISAID) [25–27]. Leveraging global sequencing data, the tool NextStrain [28] was developed to monitor various infectious viruses, including IAVs, in real time. However, it lacks the ability to provide reassortment information, as public datasets rarely include annotations for such events. Detecting viral reassortment directly from sequencing data is crucial, as it offers valuable insights for tracking and understanding reassortment dynamics. However, detecting the reassortment events is far more complex than it appears. The sequential and cumulative nature of IAV reassortment has led to nearly all existing IAV strains carrying a history of reassortment. For instance, the currently circulating human H3N2 lineage originated from a single reassortment event in 1968 [29], while the present H1N1 strains (and the previously pandemic H1N1) emerged from a triple reassortment, as mentioned earlier. Consequently, virtually no strain can be considered truly “non-reassortant.” Since reassortments occur continuously, focusing on detecting recent or emerging events provides a more biologically meaningful and practical approach, such as monitoring novel human IAV reassortant strains with unique segment genotypes for public health purposes. Accordingly, this study focuses on identifying reassortant strains from emerging reassortment events within a defined time frame, treating those from earlier reassortments as non-reassortant.

To validate whether a strain is reassortant, researchers commonly construct and compare phylogenetic trees for different segments [23]. Since reassortant strains contain segments from different origins, they tend to exhibit distinct positions and phylogenetic distances in the respective segment trees. This allows reassortment to be identified by detecting discordance between the phylogenetic trees of multiple segments [30]. However, manually examining discordance between trees with numerous strains to identify reassortant strains is labor-intensive [31]. Additionally, distinguishing reassortant strains from closely related strains in noisy phylogenetic trees is further complicated by the subtle discordance patterns. Several methods have been developed to identify reassortant strains directly from omics data [32]. For example, FluResort [33] uses the patristic distance from the phylogenetic tree of viral proteins to determine the most probable protein origins for a query strain. If different origins are detected for proteins in different segments, the strain is predicted to be a reassortant strain. However, this tool is specifically designed for mass spectrometry data and is currently unavailable on its website. GiRaF [31] constructs a split graph to capture discordance signals between two phylogenetic trees, enabling the identification of potential reassortant strains. However, investigating the split graph is computationally complex and challenging to apply to large-scale datasets. HopPER [34] developed a model that estimates the probability of influenza reassortment by predicting the host tropism of each segment. If the host tropism varies across segments, the strain is likely to be a reassortant. However, since reassortment can also occur between strains from the same host, this method (currently unavailable online) has limitations in identifying various reassortant strains. Two tools, CoalRe [35] and TreeKnit [36], aim to infer ancestral reassortment graphs involving historical and recent reassortment events by resolving discordance between segment trees. But they do not directly specify which strains are recent reassortant, and the inferred ancestral reassortment graphs may include numerous spurious connections due to phylogenetic noise. This is especially problematic given the complexity of evolutionary histories in large-scale datasets. A recent study [37] proposed a method to identify reassortant strains by assessing the consistency of segment genotypes, which are defined by another tool. While this method can identify some reassortant strains, its performance is limited by the defined genotypes, which require a large number of genome references for accurate identification.

In this work, we developed a tool, VReassort, to identify emerging reassortant strains between pairs of genome segments using sequencing data. VReassort employs an effective method to generate features that represent strains across phylogenetic trees by incorporating both structural information and phylogenetic distances. It then trains a Siamese network on these features to detect reassortment events. This learning-based approach addresses key challenges, such as distinguishing discordance caused by reassortment from background noise, which is often difficult to quantify. Moreover, the trained model can be directly applied, significantly accelerating the identification process. By integrating a fast phylogenetic tree construction method with the trained model, VReassort is both efficient and reliable, demonstrating strong performance on diverse datasets and large-scale analyses. While this study primarily focuses on the reassortment of IAV, we also applied VReassort to another segmented virus, the rotavirus.

Materials and methods

Overview

Technically, nearly all IAV strains are reassortant due to their history of reassortment events. However, since detecting emerging reassortant strains provides more valuable information for monitoring novel strains, this study focuses on identifying emerging reassortant strains while treating those with previous reassortment events as non-reassortant within the current period. Since reassortant strains inherit genetic segments from different parental strains, their evolution exhibits inconsistencies among the segments. Analyzing these inconsistencies between the phylogenetic trees of segments provides an effective approach to identifying reassortant strains. However, inherent differences between segments and noise in the construction of phylogenetic trees can also result in disagreements between segment trees, making it challenging to pinpoint the true reassortment patterns of some strains. Additionally, tracing reassortment patterns in large-scale genome datasets is both time-consuming and complex. In this study, we developed a tool, VReassort, to efficiently identify emerging reassortant strains. Given the segment sequences of various strains, VReassort detects whether recent reassortment events have occurred between any two segments by applying a Siamese network trained on carefully designed features derived from phylogenetic trees. By analyzing the genomic segments of these strains, VReassort detects reassortant strains through pairwise comparisons of their segments. Using features extracted from pairwise phylogenetic trees as input to a trained neural network for each strain, VReassort assigns higher scores (e.g. 0.99) to strains containing segments from different origins (e.g. segments 2 and 6 in the strain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $S_4$\end{document}), thereby identifying reassortant strains. Two major challenges in developing the model were the lack of annotated reassortment information in publicly available genome sequences and the need for effective feature extraction from phylogenetic trees. To address these challenges, we generated synthetic sequences by simulating reassortment events between segments. Training the model on these synthetic sequences enables VReassort to accurately identify reassortant strains and optimize performance for large-scale datasets. In the following sections, we provide a detailed description of the processes involved in synthetic data generation, feature extraction, model training, and reassortment identification.

Schematic diagram of VReassort for identifying reassortant strains. Given the genome segments of a set of strains, VReassort detects reassortant candidates (e.g. strain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $S_4$\end{document}) by iteratively comparing phylogenetic trees constructed from segment pairs. For each strain, it derives two feature vectors from the corresponding segment trees and applies a trained Siamese network to evaluate whether the strain originates from different sources due to reassortment. A high output score (e.g. 0.99) indicates that the strain is reassortant.

Synthetic data generation

Reassortant strains exhibit location discordance in the phylogenetic trees of different segments due to inheriting segments from distinct parental strains. Following the simulation process outlined in GiRaF [31], we simulate reassortment events between two segment trees by detaching selected clades (subtrees) from a given tree and grafting them onto new locations, resulting in a modified tree (as illustrated in Fig. 2a). These two trees represent the same set of strains, differing only in the locations of the selected clades (e.g. two clades in the given example). Strains within the selected clades are labeled as reassortant strains. For each clade, we calculate the patristic distances between its original and new locations to quantify the divergence between the parental strains (unobserved), which we define as the reassortant distance. Lower reassortant distances indicate higher genetic similarity between the parental strains, while higher values represent greater divergence. This enables us to evaluate the performance of tools in detecting reassortment events originating from parental strains with varying levels of genetic diversity.

The weight consists of two components: patristic distance and node counts. The patristic distance is the sum of branch lengths along the path between strains. The node count between two strains includes the number of internal nodes along the path and the leaves directly connected to the path.

Using the original and modified trees, we employed the phylogenetic sequence simulator AliSim [38] to simulate segment sequences for all strains under the evolutionary conditions defined by each segment tree, respectively. Each strain was assigned two sequences representing two different segments. The two sets of segment sequences were then used as input for phylogenetic tree reconstruction following a standard workflow, including multiple sequence alignment using MAFFT [39] and phylogenetic inference with FastTree [40]. The segment trees reconstructed from the simulated sequences are not identical to the corresponding reference trees used to generate those sequences, highlighting the realistic noise inherent in phylogenetic reconstruction (see Supplementary Fig. S3).

This simulation process directly mimics the phylogenetic discordance introduced by reassortment while incorporating the diversity of parental strains. Although it does not capture the full complexity of real evolutionary processes, such as selection or mutation hotspots, the resulting discordance patterns provide a strong and informative signal for reassortment analysis in both an effective and efficient manner.

Feature processing

Overview

To identify the discordance caused by reassortant strains between two phylogenetic trees, we analyze the relationships between the strains in both trees. Using patristic distances (i.e. branch lengths between strains) offers a straightforward method to illustrate these relationships. Coupled with the noise in tree construction, it becomes challenging to distinguish some discordance caused by reassortment from noise. Third, patristic distances do not fully reflect the evolutionary relationships among strains, as they ignore the structural information within the tree. Although the tree structure is more responsive to changes caused by reassortment, it is also sensitive to irregular fluctuations and discrepancies arising from similar strain and noise in tree construction. These challenges make it difficult to generate effective strain features for identifying disagreements caused by reassortment in two phylogenetic trees. To address these issues, we propose a method to derive a more robust feature representation for each strain across different segment trees.

In each segment tree, we first record the patristic distances between every pair of strains, along with the number of internal nodes and strains on the path connecting them. For each strain, we then use these distances and node counts to compute weights that represent its closeness to all other strains. In the following section, we describe how the orderings are determined and how the weights are calculated.

Orderings determination

We rank the strains from closest to farthest, considering both patristic distance and topological proximity. Patristic distance is determined by branch lengths, while topological proximity is quantified using subtree sizes. Weights calculation

The combined distance described above effectively captures the closeness between strains by incorporating both patristic and topological information. However, it represents the closeness between strains on an uneven scale as subtree sizes increase unevenly from the terminal leaves to higher levels. Thus, we calculate the weights of closeness through another process.

Before calculating the weights, internal branches shorter than 0.001 may be collapsed if they are shorter than their proximal branches or if their lengths are below 0.0003. These operations reduce unnecessary bifurcations in trees with highly similar sequences, making the topology more stable and less noisy. Using the revised trees, we quantify the closeness between strains using both the number of nodes along the connecting path (including internal nodes and directly connected leaves) and the patristic distance, as shown in Fig. 2c. Each measure has its own strengths and limitations. Node counts, which increase as strains become more topologically distant, help detect reassortant strains with very similar origins that still show subtle topological differences. These differences generally cannot be captured well by patristic distance alone. However, node counts can be influenced by the number of strains in the tree. If reassortant strains show large differences in patristic distances between trees but have relatively few neighboring strains, patristic distance becomes a more effective measure of their closeness. Thus, we integrate these two measurements to quantify strain closeness.

To ensure that the model trained on the weight vectors can generalize to trees of varying sizes, we retain only the top 40 values in each vector and normalize the weights. These values represent the weights from the most related strains, which are more likely to vary significantly during reassortment. Since vectors may still include distant strains due to noise or a limited number of strains, they can exhibit significant discordance when comparing distant strains. To mitigate this, we cap the large values of the node counts at certain thresholds, minimizing the influence of distant strains while preserving the ability to detect discordance.

Model training and reassortment identification

Model structure

We employ a Siamese network (a contrastive learning model) to classify whether a strain is reassortant by evaluating the cosine similarity between two learned embeddings of input features derived from two segment trees (as shown in Fig. 1). Each input feature is processed through a convolutional neural network layer (with a kernel size of 5 and a stride of 1), followed by a max-pooling layer (with a size of 2 and a stride of 2), and then two fully connected layers (with dimensions of 128 and 32) to generate embeddings. Reassortant strains typically exhibit distinct input vectors from the two segment trees, leading to differences in their embeddings. In contrast, non-reassortant strains produce highly similar embeddings from the two segment trees. To quantify this difference, we calculate the reassortant score (ranging from 0 to 1) for strain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $i$\end{document} using the formula in Eq. 2, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $E^{u1}_i$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $E^{u2}_i$\end{document} represent the embeddings of strain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $i$\end{document} using the weights from tree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $u$\end{document}. A score close to 0 indicates that the strain’s embeddings from the two segment trees are highly similar, classifying it as non-reassortant, while higher scores (e.g. 0.9) indicate reassortant strains. Since each strain is associated with two pairs of inputs, VReassort calculates two scores by processing each pair of inputs separately and uses the average score as the final reassortant score.

Model training

We employed the mean squared error loss function to train the model, assigning a label of 1 to reassortant strains and 0 to non-reassortant strains. However, the lack of publicly annotated data for reassortment presents a significant challenge, resulting in a shortage of labeled training data. To overcome this, we generated a series of synthetic datasets, a common strategy when labeled data is unavailable [41, 42]. To create the training data, we downloaded all HA segment sequences of the IAV from the NCBI database. Low-quality sequences (e.g. those with short lengths or containing non-ACGT characters) and redundant identical sequences were removed, resulting in a total of 75 583 HA segment sequences. Among these, the three most dominant subtypes were H3N2 (23 338 sequences), H1N1 (21 160 sequences), and H9N2 (6641 sequences). For each of the three subtypes, we randomly sampled 500 sequences twice and constructed phylogenetic trees using MAFFT and FastTree with the GTR+CAT+G model, generating two phylogenetic trees per subtype (six trees in total). For each tree, we simulated 1000 datasets (each consisting of sequences from two segments): 500 datasets with a single reassortment event, 300 with three events, and 200 with five events. Each reassortment event involved 1–10 randomly selected strains. Additionally, 60% of the datasets had normalized reassortment distances between 0.2 and 0.5, while those with distances between 0.1 and 0.2 or 0.5 and 1 each accounted for 20% of the total. Since the total number of non-reassortant strains was over 95 times greater than the number of reassortant strains across all datasets, we randomly selected five times the number of reassortant strains per dataset to balance the training data. In total, we used 31 169 reassortant strains and 155 845 non-reassortant strains to train the model.

Reassortment identification between pairwise segments

VReassort generates two pairs of feature vectors and inputs them into the model to calculate the reassortant scores. While the feature generation process effectively captures the discordance caused by reassortment, the input features of non-reassortant strains can be affected when their neighbors include reassortant strains. As a result, the estimated scores for these non-reassortant strains may be elevated. To address this, VReassort calculates the scores for each pair of input features twice. In the second calculation, it excludes the strains with large estimated scores (default threshold: 0.8) from the feature vectors and re-estimates the scores for all strains. The scores derived from the second calculation are then used to compute the average score. Each strain is associated with two pairs of vectors, and the model processes each pair separately to produce two scores. If the average score exceeds a threshold (default: 0.99/2 = 0.495), the strain is classified as a reassortant strain in this study. While VReassort primarily focuses on identifying individual reassortant strains, its functionality can be extended to clades by first matching internal nodes between trees and then generating features for the matched internal nodes using a similar approach. This capability allows VReassort to identify reassortment events in large clades. In the output, VReassort presents identification results for both individual strains (leaves) and clades. By applying VReassort to sequences of every pairwise segment, we can identify the reassortment pattern of strains between multiple segments, i.e. which segments are from the same origins. Since the identification results across all segments may not be 100% accurate, the grouping of segments for reassortant strains may not naturally emerge. In the experiment with multiple segments, we applied a clustering strategy to determine which segments originate from the same origins by finding the cliques in segment graphs. Further details are provided in Supplementary Section S6.

Datasets and validation

Simulated data

In the simulated experiments, we generated three types of datasets using different phylogenetic trees of the IAV. These phylogenetic trees contain entirely different strains from those in the training datasets, ensuring more robust validation. The first type of dataset used a phylogenetic tree constructed from the HA segment of 156 H3N2 subtype IAV strains (Human), previously utilized for simulation experiments in GiRaF [31]. By randomly selecting and relocating a subtree (clade) containing up to 10 strains in the phylogenetic tree repeatedly (see Fig. 2a), we simulated 500 datasets with an even distribution of reassortant distances. This simulation allows us to evaluate the performance of identifying reassortant strains with varying degrees of similarity among parental strains. Since a dataset may contain multiple reassortment events, we also simulated 100 datasets by relocating K (where K = 3 and K = 5) non-overlapping subtrees per dataset.

While the first type of data serves as the basis for evaluating the performance of the tools, the second type focuses on testing their robustness and efficiency. While the training data used HA segments from the three dominant IAV subtypes (H3N2, H1N1, and H9N2), we intentionally employed NA segments from different subtypes to simulate the data and further evaluate the generalizability of VReassort. We downloaded all NA segments of IAV from the NCBI database. After removing redundant and low-quality sequences, we identified six dominant subtypes with >1000 sequences: H3N2, H1N1, H9N2, H1N2, H5N1, and H3N8. Excluding the subtypes used in the training data, we selected NA segment sequences from the subtypes H1N2, H5N1, and H3N8. For each subtype, we used CD-HIT [43] to cluster sequences with over 95% identity, ensuring that the constructed phylogenetic trees excluded isolated clades that could interfere with the normalization of reassortant distances. Among the sequence clusters, the largest belonged to the H1N2 subtype, containing 953 sequences. Based on the subtypes and cluster sizes, we selected three clusters to construct phylogenetic trees: H3N8 with 156 strains, H5N1 with 452 strains, and H1N2 with 953 strains. Phylogenetic tree visualizations are provided in the Supplementary Figs S15–S17. Using each phylogenetic tree, we simulated 100 datasets with a similar approach, incorporating three reassortant clades and an even distribution of reassortant distances.

As reassortment may confer enhanced fitness to new strains and lead to the formation of large clades in the dataset, the ability to identify such large reassortant clades facilitates the surveillance and control of potential epidemics. Thus, the third type of data mimics the reassortant clades of large sizes. Using the phylogenetic tree of NA segments from the 953 H1N2 strains, we generated 100 datasets similar to the second type, with clade sizes ranging from 40 (matching the feature size) to 100.

Real sequencing data

We used three real datasets of the IAV to validate the performance of VReassort. The first dataset contains sequences of the HA and NA segments from 156 IAV strains of the H3N2 subtype [23]. Although it lacks ground-truth labels, the study [23] identified five reassortant strains between the HA and NA segments in this dataset through manual comparison. The second dataset comprises sequences from all eight segments of 202 IAV strains [44]. These sequences were collected from pigs co-infected with H1N1 and H3N2 strains and include known ground-truth labels. Of the 202 strains, 54 are reassortant, with different segment combinations derived from the H1N1 and H3N2 strains. The third dataset includes the reassortant strains (pH1N1) responsible for the 2009 swine flu pandemic. We downloaded 2852 complete genome sequences of IAV strains that infected humans and swine between 2007 and 2009 in Europe and North America. CD-HIT was applied to remove highly similar sequences (i.e. >99.5% identity), resulting in a final dataset of 388 genome sequences. Although most pH1N1 strains were filtered out by CD-HIT, 115 pH1N1 strains remained due to evolutionary or sequencing biases. An overview of these three datasets is summarized in Table 1.

Benchmarking tools and evaluation metrics

While numerous methods have been proposed to identify reassortment events [32], we did not benchmark VReassort against all of them due to several limitations. Some tools are no longer available [34, 37], others require input data beyond genome sequences [33], and some depend on additional metadata such as collection year or subtype [35, 37, 45]. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the availability, input requirements, and key features of several major tools. In this study, we primarily benchmarked VReassort against GiRaF [31], a widely used tool [24] that accepts genome sequences as input and outputs reassortant strains. We also compared VReassort with TreeKnit [36] and RDP4 [46], which employ approaches distinct from those used by VReassort and GiRaF. Because these two tools require additional post-processing to obtain the reassortant strains, we included them as supplementary benchmarks. TreeKnit is designed to infer an ancestral reassortment graph rather than directly identify reassortant strains. The resulting graph represents all historical reassortment events, and additional effort is required to extract recent reassortant strains for a fair comparison. RDP4, on the other hand, was originally developed for detecting recombination events and reports the positions of recombinant breakpoints. To adapt it for reassortment detection, we applied it to concatenated segment sequences and considered events as reassortments when the detected breakpoints occurred at segment junctions.

We mainly evaluated the tools on both simulated and real IAV datasets. In the simulated data and real data with ground truth labels, we evaluated the performance of each tool using the following metrics: precision, recall, and F1-score for the reported reassortant strains, as well as accuracy for all correctly classified strains (both reassortant and non-reassortant). Since VReassort outputs a reassortant score for each strain and provides supplementary scores for large clades by analyzing the features of internal nodes between phylogenetic trees, we additionally included the strains within the identified reassortant clades when calculating the evaluation metrics to ensure a fair comparison with the other tools. Strains are regarded as reassortant by VReassort if their output scores exceed the specified thresholds in this study (see Supplementary Section S1 and Supplementary Fig. S2 for the threshold analysis). The metrics were calculated according to Eq. 3. In the real dataset without ground truth labels, we validated the predicted reassortant strains by cross–referencing them with previously reported reassortment events and phylogenetic evidence documented in the literature.

VReassort achieves accurate reassortment detection across diverse parental strain similarities

Since reassortments can occur between strains with varying levels of similarity (reassortant distance), we first evaluated reassortment identification tools using 700 simulated datasets, where reassortant distances were evenly distributed. Following GiRaF [31], the datasets were generated from a phylogenetic tree constructed using 156 HA segments of the H3N2 subtype, which includes many sequences with high identity (e.g. >99%). Each dataset contains 156 strains, with 1, 3, or 5 clades of reassortant strains, and each clade comprises up to 10 strains. Details of the generation process are provided in the “Materials and methods” section. While reassortant strains exhibit topological discordance between segment trees, noise in the segment trees can also create discordance, making it difficult to distinguish between noise and some reassortant strains with small reassortant distances. Figure 4a shows the Robinson–Foulds (RF) distance distributions between two reconstructed trees generated from simulated sequences, both with and without reassortant strains. The RF distance serves as a measure of dissimilarity between two phylogenetic trees. The significant overlap in RF distance distributions between trees with and without reassortant strains underscores the challenge of differentiating true reassortment events from background noise.

By applying tools to the datasets, we summarized the performance of VReassort and GiRaF in Fig. 4b. VReassort demonstrates higher precision (>0.9) and comparable recall (>0.95) to GiRaF in identifying reassortant strains in datasets with the normalized reassortant distances greater than 0.2. However, when reassortant strains have highly similar parental strains with a normalized reassortant distance of <0.2 (original reassortant distance <0.02, as shown in the Supplementary Fig. S1), both VReassort and GiRaF face challenges in identifying many of them, leading to lower recall. Precision also decreases significantly in such cases because the number of reassortant strains in a dataset is small (e.g. 5 reassortant strains). Compared to VReassort, GiRaF exhibits greater sensitivity in identifying reassortant strains with highly similar origins but produces more false positives. As the number of reassortment events increases, GiRaF misclassified a greater number of non-reassortant strains as reassortant, resulting in a decline in precision. This misclassification occurs because an increased number of reassortant clades influences the inferred positions of non-reassortant strains during phylogenetic tree construction, and GiRaF is highly sensitive to tree discordance. In contrast, VReassort demonstrates stable performance across datasets with varying numbers of reassortant clades, making it more suitable for application to real datasets involving multiple reassortment events. Although VReassort may occasionally fail to identify strains with highly similar parental strains, it successfully detects the majority of reassortant strains, yielding recall comparable to that of GiRaF across most datasets. Similar to VReassort, TreeKnit and RDP4 did not exhibit significant bias with respect to changes in the number of reassortments, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S9. TreeKnit successfully identified more reassortant strains when the reassortant distance was small. However, it also produced many false-positive reassortant strains because it is not robust to noise in phylogenetic trees, resulting in low precision. In contrast, RDP4 demonstrated higher precision in identifying reassortant strains but primarily detected those with large reassortant distances (e.g. 0.4–1.0), which are relatively easier to identify. Overall, VReassort exhibited a more balanced performance on these simulated datasets compared with the other tools.

VReassort demonstrates fast and robust performance with an increasing number of strains

While the experimental results above demonstrate VReassort’s ability to effectively identify multiple reassortant strains across varying reassortant distances, we further tested VReassort on 300 simulated datasets with an increased number of strains. Each dataset contained three reassortant clades, with up to 10 strains per clade. Unlike the training data, which utilized trees from the HA segments, these simulated datasets were generated from phylogenetic trees based on the NA segments of IAV strains across three entirely different subtypes. As a result, this dataset provides a more robust validation of VReassort.

Figure 4d summarizes the performance of VReassort and GiRaF on these datasets. Both GiRaF and VReassort identified reassortant strains with high recall in the majority of datasets with various strain numbers. However, as the number of strains in the datasets increased, both VReassort and GiRaF identified more false reassortant strains. Since the maximum number of reassortant strains per clade in each dataset remained unchanged, the decrease in precision was expected. Despite this, VReassort maintained a higher precision (~0.75) in most datasets. It reported only a small number of false reassortant strains, while GiRaF had more false-positive cases. For example, most of the datasets generated from the H1N2 tree had >900 non-reassortant strains. However, VReassort reported only about two isolated false reassortant strains (not including strains in large clades) on average. Similarly, in datasets generated from the H3N8 tree, which contained >100 non-reassortant strains, an average of 0.35 isolated false reassortant strains was reported by VReassort. These results demonstrate the robust performance of VReassort across diverse datasets of varying sizes. In this experiment, the two additional tools, TreeKnit and RDP4, again exhibited an opposite preference, consistent with the results of the previous simulation experiment. TreeKnit tended to identify reassortant strains more aggressively, leading to a larger number of false reassortments. In contrast, RDP4 achieved substantially higher precision but primarily for reassortant strains with large reassortant distances. Their performance is shown in Supplementary Fig. S10.

Furthermore, we measured the running time of VReassort and GiRaF (including the tree construction process) on these datasets using an HPCC CentOS 7.6.1810 node equipped with 2.70 GHz Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6338 CPUs and 160 GB of memory (see Table 2). VReassort completed the analysis of the dataset with 953 strains in ~1.5 min, whereas GiRaF required ~308 min (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim$\end{document}5 h) to produce results. In addition, we measured the memory usage of VReassort across datasets of different sizes using GNU time, which reports the maximum resident set size during execution. The peak memory usage was ~0.29 GB for 156 sequences (H3N8), 0.30 GB for 452 sequences (H5N1), and 0.47 GB for 953 sequences (H1N2). In the simulated experiments above, we classified strains with an average score greater than a threshold (0.99/2 = 0.495 by default) as reassortant strains across all datasets. The results demonstrate that VReassort can identify most reassortant strains with high precision, missing only those with highly similar parental strains. To investigate the confidence of the scores for reassortant strain identification, we analyzed 4553 reassortant strains and 260 747 non-reassortant strains (approximately a 1:57 ratio) across the simulated datasets. Figure 4c shows the score distributions of all reassortant and non-reassortant strains among the experiments. The two distributions exhibit significant differences: most non-reassortant strains have scores below 0.2, while most reassortant strains have scores close to 1. The primary overlap between the two distributions occurs in the score range of 0–0.3, mainly due to reassortant strains with small reassortant distances. Only a small proportion of non-reassortant strains have scores greater than 0.4. Thus, VReassort demonstrates strong performance in distinguishing reassortant strains from non-reassortant strains.

We also examined the score distribution relative to changes in reassortant distance, as well as the performance of VReassort under various score thresholds. In addition to the score–threshold analysis, we evaluated the robustness of VReassort under different settings of phylogenetic tree construction, including the use of different multiple sequence alignment tools, phylogenetic tree inference tools, and substitution models. The results indicate that VReassort is robust to both noise and variation introduced by different tree-related methods across these datasets. Further details are provided in Supplementary Figs S4–S6 in the Supplementary Section S2. Because phylogenetic tree quality may affect the performance of VReassort, we further tested the robustness of VReassort to tree uncertainty via sequence-level perturbations. The results demonstrate stable performance under moderate perturbations (e.g. an additional error rate of 1.0%). Details are provided in Supplementary Figs S7 and S8 and Supplementary Section S3.

VReassort is able to identify large-clade reassortant strains

While some reassortant strains may disappear over time, others may gain greater fitness to their environment, forming large clades. A notable example is the reassortant strains responsible for the flu pandemic in 2009. Therefore, being able to monitor these large-clade reassortant strains is crucial for epidemic control. We conducted simulated experiments on datasets generated using the NA segments of 953 H1N2 strains. Each dataset contains three reassortant clades, with sizes randomly ranging from 40 to 100. Supplementary Fig. S12 summarizes the performance of the tools in identifying large reassortant clades. VReassort shows similar performance by identifying most of the large-clade reassortant strains, as seen in other simulated experiments. Since the internal nodes in the two phylogenetic trees may not perfectly match due to noise, VReassort identified the reassortant clades by allowing minor differences between clades from different trees. VReassort compared the clades containing similar strains (e.g. Jaccard Index > 0.8 and fewer than 10 different strains) and reported the identification results of the union of strains between the clades. While this approach may not perfectly identify the large reassortant clades, the high recall (>0.9) indicates that VReassort identified the large reassortant clades with similar sizes, demonstrating its ability to detect large-clade reassortments. Furthermore, the real data experiment on the dataset from the 2009 flu pandemic also demonstrates VReassort’s ability to identify large-clade reassortant strains (see Fig. 6).

VReassort demonstrates the capability to detect reassortant strains in real data

We validated the performance of VReassort and GiRaF using three real datasets of IAV. These include two datasets with intra/inter-subtype reassortant strains reported by previous studies [23, 31, 44] and one dataset related to the 2009 flu pandemic [17]. Table 1 summarizes the three real datasets.

Intra-subtype reassortment: 156 Human (H3N2) IAV strains

The first dataset contains the HA and NA segments of 156 H3N2 (human) IAV strains from a previous research [23] in which some strains were identified as reassortant. In this dataset, many strains exhibit high similarities (greater than 99%) in both HA and NA segments, complicating the identification of reassortment among these closely related strains. In the first dataset, VReassort and GiRaF reported the same 6 strains as reassortant. The left panel of Fig. 5a shows the score distribution of the 156 strains, which has a noticeable gap between 0.3 and 0.9. Most strains have scores lower than 0.1, while only six strains have scores of 0.9 or higher. To validate whether the six strains with high scores are indeed reassortant strains, we investigated their evolutionary relationships with other strains in two phylogenetic trees of the segments. Following GiRaF, we present the phylogenetic trees for the HA and NA segments of the 156 H3N2 strains in Fig. 5b. These trees were constructed using FastTree and visualized with Phylo.io [48]. The reported reassortant strains are highlighted in red, blue, and green, and their scores are presented in Fig. 5a. Among the 6 reported reassortant strains, 5 were consistent with those reported in a previous study [23] via manual comparison. These 5 strains, highlighted in green and blue, exhibit significant evolutionary discordance between the two trees, supporting their classification as reassortant strains. In addition to these 5 strains, VReassort and GiRaF reported one additional strain, “A/New_York/105/2002.” Its locations (highlighted in red) in the two phylogenetic trees display evolutionary discordance, supported by the significant differences among many of its neighboring strains. Notably, these neighboring strains exhibit high consistency in their positions across the two trees, providing a reliable reference for comparison. This suggests that “A/New_York/105/2002” is likely a reassortant strain with similar parental strains, rather than a non-reassortant strain affected by irregular fluctuations during tree construction. Thus, this real experiment showcases the ability of VReassort to detect reassortant strains, including those with similar parental strains, in real data.

Inter-subtype reassortment: 202 Swine (H3N2/H1N1) IAV strains

The second dataset has 202 strains of the IAV virus with 8 segments. These segment sequences were collected from pigs co-infected with H1N1 and H3N2 IAV strains in laboratory [44]. There are 54 strains that contain different segments from both the H1N1 and H3N2 strains, while a total of 137 strains and 11 strains contain segments only from H3N2 and H1N1, respectively. Supplementary Fig. S13 illustrates the number of reassortant strains on pairwise segments. Due to the incomplete lengths of some segment sequences, the multiple sequence alignment contains numerous gap columns, which introduce noise into the tree construction. Thus, we utilized trimAI [49] to trim the alignment before constructing the phylogenetic trees for VReassort and GiRaF. By applying VReassort and GiRaF to 28 pairwise segments respectively, we compared the identified reassortant strains to the ground truth. The results of VReassort and GiRaF are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S14. Because the reassortments occurred between two strains with different subtypes, the identification of reassortment through phylogenetic trees is not difficult. However, GiRaF only reported a few reassortant strains on this dataset, resulting in low recall and accuracy. A possible reason is that the phylogenetic trees have a simple structure, but GiRaF needs a sufficient number of informative splits to identify the reassortment. Among the 28 pairwise segments, VReassort achieved high accuracy by identifying almost all reassortant strains while maintaining high precision at the same time. The average precision and recall of VReassort among the 28 pairwise segments are ~0.98 and 0.95. It only missed a few reassortant strains on some segment pairs containing the PB2 segment, likely because there were only a few PB2 segments (17 out of 202) from the H1N1 strain. Additionally, a few non-reassortant strains were mistakenly identified as reassortant due to noise and the large proportion of reassortant strains in the phylogenetic trees. This experiment further demonstrates the robust performance of VReassort in detecting inter-subtype reassortant strains in real data.

Large-clade reassortment: 388 Human/Swine IAV strains (115 pH1N1 reassortant strains)

The third dataset contains the reassortant strains of IAV strains (pH1N1) responsible for the 2009 swine flu pandemic. Since its emergence, pH1N1 has spread and infected numerous people. Many of its genomic sequences have been sequenced and are available in databases, forming a large clade in the phylogenetic trees of IAV strains. We downloaded 2852 complete genome sequences of IAV strains that infected humans and swine in Europe and North America between 2007 and 2009. We then applied CD-HIT to remove highly similar sequences (i.e. >99.5% identity), resulting in a final dataset of 388 genome sequences. Although most pH1N1 strains were filtered out by CD-HIT, 115 pH1N1 strains remained due to evolutionary or sequencing biases. Figure 6a shows two phylogenetic trees of the HA and NA segments from the 388 strains. The clades highlighted in red, green, and blue represent the pH1N1 strains, North American swine strains, and European swine strains, respectively. Since the pH1N1 strain inherited the HA and NA segments from different origins through reassortment, the pH1N1 clade exhibits distinct evolutionary relationships with other strains in two segments, as clearly illustrated in the phylogenetic trees. We applied VReassort and GiRaF to the dataset for all 28 segment pairs of 8 segments and evaluated whether any reported reassortment events with large clades are related to the clade of 115 pH1N1 strains. Figure 6b summarizes the reported reassortment events that match the clade of the 115 pH1N1 strains (with at least 90% of the strains shared between them) among the 28 pairwise comparisons between segments. The two triangular sections of the central matrix display the reassortment identification results for the pH1N1 clade from both tools. Darker cells indicate segment pairs inferred to have different origins, while lighter cells indicate no reassortment events for the pH1N1 strains reported by either tool.

The two tools reported consistent reassortment patterns for the pH1N1 clade across 25 segment pairs and different patterns across 3 segment pairs. Among the 25 segment pairs with consistent results, the inferred reassortment events occurred between segments NA/M and the remaining six segments. Previous studies have shown that the NA and M segments (highlighted in blue) were derived from the Eurasian swine lineage, whereas the other six segments (highlighted in green) originated from the North American swine lineage [17]. Therefore, the reassortment patterns of the pH1N1 clade commonly identified by both tools are consistent with previous findings. For the three segment pairs with discrepant results, two pairs (M–NP and M–PB1, highlighted in red boxes) were identified by VReassort as involving reassortant segments within the pH1N1 clade, whereas one pair (HA–NP, highlighted in a black box) was indicated by GiRaF to include reassortant segments. Notably, only the two segment pairs reported by VReassort contain segments originating from different lineages, consistent with true reassortment. Thus, VReassort correctly inferred the reassortment relationships among these three segment pairs, whereas GiRaF missed two true cases of reassortment and incorrectly reported one spurious case. Based on the identification results for the pH1N1 clade across all 28 segment pairs, we constructed two segment-origin networks (Fig. 6b) in which nodes represent segments and edges connect segments identified as sharing the same origin by each tool. The left network (by VReassort) exhibits a perfect pattern showing two separated segment components from two origins, whereas the right network (GiRaF) includes two false connections and omits one true connection. Consequently, VReassort not only identified the pH1N1 strains as reassortant but also accurately detected the reassortment patterns between segments. This experiment demonstrates the capability of VReassort in identifying reassortant strains forming large clades, further aiding the surveillance of well-adapted and widespread reassortant strains of IAVs.

Application of VReassort in mining reassortment profiles of IAV

IAVs have a large family of lineages due to rapid evolution through multiple mechanisms [8]. While reassortment significantly amplifies the diversity of IAV strains, this increased diversity can potentially enhance strain fitness, posing challenges to global health [17, 50]. Previous studies have indicated that the reassortment of IAVs is influenced by multiple factors, including subtypes and hosts [22, 51, 52]. While some studies have focused on reassortment patterns on data with specific subtypes or lineages [35, 50, 53], we aimed to investigate the reassortments among various of strains by including a broader range of strains with diverse subtypes and hosts. We downloaded all complete genome sequences (comprising 8 segments) of IAVs collected between 2010 and 2023 from NCBI (accessed on 10 March 2025), resulting in 11 367 genomes. After filtering highly similar (>99.5%) sequences using CD-HIT, we retained 8829 genomes spanning diverse hosts, subtypes, and geographic regions. Since only 17 genome sequences were available for 2023, we excluded them from further analysis. As reassortant strains and the lineages of their parental strains are likely sampled within a short time frame, many strains in the complete dataset are irrelevant to the reassortant strains. Therefore, instead of applying VReassort to the entire dataset, we constructed temporal datasets by extracting sequences using a sliding three-year window (e.g. 2010–2012, 2011–2013), resulting in 11 datasets with ~1704 strains per dataset. While other studies [37] use a one-year time frame to identify reassortant strains, we opted for a three-year window to increase the likelihood of including more related strains as references. For each dataset, we applied VReassort to sequences of all pairwise combinations of genome segments (28 in total). Within each dataset, we focused on identifying emerging reassortment events in strains from the most recent year, while taking strains from the preceding two years as the reference. This approach yielded reassortment identification results for strains from 2012 to 2022. Each strain has 28 identification results indicating whether any two segment pairs are derived from different parental strains through reassortment. Based on this, we can determine which segments share the same origin, with reassortant strains exhibiting multiple segment clusters from different origins (See Supplementary Section S6 and Supplementary Figs S21 and S22 for the clustering). Among the 7304 strains (2010–2022), 2336 were identified as containing more than one segment clusters (indicating multiple origins or parental strains) and were thus classified as reassortant strains for downstream analysis.

Influenza A virus exhibits heterogeneous reassortment patterns across hosts and subtypes.

As different strains of IAVs can infect various hosts, including humans, swine, avian species, and others, we investigated the relationship between reassortment and host types. Figure 7a illustrates the strain counts across various hosts. Most strains are associated with avian (51.6%), swine (32.5%), and human (12.4%) hosts, while only a small proportion infect other organisms (see central pie chart). Among the three dominant hosts, swine strains exhibit a significantly larger proportion (61.7%) of identified reassortant strains, nearly double the second-largest proportion found in human strains (30.9%). This high proportion of identified reassortant strains aligns with the observation that swine serve as mixed vessels, capable of being infected by multiple types of IAV strains [54]. Although avian strains comprise the largest number in the dataset, their reassortant proportion is much smaller (14.3%) compared to that of swine and human strains. Furthermore, we observed stable yearly reassortant proportions with various hosts by calculating the identified reassortant strains with specific hosts across the year (see Fig. 7b).

More than 40% of strains in these two subtypes were identified as reassortant, with swine being the significantly dominant hosts for the reassortant strains. The four subtypes H9N2, H5N1, H5N2, and H7N9 have similar medium reassortant proportions (~22%). Among the seven subtypes, those associated with avian influenza (e.g. H9N2, H5N1, H5N6, H7N9) [55–58] show higher reassortant proportions compared to subtypes like H4N6 and H3N8, which have limited or no impact on human health. Therefore, monitoring the reassortment of strains in these subtypes is beneficial for controlling zoonotic infections and mitigating pandemic potential.

Segment exchanges in the reassortment of influenza A virus are not random

Although the mechanism of packing segments from multiple parental strains during reassortment is not fully understood [60], several studies have revealed that the segment exchanges in reassortant strains are not random. This non-randomness is influenced by multiple factors, including molecular interactions between segments and variations among parental strains [10, 22, 52, 61–63]. Thus, we investigated the exchange patterns between the eight segments in the identified reassortant strains. Figure 8a shows the 20 most frequent segment reassortment patterns. Most reassortant strains were generated from two parental strains, with a smaller proportion estimated to originate from three parental strains. The higher frequency of different colors between HA/NA and other segments indicates that the HA and NA segments are more likely to originate from distinct sources compared to other segments, leading to a greater number of heterogeneous (different origins) segment pairs between HA/NA and the other segments, as shown in Fig. 8b. One potential factor is that the HA and NA segments are essential for viral transmission and infection [63]. Reassortant strains involving these segments are more likely to acquire advantageous properties that enhance their retention or spread in the population. Another factor is that interactions among certain internal genes can constrain reassortment because of co–adaptation requirements [21]. As a result, the observed exchange rate involving HA/NA segments appears higher. However, we observed a much smaller proportion of heterogeneous segments between the HA and NA segments, suggesting that reassortant strains tend to pack HA and NA segments from the same origin. Previous studies [63, 64, 65] have indicated that HA and NA are interdependent and require a functional balance, aligning with our findings regarding the HA and NA segments in IAVs. Furthermore, the three RNA polymerase segments, PB2, PB1, and PA, are responsible for viral replication and transcription [66]. Previous studies have found that their exchange in reassortant strains is less frequent because of the compatibility requirements between them [21, 61]. The significantly lower heterogeneous proportions between these three segments among the identified reassortant strains, as shown in Fig. 8b, support these findings and further demonstrate the effectiveness of VRreassort in identifying reassortant strains for reassortment analysis.

Application of VReassort to identify reassortant strains of another segmented virus (Human Rotavirus A)

Given the health significance and largely available data, we applied VReassort to the IAV for reassortment analysis. In this section, we demonstrate the utility of VReassort to other segmented viruses. Human Rotavirus A, a segmented virus responsible for severe dehydrating gastroenteritis in young children [67], serves as an example. It consists of 11 genome segments, with the VP4 and VP7 segments encoding structural proteins critical to the virus’s infectivity and immune response, while also defining its genotypes. Due to the limited availability of rotavirus sequences, particularly complete 11-segment genomes, we applied VReassort to the VP4 and VP7 segments of 160 strains, primarily collected in Japan and China between 2009 and 2022. VReassort identified several reassortant strains, including a few equivocal ones with scores near 0.5. Without knowing the ground truth, we repeated the experiment 100 times and reported a reassortant strain only if its score exceeded 0.495 in 80% of the repeated experiments and its average score was above 0.55. The purpose of this is to reduce the inherent noise from the multiple sequence alignment and tree construction process.

Figure 9a shows the distribution of repeats reporting various numbers of reassortant strains with scores exceeding the cutoff. The distribution resembles a normal distribution, with the majority of repeats identifying ~25 reassortant strains, while a few report fewer (e.g. 15) or more (e.g. 35). These differences arise because some strains have scores near the cutoff, as shown in Fig. 9b. While the average scores across 100 repeats were insufficient to clearly distinguish suspicious reassortant strains, the ratio of repeats in which each strain exceeds the cutoff (0.495) provided a more distinct boundary, as shown in Fig. 9c. By considering both, we identified 15 reassortant strains with higher confidence. These 15 strains originate from 6 distinct reassortant events, as highlighted in the two phylogenetic trees (visualized by iTOL [68]) in Fig. 9d. The 15 strains all exhibit significantly different positions between the two segment trees, suggesting they originated from reassortment events between the two segments. Some of the reported reassortant strains (blue and purple) have long branches in the phylogenetic trees, as they belong to different genotypes. However, due to the large divergence, these long branches may introduce noise in sequence alignment and tree construction, raising the possibility that these strains or clades are not true reassortants. Nevertheless, VReassort reported these long-branch strains because they exhibit significant differences and are easily distinguishable in the trees.

While this rotavirus experiment demonstrates the applicability of VReassort to other segmented viruses, the lack of ground–truth labels limits evaluation to inspection of phylogenetic trees. To address this, we simulated 500 datasets using the VP4 segment phylogenies and applied VReassort to these simulated data (see Supplementary Fig. S18). Although VReassort was trained exclusively on IAV data, it achieved high precision (0.841), recall (0.800), and accuracy (0.960) in identifying reassortant strains, including those with low reassortant distances, in the rotavirus datasets. Most missed detections involved cases with very small reassortant distances, consistent with the observations for IAV. These results further confirm the robustness and transferability of VReassort to other segmented viruses.

Discussion

This study presents VReassort, an automated tool for identifying reassortant strains through pairwise comparisons of viral genome segments. By combining structural information and patristic distances from phylogenetic trees, VReassort leverages a Siamese network trained on simulated datasets to detect reassortment-induced discordance, ensuring accurate and efficient identification across varying strain numbers. Evaluations on simulated and real sequencing datasets demonstrated VReassort’s strong generalization ability to detect reassortant strains across diverse scenarios.

While VReassort can identify most reassortant strains from genome sequencing data with robust performance, reassortment detection remains challenging. When parental strains are highly similar, it becomes difficult to distinguish subtle discordance caused by reassortment from noise introduced by factors such as sequencing quality, multiple sequence alignment, and phylogenetic inference. To achieve high-confidence calls while maximizing the detection of reassortment events between closely related parental strains with weak signals, performing multiple repetitions is an effective strategy. This approach is feasible due to VReassort’s efficiency, as demonstrated in the rotavirus experiment.

Beyond cases involving similar parental strains, VReassort may also fail to detect reassortments when the dataset contains only a limited number of parental or closely related strains. Because VReassort infers reassortments by analyzing relationships among a set of strains, the presence of appropriate reference strains is essential. When such references are missing, reassortant strains may exhibit large weights to their neighboring strains in both segments, which can lead to missed detections. For example, if the dataset includes reassortant strains from the H3N2 subtype but most other strains belong to a distant subtype such as H5N1, VReassort will lack the necessary context to identify segment-specific discordance. In addition, when closely related reference strains are lacking, the use of distant reference strains may sometimes introduce bias, leading to false reassortment detection. Given these limitations, careful dataset design becomes especially important. Including additional reference strains can improve sensitivity, but adding too many strains may increase complexity and introduce noise into the phylogenetic trees. To reduce irrelevance and noise, it is advisable to focus on strains collected within an appropriate time window, as older or distantly related strains may not meaningfully aid reassortment inference.

In addition to sequencing data, metadata such as host species, genotypes, geographic locations, and sequencing times, can provide valuable context for analyzing discordance between genome segments, creating additional opportunities for reassortment identification. Users can incorporate this metadata to further examine identified reassortments. Moreover, we plan to integrate these metadata-driven insights into future developments of VReassort.
