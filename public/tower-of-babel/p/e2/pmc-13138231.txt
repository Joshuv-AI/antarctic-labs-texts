Bidirectional effect modification between diurnal temperature range and particulate matter on acute myocardial infarction risk
Authors: Yuansong Zhuang, Yakun Zhao, Yuxiong Chen, Yitao Han, Siqi Tang, Chenglin Li, Jinyan Lei, Yanbo Liu, Zhongjie Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBoth ambient particulate matter and temperature variability influence acute myocardial infarction (AMI) risk. However, epidemiological evidence on whether diurnal temperature range (DTR) and particulate matter mutually modify each other’s cardiovascular effects remains limited. We conducted a time-stratified case-crossover study including 149,632 AMI hospitalizations in Beijing, China, from 2013 to 2019. Our results showed that short-term exposure to PM2.5 and PM10 increased AMI hospitalization risk, while DTR had a nonlinear U-shaped association, with higher risks at both low and high DTR levels. Interaction analysis revealed that the effect of PM2.5 was stronger under low DTR, while the impact of PM10 was more significant under medium to high DTR. The effect of DTR was amplified at lower levels of particulate matter but diminished at higher pollution levels. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating both temperature variability and air pollution into cardiovascular risk assessments and early warning systems.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical specialty, cardiovascular medicine, public health

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains one of the leading causes of mortality and disability worldwide, placing a heavy burden on public health and medical systems.1 Beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors, accumulating evidence suggests that environmental exposures play an important role in triggering acute coronary events.2,3 Particulate matter (PM), including PM2.5 and PM10, a major component of ambient air pollution, has been shown to increase the risk of AMI through multiple biological pathways, including the induction of systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and autonomic nervous system imbalance.4,5 Consistent findings from large-scale time-series and case-crossover studies across multiple countries have demonstrated a significant positive association between short-term particulate matter exposure and AMI-related hospital admissions.6

In addition to air pollution, temperature is also recognized as an important environmental determinant of cardiovascular events. Diurnal temperature range (DTR), defined as the difference between the daily maximum and minimum temperatures, reflects short-term temperature variability and thermal instability. Compared with absolute temperature, DTR more effectively captures the physiological stress imposed by pronounced day-night temperature fluctuations.7,8,9,10,11 Previous studies have suggested that larger DTR may increase the risk of AMI by disrupting thermoregulatory processes, enhancing sympathetic nervous system activity, exacerbating blood pressure variability, and inducing endothelial dysfunction.12,13 These physiological responses collectively elevate myocardial oxygen demand and promote thrombus formation.12,13

Importantly, particulate matter and temperature variability activate several overlapping biological pathways, including autonomic dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and prothrombotic responses. Such shared biological mechanisms suggest that their joint effects may not be simply additive. Large diurnal temperature fluctuations may alter respiratory patterns, airway caliber, and particle deposition efficiency, thereby modifying the toxicokinetics and acute toxicity of inhaled particles. Conversely, particulate matter exposure may impair vascular stability and thermoregulatory capacity, potentially amplifying the cardiovascular stress induced by rapid temperature changes.14,15 If such interaction exists, reliance on single-exposure models may underestimate AMI risk under specific environmental combinations, particularly during periods characterized by both high pollution levels and pronounced temperature variability. Given that individuals are simultaneously exposed to multiple environmental stressors in real-world settings, identifying high-risk composite exposure patterns is critical for accurate risk assessment and targeted prevention strategies.

Despite this biological plausibility, epidemiological evidence regarding potential bidirectional effect modification between DTR and particulate matter remains limited. Most prior studies examining temperature-pollution interactions have focused on daily mean temperature,16 which does not adequately reflect intraday thermal variability. Whether DTR modifies the short-term association between particulate matter and AMI, and whether particulate matter reciprocally modifies the DTR-AMI relationship, has not been systematically investigated.

Therefore, we conducted a time-stratified case-crossover study among patients hospitalized for AMI in Beijing from 2013 to 2019. This study aimed to evaluate the short-term independent effects of DTR and ambient particulate matter on AMI hospitalization risk and to further investigate whether DTR modifies the particulate matter-AMI association, as well as whether particulate matter reciprocally modifies the DTR-AMI relationship. By explicitly characterizing these bidirectional interactions, we seek to provide more precise evidence for environmental cardiovascular risk assessment and to inform coordinated prevention strategies targeting co-occurring meteorological and pollution exposures.

Results

Descriptive results

From 2013 to 2019, a total of 149,632 patients hospitalized for AMI were included in this study. Among them, 69.31% were male and 53.73% were aged ≥65 years. Slightly more AMI admissions occurred during the cold season (52.99%) than during the warm season (47.01%) (Table 1). Independent effects

Independent effect analysis showed significant associations between PM2.5 and PM10 exposure and the risk of AMI hospitalization (Table 3). DTR exhibited a nonlinear association with AMI hospitalization risk (Figure S4). Using the DTR corresponding to the minimum morbidity risk (MMDTR = 9.0°C) as the reference, low DTR (4.5°C) was significantly associated with an increased risk of AMI hospitalization (RR = 1.159, 95% CI: 1.092–1.230). High DTR (16.9°C) was also associated with an elevated risk (RR = 1.054, 95% CI: 1.025–1.083).

Linear modification results

Figure 2 illustrates the bidirectional effect modification between particulate matter and DTR. As shown in Figures 2A and 2B, the association between PM2.5 and AMI risk gradually weakened as DTR increased; at very high DTR levels, the confidence intervals partially crossed the null value. In contrast, the association between PM10 and AMI risk gradually strengthened with increasing DTR and became more pronounced under medium to high DTR exposure.

In the reverse modification analysis examining the influence of particulate matter on DTR-related AMI risk (Figures 2C–2F), PM2.5 and PM10 showed similar modification patterns. Specifically, within the low to moderate concentration ranges of particulate matter, the risk associated with low DTR gradually increased, whereas it tended to decrease at higher particulate matter concentrations, with the peak effect occurring approximately within the medium concentration range. For high DTR relative to the MMDTR, the risk estimates increased with rising particulate matter concentrations and subsequently declined at extremely high pollution levels.

Categorical modification results

Figure 3 presents the bidirectional categorical effect modification between particulate matter and DTR. For PM2.5 (Figure 3A), the risk estimate in the medium DTR stratum (RR = 1.022, 95% CI: 1.016–1.027) was significantly higher than that in the high DTR stratum (RR = 1.014, 95% CI: 1.002–1.026; p = 0.031). No statistically significant difference was observed between the medium and low DTR strata (p = 0.474). For PM10 (Figure 3B), the risk estimate in the medium DTR stratum (RR = 1.022, 95% CI: 1.016–1.027) was significantly higher than that in the low DTR stratum (RR = 1.012, 95% CI: 1.003–1.022; p = 0.028), whereas the difference between the high and medium DTR strata was not statistically significant (p = 0.387).

In analyses examining particulate matter modification of DTR effects (Figures 3C–3F), the association between DTR and AMI hospitalization risk showed clear heterogeneity across different pollution backgrounds. For low DTR exposure (Figures 3C and 3D), PM2.5-stratified estimates largely overlapped across low, medium, and high concentration levels (RR approximately 1.17–1.18), with no significant differences observed for low vs. medium (p = 0.756) or high vs. medium (p = 0.428). In contrast, under PM10 stratification, the risk associated with low DTR was highest at moderate PM10 levels (RR = 1.228, 95% CI: 1.162–1.298), exceeding that at both low (RR = 1.195, 95% CI: 1.127–1.267) and high (RR = 1.131, 95% CI: 1.066–1.199) levels, presenting an inverted U-shaped pattern.

For high DTR exposure (Figures 3E and 3F), PM2.5 stratification indicated that the risk at high pollution levels (RR = 1.029, 95% CI: 1.003–1.055) was significantly lower than that at moderate pollution levels (RR = 1.046, 95% CI: 1.026–1.067; p = 0.045), while the difference between low and medium pollution levels was not statistically significant (p = 0.106). Similarly, under PM10 stratification, the risk estimate at moderate pollution levels (RR = 1.052, 95% CI: 1.032–1.073) was significantly higher than that at low pollution levels (RR = 1.030, 95% CI: 1.004–1.057; p = 0.014), whereas the difference between high and medium levels was not statistically significant (p = 0.176).

Subgroup analysis

As shown in Figures S5–S11 and Table S2, subgroup analyses revealed substantial heterogeneity in the effect modification of DTR on the association between particulate matter and AMI hospitalization risk across population subgroups. In analyses examining DTR modification of particulate matter effects, the modifying pattern of DTR on the PM2.5-AMI hospitalization risk association was generally consistent with the main analysis results among males and across age subgroups. However, in the female subgroup, no significant variation in AMI hospitalization risk was observed across different DTR levels. For PM10, results across sex subgroups were also consistent with the main analysis, showing a more pronounced risk estimate under medium-to-high DTR exposure conditions. In the subgroup aged <65 years, AMI hospitalization risk did not change significantly with varying DTR levels.

In reverse effect modification analyses assessing particulate matter modification of the DTR-AMI association, under low DTR exposure, variations in PM2.5 concentration were associated with significant differences in AMI risk only among females, with the risk at moderate PM2.5 levels being significantly higher than that at low levels (p = 0.049). In the remaining subgroups, the influence of PM2.5 concentration variation on AMI risk under low DTR conditions did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, under low DTR exposure, the effect of PM10 exhibited an inverted U-shaped pattern in all subgroups except males, with the highest AMI hospitalization risk observed at moderate PM10 levels.

Under high DTR exposure, the influence of PM2.5 concentration variation on AMI risk was primarily observed in the female and ≥65-year-old subgroups, manifested as a higher risk at the medium PM2.5 level compared to the low level. In other subgroups, AMI risk did not change significantly with variations in PM2.5 concentration. Similarly, under high DTR exposure, the effect of PM10 also displayed an inverted U-shaped pattern in the female and ≥65-year-old subgroups, indicating the highest AMI hospitalization risk at the medium PM10 level.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses showed that the bidirectional effect modification between particulate matter and DTR remained directionally consistent across different modeling specifications. In seasonal stratified analyses, the interaction patterns observed in both the cold and warm seasons were highly consistent with those in the main analysis, with no substantial changes in effect direction, indicating good robustness of the study findings (Figures S12–S25).

Discussion

To our knowledge, few studies have systematically evaluated the bidirectional effect modification between ambient particulate matter and DTR in relation to the risk of AMI hospitalization. Three main findings emerged from this study. First, exposure to PM2.5 and PM10 was positively associated with AMI hospitalization risk. The DTR corresponding to the minimum AMI hospitalization risk was 9.0°C; both low (4.5°C) and high (16.9°C) DTR were significantly associated with an elevated AMI risk. Second, DTR significantly modified the health effects of particulate matter: the adverse effect of PM2.5 was most pronounced under lower DTR conditions, whereas the effect of PM10 was higher within medium to high DTR ranges. Third, in the reverse modification analyses, the risk associated with low DTR increased gradually with particulate matter concentrations within low to moderate ranges and attenuated at higher concentrations, while the risk associated with high DTR tended to be more evident under moderate to relatively high pollution levels. Overall, these findings reveal a complex bidirectional interaction between particulate matter and temperature variability, suggesting that a single-exposure framework may be insufficient to fully explain the occurrence patterns of acute cardiovascular events in urban environments where air pollution levels remain high and meteorological fluctuations are significant. These findings provide evidence for risk prediction and health warning systems under multi-exposure scenarios.

In the analysis of independent effects, this study found that for each standard deviation increase in PM2.5 and PM10 concentration, the risk of AMI hospitalization increased by approximately 2.3% and 1.8%, respectively. A study from Taiwan, China, reported that each 10-μg/m3 increase in short-term exposure to PM2.5 and PM10 was associated with a 6.5% and 1.1% increase in AMI risk, respectively.17 A study conducted in Seoul, Korea, found that for each 10-μg/m3 increase in PM2.5 at lag 1 day, the AMI risk increased by about 1%.18 Similarly, a study from Belgium reported that each 10-μg/m3 increase in PM10 was significantly associated with an approximately 1.9% increase in AMI risk at lag 3 days.19 The effect estimates observed in our study differed from those reported in previous studies. Such differences may be related to variations in pollution levels and exposure distributions across study regions. In addition, particulate matter is a heterogeneous mixture, and its chemical composition may vary substantially across locations due to differences in emission sources and atmospheric processes,20 which may contribute to regional differences in health effect estimates. Differences in lag structures and heterogeneity in study populations may also partly explain the variation in effect estimates across studies. From a biological perspective, particulate matter may trigger AMI through multiple pathways.21 First, inhalation of particles can induce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, leading to endothelial dysfunction and destabilization of atherosclerotic plaques.22 Second, particulate matter exposure can activate the sympathetic nervous system and disrupt autonomic balance, resulting in reduced heart rate variability and increased myocardial oxygen demand.23 In addition, particulate matter exposure can increase blood viscosity and enhance coagulation activity, thereby accelerating thrombosis formation.24 These pathological processes may reduce the cardiovascular system’s tolerance to acute environmental stressors, making individuals with pre-existing cardiovascular disease more susceptible to acute events during periods of pollution fluctuation.

Furthermore, this study found that both low and high DTR were associated with increased AMI hospitalization risk, independent of mean temperature. Low DTR represents minimal day-night temperature variation. Although this pattern may superficially indicate meteorological stability, the observed association after adjustment for mean temperature suggests that limited diurnal variability itself may impose physiological stress. When temperatures remain relatively constant over a 24-h period, thermoregulatory mechanisms may remain continuously activated without the usual nocturnal recovery phase. Under warm conditions, the absence of nighttime cooling may prolong sympathetic activation and maintain elevated cardiac output.25 Under cold conditions, persistent vasoconstriction may occur without sufficient daytime relief.26 As a result, the cumulative physiological burden may contribute to cardiovascular fatigue, impaired nocturnal blood pressure dipping, and increased susceptibility to acute cardiovascular events.25,26

In contrast, high DTR reflects pronounced temperature fluctuations within a short period. Rapid shifts between warmer and cooler conditions require repeated vasoconstriction and vasodilation, which may lead to greater blood pressure and heart rate variability, enhanced sympathetic activation, and a higher likelihood of plaque rupture or coronary artery spasm in susceptible individuals.27 Larger temperature variability may also increase blood viscosity, oxygen consumption, and endothelial dysfunction, findings that are consistent with studies linking DTR to stroke and hypertension risk.28 The lag structures observed in our study provide further support for these mechanisms. The effect of low DTR was most evident over a cumulative lag of approximately 20 days, suggesting a gradual accumulation of physiological stress. In contrast, the effect of high DTR peaked at a shorter lag of about 4 days, which is consistent with an acute triggering process. Taken together, these findings suggest that low and high DTR may influence cardiovascular risk through different physiological pathways. Low DTR may exert its effect through sustained hemodynamic and autonomic load that accumulates over time, whereas high DTR may act as an immediate environmental stressor that precipitates acute coronary events in susceptible individuals.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting adverse cardiovascular effects of DTR. An early study from Shanghai reported that each 1°C increase in DTR at lag 2 days was associated with a 2%–3% increase in coronary heart disease mortality.13 Subsequent studies from northwest China observed a U-shaped association between DTR and cerebrovascular hospitalization, with a minimum morbidity DTR of approximately 9.2°C, and significantly elevated risks at both low and high DTR.29 Similar patterns have also been reported in rural populations in western China.12 Moreover, studies from Korea and other countries have shown that sharp temperature changes within or across days significantly increase emergency visits for AMI, highlighting temperature variability as an important meteorological trigger of acute coronary events.30

One of the key findings of this study is the significant bidirectional effect modification between particulate matter and DTR on AMI risk. From the perspective of DTR modifying particulate matter effects, the risk estimate of PM2.5 was significantly higher under low DTR conditions than under higher DTR exposure. Low DTR represents minimal day-night temperature variation and may lead to sustained thermoregulatory load and reduced physiological recovery over extended periods.31 This prolonged cardiovascular strain may create a physiological environment in which the cumulative systemic toxicity of PM2.5 becomes more evident. Fine particles can penetrate deeply into the alveoli and enter the circulation, inducing oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction.32,33 Under conditions where adaptive capacity is already compromised by sustained thermal stress, PM2.5-induced inflammatory and autonomic responses may accumulate, thereby increasing AMI risk.34 In contrast, larger temperature variability may induce acute autonomic instability and blood pressure fluctuations, which may dominate the physiological response and partially obscure the more gradual effects of PM2.5.35,36

Conversely, PM10 exhibited higher risk under medium to high DTR conditions. One possible explanation is that PM10 contains a higher proportion of coarse inorganic particles and dust, which exert stronger airway irritation and localized inflammatory effects.14,37 Previous studies have suggested that PM2.5 primarily impairs lung volume, whereas PM10 has a more pronounced impact on airway function.38 As a result, the health effects associated with PM10 may become more evident under conditions of greater temperature variability. Pronounced temperature fluctuations may exacerbate airway responses and local inflammation, thereby amplifying PM10-related cardiovascular stress.39 However, it is important to acknowledge that PM10 is a heterogeneous mixture containing both fine and coarse particles.40 In this study, we did not isolate the coarse fraction (PM10-2.5) as a separate exposure due to data limitations. Therefore, the observed modification pattern for PM10 may partially reflect the influence of its fine particle component. Future studies should distinguish between PM2.5 and PM10-2.5 to better elucidate the differential interaction patterns with temperature variability.

From the reverse perspective of particulate matter modifying DTR effects, we observed nonlinear changes in DTR-related risk across particulate matter concentration ranges. Under low DTR conditions, AMI risk increased with particulate matter concentrations within low to moderate ranges and then attenuated at higher concentrations. Under high DTR conditions, both PM2.5 and PM10 exhibited risk peaks at moderate to relatively high pollution levels, followed by attenuation or plateauing at higher concentrations. Similar nonlinear interaction patterns between air pollution and temperature have been reported in studies based on US Medicare cohorts and East Asian populations.41,42 Several explanations may account for this pattern. Moderate particulate matter exposure may amplify systemic inflammation and autonomic instability, thereby lowering physiological tolerance to temperature variability.43 At extremely high pollution levels, inflammatory responses may approach physiological saturation.44 In addition, behavioral avoidance may play a role. During severe pollution episodes, individuals may reduce outdoor activity or adopt protective behaviors, resulting in lower personal exposure than indicated by ambient monitoring data.45 This may partly explain the attenuation of risk observed at the highest concentration ranges.

Nevertheless, studies on interactions between DTR and particulate matter remain limited. The proposed mechanisms are based primarily on our empirical observations combined with existing epidemiological and toxicological evidence. Further investigations using large-scale cohorts and experimental models are warranted to validate the underlying biological mechanisms.

Regarding the sex-specific findings, previous studies have consistently reported higher sensitivity to particulate matter exposure among women compared with men.46,47,48 Such sex differences are commonly attributed to a combination of physiological, hormonal, and behavioral factors. However, it is noteworthy that within the context of DTR variation in this study, the risk of PM2.5 exposure on AMI hospitalization among women did not show significant variation across different DTR levels. One possible explanation is that women generally have a higher proportion of subcutaneous adipose tissue, which may alter thermoregulatory responses compared with men.49 Greater thermal insulation from subcutaneous fat may buffer temperature fluctuations, reducing the direct cardiovascular stress associated with DTR variation.50 This may help explain why the female subgroup did not display a modification pattern as pronounced as males in the analysis of DTR modifying the effect of PM2.5.

In contrast, PM10, as a coarser particulate fraction, predominantly deposits in the upper airways and tracheobronchial regions. Although it penetrates less deeply into the lower respiratory tract than PM2.5, PM10 may exert more direct physical irritation and induce localized inflammatory responses in the respiratory mucosa.38 Compared with PM2.5, whose health effects more strongly depend on systemic oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways following alveolar deposition and translocation, the adverse effects of PM10 may involve, to a greater extent, rapid pathways such as airway irritation-related neural reflexes (e.g., activation of pulmonary sensory nerves) and local inflammatory mediator release. Because oxidative stress appears to play a relatively smaller role in PM10-related pathophysiology,51 its health effects may be less influenced by thermoregulatory buffering mechanisms. Consequently, the association between PM10 exposure and AMI risk showed patterns similar to those observed in the main analysis across sexes, with stronger effects under moderate-to-high DTR conditions.

In the reverse effect modification analysis, women exhibited greater sensitivity than men. This may be related to a higher propensity for oxidative stress responses among women under particulate matter exposure, such that moderate to high pollution levels further amplify the adverse cardiovascular effects associated with low or high DTR.

Furthermore, age-related heterogeneity played an important role in effect modification. Among individuals aged 65 years and older, the effect modification patterns involving both PM2.5 and DTR were similar to those observed in the main analysis. This pattern may be explained by age-related declines in physiological adaptability. With aging, the deterioration of physiological functions, particularly the vulnerability of the cardiovascular system, impairs their ability to adapt to air pollution and temperature changes, thereby making them more susceptible to these combined environmental factors.52,53

The findings of this study have important implications for urban environmental health management. In the context of frequent air pollution and extreme meteorological events, risk assessment based on a single exposure is often insufficient to fully reflect actual health threats. A multi-exposure framework may more accurately identify high-risk periods and vulnerable populations. The bidirectional effect modification between particulate matter and DTR observed in this study suggests that particulate matter-related health risks are substantially influenced by temperature variability. These findings indicate that future air pollution early warning systems may benefit from incorporating indicators of temperature variability. In addition, the identification of women and older adults as susceptible subgroups supports the development of targeted public health interventions during high-risk periods.

This study has several notable strengths. First, the time-stratified case-crossover design effectively controlled for long-term trends and seasonal confounding, providing robust estimates of short-term exposure-health associations. Second, the use of DLNM allowed simultaneous characterization of nonlinear exposure-response relationships and lagged effects. Third, this study provides a systematic evaluation of bidirectional effect modification between particulate matter and DTR, with robustness confirmed using both continuous and categorical interaction approaches. Finally, multiple sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent effect directions across alternative modeling specifications, strengthening the credibility of the findings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated a bidirectional interaction between particulate matter and DTR on AMI risk. Specifically, the adverse effect of PM2.5 was more pronounced under lower DTR, whereas PM10-associated risk increased under higher DTR. Conversely, the effect of DTR was amplified under low-to-moderate pollution levels. These findings demonstrate that in real-world urban environments, the health effects of air pollution and temperature variability are interdependent. Health risk assessments based on a single environmental factor may be insufficient to identify the high-risk exposure combinations, leading to an underestimation of acute cardiovascular risk.

Limitations of the study

This study also has some limitations. First, exposure assessment relied on city-level daily average concentrations from fixed monitoring stations, which may not fully capture individual-level exposure variability and could introduce nondifferential exposure misclassification, potentially biasing effect estimates toward the null. In addition, particulate matter concentrations were measured using the beta attenuation method or tapered element oscillating microbalance. Although these monitoring techniques are widely used and highly correlated with the Federal Reference Method, previous studies have suggested that measurements obtained using the beta attenuation method may be slightly underestimated, and measurement uncertainties may vary across monitoring stations.54 Second, information on particulate matter chemical composition, size distribution, and sources was unavailable, limiting further exploration of component-specific interaction effects. In particular, coarse particle concentrations (PM10-2.5) were not available in the monitoring dataset, preventing separate evaluation of coarse particle effects. Third, DTR could not fully capture the duration of daytime maximum temperature or the timing of nighttime minimum temperature. Fourth, unmeasured factors such as lifestyle behaviors, outdoor activity patterns, and air conditioning use may have contributed to residual confounding. In addition, this study was conducted in a single megacity in northern China and the findings may not be fully generalizable to regions with different climatic conditions, pollution mixtures, or population characteristics. Finally, although multiple analytical approaches were used to assess effect modification, interaction analyses in observational studies should be interpreted with caution.

Experimental model and study participant details

Hospitalization data for all public and private medical institutions in Beijing from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2019, were obtained from the Beijing Municipal Health Commission Information Center (http://www.phic.org.cn/). To account for potential differences in baseline environmental exposures, the study population was restricted to permanent Beijing residents aged 20 years or older. De-identified patient information, including admission date, sex, age, discharge date, and discharge diagnosis, was extracted from the structured electronic medical record system. Patients with AMI were identified based on the primary discharge diagnosis using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes I21-I22.55 As the analysis was based on a daily-level time series and did not involve individual follow-up, multiple hospital admissions for the same patient during the study period were treated as independent events. Based on data availability, this study included 149,632 patients hospitalized for AMI between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2019; detailed distributions of age and sex are shown in Table 1. This work has received approval for research ethics from the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (approval number: S-K1097) and a proof of approval is available upon request.

Method details

Study area

Beijing (39°26′-41°03′ N, 115°25′-117°30′ E) is the capital of China, located in the northern part of the North China Plain and is one of the world’s largest megacities. According to data from the Beijing Municipal Bureau of Statistics (https://tjj.beijing.gov.cn/), the resident population ranged from approximately 21.3 to 21.9 million between 2013 and 2019. Beijing has a temperate monsoon climate with four distinct seasons, characterized by hot, rainy summers and cold, dry winters. As a rapidly industrialized city that has implemented extensive air quality control policies, Beijing has experienced improvements in air quality in recent years. However, during the study period (2013-2019), the city’s annual average PM2.5 concentration remained at a high level, ranging from 41 to 85 μg/m3, which significantly exceeded the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended annual guideline of 5 μg/m3.56

Data collection

Daily average concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, and O3 were obtained from 12 national air quality monitoring stations in Beijing operated by the China National Environmental Monitoring Center (details on station locations are provided in our previous studies56,57). These monitoring stations operate in accordance with the China National Environmental Air Quality Monitoring Specifications and employ standard reference methods for continuous automatic monitoring. Specifically, PM2.5 and PM10 were measured using the beta attenuation method or tapered element oscillating microbalance (TEOM) method, while gaseous pollutants were measured using standard methods, including chemiluminescence for NO2, ultraviolet fluorescence for SO2, and ultraviolet photometric methods for O3. The city-wide daily average concentration for each pollutant was calculated as the arithmetic mean of measurements from the 12 monitoring stations. Meteorological data (daily average temperature, daily maximum temperature, daily minimum temperature, and relative humidity) were obtained from the Beijing Southern Station operated by the Beijing Meteorological Bureau (station distribution details are available in our previous research58,59). The DTR was calculated as the daily maximum temperature minus the daily minimum temperature. Furthermore, information on public holidays during the study period was sourced from officially published holiday schedules. To ensure the quality and completeness of the exposure data, daily city-wide pollutant concentrations were calculated only when valid measurements were available from all 12 monitoring stations. If data from any monitoring station were missing on a given day, that day was treated as missing and excluded from the analysis. A similar data quality control procedure was applied to meteorological data. Days with incomplete meteorological observations were excluded from the analysis.

Study design

A time-stratified case-crossover design was employed in this study, combined with conditional quasi-Poisson generalized linear regression models and distributed lag non-linear models (DLNM), to assess the associations between ambient particulate matter, DTR, and the risk of AMI. By comparing exposure levels on case days with those on control days, this design effectively controls for time-invariant and slowly varying confounders, while accounting for seasonality, long-term trends, and day-of-week effects.60 Specifically, each case day (defined as the AMI admission date) was matched with all control days falling on the same day of the week within the same calendar month and year, thereby constructing a time-stratified case-crossover structure. The case-crossover design was implemented using conditional quasi-Poisson models, which are mathematically equivalent to conditional logistic regression while allowing for overdispersion.60

Quantification and statistical analysis

A two-stage analytical strategy was applied in this study. In the first stage, baseline models were constructed to evaluate the exposure-response relationships between individual environmental variables and AMI hospitalizations. Estimating these independent associations provides baseline risk estimates and establishes the basis for subsequent interaction analyses. In the second stage, the bidirectional modification effects of DTR and particulate matter on the association with AMI admissions were examined. Linear modification analysis was employed to explore trends within the exposure distribution, while categorical analysis facilitates more interpretable comparisons between exposure strata and enables formal statistical testing of differences between strata.

Descriptive statistics were performed for daily AMI hospital admissions, air pollutant concentrations (PM2.5, PM10, SO2, and NO2), and meteorological variables (daily maximum temperature, daily minimum temperature, daily mean temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed) over the study period. For each variable, the mean, standard deviation, minimum, median, interquartile range, and maximum values were calculated. To visualize temporal patterns, we used calendar heatmaps to depict daily AMI hospitalizations and time-series plots to illustrate environmental variables. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were used to assess collinearity among environmental variables.

Based on the time-stratified case-crossover design, conditional quasi-Poisson generalized linear regression models were fitted in combination with DLNM to model the lagged exposure-response relationships for environmental factors. In the baseline models, each environmental variable (PM2.5, PM10, or DTR) was introduced separately using a DLNM-based cross-basis function to assess its independent cumulative lagged effect on AMI hospitalizations.

Previous studies have suggested that the associations between particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10) and cardiovascular outcomes are approximately linear within typical exposure ranges.61,62 Therefore, the effects of PM2.5 and PM10 were expressed as cumulative lagged relative risks (RRs) associated with a one-standard-deviation increase in concentration. In contrast, the relationship between DTR and cardiovascular risk has often been reported to exhibit a U-shaped pattern.29,31 To capture the health effects associated with both sides of the exposure distribution, DTR effects were estimated relative to the minimum morbidity DTR (MMDTR), which was identified from the cumulative lag exposure–response curve as the DTR value associated with the lowest estimated risk. As no universally accepted thresholds exist for defining “low” and “high” DTR, extreme DTR levels were defined a priori using the tails of the exposure distribution.29,31 Specifically, low DTR was defined as the 5th percentile of the DTR distribution (4.5°C), whereas high DTR was defined as the 95th percentile (16.9°C).

According to previous literature and the physiological lag characteristics of cardiovascular effects,16,63,64 the maximum lag periods for each exposure were determined based on the observed lag structures in the distributed lag models (2 days for PM2.5, 2 days for PM10, 20 days for low DTR, and 4 days for high DTR; the selection process is detailed in the Method S1 and Figure S1). In the exposure-response dimension, linear functions were specified for PM2.5 and PM10, whereas DTR was modeled using a natural cubic spline with 3 degrees of freedom (df). In the lag dimension, all exposure variables were modeled using natural cubic splines with 3 df.

As shown in Figure S2, PM2.5 and PM10 exhibited strong correlation with each other but showed no substantial collinearity with the other covariates. To control for potential confounders, several covariates were included in the model: daily mean temperature (Tmean), relative humidity (RH), and wind speed (WS) were incorporated using natural cubic splines, while public holiday status was included as a categorical variable. The degrees of freedom for the spline terms were selected based on the minimum quasi-Akaike information criterion (Q-AIC), resulting in 4 df for Tmean and RH, and 3 df for WS.

To address this issue, we used a reparameterization approach in which the medium DTR stratum was specified as the reference group. To identify potentially susceptible populations, subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by sex (male, female) and age (<65 years, ≥65 years). Within each subgroup, the same modeling framework as in the main analysis was applied to estimate the cumulative lagged effects of PM2.5, PM10, and DTR, as well as their bidirectional effect modification patterns.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the bidirectional effect modification between particulate matter and DTR. First, multi-pollutant models were constructed by incorporating SO2, NO2 and O3 into the single-pollutant baseline models to evaluate the stability of the bidirectional effect modification.66,67 Second, alternative exposure-lag specifications were examined to determine whether the estimated effect modification was sensitive to assumptions regarding lag structure. Specifically, lag periods for particulate matter were set to 1 day and 3 days, while lag windows for high-DTR and low-DTR effects were adjusted to 2 and 6 days, and 18 and 22 days, respectively. Third, the smoothness of meteorological covariates was varied to assess the stability of the effect modification estimates with respect to model flexibility. The degrees of freedom for natural spline terms were set to 3 and 5 for daily mean temperature, 3 and 5 for relative humidity, and 2 and 4 for wind speed. Fourth, to assess whether the observed effect modification was disproportionately driven by extreme particulate matter concentrations, the 95th percentile of daily mean PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations over the entire study period was calculated, and observations exceeding this threshold were excluded. The bidirectional interaction models were then refitted using the truncated datasets. Fifth, to evaluate the robustness of the categorization strategy, PM was categorized using deciles instead of the 5-percentile intervals used in the main analysis. Sixth, to assess the potential influence of recurrent myocardial infarction cases on our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding cases coded as I22 (subsequent myocardial infarction) and retaining only I21 (first myocardial infarction) as the outcome. Finally, to evaluate the robustness of the effect modification across seasons, the year was divided into a cold season (October to March of the following year) and a warm season (April to September), and stratified models were fitted separately for comparison.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1). The primary R packages used included mgcv, dlnm, splines, ggplot2, and corrplot. Pollutant concentrations were uniformly reported in μg/m3. Effect estimates derived from conditional Poisson regression models were expressed as relative risks with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided tests, with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
