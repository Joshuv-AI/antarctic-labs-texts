Cytotoxic T cell recognition of α-synuclein drives pathogenic immune responses in multiple system atrophy
Authors: Jae-Seung Moon, Salvinaz I. Moutusy, Mengrui Zhang, Alain Ndayisaba, Diego Rodriguez, Daniel N. El Kodsi, Anastasia Kuzkina, Shady Younis, Shaghayegh Jahanbani, Laura S. van Dam, Ya’el Courtney, Adi Netanel, Orr Sharpe, Mitchell G. Miglis, Lawrence Steinman, Vikram Khurana, Fereshteh Jahanbani, William H. Robinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMultiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rapidly progressive synucleinopathy, yet the mechanisms linking α-synuclein pathology to neurodegeneration and the role of adaptive immunity in this process remain poorly understood. We used single-cell transcriptomics, flow cytometry, and functional assays to identify highly activated, inflammatory, and cytotoxic CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in MSA, including clonally expanded CD8+ T cells expressing brain-homing programs. These T cells directly recognized α-synuclein monomers and fibrils, acquiring potent effector functions. Postmortem MSA brains similarly contained cytotoxic and proinflammatory T cells. These findings reveal a mechanistic immune axis linking α-synuclein recognition to pathogenic T cell responses in MSA.

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a progressive, rare neurodegenerative disorder characterized by autonomic dysfunction, parkinsonism, cerebellar ataxia, and pyramidal signs, and is clinically classified into parkinsonian (MSA-P) and cerebellar (MSA-C) subtypes (1–4). As an α-synucleinopathy, like Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Lewy body dementia (LBD), MSA is defined by abnormal fibrillar α-synuclein (α-syn) aggregation primarily in oligodendroglia, forming glial cytoplasmic inclusions (GCI), a feature that distinguishes MSA pathology from the neuronal Lewy bodies observed in PD and LBD (5, 6). Despite these pathological features, only a few genetic or environmental risk factors have been identified, leaving the mechanisms of disease onset and progression unclear and making diagnosis and treatment particularly challenging (7–9). Recent integrative reviews have emphasized that immune dysregulation may represent a critical, yet incompletely understood, component of MSA pathogenesis (10).

Previous studies have underscored the pivotal contribution of the immune system to the pathophysiology of synucleinopathies, including PD and LBD (11–14). Recent neuropathological and transcriptomic studies in the synucleinopathies have shown that microglia become activated in response to α-syn pathology, upregulating proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNFα) and chemokines while losing homeostatic functions (15, 16). Similarly, astrocytes are found to accumulate modified α-syn species, exhibit reactive astrogliosis, and secrete factors that exacerbate synaptic dysfunction and promote neuronal loss (17). Furthermore, in PD brains, microglia and astrocytes exhibit increased antigen-presentation machinery, including MHC class I or class II molecules, or costimulatory markers, in conjunction with T cell infiltration or activation, indicating that glial-mediated T cell responses may play a role in disease pathophysiology (18–22). These findings support a broader model in which neuroinflammation and adaptive immune activation can actively contribute to neurodegeneration, rather than merely reflecting secondary responses to tissue injury (23).

The increased number of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells across these disorders reflects distinct patterns of immune activation in the blood and central nervous system (CNS) (24, 25). Notably, these T cell responses may be deleterious or, in some contexts, protective in shaping disease pathogenesis (26–31). In the case of MSA, evidence is more limited; however, studies using viral vector-based animal models and postmortem patient samples have demonstrated elevated neuroinflammatory responses and aberrant adaptive immune responses, consistent with observations in other neurodegenerative diseases (32–38). In a viral vector-based MSA rodent model that selectively overexpresses α-syn in oligodendrocytes (Olig001-SYN) (39), IFNγ has been shown to promote neuroinflammation and demyelination, accompanied by infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells into the CNS (40). These observations suggest that the adaptive immune system, particularly cytotoxic T cell responses, may play a key pathogenic role in MSA. In contrast, whether T cells in MSA exhibit comparable α-syn specificity, clonal expansion, or cytotoxic effector programs has not been systematically examined.

Genome-wide association studies have linked PD to immune haplotypes involving MHC class II alleles such as HLA-DRB5*01 or HLA-DRB1*15:01, and regulatory variants that enhance MHC class II expression (41, 42). Notably, Cebrián et al. further demonstrated that microglia activated by α-syn or neuromelanin can induce MHC class I expression on catecholaminergic neurons, making these neurons susceptible to CD8+ T cell–mediated degeneration (43). Furthermore, several studies have shown that T cells in PD recognize α-syn peptides in an MHC class I or II-dependent manner and exhibit autoimmune characteristics (44, 45). While recent work has shown that synucleinopathy patients, including some with MSA, can mount α-syn-specific T helper (Th) 17 responses to phosphorylated peptide pools, this study relied on bulk cytokine readouts and did not resolve clonal architecture, CD8+ T cell involvement, or responses to full-length and fibrillar α-syn species (46). Thus, whether T cells in MSA exhibit similar α-syn-specificity remains unknown.

Here, we combined single-cell transcriptomics, flow cytometry, and functional assays to profile peripheral T cells in MSA in comparison with those from healthy controls (HCs) and PD. We found that CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in MSA display highly activated and cytotoxic phenotypes, with GZMB+GNLY+ CD8+ T cells showing clonal expansion and upregulation of brain-homing programs. We further demonstrated that stimulation of MSA T cells with both α-syn monomers and preformed fibrils (PFF) induce proliferation, clonal expansion, and acquisition of cytotoxic features. Notably, cytotoxic and proinflammatory CD8+ T cells were also present within the brains of MSA patients. Together, our findings indicate that cytotoxic CD4+ and CD8+ T cells recognizing α-syn become activated and plausibly contribute to MSA pathogenesis and associated neurodegeneration.

Results

Single-Cell Transcriptomics Reveals Clonally Expanded Cytotoxic CD8+ T Cell Expressing Brain-homing Signatures in MSA.

To characterize peripheral T cell phenotype and functions in MSA blood, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), flow cytometry, and functional assays of T cells isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) of healthy controls (HCs), MSA, or PD patients (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Demographic and clinical information are shown in the Methods and SI Appendix, Table S1. We first performed single-cell RNA and T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire sequencing on T cells from the PBMCs of HCs (n = 5), MSA (n = 14), and PD (n = 5) patients (47). Based on canonical marker expression at both RNA and protein levels, we defined 9 distinct T cell clusters using unsupervised clustering, including CD4 naive, CD4 activated naive, CD4 memory-like naive, CD4 CM, CD4 GATA3+ EM, CD4 proliferating Treg, CD8 naive, CD8 GZMK+, and CD8 GZMB+GNLY+ (Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). The proportion of CD4 activated naive was reduced in MSA compared to both HCs and PD, whereas the frequency of CD4 CM T cells was increased (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). In addition, naive CD4+ T cells significantly decreased in both MSA and PD compared with HCs. Among CD8+ T cells, the frequency of GZMB+GNLY+ cluster was significantly higher in both MSA and PD when compared to HCs (Fig. 1C).

Pseudobulk gene analysis showed higher expression of cytotoxicity and activation associated genes, including GZMB, GNLY, and CD69 in T cells from MSA and PD compared with HCs (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). As an illustrative example, KLRD1 (CD94) was preferentially upregulated in the GZMB+GZMK+ CD8+ subset and elevated in MSA and PD versus HCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B), indicating that KLRD1 marks highly cytotoxic CD8+ T cell populations in these synucleinopathies and may serve as a candidate surface marker for future targeting studies. Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis of CD8+ T cells between MSA and HC, followed by gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, revealed upregulation of pathways related to cytotoxicity, immune effector, and inflammatory responses in MSA (Fig. 1E). Consistently, average cytotoxicity scores were significantly elevated in both MSA and PD compared with HCs (Fig. 1F). Together, these findings indicate that cytotoxic transcriptional programs are enhanced in CD8+ T cells in MSA and PD and are consistent with a potential contribution of cytotoxic T cells to disease-associated immune activation.

To further characterize their CNS-homing abilities in MSA, we next assessed the expression of chemokine receptors and integrins required for T cell trafficking into the CNS. MSA T cells exhibited elevated CNS-trafficking signature scores, comparable to PD and higher than HCs (Fig. 1G). Analysis of individual genes showed that the CD8 GZMB+GNLY+ cluster in MSA displayed the strongest migratory nature compared to HCs, with CCL4, CCL5, ITGAL (CD11a), ITGB2 (CD18), and CD99 upregulation in MSA (Fig. 1H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Of note, ITGA4, a major homing molecule in neuroinflammation (48), was elevated in CD8 GZMB+GNLY+ cells and CD8 GZMK+ T cells (Fig. 1H).

These findings suggest that cytotoxic CD8+ T cells in MSA as well as PD are not only transcriptionally primed for cytotoxic effector function but also for CNS trafficking, identifying them as a key population likely to drive neuroinflammation through target cell killing.

Cytotoxic GZMB+GNLY+ CD8+ T cells Are Clonally Expanded in MSA.

To investigate TCR clonal lineages in MSA peripheral blood T cells, we performed an integrated analysis of single-cell transcriptomic and TCR sequencing data. Paired TCRαβ sequences were used to define clonal sizes (X); large (X > 20), medium (20 > X ≧ 5), small (5 > X ≧ 2) expanded clones, or singletons (X = 1). We found that the overall proportion of clonally expanded T cells was highest in PD, intermediate in MSA, and lowest in HC (Fig. 2 A and B). While most CD4+ T cells, including naive and memory subsets, and CD8 naive were predominantly composed of singletons, expanded clones were enriched in the CD8 GZMB+GNLY+ and GZMK+ clusters (Fig. 2C), which were seen in brain homing ITGA4+ T cells (48). It implies that these cytotoxic effector T cells are antigen experienced. In addition, we found that clonally expanded T cells are not restricted to a single transcriptional state but are shared across multiple clusters (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), indicating dynamic transitions between functional subsets. The proportion of largely expanded GZMB+GNLY+ clones was substantially higher in MSA (~35% of total clones) compared to HCs (~15% of total clones), though lower than in PD (~50% of total clones) (Fig. 2D). A similar pattern was observed in the CD8 GZMK+, CD4 memory-like naive, and CD4 GATA3+ EM clusters (Fig. 2 E–G). Notably, T cells in PD, previously shown to recognize and respond to α-syn (44, 45), exhibited the greatest degree of clonal expansion, with MSA T cells showing intermediate expansion and HCs the lowest. These findings indicate that T cells, particularly cytotoxic subsets, are clonally expanded in both MSA and PD in a manner consistent with antigen-driven activation and a potential contribution to disease-associated immune activation.

Next, to explore transcriptomic profiling of clonally expanded T cells in MSA, we analyzed differentially expressed genes in expanded clones compared to singletons within MSA CD8+ T cells. We observed that clonally expanded CD8+ T cells express higher levels of cytotoxic mediators (GZMB, GNLY, PRF1) together with migration-associated molecules (CCL4, CCL5, CD99), whereas singletons predominantly expressed naive-associated markers such as CCR7, SELL, or TCF7 (Fig. 2H). Consistent with this, GO analysis of DEGs from expanded CD8+ clones revealed enrichment of pathways related to cell killing, cytotoxicity, and migratory functions (Fig. 2I).

Further, to examine the differentiation dynamics of CD8+ T cells in MSA, we performed trajectory analysis integrating pseudotime and transcriptional states. This analysis showed a developmental progression from naive CD8+ T cells through a GZMK+ intermediate state toward terminally differentiated GZMB+GNLY+ effector subsets (Fig. 2J). Along this trajectory, clonally expanded TCR lineages were enriched in the terminal GZMB+GNLY+ state (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), indicating that antigen-driven TCR clonal expansion accompanies acquisition of cytotoxic and migratory programs. Together, these findings support a model in which CD8+ T cells in MSA as well as PD undergo progressive activation and clonal expansion, giving rise to terminal effector subsets that exhibit transcriptional programs associated with tissue infiltration and target cell killing.

Flow Cytometric Validation of Activated, Cytotoxic, and Proinflammatory T Cell Phenotypes in MSA.

To validate our scRNA-seq results, we performed flow cytometry on PBMC from HCs, MSA, or PD patients. We found that the proportion of CD8+ T cells within CD3+ T cells was markedly elevated in MSA (35.79% ± 3.24) and PD (37.58% ± 6.09) compared with HCs (14.38% ± 1.86) (Fig. 3A), whereas the frequency of CD4+ T cells was similar across groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in MSA patients showed activated features, with increased expression of CD69 and the frequency of degranulation marker CD107a-expressing CD8+ T cells was also higher in MSA blood (Fig. 3 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). We next assessed the expression of cytotoxic mediators, granzyme B (GZMB), and granzyme K (GZMK). GZMK, which has been implicated in inflammatory responses and complement cleavage, was significantly elevated in PD and was modestly increased in MSA compared to HCs. Moreover, the proportion of CD8+ T cells expressing GZMB, as well as those coexpressing GZMB and GZMK, was increased in MSA and showed a higher trend in PD relative to HCs, as described in other autoimmune diseases. CD4+ T cells exhibited lower granzyme expression, with only GZMK+ cells showing increased frequency in MSA patients (Fig. 3 D–F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C–E). Perforin, which directly mediates target cell killing, was most highly expressed in PD, while in MSA its expression was elevated compared to HCs but did not have statistical significance (Fig. 3G). We also found that the proportion of IL-17A+ CD4+ T cells, which are elevated in various autoimmune and neuroinflammatory diseases, was significantly increased in both MSA and PD compared to HCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). In addition, the frequency of peripheral helper T cells (Tph), which support B cell autoantibody production in various autoimmune diseases, was increased within both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell compartments in MSA compared to HCs, whereas PD values were intermediate and not significantly different from controls, suggesting autoantibody production may be involved in MSA pathogenesis similar to PD (Fig. 3H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). The frequency of circulating CD4+ follicular helper T cells (Tfh) also showed an increasing trend in MSA and PD compared to HCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S6H).

Next, we compared memory T cell subsets, including naive (N), central memory (CM), effector memory (EM), and effector memory CD45RA+ (EMRA) cells based on CCR7 and CD45RA expression. In CD8+ T cells, MSA patients exhibited a reduced proportion of naive cells and an increased frequency of EMRA cells compared to HCs, a pattern similar to that observed in PD (Fig. 3I). Further, naïve CD4+ T cells were significantly reduced, which is consistent with single-cell RNA seq analysis, and EM CD4+ T cells were markedly increased in MSA (SI Appendix, Fig. S6I). These findings indicate that MSA T cells display an activated effector memory phenotype, with CD8+ T cells skewed toward a highly cytotoxic state and CD4+ T cells toward an inflammatory function.

Given prior reports of impaired regulatory T cells (Treg) homeostasis and dysfunction in autoimmune and neuroinflammatory diseases (49, 50), we next assessed the frequency and function of Tregs. The frequency of CD4+ Tregs (FOXP3+CD25+) at the flow cytometry and scRNA-seq level, as well as the proportion of CD4+ T cells expressing coinhibitory molecules such as CTLA-4 and TIGIT, were comparable across MSA, PD, and HCs cohorts (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–D). By contrast, the frequency of KIR+ CD8+ Tregs, which have been implicated in the elimination of autoreactive CD4+ T cells (51), was markedly elevated in MSA patients compared to HCs, whereas PD patients showed intermediate levels (Fig. 3J). Functional suppression assays further demonstrated that MSA CD4+ Tregs (CD25+CD127low) failed to suppress effector T cell proliferation, although they retained the ability to dampen effector T cell activation (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E and F). Consistent with this functional impairment, scRNA-seq analysis revealed an increased proportion of IFNG-expressing inflammatory Tregs within the CD4+FOXP3+ compartment in MSA compared to HCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 G and H). This effector-like Treg phenotype has been described in other autoimmune and neuroinflammatory diseases and is associated with loss of suppressive capacity (52, 53). These findings indicate that although circulating Treg numbers are preserved in MSA, their functional state is altered toward a proinflammatory program, suggesting that strategies aimed at restoring functional Tregs or adoptive functional Treg transfer therapy may hold therapeutic potential in MSA.

Cytotoxic T Cell Responses to α-synuclein in Patients With MSA.

Previous studies have demonstrated that T cells from patients with PD recognize α-syn-derived peptides, supporting the nature of autoimmune reactivity in synucleinopathies (44, 45, 54). Given MSA is also defined as α-synucleinopathy, we aimed to determine whether activation and clonal expansion of T cells in MSA could similarly reflect recognition of the autoantigen α-syn. To this end, we stimulated 8 MSA PBMCs with α-syn monomers, α-syn preformed fibrils (PFFs), or β-syn monomers to assess T cell activation and clonal expansion (Fig. 4A). We also added anti-HLA class I and II antibodies to block HLA-mediated antigen presentation of α-syn. Stimulation of α-syn monomers increased the proportion of CD69+ and GZMB+IFNγ+ populations among both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells from MSA blood compared to no treatment, while β-syn monomers did not significantly induce T cell activation (Fig. 4 B–E). This activation was substantially reduced when anti-HLA class I and II antibodies were added, indicating MHC-dependent recognition of α-syn. In contrast, T cells from healthy PBMCs did not exhibit α-syn reactivity (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A-D).

We next used pooled α-syn PFFs prepared from recombinant α-syn and templated upon α-syn seeds present in postmortem brains of 3 MSA patients, which recapitulate the pathogenic fibrillar species observed in synucleinopathies, to assess disease-relevant antigenic stimulation. We showed the frequencies of CD69+, GZMB+IFNγ+, or IFNγ+ populations in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in MSA PBMCs were increased in response to stimulation of α-syn PFFs, and this activation was revoked by anti-HLA class I/II blockade (Fig. 4 F–I), mirroring the HLA-dependent effects observed with α-syn monomers. Together, our findings suggest MSA T cells also exhibit α-syn reactivity, similar to PD, suggesting that autoimmune responses against α-syn may contribute to CNS neuroinflammation in MSA.

To further characterize α-syn-reactive T cells in MSA, we performed integrated single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing of PBMCs stimulated with α-syn with or without anti-HLA class I/II antibodies. To assess T cell proliferation and TCR clonal expansion, we stimulated MSA PBMCs from the same donors used in Fig. 1 with α-syn for 3 d and compared transcriptomic and TCR clonality profiles. Unsupervised clustering identified 9 distinct clusters, based on key T cell subset markers, encompassing CD4 naive, CD4 EM, CD4 type I IFN responsive, CD4 proliferating, CD4 TNF+ or GATA3+ inflammatory, CD8 naive, CD8 cytotoxic, and CD8 proliferating cytotoxic (Fig. 5 A and B). In MSA samples, α-syn stimulation markedly increased the proportion of CD4 GATA3+ inflammatory, CD4+ proliferating, and CD8+ proliferating cytotoxic clusters relative to baseline, whereas blockade with anti-HLA class I/II antibodies substantially reduced these populations (Fig. 5C). Conversely, the frequencies of CD4+ naive, CD4+ effector memory, and CD8+ naive subsets were reduced following α-syn stimulation. In contrast, HCs displayed some differences in cluster proportions between baseline and α-syn stimulation; however, very little or no differences were observed between α-syn and α-syn with HLA class I/II blockade (Fig. 5C). This suggests that the observed changes in HC were likely due to the effects of prolonged in vitro culture rather than α-syn specific stimulation. Although α-syn–stimulated cells were compared with ex vivo baseline samples rather than parallel 3-day unstimulated cultures, the dependence on HLA class I/II and the lack of analogous changes in HC cultures support a substantial α-syn–specific component. Gene score analysis further revealed that cytotoxicity and proliferation signatures were elevated in α-syn stimulated T cells compared to baseline, and blockade of HLA class I/II abrogated these α-syn mediated responses (Fig. 5 D and E). We further compared the expression of key markers of naive (SELL, CCR7), activation (HLA-DR, IL2RA), cytotoxic (GZMB, GNLY, IFNG), and proliferative states (MKI67) across the three groups. Compared to baseline, α-syn stimulation increased the expression of activation, cytotoxic, and proliferation markers, while this increase was attenuated by HLA class I/II blockade, while expression of naive markers was reduced following α-syn stimulation (Fig. 5F). We further observed α-syn stimulation robustly induced upregulation of IFNG and TBX21 in CD4+ T cells from MSA patients compared to baseline, whereas CD4+ T cells from HCs exhibited no transcriptional response (Fig. 5G). These data are consistent with the in vitro stimulation assay in Fig. 4C, indicating α-syn-specific immune activation in MSA includes proinflammatory CD4+ T cells. Together, these data indicate that T cells respond to α-syn by acquiring cytotoxic, proinflammatory, and proliferative gene programs in an antigen-presentation-dependent manner.

To determine whether α-syn stimulation induces clonal expansion of T cells, we analyzed integrated data of transcriptome and TCR repertoire. Analysis of the top 20 clonally expanded TCR lineages showed that α-syn stimulation markedly increased clonal expansion compared to baseline, whereas HLA class I/II blockade inhibited the expansion (Fig. 5H). Among these top 20 clonotypes, one of the most strongly α-syn–responsive clones (TRAC_TRAV5_TRAJ6_TRBC2_TRBV19_TRBJ2-1) exhibited a striking expansion following α-syn stimulation. Notably, approximately 30% of this clone mapped to the CD8 cytotoxic cluster, and nearly 70% localized to the CD8 proliferating-cytotoxic cluster, indicating that α-syn not only activates cytotoxic CD8+ T cells but also drives their proliferative expansion (Fig. 5I). These findings suggest that MSA T cells undergo α-syn-driven clonal expansion through HLA-dependent TCR recognition, and that expanded clones preferentially express cytotoxic programs with the potential to drive neuroinflammation.

Infiltration of Cytotoxic and Proinflammatory T Cells in MSA Brain.

Previous studies have reported infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the brains of patients with neurodegenerative diseases, including MSA, where these cells are positioned to contribute to neuroinflammation (25, 27, 29, 31, 33, 55). In MSA, a recent neuropathological study has described T cells within cerebellar white matter lesions, particularly in early disease stages, suggesting that adaptive immune engagement within the CNS may be temporally dynamic (56). Building on these observations, we sought to determine whether brain-infiltrating T cells in MSA exhibit cytotoxic and proinflammatory effector phenotypes consistent with antigen-driven immune activation. Quantification of CD8+ T cell density revealed a higher number of CD8+ T cells in the parietal cortex of MSA brains compared with healthy controls (Fig. 6A). In addition, we detected CD8+ T cells in the parietal cortex of MSA brains expressing GZMB in combination with GZMK, consistent with a cytotoxic phenotype in MSA blood T cells, whereas CD8+ T cells in the same region of control postmortem brains lacked expression of both GZMB and GZMK (Fig. 6 B and C). In addition, subsets of CD8+ T cells expressing IFNγ were significantly increased in MSA brains compared to in HC brains, indicating a presence of proinflammatory population in MSA brain (Fig. 6 D and E). Together, these findings raise the possibility that α-syn-mediated activated CD8+ T cells migrate to the brain through brain-homing chemokine receptors and contribute to neuroinflammation.

Discussion

Despite extensive clinical investigation, no disease-modifying therapies are currently available for MSA, and patient management remains limited to symptomatic and supportive care. The repeated failure of clinical trials targeting neurodegeneration highlights the need for improved mechanistic insight, including a better understanding of immune contributions to disease pathogenesis (1, 3, 57, 58), and potential heterogeneity of disease processes among patients. Natural history studies for MSA in which biofluid samples are matched to patient and tracked through clinical stages and trials are warranted to better understand this heterogeneity (59). Neuroinflammation is a recognized hallmark of MSA, yet the contribution of cytotoxic T cells to this process, their activation mechanisms, and their role in disease progression remain poorly defined. Although MSA belongs to the spectrum of α-synucleinopathies, along with PD, the immunological distinctions between PD and MSA T cell responses have not been delineated. Here, we demonstrated that peripheral T cells in MSA are activated, with cytotoxic CD8+ T cells showing pronounced clonal expansion and upregulation CNS trafficking chemokine receptor program, comparable to PD, implicating them in CNS neuroinflammation. In vitro stimulation assays further demonstrated that both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells recognize and are activated in response to α-syn in an HLA-dependent manner, consistent with prior reports of α-syn-specific T cell responses in patients with PD. In concordance with these findings, we further detected CD8+ T cells expressing granzymes and proinflammatory cytokine in the postmortem MSA brain tissue. Taken together, these data support a model in which α-syn-reactive cytotoxic T cells contribute to neuroinflammatory responses in MSA and point to adaptive immune pathways as promising targets for therapeutic intervention.

Unlike neuronal Lewy bodies seen in PD, MSA is characterized by aberrant filamentous α-syn accumulation in oligodendrocytes, forming GCIs (5, 6). Beyond the better-known neuronal pathologies in synucleinopathies, neuroinflammation has emerged as a key contributor to disease progression operating through multiple and partly overlapping mechanisms (10). Aggregated α-syn triggers the release of inflammatory mediators such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), IL-1β, or TNFα, which have been associated with neuronal injury and activation of microglia as antigen-presenting cells, and are thought to promote CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in PD (15, 18, 20–22). Indeed, α-syn-specific T cells have been identified in PD, capable of recognizing epitopes derived from both native and fibril forms of α-syn (44, 45). Here, our data extend these observations to MSA by showing that α-syn-reactive T cells are also present in MSA, responding to both native and preformed fibrils of α-syn in an HLA class I- or class II-dependent manner. These findings indicate that, similar to PD, α-syn in MSA aberrantly engages the adaptive immune system, driving autoreactive T cell responses that may contribute to disease pathogenesis. Notably, disease-specific differences in T cell responses between PD and MSA may be further influenced by distinct α-syn conformations present in each disorder. Previous work using protein misfolding cyclic amplification has demonstrated that MSA-derived α-syn adopts structurally distinct and more cytotoxic conformations compared to PD-derived α-syn (60), raising the possibility that strain specific α-syn species may differentially shape immune recognition and downstream T cell activation. Although α-syn was the dominant antigenic driver of T cell activation, we observed that β-synuclein, another synuclein isoform, elicited minimal T cell reactivity, with only a few patient samples showing low-level reactivity. This finding is consistent with previous reports describing pathogenic β-synuclein-specific T responses in multiple sclerosis (MS) (61) and raises the possibility that β-synuclein reactive T cells may also be present in MSA. However, the relevance of such responses in MSA remains unclear and will require further investigation, particularly in comparison to the more prominent α-syn-reactive T cell responses. Further, a limitation of this study is that we did not investigate a potential role for anti-myelin or other anti-CNS T cell responses. In future studies it will be informative to investigate T cell reactivity to myelin and CNS antigens beyond α-syn, including potential myelin-reactive T cell responses given the demyelination and CNS inflammation observed in MSA (10). In the present study, our analyses were focused on α-synuclein specific T cell responses to address a defined mechanistic question.

In addition to cytotoxic T cell activation, our data point to dysregulation within the regulatory T cell compartment in MSA. We observed an expansion of KIR+ CD8+ T cells, a population that has been reported to exert immunoregulatory functions in other inflammatory contexts (51). In contrast, the frequency of FOXP3+, TIGIT+, and CTLA-4+ CD4+ T cells was comparable across healthy controls, MSA, and PD, suggesting that the overall frequency of conventional CD4+ Tregs is preserved. However, functional suppression assays revealed that CD4+ Tregs from MSA patients exhibited reduced suppressive capacity, suggesting that regulatory dysfunction in MSA reflects impaired function rather than altered abundance. Consistent with this functional impairment, transcriptomic analysis further revealed an increased proportion of IFNG-expressing inflammatory Tregs within the CD4+FOXP3+ compartment in MSA. This effector-like Treg phenotype has been described in other autoimmune and neuroinflammatory diseases and is associated with loss of suppressive function (52, 53), providing a potential mechanistic link between preserved Treg numbers and impaired regulatory function. Together, these findings support a model in which dysregulated regulatory networks, rather than deficiency of Tregs, contribute to immune imbalance in MSA, providing a conceptual rationale for therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing regulatory T cell function, as has been explored in other immune-mediated diseases (e.g., low-dose IL-2-based or nasal anti-CD3 antibody approaches) (23).

Tissue-resident T cells are increasingly recognized as key effectors of tissue damage in both autoimmune and neurodegenerative diseases (27, 62, 63). Previous studies have shown CD8+ T cell accumulation in the putamen and substantia nigra of MSA brains, and experimental models using viral vectors to overexpress α-syn in oligodendrocytes have demonstrated that CD4+ T cells can drive demyelination and neuroinflammation (32, 33). Infiltrating T cells in this model also produced IFNγ, supporting a proinflammatory role (40). However, the contribution of CD8+ T cells remain less clear, with evidence from various diseases suggesting both pathogenic and protective functions. In this study, we demonstrated that peripheral CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in MSA are more activated and clonally expanded than in HCs, and that cytotoxic CD8+ T cell clusters exhibit high expression of brain-homing genes including ITGA4 (48). Clonally expanded CD8+ TCR lineages were enriched in a terminal GZMB+GNLY+ state and expressed ITGA4, consistent with an effector population capable of tissue trafficking and cytotoxicity. α-syn stimulation further increased the frequency of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells coexpressing GZMB and IFNγ, indicating that α-syn-reactive T cells can acquire cytotoxic and proinflammatory effector programs within the CNS. Importantly, quantitative analysis revealed an increased density of CD8+ T cells in the parietal cortex of MSA brains compared with controls, which do not have a neurological disorder. Further, immunostaining of brain samples supported the presence of GZMB+/GZMK+ and IFNγ+ CD8+ T cells in the MSA cortex. Although we demonstrated anti-α-syn IFNγ+ CD4+ T cell responses in the peripheral blood of MSA patients (Figs. 4 C and H and 5G), we did not perform CD4+ T cell immunostaining in the MSA brain tissue samples. Prior studies have reported the presence of CD4+ T cells in human MSA brain tissue as well as infiltration of IFNγ+ CD4+ T cells in α-syn overexpression models of MSA (33, 40). Further work will be needed to define the relative contributions of activated CD4+ and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells to CNS neuroinflammation and tissue injury in MSA. Although these histologic data are limited by sample number, they are consistent with the idea that α-syn-reactive T cells primed in the periphery may traffic to the CNS or be reactivated locally by brain resident antigen-presenting cells, thereby amplifying neuroinflammatory processes in MSA.

Although our study was not powered to definitively resolve immune differences between MSA and PD, several readouts suggest that MSA may exhibit partially distinct T cell phenotypes. Our single-cell analysis revealed that MSA patients exhibits increased central memory CD4+ T cells, suggesting chronic antigen exposure, alongside reduced activated naïve CD4+ T cells compared to PD patients. In contrast, PD patients showed lower naïve CD4+ T cells than MSA patients, and pronounced clonal expansion of cytotoxic CD8+ (GZMB+GNLY+, GZMK+) and CD4+ memory-like subsets (naïve, and CD4 GATA3+ effector memory). In flow cytometric analysis, we found that increases in IL-17A+ CD4+ T cells, a more pronounced shift toward CD4 effector memory, and higher frequencies of Tph cells were most evident in MSA compared with healthy controls, with PD often showing intermediate or less marked changes. KIR+ CD8+ T cells were also increased in MSA relative to controls, indicating additional remodeling of regulatory/cytotoxic CD8+ T cell compartments, although their net functional impact (pathogenic, protective, or context-dependent) remains unclear. These observations highlight the need for larger, stratified cohorts to determine which T cell features are shared across synucleinopathies and which may be relatively enriched or specific to MSA.

Our study has several limitations. First, the observed differences in T cell profiles between PD and MSA may be due to variations in sample size, duration of disease, or other cohort-specific factors. In addition, our analysis was largely restricted to peripheral blood T cells, and while we identified clonally expanded and activated phenotypes, it remains unclear whether these immune programs are mirrored in T cells infiltrating the CNS or present in cerebrospinal fluid. Therefore, in order to confirm disease-specific immune changes, these results should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger, well-matched cohorts and ideally longitudinal studies integrating blood, CSF, and brain tissue with clinicopathologic correlation to define which T cell features track most closely with disease stage and progression. Second, because MSA brain tissue can only be obtained postmortem, we were able to demonstrate the presence of cytotoxic and proinflammatory CD8+ T cells in situ but could not directly show whether α-syn is presented by brain resident antigen-presenting cells such as oligodendrocytes, nor whether autoreactive T cells directly mediate neuroinflammation in vivo. Future studies using ex vivo systems such as iPSC-derived neural cultures or organoid-based MSA brain models (10, 64) will be required to define whether T cells act through direct cytotoxicity or via secreted mediators to drive neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. Finally, given the challenges of obtaining truly pathology-free, age-matched control brains in this age range, our control brains lacked a primary neurodegenerative diagnosis but may still harbor age-related vascular or other comorbid changes, which we consider an inherent limitation of the study.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that T cells in MSA are aberrantly activated and clonally expanded, with cytotoxic CD8+ T cells showing enhanced brain-homing potential and the capacity to mediate proinflammatory and tissue-damaging responses upon recognition of α-syn. These autoreactive T cells are detectable in the periphery and present within MSA brain tissue, supporting their potential involvement in MSA-associated neuroinflammation. While causality and antigen presentation pathways remain to be fully delineated, our findings highlight autoreactive T cells and their upstream antigenic drivers as attractive candidates for further mechanistic study and as possible targets for immune-modulating therapies aimed at modifying disease course in MSA. These findings highlight a potential central role for autoreactive T cells in MSA pathogenesis and support the possibility of T cell–directed therapeutic strategies as a potential approach for disease modification.

Methods

Human Subject Research.

The aim of this study was to investigate whether cytotoxic CD8+ or CD4+ T cells contribute to the pathogenesis of MSA, including whether i) cytotoxic T cells target α-synuclein in MSA and ii) these cytotoxic T cells have the potential to mediate brain tissue damage in MSA, with comparisons made to PD. Human samples were obtained following written informed consent under protocols approved by the institutional review boards (IRBs) of Stanford University, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, and Harvard Medical School prior to blood collection (IRB 2024P000677). Fresh-frozen MSA and control brain tissue samples were obtained from Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School (IRB 2020P002649). Demographics and clinical information of the samples used in this study are provided in SI Appendix, Table S1. Brigham and Women’s Hospital/Harvard Medical School samples were collected within the Harvard Biomarkers Study and MyTrial programs, as previously described (59).

Sample Preparation and Immune Cell Isolation.

Whole blood was collected in heparin blood collection tubes (BD Biosciences). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated via Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (VWR International) using Leucosep tubes (Greiner Bio-One) for all experiments in this study. Isolated PBMCs were cryopreserved in Recovery™ Cell Culture Freezing Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Human brain samples were obtained from Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School. Written informed consent for autopsy and research use was obtained from next of kin in accordance with institutional policies. Immediately following removal and sectioning at autopsy, all tissue sections were rapidly frozen by exposure to liquid nitrogen vapor, placed into airtight plastic freezer bags (6 × 4-inch or 15 × 12-inch Minigrip zipper bags; Fisher Scientific, catalog numbers 22310023 and 22310029), and stored at −80 °C until use. For the present study, frozen tissue blocks from the parietal cortex with known MSA pathology were cryosectioned at 10 µm and processed for immunofluorescence staining as described below. Formalin-fixed sections from matched regions were reviewed by a board-certified neuropathologist to confirm diagnostic features of MSA and to exclude copathology. For single-cell RNA sequencing, CD3+ T cells were isolated from PBMCs using the EasySep Human T Cell Isolation kit (Stem Cell Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

T Cell Stimulation Assay.

For the PBMC stimulation, cryopreserved PBMCs from 8 MSA patients and 5 HCs were thawed, and seeded into 96 round-bottom plates. Cells were preincubated with 10 μg/mL of Polymyxin B (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min and then incubated with 0.5 or 5 μg/mL of recombinant human α- or β-synuclein (Sigma Aldrich), 2.5 μg/ml of pooled preformed α-synuclein fibrils (PFF) from MSA patients, or anti-CD3 antibody (1 μg/ml, BD Biosciences) for 16 h (for monomer stimulation) or 72 h (for PFF stimulation) in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM, Thermo Fisher) containing 10% FBS (ATCC) and 1% glutamine (Gibco), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) together with 2 μg/mL of anti-CD28 antibody (BD Biosciences). To block the interaction of HLA class I/II complex and T cell receptor, anti-HLA-A, B, C antibody and anti-HLA-DR, DP, DQ antibody (BioLegend, 1 μg/mL) was added with each antigen. In the last 5 h, eBioscience™ Protein Transport Inhibitor Cocktail (500X, Thermo Fisher) was added to detect expression of intracellular molecules. After 16 or 72 h of culture, the cells were intracellularly stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies and detected with BD LSR Fortessa flow cytometer.

Single-Cell Whole Transcriptome and TCR Repertoire Sequencing.

For single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing for baseline samples, CD3+ T cells were isolated from PBMCs of healthy controls (n = 5), MSA patients (n = 14), and PD patients (n = 5) using the EasySep Human T cell isolation kit and processed following the 10X Genomics protocol. Prior to loading, CD3+ T cells were stained with Human TruStain FcX, CITE-seq antibodies (SI Appendix, Table S3) for surface protein detection, and TotalSeq-C Hashtags (BioLegend) to enable sample multiplexing. For scRNA/TCR-seq of CD3+ T cells derived from stimulation assays, PBMCs from MSA patients were cultured under the same conditions described above, and single-cell suspensions were prepared, counted, stained, and loaded onto the Chromium Controller (10X Genomics). Libraries for gene expression and TCR-seq were prepared using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ v2 (Dual Index) with Feature Barcode technology (10X Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Pooled cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and raw data were processed with Cell Ranger (v9.0.0) and aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome.

Bioinformatic Analysis of scRNA and scTCR-seq Data.

Downstream analysis of scRNA-seq data was carried out in R through multiple Bioconductor (65, 66) packages. Hashtag libraries were demultiplexed with DropletUtils (67) (v1.26.0), and datasets were integrated with scMerge (68) and stored as a SingleCellExperiment (66) (v1.28.0) object. Quality control was performed using the median absolute deviation (MAD)-based outlier detection (Scuttle (69) v1.16.0), with exclusion of cells containing >10% mitochondrial transcripts, <800 or >50,000 UMIs, or <500 or >9,000 detected genes. UMI count matrices were normalized and log-transformed with a deconvolution strategy (scran (70) v1.34.0). PCA was conducted on the top 1,500 highly variable genes, and the first 50 PCs were used for UMAP visualization. Batch correction and integration were performed using Harmony (71) (v1.2.3). Clustering was performed on a k-nearest neighbor graph (k = 30) using the Louvain (72) algorithm. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were defined by CD4 or CD8A expression and reclustered. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were calculated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction (q < 0.05). Clusters were annotated manually based on canonical markers and DEGs. Gene module scores for cytotoxicity, brain trafficking, proliferation, inflammation, costimulation-related signatures were computed with UCell (73) (v2.10.1) with the gene sets in SI Appendix, Table S4. Visualizations including UMAP plots, heatmaps, and kernel density plots were generated with dittoSeq (74) (v1.18.0) and Nebulosa (75) (v1.16.0).

For TCR repertoire analysis, contigs were annotated with Cell Ranger vdj, and clonotypes were defined by unique paired TCRαβ gene usage. Clonal expansion was quantified by the frequency of each clonotype (X) and categorized as Large-expanded clones (X > 20), Medium-expanded clones (20>X ≧5), Small expanded clones (5 > X ≧ 2), and Singletons (X = 1). TCR clonotype information was integrated with gene expression clusters to assess transcriptional features of expanded populations. Alluvial plots were generated to visualize the top 20 expanded clonotypes across samples. Circular chord diagram was generated by first calculating interconnected clonotypes across different cell types. Then visualized using chordDiagram in circlize (76) (v0.4.16).

Immunofluorescence Staining.

For detection of T cells in MSA brain, fresh-frozen brain tissues from MSA patients and healthy controls were cryosectioned at a thickness of 10 µm using a cryostat and mounted onto glass slides. Sections were air-dried, fixed in cold acetone for 10 min, and rinsed with PBS. For blocking, sections were incubated with 5% normal goat serum in TBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies against CD8, GZMB, GZMK, or IFNγ were applied to sections in the blocking media and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. Primary antibodies in this study include 5 μg/mL of CD8a monoclonal antibody (clone AMC908, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5 μg/mL of GZMK polyclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5 μg/mL of GZMB monoclonal antibody (clone 496B, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5 μg/mL of IFNγ polyclonal antibody (R&D Systems). After PBS washes, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, including anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody-Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500), anti-Rat IgG (H + L) secondary antibody-Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500), or anti-Goat IgG (H + L) secondary antibody-Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500), were added for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were mounted with Vectashield mounting media with DAPI (Vector Laboratories) for nuclear counterstaining. Confocal imaging was performed on a Nikon confocal microscope with identical laser power and acquisition settings applied to all samples. T cell densities were quantified in 3 cortical sections per case. For each section, nonoverlapping fields were imaged using a Nikon confocal microscope with a 20× objective under identical acquisition settings. CD8+ T cells were counted on maximum intensity projection images using ImageJ. T cell density (cells/mm2) was calculated by dividing the number of positive cells by the measured area for each field, and values from multiple fields and sections were averaged per case. Images were processed and analyzed using IMARIS software (Oxford Instruments) for cell quantification and colocalization analyses.

Statistical Analysis.

GraphPad Prism v10 or R v4.41 were used for statistical analyses. Data are represented as means ± SEM. Specific statistical tests for each figure are indicated in the respective figure legends.
