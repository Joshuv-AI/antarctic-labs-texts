Right Atrial Pressure Estimation Using Point-of-Care Ultrasound of the Superior Vena Cava in the Cardiovascular Intensive Care Unit: A Case Series
Authors: Govind Pandompatam, Brandon Wiley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

The estimation of right atrial pressure (RAP) is a vital component of the hemodynamic assessment of cardiovascular intensive care unit (CVICU) patients. The most frequently used point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) method for RAP assessment involves the size and collapsibility of the inferior vena cava (IVC). However, sonographic evaluation of the IVC has limitations and cannot accurately estimate RAP in mechanically ventilated patients.1,2 Hepatic vein (HV) Doppler interrogation improves RAP estimation during mechanical ventilation; however, in the CVICU, postoperative dressings and drainage catheters often obscure the subcostal and transhepatic imaging planes. Superior vena cava (SVC) spectral Doppler waveforms can accurately estimate RAP;3 however, the technique is not commonly used in POCUS. Here we discuss 4 clinical examples of how SVC Doppler waveforms obtained with POCUS can help guide clinical management in postoperative CVICU patients.

Case Presentation 1

A 66-year-old man underwent bilateral lung transplant for interstitial lung disease. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography showed normal biventricular function. The patient was admitted to the CVICU postoperatively, intubated, and sedated. Vasopressin infusion was required to maintain adequate mean arterial pressure. Bronchoscopy showed thin, frothy secretions, suggestive of pulmonary edema. A pulmonary artery catheter placed during the operation was malpositioned, and invasive hemodynamics were not immediately available. Therefore, POCUS was performed, which demonstrated normal biventricular systolic function, trace tricuspid regurgitation (TR), and no other hemodynamically significant valvular disease (Video 1). The IVC measured 2.1 cm with minimal respirophasic variation on volume control mechanical ventilation. Hepatic vein and SVC spectral Doppler waveforms demonstrated systolic predominant flow consistent with normal to low RAP (Figure 1). Given the Doppler findings, diuresis was held in the immediate postoperative period. The pulmonary artery catheter was eventually repositioned, and RAP was 3 mm Hg. The patient was gently resuscitated and subsequently weaned off vasopressor therapy. The patient was eventually extubated on postoperative day 3 with an uncomplicated postoperative course.

Case Presentation 2

A 70-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy (left ventricular [LV] ejection fraction 15%) and prior percutaneous coronary interventions presented with non-ST elevation acute coronary syndrome. Coronary angiogram demonstrated in-stent restenosis of the left anterior descending artery, and percutaneous coronary intervention was performed with supplemental percutaneous mechanical circulatory support. Postprocedurally, the patient developed progressive cardiogenic shock and pulmonary edema. The microaxial mechanical circulatory support device was surgically repositioned into the right axillary artery for increased hemodynamic support. The patient arrived to the CVICU postoperatively and was extubated shortly thereafter. POCUS on high-flow nasal canula showed severely reduced LV systolic function, moderate right ventricular (RV) systolic dysfunction, and mild-to-moderate TR (Video 2) with IVC dilation (2.8 cm). Superior vena cava and HV Doppler waveforms illustrated diastolic flow predominance consistent with high RAP (Figure 2). The transduced RAP via central venous catheter was 15 mm Hg. The patient was aggressively diuresed with consequent improvement in pulmonary edema and right heart congestion.

Case Presentation 3

A 45-year-old man with a durable LV assist device was found unresponsive and hypotensive at home during the late evening. Interrogation of his implantable cardioverter-defibrillator revealed 20 recent defibrillations for ventricular tachycardia. The patient was admitted to the CVICU, intubated, and sedated and was on multiple infusions of vasopressors and antiarrhythmic medications. POCUS was remarkable for flattening of the interventricular septum with concomitant severe RV enlargement and systolic dysfunction. There was severe TR (Video 3), and the IVC was dilated (2.2 cm) on volume control mechanical ventilation. SVC and HV spectral Doppler waveforms demonstrated systolic flow reversal (Figure 3). Telemetry revealed sequential atrial-ventricular pacing at the time of POCUS. Aggressive diuresis was initiated based on the POCUS findings. On hospital day 1, a pulmonary artery catheter was placed that confirmed elevated RAP (20 mm Hg). Over the next week, the patient was diuresed over 10 L, successfully extubated, and transitioned to oral antiarrhythmics.

Case Presentation 4

A 76-year-old man with a history of oxygen-dependent chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension was admitted with respiratory failure requiring endotracheal intubation. Postintubation he developed profound circulatory shock requiring multiple inotrope and vasopressor infusions. Inhaled epoprostenol was initiated for RV support. POCUS revealed severe RV dilation and systolic dysfunction as well as mild TR in limited subcostal imaging (Video 4). The IVC was dilated (2.5 cm) without significant respirophasic variability on volume control mechanical ventilation. SVC and HV spectral Doppler interrogation demonstrated systolic predominant flow with prominent atrial waves suggestive of RV diastolic dysfunction and elevated RV end-diastolic pressure (Figure 4). A pulmonary artery catheter was placed revealing suprasystemic pulmonary artery pressure of 124/38 mm Hg, and mean RAP was 8 mm Hg with prominent A waves.

Discussion

Noninvasive estimation of RAP via POCUS is commonly performed by measuring the diameter and respiratory variation of the IVC using the subcostal window. However, in the intensive care unit (ICU), this technique has limited utility as mechanical ventilation results in inaccurate RAP assessment via IVC ultrasound.4 HV Doppler can provide complementary insights into RAP, as HV systolic filling fraction (HV SFF) <55% is both sensitive and specific for elevated RAP and has been validated in mechanically ventilated patients.2,4 However, in the CVICU, subcostal imaging planes are often partially or completely obscured by surgical incisions, dressings, chest tubes, or bowel gas. Using the transhepatic imaging plane to measure the IVC offers one potential workaround; however, IVC diameter correlation between subcostal and transhepatic acoustic windows is suboptimal.5

Spectral Doppler of the SVC from the right supraclavicular approach is another option to estimate RAP noninvasively. It has the advantage of avoiding common sites of tubes and incisions. Spectral Doppler interrogation of the SVC is feasible in the majority of patients, even if detailed two-dimensional echocardiographic anatomic evaluation of the SVC is not always clear.6 Superior vena cava Doppler has been shown to accurately estimate RAP from both the supraclavicular and subcostal windows in non–critically ill patients.7 The normal SVC flow waveform begins at the beginning of systole with a prominent systolic wave below the baseline, followed by a smaller wave in diastole. Small ventricular (v) and atrial (a) waves can sometimes be seen as well, similar to HV spectral Doppler waveforms.6 When RAP is elevated (≥8 mm Hg), a predominant diastolic wave is seen, with a smaller systolic wave.3 A summary of common findings with SVC Doppler waveforms is detailed in Table 1.

Despite apparent clinical usefulness, using SVC spectral Doppler to estimate RAP has not been widely adopted in general POCUS practice, likely due to the perception that it is difficult and cumbersome. Currently, the assessment of the SVC remains specific to the echocardiography laboratory for diagnosis of pericardial disease. We propose a simplified method to obtain SVC Doppler from the right supraclavicular approach. In this technique, the patient is supine with the head turned slightly leftward. The practitioner obtains a short-axis view of the right internal jugular vein (RIJ) similar to that used for central venous catheterization. Color-flow Doppler is placed over the region of the RIJ with increased depth. The transducer is then tilted caudally to visualize the RIJ as it becomes a tributary of the right innominate vein, which then continues into the SVC (Figure 5). Characteristic laminar flow away from the transducer (blue flow) is identified via color-flow Doppler (Video 5), as detailed two-dimensional visualization of the SVC is often difficult. The sample gate is placed in this region approximately 5 to 7 cm from the transducer, and pulsed-wave Doppler is activated to obtain the corresponding spectral Doppler waveform.6 The technique allows relatively simple identification of the SVC by color-flow Doppler as most critical care practitioners are familiar with the RIJ as a sonographic starting point. With practice, we find that SVC Doppler can be obtained in this manner in the majority of CVICU patients, even in patients with RIJ central venous catheters in place. Broad adoption of SVC Doppler in critical care POCUS requires training; however, using the RIJ as a starting point decreases the initial learning curve for most practitioners. Interobserver variability for SVC measurements from the right supraclavicular window was shown to be adequate in one study.7

We routinely use POCUS-derived SVC and HV Doppler waveforms to estimate RAP, evaluate right heart function, and help guide resuscitation and diuresis in CVICU patients. These cases show that SVC spectral Doppler appears feasible despite common CVICU imaging obstacles including mechanical ventilation and the inability to ideally position critically ill patients. The cases described here demonstrate concordant relationships between SVC Doppler, HV Doppler, and invasive RAP measurements. The incremental data provided by SVC spectral Doppler guided our clinical assessments of volume status and right heart filling and function in these cases. When we see systolic predominant SVC and HV waveforms in our CVICU patients, they often have a RAP <8 mm Hg, and when diastolic predominance or systolic flow reversal is present, RAP is commonly >8 mm Hg. Certain disease states could potentially cause discordance between SVC and HV Doppler waveforms. For example, intra-abdominal hypertension, which is known to be highly prevalent in the ICU population,8 can cause compression of the IVC, creating inaccurate estimations of RAP via IVC and HV Doppler. In such cases, SVC Doppler may be more accurate for RAP estimation. Liver cirrhosis is another situation where HV Doppler may not estimate RAP accurately. Cirrhosis can result in uncoupling between HV Doppler and right heart filling, resulting in monophasic low-velocity flow.9 Evaluation of SVC Doppler in these patients may be more accurate to evaluate right heart filling pressures. In one study of non–critically ill patients, SVC systolic to diastolic wave ratio (SVC S/D) correlated with RAP with higher accuracy than HV SFF. An SVC S/D <1.9 showed a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 80% for elevated RAP, whereas HV SFF <55% demonstrated a sensitivity of 54% and specificity of 69%.10 The study, however, utilized a subcostal imaging plane to image the SVC, a different technique than the one described in this report.

Another parameter that can be used to estimate RAP noninvasively is Doppler assessment of the portal vein. The portal vein sits between the capillary network of the splanchnic circulation and the hepatic sinusoids,11 and portal vein pulsatility (PVP) can be used to assess venous hypertension noninvasively. Elevated PVP has been shown to correlate with elevated RAP12 and more recently was shown to have prognostic significance at 1 year in patients hospitalized with heart failure.11 Although Doppler evaluation of PVP may be less technically demanding to acquire compared with other Doppler techniques, limitations should be noted. Portal vein pulsatility may be inaccurate in assessing systemic venous congestion in patients with intrinsic liver disease, portal venous hypertension, and intra-abdominal hypertension, and increased pulsatility may be seen in young healthy adults.13, 14, 15 Combining PVP with IVC size, HV flow, and renal vein pulsatility in a comprehensive Doppler venous congestion exam has also gained recent interest. This exam showed correlation between congestion score and RAP in a study of hospitalized patients undergoing right heart catheterization.16 However, limitations of this approach should be noted as well, as intrarenal vein flow can be difficult to obtain in many patients and the effects of chronic kidney disease on renal waveforms remain unclear. The SVC, being an extra-abdominal conduit, may overcome some of these limitations and local factors that are present with assessment of splanchnic circulation Doppler waveforms. Ultimately, each patient scenario is unique, and attention should be paid to the strengths and weaknesses of each Doppler assessment when assessing right heart hemodynamics noninvasively.

Limitations of SVC spectral Doppler should be discussed. Although adequate signals are obtainable in most CVICU patients, there are some in which supraclavicular imaging is impossible, due to positioning constraints or poor acoustic windows. Another observation is that spectral Doppler SVC waveforms above the baseline (e.g., systolic flow reversal) have lower velocities and clarity in comparison to HV waveforms. This is seen as the tricuspid valve is farther away from the upper SVC where the velocity is sampled, in comparison to the HV.6 Respiratory variation can sometimes make waveforms difficult to analyze as well, and the use of a respirometer is encouraged, although it is not always available in the CVICU setting. Obtaining end-expiratory waveforms is ideal, and can be achieved with coached breathing, or if mechanically ventilated, by using a brief end-expiratory breath hold maneuver. Furthermore, it has recently been shown that HV SFF may not correlate well with central venous pressure and correlates better with RV systolic function in ICU patients.17 It is possible that a similar relationship may been seen with SVC spectral Doppler, but this requires further study. In our cases, SVC waveforms correlated reliably with RAP; however, these findings are hypothesis generating, as larger prospective cohorts are needed to confirm this correlation across various critically ill patient populations. Finally, SVC Doppler waveforms are characteristically affected by certain disease states including constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. In the presence of these conditions, spectral Doppler correlation with RAP should be employed with caution. Further study is necessary to delineate nuances of SVC Doppler waveforms and their ability to assess RAP accurately in CVICU patients.

Conclusion

Obtaining point-of-care SVC spectral Doppler waveforms is feasible in CVICU patients and can be helpful in estimating RAP and right heart hemodynamic data.
