Author Reply: Isolated Aripiprazole‐Associated Cervical Tremor‐Like Movement and Possible Dystonic Features
Authors: Ryuichi Minoda Sada, Saki Sada Minoda
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We thank Gaber et al. for commenting on our clinical image article describing isolated neck tremor after long‐term aripiprazole therapy [1, 2]. We agree that the video raises the possibility that the cervical movement was not a pure tremor, but a tremor‐like movement with dystonic features. Because the video was brief and because the patient was examined directly before and after drug withdrawal, we would like to add several bedside observations that informed our original description.

During the clinical assessment, simple head support against a wall did not clearly lessen the abnormal movement; therefore, we did not identify a clear alleviating maneuver or sensory trick. We also did not observe sustained abnormal cervical posture, torticollis, laterocollis, retrocollis, hypertonia, rigidity, bradykinesia, limb tremor, or other dystonic movements. The symptom resolved completely and rapidly after discontinuation of aripiprazole. This time course supported a drug‐related tardive phenomenon, but it was not typical of established tardive cervical dystonia, which may persist or evolve after exposure to dopamine receptor‐modulating agents.

We agree that cervical dystonia can include phasic contractions and may appear tremulous. The consensus definition describes dystonia as sustained or intermittent muscle contractions that cause abnormal, often repetitive, movements, postures, or both, and notes that dystonic movements may be tremulous [3]. The International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society tremor classification also recognizes overlap between tremor and dystonia, including dystonic tremor and tremor associated with dystonia [4]. In this sense, and without assuming that the video alone can settle the phenomenology, our case may be described retrospectively as an isolated aripiprazole‐associated cervical tremor‐like movement with possible dystonic features.

Even so, we would be cautious about reclassifying the case as definitive tardive cervical dystonia. The lack of a clear alleviating effect with simple head support against a wall, the absence of persistent dystonic posture or other dystonic manifestations, and rapid resolution after drug cessation leave uncertainty as to whether the movement represented dystonia itself, tremor associated with a tardive syndrome, or a transient drug‐related tremor‐like cervical movement. Our original description was based on the bedside appearance, the absence of other extrapyramidal signs, and the temporal relationship with aripiprazole withdrawal. We consider the key point to be the recognition of a delayed‐onset, isolated cervical involuntary movement after long‐term aripiprazole exposure, rather than a rigid distinction between tremor and dystonia.

Gaber et al. also point to a practical issue for generalists: apparent rhythmicity alone is insufficient when evaluating neck movements. In similar cases, clinicians should assess the direction, regularity, frequency, and amplitude of the movement; look for sustained or intermittent dystonic posturing; evaluate positional modulation and sensory tricks; and follow the course after drug withdrawal [2, 3, 4]. We therefore appreciate their refinement of the phenomenology of our case. Their interpretation adds an important educational perspective while preserving the central message of our report: even low‐dose, long‐term aripiprazole therapy can be associated with delayed, isolated cervical involuntary movements that may improve promptly after discontinuation of the suspected drug.
