Alterations in systemic and skeletal muscle myostatin regulation are most prominent at disease onset and associate with muscle-related outcomes in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies
Authors: Lucia Vernerová, Lucie Lážnovská, Jiří Baloun, Veronika Balounová, Martina Vokurková, Martin Klein, Zdeněk Verner, Tomáš Kičura, Heřman Mann, Sabína Oreská, Kristina Svobodová, Tereza Kropáčková, Michal Tomčík, Jozef Ukropec, Barbara Ukropcová, Jiří Vencovský
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent findings indicate attenuated myostatin (MSTN) signalling in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) and an inverse association between circulating MSTN and disease activity. The temporal and mechanistic relationship of these alterations to disease pathogenesis remains unclear. We therefore investigated systemic and skeletal muscle MSTN regulation in newly diagnosed patients at disease onset, after six months of therapy, and in chronic IIM.MethodsA total of 101 patients with IIM and 134 healthy controls (HC) were enrolled. Muscle biopsies were obtained from 59 newly diagnosed untreated or shortly treated patients, 19 patients after six months of therapy, 19 chronic IIM patients, and 21 HC. Serum samples and clinical data were collected during routine visits. Circulating MSTN, follistatin (FST), follistatin-like 3 (FSTL3) and activin A (ActA) were measured by ELISA. mRNA and protein expression of MSTN pathway components in muscle tissue were assessed by qPCR (TaqMan®) and immunoblotting. Associations of serum MSTN, FST, FSTL3 and ActA with clinical outcomes were analysed using linear mixed-effects models (LMM) and partial least squares regression during the first six months of therapy.ResultsNewly diagnosed IIM patients had significantly lower circulating MSTN and higher FST levels than chronic patients and HC, whereas ActA was elevated only in chronic disease. After normalization to body cell mass, MSTN remained reduced in newly diagnosed patients and increased after six months of therapy, while FST, ActA and FSTL3 did not change significantly. LMM analysis demonstrated strong associations of FSTL3 with systemic inflammation, disease activity and reduced physical and mental health scores, predominantly in female patients. In muscle tissue, newly diagnosed patients showed increased expression of MSTN pathway genes and proteins, including MSTN, SMAD2/3 and ubiquitin–proteasome components. Multivariate analyses integrating clinical, biochemical and molecular parameters identified a latent component linking MSTN pathway activation with muscle disease activity.ConclusionEarly IIM is characterized by dissociation between reduced circulating MSTN and activation of MSTN signalling in skeletal muscle, which diminishes with treatment and disease chronicity. Circulating FSTL3 is associated with systemic inflammation, disease activity and patient-reported outcomes, with pronounced associations in female patients. These findings highlight stage- and sex-dependent alterations of the MSTN regulatory network in IIM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13075-026-03847-1.Keywords: Inflammatory myopathies, Myostatin pathway, Muscle function

Introduction

Muscle weakness and fatigue, elevated serum muscle enzymes, mononuclear cell infiltration, and myofibre degeneration or fibrosis are key clinical and histological features shared by all subtypes of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) [1]. While initial immunosuppressive treatment effectively reduces inflammation in most patients, only a few fully regain their previous muscle function, and physical limitations often persist [2]. Therefore, beyond inflammation, non-immune factors such as endoplasmic reticulum stress [3], hypoxia [4], autophagy [5], mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction [6] also play a significant role in the loss and restoration of muscle function in IIM.

Skeletal muscle mass is regulated by the balance between protein synthesis and degradation. Myostatin (MSTN), primarily produced and secreted by skeletal muscle, functions locally in an autocrine or paracrine manner as a negative regulator of muscle homeostasis [7]. Inhibition or reduced expression of MSTN enhances muscle regeneration, whereas elevated MSTN levels contribute to muscle atrophy by suppressing protein synthesis and promoting ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation [8, 9]. On the surface of muscle cells, MSTN binds to the activin type IIB receptor, triggering the phosphorylation and activation of SMAD2 and SMAD3 proteins. These activated SMADs then translocate to the nucleus, where they regulate the transcription of muscle-specific ubiquitin ligases, such as atrogin-1 and MuRF1, activate the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS), and promote protein degradation [10]. Additionally, MSTN signalling counteracts the IGF-1/PI3K/AKT hypertrophic pathway by inhibiting AKT phosphorylation, which leads to increased activation of FOXO1 and subsequent upregulation of atrophy-related gene expression [8].

MSTN activity is detected in serum only after activation by acid, suggesting that native MSTN circulates as a latent complex. The majority (> 70%) of MSTN in serum is bound to its propeptide and the follistatin-related protein (also known as follistatin-like 3 protein - FSTL3 or FLRG) [11].

Within skeletal muscle, follistatin (FST) functions as a natural inhibitor of MSTN by blocking its receptor interaction and thereby limiting its biological activity [12]. Although FST has been tested as potential MSTN antagonist in inclusion body myositis (IBM) [13], the role of the endogenous protein remained underexplored. Mariot et al. [14] observed elevated FST in serum as well as in muscle of IBM and also in muscle wasting or atrophying diseases such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy. To date, quantitative assessments of FST in inflammatory myopathies other than IBM have been limited [13, 15, 16]. FST, by binding to and inhibiting activins, can potentially mitigate the inflammatory and immune responses associated with IIM by suppression of the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the development of certain immune cell types [17].

Lee et al. [18] demonstrated that FST regulates muscle mass through inhibition of multiple transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) family ligands, with genetic data implicating activin A (ActA) as an additional contributor to muscle growth restriction. ActA is rapidly mobilized into the circulation under inflammatory conditions stimulated by inflammatory mediators including interleukin-1, Toll-like receptor ligands such as lipopolysaccharide, and tumour necrosis factor (TNF) [19]. On the other hand, ActA exerts broad immunomodulatory effects by regulating pro-inflammatory cytokine release, promoting nitric oxide synthesis, and influencing the activity of multiple immune cell populations [20, 21].

FSTL3 is a secreted glycoprotein expressed by multiple tissues and involved in systemic metabolic regulation [22, 23]. FSTL3 contains two follistatin-like domains and similarly to FST, it can bind and inhibit both ActA and MSTN in vitro [11, 24]. Its role in MSTN regulation is further supported by evidence of FSTL3 forming detectable complexes with MSTN in both murine and human blood samples [18]. FSTL3 mRNA is increased in muscle of IIM patients [15], however, circulating FSTL3 has not yet been analysed in inflammatory myopathies.

IIM patients suffer from muscle weakness and muscle wasting, a condition in which increased pro-atrophic and decreased anabolic factors would be expected. Even though MSTN is considered a significant negative regulator of muscle mass growth, recent data showed reduced circulating MSTN accompanied by attenuation of its signalling in muscle tissue of chronic IIM patients compared to healthy individuals, together with increased levels of MSTN blocking FST. Moreover, the disturbance was more profound in patients with more active disease [15, 16]. We hypothesized that these effects may point to an existence of internal mechanisms aimed at reducing further loss of muscle proteins during myositis. However, MSTN alterations in IIM have not been examined longitudinally in different stages of myositis.

Therefore, in this study we analysed systemic and muscle-specific regulation of the MSTN network in newly diagnosed IIM patients at disease onset and after six months of pharmacological therapy, in comparison with chronic IIM and healthy donors. The primary aim was to determine whether alterations in MSTN biology are linked to early inflammatory muscle impairment or emerge secondarily with disease progression and treatment.

Methods

Study design and population

A total of 101 IIM patients, of those 39 patients with established disease (chronic IIM), 62 newly diagnosed patients (new IIM), and 134 healthy controls (HC) matched for age, sex, and body mass index were recruited. Peripheral blood sampling and clinical examinations were performed at each study visit. Newly diagnosed IIM patients were evaluated at diagnosis and after six months of standard-of-care pharmacological therapy. All IIM patients were recruited from inpatient and outpatient departments of the Institute of Rheumatology in Prague.

IIM diagnoses were established according to the 2017 EULAR/ACR classification criteria [25] and the ENMC criteria for immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) [26]. Patients were classified into myositis subgroups: antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS), dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), IMNM, mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) and overlap syndrome (PM/SSc). Patients with ASyS fulfilled Connors criteria [27], MCTD was classified according to Kasukawa criteria [28, 29], and overlap syndrome was defined as IIM fulfilling 2017 EULAR/ACR criteria together with another connective tissue disease fulfilling established classification criteria. Patients with IBM were excluded. All participants were ≥ 18 years of age and provided written informed consent. The study protocol and informed consent procedures were approved by the local ethics committee of the Institute of Rheumatology in Prague. To ensure a clear and biologically meaningful distinction between early and established disease stages, patients were stratified according to both disease duration and duration of immunosuppressive treatment. Newly diagnosed patients were defined as individuals with disease duration of less than 1 year from symptom onset and immunosuppressive treatment duration shorter than 3 months. Chronic patients were defined as individuals with disease duration exceeding 1 year and immunosuppressive treatment duration longer than 6 months. Patients with discordant disease and treatment duration characteristics that did not meet these subgroup definitions were excluded from subgroup analyses to minimize overlap between early and established disease stages.

Clinical disease activity was evaluated by the disease activity core set measures proposed by International Myositis Assessment & Clinical Studies Group (IMACS): myositis disease activity assessment (MYOACT), patient and physician global activity using visual analogue scales (VAS), myositis intention-to-treat activity index (MITAX) [30]. Physical functioning was evaluated by MMT-8 (manual muscle test scores of individual eight muscle groups), Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) [30], Functional Index-2 (FI-2) [31] and Medical Outcomes Study 36-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36: the physical component score (PCS) and the mental component score (MCS). SF-36 PCS and MCS were calculated using a correlated (oblique) factor scoring approach instead of the traditional orthogonal model in order to account for the interrelationship between physical and mental health domains [32]. Bioimpedance analysis (BIA) (Data Input GmbH, Germany) was used to assess body composition parameters including body cell mass (BCM), extracellular mass (ECM), the ECM/BCM index, fat mass, and total body water. Laboratory analyses of biochemical parameters for clinical practice (e.g. serum C-reactive protein (CRP) and markers of muscle impairment such as creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LD), myoglobin and other) were performed in an accredited laboratory of Clinical Biochemistry and Hematology of the Institute of Rheumatology using Beckman Coulter AU 680 (Beckman Coulter, USA), Roche Cobas e601 (Roche, Switzerland) and Liaison XL (Diasorin, Italy) analytical systems.

Muscle biopsy

Fifty nine newly diagnosed IIM patients underwent a standard diagnostic evaluation that included an open biopsy of the m. vastus lateralis muscle performed under local anesthesia, along with routine blood sampling. Nineteen of these patients underwent a second open biopsy six months later to evaluate disease progression and treatment effects. In addition, muscle biopsy samples were obtained from 19 patients with chronic IIM and 21 healthy controls (HC); in these groups, biopsies of the m. vastus lateralis were performed under local anesthesia using a Bergström needle. All muscle samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until RNA and protein extraction. Muscle biopsy samples for histopathological staining were immediately snap-frozen in isopentane cooled in liquid nitrogen. Serial cryosections were subjected to routine histological and histochemical staining, including hematoxylin and eosin, modified Gomori trichrome, NADH-tetrazolium reductase, and cytochrome c oxidase. Immunohistochemical analyses were performed to assess major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) expression and to characterize inflammatory infiltrates (CD3, CD4, CD8, CD68). In addition, selected specimens were examined by electron microscopy to evaluate ultrastructural features, including myofibrillar integrity, mitochondrial abnormalities, and the presence of inclusions or vacuolar changes. Biopsy specimens were evaluated for diagnostic features of inflammatory myopathies, including endomysial or perimysial inflammation, myofiber necrosis and regeneration, perifascicular atrophy, and structural abnormalities. The histopathological findings were used for diagnostic purposes only and were not further associated with mRNA and protein analyses of muscle tissue.

Autoantibodies detection

Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were determined by HEp-2 ANA Fluorescent Test System Indirect Immunofluorescence -(IMMUNOCONCEPTS, United States), and results were reported as titers and fluorescence patterns. Myositis-specific and myositis-associated autoantibodies were analysed using a line immunoassay (LIA) panels according to the manufacturer’s protocols: IMTEC Myositis LIA – (Human Diagnostica, Germany), antigens: Jo-1, Mi-2, PM/Scl, U1-snRNP, Ku, PL-7, PL-12; and Euroline Autoimmune Inflammatory Myopathies 20Ag (IgG) (Euroimmun, Germany), antigens: Mi-2 alfa, Mi-2 beta, TIF-1 gamma, MDA5, NXP2, SAE1, Ku, PM/Scl 100, PM/Scl 75, Jo-1, SRP, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ, Ro-52, cN-1 A, Ha, KS, Zo.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and multiplex immunoassay

Circulating levels of myostatin (Cat.# DGDF80), follistatin (Cat.# DFN00), activin A (Cat.# DAC00B), and FSTL3 (Cat.# DFRG0) were measured by ELISA using commercially available kits (R&D Systems, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Assays were performed from isolated serum samples stored at -80 °C until analysis. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader (SUNRISE, Tecan, Austria).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from muscle tissue collected during muscle biopsy using TRIzol (Ambion, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The RNA concentration and purity were assessed via spectrophotometry (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in a thermal cycler (MyCycler, Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on the QuantStudio 7 Flex RT-PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Data were analysed using the ΔCT method for relative quantification, expressed as ΔCt = [Ct(housekeeping gene) – Ct(gene of interest)]. Following an evaluation of four housekeeping genes (GAPDH, ACTB, RPL13, and B2M) using NormFinder software (MOMA, Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark), ACTB and RPL13 were selected as endogenous controls.

Immunoblotting

For the protein analysis, muscle tissues were homogenized in MagnaLyser tubes using a homogenizer (Roche, Germany) with the RIPA lysis and extraction buffer (Cat.# 23T1.3, Carl Roth GmbH + Co., Germany) which included protease/phosphatase inhibitors, and then centrifuged. The protein concentrations of the supernatants were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat.# 23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein from each muscle biopsy sample were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). After transferring the membranes and blocking with EveryBlot Blocking Buffer (Cat.# 12010020, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., USA), the membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies (1:1000) against MuRF1/TRIM63 (Cat.# 55456-1-AP, Proteintech Group, USA), Atrogin1/FBXO32 (Cat.# PA5-91959, Invitrogen, USA), Myostatin/GDP8 (Cat.# 19142-1-AP, Proteintech Group, USA), FOXO1 (Cat.# MA5-14846, Invitrogen, USA), pFOXO1 (Cat.# PA5-105996, Invitrogen, USA), SMAD2/3 (Cat.# 3102, Cell Signalling Technology, USA), pSMAD2/3 (Cat.# 8828, Cell Signalling Technology, USA), ACVR2B (Cat.# ab180185, Abcam, UK) at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were washed five times with Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST) buffer and then incubated with the secondary antibody anti-rabbit IgG (Cat.# 1706515, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., USA) for 1 h at room temperature and washed five times with TBST buffer. The protein signals were measured using Azur C300 (Azure Biosystems Pte. Ltd., Singapore), and the band density was quantified by densitometry using the Image Lab programming software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., USA). The expression of each target protein was normalized to Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as a loading control.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation for the 6-month follow-up was based on a power analysis for a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with one between-subject factor (group) and one within-subject factor (time). Assuming an effect size of 0.7–0.8, α = 0.05, and power of 0.80, a balanced design required 23–34 participants per group. For correlational analyses within the experimental group, assuming r > 0.40 and α = 0.05, a sample size of 36 was sufficient to achieve 80% power. This sample size also allowed inclusion of 4–6 predictors in multiple linear regression models predicting post-intervention outcomes. Accordingly, a total sample size of 46–72 participants (23–36 per group) was considered adequate to meet the study objectives.

Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by appropriate post hoc analyses, were applied for comparisons among three or more groups. Statistical analyses and graphical outputs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Associations between circulating myokines and clinical or functional parameters were assessed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). Clinical measures were specified as dependent variables, with individual myokines (MSTN, FST, FSTL3, or ActA) included as fixed effects and participant-specific random intercepts to account for within-subject variability. Models were sequentially adjusted for age, glucocorticoid dose, body cell mass, and serum creatinine. Model assumptions were verified by residual inspection, and significance was determined using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Analyses were performed in R (v4.5.2) (R Core Team, 2025) using the lme4 [33] and lmerTest [34] packages.

To identify integrated molecular and clinical patterns associated with muscle disease activity, Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression was performed on scaled and centered biochemical, molecular, and clinical variables, with muscle disease activity (VAS) as the outcome. Muscle disease activity categories were defined as MDA 0 (0–29.9 mm), MDA 1 (29.9–49.9 mm), and MDA 2 (50–100 mm) based on clinical experience. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.2) using the mixOmics package [35]. Model optimization employed 10-fold cross-validation, with variable importance assessed by VIP scores. Model performance was evaluated using R² and Q² statistics, and stability was confirmed by permutation testing (N = 1,000). Variables contributing most to disease activity were identified from loadings of the first latent component.

Results

Characteristics of study population

One hundred and one patients with IIM and 134 healthy controls were enrolled. Based on the biological material and follow-up observations available, subsequent analyses with the data were performed in patient subgroups (Table 1). To study the selected proteins in circulation, 62 newly diagnosed patients (new IIM), 39 myositis patients with established disease (chronic IIM), and 134 healthy controls (HC) with serum samples were studied. In addition to that, repeated serum measurements from 50 new IIM patients after 6 months from diagnosis were investigated. To study the myokines and MSTN signalling in muscle tissue, subgroups with baseline biopsies from 59 new IIM patients, 19 chronic IIM patients, and 21 HC, were compared. Finally, 19 new IIM patients with baseline and repeated muscle biopsy after 6 months of therapy were examined. The clinical characteristics of IIM patients and HC from all subgroups are summarized in Table 1.

To assess potential selection bias in muscle tissue longitudinal data, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) using 30 baseline clinical and biochemical variables in newly diagnosed patients with and without follow-up biopsy. Patients undergoing repeat biopsy did not form a distinct cluster and were distributed across the overall variability of the newly diagnosed cohort, suggesting that the longitudinal subgroup was broadly representative of the baseline population (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Serum levels of MSTN and its inhibitors differ significantly in newly diagnosed IIM compared with chronic patients and healthy controls

An unpaired comparison of circulating levels of MSTN, ActA, and proteins with inhibitory effects on MSTN - FST and FSTL3 - was performed between newly diagnosed patients, patients with an established disease, and healthy controls (Fig. 1). Compared to chronic patients and healthy donors, newly diagnosed patients showed significantly lower MSTN levels and higher FST levels. ActA levels did not differ from healthy controls at disease onset but were significantly elevated in chronic patients compared to both healthy controls and newly diagnosed patients. In the group of new IIM patients, a follow-up measurement of serum concentrations after 6 months of standard therapy showed a significant increase in MSTN (in 80% of patients) and decrease in FSTL3 levels (in 59% of patients), whereas the levels of FST and ActA remained unaltered (Fig. 2). Consistently, newly diagnosed IIM patients exhibited significantly lower MSTN levels, even after normalization to body cell mass (BCM), compared with both chronic IIM patients (p = 0.0025) and HC (p = 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 2.A). BCM-normalized MSTN levels increased significantly during the first 6 months following diagnosis (p = 0.0012) (Supplementary Fig. 2.B). In chronic IIM patients, BCM-normalized MSTN levels were comparable to those observed in healthy controls; however, this analysis was limited by the smaller number of patients with available body composition measurements.

Multivariate analyses of the relationships between serum myokines and clinical parameters over the study period

In addition to evaluating changes in individual parameters over the study period, associations between serum MSTN, FST, ActA and FSTL3 levels and clinical parameters were analysed using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) to account for repeated measurements within individuals across two timepoints (baseline, M0; and after 6 months of therapy, M6). Regression coefficients (β) represented the estimated change in the outcome variable associated with a one-unit increase in the predictor, whereas standardized β coefficients reflected the effect size relative to one standard deviation change in the predictor, enabling comparison across variables measured on different scales. First analysis was performed on the whole dataset, while models were sequentially adjusted for potential confounders discussed in the literature, including age, daily glucocorticoid dose (mg/kg body weight), BCM, and serum creatinine concentration [36–38] (Table 2).

In the whole dataset, FSTL3 showed consistent and strong positive associations with markers of disease activity and impaired function. Specifically, FSTL3 correlated positively with CRP (p < 0.001 across all models) and MYOACT (p = 0.012 unadjusted; p = 0.006–0.032 after adjustment), and negatively with SF-36 mental (MCS) and physical (PCS) component scores (p = 0.003–0.037), suggesting its association with myositis activity and poorer perceived health status. FST demonstrated weaker but still significant positive relationship with CRP and negative with FI-2 (p ≈ 0.01–0.03). Lower serum MSTN levels were associated with worse muscle strength, as reflected by a positive association between MSTN concentrations and the MMT-8 total score (p = 0.003). This association remained significant after adjustment for confounders, with the exception of BCM (p = 0.151).

Based on results from the whole dataset, we decided to perform an additional model adjusted for BCM. As sex differences in relation to MSTN [39–41] were previously reported, this model included timepoint and sex as fixed effects to evaluate parameter relationships within particular groups [37–39]. Even though no significant difference in circulating FSTL3 levels was observed between females and males (N, mean ± SD; F: 39, 10821 ± 3429 pg/ml vs. M: 22, 10980 ± 4087 pg/ml; p = 0.872), post hoc sex-stratified analyses revealed distinct associations between circulating FSTL3 concentrations and clinical parameters in female but not male patients (Fig. 3). FSTL3 showed a significant positive association with CRP levels in females during the 6 month observation (p = 0.00034), and also at baseline (p = 0.0125) as well as after six months (p = 0.0025). A positive association between FSTL3 and global disease activity (MYOACT) was observed in female patients during the 6 months (p = 0.0030), and particularly at baseline (p = 0.0024). In female patients, higher FSTL3 levels were significantly associated with lower (worse) SF-36 MCS during the six month observation period (p = 0.03) and particularly at baseline (p = 0.0198). Associations between FSTL3 and the SF-36 PCS were weaker, showing a trend toward a negative relationship in female patients at both baseline and follow-up, however significant when changes over the 6-month period were taken into account (p = 0.0152). In male patients a significant negative association with SF-36 PCS was observed during the observation period (p = 0.043) and particularly at six months (p = 0.05). In contrast, no significant associations between FSTL3 and SF-36 MCS, CRP, or MYOACT were detected in male patients at either timepoint (all p > 0.1). Together, these findings suggest sex-specific differences in the relationship of circulating FSTL3 and clinical outcomes in patients with new IIM.

Upregulation of MSTN signalling genes in muscle tissue of newly diagnosed patients suggests an activation of the MSTN pathway and muscle catabolic signalling early in IIM

Expression levels of 12 genes involved in the MSTN signalling pathway were compared between new IIM, chronic IIM patients, and HC (Fig. 4.A). Significant differences were observed for several key components of the pathway. MSTN, ACVR1B, ACVR2B, SMAD2, SMAD3, SMAD4, FBXO32, and TRIM63 exhibited markedly elevated expression in new IIM patients compared to both chronic IIM and HC (p < 0.01 to p < 0.0001). FST, FSTL3, and FOXO1 showed no significant intergroup variation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of study participants. The first two components (PC1 = 44.4%, PC2 = 13.4%) show partial separation between new IIM (blue), chronic IIM (red) patients and HC (green), suggesting disease-related variation in the analysed profiles

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to explore overall variation in the gene expression across the three study groups (Fig. 5). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 44.4% and 14.4% of the total variance, respectively. The PCA score plot revealed a partial separation of the groups along PC1, with samples from newly diagnosed patients tending to cluster toward the positive side of PC1, while healthy and chronic cases were distributed more toward the center or negative side of the axis. Despite some overlap between groups, the overall distribution suggests differences in the expression of MSTN pathway components in muscle tissue associated with IIM and disease stage. The absence of distinct clustering along PC2 indicates that most of the variance captured by this component reflects intra-group heterogeneity rather than differences between patient categories.

The individual changes in MSTN pathway gene expression were also evaluated in repeated muscle biopsies of 19 patients after 6 months of therapy. However, no significant difference compared to baseline measurement was observed (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Because most newly diagnosed patients had received short-term glucocorticoid therapy before muscle biopsy, a treatment-related contribution to MSTN pathway activation cannot be fully excluded. However, exploratory comparison of glucocorticoid-exposed and glucocorticoid-naïve patients did not indicate a systematic glucocorticoid-related increase in MSTN pathway gene expression, although this analysis was limited by the small number of untreated patients (Supplementary Fig. 4).

The signal component of MSTN pathway SMAD2/3 was significantly higher in new IIM compared to HC and in chronic IIM was slightly (but not significantly) higher than in HC. The phosphorylated form, p-SMAD2Ser465/467 /3Ser423/425, was significantly higher in new IIM compared with both chronic IIM and HC. Additionally, MuRF-1 (TRIM63) protein expression was elevated in new IIM compared to HC and was comparable to the chronic IIM group. In contrast, atrogin-1 (FBXO32) expression in new IIM was similar to HC but was significantly reduced in chronic IIM. Protein FOXO1, a MSTN-responsive transcription factor, had tendency to be higher in both IIM groups compared to HC while its phosphorylated form, p-FOXO1Ser249, showed a tendency toward higher levels in chronic IIM compared with new IIM and HC (Supplementary Fig. 5). MSTN expression in muscle tissue decreased after the first 6 months of therapy (Fig. 6.D). Other proteins remained unchanged or the reduction in expression after the first 6 months of therapy was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Multivariate analysis of the relationships between molecular and biochemical parameters and clinical outcomes in newly diagnosed IIM

Partial least squares (PLS) analysis integrating baseline clinical, biochemical, and molecular parameters was performed in new IIM patients to identify Component 1 (Comp 1) as the primary latent variable explaining variation in relation to muscle disease activity (MDA category) (Fig. 7). Variables contributing most strongly to the negative direction of Comp 1 were predominantly associated with highest MDA score (category 2) and reflect active muscle disease and functional impairment. Among these variables, physician and patients activity on VAS, serum concentrations of LD, CK, ALT, AST and myoglobin, exertion on Borg scale perceived during FI-2 testing, HAQ and daily glucocorticoid dose were included together with higher muscle gene expression of TRIM63, FST, ACVR1B and 2B, SMAD3,4, FBXO32, FSTL3 and FST serum levels. In contrast, positive loadings on Comp 1 corresponding to lower muscle disease activity (MDA category 0) were driven by higher MMT8 and FI-2 scores, SF-36 mental and physical component scores, extramuscular disease activity (pulmonary and skeletal) together with higher MSTN gene expression in muscle and MSTN serum levels. Overall, Comp 1 delineates a biologically meaningful axis linking MSTN pathway with myositis clinical activity, distinguishing patients with low versus high muscle disease activity based on combined molecular and clinical signatures.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine MSTN biology at different stages of inflammatory myopathies to better understand previously reported MSTN pathway alterations in the context of IIM pathogenesis. Our data indicate that newly diagnosed IIM is characterized by a distinct pattern of locally amplified MSTN signalling in muscle despite significantly reduced MSTN levels in circulation. Moreover, we identified serum FSTL3 as an interesting new marker reflecting clinical outcomes in IIM.

Our data show a marked upregulation of MSTN, ACVR1B (ALK4), ACVR2B, SMAD2, SMAD3, SMAD4, as well as the downstream ubiquitin–proteasome genes FBXO32 (atrogin-1) and TRIM63 (MuRF1) in newly diagnosed IIM, strongly suggesting that muscle tissue at the disease onset is in a catabolic, atrophy-prone transcriptional state. Moreover, muscle biopsies in early disease also demonstrated elevated MSTN protein together with marked activation of its canonical signalling cascade, as evidenced by significantly higher SMAD2/3 protein expression and robust SMAD2/3 phosphorylation alongside with increased MuRF-1 protein, further supporting the proteolytic state targeting myofibrillar proteins. These findings, together with reduced circulating MSTN in early IIM, suggest a hypothetical model in which acute inflammation and cellular stress may promote MSTN retention and signalling within muscle, while simultaneously impairing its release into the circulation. We also observed a parallel rise in serum FST at disease onset which may reflect a systemic attempt to counterbalance heightened local TGF-β superfamily activity. Interestingly, serum ActA levels in newly diagnosed IIM were comparable to healthy donors, and increased in chronic IIM.

As IIM becomes chronic, muscle transcriptional program shifts away from MSTN/SMAD-driven catabolism. In addition to different transcriptional profile, muscle from chronic IIM patients exhibited reduced MSTN protein and substantially attenuated SMAD2/3 phosphorylation. While MuRF-1 protein levels remained elevated in chronic IIM, atrogin-1 was significantly suppressed compared with healthy controls and new IIM. These changes may reflect therapeutic control of acute inflammation, adaptation to sustained injury, transition toward more fibrotic, less metabolically active tissue or, as previously suggested, potential compensatory mechanism for further muscle loss [15]. Data obtained from longitudinal sampling in newly diagnosed patients support this transition. Over the first six months of therapy, muscle MSTN protein expression declined, while its serum concentration increased, suggesting a restoration of secretion capacity possibly in line with resolution of acute inflammation. Interestingly, ACVR2B, SMAD2/3, MuRF-1, atrogin-1 and FOXO1 remained largely unchanged during the first 6 months of therapy even as ligand levels normalized. Collectively, these findings delineate a possible regulatory pattern in IIM: an early MSTN-high, pSMAD-high, MuRF-dominant catabolic setting that transitions into a chronic environment with attenuated MSTN signalling.

In newly diagnosed IIM, MSTN production within the muscle tissue may increase as a response to acute inflammation and muscle damage. Various factors, including chronic or acute inflammation [42], oxidative stress, angiotensin II, and glucocorticoids, can increase MSTN production [43]. TNF enhanced MSTN expression in cultured muscle cells via the NF-κB signalling pathway. In turn, MSTN stimulated the production of interleukin-6 (IL-6) indicating that MSTN functions in a feedback loop with proinflammatory cytokines [44]. Inflammatory processes can also affect the transport and secretion of MSTN into the circulation [45], leading to its accumulation in the muscle and reduced serum levels. This may help to explain the discrepancy between levels of tissue and serum MSTN in newly diagnosed patients in our study. However, while there is indirect evidence and mechanistic plausibility that inflammation can affect the secretion and transport of MSTN from muscle to circulation [46] - through effects on extracellular matrix remodelling, endothelial function, and cytokine signalling - direct studies specifically demonstrating this mechanism in humans are limited. Most of published work focuses on changes in MSTN expression, not its transport. Moreover, high doses of glucocorticoids (GCs), often used in treatment of recent onset IIM, may play a role in the observed MSTN upregulation since GCs directly stimulate MSTN transcription through interactions with a glucocorticoid response element located in its gene promoter [47, 48].

MSTN secretion does not necessarily lead to immediate receptor engagement and activation, as it is secreted as proMSTN, its inactive precursor containing a prodomain which prevents binding of mature MSTN peptides [49]. The two step conversion of proMSTN in the active form by proteolytic cleavage may be a key regulatory step in the biosynthesis of active MSTN in vivo. Furthermore, MSTN: receptor interactions are regulated by secreted and membrane associated naturally occurring binding proteins that function to antagonize receptor activation or as extracellular stores. These include FST, FSTL3 protein as well as growth and differentiation factor associated serum protein-1 (e.g. GASP-1) [50]. FST-targeted gene therapy has been evaluated in inclusion body myositis (IBM) [13]; however, to our knowledge, this is the first study to assess FST protein levels in muscle tissue of both newly diagnosed and established patients with IIM other than IBM, as well as to examine corresponding changes in serum and muscle during the early months following diagnosis.

In a study by Mahoudeau et al., serum MSTN protein levels and MSTN mRNA expression were reduced in IIM patients, and serum protein levels inversely correlated with disease activity. Inactive ASyS and DM patients had higher serum MSTN than active patients [16]. In contrast, no difference in MSTN serum levels between DM and PM patients and control subjects was observed in a smaller study [51]. Serum levels of circulating MSTN were also decreased in IBM together with MSTN mRNA downregulation in muscle tissue of these patients [14]. On the other hand, expression of MSTN propeptide protein was found elevated in IBM muscle biopsies in another study [52]. Our findings in non-IBM IIM are generally consistent with these observations at the systemic level, but extend them by demonstrating that early IIM is characterized by a marked dissociation between circulating MSTN levels and enhanced MSTN signalling within skeletal muscle.

Associations with disease activity and functional status

Associations between circulating myokines and clinical and functional parameters over the first 6 months from diagnosis were analysed in newly diagnosed IIM using linear mixed-effects models to account for repeated measurements within subjects. In the analysis on the entire cohort, circulating FSTL3 was strongly associated with systemic inflammation, global disease activity, and impaired physical and mental health, independent of covariate adjustment. These findings possibly indicate that FSTL3 reflects overall disease burden rather than muscle-specific pathology alone. Given its regulation by inflammatory [53] and STAT3-dependent pathways [22], elevated FSTL3 may represent a marker of sustained immune activation and metabolic stress, integrating inflammatory activity with patient-reported outcomes in IIM. Presented data on FSTL3 in newly diagnosed patients extend our previous work on the activin A–myostatin–follistatin system in chronic IIM (low MSTN, high FST, attenuated SMAD signalling in muscle) [15] by introducing FSTL3 as an additional circulating antagonist that seems to track disease stage and inflammatory load. In contrast, circulating MSTN showed weaker association profile. Although lower serum MSTN correlated with increased muscle strength after correction for important confounders, this relationship was partly attenuated after adjustment for muscle mass surrogate (BCM), suggesting partial mediation by body composition. However, differences in serum MSTN between patient groups as well as its change in first months of therapy remained unchanged after normalization to BCM, suggesting that circulating MSTN is not solely determined by muscle quantity and may reflect additional biological processes (e.g. inflammation, secretion/retention dynamics, treatment effects).

Apart from body cell mass, the association of circulating levels of studied proteins has been adjusted to age and sex as an association between MSTN levels and sex hormones has been reported, although findings across studies remain inconsistent [41]. Age appears to be positively associated with increased MSTN levels in women [39], who exhibit higher concentrations than their age-matched male counterparts, whereas in younger populations, men tend to have higher MSTN levels than women [54]. On the other hand, MSTN levels were not related to age or body composition in another study [55]. Baseline fat-free mass was not associated with baseline serum MSTN concentrations neither were MSTN levels associated with BMI even after adjustment to age [56]. Although reduced muscle mass is generally associated with lower circulating MSTN levels [57], this relationship may not persist longitudinally as MSTN expression is regulated by multiple factors beyond muscle volume alone. Notably, MSTN levels can decline acutely in response to muscle injury [58] or long term exercise [59], thereby facilitating muscle regeneration, hypertrophy, and adaptive remodelling.

Available studies have investigated FSTL3 in various physiological and pathological contexts, including reproductive development, metabolic disorders, and cancer, but have not demonstrated consistent sex-specific differences in serum levels or biological activity [60, 61]. However, sex-stratified analyses in our study revealed that associations between FSTL3 and clinical outcomes were largely confined to female patients, in whom higher FSTL3 was related to higher inflammation, disease activity, and poorer mental health. These relationships were absent in male patients, highlighting a sex-specific modulation of the FSTL3–inflammation axis, even in the absence of serum concentration differences between women and men. Collectively, these findings underscore a functional divergence between FSTL3 and MSTN in IIM, with FSTL3 reflecting systemic inflammatory burden and MSTN relating more closely to muscle function and disability.

To combine clinical, biochemical, and molecular features of muscle disease activity in IIM a multivariate PLS analysis was performed in newly diagnosed patients. The analysis identified Component 1 as a biologically meaningful axis integrating all studied parameters. Negative loadings on Comp 1, corresponding to the highest muscle disease activity, were driven by elevated markers of muscle damage, worse patient- and physician-reported outcomes, higher functional impairment, and increased glucocorticoid exposure. Importantly, this clinical phenotype clustered with coordinated upregulation of key components of the MSTN signalling pathway in muscle, including ACVR1B, SMAD3/4, FOXO1, TRIM63 (MuRF-1), and FBXO32 (atrogin-1), indicating activation of catabolic and proteolytic programs tightly linked to active myositis. Higher muscle disease activity was also associated with increased FST expression in muscle and serum, which may suggest a compensatory response to heightened TGF-β superfamily signalling in inflamed muscle. However, we hypothesise that rather than reflecting effective pathway inhibition, elevated FST likely represents a marker of inflammatory and metabolic stress that coexists with persistent SMAD activation and ongoing muscle breakdown.

In contrast, positive loadings on Comp 1—reflecting low or absent muscle disease activity—were driven by better muscle strength and endurance, improved quality of life, and higher MSTN expression in muscle together with increased circulating MSTN. This pattern is in line with previously shown observation that lower serum MSTN correlates with higher disease activity [15, 16] Similarly in other muscle wasting diseases, serum MSTN concentrations correlated negatively with functional capacity in individuals with muscular dystrophies, as well as with muscle mass in cases of sarcopenia [62, 63].

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The heterogeneity of the IIM cohort and the limited availability of muscle tissue resulted in smaller sample sizes for selected protein analyses. In addition, comparisons between newly diagnosed and chronic patients were partly based on cross-sectional data rather than fully longitudinal observations from the same individuals. Nevertheless, the longitudinal follow-up of newly diagnosed patients over the first six months of therapy supports the proposed stage-dependent regulatory model. Because the majority of newly diagnosed patients received short-term glucocorticoid therapy at the time of biopsy, a potential treatment-related contribution to the observed MSTN upregulation cannot be fully excluded. Although glucocorticoids may contribute to MSTN pathway activation, the observed molecular pattern—particularly the discordance between reduced circulating MSTN levels and increased intramuscular pathway activation, together with attenuation of signaling in chronic disease despite longer treatment exposure—suggests that inflammatory disease-related mechanisms likely also contribute to the observed alterations. Despite refinement of subgroup definitions, the chronic IIM cohort remained heterogeneous regarding disease duration, treatment exposure, and the balance between active inflammation and accumulated muscle damage, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Furthermore, while mechanistic experimental data directly addressing MSTN secretion or transport were not included, the consistency of our findings across serum, muscle transcriptomic, protein, and multivariate analyses strengthens the overall conclusions. Future studies combining longitudinal muscle sampling with experimental models of inflammatory stimulation and targeted analyses of MSTN processing, secretion, and signalling will be required to directly investigate the mechanisms underlying MSTN pathway dysregulation in IIM.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that early idiopathic inflammatory myopathy is characterized by a marked dissociation between reduced circulation MSTN levels and enhanced MSTN signalling within skeletal muscle, reflecting a catabolic and proteolytic state driven possibly by acute inflammation and cellular stress at disease onset. With treatment and disease chronicity, muscle MSTN signalling is attenuated and circulating MSTN levels recover, indicating a stage dependent shift in MSTN regulation. In parallel, circulating FSTL3 emerges as a marker of systemic inflammatory burden and patient reported disease impact, in contrast to MSTN, which relates more directly muscle-centred disease processes.
