Brain corticogenesis promotes SARS-CoV-2 neuro-glial tropism through lipid-dependent viral replication
Authors: Byoung-San Moon, Dae-Gyun Ahn, Jieun Park, Nhu Thi Quynh Mai, Da-Jin Jeong, Hyowon Hong, Sae-Bom Yoon, Sunhee Lee, Gun Young Yoon, Chonsaeng Kim, Keun Bon Ku, Hye Jin Nam, Ihn-Sil Kwak, Seong-Jun Kim, Heeyeong Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhile extensive research has examined the neuroinvasiveness of SARS-CoV-2, its relationship with brain maturation remains unclear. Using a multi-omics approach, we established cerebral organoid (CBO) at day 60 (EB60) and day 120 (EB120) to model immature and mature developmental stages. We found that enhanced corticogenesis and gliogenesis during maturation are associated with substantial alterations in lipid metabolism, functionally linking these processes to coordinated molecular and functional brain development. Leveraging this model, we provide the first quantitative insights into long-term viral propagation kinetics up to 20 days post-infection, revealing significantly higher infectivity in mature CBOs. Single-cell transcriptomics, RT-qPCR, and immunohistochemistry revealed the upregulation of lipid-associated genes in EB120, suggesting a key driver of increased susceptibility. Consistently, pharmacological lipid reduction attenuated SARS-CoV-2 infection. Our findings establish a mechanistic link between intrinsic brain maturation and viral susceptibility mediated by lipid remodeling, suggesting lipid-lowering strategies as potential candidates for therapeutic repurposing in COVID-19.

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection is associated with functional failure across most organ systems, which is not restricted to the acute phase but persists for weeks or months, even in patients with mild disease (Sudre et al., 2021; Hirschtick et al., 2021; Ellul et al., 2020). Among the reported post-acute sequelae, neurological and psychiatric symptoms are frequent and diverse (Al-Aly et al., 2021; 2022). These complications may result from brain injury caused by multiple pathogenic processes following the direct infection of the central nervous system (Balcom et al., 2021) or as a consequence of neuroinflammation (Mazza et al., 2020). Therefore, the multifactorial nature appears to be a critical characteristic of SARS-CoV-2-induced neurological diseases, with direct viral effects potentially playing a role. While SARS-CoV-2 neurotropism (Boldrini et al., 2021; Deinhardt-Emmer et al., 2021) and the pathomechanisms underlying associated neurological symptoms (Lukiw et al., 2022; Bauer et al., 2022) have been increasingly characterized, conclusive evidence for SARS-CoV-2 neuroinvasion, particularly under conditions reflecting asymptomatic and mild disease rather than in postmortem analyses, remained limited. Capturing viral behavior during the post-infection period in living systems and identifying factors that drive viral propagation in parallel with ongoing brain development are, therefore, critical for advancing our understanding of neurological complications and improving therapeutic strategies for post-COVID-19 sequelae.

To determine the pathogenicity of SARS-CoV-2 in the brain, diverse models have been employed. Rodents, which are typical animal models for scientific research, require the transgenic entrance receptor human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) for infection manifestations (Bao et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2020); hence, this method is laborious. Even with successful transgenesis, their ability to recapitulate human symptoms is limited. Additionally, several human cell lines have been used but fail to mimic the complexity of the native brain. In this context, three-dimensional (3D) cerebral organoids (CBOs) derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), which can reproduce the molecular characteristics of cell donors after defined differentiation (Watanabe et al., 2017), have become an ideal platform for in vitro modeling of the human brain in virus infection studies. Given that 3D culture can closely mimic in vivo conditions, allowing the maintenance and development of cytoarchitecture complexity, functionality, and interactions among brain cells (Yoon et al., 2019), CBOs have been used in previous studies, showing the potential to recapitulate the functional responses of native tissues to SARS-CoV-2 infection (Jacob et al., 2020; Pellegrini et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020). However, a significant gap remains in understanding how brain maturation influences SARS-CoV-2’s infection dynamics and the factors governing susceptibility in the human brain.

In this study, through a pioneering report on the long-term kinetics of SARS-CoV-2 propagation in CBOs, we demonstrated the susceptibility of human brain cells to COVID-19 and noted the periodic decreases and increases in viral genome copy number within CBOs over time. Our findings validate the strong correlation between SARS-CoV-2 propagation efficiency and brain maturation, which is governed by the enhancement of lipid metabolism and the upregulation of translational machinery-associated genes. Given evidence of SARS-CoV-2 persistence in the human brain despite negative PCR tests (Zuo et al., 2024) and the ongoing burden of long-term COVID-19 sequelae, this study provides insight into neurotropism in the context of brain development and highlights potential targets for interventions aimed at mitigating neurological complications associated with long COVID.

Results

Susceptibility of CBOs to SARS-CoV-2 infection is increased during late-stage development

To investigate the post-acute effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection in the human brain, we generated CBOs from hiPSCs using a stepwise differentiation process (Figure S1A). The organoids were exposed to SARS-CoV-2 for 1 h, and analyses were extended to 20 days post-infection (dpi) to study the potential progressive alterations in the infected brain. We first characterized day 60 embryoid bodies (EB60) by assessing the expression of the cortical neuron marker (SATB2) and verified SARS-CoV-2 infection by detecting nucleocapsid protein (NP) (Figure S1B). Furthermore, we examined the expression levels of mature neuronal (MAP2) and astrocytic (GFAP) protein markers in EB120 at 20 dpi. A significant increase in viral NP protein levels was observed (Figures S2A–S2C), demonstrating the neuroinvasiveness of SARS-CoV-2 and suggesting its effect on the neuronal and astrocytic populations within the CBOs.

Focusing on the long-term effects of SARS-CoV-2 on the brain to identify factors influencing viral propagation, we analyzed viral titer kinetics in EB60 and EB120 over 20 dpi using RT‒qPCR. Viral RNA levels increased to 1 dpi (Figure 1A) and subsequently declined, indicating that viral propagation did not persistently increase linearly with time. This observation promoted us to examine whether CBO diameter influences SARS-CoV-2 replication. However, viral copy number analysis in small and large EB60 organoids revealed no significant difference (Figure S2D), suggesting that SARS-CoV-2 replication efficiency is independent of CBO size discrepancies. We therefore examined whether the developmental stage of CBOs affected viral replication. Diameter measurements showed a progressive increase in CBO size during differentiation (Figure S2E), with significant enlargement beginning at day 60. In parallel, H&E staining revealed the progressive formation of cortical-like structures that differentiated the later stages from the earlier stages, especially at days 60 and 120 (Figures 1B and 1C). To assess the susceptibility of early- and late-stage CBOs to SARS-CoV-2 infection, EB60 and EB120 organoids were exposed to viruses, with mouse preserum (PS) and neutralizing antibody (NA) pretreatments as controls (Figures 1D and 1E). Viral titers were monitored for 20 dpi by RT-qPCR in EB60 (Figure 1F) and EB120 (Figure 1G). No significant difference was observed between the PS-pretreated and non-pretreated groups at either developmental stage. In contrast, NA-pretreated markedly reduced viral RNA levels, indicating impaired viral propagation due to blocked viral entry. Importantly, the EB60 group showed limited viral replication during the early phase of infection when viral RNA levels initially declined until day 7 and then gradually increased, reaching a peak of approximately 107 genome copies/mL. In contrast, EB120 group exhibited sustained increases in extracellular viral RNA throughout the investigated period, ranging from comparable levels to those in EB60 during the first day to approximately 108 genome copies/mL, indicative of more robust viral release. Consistent with these findings, focus-forming assay (FFA) results showed a gradual increase in infectious viral particles in EB60 over time, whereas EB120 displayed a rapid increase in infectious titers during the first 5 days, reaching levels markedly higher than those in EB60, followed by a substantial decline thereafter (Figure 1H).

To investigate the cause of this decline, we examined SARS-CoV-2-induced apoptosis by measuring DNA fragmentation via TUNEL staining. TUNEL-positive cells were significantly more abundant in infected EB120 than in EB60 (Figures S3A and S3B), indicating greater virus-induced apoptosis in late-stage organoids. To evaluate the pathogenic effect of newly produced viral particles, conditioned media collected from infected EB60 and EB120 cultures at 20 dpi were applied to Vero cells, resulting in cell detachment in both groups (Figures S3C and S3D). Notably, conditioned medium from EB120 caused greater Vero cell detachment and a significantly higher of NP-positive cells than medium from EB60 (Figures S3E and S3F), indicating enhanced production of infectious viral particles in EB120. These findings suggest that the drastic reduction in virus titers in EB120 may result from extensive virus-induced apoptosis which leads to the depletion of susceptible host cells for infection. This notion was supported by immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of the viral entry factors ACE2 and TMPRSS2, which revealed markedly higher and more persistent levels in EB120 than in EB60 (Figures S4A–S4D), consistent with previous findings (Lee et al., 2020). Increased ACE2 expression correlated with a higher proportion of NP-positive infected cells in EB120 (Figures 1I and 1J). Interestingly, infected cells were more abundant at the organoid periphery than in the inner regions, suggesting heterogeneous expression of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 within the organoids. Together, these results indicate that although elevated expression of viral entry factors contributes to the increased susceptibility of late-stage CBOs, it does not fully account for the markedly enhanced viral propagation observed. Notably, the increase in NP-positive cells coincided with an increase abundance of SATB2-positive cortical neurons (Figure 1I), suggesting a potential factor further enhancing SARS-CoV-2 replication efficiency. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that late-stage CBOs provide a more permissive environment for SARS-CoV-2 propagation, driven by factors beyond ACE2 expression alone.

Differences in the cellular composition and gene expression between EB120 and EB60

Having observed enhanced propagation of SARS-CoV-2 in EB120, we next investigated the structural differences in CBOs across developmental stages. IHC analysis revealed that more than 90% of cells in EB20 expressed SOX2 and PAX6 (Figures 2A and 2B), indicating a predominance of neural stem cells at this stage. The expression of these markers was slightly reduced in EB30 and significantly decreased in EB60 (Figures 2C and 2D), consistent with progressive neuronal differentiation. To comprehensively characterize the developmental changes in cellular composition, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on EB60 and EB120, which exhibited marked morphological differences in earlier analyses (Figure 2F). Comparing the pseudobulk gene expression profiles from our EB60 and EB120 with those of a reference human brain dataset spanning the second trimester to adulthood (Velmeshev et al., 2023) (Figure S5), we found strong transcriptional similarity of both stages to fetal human brain during the second and third trimesters, indicating that our CBOs recapitulate key features of mid-gestational in vivo human brain development.

Unsupervised clustering identified 28 distinct cellular subtypes, which were grouped into 12 major populations (Figure 2G) by manually correlating their transcriptional profiles with a reference CBO dataset (Kanton et al., 2019) using Pearson’s correlation metric (Data S1). Annotation was validated by the expression of cluster-specific marker genes (Figure 2H) and established lineage markers for four major cell lineages (Figure 2I). Although EB60 and EB120 shared similar cell types, their relative proportions differed markedly. In particular, EB120 showed pronounced enrichment in cortical neuron (CN) subtypes 1, 2, and 3 (Figure 2J), indicating increased corticogenesis and confirming that EB120 represents a more mature brain model compared to EB60 (immature brain).

To identify molecular changes associated with CBO maturation, we compared gene expression profiles between EB60 and EB120 across the 12 annotated cell populations and identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in 8 cell groups (Data S2). While most DEGs were cell-type specific, a subset was conserved across multiple cell types, suggesting a coordinated lineage differentiation of progenitor cells (PCs) (Figure 2K). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further revealed stage-specific transcriptional programs (Figure S6). EB60 was enriched for pathways related to neuronal differentiation, neurite extension, and early synaptogenesis, reflecting early neurodevelopmental processes. In contrast, the EB120 signature reflects functional synaptic transmission, neuronal excitability and inhibitory neurotransmission, indicative of advanced neural circuit integration and synaptic specialization. These findings further support that EB120 represents a later developmental stage than EB60.

Since the cortical neurons exhibited the most prominent change in cellular composition between immature and mature CBO, we further examined the CN population to identify molecular distinctions potentially associated with enhanced viral susceptibility. Among 24,936 genes analyzed, 216 were significantly upregulated in EB120 cortical neurons (FDR <0.05). Notably, the top upregulated genes were enriched for ribosomal proteins, suggesting elevated translational activity (Figure 2L).

Based on the distinctive cellular and transcriptomic features of mature CBO, particularly within the CN group, we hypothesized that genetic factors promoting viral propagation might be localized to this cluster. GO analysis of these DEGs revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to translational regulation, neuronal differentiation, and viral transcription (Figure S7). Further profiling the CN cell type in EB120 via Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis, we identified coronavirus disease-COVID-19 and ribosomes as the most significantly enriched (Figure 2M), suggesting that enhanced translational capacity in mature neurons may support more efficient SARS-CoV-2 replication.

Collectively, these developmental shifts in cellular composition, tissue architecture, and gene expression from EB60 to EB120 validate these models as representing immature and mature cortical developmental stages, respectively. The increased corticogenesis and expression of ribosome- and translation-associated genes in EB120 potentially contributed to the enhanced permissiveness of mature CBOs to SARS-CoV-2 replication.

Enhanced corticogenesis and gliogenesis characterize brain maturation

Since transcriptomic analysis indicated enhanced corticogenesis in mature CBOs, we visually verified this developmental progression through IHC analysis. The expression levels of the cortical layer markers SATB2, CTIP2, and TBR1 remained significantly low at days 20 and 30, began to increase at day 60, and were markedly elevated by day 120 (Figure 3A). To further characterize cortical plate formation, we quantified neurons across six cortical layers which were arranged in deep layer VI (TBR1-positive), layer V, and upper layers II-IV (SATB2+/CTIP2low), and found substantial expansion in late-stage CBOs compared with EB60 (Figure 3B). The temporal progression of cortical plate formation in our CBOs is illustrated in Figure 3C, demonstrating an inside-out pattern of development during which descendant neurons migrate further away from their progenitors, sequentially passing the earlier-born deep-layer neurons to establish upper cortical layers. By the late stage, CBOs displayed a well-developed cortical plate with expanded neuronal layers and a reduced progenitor population. Although scRNA-seq revealed a marked increase in cortical neurons in EB120, changes in radial glial cells and astrocytes were less apparent, likely due to sampling limitations. To validate the development of these populations, we performed RT‒qPCR on whole EB60 and EB120 organoids and observed increased expression of astrocyte- and oligodendrocyte-associated markers in EB120 (Figures S8B and S8C), indicating enhanced gliogenesis. IHC analysis further demonstrated that GFAP was undetectable at EB60 but increased significantly by day 80, doubled by day 100, and remained elevated thereafter (Figures 3D and 3E), confirming the emergence of astrocytes in mature CBOs. Additionally, ClCas3-positive cells remained below 5% across all stages examined (Figure 3F), suggesting that astrocyte expansion was not correlated with apoptosis rate. This gliogenic transition coincided with a marked reduction in the Ki67-positive self-renewing progenitor population (Figures 3G and 3H) and an increase in MBP-positive myelinated cells beginning at day 80 (Figure 3I), indicating coordinate maturation of glial populations during later developmental stages. Together, these findings demonstrate that gliogenesis is progressively established in mature CBOs beginning around day 80.

To determine whether corticogenesis and gliogenesis reflected a developmental time-dependent process rather than a sex-specific effect, we analyzed CBOs generated from an independent male-derived hiPSC line, hFmiPSC2 (Figure S9A), in parallel. IHC analyses revealed aligned developmental patterns to that of IMR-90, with high SOX2 expression at early stages followed by progressive increases in MAP2-positive mature neurons, suggesting a comparable pattern of progenitor differentiation (Figure S9B). Cortical plate formation was similarly observed through progressive increases in neurons across region-specific cortical layers (Figure S9C) and gliogenesis was confirmed by the emergence of MBP- and GFAP-positive cell populations beginning at EB80 (Figure S9D). Consistently, RT–qPCR analysis further demonstrated significant upregulation of neuronal and glial marker genes in EB100 compared with EB60 (Figure S10), confirming reproducible developmental trajectories of corticogenesis and gliogenesis across these hiPSC lines.

Together, these findings demonstrate that progressive corticogenesis and gliogenesis are hallmark features differentiating mature CBOs from immature CBOs.

Permissiveness of neurons and astrocytes to SARS-CoV-2 and its copromoting effect on virus infection efficiency

Since neuron and glial populations differed markedly between immature and mature CBOs, we next investigated whether these cellular changes synergize with SARS-CoV-2 propagation. To this end, we performed double immunohistochemical staining for viral NP together with MAP2 or GFAP in EB60 and EB120 post-infection (Figure 4A). The proportions of infected neurons (NP+ MAP2+) (Figure 4B) and astrocytes (NP+ GFAP+) (Figure 4C) were significantly greater in EB120 than in EB60 (Figures 4D and 4E) under both non- and PS-pretreatment conditions. Meanwhile, no infection was detected in neural stem cell (SOX2+) or glial progenitor cell (PDGFR-α+) at either stage (Figure S11). We next examined whether this increased susceptibility was associated with developmental changes in viral entry factors. Time course analysis revealed that ACE2 expression increased substantially from EB80 onward (Figures S12A and S12B), indicating that ACE2 levels are dynamically regulated during CBOs maturation. Interestingly, this increase coincided with the expansion of neuronal and astrocytic populations, as shown by increased abundance of ACE2+MAP2+ and ACE2+GFAP+ cells (Figures S12C and S12D), suggesting that ACE2 expression rises as the CBOs mature. In contrast, TMPRSS2 expression showed no significant change throughout CBO development, both in total expression and within neuronal (TMPRSS2+MAP2+) or astrocytic (TMPRSS2+GFAP+) populations (Figures S12E–S12H). These results suggest that increased ACE2 expression, rather than TMPRSS2 expression, contributes to the increased susceptibility of mature CBOs to SARS-CoV-2 infection.

To determine whether cellular composition alone influences viral susceptibility independent of organoid architecture, we infected monocultures of mature neurons, astrocytes, and neuron-astrocyte cocultures (1:1 ratio) with SARS-CoV-2. Immunocytochemical (ICC) analysis confirmed viral infection of both neurons and astrocytes by the colocalization of NP with MAP2 or GFAP (Figures 4F and 4G). Consistent with the findings in CBOs, the level of viral NP was greater in neuron monocultures than in astrocyte monocultures and was highest in the cocultures (Figure 4H). These results indicate that neurons are more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection and that neuron-astrocyte further promotes viral infectivity.

Brain maturity increases lipid metabolism

Our findings demonstrated that the increased susceptibility of mature CBOs to SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with expanded neuronal and astrocytic populations and positively correlates with the increased expression of the viral entry receptor ACE2. However, since ACE2 expression was heterogeneous across the organoids, this factor alone could not fully explain this relationship. Given the role of neurons and astrocytes in regulating lipid metabolism in the brain (Barber and Raben, 2019; Zhang et al., 2023), we next investigated whether changes in lipid metabolism contribute to the relationship between SARS-CoV-2 susceptibility and CBO maturation.

Single-cell transcriptomic SPRING analysis of EB60 and EB120 revealed that mature neuronal and glial populations were predominantly enriched in the EB120 clade (Figure 5A), consistent with the increased abundance of these cell types in mature CBOs. To determine whether lipid metabolism correlates with this maturation process, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) across annotated cell groups and identified significant enrichment of lipid-associated genes in four EB120 cell types. Compared with EB60, EB120 exhibited broad upregulation of lipid-associated genes in both cortical neurons and glia clusters (Figures 5B and S13; Data S3). To further define these changes, we filtered DEGs with log2FC > 2 and p-adj <0.05 (Table S1), revealing widespread upregulation of lipid-related genes across multiple EB120 cell clusters. The most prominent increases were observed in the oRG&Astro, IP&eCN, aRG1, and CyPs clusters, suggesting that both differentiating and maturing cell types undergo substantial lipid metabolic remodeling during organoid maturation. To validate these findings, we assessed the expression of FASN, a key regulator of de novo lipid synthesis, in IMR-90 and hFmiPSC2-derived CBOs (Figures S14A and S14B) and observed a significant increase in EB100 relative to EB60 in both lines. Furthermore, from the top lipid-associated genes upregulated in each cell cluster of IMR-90–derived EB120 (Figure S11), we selected genes involved in lipid biosynthesis or metabolism that are shared across cortical neuron and glial clusters for RT-qPCR analysis. Consistently, all examined genes were significantly increased in EB100 compared to EB60 in both lines (Figures S14C and S14D), supporting that enhanced lipid metabolic regulation during CBO maturation is largely independent of sex. STRING analysis of the cluster-shared genes identified a functional network of six interconnected regulators of lipid metabolism (Figure 5C and Data S4). IHC analysis further confirmed increased protein expression of four of these genes in EB120 compared with EB60 (Figures 5D and 5E). In addition, MSMO1, an important regulator of sterol biosynthesis, was significantly elevated in EB120. Consistent with these findings, Oil Red O (ORO) staining revealed a marked increase in lipid droplet (LD) accumulation in EB120 (Figures 5F–5H), indicating enhanced lipid storage in mature organoids.

Further delineating lipid-associated differences between EB60 and EB120, we performed lipidomic profiling. PCA revealed clear stage-dependent separation of lipid signatures (Figure S15A), accompanied by widespread differential regulation of metabolites specific to each developmental stage (Figure S15B). Visualizing the top 10% most significantly altered metabolite subclasses ranked by log2FC revealed marked enrichment of glycerolipids, saccharolipids, sphingolipids, and fatty acyls in EB120 (Figure S15C and Data S5). These lipid classes are shown to play critical roles in brain development, including myelination (Baumann and Pham-Dinh, 2001; Olsen and Færgeman, 2017) and long-term maintenance of neuronal network integrity (Zöller et al., 2008). Together, these findings highlight that lipid remodeling in CBOs is developmentally regulated and cell-type specific, suggesting a mechanistic basis for the increased susceptibility to SARS-COV-2 infection observed in certain cell populations in mature CBO.

Pharmacological lipid reduction impairs SARS-CoV-2 replication and infection efficiency

To elucidate the relationship between host brain lipid metabolism and SARS-CoV-2 infectivity, we examined whether pharmacological modulation of lipid levels influences viral infection. Neurons treated with two different lipid-lowering agents, Gemfibrozil (Gem) and Lovastatin (Lov), either alone or in combination, showed remarkable decreases in the proportion of NP-positive cells in all treatment groups, with the strongest suppression observed in the combined treatment (Figures 6A and 6B). Interestingly, examining this effect on neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes through ICC, we found that SARS-CoV-2 infectivity was significantly greater in the triculture group than in neuron monocultures, indicating that the presence of glial cells enhances susceptibility to infection. To further validate these findings in CBOs, we treated infected organoids with Gem and Lov. All treatment groups showed substantial reductions in SARS-CoV-2 RNA levels, with the combined Gem/Lov treatment producing an approximately 12-fold decrease compared with untreated controls (Figures 6E and 6F). Consistent with this reduction, IHC analysis across all treatment groups (Figures 6G–6J). The lowest infection rate was observed in the Gem50/Lov10 combination, suggesting that lowering lipid metabolism can efficiently suppress SARS-CoV-2 replication and infectivity.

To assess whether reduced viral propagation was associated with improved cell survival, we performed a TUNEL assay on infected EB120 following lipid-lowering treatments and observed markedly reduced proportions of apoptotic cells post-infection. The combined Gem/Lov exerted the strongest protective effect, with 12.81% TUNEL-positive cells, which was comparable to that in uninfected controls (11.85%, p < 0.05) (Figures 6K and 6L).

Collectively, these findings indicate the dependence of SARS-CoV-2 on host lipid physiological levels for replication and infection and highlight the significance of lipid level modulation in effectively suppressing viral infectivity and virus-induced apoptosis.

Discussion

In this study, we generated CBOs from hiPSCs following Pasca’s protocol (Paşca et al., 2015). By day 60, the proportion of neural progenitor cells decreased, accompanied by increases in neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes. This developmental shift, together with the emergence of cortical-like structures, particularly evident at day 120, represents key features of brain maturation and demonstrates that our model extends prior systems that lacked major glial cells (Jacob et al., 2020). Lipidomic profiling revealed significant remodeling of lipid compositions in EB120 relative to EB60. In parallel, scRNA-seq demonstrated a close similarity of our CBOs to mid-to-late second-trimester human fetal brain and identified distinct transcriptional signatures for each stage, highlighting neurodevelopmental processes in EB60 and synaptic network progression in EB120. Together, this multi-omics approach demonstrated CBO maturation, establishing EB60 and EB120 as representative immature and mature stages in our model.

Prior studies have examined SARS-CoV-2 replication kinetics in somatic cells (Chu et al., 2020; Ogando et al., 2020) and CBOs (Jacob et al., 2020; Pellegrini et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020), but were limited to short observation periods (3–6 days), generally reporting low neurotropism and declining viral replication. However, such time frames are insufficient to capture the dynamic behavior of CBOs during persistent infection of SARS-CoV-2. Here, we extended these observations by characterizing viral propagation over 20 days post-infection. The increased infection observed in mature CBOs is unlikely to reflect a simple cumulative effect, as it persisted despite post-infection change and repeated media replacement after each sampling point. FFA revealed a peak in virus titers followed by a decline in infectious particles, which may reflect host cell depletion due to productive viral replication in mature CBOs.

Our findings are based on the original SARS-CoV-2 strain and may not generalize to other variants, as critical mutations, particularly in the spike protein, can alter ACE2 binding and neural infectivity. For instance, Omicron variants exhibit TMPRSS2-independent entry and reduced syncytia formation (Willett et al., 2022), differing from our observations. Earlier studies using the original SARS-CoV-2 strains reported minimal infection in neurons and astrocyte monolayer cocultures (Jacob et al., 2020; Pellegrini et al., 2020), likely due to lower virus input, shorter exposure, and, potentially, differences in cell lines. In contrast, both prior works and our data indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infects neurons more efficiently than astrocytes and neural progenitor cells and that more mature neural systems exhibit increased susceptibility (Ramani et al., 2020; Tiwari et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021), consistent with the enhanced availability of host factors that support replication (Ramani et al., 2020).

To delineate maturation-dependent differences, we performed scRNA-seq on uninfected organoids. Maturation was characterized by coordinated corticogenesis and increased cortical plate complexity, supported by IHC. A limitation of this approach is that scRNA-seq was conducted on randomly sampled cortical regions rather than whole organoids, which may underrepresent non-neuronal populations. We therefore complemented these analyses with RT‒qPCR and IHC on whole organoids, confirming the enrichment of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes in mature CBOs. These changes coincided with the marked increase in TUNEL+ cells in infected EB120 (>30%). As ClCasp3 levels did not differ significantly across developmental stages in uninfected organoids, the colocalization of TUNEL+ cells with glial markers, together with the coexpression of viral NP with MAP2 and GFAP, indicates increased infection in EB120. Notably, the pronounced upregulation of gliogenesis around EB80 aligned with the peak of SARS-CoV-2 copy numbers observed at 20dpi in EB60, supporting the contribution of glial expansion to increased viral propagation. Collectively, corticogenesis and gliogenesis not only mark brain maturation but are also associated with increased susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 in this system.

Given the essential role of lipid metabolism in neuro- and oligo-genesis (Knobloch et al., 2013; Molina-Gonzalez and Miron, 2019), the observed remodeling of lipid landscapes in mature CBOs likely reflects functional changes accompanying development. Pharmacological modulation of lipid metabolism with Gem and Lov reduced SARS-CoV-2 infectivity in EB120 organoids and neuron–astrocyte cocultures, supporting a link between lipid homeostasis and viral propagation. Although the precise mechanisms remain to be fully defined, our findings align with prior studies showing that Gem and Lov reduce lipid raft formation (Palacios-Rápalo et al., 2021), thereby suppressing viral entry (Davies et al., 2021) and replication (Karampoor et al., 2021) through limiting spike-ACE2 interactions. In addition, lipid metabolic pathways, including cholesterol (Sanders et al., 2021; Cure and Cumhur Cure, 2021; Li et al., 2021; Devignot et al., 2023) and triglyceride (Farley et al., 2022) metabolism, have been implicated in regulating viral replication through effects on ACE2 localization (Li et al., 2003), conformation (Glende et al., 2008), and membrane distribution (Sanders et al., 2021), and SARS-CoV-2 life cycle (Theken et al., 2021). SARS-CoV-2-induced ER stress further promotes lipid synthesis to support viral production (da Fonseca et al., 2024). Consistently, LD accumulations following infection, and their reduction upon the inhibition of lipid synthesis or LD biogenesis, have been linked to viral replication suppression (Dias et al., 2020; Farley et al., 2022), indicating that LDs act both as a consequence of infection and a facilitator of viral propagation. Beyond metabolism, LDs regulate immune responses by serving as hubs for innate immune signaling and inflammatory lipid mediator production (Bosch et al., 2020; Monson et al., 2018), such as leukotrienes (LTB4 and cysLT) (Dias et al., 2020), which are induced during SARS-CoV-2 infection and reduced upon the pharmacological inhibition of LD (Dias et al., 2020). Collectively, our findings support a model in which maturation-associated lipid metabolic enhancement creates a permissive environment for SARS-CoV-2 propagation in mature CBOs.

Lipid-targeting interventions may also attenuate inflammatory responses (Karampoor et al., 2021) and inhibit microglial activation (Jana and Pahan, 2012), thereby reducing neuronal damage driven by excessive production of neurotoxic astrocytes and proinflammatory mediators triggered by microglia (Albornoz et al., 2022; Boldrini et al., 2021). Although lipids have not been established as a direct causal factor in long COVID neuropsychiatric complications, mounting evidence links dysregulation and reduced levels of certain lipid types to disease severity (Al-Zadjali et al., 2024; López-Hernández et al., 2023; Berezhnoy et al., 2023). Moreover, metabolic disorders such as obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia are associated with worse COVID-19 outcomes (Loosen et al., 2022; Steenblock et al., 2021), suggesting that systemic metabolic states, particularly cholesterol levels, may contribute to epidemiological patterns. However, because brain lipid metabolism is largely compartmentalized by the BBB, the relationship between central lipid profiles and neurological sequelae remains to be elucidated.

In summary, our findings provide a conceptual framework linking the intrinsic developmental status of CBOs, particularly corticognesis and gliogenesis, to their susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. We showed that promoted viral propagation in mature organoids is associated with enhanced lipid metabolic activity. By extending the analysis to long-term infection dynamics, this study highlights the importance of developmental context in viral susceptibility and suggests that targeting lipid metabolism could offer a broader therapeutic stategy, especially for addressing neurological consequences of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Limitations of the study

It is also important to acknowledge the limitations of CBO models when extrapolating to the human brain. CBOs lack key in vivo features, including vasculature, BBB, immune components such as microglia, and higher-order structural organization, all of which may influence viral tropism and the heterogeneity of host responses (Bhaduri et al., 2020; Di Lullo and Kriegstein, 2017; Ormel et al., 2018). Although sex-based differences in COVID-19 clinical outcomes have been reported (Bunders and Altfeld, 2020; Takahashi et al., 2020), consistent mechanistic differences in pathogenesis remain inconclusive (Lakbar et al., 2020; Lemarchand et al., 2025). In the current study, although SARS-CoV-2 infection was not directly assessed in hFMiPSC2-derived CBOs, the aligned developmental patterns support our interpretation. Future studies using more physiologically representative systems and multiple hiPSC lines will be necessary to validate these findings and to further define how LD dynamics, cellular composition, and immune-related features shapes viral susceptibility and host responses.

Experimental model details

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (strain# 000664) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Both adult male and female mice (4–6 months old) were used for neutralizing antibody generation. All animal use was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology (permit no. 2021-8A-04-01).

Cell lines

hIPSC

HiPSC lines IMR-90, derived from human fetal lung fibroblast of a Caucasian female donor at 16 weeks of gestation, and hFmiPSC2 (KSCBi002-B-1) (Uhm et al., 2017), derived from human dermal fibroblasts of an adult male donor were cultured on 6-well plates coated with 0.5% Matrigel (Corning). These cells were maintained in medium composed of mTeSR-1 basal medium (STEMCELL Technologies Inc.) and mTeSR supplement (STEMCELL Technologies Inc.) at 37°C and 5% CO2. The medium was replenished every day, and cell passage was conducted when the cell confluency reached 40–60% (every 3–4 days) using Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (STEMCELL Technology, Inc.).

Neuron, astrocyte, and oligodendrocyte

For monolayer culture, we induced the differentiation of the SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cell line (CRL-2266, ATCC) as described elsewhere (AU - Shipley et al., 2016; Encinas et al., 2000) to generate human neuron-like cells, which were subsequently maintained in neurobasal medium supplemented with 1X B27, 1X penicillin/streptomycin, 1X GlutaMAX, 20 mM KCl, 50 ng/mL BDNF, 2 mM db-cAMP, and 10 μM retinoic acid (RA) at 37°C and 5% CO2. The generated cells were confirmed by qPCR and immunocytochemistry to target mature neuron markers (MAP2+ and Tau+) before being used for experiments. The astrocyte and oligodendrocyte cell lines used were immortalized mature human astrocytes (T0281-GVO-ABM, BioCat GmbH) and MO3.13 mature oligodendrocytes (CLU301, Cedarlane), which were maintained in DMEM at 37°C and 5% CO2. All the cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma.

Virus

SARS-CoV-2 (BetaCoV/Korea/KCDC03/2020, NCCP43326) was provided by the National Culture Collection for Pathogens (Kim et al., 2020) (NCCP; numbers 43326 and 43382) of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. All the experiments involving virus infection were conducted in a biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) laboratory at the Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology (KRICT).

Method details

Differentiation of hiPSCs and cerebral organoid (CBO) culture

CBOs were generated from hiPSCs following Pasca’s protocol with minor modifications (Paşca et al., 2015). Briefly, hiPSC colonies at 60–70% confluence in Primaria dishes (Corning 100 mm ULA dish, Cat. 4615) were detached using 10% Dispase (STEMCELL Technologies Inc.) and transferred to ultra-low attachment plates to form embryoid bodies in mTeSR medium supplemented with 10 μM SB431542, 5 μM dorsomorphin, and 10 μM Y27632, with daily medium changes. On day 6, neuroectoderm induction was initiated in Neurobasal-A medium containing 1X B27 supplement without vitamin A, 1X penicillin/streptomycin, 1X GlutaMAX, 20 ng/mL bFGF, and 20 ng/mL EGF, with daily medium replenishment. From days 26–43, neural differentiation was promoted by supplementing the medium with 20 ng/mL NT3 or 20 ng/mL BDNF, with medium changes every other day. From day 44 onward, organoids were matured in Neurobasal-A medium supplemented with 1X B27, 1X penicillin/streptomycin, and 1X GlutaMAX, with medium replacement every other day. Unless otherwise indicated, all results shown were obtained using IMR-90-derived CBOs.

Neutralizing antibody production

Neutralizing antibodies were generated by immunizing mice with the His-tagged receptor binding domain (RBD-His) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. Briefly, C57BL/6 mice (female, 8–10 weeks old, n = 3) were intramuscularly injected with 100 μg of purified RBD-His or Alhydrogel Adjuvant (21645–51–2, InvivoGen) as a negative control three times every 2 weeks. Mouse serum was collected 8 weeks postpriming (containing neutralizing antibodies). No blinding was applied. The neutralizing capacity of the serum was measured via a plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT50).

Viral infection

CBOs (5 organoids per group, pentaplicate) were exposed to 1 × 106 plaque-forming units of SARS-CoV-2 for one hour in a 24-well plate (1 mL of medium/well). The CBOs were then washed twice with fresh medium and subjected to subsequent culture on a 24-well plate (1 mL of medium/well). Half of the medium was harvested for analysis (RT‒qPCR) and replaced with fresh media. This procedure was repeated every other day.

Monolayer neuron, astrocyte, and oligodendrocyte cultures

For the 2D co and tri-culture cell culture experiments, SH-SY5Y-derived mature neurons, immortalized mature human astrocytes, and MO3.13 mature oligodendrocytes were seeded onto 24-well imaging plates at a ratio of 1:1 (neurons: astrocytes) or 1:1:1 (neurons: astrocytes: oligodendrocytes) at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well in neurobasal medium supplemented with 1X B27, 1X penicillin/streptomycin, 1X GlutaMAX, 20 mM KCl, 50 ng/mL BDNF, 2 mM db-cAMP, and 10 μM RA.

Lipid modulation in monolayer cell culture and CBOs

Two lipid-lowering drugs, Gemfibrozil (Selleck Chemicals, S1729) and Lovastatin (Selleck Chemicals, S2061), were used. We first performed drug screening for the lowest cytotoxic effect and determined 400 μM Gemfibrozil and 40 μM Lovastatin for the coculture experiment, with DMSO used as the control. The cells were treated with these agents (either individually or in combination) for 24 h and then infected with SARS-CoV-2 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1. The neuronal cells were exposed to the virus for 48 h under drug and vehicle conditions and then analyzed for the infection rate. For CBOs, EB120 cultured in 48-well ULA plates (1 mL/well) was first treated with lipid-lowering drugs for 24 h, followed by SAS-CoV-2 infection for 1 h. After that, the medium was changed with lipid-lowering drug supplements. The medium was harvested on day 5 for RT‒qPCR analysis. CBOs were cultured under drug conditions until day 20, when they were collected for IHC analysis.

Focus-forming assay (FFA)

The titer of infectious virus in each group was determined by FFA. In brief, supernatants from the IF, PS & IF groups of EB60 and EB120 were collected and added to Vero cell monolayers seeded in 96-well plates for 8 h. The treated cells were fixed with 4% PFA, followed by immunostaining with primary anti-NP antibody for 2 h at RT. After incubation, the wells were washed three times with wash buffer (0.01% (v/v) Tween 20 in PBS) and incubated in avidin-HRP (1:2000 in dilution buffer) for 45 min at RT, followed by a washing step with wash buffer (three times) and PBS (once). Next, TMB chromogenic substrate (Mabtech, 3651–10) was added to yield colorimetric reactions for 3–10 min at RT until optimal development. The reactions were stopped by rinsing with distilled water. The plate was dried for more than 24 h in the dark prior to automated focus counting via an AID ELR08 ELISpot reader (Autoimmun Diagnostika).

RNA extraction and RT‒qPCR analysis

Viral RNA was extracted from CBO culture supernatants via the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, 1048147) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For the detection of SARS-CoV-2, the N gene target of the 2019-nCoV CDC RUO kit (Integrated DNA Technologies, 10006713) (primer sequence: 5′-6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM)-ACAATTTGCCCCCAGCGCTTCAG-BHQ1) was used. Quantitative real-time RT–PCR was performed with One Step PrimeScript III RT‒qPCR mix (Takara) via the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Quantitative analysis was performed by comparing cycle threshold (Ct) values for each rection with a glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) reference: forward: 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3’; reverse: 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCACGG-3’.

Total RNA from CBOs was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Nano400A, Allsheng). Samples that passed quality control (A260/A280 > 1.6) were used for cDNA synthesis using ReverTra Ace™ qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo, FSQ-201) and a Master Cycler (Eppendorf). Primer sets are listed in Table S2. qPCR was carried out via the CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Biorad) with Thunderbird™ Next SYBR™ qPCR Mix (Toyobo, QPX-201); the conditions were 95°C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 1 min. Quadruplicate reactions were carried out for each sample. The gene expression level of each gene was normalized to that of GAPDH.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed in Tris-buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 130 mM NaCl, 10 mM NaF, 2 mM EGTA, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.5% NP-40, 5% glycerol, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)) supplanted with protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 4°C, 5 min) to collect supernatant. Proteins were subsequently separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE in Tris-glycine‒SDS buffer (TGS, Enzynomics) and transferred onto membranes. The bands of interest were excised, and blocked with 5% skim milk (Elabscience), incubated with the indicated primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000). Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and iBright 750 Imaging System (CL750, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

H&E staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

CBO samples were fixed in 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4°C and then washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Next, we embedded the fixed CBOs in a paraffin block and then sectioned them at a thickness of 4 μm via a rotary microtome (Roundfin RD-315). The sections were transferred onto glass slides for drying. Prior to staining, deparaffinization and rehydration were performed by transferring the slides through three changes of xylene, followed by xylene: 100% ethanol (1:1 ratio, v/v), 100%, 95% and 80% ethanol (10 min per change), and eventually DW.

For H&E staining, the slides were incubated with hematoxylin for 10 min, followed by residue removal by rinsing with running tap water (10 min). Next, the slides were stained with eosin for 10 min and then rinsed with running tap water (10 min). The samples were dehydrated by transferring the slides to a series of step-by-step ethanol solutions (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%), followed by three changes of xylene (5 min per change) for clearing. Finally, the slides were mounted with aqueous mounting solution (DAKO) and subjected to imaging.

For immunohistochemical staining, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0) at boiling temperature for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature (RT), samples were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing 3 times with PBS (10 min each), sections were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies in 1% BSA for 1 h at RT, counterstained with DAPI, followed by PBS washes (2 times, 10 min each) and mounted. Images were acquired using confocal microscopy (K1 Fluo, Nanoscope Systems, Inc.). Antibodies are listed in key resources table.

Oil Red O (ORO) staining

Lipid droplets in CBOs were detected in formalin-fixed frozen sections via an ORO staining kit (VB-3007; VitroVivo Biotech) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

TUNEL assay

TUNEL assay for DNA fragment detection was conducted on rehydrated paraffin-embedded sections via a one-step TUNEL in situ apoptosis kit (E-CK-A325, Elabscience) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell isolation and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

For dissociation, CBOs on days 60 and 120 were treated with papain containing deoxyribonuclease I (DNase) at 37°C for 30 min, and the samples were triturated to form a single-cell suspension. A total of 24,930 cells (EB60) and 37,211 cells (EB120) were pelleted and lysed for 3 min in 100 μL of cold lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Tween 20, 0.1% Igepal CA618 630, 0.01% digitonin, and 1% BSA). The lysed cells were then washed with 1 mL of chilled wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Tween 20, and 1% BSA), and the nuclei were pelleted at 500 rcf for 5 min at 4°C. For scRNA-seq, library construction was performed via Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Kit v3.1 (10X Genomics). Specifically, single cells were loaded onto Chromium controller chips. Within the Chromium system, gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs) with specific barcodes allow cell separation with a capture target of 10,000 cells per sample. Next, single-cell GEMs underwent reverse transcription, generating 10X barcoded cDNA. Libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq4000 platform (with paired-end 100 bp reads) with an average of 50,000 read pairs per cell. BCL files from sequencing were then used as inputs to the 10X Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline.

scRNA-seq data processing

We used Cell Ranger (version 3.1.0), a set of analysis pipelines for processing Chromium single-cell data, to demultiplex raw base call files to FASTQ files and to perform alignment to the GRCh38 human transcriptome, filtering, barcode counting and unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting. The resulting filtered feature‒barcode matrices (genes × cells) were imported into R Studio for further data processing via the Seurat single-cell analysis package (version 4.1.1) (Hao et al., 2021). To filter out low-quality cells, we excluded cells with fewer than 500 UMIs or fewer than 250 detected genes, as well as those with mitochondrial transcript proportions higher than 20%. Each sample was normalized and scaled via the SCTransform normalization method (Hafemeister and Satija, 2019) while regressing out percent mitochondria. The samples from the same timepoint (EB60 or EB120) were integrated (via canonical correlation analysis) into each other via 5000 highly variable genes. We then merged all the samples and used principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. Clustering and visualization of the dataset were performed via uniform manifold approximation and projection for dimension reduction (UMAP), with the first 40 principal components at a resolution of 1. The trajectories of the cell clades occupied by EB60 and EB120 as well as their subcell types were visualized via SPRING analysis (https://github.com/AllonKleinLab/SPRING_dev) (Weinreb et al., 2018).

Cross-dataset correlation analysis with public organoid reference

To compare our data to a published single-cell brain organoid dataset (Kanton et al., 2019), the raw count matrix of the Kanton dataset was processed via Seurat. Pearson residuals from the regression model (seurat.object@assays$SCT@scale.data) were used to quantify transcriptional similarity between the clusters of the two datasets. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated across a subset of features restricted strictly to genes intersecting both datasets. The resulting correlation matrix was plotted as a heatmap.

Developmental alignment to primary human brain trajectories

To stage the developmental maturity of our EB60 and EB120 CBOs relative to native human cortical development, we mapped our data against a comprehensive human brain single-cell atlas spanning from the second trimester through adulthood (Velmeshev et al., 2023). Reference and organoid single-cell expression profiles were aggregated by developmental stage and sample identity to generate pseudobulk expression matrices. The feature space was subsequently filtered to retain only mutually intersecting genes between the two datasets. PCA was performed on the unified, shared-gene expression matrix using SCTransform Pearson residuals (scale.data). Prior to PCA, expression values were Z score scaled across samples for each intersecting gene. PCA was computed with data centered and scaled to resolve global expression trajectories and evaluate developmental alignment.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis between the two timepoints (EB60 vs. EB120) was performed with edgeR (version 3.36.0) (Robinson et al., 2010), similar to that described for single-cell analysis in Soneson and Robinson (2018) (Soneson and Robinson, 2018). Briefly, the raw counts were used as input, and cells with less than 10 UMIs were excluded from the analysis. The dataset was aggregated into sample-level replicates to generate pseudobulk data. Counts within each cell were divided by its size factor and log transformed to calculate log2-transformed normalized expression values. We estimated dispersion by fitting a quasi-likelihood negative binomial generalized log-linear model (glmQLFit) with different time points being analyzed. We determined the statistical significance of the DEGs between the EB60 and EB120 groups via the QL F test. Genes with FDR <0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 were defined as differentially expressed genes. The upregulated and downregulated gene lists for each CBO developmental stage were used for gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis via ShinyGo (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) (Ge et al., 2020) and for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (http://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html) (Yu et al., 2012).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

For the GSEA (https://github.com/ctlab/fgsea/), hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) (Liberzon et al., 2015) were used. Our single-cell dataset was aggregated into sample-level replicates to generate pseudobulk data. The limma package (Ritchie et al., 2015) was used for processing the data. In brief, we transformed count data to log2 counts per million (logCPM) and estimated the mean‒variance relationship to prepare for linear modeling. We fitted a linear model for each gene (lmFit) and computed moderated t statistics, moderated F statistics, and log odds of differential expression. Given a linear model fit of the pseudobulk data, we computed estimated coefficients and standard errors for the EB120 vs. EB60 contrast. We then evaluated whether a set of genes in each hallmark gene set was highly ranked relative to other genes in terms of differential expression. Hallmark gene sets in the cortical neurons and glia cell types were selected and plotted as a heatmap via PermutMatrix version 1.9.3 (http://www.atgc-montpellier.fr/permutmatrix/) (Caraux and Pinloche, 2005).

To establish protein‒protein interaction networks for lipid metabolism in EB120, we filtered the highly expressed lipid-associated genes that were shared between cortical neurons and glial clusters and performed STRING analysis via STRING functional protein association networks (string-db.org).

Lipidomics – Lipid extraction

Uninfected EB60 and EB120 organoids were washed with PBS and stored at −80°C prior to extraction. Up to 25 mg of each sample was suspended in 800 μL of pre-chilled dichloromethane/methanol (3:1, v/v) containing 10 μL of an internal standard cocktail, with two steel beads added for homogenization. Samples were homogenized using a TissueLyser (5 min), sonicated on ice (10 min), and incubated overnight at −20°C. Following centrifugation (25,000 × g, 4°C, 15 min), 600 μL of the supernatant was collected and dried under vacuum. The dried residue was reconstituted in 120 μL of isopropanol/acetonitrile/water (2:1:1, v/v/v), shaken for 10 min, and sonicated on ice for 10 min. Samples were centrifuged again (25,000 × g, 4°C, 15 min), and 20 μL from each sample was pooled to prepare a QC sample. The remaining supernatant was subjected to ultra-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) analysis at BGI, following the methods described in a previous study (Zhong et al., 2017).

Lipidomics – Lipid identification

Mass spectrometry data were imported into LipidSearch v4.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for lipidomic analysis. Following data import, metabolite ion peaks were extracted and lipid-containing molecules were identified. The software generated identification results, quantitative data matrices, and associated metadata, which were subsequently analyzed and refined. The identification was performed using Product Search mode with a mass accuracy of ±5 ppm for both parent and fragment ions, a relative response deviation threshold of 5.0%, and retention time deviation of ±0.1 min. Identification confidence was based on M-score ≥5.0 and C-score ≥2.0, with results categorized into levels A–D. Adducts included [M + H]+, [M + NH4]+, and [M+Na]+ in positive ion mode, and [M−H]−, [M−2H]−, and [M + HCOO]− in negative ion mode.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 9 software. The data were tested for normal distribution and within-group variance prior to statistical analysis. All the statistical tests and sample sizes are included in the figure legends. The data obtained from at least three independent experiments were averaged and are presented as the means ± SDs as stated in the figure legends. In all cases, a p value less than 0.05 was considered significant: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
