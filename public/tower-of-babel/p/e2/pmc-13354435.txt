Efficacy and safety of rupatadine in allergic rhinitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Abdur Rehman, Arham Ihtesham, Hafsa Arshad Azam Raja, Muhammad Ibrahim, Muhammad Ahmad Khalid, Sadia Chaudhry, Shahzaib Maqbool, Rahmat Gul Omarzai, Jazza Aamir, Haissan Iftikhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Rupatadine, a dual H1-histamine and platelet-activating factor antagonist, has shown promise in allergic rhinitis (AR), but its overall efficacy and safety remain variably reported. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the therapeutic effectiveness and tolerability of rupatadine in adults with seasonal and perennial AR.Methods:Following PRISMA guidelines, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched from inception to April 2025 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing rupatadine with placebo after registration on PROSPERO (CRD42025065190). Data on nasal symptom reduction, quality of life, and adverse events were pooled using a random-effects model, expressed as standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB-2 tool.Results:Eight RCTs comprising 2442 participants were included. Rupatadine significantly reduced total nasal symptom scores (SMD = −1.36; P = 0.001) and improved nasal congestion (SMD = −0.60; P = 0.008), rhinorrhea (SMD = −1.17; P < 0.001), sneezing (SMD = −1.23; P < 0.001), and nasal itching (SMD = −1.02; P < 0.005) compared with placebo. A dose-response trend favored 20 mg over 10 mg in most outcomes. Quality-of-life improvements were statistically inconclusive (SMD = −1.52; P = 0.24). No serious adverse events were reported; somnolence was the most common adverse drug reaction.Conclusion:Rupatadine is an effective, well-tolerated therapy for AR, providing significant relief from key nasal symptoms, particularly at higher doses. Although quality-of-life benefits were modest, the findings support rupatadine’s role as a safe and potent antihistamine option, warranting further long-term and real-world evaluation.

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR), a condition characterized by IgE-mediated non-infectious inflammation of the nasal mucosa, typically manifests as nasal congestion, sneezing, rhinorrhea, and itching. Although AR alone is not inherently life-threatening, it can pose serious risks when accompanied by severe asthma or anaphylaxis[1]. It is a widespread public health concern, impacting global populations across all strata of income, with an adult prevalence ranging from 10% to 30%[2]. Moreover, the prevalence shows an increasing trend every year[3,4]. The International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood is a global initiative that evaluates allergic diseases, including AR. They reported that the prevalence, severity, and risk factors of AR are influenced by genetics, environmental exposure, and lifestyle factors[5]. AR management includes allergen avoidance, pharmacological therapy, and immunotherapy.

The preventive treatment for AR involves allergen avoidance, humidity control, and high-efficiency particulate air filters[6]. The primary treatment options for AR consist of H1 antihistamines, intranasal corticosteroids (INCS), or a combination of both, tailored to symptom severity. Various pharmacological treatments are available for AR, each of which targets a different aspect of the condition. Antihistamines effectively reduce symptoms such as sneezing and itching, with intranasal options, such as azelastine, offering faster relief[7]. Other treatment options include INCS, suppression of inflammatory cytokines to mitigate nasal inflammation, leukotriene receptor antagonists, mast cell stabilizers, and biological therapies such as omalizumab (anti-IgE) and dupilumab (IL-4/IL-13 inhibitor), which are reserved for severe or refractory cases[7,8]. Each treatment strategy is tailored based on symptom severity, patient preference, and underlying conditions to optimize AR management. Rupatadine is gaining recognition as a dual-action antihistamine with H1 receptor and platelet-activating factor (PAF) antagonist, making it particularly effective against AR[9]. It is a second-generation antihistamine known for its rapid action, long-lasting symptomatic relief, and minimal sedation, effectively alleviating nasal and ocular itching symptoms. A recent network meta-analysis by Hong et al compared rupatadine with other antihistamines and highlighted its role in alleviating nasal symptoms[10]. High doses of rupatadine showed a higher responder rate, quicker onset of action, and prolonged symptom relief in patients with perennial allergic rhinitis (PAR) compared to placebo[11]. Extensive individual patient data trials conducted in 2019 and 2024 advocate the efficacy of rupatadine fumarate for AR[12,13]. However, a meta-analysis examining the safety and efficacy of rupatadine is lacking.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis available in the literature to assess randomized controlled trials (RCTs) focusing solely on rupatadine in managing AR, while addressing key gaps in the current literature. Its novelty lies in the comprehensive evaluation – unlike previous trials that were constrained by small sample sizes, diverse populations, or methodological inconsistencies, this systematic review and meta-analysis seeks to integrate high-quality evidence to derive a robust, pooled estimate of rupatadine’s efficacy and safety in clinical practice for both seasonal and PAR.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and followed the PRISMA and TITAN 2025 reporting guidelines for transparency and responsible AI disclosure in research workflows[14–16]. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO before commencing the review (CRD420250651901).

The methodological quality of this systematic review was assessed and reported in line with the AMSTAR 2 (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews) guidelines to ensure rigor and reproducibility[17].

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception to 30 April 2025. The search was originally planned until February 2025, as per the registered PROSPERO protocol, but was extended to 30 April 2025 to maximize the retrieval of recent eligible trials; this amendment was made prior to data extraction. A sensitivity-maximizing single-concept drug-based search strategy was employed across all four databases without combining with condition terms, as rupatadine has a narrow therapeutic indication, and adding rhinitis terms paradoxically reduced retrieval. All retrieved records were screened manually to identify eligible RCTs, consistent with Cochrane sensitivity-maximizing search principles. Reference lists of all included studies were manually reviewed to identify additional eligible trials. Gray literature and non-English studies were excluded; given that rupatadine trials are predominantly conducted as registered industry-sponsored RCTs indexed in major databases and published in English, the likelihood of meaningful selection bias from these exclusions is considered low.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: Population: Adult patients diagnosed with AR; Intervention: rupatadine; Comparator: placebo; and Outcome: efficacy in reducing symptoms of AR and side effect profile. Only studies that were RCTs in design were included. Non-randomized studies, observational designs (cohorts, case reports, and series), animal research, reviews, and studies not in the English language were excluded.

Screening and data extraction

Articles identified through the database search were imported into Rayyan AI (Rayyan Systems, Inc.), where duplicates were removed automatically[18]. Two independent reviewers (AR and AI) screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts according to the eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with the senior investigator (H.I.). Data extraction was performed using a structured Excel spreadsheet (M.A.K. and H.A.A.R.), collecting details such as author, publication year, study location, sample size, study design, and demographics, including mean age in years, sex distribution, dose of rupatadine, primary disease type of AR, comorbidities, and duration of follow-up.

Use of artificial intelligence tools

During the study selection process, the Rayyan AI platform (Rayyan Systems Inc., web-based version, accessed April 2025) was used to assist with duplicate removal and to facilitate the title and abstract screening of retrieved records. Only bibliographic metadata (titles, abstracts, and DOIs) from publicly available studies were uploaded, with no patient or identifiable data involved. All screening decisions suggested by Rayyan were independently verified by two human reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus with the senior investigator to ensure accuracy and reduce bias.

Additionally, Paperpal for Manuscripts (Writefull B.V., version 2025.1, accessed June 2025) was employed after the initial draft for English language refinement and style correction.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the reduction in the total nasal symptom score (TNSS). The TNSS is a composite score comprising four individual nasal symptom domains: nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, nasal itching, and sneezing. Each domain is rated on a four-point categorical scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe), yielding a total TNSS range of 0–12, where lower scores indicate greater symptom relief. When studies used a six-point scale (0–3 per symptom with half-point increments) or a visual analog scale (VAS, 0–100 mm), scores were recorded as reported, and standardized mean differences (SMD) were used in pooling to account for scale heterogeneity.

Secondary outcomes included individual domain scores for nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, nasal itching, and sneezing (each 0–3, lower = better), ocular itching (0–3, lower = better), and quality of life (QoL) assessed via the Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RQLQ, range 0–6 per domain, lower = better) or equivalent validated instruments where reported. For QoL outcomes, lower scores on all instruments (RQLQ and work productivity and activity impairment (WPAI)) indicate better health-related QoL; thus, a negative SMD favors rupatadine. SMD was chosen due to different scales used by the reporting studies.

For all outcomes, change-from-baseline scores were extracted preferentially where reported. Where only end-of-treatment absolute scores were available, these were used directly. The time point analyzed was the final scheduled assessment of each trial (ranging from 2 to 12 weeks, depending on study duration). Where multiple time points were reported, the end-of-treatment time point was selected for the primary analysis to ensure consistency across studies.

Risk of bias assessment

To evaluate methodological quality, the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB-2) was used across all included studies[19]. This instrument systematically assesses potential bias within five critical domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain contributed to an overall risk rating, categorized as low risk, high risk, or some concern. Two reviewers independently performed the assessments (S.M. and A.I.) to ensure methodological rigor and reproducibility. Any discrepancies were resolved collaboratively through consensus with a senior investigator (H.I.), thereby maintaining consistency and reliability in bias evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using RevMan version 5.4.1 (Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, 2020). For continuous outcomes, the inverse variance method was used, and the results were expressed as SMD and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). SMD was chosen as the effect measure to accommodate variation in symptom scoring instruments across trials (four-point categorical scales, six-point scales, and VAS), allowing pooling of conceptually equivalent outcomes measured on different scales. Where studies reported medians and interquartile ranges, data were converted to mean ± standard deviation (SD) using a standardized method. In multi-arm trials including both rupatadine 10 mg and 20 mg arms, the two rupatadine dose arms were combined into a single intervention group using weighted means and pooled SDs, and compared against the single placebo group without splitting, as the placebo was used only once in a single pairwise comparison. Active comparator arms were excluded from all pooled analyses. Dichotomous outcomes were analyzed using the Mantel-Haenszel method, with results presented as risk ratios and 95% CIs[20]. A random-effects model was applied to account for inter-study variability, and pooled estimates were visualized using forest plots[21].

Heterogeneity was quantified using the Higgins I2 statistic[22]. Heterogeneity was quantified using the Higgins I2 statistic and Tau2 (τ2), with I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% considered to represent low, moderate, and substantial heterogeneity, respectively. In anticipation of substantial heterogeneity, given the diversity of study populations, dosing regimens, and AR phenotypes, a random-effects model was pre-specified for all analyses. Pre-planned methods to explore heterogeneity sources included subgroup analysis by rupatadine dose (10 mg vs. 20 mg) and narrative exploration of clinical and methodological diversity across trials.

Results

Study characteristics

Eight RCTs with a total sample size of 2442 patients, including 1430 participants in the rupatadine group and 1012 in the control group, conducted between 2004 and 2024 across various countries (Spain, Argentina, Poland, France, Germany, Japan, and Korea), were included in this meta-analysis. These studies evaluated both seasonal allergic rhinitis (SAR) and PAR, with treatment durations ranging from 2 to 12 weeks. The included trials compared rupatadine with a placebo and, in some studies, with other antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, ebastine, desloratadine, and bepotastine). Outcome variables and overall results

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across most outcomes, consistent with the diversity of study populations, AR phenotypes, dosing regimens, and follow-up durations among the included trials. 8. Subgroup analysis by dose

A subgroup analysis was performed based on studies that compared the effects of 10 mg and 20 mg rupatadine. The results for the outcomes are discussed below, and the forest plots are available in Supplemental Digital Content Figures S3–S8, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B244.

TNSS by dose

Subgroup analysis comparing the 10 mg and 20 mg rupatadine subgroups for the TNSS showed an overall effect size of −1.47 (95% CI: −2.08, −0.87; P < 0.0001), confirming the efficacy of rupatadine in reducing nasal symptoms compared to placebo. The 20 mg subgroup showed a numerically larger SMD (SMD = −2.70) than the 10 mg subgroup (SMD = −1.20), supporting a possible dose–response relationship (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S3, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B244).

Nasal congestion by dose

The subgroup analysis of nasal congestion, comparing the 10 mg and 20 mg rupatadine subgroups, showed an overall effect size of −0.72 (95% CI: −1.08, −0.36; P < 0.0001). The 20 mg subgroup had a larger estimated SMD (SMD = −1.07), indicating greater symptom reduction at higher doses, whereas the 10 mg group also showed significant improvement (SMD = −0.60; Supplemental Digital Content Figure S4, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B244).

Rhinorrhea by dose

Subgroup analysis comparing the 10 mg and 20 mg rupatadine subgroups for improvement in rhinorrhea showed an overall effect size of −1.44 (95% CI: −2.06, −0.82; P < 0.0001). The 20 mg subgroup showed a larger but non-significant SMD (SMD = −2.37), whereas the 10 mg subgroup was smaller but statistically significant and consistent across studies (SMD = −1.21; Supplemental Digital Content Figure S5, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B244).

Nasal itching by dose

Subgroup analysis comparing the 10 mg and 20 mg rupatadine subgroups for improvement in nasal itching showed an overall effect size of −0.92 (95% CI: −1.78, −0.06; P = 0.04). 9. Adverse Events

9. Adverse Events

Across all eight included trials, no serious adverse events meeting ICH-E2A criteria (defined as events resulting in death, hospitalization, persistent disability, life-threatening conditions, or congenital anomalies) were reported in either the rupatadine or placebo groups. No adverse events resulted in premature study discontinuation or withdrawal in any included trial.

Overall, adverse event rates were numerically higher in rupatadine-treated patients compared with placebo across trials, though no statistically significant between-group differences in total adverse event incidence were reported. Somnolence was the most consistently reported adverse drug reaction attributable to rupatadine, with rates varying by dose: rupatadine 10 mg (range: 7.0–11.0%), rupatadine 20 mg (range: 7.3–20.0%), and placebo (<1.0–3.0%). The higher somnolence rates observed with rupatadine 20 mg compared with 10 mg were noted in individual trials but did not reach clinical significance thresholds, and all episodes were self-limiting and classified as Grade 1 severity, where grading was reported. No clinically significant QTc prolongation, hepatotoxicity, or other serious adverse drug reactions were identified across included trials.

Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesizes evidence from eight RCTs encompassing 2442 patients, evaluating rupatadine in AR, and offers one of the most comprehensive overviews to date regarding its dual-receptor pharmacodynamics, clinical efficacy, and therapeutic role in AR management. In our pooled analysis, rupatadine significantly improved the TNSS and individual domains, including nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, sneezing, and nasal itching, compared with placebo, confirming its broad efficacy across both seasonal and PAR. These findings are consistent with earlier single-center trials that reported similar symptom reduction, particularly in congestion and sneezing[11,23,24]. Our analysis strengthened this evidence by providing quantitative confirmation across diverse populations and study designs. Importantly, the absence of serious adverse events aligns with prior pharmacovigilance data, reinforcing the favorable safety profile[25,26]. Somnolence, while more frequent with rupatadine than placebo, particularly at the 20 mg dose, was uniformly mild and self-limiting across all trials, and no withdrawals due to adverse events were recorded. This distinguishes rupatadine favorably from first-generation antihistamines and is consistent with its minimal central nervous system penetration. Clinicians should nonetheless counsel patients regarding the possibility of mild somnolence, particularly at treatment initiation with the 20 mg dose. However, the limited improvement in quality-of-life scores, despite robust symptom control, echoes observations from other antihistamine trials, suggesting that short-term studies may underestimate the broader functional impact of treatment[27,28].

Based on these findings, subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential dose–response relationships and assess whether higher doses of rupatadine confer additional therapeutic benefits.

Our dose-based subgroup analyses suggest that rupatadine 20 mg may confer greater reductions in nasal congestion and sneezing than the standard 10 mg dose, which is consistent with prior findings[11,24]. However, the interpretation of this dose–response gradient is complex. Should this be viewed as evidence for routine up-titration in refractory cases or as a justification for repositioning rupatadine 20 mg as the standard dose? The answer hinges on the safety, tolerability, and marginal benefits. The included trials did not show a commensurate increase in adverse events at higher doses, and previous pharmacokinetic data confirmed a wide therapeutic window[29].

Our study confirms and extends earlier syntheses. Hong et al compared multiple oral H1-antihistamines and reported rupatadine as an effective agent for nasal symptom control but evaluated it in the context of class-wide comparisons rather than as a single-molecule pooled estimate[10]. Likewise, pooled responder and pooled data analyses have previously suggested greater efficacy at higher rupatadine doses (e.g., 20 mg) for perennial rhinitis[11]. Compared to previous studies, our analysis differs in three important ways. First, by restricting to randomized trials of rupatadine only, we provide a direct, molecule-specific pooled effect size for total nasal symptoms and each individual nasal domain (congestion, rhinorrhea, sneezing, and itching), rather than a relative ranking within an antihistamine network. Second, we observed a clear and consistent signal of a dose–response favoring 20 mg across multiple outcomes (notably congestion and sneezing), strengthening prior pooled observations that suggested a higher dose benefit. Third, although some broader analyses and trials have reported modest improvements in patient-reported QoL, our pooled QoL estimate did not reach statistical significance. It is important to note that the non-significant QoL result does not reflect an absence of effect but rather a lack of statistical precision driven by only two contributing trials with heterogeneous measurement instruments and short follow-up periods. The wide CI (−4.02 to 0.99) crossing zero renders the pooled estimate inconclusive. Future trials should prespecify QoL as a primary endpoint, utilize disease-specific validated tools such as the RQLQ consistently, and ensure adequate follow-up duration of at least 8–12 weeks to detect meaningful changes in sleep, productivity, and psychosocial functioning. Overall, our findings complement previous network and pooled analyses by delivering a molecule-level quantification of rupatadine’s efficacy, clarifying the dose relationship, and underscoring persistent uncertainty about QoL benefits in short-term RCTs.

Beyond these surface-level findings, we also investigated the mechanistic, clinical, and strategic dimensions of the role of rupatadine, drawing attention to unresolved questions and opportunities for research and innovation in AR therapeutics.

Dose escalation in antihistamines often leads to cumulative sedative effects, particularly in real-world polypharmacy contexts. Although rupatadine has minimal penetration into the central nervous system, individual variability in blood–brain barrier permeability or cytochrome P450 metabolism could render some patients vulnerable. A precision medicine approach that incorporates pharmacogenomics and weight-based titration may help resolve this uncertainty.

Although symptom-based outcomes were significantly improved, QoL metrics failed to reach statistical significance in the pooled analysis. This discordance requires further investigation into the potential overreliance on physician-reported symptom scores, which may neglect the patient’s perspective and overall experience. QoL in AR is influenced by sleep quality, productivity, and psychosocial burden, and previous meta-analyses have reported variable QoL benefits, likely due to heterogeneity in instruments and follow-up. Our drug-specific pooled analysis failed to show consistent QoL improvement[27]. The included studies used heterogeneous QoL tools, often lacking disease-specific sensitivity (e.g., RQLQ or WPAI). Moreover, trial durations are often short (2–4 weeks), which may be insufficient for detecting meaningful changes in sleep patterns, absenteeism, or social functioning.

It is also possible that treatment fatigue, even with non-sedating drugs, blunts long-term adherence and the perceived benefits. Future studies should incorporate qualitative outcomes and patient-centered endpoints aligned with ARIA's 2020s shift toward integrated care models[28,30].

While traditional second-generation antihistamines act primarily via selective H1 antagonism, rupatadine acts by antagonizing the PAF receptor. This is more than a biochemical novelty; it has potential pathophysiological significance. PAF has been implicated in late-phase allergic responses by promoting leukocyte recruitment, microvascular leakage, and mucosal inflammation[9]. Standard antihistamines largely target early-phase mediators (e.g., histamine), but the dual mechanism of rupatadine may inhibit both phases of the allergic cascade. This is especially relevant for PAR, in which chronic low-grade inflammation dominates the intermittent allergen spikes[25].

However, despite this theoretical advantage, there is limited direct comparative evidence showing that rupatadine leads to reduced eosinophilia, nasal biopsies with lower inflammatory infiltrates, or cytokine modulation compared to other antihistamines. Only a few studies have measured surrogate inflammatory biomarkers[31]. Thus, biological plausibility remains ahead of empirical confirmation – a gap that future studies must address.

Clinical guidelines often recommend second-generation antihistamines as interchangeable agents. However, this “class effect” approach obscures real differences in the onset of action, receptor-binding affinity, metabolic half-life, and off-target profiles[10,26]. Therapy selection should, therefore, shift from class-based to molecule-specific decision-making, particularly in patients with refractory congestion, nocturnal symptoms, or partial response to standard therapy. Moreover, the anti-PAF effect may render rupatadine preferable in multimorbid patients, such as those with asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, or mast-cell activation disorders, although high-quality trials in these subgroups are lacking.

Most trials included in this analysis were conducted in high- or middle-income countries, limiting the generalizability to resource-limited settings where AR remains underdiagnosed and undertreated[32]. Rupatadine’s patent status, cost, and market access variability may affect its global uptake. Availability as an over-the-counter or prescription product differs by region, and pricing remains a barrier in some Asian and Latin American markets. The future of AR management must reconcile scientific merit with health system realities, emphasizing cost-effectiveness and availability in primary care settings.

Limitations

Although this meta-analysis provides robust pooled estimates, its conclusions are constrained by several limitations. Among these, the high statistical heterogeneity (I2 > 90%) likely stems from variations in the study populations and dosing regimens. The pre-planned dose subgroup analysis partially addressed this heterogeneity by identifying a consistent dose-response signal favoring 20 mg, though residual heterogeneity remained high within subgroups, suggesting additional unmeasured sources such as baseline symptom severity, geographic variation in allergen exposure, and differences in outcome assessment timepoints. Sensitivity analyses excluding high-risk-of-bias studies were not performed due to the uniformly low ROB across included trials, and funnel plot asymmetry assessment was limited by the small number of included studies (n = 8). Subgroup analysis by AR phenotype (SAR vs. PAR) was not performed due to the unequal distribution of trial types (five PAR and three SAR) and the inclusion of mixed populations in several trials, which would render such analyses underpowered and potentially misleading. The lack of standardized metrics assessing blinding quality and limited reporting on long-term safety outcomes further restricts the interpretability and generalizability of the findings. Exclusion of gray literature and non-English studies represents an additional limitation, though its impact on the pooled estimates is expected to be minimal given the nature of the rupatadine trial evidence base. Finally, a formal GRADE Summary of Findings table was not produced; future updates of this review should incorporate GRADE evidence profiling to facilitate translation of findings into clinical guideline recommendations. To address these gaps, future studies should prioritize individual patient data-based meta-analyses stratified by phenotype, dose, and age. Mechanistic trials exploring biomarker modulation, particularly involving eosinophils and the IL-4/IL-13 axis, are warranted to elucidate therapeutic pathways. Moreover, implementation studies in primary care and pediatric settings, along with comparative effectiveness trials using pragmatic endpoints such as missed workdays or emergency department visits, will be essential to inform real-world clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

This review confirms that rupatadine is an effective and well-tolerated option for AR, especially in patients with nasal blockage-dominant phenotypes or those inadequately controlled with standard antihistamines. Its dual-action mechanism, rapid onset, and dose flexibility offer unique advantages but also raise important questions about optimal dosing, real-world utility, and individualized care. A shift toward molecule-specific, patient-centered therapy is needed, and rupatadine may lead to the next generation of AR pharmacotherapy if evidence keeps pace with its potential.
