Comparative assessment of image quality and radiation dose: spectral purification computed tomography (Sn100kV) with adaptive iterative reconstruction for tuberculosis follow-up
Authors: Chao Yuan, Jie Yang, Yantao Huang, Chun Qin, Chao Wang, Hao Feng, Jianquan Zhong, Chaoliang He, Hongfu Dan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring tuberculosis treatment, multiple chest computed tomography (CT) scans are required for follow-up, each with an effective radiation dose (ED) of approximately 7 mSv. If treatment outcomes are poor or complications arise, additional scans may be necessary, thereby increasing the cumulative radiation burden. This study aimed to evaluate the effects on image quality of low-dose CT using spectral purification technology, in combination with varying levels of sinogram-affirmed iterative reconstruction (SAFIRE) algorithms, with the goal of identifying the optimal SAFIRE level for imaging in follow-up of active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis in adults.MethodsA total of 90 patients diagnosed with active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis between January and May 2024 undergoing follow-up chest CT scans after treatment were randomly assigned to three groups: conventional dose scanning (Group A), optimal tube voltage scanning with intelligence (Group B), and low-dose scanning (Group C). In Groups A and B, images were reconstructed using SAFIRE level 3 (A3 and B3), while Group C images were reconstructed with SAFIRE levels 1 through 5 (C1 to C5). The overall image quality was evaluated, double-blind, independently by two senior radiologists. Radiation doses, subjective assessments of image quality, and objective image quality metrics were analyzed and compared across the groups.ResultsThe ED was significantly lower in Group C compared to Groups A and B (0.28±0.05 vs. 2.79±0.65 and 1.75±0.47 mSv respectively, P<0.001). Within Group C, image noise decreased and both the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) increased with higher SAFIRE levels. SNR was significantly higher in the C5 group compared to the C1 to C4 groups (P<0.05). Subjective assessments indicated significant differences in the visibility of lung fissures, peripheral bronchi, adjacent pulmonary vessels, pleura, and subpleural regions between different SAFIRE levels (P<0.05). The best subjective scores for visualization of parenchymal lesions, including patches and nodules, were at SAFIRE level 2, while images reconstructed at level 5 scored significantly lower (P<0.05). Consistency in subjective evaluations between the C1 to C5 groups was high (Kappa =0.682).ConclusionsThe combination of spectral purification technology with SAFIRE reconstruction effectively reduced radiation exposure and image noise, and maintained diagnostic adequacy during follow-up for patients with active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis.Keywords: Low-dose computed tomography (low-dose CT), tuberculosis, radiation dose, spectral purification technology, iterative reconstruction

Highlight box

Key findings

• Low-dose computed tomography (CT) with spectral purification technology (Group C) significantly reduced the effective radiation dose (ED) by approximately 89.9% compared to conventional dose CT (Group A) while maintaining diagnostic image quality.

• Among different sinogram-affirmed iterative reconstruction (SAFIRE) levels, level 2 provided the best subjective image quality for visualizing key pulmonary parenchymal lesions such as nodules and exudates, whereas higher levels (e.g., level 5) resulted in lower clarity despite better noise reduction.

What is known and what is new?

• Chest CT is crucial for monitoring tuberculosis treatment, but repeated conventional scans raise radiation concerns. Low-dose CT protocols and iterative reconstruction algorithms are established methods for reducing radiation dose and improving image quality.

• This study specifically evaluates the novel combination of spectral purification technology with different levels of SAFIRE iterative reconstruction for follow-up after treatment of active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis, identifying SAFIRE level 2 as the optimal setting for lesion assessment.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The combination of low-dose CT spectral purification and SAFIRE level 2 reconstruction enabled a substantial reduction in radiation exposure (average ED: 0.28 mSv) without compromising diagnostic assessment of tuberculous lesions. This protocol should be considered for clinical adoption in the follow-up of patients with active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis to enhance patient safety by minimizing cumulative radiation risk.

Introduction

Tuberculosis remains a major global health concern due to its high incidence and significant infectious potential (1). For patients with secondary pulmonary tuberculosis, accurately assessing the progression or resolution of pulmonary lesions during treatment is essential for guiding clinical decision-making (2,3). Chest computed tomography (CT) is recognized as more sensitive than traditional X-ray radiography for monitoring the effectiveness of tuberculosis treatment and is widely employed in clinical settings. However, patients with active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis typically require multiple CT scans throughout the course of treatment. Repeated exposure to conventional dose chest CT scans raises concerns about cumulative radiation exposure, which may increase the risk of radiation-induced injury and the potential for cancer development (4-6). Adherence to the “As Low As Reasonably Achievable” principle is critical, which advocates for minimizing radiation doses while still achieving sufficient diagnostic quality.

To mitigate the risks associated with radiation exposure, several approaches have been developed in clinical practice. One effective strategy for reducing radiation dose is to optimize CT scan protocols (7). Key parameters, such as tube voltage, tube current, pitch, detector width, scan mode, and reconstruction algorithms, can be adjusted to minimize dose without compromising diagnostic accuracy (8). While there has been extensive research on low-dose lung CT, particularly for evaluating pulmonary nodules (9,10), fewer studies have focused on low-dose lung CT for the follow-up of tuberculosis using spectral purification technology. A common issue with low-dose CT is the increase in image noise and the corresponding decline in image quality. To address these challenges, advanced techniques such as iterative reconstruction algorithms and deep learning have been introduced to reduce noise and improve both image quality and diagnostic precision (11-14). However, there remains limited investigation into the effect of varying levels of iterative reconstruction algorithms on image quality during low-dose lung CT in tuberculosis patients.

Tin has a relatively high atomic number, with a K-edge energy of 29.2 keV. Tin filtration actively and controllably “pre-hardens” the X-ray beam before it leaves the tube and reaches the patient. While preserving lung tissue contrast, tin filtration blocks low-energy photons that contribute significantly to patient dose but are useless for imaging, thereby reducing patient radiation exposure (15,16). Clear visualization of the fine structure (e.g., dendritic patterns) of pulmonary tuberculosis lesions strongly suggests active or infectious disease. Existing studies evaluating pulmonary tuberculosis lesions have rarely employed iterative reconstruction techniques for grade-related assessment. In this study, we combined low-dose scanning technology with different sinogram-affirmed iterative reconstruction (SAFIRE) grades to evaluate the visualization of pulmonary lesions.

This study aimed to assess the impact of different levels of SAFIRE algorithms on image quality in follow-up chest CT scans of adult patients with active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis. By comparing radiation doses and image quality across different patient groups, the study aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of the use of low-dose lung CT in tuberculosis management. Our results contribute to the growing body of literature supporting the application of low-dose CT protocols for tuberculosis follow-up, promoting safer imaging practices while maintaining clinical efficacy. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2330/rc).

Methods

Study population

Ninety patients who underwent follow-up examinations after treatment for active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis, diagnosed at the Zigong First People’s Hospital between January and May 2024, were selected for this study. The inclusion criteria were: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) positive Mycobacterium tuberculosis results from sputum smear or culture; (III) CT imaging revealing pulmonary lesions such as a tree-in-bud pattern, nodules, patchy shadows with indistinct margins, and liquid bronchogram signs (indicative of active secondary pulmonary tuberculosis); and (IV) good compliance and willingness to cooperate. The exclusion criteria were: (I) refusal to participate or inability to adhere to respiratory training; (II) presence of other severe pulmonary conditions; and (III) interference caused by chest metal artifacts. A total of 90 patients were allocated (using a random approach) into three groups: A, B, and C, each containing 30 individuals (Figure 1). All participants were thoroughly briefed on the lung CT follow-up procedure, including details of the scanning equipment, parameters, and associated protocols. This retrospective study received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Zigong First People’s Hospital [No. Ethics (M) 2024-031]. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Informed consent was obtained from each participant, who signed the “Informed Consent Form for Low-Dose Lung CT Examination in Secondary Pulmonary Tuberculosis” prior to the commencement of the study.

Flowchart of the study design. CT, computed tomography; SAFIRE, sinogram-affirmed iterative reconstruction.

CT image acquisition

CT examinations were performed using a Siemens 64-slice SOMATOM go. Top scanner. Patients were positioned in a supine, head-first orientation, with arms elevated, and the scans were conducted from the apex to the base of the lungs, during a deep breath hold, to cover the entire pulmonary parenchyma. The scanning parameters varied by group: Group A received conventional dose imaging with a tube voltage of 100 kV and a reference tube current of 138 mAs. Group B was scanned using an intelligent optimal tube voltage setting (Care kV) that adjusted between 70 kV and 140 kV, maintaining a reference tube current of 138 mAs. Group C underwent low-dose imaging, utilizing a tube voltage of Sn 100 kV and a reference tube current of 128 mAs. All groups employed automatic tube current modulation. Additional scan parameters included a pitch of 1.30, collimator width of 64 mm × 0.6 mm, tube rotation time of 0.5 seconds, and slice thickness of 5 mm.

Image reconstruction

The reconstruction of seven sets of CT images was performed as follows: Group A images were reconstructed using iterative reconstruction at SAFIRE level 3, denoted A3; Group B images were also reconstructed at SAFIRE level 3, denoted B3; while Group C images were reconstructed at SAFIRE levels 1 through 5, designated as C1 to C5. All reconstructions were carried out in the axial plane, with a slice thickness of 1 mm and an interval of 0.7 mm, utilizing the Br40 convolution kernel. The reconstructed images were subsequently transferred to the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) for evaluation and analysis.

Radiation dose

The dose length product (DLP) and the volume CT dose index (CTDIvol) were automatically recorded for each patient’s chest CT scan, excluding radiation doses from positioning scans. The effective radiation dose (ED) was calculated using the formula ED = DLP × k, where k is 0.014 mSv/(mGy·cm−1) (17).

Subjective evaluation of image quality

The overall quality of images from groups A3, B3, and C1 to C5 was evaluated using a five-point scale. The same scale was applied to assess pulmonary parenchymal lesions using different iterative reconstruction algorithms (C1 to C5). The evaluation window width was set to 1,200 Hounsfield unit (HU), and the window level to −600 HU.

Overall image quality assessment

The overall image quality was evaluated independently by two senior radiologists using a double-blind method, focusing on four key anatomical lung structures: the lung fissures, proximal bronchi and adjacent pulmonary vessels, peripheral bronchi and adjacent pulmonary vessels, and the pleura with subpleural lung regions (18). Visibility of these structures was graded on a five-point scale: 1= indistinct margins, unrecognizable; 2= blurry margins, poor visualization; 3= acceptable margins, moderate visualization; 4= suboptimal margins, good visualization; and 5= clear margins, excellent visualization. A score of 4 or above was considered adequate to meet the diagnostic imaging requirements (19).

Significance assessment of pulmonary lesions using different SAFIRE reconstruction algorithms in Group C

Pulmonary abnormalities were classified into seven categories: lesion exudates, pulmonary nodules (both solid and non-solid), consolidation, cavitation, pleural thickening, bronchiectasis, and calcification. Images from 180 lung lobes of 30 patients (right upper, middle, and lower lobes; left upper, lingula, and lower lobes) were analyzed. Image quality of these specific pulmonary abnormalities was evaluated independently by two senior radiologists using a five-point scale: 1= non-diagnostic image quality; 2= severe blurring of structures, making assessment difficult; 3= moderate blurring of structures, slightly impeding assessment; 4= minor blurring of structures, no limitations on assessment; 5= excellent image quality with clear structural delineation (14). A score of 4 or higher was considered to meet the diagnostic requirements for imaging.

Objective evaluation of image quality

At the level of the tracheal bifurcation, a region of interest measuring 50±5 mm2 was defined to assess the CT values and noise [standard deviation (SD)] in the descending aorta and infraspinatus. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was calculated using the formula: (SNR = CTdescending aorta/SDdescending aorta). Additionally, the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) was determined using the formula [CNR = (CTdescending aorta−CTinfraspinatus muscle)/SDinfraspinatus muscle].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. The χ2 test was employed for gender comparisons, while age, body mass index (BMI), radiation dose, and image evaluation scores were assessed using one-way analysis of variance. The normality of continuous variables was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data conforming to a normal distribution, results were presented as mean ± SD (x¯±SD), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the Tukey post-hoc test. In cases where data deviated from a normal distribution or exhibited unequal variances, the Games-Howell test was applied for group comparisons. The inter-observer consistency between the two radiologists was assessed using the Kappa coefficient. A Kappa value of <0.2 indicated poor agreement, 0.21–0.40 indicated fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 indicated moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 indicated good agreement, and values >0.8 indicated very good agreement. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical information

Of the 90 patients, 61 were male and 29 were female, with ages ranging from 18 to 83 years (mean age: 55.9±18.0 years). BMI (kg/m2) was calculated as body mass (kg) divided by height squared (m2). There were no statistically significant differences in gender (P=0.52), age (P=0.87) and BMI (P=0.18) among the three groups (Table 1).

Radiation dose comparison

For patients in Groups A, B, and C, with consistent smart tube current and other scanning parameters, the CTDIvol, DLP, and ED values in Group B were all significantly lower than those in Group A (3.68±0.87 vs. 6.08±1.53 mGy/124.64±33.74 vs. 199.23±46.184 mGy·cm/1.75±0.47 vs. 2.79±0.65 mSv, P<0.001). Notably, the ED values in Group C were approximately 90% lower than those in Group A and 75% lower than those in Group B (Figure 2).

Comparison of objective image evaluation

Statistically significant differences were observed in image noise, SNR, and CNR between the groups (P<0.001). In Groups A3, B3, and C, as the radiation dose decreased, image noise increased while both SNR and CNR decreased, with statistically significant differences (A3:B3 P=0.008; A3:C3 P<0.001; C3:B3 P<0.001). Within Group C, increasing the level of iterative reconstruction led to a decrease in image noise, accompanied by a significant increase in both SNR and CNR, with all differences being statistically significant (HU/SNR C5: C1–C4, P<0.001; <0.001; <0.001; 0.001/P<0.001; <0.001; <0.001; 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study conducted a comparative analysis of radiation doses across three patient groups, demonstrating that low-dose pulmonary CT using spectral purification technology at 100 kV significantly reduced radiation exposure in follow-up examinations after treatment of secondary pulmonary tuberculosis. A lower radiation dose inevitably increases image noise and compromises image quality, but the application of iterative reconstruction techniques can mitigate noise and enhance image clarity (20). Subjective assessments in this study indicated that higher levels of iterative reconstruction effectively reduced image noise; however, further increments in reconstruction intensity led to a decline in image clarity. Notably, image reconstruction at SAFIRE level 2 proved to be the most effective, producing images that met clinical diagnostic criteria when using low-dose pulmonary CT with spectral purification technology at 100 kV.

Chest CT scans are a valuable tool in diagnosing and monitoring pulmonary lesions, particularly for tuberculosis treatment evaluation (21). However, frequent or inappropriate use of CT scanning increases the risk of radiation-induced harm. In clinical settings, radiation exposure can be minimized by optimizing parameters such as tube voltage, tube current, and reconstruction algorithms (22). Spectral purification technology enhances the efficiency of X-ray photon use by placing a tin filter in front of the X-ray tube to filter out low-energy X-rays, thereby raising the average energy of the remaining X-rays and reducing the radiation dose delivered to the patient (23). This study demonstrated that applying spectral purification technology at 100 kV reduced the radiation dose by approximately 90% compared to conventional 100 kV scanning and by 75% compared to Care kV scanning. Group C achieved a statistically significant 84% reduction in radiation dose relative to Group B. The Group C ED averaged 0.28 mSv, substantially lower than the 0.8–1.5 mSv typically reported for standard low-dose CT scans (24), representing safer imaging, particularly in vulnerable populations such as minors and pregnant women undergoing tuberculosis-related CT examinations.

Previous research suggested that low-dose CT scans maintained high diagnostic accuracy for pulmonary parenchymal lesions, despite increased image noise (25). Consistent with these findings, we observed sequential increases in image noise across Groups A3, B3, and C1 to C5, with corresponding reductions in SNR and CNR. However, diagnostic requirements were met for all pulmonary structures, in line with previous work by Martini et al. (25). The use of iterative reconstruction technology allows low-dose CT scans to reduce image noise and improve image quality (20), although research specifically addressing varying levels of iterative reconstruction in tuberculosis-related pulmonary CT remains limited. This study demonstrated that spectral purification technology at 100 kV reduced the radiation dose by nearly 90% compared to conventional 100 kV protocols. Image noise levels varied across groups; Group C1 exhibited the highest noise, followed by C2, with moderate noise in C3 and C4, and the least noise in C5. Higher levels of iterative reconstruction corresponded to improved SNR.

Among different SAFIRE reconstruction levels, basic visualization of normal lung structures was achieved under conventional dose conditions. Although SAFIRE level 5 resulted in lower subjective scores for lung fissures than levels 1 through 4, level 2 provided optimal visualization. As the SAFIRE level increased, both SNR and CNR improved, with significant reductions in image noise. However, in the evaluation of abnormal pulmonary structures under the 100 kV spectral purification protocol, visualization of both solid and non-solid pulmonary nodules was improved in C2 compared to levels 3 to 5, likely due to enhanced SNR and spatial resolution. Nevertheless, performance across SAFIRE levels for diagnosing solid nodules was comparable. Aside from lower visualization of lung fissures, pleura, subpleural lung, and nodules, SAFIRE level 5 demonstrated good capability in evaluating other pulmonary abnormalities.

BMI is one of the core factors affecting CT image quality. A key advantage of the Sn100kV protocol is its ability to reduce radiation dose. This benefit is most pronounced in thinner patients (low BMI), allowing the acquisition of high-contrast, diagnostic-quality images at extremely low radiation doses. For high BMI patients, to compensate for excessive image noise caused by increased tissue thickness, the system automatically, or manually, requires a significant increase in tube current. This largely negates the dosimetric benefits achieved by the Sn100kV protocol. Consequently, in high-BMI patients, the Sn100kV protocol may fail to achieve the intended low-dose objectives. It may even necessitate radiation doses comparable to or exceeding those of conventional kV protocols to maintain acceptable image quality, thereby undermining the core purpose of this technology.

Our study was limited by its focus on pulmonary parenchyma, excluding the evaluation of mediastinal structures, as the primary aim was to assess pulmonary findings in tuberculosis patients under low-dose conditions. Additionally, this study did not compare other reconstruction algorithms, such as ADMIRE, with SAFIRE. Future research will expand the scope to include a variety of reconstruction methods to enhance the reliability of assessments (25).

Conclusions

Low-dose CT spectral purification technology effectively reduced the ED for patients undergoing follow-up for treated secondary pulmonary tuberculosis, with an average dose of 0.28 mSv. This reduction potentially enhanced the safety of follow-up in such patients by minimizing the radiation risks including possible carcinogenicity. Combination with the SAFIRE level 2 reconstruction algorithm, provided the best image quality for evaluating pulmonary parenchymal lesions, thus improving the accuracy in assessing tuberculosis treatment.
