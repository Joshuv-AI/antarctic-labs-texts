Tooth mobility revealing a brown tumor associated with normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism: case report and literature review
Authors: Sara Boukssim, Amal Damiri, Bassima Chami
Published: 2026

Background

Hyperparathyroidism (HPT) is a disorder characterized by excessive secretion of parathyroid hormone (PTH), which disrupts calcium and phosphate homeostasis and leads to skeletal, renal, and systemic complications. Depending on the underlying cause, HPT is classified into primary (PHPT), secondary (SHPT), and tertiary (THPT) hyperparathyroidism [1]. PHPT is most often due to parathyroid adenomas [1], while SHPT typically develops in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) as a compensatory response to hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, and vitamin D deficiency [2]. THPT generally arises after long-standing SHPT, when parathyroid hyperplasia or adenomas acquire functional autonomy and PTH secretion persists despite correction of the original renal stimulus [3]. Classically, THPT is associated with hypercalcemia, but normocalcemic forms have been reported and remain poorly defined.

One of the skeletal manifestations of HPT is the development of brown tumors (BT), which are localized osteolytic lesions caused by excessive osteoclastic bone resorption and fibroblastic proliferation [4]. Although histologically benign, these lesions can mimic neoplasms and lead to functional and aesthetic problems. Brown tumors are rare in the jaws, with an estimated prevalence of 1–2% in SHPT and THPT cases [5]. They are more often described in association with PHPT, and their occurrence in normocalcemic HPT is exceptional.

Distinguishing between refractory SHPT and atypical THPT can be challenging, especially in dialysis patients who remain normocalcemic but exhibit persistently high PTH. Most surgical guidelines for parathyroidectomy emphasize hypercalcemia as the main indication in THPT [6], whereas indications for SHPT are clearer and usually relate to bone pain, fractures, pruritus, or calciphylaxis. Thus, cases of normocalcemic HPT with destructive bone lesions raise questions about optimal management.

The purpose of this paper is to present a rare case of mandibular brown tumor in a dialysis patient with severe normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism and a history of two parathyroidectomies. We discuss the diagnostic dilemma between secondary and tertiary forms of HPT and highlight the clinical implications for oral and maxillofacial surgeons in recognizing hyperparathyroidism in the differential diagnosis of jaw giant cell lesions.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria [7].

Case presentation

A 41-year-old north African male presented at the oral surgery service with a chief complain of mobility in the left second mandibular molar.

The medical history of the patient revealed a chronic kidney disease requiring hemodialysis 3 times a week since 2006, along with a past parathyroidectomy performed in 2017. Unfortunately, no operative report or histological data from this first surgery were available.

The extraoral examination revealed no facial asymmetry or deformity. Intraoral examination exposed a compromised oral hygiene with evident tartaric deposits. Palpation identified a firm, painless vestibular swelling involving the region of the left second mandibular molar. The swelling was covered with mucosa displaying normal color (Fig. 1).

Intraoral view showing a vestibular swelling in the region of the left second mandibular molar

Tooth 37 was vital and showed grade 3 mobility in the Miller Index.

The orthopantomographic examination revealed a well-defined peri apical radiolucent image attached to the apices of tooth 37 (Fig. 2).

Panoramic radiograph showing a well-defined radiolucent osteolytic lesion attached to the apices of tooth 37

It also revealed a generalized moderate horizontal bone loss.

Based on the past medical history, this lesion was highly suspicious for BT due to HPT.

Due to the poor prognosis of tooth 37, extraction was indicated.

The extraction was performed simultaneously with an incisional biopsy.

The histopathological examination showed a double contiguous tumor proliferation, characterized by a combination of mononuclear cells with multinucleated cells (Fig. 3).

Histological section of the mandibular lesion showing multinucleated giant cells distributed within a fibrous stroma, intermixed with areas of hemorrhage and hemosiderin deposition. The overlying oral mucosa is intact. Hematoxylin and eosin stain, original magnification ×40

This aspect is in favor of BT related to HPT.

To confirm this diagnostic, parathyroid hormone blood test and calcium/phosphorus balance were indicated. The biochemical investigations showed: an elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) level of 4868.0 pg./ml (normal range: 15.0–65.0 pg./ml), normocalcemia (94 mg/l, normal range: 86–100 mg/l), hyperphosphatemia (67 mg/l; normal range: 27–45 mg/l), Vitamin D levels of 22 ug/l (normal range: >20 ug/l).

The findings were compatible with a brown tumor in the context of long-standing renal disease and severe hyperparathyroidism. Although the clinical data suggested THPT, the persistent normocalcemia raised the possibility of refractory SHPT.

Further investigations were carried out, in search of secondary bone lesions.

Cephalometric radiography showed no additional bone lesions but exhibited a “salt and pepper” appearance of the skull associated with hyperparathyroidism (Fig. 4).

Lateral cephalometric radiograph demonstrating generalized loss of normal trabecular bone pattern with a granular “salt and pepper” appearance of the skull vault, characteristic of hyperparathyroidism-related bone changes

Whole-body bone scintigraphy (Tc-99 m MDP) demonstrated intense and homogeneous uptake in the mandible and skull, diffuse uptake in the axial skeleton, and intense heterogeneous uptake in the sternum. The imaging findings were consistent with patterns typically observed in hyperparathyroidism (Fig. 5).

Whole-body bone scintigraphy (technetium-99 m methylene diphosphonate, Tc-99 m MDP) showing multiple areas of increased tracer uptake in the craniofacial bones, ribs, pelvis, and long bones, consistent with metabolic bone disease secondary to hyperparathyroidism

Additionally, an 18 F-Fluorocholine PET-CT revealed the presence of residual parathyroid adenomas.

A second parathyroidectomy was conducted and the anatomopathological examination of the specimen confirmed the presence of parathyroid adenomas.

Two weeks after the surgery, the serum level of PTH dropped from 4868.0 pg./ml to 1762,60 pg/ml. The calcium level was at 92 mg/l. However, 3 months later, we observed an increase in PTH level reaching 2250,4pg/ml. The calcium level was at 96 mg/l (normal range: 86–100 mg/l)and phosphore level at 47 mg/l (normal range: 27–45 mg/l).

The patient was readdressed to endocrinologist for reevaluation.

In the follow-up period, after the parathyroidectomy, (7 months), the radiological image showed a decreased size of the tumor (Fig. 6).

Intraoral periapical radiograph of the left mandibular molar region at follow-up, showing a reduction in the size and radiolucency of the osteolytic lesion consistent with regression of the brown tumor after parathyroidectomy

Discussion

In the present case, the histopathological appearance could have suggested a central giant cell granuloma; however, the markedly elevated PTH levels and systemic skeletal involvement confirmed a diagnosis of BT secondary to HPT.

Compared with the case reported by Singh et al. [14], this case presents several unique diagnostic and biochemical features. Singh et al. described a mandibular brown tumor occurring in the context of hypercalcemic tertiary hyperparathyroidism, which remains the classic biochemical profile of THPT in dialysis patients. In contrast, our patient exhibited extremely elevated PTH levels but persistent normocalcemia, a presentation that is exceptionally rare and not documented in their report. Furthermore, Singh et al. [14] reported facial swelling as the primary clinical complaint, whereas in our case isolated tooth mobility was the initial symptom, highlighting an even more subtle presentation that may easily be overlooked by clinicians. Radiologically, the Singh et al. [14] case demonstrated single-site mandibular involvement, while this patient showed diffuse metabolic bone disease on scintigraphy and PET-CT as well as residual parathyroid adenomas, demonstrating a more advanced systemic involvement. These distinctions underline the originality and clinical significance of the present case.

Beyond the comparison with Singh et al. [14], a review of published reports of jaw BTs associated with HPT from 2010 to 2023 was carried out, (see Table 2). Most cases were linked to SHPT and THPT in patients with CKD, and the mandible was the most frequent site of involvement. The typical biochemical profile included very high PTH levels accompanied by hypercalcemia, whereas cases with normocalcemia were exceedingly rare. The initial presentation varied, ranging from facial swelling and pain to tooth mobility and pathological fractures. Imaging modalities used across reports included panoramic radiographs, CT scans, and bone scintigraphy, while histology consistently revealed multinucleated giant cells in a fibrous stroma with hemorrhagic areas. Treatment strategies also differed: some patients improved with optimized medical management (dialysis, vitamin D analogues, calcimimetics), whereas others required parathyroidectomy for definitive control. Regression of the bone lesions was generally observed after adequate metabolic correction.

The biochemical profile in this case illustrates the diagnostic ambiguity between refractory SHPT and atypical THPT. Although the patient had long-standing dialysis dependence, severe PTH elevation, and histological evidence of parathyroid adenomas suggesting parathyroid autonomy, the persistence of normocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia pointed toward inadequately controlled SHPT. This overlap highlights the limitations of current definitions, particularly in advanced renal disease.

Although THPT is traditionally described after renal transplantation [6], it may also occur in dialysis patients with severe SHPT, even in the absence of hypercalcemia, as illustrated here.

Management of BT’s depends on the underlying type of HPT. In PHPT, removal of the offending parathyroid gland is the treatment of choice, although intralesional steroid therapy has been proposed in selected cases [15–18]. Surgical removal of the lesions may be required for large tumors that persist beyond six months or cause functional or aesthetic problems [19].

In SHPT and THPT, treatment focuses on controlling chronic renal disease and correcting metabolic abnormalities with hemodialysis, dietary phosphate restriction, vitamin D analogues, and calcimimetics [20, 21]. Parathyroidectomy is indicated in patients resistant to medical therapy or in those with severe skeletal or systemic complications [16].

In the present case, despite calcimimetic therapy, PTH levels remained profoundly elevated, prompting consideration of parathyroidectomy even in the absence of hypercalcemia. Current guidelines primarily emphasize hypercalcemia as the main surgical indication in THPT [6, 22], and there is limited evidence on surgical outcomes in normocalcemic patients. One study reported that parthyroidectomy in normocalcemic THPT achieved similar biochemical control as in hypercalcemic cases, although permanent hypocalcemia was more common but manageable [22].

In this case parathyroidectomy was performed despite normal calcium levels because of the destructive mandibular lesion and extremely high PTH. Postoperatively, PTH decreased by approximately 50% (from 4868.0 pg/ml to 1762.6 pg/ml), while serum calcium remained normal (92 mg/l). However, three months later, PTH rose again to 2250.4 pg/ml, with calcium at 96 mg/l (normal range 86–100 mg/l) and phosphate at 47 mg/l (normal range 27–45 mg/l). The patient was readdressed to endocrinology, and calcimimetic therapy was intensified.

Seven months after surgery, the mandibular brown tumor showed radiographic signs of regression, underscoring the importance of controlling the underlying hyperparathyroidism. The patient remains under follow-up for both the oral lesion and biochemical parameters.

Conclusion

Brown tumors are uncommon manifestations of hyperparathyroidism and may mimic primary giant cell lesions of the jaws. This case illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing severe hyperparathyroidism in a dialysis patient with persistently high PTH but normal calcium levels. The distinction between tertiary and inadequately treated secondary hyperparathyroidism in such patients is often unclear, yet it has important implications for surgical decision-making. Our case highlights the importance of considering hyperparathyroidism in the differential diagnosis of jaw lesions, the role of dentists and oral surgeons in early detection, and underscores the uncertainty surrounding current surgical indications in normocalcemic patients, suggesting that clearer guidance may be beneficial.

Limitations

This case has several limitations. First, detailed operative and pathological records from the first parathyroidectomy in 2017 were unavailable, preventing full assessment of disease progression. Second, the absence of hypercalcemia complicates classification, raising the possibility that this case represents inadequately treated secondary rather than tertiary hyperparathyroidism. Finally, as a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized, but they do provide insight into the diagnostic and therapeutic dilemmas in similar patients.
