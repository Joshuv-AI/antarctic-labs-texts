Exploring knowledge, attitudes and readiness to practice toward disaster medicine and preparedness among university health profession students in Sana’a city, Yemen
Authors: Gamil Q. Othman, Faisal Ali, Abdulrazzaq Y. Al-Khazzan, Mohammed M. Battah, Mohamed Izham Mohamed Ibrahim, Mahmoud Abdullah Al-Areefi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisasters can influence public health and the socioeconomic status of affected populations. Preparedness plans are a cluster of actions taken in advance to mitigate potential disasters and effectively cope with their negative consequences. Yemen is frequently vulnerable to both human-made and natural disasters.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to assess the knowledge (K), attitude (A), and readiness to practice (rP) levels regarding disaster medicine and preparedness among health profession students in both public and private universities in Sanaa, city, capital of Yemen.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted to evaluate 420 medical, pharmacy, and health sciences students’ KArP levels using validated self-administered disaster medicine and preparedness questionnaire. The Chi-square test, analysis of variance, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regressions, were used at an alpha level of 0. 05.ResultsThe study revealed that most of the health profession students had a moderate knowledge, attitude, readiness to practice, and total KArP scores. The study also found that difference in the mean KArP between the sexes was significant (P < 0.05). Students from the public universities had significantly higher knowledge, attitude, and readiness to practice scores and total KArP scores than those from private universities (P < 0.05). Moreover, K, A, and rP scores were significantly associated with overall KArP scores. Overall, Knowledge and attitude factors were significant predictors of readiness to practice (P < 0.001).ConclusionHealth profession student from Sana’a Universities had moderate positive attitudes toward readiness to practice disaster medicine preparedness. Nevertheless, its highly recommended that health collages and policymakers of health system should have a solid curricula and training programs in disaster medicine management and preparedness to build competent healthcare professionals for the victims of natural and human-made disasters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13539-2.

Background

Disasters are defined by WHO/UNISDR as severe societal disruptions causing overwhelming human, material, economic, or environmental losses exceeding local coping capacity [1, 2]. This creates an immediate imbalance between healthcare demands and available resources [2]. Hazards driving mass disasters include natural (e.g., floods, earthquakes), anthropogenic (technological/social), and health crises [2, 3]. Yemen is very exposed to disasters, with frequent floods and conflicts, which are major reasons for displacement; in 2021 alone, the conflict resulted in the displacement of about 377,000 individuals [4]. Concurrently, the outbreak of the main infectious diseases, such as the cholera epidemic that occurred between 2016 and 2018, which was estimated at more than a million cases [5], exacerbates the situation and increases the burden.

Disaster medicine integrates multiple health disciplines and disaster management for prevention, immediate response and rehabilitation of the health and humanitarian problems, by relying on medical emergency services, hospital services, and public health [6, 7]. Effective response requires healthcare workforce with awareness of dangers and personal plans [8]. However, the healthcare system in Yemen faces decisive deficiencies, such as the severe shortage of HCPs (5 doctors per 10,000 people), equipment, ambulances, and health facilities (only 50% operating after the war), which leads to high death rates, as happened during the Covid-19 pandemic [9]. Moreover, nearly two -thirds of health professionals lacks sufficient knowledge of disasters and how to deal with them, due to a defect in curricula design and poor training [10].

Despite the challenges, there were efforts that have been made and resulted in the establishment of organizations such as the Yemeni Association of Emergency Medicine and Disasters, and the Yemeni Board of Emergency Medicine, as well as obtaining recognition from IFEM and held national conferences [11]. A study indicated that self-efficacy and advanced education are associated with healthcare workers’ willingness to respond quickly disasters [12], highlighting the need for targeted training.

Collectively, due to the high incidence along with massive intensity of different types of disasters in Yemen and the limited number of available studies that investigate the perspectives of future healthcare professionals on disaster medicine preparedness. This study, therefore, aims to assess the level of disaster medicine preparedness among health profession students in Yemen by evaluating their knowledge (K), attitude (A), and readiness to practice (rP) regarding disaster medicine and preparedness.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional method was carried out to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and readiness to practice level of the students. The questionnaire was distributed to healthcare profession students at three private universities and two government universities in Sana’a city. Participants had to complete all items in the survey, and any questionnaires that were incomplete were excluded. The data were collected from 1st August 2022 to 28th February 2023.

Study population (including inclusion and exclusion criteria)

The research was conducted among health profession students studying in the medical and health sciences collages amongst Sana’a Universities. The study included all undergraduate students from the College of Pharmacy, College of Medicine, and College of Health Sciences with no restrictions on age, gender, or study level/academic year. Postgraduate students and students who participated in the pilot phase were excluded.

Sample size and sampling methods

The group of students captivated in this study was wholly undergraduate students from the College of Pharmacy (CPH), College of Medicine (CMED) and College of Health Sciences (CHS). A sample of 420 students was participated. The sample size was estimated using Raosoft® (http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html) based on a 5% margin of error and considering a 95% confidence level and a response rate of 80%. Choosing different colleges, students were approached on the basis of convenience through the stratification of universities and health colleges.

Study instruments

In this study, the authors selected a questionnaire that had been previously validated and used by other researchers in Qatar [13]. The questionnaire was further modified when two studies were conducted in Pakistan and China among healthcare professionals [14, 15].

To ensure the appropriateness, relevancy, clarity, and adequacy of the questions, five researchers reviewed the questionnaire tool. The questionnaire was divided into four parts. The first part collected demographic data such as age, gender, degree major, and academic year of the participants. The second part focused on knowledge and consisted of 20 closed-ended binary questions with scores ranging from 0 to 20. To categorize the scores, cut-off points were set as follows: below 7 points was considered low (25thquartile), 7–12 points was moderate (more than the 25th quartile and less than the 75th quartile), and above 12 points was high (75th quartile).

The attitude section had a total of 14 scale questions (strongly agree, agree, neither agree nor disagree, disagree, strongly disagree) and scores could have a minimum of 14 and a maximum 70 points. Similar to the knowledge domain, a score between 42 points and 56 points was considered moderate (25th-75th quartiles), a score below 42 points was low, and a score above 56 points was high. The readiness to practice section consisted of 10 scale questions (strongly agree, agree, neither agree nor disagree, disagree, strongly disagree, and not applicable) that were worth a maximum of 44 points. A score below 31 points was considered low (25th quartile), a score between 31 and 38 points was moderate (above the 25th quartile and less than the 75th), and a score above 38 points was high (75th quartile). The language used in questionnaire was English and Arabic and the language used for data entry was English. A group of thirty students participated in preliminary testing to assess the content, design, readability, and comprehension of the final version of the questionnaire. Based on their feedback, the questionnaire was revised to improve its clarity and accuracy, making it more understandable. To establish the validity and reliability of the research instrument, a pilot study was conducted. The pilot study aimed to evaluate the questionnaire’s performance and determine its reliability and validity. The results of the pilot study indicated a Cronbach’s Alpha value of 0.70, confirming that the questionnaire displayed satisfactory levels of both reliability and validity.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol obtained ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee for Medical Research at the University of Science and Technology, with the reference number MECA No: (EAC/UST232). Prior to their participation, all participants were provided with detailed information about the research objectives, significance, and potential benefits. The voluntary nature of participation was emphasized, and verbal consent was obtained from each participant. Receipt of the questionnaire and filling it out by the targets indicates their acceptance of participation. They were assured that all data collected would be treated with strict confidentiality and used solely for research purposes.

Data collection

The purpose of the study was explained to the health profession students before distributing the questionnaires to them. A total of 420 questionnaires were handed out among healthcare professional students in Sana’a universities. The students were requested to complete the questionnaires and return them immediately after finishing.

The answers provided in the questionnaires were manually collected, and the number of responses for each question option (such as yes, no, strongly agree, agree, disagree, neither agree nor disagree, strongly disagree, and not applicable) was calculated. After the completion of the interviews, the collected data were carefully checked and prepared for entry into statistical software for subsequent analysis.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS V-21 (IBM Corp. Released 2016. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0. Armonk, NY, USA). To check for the normality distribution of the results, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used. The results of the descriptive analysis were reported as the frequency (%) or mean (± SD). Independent Student’s t-test was used to compare the differences in gender and KArP scores. The relationship between the characteristics of the categorical variables and the level of knowledge were examined using the Chi-square test. Pearson’s correlation test was used to examine the strength of the correlation between the 3 variables (K, A, and rP). Multiple linear regressions were performed to predict readiness to practice (dependent variable) based on knowledge, and attitude (independent variables. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Most of the students were male (n = 235, 58.8%), majoring in pharmacy and medicine (n = 110, 27.5%, 101, 25.3%), in their senior years, that is, their third and fifth years (n = 116, 29%, 102, 25.5%).

Table 2: The questions assessed the students’ knowledge regarding disaster medicine preparedness. The majority of participants responded positively to 13 out of 20 items. Discussion

In this research, the levels of knowledge, attitude, and readiness to practice among healthcare profession students in many universities in Sana’a city, capital of Yemen concerning disaster medicine management and preparedness were evaluated.

Our results demonstrated generally that, the overall scores for K, A, rP, and KArP of the students were moderate. These final scores are consistent with the collective findings of the previous studies that report conclusion of lack of knowledge, positive attitude, and level of readiness to practice among health profession students, particularly in low -income countries. Hence, the cooperative and integrated efforts are needed to overcome these gaps. More specifically, the integration of disaster medicine management and preparedness in higher education institutions is essential to prevent disasters or to reduce its consequences [13].

Our data are also consistent with other studies conducted among health students in Pakistan and China [14, 15]. A study documented that students have moderate knowledge about disaster medicine and gave a recommendation to include disaster medicine topics in medical school’s curriculum [16]. The possible explanation for that being Yemen a country that has experienced various disasters, both natural and human-made, might have heightened the awareness and students understanding about disaster medicine. Living experiences of the students in Yemen could have contributed to their knowledge level in this field.

Due to the importance of early education and training to face the elevated number of disasters, government and many scientific institutions in Italy have agreed to involve disaster medicine education in the medical curriculum [16]. A survey was conducted among medical students. Approximately 40% had never heard about disaster medicine, 91% had never attended elective academic courses on disaster medicine, and 87.6% had never attended nonacademic courses on disaster medicine [16]. Our study showed that 33.5% have previous experience in dealing with disasters, 54.8% had never attended courses on disaster medicine (either in college or in external courses), and 91.8% think Yemen is at risk of disasters (natural or human made). At some context bout 55% had never read journal articles related to medicines disaster preparedness. In regard with attitudes and readiness to practice, more than four-fifths (91.4%) of Italian medical students reported that they would welcome the introduction of a course on disaster medicine in their core curriculum, and almost all the respondents (94.1%) considered knowledge of disaster medicine important for their future careers [16].

Our study found that more than 50% of health students had a positive attitude toward disaster preparedness, with several statements within the survey. The majority of the respondents (84%) reportedly welcomed the need for more workshops and simulated training, to be ready for dealing with disaster medicine. Our study also illustrated that more than four-fifths of health students had a positive attitude toward interest in educational classes specific to the status of the country, willingness to be a future member of healthcare providers, confidence regarding abilities as a future healthcare member.

Ability to show their task in the response part, confidence in providing medical information, feeling prepared for the management of disaster medicines, and feeling confident in implementation of emergency plans and procedures. However, only 44.3% agreed that there is reasonable awareness on ways to handle wars and emergencies among undergraduate students in UST. This work unveiled a strong inclination towards disaster medicine and readiness. This result contrasts with findings conducted in Qatar, Pakistan, and China, which reported moderately level of attitude toward disaster medicine and preparedness [13–15]. Furthermore, studies in India and Germany showed somewhat positive attitudes among students toward disaster medicine and preparedness, with room for improvement through educational and training interventions [17, 18].

With regard to readiness to practice disaster medicine, the majority of students in our study agreed with statements such as willingness to attend emergency medicine education, need for more training on patient-centered care, recognition that preparation requires effort and time, acknowledgement of lack of knowledge about medications during disasters, readiness to practice under disaster despite their knowledge in essential medicines may be lacking, belief that workshops/seminars provided sufficient preparation, clarity of their role in disaster medicine situations, belief that undergraduate coursework enables readiness, and the availability of extracurricular resources for readiness. However, only 34% believed that disasters medicines are not expected in Yemen. Similar to a study conducted in China, the readiness to practice disaster medicine was generally positive between the students participated in the study [15]. Another study conducted in Germany reported high motivation among students despite the lack of basic information and education on disaster medicine [18]. However, other studies in Qatar, and Pakistan reported a moderate level of practice among students, indicating areas for improvement [13, 14]. In terms of gender differences, the study found that male students had significantly better knowledge compared to female students, potentially due to their greater familiarity with new developments in their field. This finding contrasts with a study conducted in Pakistan, which did not find significant differences in knowledge between males and females [14].

Limitations and strengths of the study

This study has some limitations. The most significant limitation was concerning the selection bias, as the students at each university were selected via convenience sampling. Moreover, the sample size was relatively small. Therefore, the findings might not be suitable for generalization to other cities or regions in Yemen. However, the impacts of selection bias were minimized by incorporating students from both private and public universities. Lastly, the study relied on a survey, self-reported data and self-perception might have caused biases. Nevertheless, the current study is the first study conducted in Yemen that has provided significant findings and perceptions regarding KArP level of disaster medicine, which warrant further research.

Clinical and practical applications of study

Yemen is highly subjected to different kinds of disasters, including natural and human-made disasters such as long civil conflicts and blockage. In the recent decade, the United Nations and its organizations have reported a significant increase in the intensity and frequency of human-made disasters in Yemen. As a result, special attentions and various aids have been provided to affected population and students throughout the country. Given the lack of studies assessing the level of disaster medicine preparedness among health-care professionals and students, as future professionals, are deficient. Moreover, a lack of adequate educational curriculum programs regarding disaster medicine among health colleges and health authorities in Yemen. Thus, there is a need for collective efforts from various institutions and health sectors to overcome these limitations. Health education is one of the central factors of controlling disasters incidence and reducing its negative consequences in the affected people. Therefore, it’s worth to mention that the findings of this research can be positively exploited by health colleges and health policymakers to improve their educational programs and healthcare system accordingly. Furthermore, health colleges and teaching hospitals should hire the competent and qualified members who can teach and train students in disaster management field. In addition, the study plans and curricula of health collages need to be scientifically reviewed and equipped by including most relevant modules as either mandatory or elective options for all health-care profession students to be competent and willing to effectively respond to disaster events when they become licensed healthcare professionals.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate the importance of medical and health colleges for empowering students’ knowledge, attitudes, and readiness to practice disaster medicine and preparedness before practicing their profession. Collectively, the current research found that the levels of knowledge, attitude, and readiness to practice of health profession students in Sana’a universities, capital of Yemen with regard to disaster medicine management and preparedness were moderate. However, challenges to disaster medicine education and awareness. should be addressed and implicated at the institutional level by building a powerful curriculum, and training programs for healthcare profession students in Yemeni health colleges.
