The WhiA and WhiB2 transcription factors mediate the Mycobacterium tuberculosis cell cycle by regulating DNA replication and cell division
Authors: Frances Marks, Mirjana Lilic, Pranav Nalam, Leah Mclelland, Xiaoxian An, Sarah M Fortune, Elizabeth A Campbell, Sabine Ehrt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), a pathogen with a remarkable ability to survive within its human host by balancing replication and persistence throughout the course of infection. The success of Mtb depends on its ability to adapt to diverse and hostile environments imposed by the host. This adaptation is primarily mediated through transcriptional regulation of gene expression. While Mtb encodes several transcription factors essential for growth, it remains unclear which transcription factors directly regulate the bacterial cell cycle. In this work, we characterized two essential transcription factors, WhiA and WhiB2, and defined their role in regulating Mtb replication. Using CRISPR interference, we demonstrated that whiA and whiB2 are essential for bacterial cell division. Results from functional genomic studies revealed that WhiA and WhiB2 regulate key genes involved in DNA replication and repair, ribosome function, cell wall synthesis, and septation. Subsequent in vitro studies demonstrated that WhiA and WhiB2 are dually required for activation of selected target genes, suggesting that they function cooperatively as regulators of the Mtb cell cycle.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by the airborne human pathogen Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), is the leading cause of death by a single infectious agent [1]. The success of Mtb during infection depends on its ability to proliferate within its host during periods of relaxed immune control and persist in a state of slowed or arrested growth in response to immune-imposed stresses. This altered growth rate makes Mtb refractory to antibiotic treatment and contributes to the need for a long 6-month TB treatment regimen [2]. Mechanistically, slowed growth induces drug tolerance, which can facilitate the emergence of drug resistance in Mtb [3]. Despite its clinical importance, the regulatory mechanisms that coordinate Mtb growth remain poorly understood.

Mtb must adapt to diverse and hostile environments imposed by the host, and this adaptation is primarily mediated through transcriptional regulation. Mtb encodes approximately 200 transcription factors, the regulatory targets of which have been characterized using chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) [4, 5]. However, the physiological relevance of these transcription factor-gene interactions remains incompletely characterized. Moreover, while several transcriptional regulators have been shown to be essential for bacterial growth, it remains unclear whether their essentiality comes from roles in the cell cycle or other critical cellular processes. Regulation of the cell cycle involves coordinating DNA replication, cell wall biogenesis, cytokinesis, and cell division to promote bacterial growth and survival under dynamic stress conditions encountered in vivo. In Mtb, DNA replication and cell division are temporally segregated, with genes involved in these processes being transcriptionally activated at distinct times during the cell cycle [6]. While transcriptional regulators of cell wall synthesis and cell division have been identified in Mtb [7], no transcriptional regulators of DNA replication have been described.

Two transcription factors, WhiA and WhiB2, are hypothesized to be key regulators of cell division in Mtb. WhiA and WhiB2 are the founding members of two unique transcription factor families whose functions in Mtb remain poorly understood. WhiB2 is the founding member of the actinobacterial-specific WhiB transcription factor family, which coordinate an iron–sulfur [4Fe–4S] cluster that is sensitive to oxygen and nitric oxide [8]. Mtb encodes seven WhiB proteins that play diverse regulatory roles including in virulence, antibiotic resistance, and oxidative stress response [9–11]. In contrast, WhiA is highly conserved among gram-positive bacteria and is the sole member of its family. Previous work has shown that WhiA functions cooperatively with WhiB2 as a transcription factor in actinobacteria; however, in gram-positive bacteria lacking WhiB proteins, the role of WhiA is less clear [12]. In these organisms, WhiA is not a transcription factor and is hypothesized to contribute to chromosome segregation. These transcription factors were originally characterized in Streptomyces venezuelae (Sven), where they co-regulate sporulation by activating genes involved in chromosome segregation and septation [13, 14]. The structure of the Sven transcription initiation complex demonstrated cooperative activation by a WhiA/WhiB2 heterodimer and represented a new mechanism of bacterial transcriptional activation [15]. In this model of dual activation, WhiA bound DNA in a sequence specific manner while WhiB2 made weak, nonspecific interactions with DNA. Neither transcription factor is sufficient on its own to activate transcription, a requirement that is also observed in the actinobacterial species Corynebacterium glutamicum [16]. Although the regulatory functions of WhiA and WhiB2 have yet to be defined in Mtb, a recent study in Mycobacterium smegmatis showed that WhiB2 recognized a DNA motif similar to that bound by Sven WhiA and participated in a feedback regulatory loop to coordinate cell division and DNA repair [17]. Interest in these regulators has grown due to recent analyses of clinical Mtb isolates which revealed that mutations in whiA and the intergenic region between fbiA and whiB2 are associated with TB treatment failure and the emergence of drug resistance [18].

In this study, we investigated WhiA- and WhiB2-mediated transcriptional regulation of Mtb cell division. We describe the phenotypic consequences of whiA and whiB2 silencing in Mtb, demonstrating that these genes are essential for Mtb growth and cell division. We next use functional genomics to show that WhiA and WhiB2 play critical roles in activating genes involved in cell wall synthesis and septation and uncover a novel function in regulating key components of DNA replication. Further in vitro experiments support a model in which these transcription factors co-activate their targets, establishing that WhiA and WhiB2 work cooperatively as regulators of the Mtb cell cycle.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

Mtb strains are derivatives of H37Rv.

Mycobacterial cultures

Mtb strains were grown at 37°C in Difco MiddleBrook 7H9 broth (BD Diagnostics, #271310) or 7H10 agar (BD Diagnostics, #262710) supplemented with 0.2% glycerol (7H9) or 0.5% glycerol (7H10), 0.05% tyloxapol (7H9), and 1× oleic acid-albumin-dextrose-catalase (OADC), and the appropriate antibiotics (kanamycin 25 μg/ml, hygromycin 50 μg/ml, streptomycin 25 μg/ml, and anhydrotetracycline [ATc] 500 ng/ml). Mtb cultures were grown standing in tissue culture flasks (unless otherwise indicated) at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Plasmid construction and cloning

plRL66 was used to generate all Mtb CRISPRi strains, as previously described by Rock et al. [19]. plRL67 and plRL66 are integrated into the chromosome at the L5 integration site. The CRISPRi plasmid backbone was digested with BsmBI-v2 (New England Biolabs R0739L) and agarose gel purified. Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) bearing appropriate overhangs and designed to hybridize to the nontemplate strand of the target gene open reading frames were annealed and ligated (T4 ligase, New England Biolabs M0202M) into the digested backbone. Identification of desired clones was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. A list of sgRNAs used in this work can be found in the Supplementary Table S6.

Plasmids were cloned using the Gateway® Cloning Technology (Life Technologies) protocol. Hygromycin resistant backbones were cloned into the tweety chromosomal integration site and streptomycin resistant backbones were cloned into the giles integration site. A list of plasmids used in this study can be found in the Supplementary Table S7.

Plasmids were transformed into Mtb and streaked on 7H10 agar containing the appropriate antibiotics for selection. The transformants were incubated at 37°C for 3–4 weeks until single colonies were visible. At least three individual colonies were picked, expanded and saved as glycerol stocks for use as biologic replicates in experiments.

Growth assays

Liquid growth assays were performed in tissue culture flasks in biological triplicates. For growth assays, Mtb cultures were passaged to a starting OD600 of 0.01 and OD600 was measured every 2–3 days. For quantifying colony forming units (CFUs), 100 μl of cell suspension was aliquoted from liquid cultures and used to create 10-fold serial dilutions to be plated on 7H10 agar containing kanamycin. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 3–4 weeks until single colonies were visible. To quantify growth phenotypes on 7H10 agar for spot plate assays, 10-fold serial dilutions of Mtb cultures (OD600 = 0.3–0.4) were spotted on 7H10 agar with 0 or 500 ng/ml ATc. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 2–3 weeks before imaging.

Fluorescent microscopy

Mtb strains were cultured in biological triplicates with or without ATc for indicated time points prior to fixation. For peptidoglycan labeling, 1 mM NBD amino-d-alanine (NADA) was added 24 h prior to fixation. The bacterial cells were collected, washed 2× in PBS + 0.05% tyloxapol (PBST), and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 4 h. After fixation, cells were washed once with PBST and removed from biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) containment.

For microscopy, a drop of fixed samples (1–2 μl) was placed on a 1% agarose pad and visualized on a glass bottom microwell dish (MatTek, 35 mm, 14 mm microwell). Imaging of fixed cells was performed using a Deltavision Image Restoration Microscope (Applied Precision/GE/Leica) mounted on an Olympus IX71 inverted stand using a ×100 oil immersion lens (NA 1.4) and 1.518 refractive index oil. Images were acquired with a PCO Edge sCMOS camera and deconvolved using SoftWoRx software employing a constrained iterative restoration algorithm with a measured PSF. Images were processed with the open-source program ImageJ [20]. Using ImageJ, we drew segmented lines that followed the length of a cell from pole to pole to measure cell length. Bacteria were manually counted to determine the percentage of cells that contained a septum.

Immunoprecipitation with FLAG-coated beads

Mtb cultures were grown shaking to mid-log phase in 50 ml of 7H9. Cultures were passaged into ATc-containing media and incubated for 4 more days shaking to deplete endogenous whiA or whiB2 expression. Once cultures reached OD600 of 0.8–1.2, samples were harvested by centrifuging at 400 RPM at 4°C. Bacteria were washed in PBST, resuspended in 1× PBS + 1 protease inhibitor tablet (Sigma 11836170001) and frozen at −80°C. After thawing, cells were lysed by zirconium bead beating 3× at 6000 RPM for 30 s, with 1 min rest on ice in between treatments. After bead beating, samples were pelleted, and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Sigma CLS8161) for removal from BSL-3 containment.

Immunoprecipitation of FLAG-tagged proteins and crosslinked DNA was performed using Pierce™ Anti-DYKDDDDK Magnetic Agarose (ThermoFisher A36797). Fifty microliters of magnetic agarose was mixed with samples for 30 min at ambient temperature with rotation. After incubation, magnetic beads bound to FLAG proteins were collected on a magnetic stand and the supernatant was removed. Magnetic beads were washed 2× with 1× PBS and 1× with nuclease-free water. Samples were eluted with 1.5 mg/ml Pierce™ 3x DYKDDDDK Peptide (ThermoFisher A36805) by agitating for 10 min at ambient temperature, placing samples on magnetic stands, and saving the supernatant. After elution from the magnetic beads, samples were boiled with 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad 1610747) + 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) by boiling at 95°C for 10 min, and were stored at −80°C.

The FLAG IP was performed as previously describe in chromatin immunoprecipitation protocol. The only difference for immunoprecipitation is after elution from the magnetic beads, samples were boiled with 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad 1610747) + 100 mM DTT by boiling at 95°C for 10 min, and were stored at −80°C.

Western blot to detect FLAG-tagged proteins

Samples were thawed and boiled at 95°C for 5 min prior to loading on a 4%–20% precast polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad 4561095) to detect WhiA or a 16% tricine gel (Invitrogen EC6695BOX) to detect WhiB2. After running at 120 V for 70 min (polyacrylamide gel) or 120 min (tricine gel), proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (iBlot Transfer Stack, Invitrogen IB23001) in an iBlot2 drying system (Invitrogen). Membranes were blocked with Intercept blocking buffer (Li-Cor 927-70 001) for 1 h at ambient temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies anti-FLAG (monoclonal anti-FLAG M2, Sigma F3165, at 1:800) in dilution buffer (1:1 PBS:Intercept blocking buffer + 0.1% tween20) on a rocking platform at 4°C overnight. After washing the primary antibody with PBS + 0.1% tween20 three times, membranes were incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody (Goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody DyLight 800, ThermoFisher SA5-10176, at 1:10 000 in dilution buffer) for 1 h at ambient temperature on a rocking platform. After three washed with PBS + 0.1% tween20, the membranes were visualized using the LI-COR Odyssey CLx Imaging System.

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Bacterial cultures were grown in biological triplicates to OD600 ∼0.8–1.0 and were mixed with an equivalent volume of 5 M guanidium thiocyanate (GTC) buffer (5 M GTC, 0.5% N-lauroylsarcosine, 25 mM sodium citrate dihydrate, and 0.1 M 2-mercaptoethanol). Cells were pelleted and resuspended in 400 μl of Trizol and frozen at −80°C. After thawing, cells were lysed by zirconium bead beating 3× at 6000 RPM for 30 s, with 1 min rest on ice in between treatments. After, cell lysates were mixed with 200 μl of chloroform and samples were centrifuged to separate phases. The aqueous phase was purified using the Zymo Direct-zol RNA mini-prep kit (Zymo R2050). Residual genomic DNA was removed using TURBO DNase treatment (Invitrogen AM2239), and samples were then cleaned up with the Zymo Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo R1017).

For making complementary DNA (cDNA), 500 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed using MulV reverse transcriptase (New England Biolabs M0253S) and random hexamers (Invitrogen, N8080127), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using the Roche Lightcycler 480 and Mastermix reagents (Roche 50-720-3232). For quantifying target gene knockdown, custom-made sequence-specific primers (0.3 μM) and probes (0.1 μM) containing either 5′ FAM/3′ BBQ or 5′ HEX/3′ BBQ labeling were used and normalized to a sigA (Rv2703) probe containing 5′ CY/3′ BBQ labeling using the ΔCt calculation. All gene-specific qPCR primers were designed using the PrimerQuest tool from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). A list of qPCR primers and probes used in this study can be found in Supplementary Table S8.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was quantified used Qubit RNA high sensitivity, and 3 μg of total RNA was used for library prep. Ribosomal RNA was depleted using custom-made Mtb rRNA targeting oligos, and ribosomal depletion was confirmed using RNA tapestation. RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit of Illumina (New England Biolabs E7760L), according to instructions for 150 ng of input mRNA. Samples were sequenced generating paired 5 million 100 bp reads per sample.

All RNA-seq data analysis was performed using R. Sequencing reads were trimmed and filtered using Rfastp and were aligned to the H37Rv reference genome (GenBank AL123456.3; RefSeq NC_000962.3; GCF_000195955.2; Genome assembly ASM19595v2) using Rsubread [21, 22]. BAM alignment files were created, sorted, and indexed using Rsamtools. Read counts were done using feature Counts, and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [23, 24]. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using a custom KEGG annotation program.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and sequencing (ChIP-seq)

To characterize DNA–protein interactions, 80 ml of bacterial cultures were grown in biological triplicates to OD600 0.8–1.2 and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde while agitating cultures at room temperature for 30 min. Crosslinking was quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 250 mM and agitating at room temperature for 15 min. Cells were pelleted, washed twice in 1× PBS + 1 protease inhibitor tablet (Sigma 11836170001) and resuspended in ChIP Buffer 1 (20 mM Na-HEPES pH 7.9, 50 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5 mM DTT) + 1 protease inhibitor tablet. Samples were mechanically lysed by zirconium bead beating 3× at 6 000 RPM for 30 s, with 1 min rest on ice in between treatments. After bead beating, samples were pelleted, and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Sigma CLS8161) for removal from BSL-3 containment.

Samples were normalized to 400 μl in ChIP Buffer 1 and were put in a Covaris M220 to shear chromatin to a uniform size centered at 250 bp using the following settings: Power = 75 W, 5% duty, 200 cycles/burst for 12 min at 6°C. Following shearing, the sample volume was adjusted to 600 μl in IPP150 Buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% NP40), and 1/60th of the sample was saved as input.

The FLAG IP was performed as previously described in immunoprecipitation protocol. The only difference for immunoprecipitation was that, after collecting the eluate from the magnetic beads, the samples were not boiled but were instead treated to reverse the crosslinks. The proteins from sample elutions were reverse crosslinked/digested with 1 mg/ml Pronase for 2 h at 42°C following by 9 h at 65°C. Immunoprecipitated DNA was subsequently column purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, #28104), according to manufacturer’s instructions and eluted with 40 μl Tris–HCl, pH 8.5.

DNA libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II Directional DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, #28104), according to manufacturer’s instructions for 500 pg of input DNA. Samples were sequencing generating paired 40–60 million 100 bp reads per sample.

Purification of Mtb WhiA for IDAP-seq

12xHis-WhiA was expressed from the pCOLA Duet-1 backbone (Novagen). Competent BL21 (DE3) codon optimized Escherichia coli were used for WhiA expression. Competent cells were induced with the addition of 1 M isopropyl-beta-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) when the OD600 reached 0.5 and incubated for an additional 6 h at 25°C. Cells were then centrifuged and pellets were lysed by continuous flow through a French press (Avestin) on ice in 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, and 1 protease inhibitor tablet. After centrifuging for 60 min, the cleared lysate was then loaded onto a 5 ml of HisTrap HP (Cytiva 17524801) using a peristaltic pump with the buffers kept on ice. Proteins were eluted with 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, and 2 mM DTT buffer over 5 column volumes. Peak fractions were concentrated, desalted using PD-10 desalting columns (Cytiva 17085101) into 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl.

In vitro DNA affinity purification and sequencing (IDAP-seq)

Mtb genomic DNA was sheared to a uniform size centered around 300 bp using a Covaris M220 using the following settings: Power = 50 W, 20% duty, 200 bursts/cycle for 65 s at 6°C. Recombinant WhiA was thawed on ice for 30 min before combining 200 nM recombinant WhiA with 4 μg of sheared DNA in a final volume of 125 μl of binding buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 0.2 mg/ml bovine serum albumin, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, and 200 ng sheared herring sperm DNA). Binding reactions were performed in duplicates. Binding reactions were incubated for 30 min at ambient temperature with rotation and then mixed with a 50 μl bed volume of Talon metal affinity resin (Takara Bio 635501) that was pre-equilibrated in in equilibration buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT). Samples were incubated for 30 min at ambient temperature with rotation to allow for binding of His tagged proteins to bind to the metal resin. After incubation, bound protein–DNA mixtures were loaded onto Poly-Prep chromatography columns (Bio-Rad 7 311 553). Columns were washed three times with 1 ml of binding buffer. To elute protein-bound genomic DNA, 500 μl of elution buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, and 1% SDS) pre-warmed to 65°C was added to the resin and the column was capped and sealed with parafilm to minimize evaporation. Samples were incubated in a hybridization chamber pre-heated to 65°C for 15 min, and after elution containing the protein-bound DNA fragments were collected. Resin was washed two times with 200 μl of elution buffer to recover all eluted DNA. Eluted DNA was subsequently column purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen 28104), according to manufacturer’s instructions and eluted with 40 μl Tris–HCl, pH 8.5. DNA libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II Directional DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs 28104), according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Analysis of ChIP-seq and IDAP-seq data

Analysis of ChIP-seq and IDAP-seq data was performed using R. Sequencing reads were trimmed and filtered using Rfastp and were aligned to the H37Rv reference genome (GenBank AL123456.3; RefSeq NC_000962.3; GCF_000195955.2; Genome assembly ASM19595v2) using Rsubread [21, 22]. BAM alignment files were created, sorted, and indexed using Rsamtools and viewed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer desktop application [25, 26]. Peak calling was performed using MACS2 (188, 189). For ChIP-seq, peaks from FLAG-tagged WhiA and WhiB2 were compared against the FLAG control. For WhiA IDAP-seq, 6xHis-WhiA peaks were compared against a no protein control. Normalized read counts were calculated using the bamCompare function on the Galaxy platform [27]. Motif analysis was performed using MEME-ChIP on the Galaxy platform [27, 28].

Drug susceptibility profiling

All compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and dispensed using an HP D300e digital dispenser in a 384-well plate format. DMSO did not exceed 1% of the final culture volume. CRISPRi strains were grown in biological triplicates and pre-depleted in the presence or absence of ATc for 4–5 days before assay for minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) analysis. Cultures were then passaged to an OD600 of 0.01, and 50 µl of suspension was plated in technical triplicates in wells containing the test compound and fresh ATc. Plates were incubated standing at 37°C with 5% CO2. OD600 was evaluated using a M2 SpectraMax Microplate reader at 7-, 10-, and 14-day post-plating, and the percent growth was calculated relative to a no drug control for each strain. MIC measurements were calculated using a nonlinear fit in GraphPad Prism. For all MIC curves, data represent the mean ± s.d. for technical replicates. Data are representative of at least two independent experiments.

Purification of Mtb WhiB2-σA

A co-expression plasmid of Mtb σA and WhiB2 was made in the pCDFDuet-1 backbone (Novagen) to overexpress His12-WhiB2 with Strep-tag II-σA. Competent Eco BL21 (DE3) (Novagen) cells were transformed with pCDFDuet-1-His12-WhiB2-Strep-tag II-σA. Cells were grown with shaking to an optical density of 0.5 at 600 nM (OD600) in LB medium with 50 µg/ml streptomycin and induced with 1 mM IPTG mM for 16 h at 18°C. Cells were spun down (14 000 RPM, 30 min, 4°C), and pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT) and lysed by continuous flow through a French press (Avestin) with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 1 mM protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC), and centrifuged (14 000 RPM for 30 min, 4°C). WhiB2 and σA proteins were first co-purified by Ni2+-affinity chromatography. The sample was then subjected to StrepTrap HP (Cytiva) chromatography, and the purified WhiB2-σA protein complex was eluted with lysis buffer plus 2.5 mM desthiobiotin. The presence of the [4Fe–4S] cluster in WhiB2 was monitored by measuring the absorbance at 420 nM. Purified σA/WhiB2 was analyzed through Coomassie-stained SDS–PAGE, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

Purification of Mtb σA

An expression plasmid of Mtb σA was made in the pAC27 backbone plasmid to overexpress His10-σA [29]. Competent Eco Rosetta 2 (Novagen) cells were transformed with pAC27-His10-σA, and cells were grown with shaking in LB medium with 50 µg kanamycin/ml to an OD600 of 0.3 at 37°C, then lowered to 30°C. When the OD600 reached 0.6, cells were induced with IPTG to a final concentration of 1 mM and induced overnight at 16°C. The cells were centrifuged (14 000 RPM, 30 min, 4°C) and resuspended in lysis buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 500 mM NaCl, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME) plus 10 mM imidazole] and lysed by continuous flow through the French press (Avestin) with 1 mM PMSF and 1 mM PIC and centrifuged (14 000 RPM, 30 min, 4°C). σA was first purified by Ni2+-affinity chromatography. σA was concentrated to 4 mg/ml and injected onto a HiLoad 16/600 SD200 120 ml column (Cytiva) with 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 0.1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 5 mM DTT, and 500 mM NaCl. Purified σA was analyzed through Coomassie-stained SDS–PAGE, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

Purification of Mtb WhiA for in vitro transcription assays

An expression plasmid of Mtb WhiA was made in the pCOLA Duet-1 backbone (Novagen) to overexpress His12-WhiA. Competent Eco Rosetta 2 (Novagen) cells were transformed with pCOLA Duet-His12-WhiA, and cells were grown with shaking to an OD600 of 0.7 in LB medium with 50 µg/ml kanamycin plus 34 µg/ml chloramphenicol and induced with 0.3 mM IPTG for 3 h at 37°C. The cells were centrifuged (14 000 RPM, 30 min, 4°C) and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 1 mM DTT, and 100 mM NaCl) plus 0.5 mM PMSF and 0.5 mM PIC and were lysed by four rounds of 30-s sonication (Branson) with 38% amplitude on ice with 1 min of rest between. To reduce viscosity, the sample was subject to one cycle through the French press (Avestin) and spun at 14 000 RPM for 30 min, 4°C. The cleared sample was loaded onto a 5 ml of HiTrap Heparin HP (Cytiva, Pittsburgh, PA), and was eluted with a salt gradient of 100 mM to 1 M NaCl. Successful fractions were individually injected into an SD200 10/300 GL 23 ml column (Cytiva) in 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT. Purified WhiA was analyzed through Coomassie-stained SDS–PAGE. Purified WhiA was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Purification of core Mtb RNA polymerase

Mtb RNAP was expressed and purified as previously described [30]. Competent Eco Rosetta 2 (Novagen) cells were transformed with pMP61 and were grown with shaking in LB medium with 50 µg/ml kanamycin and 34 µg/ml chloramphenicol to an OD600 of 0.3 at 37°C, then to an OD600 of 0.6 at 25°C. Cultures were induced with 0.1 mM IPTG overnight. The cells were resuspended in lysis buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM DTT] and lysed by continuous flow through the French press (Avestin) with 1 mM PMSF and 1 mM PIC and spun at 15 000 RPM for 30 min at 4°C. DNA and RNAP were precipitated from the cleared lysate with 0.6% (w/v) polyethyleneimine (PEI) for 15 min on ice. The PEI pellets were washed three times with 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, and 0.3 M NaCl, and RNAP was eluted four times with a solution of the same composition but with 1 M NaCl. RNAP was precipitated by gradual addition of ammonium sulfate to 35% (w/v) final concentration. The ammonium sulfate precipitated enzyme was pelleted by centrifugation at 15 000 RPM, 30 min, 4°C, and the pellet was resuspended in 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 1 M NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, and 5 mM BME and loaded on to 2× 5 ml HiTrap Ni2+ columns (Cytiva) and eluted with 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 1 M NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, and 5 mM BME. Elutions were concentrated and injected into a HiLoad 26/600 SD200 320 ml column (Cytiva) in 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, 500 mM NaCl, and 5 mM DTT. Purified RNAP was analyzed through Coomassie-stained SDS–PAGE, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

Assembly of RNAP-holo

Mtb RNAP-holo without WhiB2 was formed by mixing purified RNAP core with a 3-fold molar excess of σA. Mtb RNAP-holo -WhiB2 was formed by mixing purified RNAP core with a 3-fold molar excess of WhiB2-σA. Samples were incubated at 4°C for 15 min and at 37°C for 10 min. The formed holoenzymes were purified over a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in gel filtration buffer [10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, 500 mM NaCl, and 5 mM DTT]. Holoenzymes formed in this buffer were used in the aptamer-based transcription assays.

Purification of CarD

Mtb CarD was purified as previously described [31]. In brief, Mtb CarD was overexpressed from pET SUMO (Invitrogen) in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells (Novagen) and selected on medium containing 50 µg/ml kanamycin. Protein expression was induced by adding IPTG to a final concentration of 1 mM when cells reached an apparent OD600 of 0.6, followed by 4 h of growth at 28°C, then collected by centrifugation (4000 × g, 15 min at 4°C). Collected cells were resuspended in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM potassium glutamate, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM PMSF, 1 mM protease inhibitor cocktail, and 1 mM BME, then lysed using a continuous-flow French press. The lysate was centrifuged twice (16 000 × g, 30 min, 4°C), and the proteins were purified by Ni2+-affinity chromatography (HisTrap IMAC HP, GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Following elution Ni2+-affinity purification, the complex was dialysed overnight into 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM potassium glutamate, 5 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM BME, and the His10 tag was cleaved with ULP-1 protease (Invitrogen) overnight at a ratio of 1/30 (protease mass/cleavage target mass). The cleaved complex was loaded onto a second Ni2+-affinity column and was retrieved from the flow-through. The complex was loaded directly onto a size-exclusion column (Superdex 200 16/16, Cytiva) equilibrated with 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 150 mM potassium glutamate, 5 mM MgCl2, and 2.5 mM DTT. The sample was concentrated to 5 mg/ml by centrifugal filtration and stored at −80°C.

Transcription templates construction

DNA fragments containing the promoter regions of dnaE1 (−140 to −1), ftsZ (−132 to −1 ), and dnaB (−220 to −1 ) followed by a C-less cassette (+1 to + 26 from the transcription start site (TSS) were ordered from Twist Bioscience. The synthesized fragments were digested with EcoRI and BamHI restriction enzymes and subcloned into EcoRI–BamHI digested plasmid pJC1 that contains the rrf terminator [32]. DNA encoding the Spinach-mini RNA aptamer sequence was cloned after the C-less cassette by using HiFi DNA assembly (NEB) with overlapping sequences in plasmid and Spinach-mini fragment [33]. For fluorescence transcription assays, fragments were PCR amplified from the promoter regions of the respective genes to +119 downstream of the Spinach-mini sequence (IDT) as listed in Supplementary Table S9. The PCR fragment was used as template for the aptamer-based transcription assays. The sequence of each fragment is listed with the Spinach-mini sequence underlined in all constructs and the +1 TSS bolded for reference.

In vitro transcription assays

To measure transcription under steady-state conditions, the change in DFHBI fluorescence upon binding to full-length transcribed RNA of the Spinach-mini aptamer was measured. Data were collected using an Infinite M1000 microplate reader (Tecan Life Sciences, Switzerland) and its corresponding I-9 control software. Reactions were set up in a Greiner Bio-One 384 microplate (catalogue 07-000-057), and the DHFBI fluorescence was measured using a high energy Xenon arc discharge lamp and excited with a 470 ± 20 nm bandpass filter, and the resulting emission signal was monitored with a 528 ± 20 nm bandpass filter. Fluorescence data were acquired from a read height 15.00 mm in 20 s intervals, with varying acquisition times not exceeding 60 min.

The effects of the transcription factors were determined using a modified version of a previously described fluorescent-aptamer-based transcription assay [34]. Transcription reaction mixes containing 80% of the final volume included the NTPs, RNase inhibitor (4U/μl; Thermo Scientific # EO0381), promoter DNA, and the transcription factor CarD. From these mixes, a volume of 16 µl was transferred to an individual well in the 384 microplate (Greiner, catalogue # 07-000-057). For conditions where WhiA was omitted from the reaction mixture, gel filtration buffer was added [25 mM Tris pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, and 5 mM DTT] to account for the missing 5% of the final volume (20 µl). Water was added in place of NTPs in the no NTPs control. Final concentrations were: RNAP-holo (0.3 mM), CarD (1 mM), WhiA (1 mM), DNA (50 nM), and 500 μM of each NTP (when added) in 20 mM Tris (pH 8.0 at 25οC), 50 mM K-glutamate, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 5 µg/ml BSA. Reactions were started by adding RNAP-holo, RNAP-holo with WhiA, RNAP-holo-WhiB2, and RNAP-holo-WhiB2 with WhiA. The fluorophore 3,5-difluoro-4-hydroxybenzylidene imidazolinone (DFHBI; Sigma–Aldrich # SML1627; 20 μM) was added to the reaction mix and changes in DFHBI fluorescence were monitored in time.

Each trial had six conditions that were measured. The first condition contained all components but no NTPs. The second condition contained RNAP-holo with CarD. The third condition contained RNAP-holo with WhiA and CarD. The fourth reaction contained RNAP-holo with WhiB2 and CarD. The fifth reaction contained holo-RNAP with WhiA, WhiB2, and CarD. The last reaction contained holo-RNAP with WhiB2, WhiA, and CarD, but without NTPs. Unless explicitly stated, each condition had nucleotides added. For each measurement, three technical replicates were performed. The first and last conditions (conditions 1 and 6) from each replicate were used to measure background for reactions with NTPs and various factors.

Extraction of steady-state rates

Linear regression of the background-corrected fluorescent traces was done using the MATLAB fitting program described by [34]. Using a user inputted R2 value to define the goodness of fit and the aggregate of multiple technical replicates per each experimental condition, an unbiased determination of the slope of the linear segment was obtained in units of fluorescence/time. Here, R2 thresholds > 0.700 were used.

Statistical analysis

Statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism with the indicated analysis listed under each figure. An asterisk indicates significance difference: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001; and ns, not significant (P ≥ 0.05).

Results

WhiA and whiB2 are required for Mtb cell division

To investigate the essential molecular functions of whiA and whiB2, we employed CRISPR interference (CRISPRi)-mediated transcriptional repression in Mtb. CRISPRi utilizes an ATc-inducible catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) directed to specific DNA targets by an ATc-inducible sgRNA, preventing transcription initiation or elongation [19].

Inducible CRISPRi Mtb strains were constructed to induce strong knockdowns of whiA and whiB2. Induction of gene knockdown with ATc resulted in a 10-fold reduction in mRNA transcripts of the target genes (Fig. 1A). Knockdown of whiA or whiB2 led to growth inhibition (Fig. 1B). Bacteria did not lose viability after silencing whiA or whiB2, and instead silencing resulted in bacteriostasis (Fig. 1C). Bacterial viability could not be assessed after 10 days due to the occurrence of escape mutants in the cultures, indicated by the increase in mRNA abundance and in OD600 observed in whiB2 knockdown (KD) on day 14 (Fig. 1B and C, and Supplementary Fig. S1A).

Strains were grown ± ATc before collecting RNA at the indicated timepoint. Statistics from a two-way ANOVA and corrected for multiple comparisons are shown; ****P < 0.0001. (B) Growth (means ± s.d. of three biological replicates) of CRISPRi strains in medium ± ATc. (C) CFU (means ± s.d. of xfthree biological replicates) of cultures from panel (B) plated on 7H10 agar supplemented with kanamycin. (D) Representative microscopy images of CRISPRi strains supplemented with ATc. Bacteria were stained with 1 mM NADA to visualize the cell wall. Scale bar is 1 µm. To assess the effects of whiA and whiB2 depletion on Mtb cell division, we visualized the bacterial cell wall by labeling with a fluorescent D-alanine analog, NBD-amino-D-alanine (NADA), which is incorporated into newly synthesized peptidoglycan [35]. To visualize the Mtb cell wall, NADA was added to cultures 24 h prior to fixation. Although both whiA and whiB2 transcripts were depleted by day 2, morphology defects emerged later in the whiA KD than in whiB2 KD due to a longer phenotypic lag between CRISPRi activation and the onset of fitness defects [36]. Accordingly, for the most appropriate comparison of knockdown phenotypes, we assessed whiA KD mutants at day 4 and whiB2 KD mutants at day 2.

Knockdown of whiA and whiB2 in Mtb caused morphological abnormalities such as elongation and branching (Fig. 1D). The average cell length increased from 2.4 to 5.3 µm in whiA mutants and from 2.8 to 6.7 µm in whiB2 mutants (Fig. 1E). These findings demonstrate that whiA and whiB2 are essential for Mtb growth and cell division.

We assessed the importance of WhiB2’s [4Fe–4S] cluster by constructing a merodiploid Mtb strain containing the whiB2 KD expressing an N-terminal FLAG-tagged, CRISPRi-resistant WhiB2 allele with all four cysteines mutated to alanine (WhiB2.4C-A) or serine (WhiB2.4C-S) or the wild-type protein as control. We validated that the WhiB2.4C mutant strains were expressed as similar abundance as the wild-type protein (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Repression of the endogenous whiB2 copy abolished growth in strains expressing WhiB2.4C-A or WhiB2.4C-S mutant proteins but not when the wild-type WhiB2 protein was expressed (Supplementary Fig. S1C). These data demonstrate that WhiB2 depends on the [4Fe–4S] cluster for its essential functions.

Identifying WhiA and WhiB2 genome-wide binding sites

We next performed ChIP-seq to identify WhiA and WhiB2 genome-wide binding sites. To do ChIP-seq, we constructed CRISPRi-resistant whiA and whiB2 alleles that were FLAG tagged and constitutively expressed. For WhiA, both N-terminal and C-terminal FLAG-tagged constructs were generated (Supplementary Fig. S2A). For WhiB2, the small size of the protein (10 kDa) made immunoprecipitation challenging, likely due to the limited accessibility of the FLAG epitope. After testing multiple designs, we identified an effective whiB2 allele with an N-terminal triple FLAG tag (3xFLAG) with a flexible [Gly4Ser]3 linker. This construct was detectable by Western blot (Supplementary Fig. S2B) and has previously been used to do ChIP-seq studies of the WhiB2 homolog in Sven [14]. Complementation of whiA and whiB2 depletion with the CRISPRi-resistant FLAG-tagged alleles fully restored growth, confirming that the tags do not impair the essential functions of WhiA and WhiB2 (Supplementary Fig. S2C and D).

We performed ChIP-seq with Mtb strains containing CRISPRi sgRNAs targeting whiA or whiB2 along with their corresponding CRISPRi-resistant FLAG tagged alleles. An Mtb strain expressing only the FLAG tag was used as a negative control. Strains were cultured to early log phase and treated with ATc for 3–4 days to silence endogenous gene expression prior to crosslinking. Cultures were crosslinked at mid-log phase, and immunoprecipitation was performed by incubating anti-FLAG antibodies with whole cell lysates. As controls, we included the FLAG-tag only expressing strain to control for nonspecific FLAG binding and non-immunoprecipitated input DNA to normalize for nonuniform chromatin shearing. For analysis, we only included peaks with P < 10–4 and a fold enrichment ≥ 1.5.

Analysis of WhiA-binding sites

WhiA ChIP-seq with N- and C-terminal FLAG tags identified 149 peaks present in one or both datasets (Fig. 2A). Bioinformatic analysis revealed a primary 8 base pair (bp) GACACGCC consensus motif found in 48% of binding sites (Fig. 2B). This motif contains the 5 bp GACAC motif identified for the WhiA homolog in Sven but is longer [13]. Motif analysis also identified a second enriched motif, which suggests a secondary binding site or binding site of a co-factor (Supplementary Fig. S3A). To identify putative WhiA regulon genes, we focused on peaks within putative promoters, identifying 61 peaks located within −200 to 0 bp of an annotated transcription start site or start codon in the absence of an annotated transcription start site (Supplementary Table S1) [37, 38]. Promoters comprised 41% of the total binding peaks, indicating that ChIP-seq enriched for WhiA binding sites in promoters in vivo over peaks in intergenic or intragenic regions. Including downstream operon genes and bidirectional promoters, we identified 153 putative WhiA regulon genes based on promoter binding.

(B) MEME-ChIP analysis of WhiA binding peaks above the fold enrichment cutoff. E-value, enrichment P-value. Functional enrichment analysis showed that putative WhiA regulon genes are enriched for genes involved in ribosome function and DNA replication (Fig. 2C). WhiA putatively regulates 17 genes involved in ribosome function, including the rpsJ operon containing 11 genes encoding 30S and 50S ribosomal proteins [39]. There was also significant enrichment of DNA replication and repair genes, including the replicative helicase dnaB, the DNA polymerase III α subunit dnaE1, and the helicase uvrD2. WhiA bound the promoter of DNA damage induced recombinase recA (Fig. 2A). RecA has been shown to be regulated by two promoters in Mtb, with P1 being DNA damage inducible independently of LexA and RecA, and P2 being regulated by LexA in the classical way that was first described in E. coli [40–42]. The WhiA binding peak is located 42 bp upstream of P1 and 13 bp upstream of P2, and there are multiple consensus motifs in the recA promoter, making it difficult to speculate which promoter WhiA could be regulating.

WhiA bound to the promoter of the essential septation factor sepF, whose reduced expression has been linked to cell division defects like those observed in whiA mutants [43]. Additionally, WhiA bound to the promoter regions of several cell envelope related genes, such as lipoproteins lppL and lppW, and several PE/PPE genes. We also found WhiA binding upstream of genes involved in cellular metabolism, including four oxidoreductases (rv0794c, rv3352c, rv3741c, rv3742c), the ATP-dependent CLP protease subunit clpX, and the heat stress-induced ribosome binding protein hsp. Interestingly, we found that WhiA binds to the whiB2 promoter, suggesting a potential regulatory feedback loop between these two transcription factors.

To complement ChIP-seq, we also used in vitro DNA affinity purification sequencing (IDAP-seq) to identify genome-wide binding sites. IDAP-seq has also been used to map protein–DNA interactions in Mtb, and this method bypasses crosslinking by incubating purified recombinant protein with sheared genomic DNA in vitro [18, 44]. This method offers high accuracy and can identify binding sites independent of the environmental context. For IDAP-seq with WhiA, we purified recombinant N-terminal 12xHis-WhiA (Supplementary Fig. S4A). IDAP-seq with recombinant WhiA identified 105 binding peaks with P < 10–4 and a fold enrichment ≥ 1.8 (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Notably, IDAP-seq identified a single consensus motif that is the same 8 bp GACACGCC motif as identified by ChIP-seq (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Among the identified peaks, 42 overlapped with the WhiA ChIP-seq data (Supplementary Fig. S4D). In total, we found 23 peaks located within 200 bp of a transcriptional start site, with 19 of these promoters overlapping with promoters identified in ChIP-seq (Supplementary Table S2). The significant overlap between the ChIP-seq and IDAP-seq data validates the primary WhiA binding motif and confirms several in vivo binding sites.

Defining the WhiA regulon

Next, we aimed to determine how WhiA influences transcription of its putative regulon genes. We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on whiA knockdown mutants grown ± ATc for 4 days before collecting samples for RNA processing. RNA-seq revealed 526 and 567 genes that were significantly downregulated and upregulated, respectively, upon whiA depletion (Fig. 2D). The repression of whiA led to global transcriptomic changes that affect one-quarter of Mtb genes, which is unsurprising given that whiA is an essential transcription factor. It is likely that most of these transcriptional changes represent indirect effects of depleting an essential gene rather than direct WhiA regulon targets. Thus, we focused our transcriptomic analysis on the putative regulon genes identified by ChIP-seq.

Among the 153 putative WhiA regulon genes, whiA depletion resulted in the downregulation of 46 genes, indicating that WhiA activates these genes (Supplementary Table S3). Conversely, whiA depletion led to upregulation of 20 putative regulon genes, suggesting that WhiA represses these genes. The remaining 87 putative regulon genes had no significant change in expression. This does not necessarily exclude these genes from the WhiA regulon because it is possible that WhiA regulates them under conditions not tested in our experiment.

WhiA activated the essential septation factor sepF, consistent with septation defects observed in whiA mutants (Fig. 2D). Surprisingly, WhiA also regulated several essential genes involved in central dogma processes, such as activating key components of DNA replication, including dnaB and dnaE1 (Fig. 2D), revealing a novel regulatory role for WhiA. Additionally, WhiA activated genes encoding the essential ATP-dependent helicase UvrD2, and ribosomal proteins RpsF and RplB. WhiA repressed many immunogenic PE/PPE genes such as PE11–PPE17 and PPE35 (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, WhiA repressed whiB2, suggesting complex functional interactions within the broader transcriptional network governing cell division. Overall, the WhiA regulon demonstrates that WhiA plays a critical role in regulating essential processes for growth including cell division, DNA replication, and ribosomal proteins. WhiA’s role in repressing PE/PPE genes suggests that WhiA may also regulate virulence and host–pathogen interactions [45, 46].

Analysis of WhiB2-binding sites

ChIP-seq with N-terminal 3xFLAG-WhiB2 identified 184 binding peaks across the Mtb genome (Fig. 3A). Analysis of these peaks identified the same 8 bp GACACGCC consensus motif that was identified for WhiA (Fig. 3B). Motif analysis also identified a similar secondary motif bound by WhiB2, as well as additional enriched motifs (Supplementary Fig. S3B), which suggests secondary binding sites or binding sites of a co-factor. Among the binding sites, 104 were located within putative promoters, resulting in 239 putative WhiB2 regulon genes (Supplementary Table S4).

Functional enrichment analysis of putative WhiB2 regulon genes showed an enrichment in ribosomal genes (Fig. 3C), with WhiB2 binding to the same rpsJ operon promoter region that WhiA binds. Other genes that are shared between the datasets include dnaE1, dnaB, recA, uvrD2, lppL, lppW, rv0794c, sepF, clpX, sigE, and several PE/PPE genes (Fig. 3A). WhiB2 also bound to its own promoter, which is consistent with reports that WhiB proteins can autoregulate their expression [10, 11, 47–49].

WhiB2 putative regulon genes were also significantly enriched for peptidoglycan biosynthesis and regulation genes (Fig. 3C). WhiB2 bound upstream of ftsZ, which encodes the tubulin homolog that determines the cell division site, and its interacting partner ftsW, a critical component of the divisome that mediates septation [50]. WhiB2 also bound the promoter region of the two-component system mtrAB, which has a role in regulating cell wall synthesis, maintaining cell envelope integrity, and promoting intrinsic drug resistance [7, 51, 52]. In addition to binding to the recA promoter, WhiB2 bound to the promoter of the ruvABC operon which is induced in response to DNA damage and helps resolve Holliday junctions [53]. WhiB2 also bound to the promoters of resuscitating promoting factors rpfA and rpfB, which are peptidoglycan-hydrolyzing enzymes that are critical for virulence and resuscitating from dormancy [54, 55].

Studying WhiB proteins in vitro is challenging due to the oxygen- and nitric oxide-sensitivity of their [4Fe–4S] cluster, which is crucial for protein function [8, 14]. Previous efforts to purify recombinant WhiB proteins have relied on co-purification with other transcriptional components, including the primary housekeeping σ factor, σA [15, 56]. However, because σA independently binds DNA, this approach precludes direct assessment of WhiB2 DNA binding activity in vitro.

Defining the WhiB2 regulon

Transcriptomic profiling was performed on whiB2 knockdown mutants after 48 h incubation ± ATc. RNA-seq analysis identified 346 significantly downregulated genes and 203 upregulated genes upon whiB2 depletion (Fig. 3D). As described earlier, depletion of an essential gene leads to global transcriptomic changes that are not directly related to WhiB2 regulation, thus we focused our analysis on the putative WhiB2 regulon genes defined by ChIP-seq.

Among the 239 putative regulon genes, whiB2 depletion resulted in the downregulation of 76 putative regulon genes and the upregulation of 5 putative regulon genes, indicating that WhiB2 activates and represses these target genes, respectively (Supplementary Table S5). The remaining 158 genes had no significant change in expression upon whiB2 depletion. This does not necessarily exclude these genes from the WhiB2 regulon because it is possible that WhiB2 regulates them under conditions not tested in our experiment.

Some WhiB2 regulon genes overlapped with those regulated by WhiA, such as WhiB2 activating dnaE1 and sepF (Fig. 3D). Additional WhiB2 regulatory activity included activating ftsZ, ftsW, mtrAB, recA, and ruvABC, and repressing PE13–PPE18. Overall, WhiB2 has a crucial role in regulating key genes involved in cell division, cell wall synthesis, DNA replication, and repair.

WhiA and whiB2 depletion increase Mtb susceptibility to DNA replication targeting antibiotics

WhiA and WhiB2 regulate essential components of DNA replication, representing a novel regulatory function for these transcription factors. To explore the impact of whiA and whiB2 expression on Mtb drug susceptibility, we designed weak CRISPRi sgRNAs that partially deplete target transcripts without completely inhibiting growth (Supplementary Fig. S5). We measured the MICs of a panel of antibiotics against the CRISPRi strains after incubating with ATc for 5 days before adding antibiotics. We tested anti-tubercular drugs used in clinical settings, including isoniazid, which targets mycolic acid synthesis, and levofloxacin, which targets DNA gyrase. We also included nargenicin, an antibiotic effective against gram-positive bacteria and mycobacteria that inhibits DnaE1 in a DNA-dependent manner [57].

Partial depletion of whiA and whiB2 significantly sensitized Mtb to nargenicin, resulting in 10-fold and 100-fold increased susceptibility, respectively (Fig. 4). These MIC shifts were consistent with whiB2 being more vulnerable to gene knockdown than whiA [36]. These results indicate that whiA and whiB2 expression strongly influenced the potency of nargenicin, validating our findings that WhiA and WhiB2 directly activate dnaE1 expression. There was only a minimal shift of the levofloxacin MIC for whiA and whiB2 mutants. The target of levofloxacin, DNA gyrase, was not a target in the WhiA and WhiB2 regulons, indicating that the sensitivity of Mtb to DNA-targeting antibiotics may be dependent on the expression of genes directly regulated by these transcription factors. As expected, partial depletion of whiA and whiB2 had no effect on susceptibility to isoniazid (Fig. 4).

Comparing the WhiA and WhiB2 regulons

In total, WhiA and WhiB2 shared 52 binding sites, with 38 (73%) of these shared sites located within promoters (Supplementary Tables S1 and S4). Among the 96 putative regulon genes shared between WhiA and WhiB2, most showed no significant change in expression upon depletion of either transcription factor (Supplementary Fig. S6). However, 17 shared genes were activated by WhiA and WhiB2, providing strong evidence for dual regulation. Additionally, approximately one-quarter of shared genes were activated by one transcription factor but showed no significant change in expression upon depletion of the other, making it unclear whether both factors contribute to their regulation (Supplementary Fig. S6). Similarly, 14 shared genes were repressed by one transcription factor with no significant expression change upon depletion of the other, leaving it unclear whether WhiA and WhiB2 dually repress these genes or if they regulate them individually. Notably, two genes were bound by both transcription factors but showed opposing regulatory effects—repressed by WhiA and activated by WhiB2. While this may suggest competitive binding at these promoters, this result could also reflect technical variability in RNA-seq rather than clear evidence of direct antagonistic regulation by WhiA and WhiB2.

Our findings that WhiA and WhiB2 share a subset of regulon genes is consistent with their roles in Sven where these transcription factors were shown to be co-dependent. While WhiA and WhiB2 share some regulon genes, there are several genes for which dual regulation remains unclear or unsupported by binding and transcriptomic data. We sought to investigate whether these genes are co-regulated or if there is evidence for independent regulatory activity of WhiA and WhiB2 in Mtb.

For follow-up analysis, we chose dnaE1 and sepF, genes that have strong evidence for dual activation; uvrD2 and dnaB, which were bound by both transcription factors but only transcriptionally activated by WhiA; and ftsZ and mtrA, which were only bound and transcriptionally activated by WhiB2 (Supplementary Fig. S7A). New RNA samples were collected after CRISPRi depletion and RT-qPCR was performed on candidate target genes. Depletion of whiA resulted in downregulation of dnaE1, sepF, uvrD2, and dnaB, validating RNA-seq results (Supplementary Fig. S7B). WhiA knockdown also led to downregulation of ftsZ and mtrA, despite these genes not being hits in WhiA ChIP-seq. Depletion of whiB2 led to downregulation of the genes that were in its RNAseq-defined regulon; however, it also resulted in upregulation of dnaB and uvrD2, which is the opposite response from that observed in the whiA knockdown mutant (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Due to the essentiality of both transcription factors, directly assessing their requirement at candidate promoters in vivo is challenging. To overcome this, we next conducted in vitro experiments to evaluate the requirement of each transcription factor in transcription activation.

WhiA and WhiB2 dually activate transcription in vitro

To determine whether WhiA and WhiB2 directly and cooperatively regulate transcription at these candidate promoters, we employed a fluorescent aptamer-based in vitro transcription assay [34]. DNA templates were constructed with the promoter of interest upstream of the Spinach-mini RNA sequence (Fig. 5A). Transcriptional activity of each promoter was assessed using RNA polymerase holoenzyme (RNAP-holo) and the general transcription factor CarD, in the presence or absence of WhiB2 and WhiA. When the Spinach-mini aptamer is transcribed, it binds the fluorophore 3,5-difluoro-4-hydroxybenzylidene imidazolinone (DFHBI), causing the emission of fluorescence, which serves as a readout for transcription (Fig. 5B). To quantify transcriptional activation, we focused on the linear steady-state phase of the fluorescent signal curve to calculate relative transcription rates [34].

WhiA- and WhiB2-dependent effects on the dnaE1, ftsZ, and dnaB promoters. (A) Schematic representation of aptamer-based transcription assay as used in this work. (B) Graphical representation of the DNA construct used as template in this assay. The aptamer assay was used to assess the effects of WhiA, WhiB2, and WhiA + WhiB2 on transcription by Mtb Holo-RNAP in the presence of CarD at the dnaE1 (C and D), ftsZ (E and F), and dnaB (G and H) promoters. Data represent mean ± standard error (s.e.) from three independent experimental replicates. (D, F, and H). Histograms comparing the rates of transcription is depicted with relevant P-values comparing each condition to Holo-RNAP only. *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001; NS, not significant.

None of the promoters showed significant activity with RNAP-holo alone. In contrast, all three promoters were strongly activated when both WhiA and WhiB2 were included in the reaction (Fig. 5C–H). The dnaE1 promoter exhibited significant increase in aptamer fluorescence activity relative to RNAP-holo alone, with no significant or negligible increase when either factor was added individually (Fig. 5C and D). A similar pattern was observed for the ftsZ promoter, where both factors were required for detectable transcription (Fig. 5E and F). This result contrasts with previous ChIP-seq data that showed WhiB2, but not WhiA, bound to the ftsZ promoter (Supplementary Fig. S7A), highlighting the importance for validation of identified regulon targets. For the dnaB promoter, WhiB2 alone modestly increased transcription, but the addition of WhiA led to substantial enhancement, again supporting a model of dual regulation (Fig. 5G and H). In conclusion, all three tested promoters showed a minimal basal activity with RNAP-holo and significant transcriptional activation in the presence of both WhiA and WhiB2. These results suggest that despite discrepancies in the ChIP-seq and RNA-seq data, WhiA and WhiB2 dually activate expression of DNA replication and cell division genes in Mtb.

Discussion

This work provides a comprehensive investigation into the genetic mechanisms of WhiA and WhiB2 regulatory functions in Mtb. The major finding is that WhiA and WhiB2 are critical for Mtb growth by regulating essential cellular processes such as ribosome function, DNA replication and repair, cell wall synthesis, and cell division. We demonstrate that WhiA and WhiB2 are both required for activation of selected target genes in vitro where both proteins cooperate with the RNAP machinery to activate transcription.

Although ChIP-seq analysis revealed incomplete overlap in the DNA binding sites of WhiA and WhiB2, we found no evidence supporting independent transcriptional activation by either factor. In vitro transcription assays using the ftsZ promoter demonstrate that WhiA is required for transcription activation, despite this gene not emerging as a WhiA binding target by ChIP-seq. Thus, we are proposing a model where WhiA and WhiB2 are required to activate transcription at all target genes where there is evidence of at least one of them binding and activating expression. This is also supported by the previously identified WhiA binding motif that we identified in both the WhiA and WhiB2 ChIP-seq analysis [15]. However, this model is limited to gene activation and does not apply to gene repression. Gene repression occurs through direct promoter binding that blocks RNAP access, an interaction that does not require co-activation or engagement with the transcriptional machinery. Thus, either transcription factor could independently mediate gene repression.

This requirement for dual activation was also reported for WhiA and WhiB2 homologs in Sven [15]. ChIP-seq experiments in Sven demonstrated an overlap in binding sites and identified the same consensus motif for both transcription factors, consistent with the results of our experiments [13, 14]. Structural studies further revealed that WhiA is responsible for binding directly to the GACAC motif, while WhiB2 interacts weakly with DNA in a sequence-nonspecific manner [15]. We propose that these transcription factors function similarly in Mtb, where WhiA binds directly to the GACACGCC motif and WhiB2 binds adjacent to WhiA in a sequence-nonspecific manner. This explains why the ChIP-seq experiments identified the same binding motif. Further structural studies in Mtb are needed to fully understand WhiA and WhiB2’s interactions with RNAP at target promoters.

The finding that WhiA and WhiB2 transcriptionally activate dnaE1 and dnaB represents a novel pathway for regulating DNA replication. Previously reported mechanisms of regulating DNA replication in bacteria are posttranslational and rely on protein interactions to control the replication initiator protein DnaA’s ability to bind and unwind oriC, either by competing with DnaA to bind oriC or by interacting with DnaA to promote ATP hydrolysis [58, 59]. WhiA and WhiB2’s transcriptional control of DNA replication appears to be unique to mycobacteria and has not been reported for streptomyces [13, 14].

Our findings reveal that WhiA and WhiB2 function as regulators of the Mtb cell cycle. A previous study investigating cell cycle expression patterns in Mtb found that DNA replication and cell division are temporally segregated [6]. WhiA and WhiB2 regulate genes required across the cell cycle, including genes involved in DNA replication and repair, cell wall synthesis, and cell division, implying that they must temporally coordinate the activation of their regulon genes. WhiA was found to be cell cycle regulated, with its expression peaking during DNA replication. WhiB2 on the other hand was not cell cycle regulated, but its expression peaked before cytokinesis and dipped before DNA replication. The expression profiles suggest that expression of whiA and whiB2 peak at opposing times, which seems contradictory given that these genes function together. Their opposing expression profiles may serve as a mechanism for temporally controlling the activation of WhiA and WhiB2 regulon genes in coordination with cell cycle progression, ensuring precise regulation of target genes by modulating transcription factor expression levels over time. A similar transcriptional regulatory network is used in Sven, where whiA is constitutively expressed throughout the cell cycle and whiB2 is repressed until it is needed during sporulation when it becomes activated [13, 60].

Previously ChIP-seq experiments with >200 transcription factors in Mtb, including WhiA and WhiB2 have been reported [4]. Minch et al. performed ChIP-seq with N- and C-terminal FLAG-tagged proteins and identified 379 WhiA-binding sites and 10 WhiB2-binding sites. The top consensus motif identified in the WhiA-binding sites was CGACACGCCGn, which is similar to the primary WhiA motif identified in our experiments. The previously reported binding data overlapped with our binding results, with 126 shared WhiA-binding sites. In addition, 14 binding sites identified in our WhiB2 ChIP-seq corresponded with the published WhiA binding sites. Differences observed between the two datasets can be explained by different analysis methods for identifying binding sites. The substantial overlap provides strong evidence that the WhiA-binding sites identified here are biologically relevant. The previous study identified 10 WhiB2-binding sites, which were all also identified in our studies. The low number of binding sites identified for WhiB2 may be explained by the use of a single FLAG epitope for immunoprecipitation. Our experiments demonstrated that successful immunoprecipitation of WhiB2 required an N-terminal triple FLAG tag with a flexible linker (Supplementary Fig. S2). Although a consensus motif couldn’t be identified for WhiB2 from these experiments, a previous study in M. smegmatis using a one-hybrid system identified a cGACACGc motif critical for WhiB2 regulatory function [17], consistent with the motif observed in our study.

In parallel to the prior published ChIP-seq data, overexpression-based transcriptomic experiments revealed that WhiA and WhiB2 regulate expression of 10 and 6 genes, respectively [5]. This represents a small number of genes to be directly regulated by two essential transcription factors, particularly for WhiA which bound to numerous sites in their ChIP-seq studies [4]. The apparent lack of transcriptional regulation of these targets should not preclude these genes as part of the regulon. Our data demonstrated that WhiA and WhiB2 directly regulate the expression of dozens of genes, including ones not previously reported. It is likely that WhiA and WhiB2 regulate some genes in specific physiological conditions such as in response to stress, which were not yet explored.

Taken together, these results underscore the central role of WhiA and WhiB2 in orchestrating essential cellular functions in Mtb, potentially through a temporally controlled, cooperative transcriptional program linked to the bacterial cell cycle.
