Enterococcus faecium pneumonia diagnosed by metagenomic next-generation sequencing in a patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Lidan Li, Danyang Wang, Chujun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnterococcus faecium is a rare pathogen in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), and its diagnosis is challenging, particularly when prior antibiotic therapy hampers isolation by conventional culture. We report a 72-year-old man with a 6-year history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) who presented with fever, cough, and progressive dyspnea. Despite empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam followed by imipenem-cilastatin), his condition deteriorated into acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. All routine cultures of blood, sputum, and throat swabs were negative, and extensive atypical pathogen screening was unrevealing. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of sputum and subsequently bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, performed at the ISO 15189-accredited central laboratory of Qujing Central Hospital of Yunnan Province, detected high read counts of E. faecium (sputum: 22,710 reads; BAL: 7921 reads; opportunistic pathogen, classification B), with simultaneous routine screening for 31 resistance genes, 4 resistance loci, and virulence genes, all negative. Additionally, sputum mNGS detected Epstein-Barr virus (Human gammaherpesvirus 4, 6715 reads, normal microbiota, classification C), and BAL fluid mNGS detected HSV-1 (Human alphaherpesvirus 1, 407 reads, normal microbiota, classification C). Both herpesviruses had classification C and were interpreted as non-pathogenic "bystanders"; no antiviral therapy was administered. The patient gradually improved on imipenem-cilastatin plus moxifloxacin and was successfully extubated and discharged. This case suggests that E. faecium can cause severe CAP in COPD patients, and mNGS is a valuable diagnostic tool when conventional cultures are negative; herpesviruses with classification C detected by mNGS should not be overinterpreted.Keywords: Enterococcus faecium pneumonia, Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Community-acquired pneumonia, Culture-negative pneumonia, Opportunistic pathogen

Introduction

Enterococci are Gram-positive cocci that normally colonize the human gastrointestinal tract. Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium are important healthcare-associated pathogens causing urinary tract infections, bloodstream infections, infective endocarditis, and surgical site infections, but their role in lower respiratory tract infections is uncommon and controversial [1], [2], [3].

E. faecium has become a globally disseminated healthcare-associated pathogen due to its intrinsic ability to acquire antibiotic resistance [4]. A literature review found that most enterococcal pleuropulmonary infections occurred in elderly patients with comorbidities including immunosuppression, malignancy, renal failure, cardiovascular disease, or COPD [5]. However, community-acquired E. faecium pneumonia is even rarer [6]. Moreover, diagnosis is often delayed or missed—not because the organism is fastidious (enterococci grow readily on routine media)—but because conventional cultures may fail to isolate it from patients after antibiotic administration [7].

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as a powerful tool for pathogen detection, especially when conventional methods yield negative results. By directly sequencing microbial DNA, mNGS can identify pathogens without prespecified targets, independent of microbial viability [7], [8], [9]. Furthermore, mNGS data can be simultaneously used for screening resistance genes and virulence factors. However, mNGS often detects multiple microorganisms simultaneously, requiring differentiation between primary pathogens and "bystanders" based on pathogenicity classification, read counts, and clinical context. We report a case of severe community-acquired E. faecium pneumonia diagnosed by mNGS, with concurrent detection of HSV−1 and EBV (classification C) determined to be non-pathogenic "bystanders.".

Case report

A 72-year-old man presented with a 7-day history of cough, sputum production, wheezing, and fever. He had a 6-year history of COPD; pulmonary function testing 6 years earlier showed FEV₁/FVC < 0.7 and FEV₁ 58% of predicted, consistent with GOLD stage 2 COPD. His baseline functional status was dyspnea on climbing two flights of stairs (mMRC grade 2). In the past year, he had two outpatient acute exacerbations and one hospitalization 3 months prior (treated with piperacillin-tazobactam for 7 days). He had not used antibiotics or corticosteroids before this illness and had not received influenza or pneumococcal vaccines. He had a 30-pack-year smoking history but had quit 2 years ago.

Physical examination on admission: temperature 37.6 °C, pulse 86/min, respirations 20/min, blood pressure 145/81 mmHg, oxygen saturation 79% on room air. Chest examination revealed a barrel-shaped chest, diminished breath sounds, and scattered crackles.

Hospital day 1: Arterial blood gas on nasal cannula (FiO₂ 40%): pH 7.417, PaCO₂ 36.4 mmHg, PaO₂ 59.9 mmHg, PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio 150. Chest CT showed bilateral diffuse infiltrates and emphysema (Fig. 1). Laboratory tests (performed at the ISO 15189-accredited central laboratory of Qujing Central Hospital of Yunnan Province): leukocytosis (WBC 14.6 ×10⁹/L, neutrophils 89.7%), elevated CRP (91.2 mg/L). Blood, sputum, and throat swab cultures were obtained. A clinical diagnosis of acute exacerbation of COPD with lower respiratory tract infection and chronic respiratory failure was made. Empiric intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 g q8h) was initiated.

Chest CT on admission showing bilateral pulmonary infiltrates and emphysema.

Hospital day 3: ABG on face mask: pH 7.310, PaCO₂ 53.0 mmHg, PaO₂ 81.0 mmHg, PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio 202.5. Laboratory: WBC 14.9 × 10⁹/L, neutrophils 96.6%, CRP 217.3 mg/L, procalcitonin 0.572 µg/L. Chest CT showed progression of bilateral infiltrates and a new small pleural effusion (Fig. 2). All initial cultures were negative. Extensive atypical pathogen screening was unrevealing. Despite improved oxygenation, the patient remained febrile with hemoptysis and worsening inflammatory markers. After multidisciplinary consultation, antibiotics were changed to imipenem-cilastatin (1.0 g q8h), and blood and sputum samples were sent for mNGS (performed at the central laboratory of Qujing Central Hospital of Yunnan Province).

Endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation were initiated. Antibiotics were escalated to imipenem-cilastatin plus moxifloxacin (0.4 g qd). BAL was performed, and fluid was sent for culture and mNGS.

Microbiological methods

Conventional cultures were processed at the ISO 15189-accredited central laboratory of Qujing Central Hospital of Yunnan Province. Blood cultures were incubated in the BACT/ALERT® 3D system (bioMérieux) for 5 days. Sputum and BAL fluid specimens were inoculated onto blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar, and incubated at 35 °C in 5% CO₂ for 72 h. BAL fluid was also inoculated onto anaerobic blood agar and incubated anaerobically for 72 h. Gram staining of BAL fluid showed abundant polymorphonuclear leukocytes with no clearly visible microorganisms.

mNGS was performed at the same ISO 15189-accredited central laboratory of Qujing Central Hospital of Yunnan Province, using the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform. Nucleic acid was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Microbiome Kit (Qiagen). Libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina) and sequenced with a depth of approximately 20 million single-end reads (75 bp). Raw data were quality-filtered, human sequences were subtracted, and the remaining microbial sequences were aligned to a curated pathogen database using a proprietary bioinformatics pipeline. A species was considered detected if it had > 3 unique reads and a relative abundance > 0.1% after background subtraction. Pathogenicity classification was based on our laboratory standards: Class A = pathogenic microorganisms; Class B = opportunistic pathogens; Class C = normal microbiota.

The mNGS data were simultaneously analyzed for resistance genes, resistance loci, and virulence genes. Resistance gene screening covered 31 clinically important resistance genes, including carbapenemase genes (IMP, NDM, SPM, KPC, PER, VIM, SIM, DIM, OXA, ACT, GIM), ESBL genes (CTX-M, GES, VEB, CMY, OKP, TLA, ACC, MOX, MIR, SHV, FOX), quinolone resistance genes (QnrA, QnrB), methicillin resistance gene (mecA), colistin resistance gene (MCR), vancomycin resistance genes (vanA, vanB, vanC), and sulfonamide resistance gene (sul). Resistance locus screening covered 4 key loci of the Mycoplasma pneumoniae 23S rRNA gene (positions 2063, 2064, 2067, 2617). Virulence gene screening covered rmpA, fyuA, fepA, iroN, iutA, cnf1, sat, eta, etb, tox, and MPN372.

Discussion

This case report describes severe community-acquired E. faecium pneumonia in a patient with underlying COPD, diagnosed by mNGS after multiple negative conventional cultures. Several aspects warrant discussion: the rarity of E. faecium as a pulmonary pathogen, the diagnostic challenges posed by negative cultures in the context of antibiotic exposure, the role of mNGS in overcoming these limitations, and the clinical interpretation of mNGS results according to pathogenicity classification.

E. faecium is increasingly recognized as an important healthcare-associated pathogen, particularly in immunocompromised hosts, patients with prolonged hospitalization, or prior antibiotic exposure [1], [4]. The E. faecium clonal complex 17 (CC17) is a leading global cause of healthcare-acquired infections [2]. However, enterococcal pneumonia remains rare. Li et al. found that most enterococcal pleuropulmonary infections occurred in elderly patients with comorbidities including cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, malignancy, or structural lung disease [5]. Portero et al. reported the first case of community-acquired E. faecium pneumonia in 1994, and subsequent reports remain extremely limited [6]. The unique aspect of our case is that the patient had community-acquired infection with COPD but no recent hospitalization or healthcare exposure.

The most striking diagnostic feature is the persistent failure of conventional culture to isolate E. faecium despite high microbial loads detected by mNGS. This discrepancy is not because enterococci are fastidious—on the contrary, E. faecium grows readily on standard media such as blood agar. The reasons for persistently negative conventional cultures are likely multifactorial. Firstly, although the patient denied antibiotic use prior to admission, unrecognized or unreported antimicrobial exposure during the 7-day symptomatic period cannot be excluded, as patients often overlook over-the-counter medications or treatments administered at primary care clinics. Secondly, specimens were obtained after empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated on hospital day 1, and BAL fluid was collected after 5 days of continuous antimicrobial exposure, which may have suppressed bacterial viability while leaving sufficient microbial DNA for mNGS detection [7]. Lastly, E. faecium is an infrequent pathogen in the lower respiratory tract and may have been present in low bacterial loads, further complicating isolation by conventional methods. mNGS detects DNA from both viable and non-viable organisms, making it particularly useful in the setting of antibiotic pretreatment.

The cost of mNGS testing at Qujing Central Hospital of Yunnan Province is 600 RMB per sample, substantially lower than the reported market price (approximately 3000–4000 RMB), significantly enhancing clinical accessibility.

In this case, mNGS screening for 31 resistance genes, 4 resistance loci, and virulence genes was all negative. The negative resistance gene results have important clinical implications: negative vanA, vanB, vanC, and carbapenemase genes suggest that the E. faecium isolate may remain susceptible to vancomycin and carbapenems, partly explaining the favorable clinical response to imipenem-cilastatin plus moxifloxacin. Negative virulence gene screening suggests the strain’s virulence may be limited, and the severe presentation may be more attributable to underlying COPD-related lung damage.

Of particular note are the herpesviruses detected: EBV (6715 reads) in sputum and HSV−1 (407 reads) in BAL fluid. Both had pathogenicity classification C (normal microbiota). This classification has important clinical implications. According to our laboratory standards, Class C represents normal microbiota, generally not considered pathogenic. EBV and HSV−1 have extremely high global infection rates and can be detected in respiratory specimens as "bystanders" representing latent infection reactivation or asymptomatic shedding [10], [11]. Although HSV−1 and EBV can cause severe disease in immunocompromised patients, in most cases their detection in respiratory specimens represents colonization or shedding, not active infection [12]. In this case, the patient’s presentation (high fever, purulent sputum, markedly elevated CRP/procalcitonin) was more consistent with bacterial pneumonia; clinical and inflammatory markers improved with antibacterial therapy; there were no typical viral pneumonia imaging findings; and the patient recovered without antiviral therapy. This case suggests that mNGS results must be interpreted in conjunction with pathogenicity classification and clinical context—microorganisms with Class C (normal microbiota), even with substantial read counts, should not be readily identified as pathogens; while Class B opportunistic pathogens require comprehensive assessment based on host immune status and clinical evidence.

The diagnostic superiority of mNGS in post-antibiotic infections is increasingly recognized. Recent studies have confirmed that in immunocompromised patients with severe pneumonia, mNGS yields significantly higher positivity rates than conventional testing [8], [9]. Notably, for critically ill pneumonia patients in the ICU, mNGS had significantly higher detection rates for E. faecium and Pneumocystis jirovecii compared to conventional methods (*p* < 0.05) [9].

However, mNGS has limitations. First, despite reduced costs, widespread adoption remains constrained by equipment and personnel requirements. Second, respiratory specimens frequently contain nucleic acids from multiple colonizing organisms—the EBV and HSV−1 detected in this case are typical examples. Third, background microbial DNA from reagents or the environment can introduce noise, potentially causing false-positive results. Fourth, mNGS sensitivity for resistance gene detection depends on database coverage and sequencing depth, with possible missed low-abundance resistance genes.

This case has several limitations. First, no viable isolate was obtained for susceptibility testing. Second, the diagnosis of E. faecium pneumonia was based solely on mNGS results without culture confirmation; although high read counts strongly support a pathogenic role, colonization cannot be entirely excluded. Third, pathogenicity assessment of EBV and HSV−1 was based on classification C and clinical evaluation, without antiviral treatment validation. Fourth, the patient declined follow-up, limiting long-term outcome data.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that E. faecium can cause severe community-acquired pneumonia in patients with underlying COPD, and conventional cultures may fail to detect it due to antibiotic pretreatment. mNGS provides a valuable diagnostic tool in such challenging cases. For microorganisms with Class C detected by mNGS, clinicians should avoid overinterpretation and unnecessary therapy; for Class B opportunistic pathogens, comprehensive assessment based on host immune status and clinical context is required. Clinicians should consider E. faecium as a potential pathogen in patients with COPD, prior antibiotic exposure, and poor response to empirical broad-spectrum therapy, and should employ molecular diagnostic methods when conventional cultures are negative.
