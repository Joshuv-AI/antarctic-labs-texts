Atibuclimab (IC14) for Treatment of Acute Decompensated Heart Failure
Authors: Antonio Abbate, Michele Golino, Austin C Hogwood, Michele Marchetta, Rocio I Lopez, Kelly Parker, Jazmin Kelly, Justin M Canada, Lisa Farr, Josh West, Emily Kontos, Emily Federmann, Anna Priday, Eleonora Mezzaroma, Stefano Toldo, Jan Agosti, Brian W Ziegelaar, Mark W Appleby, David Crowe, Patrick Charmley, Garry Redlich, Benjamin Van Tassell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey words: atibuclimab, CD14, heart failure, inflammation, receptor occupancy

Heart failure (HF) is a chronic and progressive clinical syndrome induced by structural or functional cardiac abnormalities that result in impaired cardiac output and/or elevated cardiac filling pressures.1 The growing burden of cardiovascular risk factors including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and obesity, together with population aging, has led to a marked increase in the incidence and prevalence of HF worldwide.2 In parallel, the widespread adoption of guideline-directed medical therapy targeting neurohormonal pathways has substantially improved long-term survival in patients with myocardial infarction and HF, thereby further increasing the number of individuals living with the disease. Acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) represents a clinical syndrome characterized by the new onset or worsening of signs and symptoms of HF, most commonly occurring in patients with previously established chronic HF. Despite the advancement in treatment with neurohormonal blockers, approximately 80% of patients hospitalized with ADHF have a prior history of HF hospitalization. Rehospitalization rates remain unacceptably high, reaching 13% within the first month after discharge and up to 32% at 1 year.3 Reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is a major determinant of adverse outcomes, with each 10% decrease in LVEF associated with a 49% increase in the risk of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization and with patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction experiencing the highest risk.4

Although multiple experimental therapies are currently under development to address the substantial residual risk associated with HF, effective treatment options for ADHF remain limited. A large body of experimental and clinical evidence implicates inflammation as a key contributor to HF pathophysiology and progression; however, no anti-inflammatory therapies are currently approved for the treatment of HF.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 This highlights an unmet clinical need for therapies capable of targeting acute inflammatory activation in HF and potentially adding a novel mechanistic pillar to contemporary HF treatment.

Cluster of differentiation 14 (CD14) is a pattern recognition receptor that functions as a coreceptor for Toll-like receptors on monocytes and macrophages, amplifying innate immune signaling and promoting activation of myeloid differentiation factor 88 and Toll/interleukin-1 receptor domain–containing adaptor protein inducing interferon beta signaling.10 Activation of this pathway leads to the priming of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain–containing 3 inflammasome pathway and the release of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, which have direct roles in myocardial dysfunction, adverse remodeling, and HF progression.11 Once CD14 is activated, it undergoes proteolytic cleavage and releases presepsin, a circulating biomarker of innate immune activation in cardiovascular disease.12, 13, 14 Because of its proximal position within innate immune signaling cascades, CD14 blockade has the potential to simultaneously attenuate multiple downstream inflammatory pathways. In murine models of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with reperfusion, anti-CD14 therapy preserved myocardial contractility, reduced infarct size and fibrosis, and limited macrophage infiltration.11 Persistent activation of CD14-positive monocytes and macrophages has been implicated in maladaptive myocardial remodeling and progression to HF.11,15 Consistently, elevated circulating soluble CD14 levels are independently associated with incident HF in older adults and are linked to increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.16, 17, 18 Collectively, these findings support CD14 as a central upstream mediator of damage-associated molecular pattern–driven myocardial inflammation and provide a rationale for therapeutic inhibition of this pathway in ADHF.

Atibuclimab (IC14), a function-blocking monoclonal antibody targeting CD14, was therefore hypothesized to attenuate excessive inflammatory activation in this high-risk population. The present study was designed to evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of IC14 in patients with ADHF. We conducted an open-label, phase Ib clinical trial to assess the safety, tolerability, and potential biological and clinical effects of IC14 treatment in this setting.

Methods

Trial design

This was a 2-center, open-label safety, pharmacokinetic and preliminary efficacy study in 10 patients hospitalized for ADHF (Visual Abstract). The safety of IC14 was evaluated over a 90-day period. Participants were enrolled from November 11, 2024, to September 2, 2025, at the University of Virginia in Charlottesville, Virginia, and Virginia Commonwealth University in Richmond, Virginia. The University of Virginia Investigational Review Board served as the central Investigational Review Board for the trial (approval: HSR301721). The study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06556810) on August 16, 2024. The trial protocol is included in the Supplemental Appendix. No significant changes to the protocol were made after the trial began. Study stopping rules and details of safety monitoring and reporting are outlined in the study protocol. The study was performed in accordance with ethical principles derived from the Declaration of Helsinki and is consistent with the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use/Good Clinical Practice.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥21 years admitted with a primary diagnosis of ADHF, defined by acute worsening of dyspnea and objective evidence of elevated cardiac filling pressures or pulmonary congestion, including radiographic or physical examination findings and/or elevated natriuretic peptides. Key inclusion criteria included an LVEF <40% documented within the previous 12 months, moderate to severe dyspnea at enrollment, and biochemical evidence of HF and systemic inflammation, defined as plasma B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) ≥200 pg/mL or N-terminal pro-BNP ≥600 pg/mL, and evidence of systemic inflammation as shown by a high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP) level >3 mg/L. Patients were required to have received intravenous (IV) loop diuretics during the prior 24 hours or to be scheduled to receive them. Patients with alternative causes of acute symptoms, active infection, chronic inflammatory or autoimmune disease, recent or planned advanced cardiac therapies, significant hemodynamic instability, advanced renal disease, or other comorbidities expected to limit short-term survival were excluded. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. A detailed list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in the trial protocol (Supplemental Material). All participants were assessed daily during hospitalization and followed up for up to 90 ± 14 days. Adverse events (AEs) were recorded daily during hospitalization and at postdischarge follow-up visits. Blood samples were obtained at trial entry and on days 1 through 3, 10, and 29 for safety and exploratory biomarkers. Patients received the local standard of care for treatment of their ADHF and follow-up. Additional immunomodulatory drugs were discouraged through day 29.

Study drug

Implicit Bioscience Ltd provided the investigational drug, atibuclimab (IC14), to the investigational drug services at the University of Virginia and the Virginia Commonwealth University. After confirmation of eligibility and completion of baseline assessments, patients received an IV dose of 20 mg/kg (actual body weight) in a solution of 0.9% NaCl over 2 hours on study day 1. The dose of 20 mg/kg was chosen based on a dose-escalation study of IC14 in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (NCT04309604). An interim report on the study showed that a dose of 12 mg/kg IV IC14 was safe and well tolerated. The dose was subsequently increased to 16 mg/kg, then 20 mg/kg, showing safety and sustained optimal receptor occupancy in patients for up to 21 days.19

Study outcomes

The primary safety outcomes were the incidence of treatment-emergent AEs and serious AEs. Safety and tolerability of IC14 were assessed through day 90 using clinical evaluation, vital signs, AE reporting, and safety laboratory testing, including comprehensive metabolic panel, hematology, and coagulation parameters; standard-of-care laboratory results were used when available.

Exploratory biomarkers were assessed to evaluate the biological effects of IC14 on inflammatory and HF-related pathways and included CRP absolute values and change from baseline values using a high-sensitivity assay. CRP is an established prognostic marker in HF that reflects systemic IL-6 and IL-1 activities.7 Additional HF-related biomarkers included BNP absolute values and change from baseline, urine sodium during hospitalization, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. Presepsin was also measured as a biomarker reflecting activation of the CD14-mediated innate immune pathway.

Exploratory clinical efficacy outcomes included HF symptoms, congestion, functional capacity, and cardiac performance. Dyspnea was evaluated using a validated visual analog scale administered at baseline and at 24 hours, 72 hours, day 10, day 29, and 3 months (virtual). The prespecified exploratory endpoint for dyspnea was the change in the visual analog scale score from baseline to 72 hours.

Clinical congestion was assessed using a composite congestion score based on the physical examination at baseline, 24 hours, 72 hours, day 10, and day 29. One point was assigned for each of the following findings: peripheral edema, jugular venous distension ≥10 cm, pulmonary rales, or S3 gallop (range: 0-4). Considering that diminished response to loop diuretics is common during ADHF, diuretic resistance was evaluated as an exploratory endpoint. Diuretic resistance was measured as the total loop diuretic dose (in furosemide IV equivalents) during the first 72 hours of hospitalization.

Functional capacity was assessed using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire, a widely used self-reported questionnaire for assessing the quality of life in patients with HF.20,21 The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire was administered at baseline and was repeated at day 29 and day 90.

Transthoracic Doppler echocardiography was performed before study drug administration and was repeated on day 10. Standard measurements included left ventricular volumes and tricuspid annulus plane systolic excursion, according to the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography.22 The assessment included a comprehensive evaluation of diastolic function measuring transmitral flow parameters and mitral annulus tissue Doppler studies to estimate left ventricular end-diastolic pressure and other parameters reflective of impaired relaxation. All images and loops were acquired, and measurements were performed at the end of the study by 2 operators averaging the 2 measurements for measures differing ≤10%, whereas those with >10% were rereviewed and discussed by the 2 operators resulting in a consensus. Bioimpedance spectroscopy analysis (IMP SFB7, ImpediMed) was performed to estimate fluid status using the edema index (extracellular water/total body water) according to manufacturer instructions. Detailed definitions are provided in the trial protocol and supportive documents (Supplemental Appendix).

Pharmacodynamic outcomes included the assessment of CD14 receptor occupancy and assessment of ex vivo leukocyte activity by measuring IL-6 release after lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated whole-blood incubation.

Sample size and statistical analyses

Ten evaluable participants were selected as adequate for an exploratory receptor-occupancy substudy. Given expected receptor occupancy of >70% at day 10 and <30% at day 29, the primary objective was to describe the magnitude and variability of target engagement over time rather than to test a formal hypothesis. With n = 10, assuming an SD of 10% to 15%, the expected 95% CI half-width around the mean occupancy is approximately 8% to 11%, which is sufficient to determine whether observed receptor occupancy is consistent with meaningful early target engagement and substantial receptor disengagement by day 29. The sample size also limits participant exposure while providing adequate pharmacodynamic information to guide future dose selection. All patients receiving the study treatment were included in the safety and preliminary efficacy analyses (per protocol). Modified intent-to-treat analysis excluded the patient(s) with missing data at day 10. Changes from baseline were calculated for each time point during the study (ie, timepoint minus baseline), if applicable. Data are presented as median and 25th-75th percentiles [Q1-Q3] for potential deviation from normal distribution. Geometric mean ± SD was also calculated for endpoints of special interest. Continuous variables were compared within groups using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical data are presented as number (percentage) and were analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher's exact test as appropriate (expected cell count: <5). Statistical analysis and figures were made using GraphPad PRISM (version 10.3.1) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.2.0). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Safety analysis

AEs, serious AEs, and other safety data were described for all patients who received the study drug. Safety was evaluated from reported AEs, physical examination findings, vital signs, and safety laboratory values. Major adverse cardiac event rates, defined as hospitalization, death, HF admission, and nonfatal stroke, were also recorded. We considered all new symptoms or abnormal findings that could be considered AEs, and we assessed them for the expectedness of the events based on the illnesses and treatments, considering as “likely related” to an event that occurred after treatment with the investigational drug that was somewhat unexpected.

Ex vivo whole blood assay

Whole blood samples for the patients' cohort were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid vacutainer tubes at 3 time points: baseline (preinfusion), 48 hours post IC14 infusion (±24 hours), and 10 days post infusion (±7 days). Whole blood (250 μL) was incubated with 750 μL of Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium cell culture media containing 1) vehicle solution (antibody diluent); 2) vehicle + LPS (10 ng/mL) to stimulate CD-14/TLR4-mediated proinflammatory signaling; 3) IC14, (100 μg/mL); or 4) IC14 + LPS. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours. After the incubation, 55 μL of a 5.5% solution of Triton X-100 (0.5% final concentration) was added per tube, frozen at −80 °C, and later analyzed using the human IL-6 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Figure 1). Doses of LPS that are too high can bypass CD14 to activate TLR4 in the absence of the coreceptor. A pilot experiment had been performed to choose the concentrations of LPS and IC14 to use in the whole blood assay. Whole blood from a healthy patient was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid vacutainer tubes. Fifty microliters of blood was added to 150 μL of Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium containing increasing concentrations of LPS (Escherichia coli strain O111:B4, at 0, 1, 10, 100, and 1,000 ng/mL in the final volume of 200 μL) with increasing concentrations of IC14 (0, 1, 10, 100, and 1,000 μg/mL of final volume), for 24 hours at 37 °C. After the incubation, Triton X-100 (0.5% final concentration) was added to the blood. The blood aliquots were collected, frozen at −80 °C, and analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (DuoSet ELISA, R&D Systems) to measure human IL-6. The curves of IL-6 concentration for each IC14 concentration were plotted, and the LPS/IC14 concentration used in the experiment described previously was decided based on the highest CD14-dependent LPS concentration that we found with the lowest effective CD14 dose.

Ex Vivo Whole-Blood Assay

Whole blood was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes at baseline (preinfusion), 48 hours post IC14 infusion (±24 hours), and day 10 post infusion (±7 days). Aliquots of whole blood were incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium under 4 conditions: vehicle control, E. coli lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 10 ng/mL), IC14 (atibuclimab; 100 μg/mL), or IC14 + LPS. After incubation, Triton X-100 was added, and samples were mixed, frozen at −80 °C, and later analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to quantify human interleukin-6.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 306 patients were screened, and 13 patients provided written informed consent. The reasons for exclusion are listed in Figure 2. Of these, 10 participants were successfully enrolled and received the study drug, whereas 2 patients were deemed to be a screen failure after consent because they did not meet the diuretic use criteria, and 1 patient withdrew after consent, all before any study procedures were completed (Figure 2). Seven patients (70%) were male, 6 (60%) self-identified as White, and 4 (40%) as Black. Median age was 63.5 years [Q1-Q3: 56.0-72.3 years]. Safety

Atibuclimab was safe and well tolerated in all patients. Vital signs remained stable during the IC14 infusion. One participant discontinued study follow-up 9 days after receiving treatment for personal reasons. The remaining 9 participants were followed up through day 90. No treatment-related serious AEs or deaths occurred during the infusion period or throughout the follow-up. A total of 11 AEs were reported in 7 (70%) patients, all of which were considered expected in the context of advanced HF and hospitalization and unlikely related to the investigational treatment (Table 2). Of these, 8 events were classified as severe (grade 3), associated with readmission, including 3 for HF, 1 angina, 1 upper gastrointestinal bleed, 1 oral cavity bleed, 1 hypotension likely related to HF medications, and 1 shortness of breath of unclear etiology. Of the remaining 3 AEs, 2 were classified as moderate (grade 2) associated with a gout flare and recurrence of atrial fibrillation, and 1 was classified as mild (grade 1) associated with hypotension. No clinically significant unexpected abnormalities were detected in hematology, complete metabolic panels, or coagulation parameters during the study period (Table 3).

Receptor occupancy

Median CD14 receptor occupancy was 97% (Q1-Q3: 93%-99%; geometric mean: 95% ± 7%) at day 10 (n = 9) and 58% (Q1-Q3: 13%-80%; geometric mean: 36% ± 33%) at day 29 (n = 9) (Table 3, Figure 3, Supplemental Figure 1). CD14 receptor occupancy was not measured at baseline and was assumed to be zero (Supplemental Figure 2).

Whole blood assay results

A whole blood assay was performed to measure the in vitro CD14 activity in patients before and after IC14 treatment. The assay was developed based on an initial dose-response screening to define the dose of LPS that promoted a CD-14-dependent increase in IL-6 and the minimum dose of IC14 able to prevent the LPS signaling at such a dose. We found that above 10 ng/mL of LPS, IL-6 production bypassed CD14 signaling because any IC14 doses used at 100 and 1,000 ng/mL of LPS could not neutralize the LPS-induced IL-6 production (Figure 5). One hundred micrograms per milliliter was the lowest IC14 dose that could neutralize the 10 ng/mL LPS signaling in vitro (Figure 5); therefore, in the following experiments, the blood of the patients was exposed to combinations of 10 ng/mL LPS with or without 100 μg/mL of IC14.

IC14 antibody infusion significantly decreased the IL-6 response after ex vivo LPS challenge and maintained this reduction for up to 10 days post infusion (Figure 5). Compared with baseline, IL-6 response was decreased at 48 hours by a median of −97% (Q1-Q3: −99% to −96%; P = 0.005; n = 10) and by −94% (Q1-Q3: −98% to −62%; P = 0.008; n = 9) at day 10. Clinical outcomes

Median [Q1-Q3] LVEF significantly improved from 26% [19%-35%] to 32% [22%-41%] at day 10 (P = 0.008, Table 4). A nonsignificant (P = 0.074) reduction in median [Q1-Q3] left ventricular end-systolic volume from 168 [73-200] mL to 135 [67-204] mL was observed, whereas no changes were seen in left ventricular end-diastolic volume, left atrial volume, ratio of early transmitral inflow velocity to early diastolic mitral annular velocity, or tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (Table 4). Median [Q1-Q3] dyspnea analog score decreased from 80 [58-95] at baseline to 35 [21-53] at 24 hours (P = 0.002), 30 [10-41] at 72 hours (P = 0.002), 5 [0-28] at day 10 (P = 0.004), 15 [0-75] at day 29 (P = 0.008), and 30 [8-50] at day 90 (P = 0.004) compared with baseline. The median [Q1-Q3] total congestion score decreased from 3 [2-3] to 2 [1-3] at 24 hours (P = 0.031), 1 [0-1] at 72 hours (P = 0.004), 1 [0-1] at day 10 (P = 0.008), and 0 [0-1] at day 29 (P = 0.016). The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire overall score improved from a median [Q1-Q3] of 21 [14-32] to 64 [34-75] at day 29 (P = 0.012) and 75 [40-83] at day 90 (P = 0.004). Furosemide IV equivalent median dose at baseline was 140 [Q1-Q3: 75-240] mg, with no secondary diuretics. At 72 hours, furosemide IV equivalent median dose was 140 [Q1-Q3: 15-240] mg (P = 0.44), with 2 patients (20%) taking a secondary diuretic (chlorothiazide and metolazone). At day 10, furosemide IV equivalent median dose was 40 [Q1-Q3: 12.5-200] mg (P = 0.11), with 1 patient (10%) taking a secondary diuretic (metolazone). Bioelectrical impedance spectroscopy showed a median [Q1-Q3] edema index at baseline of 47.6% [42.1%-51.4%], at discharge of 44.8% [42.0%-48.9%; P = 0.10], and at day 10 of 46.4% [41.4%-47.7%; P = 0.023]. One patient reported dramatic and rapid improvement in gouty arthritis.

Discussion

In this phase Ib, 2-center, open-label pilot study, in patients admitted for ADHF, we show that a single IV infusion of atibuclimab (IC14; 20 mg/kg) was safe and well tolerated, with none of the patients experiencing drug-related AEs. IC14 is a function-blocking monoclonal antibody to CD14, a coreceptor on monocytes and macrophages which, when activated, is responsible for initiating a cascade of downstream inflammatory signaling.10 Importantly, IC14 exerts selective immunomodulatory effects by blocking CD14-mediated signaling without causing monocyte or macrophage depletion.11 The development of IC14 for the treatment of ADHF in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction is of potential interest because systemic inflammation predicts adverse outcomes,23 and there are no anti-inflammatory drugs approved for the treatment of HF.

IC14 has already been tested in humans with different conditions.24, 25, 26 In patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, IC14 reduced proinflammatory cytokines in plasma, including macrophage inflammatory protein-1 beta and tumor necrosis factor-α.24 In patients with moderate to severe COVID-19 pneumonia, IC14 showed a trend of reducing mortality, although this did not achieve statistical significance given the small study size.26 A prespecified secondary analysis suggested that patients with higher baseline presepsin concentrations derived greater benefit, proposing baseline presepsin as a potential predictive enrichment biomarker for future anti-CD14 trials.27,28

Additionally, CD14 blockade in a murine ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction reperfusion model was shown to preserve contractility, reduce fibrosis, and decrease infarct size.29 Therefore, because of the central role of CD14 in the amplification of inflammatory responses leading to myocardial decompensation in ADHF and the safety record of IC14 in humans, this open-label clinical trial was designed to test the safety and tolerability of IC14 treatment in patients with ADHF and to explore the effects on the acute inflammatory response, myocardial strain, and clinical outcomes.

Beyond safety and tolerability, the present study shows that an infusion of IC14 achieved near-complete monocyte CD14 receptor occupancy at day 10 (>95%) and maintained substantial occupancy at day 29 (>50%), indicating sustained target engagement after a single dose and supporting the feasibility of prolonged CD14 pathway modulation across the early postdischarge phase. Consistently, we observed biomarker changes compatible with attenuation of systemic inflammation (CRP) and myocardial stress (BNP) alongside early improvement in left ventricular systolic function and concurrent improvements in congestion, dyspnea, edema index, and patient-reported health status. Importantly, we found that presepsin, a subtype of soluble CD14, was elevated in acute HF with reduced ejection fraction. Presepsin was rapidly reduced after single infusion of atibuclimab. This adds to the growing body of literature indicating atibuclimab is safe to administer in humans and plays a role as an immune modulator.19,24,30 Functional testing with an ex vivo whole-blood LPS challenge provided mechanistic confirmation of CD14 pathway inhibition, demonstrating a sustained attenuation of LPS-induced IL-6 production after IC14 administration. The additional suppression observed with in vitro IC14 at baseline supports a direct pharmacologic effect on inducible CD14-dependent innate immune response.

This study has several potential limitations. This is a small, open-label trial designed to assess the safety and tolerability of a single administration of atibuclimab (IC14). We screened all consecutive patients admitted with HF. The high screening rate likely reflects the complexity of HF owing to which some patients were excluded because HF was neither the only reason nor the main reason for admission. The narrow criteria for inclusion for this phase Ib study will need to be followed by broader criteria for phase II-III clinical trials in order to establish a greater external validity. The study is a single-arm, open-label trial. Without a randomized control group, it is impossible to definitively determine if improvements were caused by atibuclimab (IC14) or other factors. Furthermore, all patients received standard-of-care treatments for ADHF, such as diuretics and neurohormonal blockers; therefore, the study cannot account for how much of the clinical improvement is due to these standard therapies vs the investigational drug. We reported all AEs and considered as “likely related” to an event that occurred after treatment with the investigational drug that was somewhat unexpected. We acknowledge however that some events may be both expected and related, and this cannot be determined in this single-arm study. The decline in receptor occupancy at day 29 also suggests that the pharmacodynamic effect of a single infusion may be transient and unlikely to cover the entire vulnerable period after hospitalization for ADHF. These considerations underscore the need for phase II randomized, placebo-controlled trials that would also include repeated injections of IC14.

Conclusions

In patients hospitalized for ADHF with reduced ejection fraction with systemic inflammation, CD14 blockade with a single infusion of atibuclimab was safe and well tolerated and achieved robust CD14 target engagement. Additionally, it reduced CRP, BNP, and presepsin levels and improved symptoms and quality of life. These findings provide preliminary evidence for immune modulation via CD14 blockade in patients with ADHF. Further phase II studies are warranted.

Competency in Medical Knowledge

In patients hospitalized for ADHF with systemic inflammation, CD14 blockade with atibuclimab was safe and well tolerated and achieved sustained target engagement, supporting inflammation targeting through the CD14 pathway as a novel therapeutic approach in HF.

Competency in Patient Care

These findings provide early clinical proof of concept that targeting inflammation through the CD14 pathway may complement standard HF care in patients hospitalized for ADHF.

Translational Outlook

Future randomized phase II trials enrolling larger numbers of patients should determine whether CD14 inhibition with IC14 improves clinically meaningful outcomes in HF. These studies should also define biomarker-based enrichment strategies to identify patients most likely to respond and incorporate clinically relevant endpoints to support therapeutic development.
