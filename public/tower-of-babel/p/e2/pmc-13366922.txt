NRSF regulation of OPRM1 through histone acetylation
Authors: Ryan Thompson, Christina Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Neuron Restrictive Silencer Factor (NRSF) is a master transcriptional regulator of neuronal differentiation. Dysregulation of NRSF in neurons contributes to multiple neurological disorder. Following peripheral nerve injury, NRSF is upregulated and has been implicated in the establishment and maintenance of neuropathic pain. While the molecular and genetic changes in nerves occur during neuropathic pain are numerous and complex, a key downstream consequence of NRSF activation is repression of the mu-opioid receptor gene (OPRM1), a critical mediator of nociception and opioid responsiveness. NRSF controls repression of gene expression through recruitment of histone deacetylases (HDACs) to target genes. Here, we demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of HDACs with valproic acid (VPA) is able to de-repress expression of OPRM1 in iPSC-derived neurons. To produce more targeted epigenetic changes to specific genomic loci, we further employed a CRISPR based epigenetic editing strategy using dCas9 fused to the histone acetyltransferase p300. Targeted acetylation of the OPRM1 gene promoter region with dCas9-p300 upregulated OPRM1 gene expression. Restoring OPRM1 expression in neurons is able to alter its responsiveness to mu-opioid receptor agonist, as evidenced by changes in calcium signaling. This study demonstrated targeted epigenetic acetylation with dCas9-p300 is a powerful strategy to reverse NRSF-medicated gene repression and restore opioid receptor function, highlighting a potential therapeutic avenue for neuropathic pain.Electronic supplementary materialThe online version of this article (10.1186/s13148-026-02146-5) contains supplementary material, which is available to authorized users.Keywords: Neuron restrictive silencer factor, NRSF, Opioid receptor, OPRM1, acetylation, epigenetics, cas9, p300, neuropathic pain, calcium signaling, histone deacetylase, HDACi

Introduction

Nerve damage is a common cause in the development of neuropathic pain. Traumatic injury to peripheral afferents causes changes in gene expression of ion channels and nociceptors that is believed to cause hyperexcitability of damaged nerves [1, 2]. Current treatments for neuropathic pain provide temporary relief for patients but fail to cure damaged nerves [3]. In addition, many standard treatment prescribes use of opioids such as oxycodone and morphine of which long term use carries a high risk of addiction among other neurological side effects [4]. Current research has heavily implicated inflammation and the master transcriptional regulator, the Neuron Restrictive Silencer Factor (NRSF) [5–7], as major drivers of changes in gene expression associated with neuropathic pain. Interestingly, NRSF plays a more specific role by using epigenetic mechanisms to repress gene expression of ion channels and pain receptors involved in analgesia and hyperexcitability of nerves [8, 9].

A key target of NRSF-mediated repression is the u-opioid receptor (OPRM1) [10–12]. OPRM1 is of key relevance as this is hypothesized to dysregulate the nerves’ inherent analgesic response. Indeed, research has shown that the promoter region contains a binding site for NRSF and that increased expression of NRSF in C-fibers repressed OPRM1 and contribute to reduced sensitivity to morphine [8]. A key function of the activation of OPRM1 receptor is to modulate calcium signaling [13, 14]. In particular, activation of OPRM1 by agonists such as morphine or the synthetic opioid, DAMGO, blocks calcium channels from opening due to cellular depolarization, which interrupts electrical signaling through the cell. Loss of OPRM1 causes loss of sensitivity to opioids and likely disrupts the cell’s overall analgesic effects in neuropathic pain. This functional relationship makes OPRM1 a key target for investigating the role of NRSF in neuropathic pain. Treatments that restore gene expression of OPRM1 to homeostasis by modulating NRSF function could offer a cure for pain [15, 16].

The role of NRSF is intriguing because it represses gene expression by recruiting chromatin modifying enzymes [9, 17, 18] to gene regulatory regions that leave repressive epigenetic marks, including histone deacetylases, histone methytransferases, and DNA methytransferases, on neural genes involved in neuropathic pain. This suggests that epigenetic treatments that reverse these repressive marks could be novel therapeutics for neuropathic pain [15]. The N-terminal domain of NRSF contains an mSin3 binding domain that is responsible for recruitment of histone deacetylases while the C-terminal domain recruits its major corepressor, REST corepressor 1 (CoREST). Through CoREST’s interaction with methyl CpG binding protein 2 (MeCP2), NRSF recognizes methylated DNA. NRSF is also able to recruit the histone methyltransferase, euchromatic histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2 (EHMT2 aka G9a) [19]. Through these various interactions, NRSF directs histone deacetylation, histone methylation, and indirectly, DNA methylation. The effector domains of NRSF and chromatin modifying enzymes they recruit make attractive therapeutic targets. Of particular interest to this study is NRSF’s role in histone deacetylation. Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors have shown efficacy in rodent neuropathic pain model and can relieve morphine tolerance [15, 16]. Epigenetic treatment of cancer is already being pursued and small molecule treatments include histone deacetylase inhibitors and bromodomain and extra-terminal motif (BET) domain inhibitors [20–22]. This suggests that small molecule epigenetic-based treatments that attenuate the ability of NRSF to disrupt gene expression could be a broad therapeutic strategy.

Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors are actively investigated as therapeutic agents in cancer and are widely used in the treatment of epilepsy. Here, we sought to determine if modulating the epigenetic-based functions of NRSF could attenuate NRSF-mediated gene repression and could therefore be an alternative therapeutic strategy for models of neuropathic pain. We tested various HDAC inhibitors on iPSC-derived differentiated neurons. We found that cultured cells tolerated treatment with valproic acid (VPA) with expected changes in acetylation and gene expression with minimal toxicity. As VPA is currently used as a safe treatment for epilepsy [23, 24], we extended its use to our study. The results could easily transfer from our study to advance the research into the regulation of OPRM1 during development of neuropathic pain.

However, because small‑molecule HDAC inhibitors act globally and lack locus specificity, we next pursued a targeted epigenetic engineering approach. It has been shown that Cas9 fused to chromatin modifying enzymes can cause specific epigenetic changes [25]. This also takes advantage of the ability of Cas9 fused to chromatin‑modifying enzymes to direct site‑specific epigenetic changes. Here, using catalytically dead, dCas9-p300, we are able to directly acetylate gene promoter regions in a targeted manner. We demonstrate that this targeted approach directly reverses NRSF‑associated repressive chromatin marks at a single genomic locus without altering the broader epigenetic landscape. Epigenetic engineering of the chromatin landscape could normalize gene expression by reversing epigenetic marks that are dysregulated in gene expression. In contrast to pan-acting epigenetic based treatments that rely on small molecules, Cas9-based systems offer the potential for locus‑specific intervention with reduced off‑target effects, highlighting epigenetic editing as a promising therapeutic framework for neuropathic pain and other disorders driven by aberrant transcriptional repression.

Animal studies have shown that silencing NRSF and mimetics that interfere with its function have potential to alleviate pain symptoms [15, 16]. Based on these studies, we investigated whether overactivation of NRSF in neuronal cell could provide an in vitro cell-based model of neuropathic pain. Additionally, we investigated whether reversal of repressive epigenetic marks left by NRSF on ion channel and pain receptor genes could restore their gene expression.

Here, we showed that NRSF repression of gene expression specifically through recruitment of HDACs, representing a key mechanism underlying repression of the mu-opioid receptor (OPRM1), which is important in neuropathic pain and that using VPA to attenuate this affect could restore function of OPRM1. We show that NRSF upregulation in iPSC-derived neurons de-acetylates genes and that VPA inhibits this effect to restore gene expression. Importantly, we demonstrated that targeted epigenetic editing using dCas9-p300, enables locus‑specific acetylation to upregulate OPRM1 gene expression with minimal disruption of other target genes. Functionally, we show that loss of OPRM1 in our cells changes their response to the mu-opioid receptor agonist DAMGO, impairing the cell’s ability to modulate calcium signaling. Lastly, treatment of VPA is able to protect cells from loss of OPRM1 expression and restore mu-opioid receptor mediated calcium signaling. Taken together, these results illustrate the interplay between NRSF, epigenetic repression of OPRM1, and neuronal function, and support targeted epigenetic modulation via dCas9-p300 as a promising framework for modeling neuropathic pain in damaged neurons.

Materials and methods

Cells

DYS-0100 (ATCC ACS-1019) iPSCs were obtained from ATCC. Cells were cultured in E8 Flex medium from Thermo Fisher (A2858501) and differentiated into neural stem progenitor cells using the pluripotent stem cell (PSC) Neural Induction Kit (Thermo Fisher A1647801) per manufacturer’s instructions. Differentiation into NSCs was assayed by sox2 and nestin mRNA expression using RT-PCR. Neural stem cells (NSCs) were seeded at 3 × 10^5 cells/mL into 6-well dishes coated with poly-l-lysine and mouse laminin and differentiated into neurons using Neurobasal Medium with B-27 Supplement (Thermo Fisher 17504044) for at least 7 days. Supplemental image for differentiated cells with neuronal morphology (see supplemental file).

Oligonucleotide sequences

Gene expression was measured by RT-PCR using the primer sets found in Supplemental Fig. 3. Primer sets were designed by Primer3. Primer sets made for sgRNA targeting and ChIP-PCR were made to amplify regions near NRSEs predicted by TRED-JASPAR (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Lentiviral overexpression

Lentiviral delivery of NRSF was used to transduce neurons. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells using pHR-NRSF-CITE-GFP (Addgene #41903), psPAX2 (Addgene #12260), and pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene #8454) as the transfer, packaging, and envelope plasmids, respectively. Briefly, 20ug of transfer plasmid, 15ug of psPAX2, and 15ug of pCMV-VSV-G were transfected with 50ul of lipofectamine 3000 into HEK293T cells overnight. Medium on cells was changed after ~ 16 h and the cells were left to incubate another 48 h. After this recovery, particles released into the supernatant were collected and concentrated in 100kda cutoff centrifugal filtration tubes at 3000 g for 30–60 min (Amicon), aliquoted, and stored at − 80 C.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using the ChIP-Enzyme (Activ Motif 53009) per manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, protein-DNA binding was fixed by treating cells with formaldehyde (1%) in growth medium for up to 10 min and neutralized with glycine (1 M). Cell nuclei were isolated then disrupted using brief sonication. Enzyme was used to digest chromatin to generate DNA fragments for pulldown. Chromatin was precipitated using either anti-acetyl H3 antibody, anti-acetyl H3K9 antibody, or anti-acetyl H3K27 antibody Cell Signaling Technologies 9715, 9649, 8173. Control chromatin was pulled down using anti-H3 antibody. Chromatin crosslinking was reversed in high salt buffer for > 16 h at 65 C, purified by PCR purification kit (Qiagen 28106), then quantified using RT-PCR.

Calcium imaging

Cells were cultured in medium loaded with 2ug/mL of the calcium binding fluo4 dye (Invitrogen F14201)(488 nm/525nm) and incubated for 30 min at 37 C. Afterwards, cells were washed with artificial cerebral spinal fluid (ACSF; 119 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.3 mM MgCl2, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 26.2 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM dextrose, 10 mM HEPES, pH = 7.4 [26] ) and further incubated with 5 μm µ-opioid receptor (MOR) agonist, DAMGO (Sigma E7384). Imaging of fluorescent calcium dye was obtained with an epifluorescence microscopy and images were set against a look up table (LUT) indicating relative fluorescence intensity. Depolarization of cells was induced with 200mM KCl and images were taken at 24 frames per second (fps) to 1 min [27]. Relative changes in intracellular calcium release were determined using peak fluorescence intensity vs. baseline.

Results

NRSF overexpression downregulates OPRM1 - lentiviral O/E

We sought to determine if upregulation of NRSF in iPSC-derived neurons had the same effect on the expression of OPRM1 as seen in nerve injury. Overexpression of NRSF in our iPSC-neurons was achieved through lentiviral delivery using the pHR-CITE-NRSF-gfp transfer vector from ATCC (Supplemental Fig. 1). In addition to increasing NRSF protein expression, these cells also show reduced target OPRM1 gene expression (by RT-PCR) (Fig. 1a). Next, we determine if HDAC inhibitor had an effect on increasing target gene expression. Treating cells with VPA for up to 48 h was able to increase OPRM1 gene expression (Fig. 1b). Treatment of the cells with VPA also showed a global increase in total H3K9 acetylation and H3K27 acetylation as well as other common neuronal genes, SCN10A (Supplemental Fig. 2). In contrast, although SCN10A has potential binding sites for NRSF, pHR-NRSF overexpressing cells did not show a reduction in SCN10A expression. SCN10A control is more complex and may depend on the context. This suggests that the regulation of SCN10A is not strictly dependent on NRSF and could be regulated by other transcription factors.

Gene Expression of OPRM1 in iPSC-derived Neurons. a Transduced iPSC-derived neurons with lentiviral vector (LV), pHR-NRSF downregulates expression of OPRM1 mRNA. b Treatment of iPSC-derived neurons with 5mM VPA for up to 48 h increases OPRM1 mRNA. (n = 4) * P-value < 0.01 b/w control/untreated and NRSF overexpressed/VPA

HDAC inhibitor VPA increases histone acetylation and gene expression in iPSC-derived neurons

We investigated whether NRSF mainly represses gene expression through recruitment of HDAC via its mSin3 domain to target genes, thus we assayed the acetylation status of OPRM1. We used Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) to determine if NRSF expression affects the acetylation status of the OPRM1 target gene. We used JASPAR to identify NRSE consensus binding sequence regions within the OPRM1 promoter region to identify where acetylation could occur (Supplemental Fig. 3). ChIP was performed using a kit to isolate nuclei from NRSF overexpressing iPSC-derived neurons and the chromatin was fragmented using enzymatic digestion. Chromatin was precipitated using antibody against histone H3, H3k9ac, and H3K27ac, histone marks that are commonly associated with increased gene expression. At the OPRM1 locus determined with JASPAR, VPA was found to increase acetylation of H3K9 and H3K27 (Fig. 2). Conversely, NRSF overexpression is able to reduce H3K9 and H3K27 acetylation. Thus, HDAC inhibitors are relevant for controlling NRSF repression as well as increasing target gene expression.

Acetylation Status of OPRM1 Gene. Overexpression of NRSF in iPSC-derived neurons through lentiviral transduction shows a reduction of acetylation at H3K9 and H3K27 assayed by chromatin immunoprecipitation. Treatment of iPSC-derived neurons with VPA shows increased acetylation of H3K9 and H3K27. (n = 3) * P-value < 0.05

P300-dCas9 can be targeted to OPRM1 to increase gene expression

Next, we showed that direct acetylation of a target gene could increase its gene expression. To accomplish this, we capitalized on the genome targeting capability of the Cas9 system to direct the histone acetyltransferase, p300, to genomic regions of interest. This should acetylate the histones in the region and promote gene expression. To target gene regions of interest JASPAR was used to identify possible NRSF-binding sites within +3 kb of the transcription start site (TSS). A strong matching site was identified at ~ 2.3 kb and an sgRNA sequence was ordered to direct Cas9-p300 to this location (Supplemental Fig. 3). Using this approach, we also identified an NRSF binding site in the SCN10A gene promoter region and designed an sgRNA to direct Cas9-p300 to this location. When co-transfected into iPSCs, OPRM1-sgRNA is able to upregulate OPRM1 gene expression over 3-fold. Likewise, the SCN10A-sgRNA is able to upregulate SCN10A gene expression over 14-fold while it did not statistically upregulate the OPRM1 gene expression. In contrast, the OPRM1-sgRNA did statistically upregulate the SCN10A gene expression, however the magnitude is low (Fig. 3). This demonstrates that histone acetylation could modulate these genes’ expression and that the dCas9-p300 can accomplish this in a targeted manner, however it does not preclude potential off-target effects of acetylation.

Targeted Acetylation of OPRM1 through dCas9-p300 Expression. Overexpression of dCas9-p300 targeting the OPRM1 gene is able to increase its mRNA expression in iPSCs. Using gRNA targeting the SCN10A gene, dCas9-p300 is also able to increase SCN10A gene expression. The gRNA targeting OPRM1 did statistically significantly increase SCN10A expression, however the magnitude is low. The gRNA targeting SCN10A did not have a statistically significant effect on OPRM1 mRNA expression. (n = 3)

iPSC-derived neurons show sensitivity to DAMGO -calcium

To study NRSF regulation of OPRM1, we differentiated mature functioning opioid receptor responsive neurons from iPSCs. Using fluorescent microscopy and the Ca2 + binding dye, fluo-4, calcium release was captured in real time. These cells depolarized with a solution of KCl to induce intracellular calcium release (Fig. 4). More importantly, the calcium release (Fig. 4 and Supplemental Fig. 4) response of these cells was inhibited by the synthetic µ-opioid receptor agonist, DAMGO, suggesting that this could be a functional opioid responsive model. In NRSF overexpressing neurons, cells pre-treated with DAMGO for 10 min were unable to repress Ca2 + release induced by depolarization. This suggests that the cells overexpressing NRSF lack the OPRM1 expression needed for DAMGO binding to inhibit calcium signaling. More interestingly, we show that NRSF overexpressing neurons that have also been treated with VPA for 48 h are sensitive to pre-treatment with DAMGO and are inhibited from releasing calcium during depolarization. This suggests that VPA increased the expression of OPRM1 in the cells to thereby respond to DAMGO (Fig. 5). We also sought to determine if we could induce OPRM1 using a combination of forskolin and IBMX (FI). While the HDACi increases acetylation through inhibiting HDAC, forskolin and IBMX work through activation of CREB and its co-factor p300. As p300 is a histone acetyltransferase (HAT) we hypothesize that promoting acetylation of NRSF target genes would have a similar effect as using HDAC inhibitors. Indeed, when we treated pHR-NRSF overexpressing cells with FI the cells showed inhibited calcium release. These cells were then collected and their mRNA was isolated to measure OPRM1 expression using RT-PCR. Treating NRSF-overexpressing cells with VPA increased OPRM1 expression over 2-fold as compared with NRSF-overexpressing cells (Fig. 6). However, FI treated cells did not increase OPRM1 expression. These results suggest that HDAC inhibition is able to reverse effects of NRSF upregulation while histone acetyltransferases may not have the intended effect.

Induction of depolarization of neurons by KCl causes calcium release as measured using the fluo-4 dye and fluorescence microscopy at 10 s after induction. Pre-treating the cells with 10mM DAMGO for 5 min is able to inhibit calcium release. (n = 3) * P-value < 0.05 between T = 0 s and T = 10 s for control, with an effect size > 2.5

iPSC-Derived Neurons Show Reduced Response to DAMGO in NRSF-Overexpressing Neurons. iPSC-derived neurons transducted with empty pLV particles show full sensitivity to the opioid receptor agonist, DAMGO, and show no calcium release during depolarization with KCl. However, neurons transducted with pHR-NRSF lose their sensitivity to DAMGO and are able to release calcium in response to depolarization with KCl suggesting loss of OPRM1 function. Treatment of pHR-NRSF expressing cells with VPA is able to recover this sensitivity to DAMGO and is shown by a loss of calcium release by depolarization with KCl suggesting restoration of OPRM1 expression. Lastly, FI appears to also restore sensitivity to calcium release. (n = 3) * p-value < 0.05

Forskolin and IBMX treatment, however, did not produce an increase in OPRM1 expression. (n = 3) * P-value < 0.05

Discussion

NRSF overexpression causes widespread changes in neuronal gene expression and is likely a key mechanism underlying many neurological diseases and disorders. The relationship between NRSF and OPRM1 expression has been shown in animal models of neuropathic pain [15, 16]. Here, our data shows that this relationship occurs in iPSC-derived neurons and more importantly, is dependent on epigenetic mechanisms. NRSF mediated epigenetic changes may produce long-term changes in gene expression. This study supports treating epigenetic mechanisms as a possible remedy for attenuating the effects of NRSF. Small molecules that affect the chromatin remodeling machinery could be a viable treatment strategy. Multiple HDAC inhibitors have been used effectively in cancer treatment. Trichostatin A and SAHA (Vorinostat) is a broad HDAC inhibitor used for T cell lymphoma, and VPA has been used to treat breast cancer and small lung cell carcinoma albeit with modest effect [20, 22]. These studies with HDAC inhibitors have established some basis of safety in humans (see also Clinical Trial: NCT07166393) [23, 28]. However, given that these HDAC inhibitors often inhibit multiple classes of HDACs and even have non-HDAC inhibiting functions, the mechanisms responsible for their effectiveness are not well delineated. Here, this study shows that the use of VPA could further be expanded in neurological disease where NRSF dysregulation is a contributing factor. While VPA is used to treat epilepsy, our data suggests that this could also be used to upregulate OPRM1 levels, which plays a critical role in neuropathic pain. Furthermore, the efficacy of VPA in upregulating OPRM1 expression is directly due to the HDAC inhibitory ability of VPA. This could serve as a model for investigating HDAC inhibitors as it pertains to treating neuropathic pain as well as other neurological diseases.

Here, we show that VPA promotes neuronal gene expression of NRSF repressed target gene, OPRM1, and increases the acetylation status of the gene loci near NRSF predicted binding sites. This is evidence that VPA directly affects the epigenetic markings that NRSF uses for gene repression. The use of VPA was also able to affect cell function. Similarly, modulating OPRM1 through NRSF also has distinct effects on cell function. Modulation of calcium signaling through OPRM1 is a measure for controlling electrical output of nerves and is a good proxy for assessing for opioid effectiveness. Since VPA was able to restore OPRM1 expression and DAMGO sensitivity to NRSF overexpressing cells to affect calcium release, this suggests that VPA could be used to restore damage nerves response to opioids. While the calcium imaging data indicate that modulation of OPRM1 affects neuronal calcium signaling, additional experiments would increase the statistical power and confirm the robustness and generalizability of these findings.

Although this work shows that HDAC inhibitors could be an interesting class of new therapeutics, their broad target effects as well as their non-HDAC functions could lead to unwanted side effects. Cas9 systems have been used for gene-editing as well as regulation of gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms. Targeting of specific gene loci with Cas9 fused to chromatin modifiers has been shown to modulate gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms [25]. Additionally, their use in vitro has provided proof of principle that epigenomic modification instead of DNA editing is less prone to off-target effects and can more robustly modulate gene expression. Here, we show that dCas9 fused to the HAT p300 (dCas9-p300) modulates gene expression of the OPRM1 gene specifically by targeting the NRSE within the OPRM1 gene promoter region. This provides evidence that OPRM1 gene expression can be modulated by controlling histone acetylation. Using Cas9 systems in living adult organisms has multiple hurdles, nonetheless pursuing strategies that could modulate acetylation of the OPRM1 gene could be an effective approach to treating neuropathic pain in the future.

Upregulation of NRSF during nerve injury represses expression of OPRM1 which could be a key part of neuropathic pain. However, nerve injury induces many changes in gene expression that lead to hyper polarization. Sodium channels are key to generating action potentials in nerves and upregulation of their expression may contribute to hyperpolarization. As sodium channels are targets for gene repression by NRSF, and should be downregulated, this is inconsistent with our model of NRSF upregulation. Interestingly, overexpression of NRSF in our model consistently downregulated OPRM1 expression (Fig. 1a) but did not downregulate SCN10A (Supplemental Fig. 2). We used JASPAR to identify potential CREB1 consensus binding sequence regions within the SCN10A promoter region and found multiple potential binding regions for CREB (Supplemental Fig. 3b). This suggests that the regulation of SCN10A is not strictly dependent on NRSF and could be regulated by other transcription factors, e.g., CREB1. Differential expression of NRSF repressed genes in neuropathic pain would be a future area of investigation. Promoter enrichment analysis might offer clues as to why some sodium channel genes may remain upregulated in pain in the presence of NRSF. Many sodium channel genes are induced by CREB activity which likely mediates gene activation through acetylation via p300. This would be directly in opposition to the HDAC mediated repression of NRSF. In addition, many of the CREB binding sites of sodium channel genes are closer to key transcriptional regulatory sites in the promoter region while NRSF binding sites are within or away from the TSS.

Limitation of the study

NRSF recruits several chromatin modifiers that could act synergistically to repress the target genes. Acetylation and methylation of H3Ks are generally considered to be mutually exclusive on the same lysine residue. Study has shown that deacetylation precedes methylation [29]. Although this study focused on HDAC inhibition, it does not preclude the potential of subsequent methylation of these histone tails after deacetylation by NRSF. While mutually exclusive on the same lysine, acetylation and methylation can coexist on different lysines within the same histone tail [30]. Additionally, it has been shown in HEK293 cells that G9a and HDAC1/2 localize to overlapping chromosomal loci to lead to stable methylation of H3K9, however it was not shown that they occurred on the same lysine residue [31].

The mechanisms involved in neuropathic pain are complex. The molecular mechanisms behind the progression of neuropathic pain is multifaceted, and involves proinflammatory cytokines and glial activation, changes in ion channel expression, dysregulation of genes involved in mediating excitation of peripheral sensory nerves, including loss of inhibitory currents that occurs as a result of dysfunctional GABAergic interneurons, and control of the analgesia [32, 33]. Therefore, the results of this study on the regulation of OPRM1 by NRSF may not fully translate to nerve injury models or clinical neuropathic pain.

Opioids are not the first-line drug for treating neuropathic pain due to its high chance of tolerance and addiction and reduced long-term efficacy. There have been small trials structured as interventional trials to evaluate valproate for neuropathic pain (Clinical Trial NCT00221637) with some trials demonstrating positive while others finding mixed results with [34, 35] VPA reducing pain in diabetic neuropathy and peripheral neuropathic pain. Nonetheless VPA, unlike opioids, are not at risk of addiction or increase tolerance, which could contribute to their potential efficacy. However, there are concerns with adverse side effects with VPA, such as hepatotoxicity, teratogenicity (neural tube defects), thrombocytopenia [36]. In addition to the side effects, HDAC inhibitors have limited specificity amongst targets. Thus, the advantages of dCas9-p300 over HDAC inhibitors are that (1) it can cause specific epigenetic changes and be directed to locations of the genome in a sequence specific manner, and (2) is not pan-acting so it could offer a targeted approach with minimal unintended side effects. This type of epigenetic engineering of the chromatin landscape could normalize gene expression and restore homeostasis to diseased cells and address the molecular basis of many types of diseases. However, there could be off-target acetylation effects. p300/CBP is a promiscuous histone acetyltransferase, acetylating many histone and non‑histone proteins. When fused to dCas9, its catalytic domain remains active, meaning acetylation can occur not only at the intended sgRNA‑targeted genomic locus but also on histones in the surrounding chromatin regions. This was observed with the targeting of SCN10A gene by p300-OPRM1 sgRNA, albeit the magnitude was low. Another challenge is in the delivery of the dCas9-p300 system. However, with the engineering of exosomes [37], it could provide an approach to overcome the large size and to efficiently package the plasmids.

A limitation of the ChIP experiments is the absence of a nonspecific IgG negative control. While appropriate positive controls such as actively transcribed genomic regions (targeting H3K9ac and H3K27ac) or total histone H3 can help confirm antibody performance, inclusion of an IgG control would have allowed assessment of nonspecific binding and background signal. Without this control, confidence in the specificity of the observed H3K9ac and H3K27ac enrichment is reduced.

In summary, this study demonstrates a simple mechanism whereby NRSF modulates OPRM1 expression through histone acetylation by HDACs. VPA and NRSF can specifically regulate OPRM1 directly through epigenetic mechanisms. However, both NRSF and HDAC inhibitors (e.g., VPA) have very broad effects affecting the expression of many hundreds to thousands of genes simultaneously, future studies of high throughput whole genome analyses to track the global gene expression changes that NRSF and VPA exert may provide a much more comprehensive understanding of the molecular biology behind how neurological disease occurs at the gene expression level and could also help to better understand how HDAC inhibitors impart their effect. In contrast, the dCas9-p300 system offers key advantages over HDAC inhibitors by enabling more specific epigenetic changes through sequence-guided targeting, and avoiding the broad, pan-acting effects of conventional inhibitors. However, dCas9-p300 may still cause off-target acetylation, modifying nearby histones or other proteins beyond the intended locus. Future approaches to make dCas9‑p300 less promiscuous, could include strategies focus on reducing p300’s intrinsic substrate range by truncating or engineering the catalytic core and tightening Cas9 targeting [38], or restricting or engineering HAT domain with RNA scaffold to limit the enzymatic activity [39]. This could provide a general therapeutic approach to treating diseases beyond neurological diseases.
