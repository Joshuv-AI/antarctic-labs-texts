Empowering youth: a school-based digital awareness campaign for sustainable Nipah virus prevention in Bangladesh

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Improved student understanding of Nipah transmission risks and prevention practices.•Students demonstrated strong retention of prevention knowledge after intervention.•School-based interventions are practical, scalable, and effective behavior change.Keywords: Nipah virus, School awareness, Bangladesh

Introduction

Infectious disease prevention in resource-poor settings often relies on educational institute-based intervention due to their low cost and scalability [1]. School-based awareness campaigns have proven to be an effective intervention in improving health literacy and promoting preventive behaviors in the context of emerging infectious diseases [2]. Worldwide, such awareness approach demonstrated substantial success in addressing diseases such as tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS, influenza, and more recently COVID-19 and Ebola. Youth-centered health education programs, especially in school settingsare particularly impactful because they promote long-term health behaviors by reaching individuals during their formative cognitive years [3,4].

Despite various public health campaigns led by government and non-government initiatives to address infectious diseases in Bangladesh, the use of digital approaches for health awareness remains limiteed. This gap is particilarly evident among school going children and adolescents, especially in the context of emerging zoonotic infections such as the Nipah virus (NiV).

NiV infection is an emerging zoonosis with no approved vaccines or treatments, posing a recurring threat to global public health [5,6]. Since 2001, several human NiV outbreaks were identified between November to April in Bangladesh, with a case fatality rate of 71% [[7], [8], [9]]. The symptoms of persons infected with NiV include fever, altered mental status, unconsciousness, headache, weakness, cough, respiratory difficulty, vomiting, diarrhea, and convulsion [7]. The virus is primarily transmitted through the consumption of raw date palm sap contaminated by infected fruit bat (Pteropus Giganteus) saliva or urine [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Close contact with an infected individual is also identified as another risk factor for NiV transmission [15].

Children in Bangladesh are mostly vulnerable to NiV due to their multiple risk behaviors, including drinking raw date palm sap, lack of awareness, tree climbing, and prolonged social interactions [16,17]. Up to 2021, 42% (135 of 322) of NiV cases in Bangladesh were reported among children aged 17 years or younger, that typically corresponds to students up to higher secondary level [7]. Despite this vulnerability, school-based awareness programs addressing NiV has not been initiated in Bangladesh. Although previous public education initiatives, such as community meetings, local announcements, posters and television messages had been implemented and demonstrated their initial success in reducing risky behaviors among adults. However, they have had limited reach and impact among school-aged children [[18], [19], [20]].

Recognizing this critical gap Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR) and icddr,b launched a school-based Nipah Awareness Program in the NiV-infected districts in Bangladesh. The intervention uses digital storytelling to provide knowledge about NiV transmission, symptoms, and prevention to the students in primary, secondary, and higher secondary levels. This interactive school-based awareness approach improved the students’ understanding of the urgency around NiV and encouraged the adoption of lasting preventive behavior [19,21]. In addition, two-way interaction between facilitators and students deepened comprehension and supported positive health practices [21,22].

In line with the World Health Organization R&D roadmap, prevention remains the only feasible strategy due to the absence of vaccines or therapeutics. In this context, effective communication and health education are the key components of NiV prevention and control strategies [19,21]. Raw date palm sap consumption, a culturally significant practice poses ongoing challenges for control [23]. School-aged students were targeted as quick learners who can effectively spread awareness, helping reduce raw date palm sap consumption and ultimately prevent NiV infection. This school campaign was designed to improve the students’ knowledge of NiV and promote sustained behavioral change. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of a school-based digital campaign in improving awareness and understanding of NiV among students.

Methods

School campaign

Since 2006, the icddr,b has been implementing the Nipah surveillance activity, in collaboration with the IEDCR, Bangladesh and technical support from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Atlanta, GA, USA [7]. As a part of the surveillance, we initiated Nipah School Campaign for two consecutive years in 2023-2024 and 2024-2025, especially before starting the Nipah season. We analyzed the preceding 10 years of Nipah surveillance data to identify the most vulnerable areas with Nipah-endemic zones in Bangladesh. In collaboration with the local administration, educational authorities, and health officials, we identified the largest educational institutions near the NiV-affected households based on the number of students to maximize student coverage in this school campaign. A total 100 educational institutions were identified and these identified institutions included primary, secondary, higher secondary, and Madrasha (religion-based institutions equivalent to secondary and higher secondary) level educational institutions.

In 2023-2024, we launched the school campaign as pilot programs in 26 randomly selected educational institutions from the identified 100 largest educational institutions across 14 high-risk Upazilas in eight districts. Building on the lessons and the foundation from the pilot phase, we expanded in the subsequent year in 100 educational institutions (including previously selected 26 educational institutions) covering 22 Upazilas across 14 districts (Figure 1).

Spatial distribution of selected educational institutions.

The awareness sessions were structured into three interactive segments. The first segment used a digital storytelling process, including a pictorial presentation and video demonstration to introduce participants to date palm trees, fruit bats, raw sap contamination, and community practices related to raw sap consumption. Students actively participated in a story-building exercise, where they illustrated how NiV spreads in their communities and discussed preventive measures. In the second segment, a question-and-answer session allowed students to clarify doubts and reinforce their understanding. At the end, we distributed informative leaflets to encourage students to share their learning with their families and respective communities.

Survey implementation

With a three months interval, we conducted baseline and follow-up surveys from November 2024 to April 2025 in 14 randomly selected educational institutions based on types and Nipah outbreak history [24]. The baseline survey was conducted immediately before the awareness program. A two-stage random cluster sampling method was implemented to select institutions and the students from class six and above. To avoid bias, we excluded the educational institutions from the pilot phase. Data were collected through a structured questionnaire covering four thematic areas: basic information on Nipah, treatment and testing facilities, virus transmission, and prevention measures. A total of 46 identical questions were included to assess the depth of the knowledge.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated based on the assumed value of several parameters, including a small effect size of 0.2, significance level of 0.05, statistical power of 90%and correlation between repeated measures of 0.5. To account for cluster sampling, a design effect of 1.5 was considered, along with an expected 6% non-response rate. Based on these parameters, the required sample size was 360 students for the baseline and follow-up surveys.

Data analysis

We summarized the demographic characteristics and outbreak history in the study area using descriptive statistics. We used a scoring system where each correct answer received one point, incorrect answers zero, resulting in a potential total score range from 0 to 46. The difference between baseline and follow-up knowledge regarding each of the considered indicators was assessed using the McNemar test, and the Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to assess the difference in the overall knowledge score between baseline and follow-up surveys. In addition, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess the difference in the overall knowledge score. Absolute change in knowledge for each indicator was calculated as the difference in paired proportions between follow-up and baseline. In addition, 95% confidence interval (CI) for the absolute change was estimated using Wald standard errors based on the number of discordant pairs. The data were analyzed using Statistical Programming Language R (version-4.3.2).

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics and Nipah outbreak history

We enrolled a total of 385 students, ranging from sixth grade to higher secondary level, for the baseline and follow-up survey activities. Among the participants, 46% (176 of 385) were male. Notably, 33% (127 of 385) of the students were in the seventh grade. Regarding father’s occupation, 35% (135 of 385) were engaged in farming. The median monthly household income among participants was Bangladeshi Taka (BDT) 15,000 (interquartile range [IQR]: 10,000-24,000). Family members or relatives of 12% of the students were directly involved in date palm sap harvesting during the season. In addition, around 2% of the students observed patients with NiV infection in their surroundings (Table 1).

Baseline knowledge about NiV

Students from general schools exhibited significantly higher overall knowledge scores than those attending religious institutions (P <0.001). A positive association was also observed between family income and knowledge level, with students from households earning more than BDT 20,000 per month demonstrating significantly higher scores than those from lower-income families (P = 0.036 and P = 0.0034, respectively). Furthermore, students residing in regions that had experienced an outbreak within the past 5 years showed notably higher knowledge scores than those from non-affected areas (P <0.001)(Figure 2).

Effectiveness of intervention

Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5 clearly illustrate the increase in knowledge levels after the intervention. Before the intervention, most students lacked basic knowledge of NiV, with 83% unaware of the country of the first case and 77-86% unaware of outbreak timelines in Malaysia (1998) and Bangladesh (2001). After the school-based awareness program, 73% correctly identified Malaysia and 57-58% recalled outbreak years in Bangladesh. Knowledge of diagnostics and treatment also improved: 67% correctly stated no specific medicine exists and 63% identified tertiary care hospitals as designated testing facilities. We found a significant improvement in overall knowledge among students (P <0.001) (Figure 3).

At the baseline survey, only 3-28% students correctly identified the symptoms of NiV. However, after the intervention, we found remarkable improvement in clinical awareness: fever (96%), headache (94%), difficulty breathing (83%), seizures (90%), muscle pain (51%), and altered mental status (59%) (Figure 4).

Understanding of natural reservoirs for NiV showed significant gains from 45% to 99% among students. With recognition of bats increasing from 45% at baseline to 99% at follow-up. Knowledge about other animals also improved significantly (P <0.005), including pigs (11% to 45%), monkeys (10% to 36%), cows (12% to 52%), and birds (7% to 20%), although awareness regarding squirrels remained unchanged. Substantial improvement was also observed in the students’ understanding of transmission pathways (Table 2).

Awareness of raw date palm sap consumption as a risk behavior increased from 31% to 97%, whereas knowledge about other transmission routes, such as eating bat-bitten fruits (31-96%), contact with infected animals (18-71%), exposure to body fluids from infected individuals (12-72%), and respiratory droplets (16-67%), also improved significantly (P <0.001). Similarly, knowledge of preventive measures rose sharply, with understanding of avoiding raw sap consumption increasing from 36% to 95% and maintaining social distance from 27% to 80%. Awareness of avoiding half-eaten fruits (22-95%), using masks (26-71%), and handwashing after exposure (21-86%) also improved notably (Table 2).

We observed a significant and consistent increase in knowledge scores, rising from a median of 6 (IQR: 4-15) at baseline to 28 (IQR: 24-31) at follow-up, with the change being statistically significant (P <0.005) (Figure 5).

Family members’ knowledge about NiV was 21% at baseline (81 of 385) and 77% at follow-up (295 of 385) (P-value <0.001).

Retention of NiV knowledge

Knowledge was significantly more persistent among secondary and higher-level students than junior-level students. Students from religion-based institutions (Madrasha) showed significant improvement compared with students from general schools after the intervention because the students in Madrasha had a significantly lower level of knowledge at baseline (Figure 6).

Discussion

A digital storytelling intervention was implemented and evaluated to observe the changes of knowledge and awareness among students from the high-risk communities in Bangladesh. Significant baseline difference emerged between students from general and religion-based schools, likely reflecting differences in access to digital tools and public health information [[25], [26], [27]]. Government and non-governmental organizations usually focus on general school-based intervention programs and teacher training, which contribute to the knowledge gap [25]. Socioeconomic disparities influence access to health information and educational resources in Bangladesh [28,29]. Families with higher income levels are generally better positioned to afford educational materials; access electronic devices; and maintain consistent exposure to information through television, the internet, and social media platforms [30,31]. School-based health education programs through digital storytelling are an inclusive and accessible platform for students from diverse educational settings and all socioeconomic groups [25,32]. In particular, messages focusing on natural reservoirs, transmission pathways, and prevention may have contributed to greater improvements in these knowledge domains. We categorized NiV-affected areas based on outbreak recency (≤5 years) and historical occurrences since 2001 and found significantly higher knowledge levels among students in areas with more recent outbreaks. Recent public health emergencies are known to enhance awareness and knowledge retention, likely due to intensified outbreak response activities, such as health education, risk communication, community engagement, and increased media coverage led by government and partner organizations [[33], [34], [35]]. In contrast, areas with more distant outbreak histories may experience declining awareness in the absence of sustained reinforcement through local health initiatives. Implementation of digital health awareness programs highlights the importance of periodic and continuous education in high-risk and previously affected regions. Integrating Nipah-related awareness into routine school curricula, particularly in historically affected regions, may help sustain a high level of preparedness and reduce the risk of future outbreaks.

We observed a significant improvement from baseline to follow-up in all groups and categories of assessment, underlining the effectiveness of this educational strategy. Performance was comparable between the two stages of evaluation for almost all the variables that were examined. We found only small knowledge differences between recent outbreak-based geographical areas, especially about NiV transmission and its prevention.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first aiming to assess Nipah-related knowledge in educational institutions in northwestern and central western areas of Bangladesh. Multiple prospective studies in different contexts worldwide showed significant improvement in infectious disease transmission and prevention-related knowledge among students (age 9-14 years) after a specific interval of intervention implementation [3,36]. Overall, these results strengthen the hypothesis of the effectiveness of school-based digital awareness campaign regarding NiV awareness [37].

Improved knowledge regarding the basic epidemiologic information, availability of testing facilities, and vaccine or treatment facilities can significantly develop a clearer understanding of its public health importance and the need of preventive behaviors [38,39]. Knowledge about the unavailability of specific antiviral treatment or vaccines reinforces the early detection, risk avoidance, and timely reporting [21]. Furthermore, awareness related to different clinical features of NiV infection and Nipah diagnostic availability, empowering students and their families to seek appropriate care during a suspected outbreak, potentially reducing delays in diagnosis in limiting further transmission [21]. Cognitive load due to excessive information, limited personal relevance, and absence of repeated reinforcement student may face challenges in memorizing certain knowledge domains, specifically, in NiV symptoms [40,41].

Increasing awareness of NiV risk factors among students is needed to reduce the risk of spillover and person-to-person transmission in high-risk communities [42]. We observed a significant improvement in knowledge of raw date palm sap consumption, contact with an infected individual or bat, and eating half-eaten fruits, which are the most prevalent risk factors found for NiV infection in Bangladesh.

Mask use during patient handling and hand washing practice, especially after contact with sick individuals or animals, is essential for successful control of Nipah outbreaks.

The active role of students in recognizing the risk behaviors, prevention measures, and clinical features highlights a critical pathway for enhancing community-level awareness through spreading knowledge to their family members and neighbors [43]. This kind of dissemination is a cost-effective promotion among family members who may have limited access to formal health education. This peer-to-family knowledge transfer can bridge information gaps, correct misconceptions, and contribute to long-term behavioral change that supports outbreak control in in resource-poor settings [43].

In Bangladesh’s expanding digital education environment, school-based digital awareness has shown to be an effective tool in sustaining knowledge about the NiV [44]. With the previous exposure to digital learning, older students are better equipped to understand complex health narratives; link them to real-life contexts; and retain key messages about disease transmission, symptoms, and prevention [[45], [46], [47]]. In contrast, junior-level students require more simplified content and repetition to retain knowledge.

Key strengths of this study include its prospective design with a 3-month follow-up period, representation of schools from Nipah-endemic regions of Bangladesh, inclusion of participants across various educational levels, and the involvement of students from religion-based and general education institutions.

Our survey also has several limitations. First, the study population may not be representative of all students in Nipah-endemic zones of Bangladesh. Moreover, in Bangladesh, a census difference is usually observed between students from general education schools and religion-based institutions. Second, due to budget limitations, we were not able to include a control group to observe the effectiveness of the intervention. Third, the intervention was implemented in November, just before the start of the Nipah season and during a period when many students typically take unofficial preparation leave for their annual exams. As a result, not all targeted students were able to attend the storytelling sessions and receive the educational materials. Forth, reliance on self-reported knowledge may have been influenced by peer interaction and recall bias. Although knowledge retention was observed, this study did not assess actual behaviors or health outcomes. The impact of the intervention should ideally be assessed in real-life settings, such as observing behaviors related to raw date palm sap consumption during the harvesting season and examining whether improved knowledge translates into increased recognition of Nipah-like symptoms and timely referral to nearby Nipah surveillance hospitals. Future efforts should consider repeating the survey to assess long-term information retention and identify opportunities to enhance the effectiveness of the school’s awareness campaign.

In conclusion, the school-based awareness campaign on NiV contributed significantly in knowledge improvement and persisted after three months, regardless of the type of educational institute, level of education, sex, and geographical area.

Such interventions targeting this age group have the potential to reduce the Nipah burden in Bangladesh by increasing knowledge, promoting preventive behaviors, and facilitating the spread of accurate information among family members and the wider community. Potential integration of similar digital awareness modules into national school curricula could promote sustainable behavioral change and epidemic preparedness.
