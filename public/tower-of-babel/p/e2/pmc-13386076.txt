You Can’t Clamp What You Can’t Find
Authors: Mark Bowyer, Pamela B Andreatta, Matthew Bradley
Published: 2026

This article by Swain et al asks what military clinicians actually think they are learning from the use of live tissue training (LTT), instead of whether they ‘like’ them.1 The themes that emerged—psychomotor learning, affective response, team dynamics, fidelity, and ethical justification—are usually overlooked. That said, the discussion misses an important consideration that must not be overlooked.

The participants talk extensively about ‘warm blood’, active hemorrhage, and the stress of operating on live tissue.1 In systematic reviews, the impact of bleeding, physiologic realism, the urgent pressure to act, and the ‘emotional impact’ of caring for a live ‘patient’ are routinely cited as advantages of using LTT.2 3 Unquestionably, these are important attributes of trauma training. However, vascular hemorrhage control is not primarily a physiology problem. It is an anatomy problem. You cannot clamp what you cannot find, and finding it requires anatomical dissection knowledge that no amount of physiologic realism can substitute for.

For the injuries that kill people in combat—injuries to the junctional and truncal areas—the gap is not managing bleeding once you are there.4 The gap is getting there in the first place, and a pig does not teach you that. Fresh cadaver training is not perfect either, but it gives you human anatomy. Performing clinically relevant exposures repeatedly, until the anatomy is automatic, is arguably more relevant preparation for operative trauma than watching a pig bleed.4 5

None of this is an argument against LTT. It does things cadavers cannot. The point is that the two modalities are not competing for the same educational space, and the study would be stronger if it engaged more directly with what each one actually teaches. Physiologic realism and anatomic fidelity are different things, and military surgeons preparing for deployment probably need both, just not necessarily from the same model. No simulation training is perfect, so the question should be which modality is best for achieving the goals and objectives of the educational instruction.

Future work should stop asking ‘is LTT beneficial’ and start asking which specific skills degrade fastest, which are hardest to acquire without live tissue, and whether cadaveric anatomy training reduces the anatomic knowledge gap that seems to be the real limiting factor in operative vascular control. To ensure that training, irrespective of the modality, becomes a ‘finishing school’, robust assessment of procedural skills should be the norm.5 That is harder to do but would actually move the field.
