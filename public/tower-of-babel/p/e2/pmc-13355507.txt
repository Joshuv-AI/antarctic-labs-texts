Smart functionalized cartilage repair microspheres: Concepts and applications
Authors: Fan Bai, Tao Yuan, Zhen Geng, Yuye Liao, Yuliang Dai, Hong Ma, Zhiming Tu, Yawei Li, Xiaocheng Wang, Jiacan Su, Bing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCartilage has a limited intrinsic capacity for self-repair, causing injuries to progress into degenerative disorders. Current conservative and surgical treatments offer only partial benefits and are often hindered by poor tissue quality, limited durability, and procedural invasiveness. Functionalized microspheres have recently gained attention in cartilage repair due to their biocompatibility, injectability, structural versatility, and ability to achieve controlled therapeutic delivery. Evolving from passive carriers to smart, bioactive platforms, these microspheres can modulate the local regenerative microenvironment through programmable release, structural engineering, and responsive functionalities. This review summarizes the structure and degeneration of articular cartilage and the cartilaginous endplate, introduces major microsphere fabrication techniques and biomaterial choices, and highlights emerging strategies for functionalization. We further discuss structural innovations that enhance therapeutic outcomes and examine the translational potential of microspheres as precise and multifunctional smart microtissues for personalized cartilage regeneration.Keywords: Functionalized microspheres, Cartilage repair, Cartilaginous endplate, Tissue engineering, Regenerative medicine

Introduction

Cartilage tissue is a vital connective tissue in the human body, primarily located in the joints of the extremities, intervertebral discs, bronchi, and the structural framework of the ear. Fibrocartilage is predominantly found in intervertebral discs, articular discs, and the pubic symphysis, whereas elastic cartilage forms the auricle and epiglottis. Hyaline cartilage represents the main cartilage type in limb joints, playing critical roles in weight-bearing, joint lubrication, and smooth limb movement [[1], [2], [3]]. Cartilage injury often presents as a partial-thickness defect, accompanied by extracellular matrix degradation and impaired chondrocyte metabolism and function [4]. Due to its low cellularity, avascular nature, and limited lymphatic support, cartilage exhibits poor intrinsic regenerative capacity, making self-repair following injury extremely limited [5]. Consequently, effective treatment of cartilage defects remains a significant clinical challenge. Untreated cartilage injuries can result in progressive functional impairment. Articular cartilage (AC) lesions, the most common form of cartilage injury, are typically caused by trauma or degenerative changes. Due to the limited self-repair capacity of AC, these lesions may eventually progress to osteoarthritis (OA), a chronic joint disorder characterized by persistent pain, impaired function, and reduced quality of life [6,7]. Moreover, degeneration or injury of the cartilaginous endplate (CEP) disrupts mechanical load transmission and nutrient transport between vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs, accelerating disc degeneration [8].

Current management of cartilage injuries encompasses both conservative and surgical strategies. Conservative approaches primarily aim to alleviate symptoms such as pain and inflammation via pharmacological interventions but do not address the underlying tissue damage [9,10]. Commonly used medications include selective cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitors, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and intra-articular corticosteroid injections [11,12]. Surgical options range from arthroscopic debridement and microfracture to autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI), osteochondral grafting, and, in advanced cases, total joint replacement [13,14]. Although these approaches provide certain therapeutic benefits, they exhibit notable limitations. For example, while arthroscopic debridement can relieve pain, there is insufficient evidence supporting its capacity to promote cartilage regeneration [2]. Microfracture stimulates repair tissue formation via bone marrow-derived cells; however, the resulting tissue is predominantly fibrocartilage, which has inferior biomechanical properties compared to native hyaline cartilage and is generally suitable only for small lesions [[15], [16], [17]]. ACI also faces challenges, including fibrocartilage formation due to chondrocyte dedifferentiation during isolation, resulting in reduced type II collagen expression and diminished activity of cartilage-specific transcription factors relative to native tissue [18,19].

In recent years, advances in tissue engineering (TE) have introduced novel strategies for cartilage repair. This interdisciplinary field aims to reconstruct, restore, and potentially replace damaged tissues through the combined use of cells, scaffolds, and bioactive molecules [[20], [21], [22]]. Among various biomaterials, injectable microspheres have garnered increasing attention in basic and preclinical studies owing to their excellent biocompatibility, structural stability, and controlled release capabilities. Compared with conventional hydrogels and three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds, microspheres offer several distinct advantages. Their uniform particle size and well-defined morphology enable minimally invasive delivery through injection while ensuring reproducible performance. In addition, microspheres can provide a favorable microenvironment that supports cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration, thereby facilitating tissue regeneration and repair. Furthermore, functionalized microspheres with specialized architectures (e.g., macroporous or core-shell structures) can serve as efficient carriers for the controlled and sustained delivery of bioactive molecules. These properties make them particularly attractive for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine applications [23,24].

Following a concise overview of microsphere fabrication techniques and commonly used biomaterials, this review systematically summarizes recent advances in functionalized microsphere-based strategies for cartilage repair (Fig. 1). Previous reviews have primarily focused on specific biomaterials, individual fabrication methods, or isolated delivery approaches. In contrast, the present review is structured around the complementary concepts of “functionalization” and “systematization”. Here, functionalization refers to the rational engineering of microspheres to endow them with specific biological, physicochemical, structural, or stimuli-responsive properties. Representative functions include controlled drug delivery, sustained release of bioactive molecules, cell encapsulation and delivery, immunomodulation, metabolic regulation, incorporation of physical cues, and targeted therapeutic delivery. Systematization, in turn, emphasizes the integration of these individual functions into comprehensive therapeutic platforms tailored to distinct cartilage repair scenarios, including focal cartilage defect regeneration, osteoarthritis treatment, osteochondral tissue repair, and cartilaginous endplate regeneration.

Schematic overview of functionalized microsphere applications in cartilage repair.

To avoid the overly broad use of the term “smart,” this review adopts a hierarchical framework for classifying functionalized microsphere systems. At the foundational level, passive delivery systems primarily serve as carriers for the sustained release and local retention of therapeutic agents. Building upon this foundation, functionalized delivery systems incorporate bioactive cargos, surface ligands, structural modifications, or cell loading capabilities to actively regulate cellular behavior and the regenerative microenvironment. A further level of sophistication is represented by stimuli-responsive systems, which can sense pathological cues, such as acidic pH, reactive oxygen species (ROS), matrix degrading-enzymes, inflammatory mediators, temperature fluctuations, or abnormal mechanical stimuli, and translate these signals into controlled therapeutic responses. At the highest level, feedback-regulated systems are designed to dynamically couple microenvironment sensing with therapeutic output, enabling adaptive and self-regulated intervention. Within this framework, microspheres are viewed not merely as passive delivery vehicles but as increasingly intelligent, modular, and multifunctional regenerative platforms. This review therefore highlights the progressive evolution of microsphere-based technologies while critically discussing their design principles, biological safety, translational challenges, and future clinical potential.

Articular cartilage and cartilaginous endplate: Structure, function, and degeneration pathophysiology

Cartilage is a specialized connective tissue characterized by its avascular, aneural, and alymphatic nature. It is widely distributed in synovial joints, the spine, ribs, external ear, nasal cavity, respiratory tract, and growth plates of children and adolescents [25,26]. Cartilage primarily comprises chondrocytes sparsely distributed within a rich extracellular matrix (ECM), which they synthesize and maintain [27]. Within this highly hydrated collagen fiber network, chondrocytes maintain tissue structure by producing and assembling the ECM, a process regulated by non-collagenous proteins, proteoglycans, and glycoproteins [25]. The resulting ECM forms a dynamic regulatory network that supports chondrocyte biological activity and plays a central role in tissue homeostasis [28,29]. Based on differences in histological structure, ECM composition, and functional properties, cartilage is classified into three types: hyaline, fibro-, and elastic cartilage. These types differ markedly in collagen and proteoglycan types, distribution, and abundance, thereby determining their unique morphological and biomechanical characteristics [30,31].

Hyaline cartilage, the most abundant type in the human body, has a translucent, glass-like appearance and is primarily located on articular surfaces of synovial joints, as well as in the ribs, nasal cavity, trachea, bronchi, larynx, and growth plates [25]. Among these, AC, a highly specialized form of hyaline cartilage located at the epiphyseal ends of synovial joints, plays a vital role in joint biomechanics despite its limited thickness [32]. Structurally, it comprises sparsely distributed chondrocytes embedded in an ECM consisting of ∼70% water and 30% organic components, including collagen fibers and proteoglycans [33]. Type II collagen, the main fibrous ECM component, provides tensile strength [34,35], whereas aggrecan, the second major component, works together with collagen to confer the matrix's remarkable water-retention capacity [36]. The collagen network resists swelling pressure from negatively charged proteoglycans via the Benninghoff arcade structure, enabling cartilage to withstand compressive and shear forces [37]. Its porous, elastic nature facilitates effective load distribution and friction reduction, ensuring smooth, pain-free joint movement [38].

Based on composition, structure, and cellular morphology, AC is zonally organized into four distinct layers: superficial, transitional, radial (deep), and calcified, from the articular surface toward the subchondral bone (Fig. 2a) [33,37,40]. However, owing to its avascular and aneural nature, AC exhibits limited regenerative capacity, relying solely on synovial fluid diffusion for metabolic maintenance [29,41]. Although chondrocytes can synthesize and maintain the ECM, their low density (∼4.2 × 106 cells/cm3) [37] and the post-maturational stability of the collagenous framework severely limit repair following injury [42,43]. Consequently, AC damage often results in persistent pain, impaired mobility, and a high risk of OA progression [44].

Chondrocyte function and fate are precisely regulated by multiple interconnected signaling pathways, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), Wnt/β-catenin, nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), and Hippo/Yes-associated protein (YAP) pathways. Dysregulation of these networks leads to chondrocyte dysfunction, accelerating OA onset and progression [45,46]. A central mechanism underlying OA is the age related disruption of cartilage metabolic homeostasis. Aberrant gene and protein expression promote chondrocyte senescence, impaired differentiation, abnormal proliferation, and apoptosis, thereby disturbing cartilage homeostasis. Consequently, cartilage metabolism gradually shifts from an anabolic state to a catabolic state, leading to progressive extracellular matrix degradation and cartilage degeneration. [[47], [48], [49]]. During this transition, overactivation of degradative enzymes (e.g., ADAMTS-4/5 and MMP-13) drives progressive ECM degradation [50]. Concurrently, cellular senescence, oxidative stress, impaired autophagy, synovial inflammation, and pro-inflammatory cytokines synergistically compromise tissue integrity and exacerbate matrix breakdown [[51], [52], [53], [54]]. These factors synergistically promote sustained AC destruction and OA progression.

The CEP is located at the superior and inferior boundaries of the intervertebral disc (IVD), where it forms interfaces with the bony endplate (BEP), nucleus pulposus (NP), and annulus fibrosus (AF) (Fig. 2b) [55,56]. As a thin layer of hyaline cartilage, the CEP consists primarily of type II collagen, proteoglycans such as aggrecan, and a high-water content [57,58]. Despite sharing compositional similarities with articular cartilage, the CEP exhibits a distinct microarchitecture. Unlike articular cartilage, it lacks a typical columnar cellular arrangement, and its chondrocyte-like cells are generally elongated and aligned parallel to the vertebral body along collagen fibers [59,60]. The BEP-CEP interface is relatively loose, whereas the CEP-NP/AF interface is more structurally integrated, with annular fibers and collagenous components partially extending into the CEP. This structural continuity contributes to the transmission of tensile and shear forces and helps maintain the mechanical integrity of the disc endplate unit [39,61].

In addition to its structural role, the CEP serves as the principal pathway for nutrient transport and metabolic exchange within the IVD. Because the mature IVD is largely avascular, oxygen, glucose, and other small molecules diffuse primarily from vertebral capillaries through the CEP into the NP, whereas metabolic waste products are removed in the opposite direction [62,63]. Consequently, CEP permeability, porosity, extracellular matrix organization, and calcification status are critical determinants of the disc nutritional microenvironment. Reduced CEP permeability restricts nutrient transport while impairing waste clearance, resulting in lactate accumulation and local acidification. These alterations compromise cellular homeostasis, promote apoptosis and matrix degradation, and ultimately accelerate NP degeneration [39,64].

Increasing evidence identifies CEP degeneration as both an initiating factor and a pathological driver of intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) [65]. Degenerative changes, including calcification, pore occlusion, matrix stiffening, inflammatory activation, and microstructural disruption, impair nutrient diffusion and mechanical buffering capacity, thereby accelerating extracellular matrix breakdown and loss of NP homeostasis [66,67]. Furthermore, CEP injury and inflammation are closely associated with Modic changes in adjacent vertebral bodies, highlighting the important role of the CEP in mediating pathological interactions between the disc and vertebral endplate [68]. Therefore, preservation of CEP structure and restoration of its transport function are considered key strategies for maintaining IVD homeostasis.

Taken together, although articular cartilage and the CEP share several characteristics, including avascularity, a type II collagen rich extracellular matrix, and limited intrinsic regenerative capacity, the CEP performs unique functions in nutrient transport, disc metabolism, and vertebral disc integration. Accordingly, CEP degeneration should be regarded as a distinct pathological process rather than merely an extension of articular cartilage degeneration. Hallmarks of this process include impaired permeability, calcification, altered mechanical buffering, and disruption of NP-CEP crosstalk. These unique pathological features provide the biological basis for discussing CEP targeted regenerative strategies as a separate translational category in the following section.

Preparation techniques and biomaterial platforms for microspheres

Microspheres with diameters under 20 μm are generally unsuitable as cell carriers because the dimensions of adherent, spread cells typically exceed this threshold [69]. Conversely, microspheres >200 μm in diameter challenge injection through conventional needles (typically 18-gauge or larger). Therefore, microspheres intended for injectable tissue engineering applications should ideally fall within the size range of 20-200 μm [23]. Fabrication strategies for microspheres

Microfluidic technologies

Microfluidics is an emerging and rapidly evolving technique widely applied in microsphere fabrication. It enables the production of highly spherical, uniformly sized, and monodisperse microspheres by precisely controlling two or more immiscible fluid microvolumes in microchannels (Fig. 3a) [104,108,109]. Key advantages include precise diameter control, rapid fabrication, generation of complex tunable microstructures, reduced injection invasiveness, enhanced specific surface area, and improved monodispersity [[110], [111], [112]]. However, challenges persist, including scalability, industrialization, raw material biocompatibility, safety concerns, and barriers to translational research and commercialization [113,114].

Emulsion-based fabrication

The emulsion method is a widely used technique for microsphere preparation. It forms uniform spherical particles through nucleation, growth, polymerization, and aggregation in discrete droplets of a stable emulsion between immiscible solvents stabilized by surfactants (Fig. 3b) [105,115,116]. Based on internal phase curing mechanisms, it is classified into emulsion solvent evaporation, emulsion polymerization, and emulsion cross-linking. Advantages include low cost, consistent morphology, and encapsulation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic agents. Nevertheless, residual oil phases, cross-linkers, or surfactants may exhibit cytotoxic effects, necessitating careful optimization and process control [111,117,118].

Spray-drying techniques

The spray drying method generates microsphere powders by atomizing a solution, emulsion, or suspension containing polymers and bioactive components into a stream of hot air (Fig. 3c) [106,119]. This simple, efficient technique yields microspheres with high encapsulation efficiency. It uses minimal organic solvents, improving biocompatibility and enabling large-scale production of core-shell and simple microspheres. Additionally, the method offers good process reproducibility [106,120,121]. However, the high-temperature processing conditions may alter the physicochemical properties of sensitive components, requiring precise control of drying parameters. Furthermore, the method is associated with relatively high energy consumption and operational costs [113,122].

Phase-separation approaches

In the phase separation method, the drug is either dissolved or dispersed within a polymer solution. Afterward, a phase separation agent is introduced to reduce the polymer's solubility, inducing precipitation and droplet formation around the drug. Subsequently, an extraction agent is added to remove the solvent, ultimately yielding solidified microspheres (Fig. 3d) [107,123]. This method requires minimal equipment and is compatible with various polymer types and drug formulations. The resulting microspheres typically exhibit good sphericity and acceptable process feasibility [122]. However, the procedure is technically demanding, requiring strict control over parameters such as polymer concentration, stirring dynamics, and solvent extraction efficiency [124,125].

Biomaterials for microsphere construction

Natural polymers

Alginate

Alginate is an anionic biopolymer composed of 1,4-β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and 1,4-α-L-guluronic acid (G), commonly found in the cell walls of brown algae [70]. It possesses a range of favorable characteristics, including biocompatibility, biodegradability, hydrophilicity, non-toxicity, low immunogenicity, cost-effectiveness, and widespread availability [71,126]. Under physiological conditions, it forms microspheres via mild ionic cross-linking gelation, offering a simple, economical option for cartilage tissue engineering. Moreover, they support cell encapsulation and tissue engineering by incorporating bioactive molecules like growth factors, proteins, peptides, and small organics [127]. As drug delivery systems, alginate microspheres provide controlled release, targeted delivery, agent protection from degradation, biocompatibility, and easy administration [72].

Despite biocompatibility, biodegradability, and injectability, alginate microcarriers have limited mechanical stability. To overcome this drawback, alginate can be combined with structural support materials such as calcium phosphate cement (CPC), preserving its injectability while enhancing mechanical integrity [73]. Furthermore, the lack of inherent cell adhesion properties limits its biological functionality. This limitation can be overcome through chemical modifications, such as incorporating Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) peptides into both two-dimensional and three-dimensional alginate matrices, significantly enhancing cellular interactions and promoting cell attachment [128]. Additional improvements in alginate's processability and injectability can be achieved by optimizing key physicochemical parameters, including polymer composition, gelling conditions, viscosity, and concentration.

Chitosan

Chitosan (CS) is a cationic polysaccharide obtained through alkaline deacetylation of chitin. It is primarily sourced from natural materials, including shrimp and crab shells, insect exoskeletons, and fungal cell walls [74]. Its molecular structure consists of β-1,4-linked glucosamine and N-acetylglucosamine units, which exhibit structural similarities to glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and hyaluronic acid found in the ECM of cartilage [75]. These structural analogies enable chitosan to mimic the native microenvironment of cartilage ECM, thereby offering distinct advantages in cartilage tissue engineering applications. Chitosan possesses excellent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties, along with favorable biocompatibility, tunable biodegradability, structural adaptability, and recombination potential [129,130]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that chitosan can significantly inhibit the release of inflammatory cytokines, such as Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and (Interleukin-8) IL-8, from chondrocytes, while also reducing oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals, thereby creating a conducive environment for tissue regeneration [76]. The surface of chitosan carries a natural positive charge. Studies have shown that highly deacetylated CS membranes exhibit strong adhesion to fibroblasts, attributed to electrostatic interactions between the positively charged CS and the negatively charged cell membrane surface [131]. This unique property has been exploited to enhance cell adhesion. For instance, Bai et al. developed polylactic acid-glycolic acid copolymer-chitosan (PLGA-CS) microspheres by coating the surface of porous PLGA microspheres with CS via an EDC/NHS reaction. The resulting PLGA-CS@KGN microspheres were capable of loading a large number of stem cells and providing space for their proliferation, thereby enhancing the chondrogenic differentiation potential of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [77]. The degradation rate of chitosan can be modulated by adjusting its degree of deacetylation (DD) and molecular weight (Mw). Chitosan with low DD and low Mw degrades more rapidly and is suitable for short-term repair applications. In contrast, high DD and high Mw chitosan degrade slowly, making it appropriate for long-term support [74]. Notably, its degradation products are non-toxic and have been shown to promote gene expression of cartilage-specific components, such as type II collagen [88].

Hybrid scaffolds that integrate chitosan with polymers, ceramics, or other functional materials have garnered increasing attention for enhancing mechanical strength and biological activity, particularly in the context of repairing AC defects. Looking ahead, future research on chitosan-based microspheres should focus on enhancing targeting precision, optimizing function-specific designs, and developing particular therapeutic platforms. Additionally, efforts should be directed toward refining production processes, reducing manufacturing costs, and advancing the development of multifunctional chitosan microspheres with broader applicability.

Collagen

Collagen is a naturally occurring high-molecular-weight protein widely distributed in animal connective tissues and serves as one of the primary components of the ECM. Due to its excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, interconnected porosity, cartilage conductivity, and low antigenicity after removal of telopeptides, collagen has become one of the most accessible natural materials for cartilage regeneration [23,78,132]. Given that type II collagen constitutes a major structural component of native cartilage, it is a rational strategy to expose MSCs to chondrogenic induction signals and guide their differentiation into cartilage-like matrix using collagen-based microspheres. Li et al. developed a collagen microencapsulation technique by embedding MSCs within a concentrated collagen network to form MSC-collagen microspheres. Within this system, encapsulated MSCs are capable of surviving, proliferating, and migrating inside the microspheres, and demonstrate favorable integration at the implantation site [133]. In another study, Yu et al. successfully achieved tissue integration and subchondral bone remodeling in a rabbit osteochondral defect model by combining collagen hydrogel microspheres with allogeneic chondrocytes to fabricate artificial cartilage particles (ACPs) [134].

Despite its numerous advantages in cartilage tissue engineering, collagen also presents several limitations, including insufficient mechanical strength and stiffness, rapid degradation, potential immunogenicity, low drug loading capacity, and relatively high cost. To address these drawbacks, collagen is often combined with other biomaterials or chemically modified to enhance its physical and biological properties. Process optimization is also employed to improve functionality. Future research should focus on developing more stable and efficient collagen-based constructs better to meet the diverse demands of cartilage tissue engineering applications [79,80,126].

Additionally, collagen derivatives, such as gelatin, hold significant promise in cartilage tissue engineering. Gelatin is a denatured product derived from collagen through partial proteolysis. Compared to native collagen, gelatin offers lower production costs, enhanced biosafety, and minimal immune response in vivo [81]. It is water-soluble and can promote cell adhesion and proliferation via its RGD motif, which interacts with integrin receptors on the cell membrane [82]. Gelatin-based biopolymers exhibit considerable versatility due to their non-covalent interactions and pH- and temperature-responsive phase behavior. Moreover, the presence of abundant functional groups in gelatin molecules allows for extensive chemical modification, enabling precise tuning of its biological and physicochemical properties [83]. For instance, gelatin microspheres (GMS) not only provide a high-density biological interface for cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation but also serve as building blocks for three-dimensional scaffold structures in tissue engineering. In recent years, GMS technology has seen notable advancements; however, several challenges remain, including the development of biocompatible cell encapsulation strategies and achieving controlled degradation kinetics. As a delivery platform, future studies should prioritize the screening and optimization of suitable cross-linking agents to enable precise regulation of GMS properties, particularly in terms of cell encapsulation efficiency, degradation profiles, and functional group modification, thereby enhancing their potential for targeted delivery and tissue regeneration applications [117].

Silk fibroin

Silk fibroin (SF) is a natural fibrous protein primarily derived from the silk of Bombyx mori [135]. Owing to its excellent biocompatibility, tunable biodegradability, favorable mechanical properties, and versatile processability, SF has attracted considerable attention in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [84]. Its β-sheet rich secondary structure provides structural stability and mechanical strength, while its degradation behavior can be tailored through processing parameters, crystallinity regulation, and composite design [85]. These characteristics make SF an attractive biomaterial for the fabrication of microspheres, hydrogels, and composite scaffolds for cartilage repair and regeneration [136].

In microsphere-based cartilage tissue engineering, SF offers several unique advantages. Its intrinsic mechanical strength contributes to the structural integrity of microsphere assemblies, which is particularly beneficial for the construction of organoid-like tissues and injectable scaffold systems [137]. In addition, SF can be processed using a variety of fabrication approaches, including microfluidics, self-assembly, photopolymerization, and composite hydrogel engineering, enabling precise control over microsphere morphology, physicochemical properties, and biological functions [138]. The abundant functional groups within SF further facilitate surface modification and biofunctionalization, allowing the incorporation of bioactive ligands, growth factors, or complementary polymers to enhance cell adhesion, chondrogenic differentiation, and extracellular matrix deposition [136]. For example, RGD modified silk fibroin DNA hydrogel microspheres have been employed as cartilage organoid precursors and demonstrated enhanced cartilage regeneration in vivo [139].

Despite these advantages, several challenges remain for the clinical translation of SF based microsphere systems. The degradation behavior of SF is highly dependent on its β-sheet content and processing history, which may lead to inconsistent degradation kinetics and mismatches between scaffold persistence and tissue remodeling. Furthermore, batch to batch variability, sterilization induced structural changes, storage stability, and large-scale manufacturing remain important barriers to standardization and commercialization [86,87]. Current evidence supporting the superiority of SF-based microspheres over alternative biomaterial platforms is also limited, as direct comparative studies are relatively scarce. Future research should therefore focus on the precise regulation of SF crystallinity, particle size distribution, degradation profiles, and surface biofunctionalization, while establishing standardized fabrication and evaluation protocols to facilitate clinical translation.

Hyaluronic acid

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a key component of the cartilage ECM, characterized by excellent biocompatibility, non-toxicity, and non-immunogenicity. It consists of repeating disaccharide units of D-glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine, making it a widely utilized natural polymer in tissue repair applications [89,140]. Compared to other natural polymers, HA possesses a unique molecular structure and distinctive physicochemical properties that support a range of physiological functions, including joint lubrication, anti-inflammatory effects, antioxidant activity, and regulation of cellular signaling pathways [90]. Despite its advantages, native HA is highly susceptible to degradation by hyaluronidase and ROS, resulting in a short half-life and poor mechanical strength. To overcome these limitations, chemical or physical crosslinking strategies can be applied through reactive functional groups such as carboxyl and hydroxyl moieties, enabling the fabrication of HA microspheres with enhanced structural stability. These modifications significantly improve resistance to enzymatic degradation and mechanical performance [78]. Notably, the degradation products are native HA fragments that do not produce toxic residues.

As a drug delivery system, HA microspheres exhibit a favorable loading capacity and inherent targeting potential. By modulating cross-linking density, surface modification, or receptor-specific binding, HA-based microspheres can achieve sustained release, controlled release, and cell-targeted delivery, thereby promoting effective cartilage regeneration. Although HA microspheres demonstrate promising potential in biomedical applications, several challenges remain, including limited encapsulation efficiency, residual pore-forming agents, and insufficient long-term stability. Future research should focus on optimizing particle size, dispersibility, encapsulation efficiency, and drug-loading capacity to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of these nanoparticles. Additionally, exploring non-toxic cross-linking agents and environmentally friendly preparation techniques will further improve their biocompatibility and safety profile [91]. The development and promotion of standardized manufacturing protocols will facilitate the translation of HA microsphere technology from laboratory research to clinical practice.

Chondroitin sulfate

Chondroitin sulfate (ChS) is a naturally occurring anionic mucopolysaccharide and constitutes a significant component of the ECM in various biological tissues. It is composed of repeating disaccharide units consisting of β-1,3-linked N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and β-1,4-linked D-glucuronic acid (GlcA). ChS exhibits favorable properties such as excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, mucosal adhesion, hygroscopicity, and minimal toxicity, making it a promising candidate for the development of biomaterials in diverse biomedical applications [92,93,141]. In addition to its structural advantages, ChS demonstrates anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities. It exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by down-regulating key inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), NF-κB, and matrix-degrading enzymes [94]. This mechanism plays a crucial role in cartilage repair by promoting the synthesis of new glucosamine, hyaluronic acid, and type II collagen, while simultaneously suppressing enzymes responsible for ECM degradation.

The anionic nature of ChS enables it to interact with positively charged growth factors in vivo, thereby playing a significant role in chondrogenic signaling. This property enhances its utility in cellular and intracellular targeting strategies. Moreover, ChS contains abundant functional groups, such as carboxyl and hydroxyl moieties, that can be chemically modified through esterification, amidation, or the incorporation of stimuli-responsive linkers, enabling tailored functionality. Hydrophobic drugs or polymers can be effectively conjugated using these modification strategies [93,141]. ChS derivatives, such as metal ion complexes including ChS-calcium (ChS-Ca), ChS-strontium (ChS-Sr), and ChS-magnesium (ChS-Mg), have been developed to enhance therapeutic efficacy in treating osteoarthritis and osteoporosis. These modified ChS formulations demonstrate improved in vivo performance through immunomodulatory activity, stimulation of osteoblast proliferation, and enhanced regeneration of AC [92].

Despite its high hydrophilicity and biological benefits, ChS has insufficient mechanical stability, thereby limiting its application in drug delivery and tissue engineering. To address this limitation, ChS is often cross-linked with other polymers to improve its physical and functional properties [95]. However, challenges such as source heterogeneity, molecular weight variability, and rapid degradation still hinder its clinical translation. Future research should focus on standardizing extraction protocols, developing multifunctional composite scaffolds, and utilizing synthetic or biotechnological approaches to produce ChS with defined structures. Optimizing molecular weight and sulfation patterns can further enhance its biological activity and safety profile. Additionally, the development of cost-effective and scalable preparation methods will facilitate the industrial and clinical application of ChS-based materials.

Synthetic polymers

PLGA

PLGA, a biodegradable and amorphous aliphatic polyester, is synthesized through the ring-opening copolymerization of two monomers: lactic acid (LA) and glycolic acid (GA) [142]. It exhibits excellent biocompatibility, safety, and mechanical strength. PLGA has been approved by both the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for use in drug delivery systems. It is non-toxic, non-immunogenic, and biodegradable, with degradation products, lactic acid and glycolic acid, readily metabolized and excreted via natural metabolic pathways, thereby avoiding long-term toxicity [96,121]. The degradation rate can be precisely modulated by adjusting the LA: GA molar ratio [97], making PLGA microspheres highly versatile for controlled drug release and targeted delivery applications.

For example, both hydrophobic and hydrophilic molecules, including growth factors such as BMP-2 and VEGF as well as antibiotics, can be effectively encapsulated within PLGA microspheres. Sustained release profiles can be achieved through surface modifications, such as polydopamine coatings, or structural designs like core-shell configurations. The drug release kinetics are further regulated by tuning parameters such as microsphere size, molecular weight, and polymer composition. For instance, smaller microspheres typically exhibit faster release, whereas larger ones provide more sustained delivery [98,121]. Moreover, the surface functionality of PLGA microspheres can be tailored to incorporate targeting ligands, anti-inflammatory agents, or pro-angiogenic moieties through chemical modifications (e.g., amination, phosphorylation) or the attachment of bioactive molecules such as aptamers or cell membrane coatings [99]. For instance, PLGA microspheres loaded with magnesium ions or doped with silicon or zinc have demonstrated enhanced osteogenic differentiation and angiogenic potential.

Despite their promising applications in cartilage repair, several challenges remain. The fabrication process is relatively complex, with significant variability from one batch to the next. Additionally, interactions between the encapsulated drugs and the polymer matrix may compromise drug stability, particularly when delivering sensitive bioactives such as growth factors or anti-inflammatory agents. Furthermore, the common phenomenon of initial burst release and inconsistent particle size distribution can lead to unpredictable drug release profiles, affecting the controllability of therapeutic outcomes. Manufacturing scalability and storage stability also pose barriers to clinical translation. To address these limitations, future research should focus on optimizing production processes using the “Quality by Design” (QbD) approach to enhance reproducibility and consistency. Emerging technologies, such as microfluidics and self-packaging methods, offer precise control over particle size and reduced burst release, thereby improving delivery efficiency. Intelligent, stimuli-responsive systems, such as pH- or temperature-sensitive microspheres, can enable environment-triggered drug release. Surface functionalization and composite material strategies also hold promise for enhancing the biological performance of PLGA microspheres and advancing their application in cartilage regeneration [98].

Polycaprolactone (PCL)

PCL is an aliphatic polyester characterized by excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and absorbability. It belongs to the poly(α-hydroxy) family of polymers, alongside polylactic acid (PLA) and polyglycolide (PGA) [101]. PCL has been approved by the U.S. FDA and is widely utilized in cartilage tissue engineering due to its favorable biological properties and thermoplastic nature. The polymer consists of repeating ε-caprolactone (C6H10O2) units, and its degree of polymerization (n value) determines its degradation time and structural stability through hydrolysis of ester bonds. The degradation rate can be modulated via chemical modification, adjustment of monomer ratios, or alteration of polymer chain length [100]. Compared with PLA and PGA, PCL exhibits a slower and more controlled degradation profile without generating significant acidic byproducts, making it more suitable for long-term implantation. Additionally, PCL demonstrates good compatibility with other polymers and exhibits high drug permeability, rendering it an ideal material for sustained-release drug delivery systems [101]. Studies have shown that PCL microspheres and nanospheres can achieve prolonged release of various therapeutic agents, as reported by Sinha et al. [143].

Moreover, PCL serves as an ideal platform for surface modification to enhance hydrophilicity and improve cell adhesion, thereby further promoting biocompatibility [102]. It can also be blended with a wide range of polymers or composite materials to enhance its mechanical strength and functional versatility [103]. Despite its widespread application, PCL does present certain limitations, such as a slow crystallization rate and relatively low thermal stability, which may restrict its use in specific applications [100]. Future research should focus on integrating materials science with advanced fabrication technologies to further optimize the functionality, performance, and applicability of PCL-based systems in biomedical and tissue engineering fields.

Beyond their intrinsic material properties, the translational potential of microsphere based systems is strongly influenced by multiple design, manufacturing, and in vivo performance parameters. These include particle size distribution, drug loading and encapsulation efficiency, burst release, release kinetics, degradation behavior, mechanical stability, tissue adhesion, intra articular retention, clearance profiles, feasibility of repeated administration, degradation product safety, and batch-to-batch reproducibility [[96], [97], [98], [99],121]. Collectively, these factors determine therapeutic efficacy, safety, manufacturing consistency, and clinical feasibility.

Importantly, the performance of microsphere systems is highly dependent on both material composition and structural design. For example, PLGA microspheres provide tunable degradation and sustained drug release but may exhibit burst release and generate acidic degradation products. Gelatin and hyaluronic acid microspheres possess excellent biocompatibility and cell interactive properties, yet often require additional crosslinking to enhance structural stability. Chitosan and silk fibroin microspheres offer intrinsic bioactivity and versatile processing characteristics but still require further optimization of degradation kinetics and manufacturing reproducibility. Composite microspheres can integrate multiple therapeutic functions within a single platform; however, this increased functionality is often accompanied by greater fabrication complexity and regulatory challenges. Therefore, systematic cross platform comparison of representative microsphere systems is essential for identifying their respective advantages and limitations and for evaluating their potential for clinical translation (Table 2).

Functionalized microspheres as injectable and modular delivery platforms

Conservative treatment of cartilage injury primarily relies on pharmacological interventions to alleviate pain and inflammation, such as the oral administration of NSAIDs like ibuprofen, celecoxib, and diclofenac, or intra-articular injections of glucosamine and hyaluronic acid [12,144]. However, due to the avascular nature of cartilage, along with drug metabolism and lymphatic clearance, achieving a balance between therapeutic efficacy and safety remains challenging with conventional delivery methods [145,146]. Microspheres have been increasingly utilized in cartilage repair strategies to enhance drug bioavailability, prolong therapeutic action, and enable controlled release and targeted delivery. Beyond serving as drug depots, microspheres function as versatile carriers for biomolecules and cells, facilitating their targeted localization and subsequent promotion of tissue regeneration. More importantly, microspheres offer dual advantages by overcoming the short half-life of therapeutic agents through sustained release profiles and by providing a three-dimensional “micro-niche” that protects encapsulated cells from mechanical stress and inflammatory cytokines present in the joint cavity. These features represent a significant advantage over traditional hydrogels or direct cell injection approaches.

Within the hierarchical framework proposed in this review, the microsphere systems discussed in this section mainly belong to the categories of passive delivery systems and functionalized delivery systems. While passive delivery systems primarily improve local retention and provide sustained therapeutic release, functionalized delivery systems further integrate bioactive cargos, cell loading capabilities, and engineered structural features to actively regulate cellular responses and the regenerative microenvironment. These advances have transformed microspheres from simple delivery vehicles into multifunctional therapeutic platforms. Building on this foundation, the next stage of development involves stimuli responsive and feedback regulated systems capable of dynamically sensing and adapting to pathological microenvironments, as discussed in the following sections.

Controlled delivery of small-molecule drugs

Small-molecule drugs often exhibit a short half-life following intra-articular injection due to their low molecular weight, rapid metabolism, and consequently limited therapeutic duration. Studies have reported that the half-lives of small molecules, such as corticosteroids and NSAIDs, within the joint cavity are typically only 1-2 h [24]. To overcome these limitations, strategies aimed at prolonging drug action, preventing burst release, and enhancing therapeutic efficacy have increasingly focused on encapsulating drugs within microspheres based on either natural or synthetic polymers, offering a more ideal approach for sustained delivery.

Kartogenin (KGN), a widely used small molecule in cartilage repair, activates the AMPK-SIRT1 signaling pathway, promotes the proliferation of MSCs [147], and induces chondrogenic differentiation by binding to FLNA and inhibiting the formation of the CBFβ-RUNX1 complex [148]. It has become an essential component in cartilage tissue engineering applications [149]. To enhance its bioavailability, Yu et al. developed MCC/PKGN-functionalized porous scaffolds using natural polysaccharide cellulose and PLGA microspheres, achieving synergistic improvements in mechanical support and controlled drug delivery. In vivo results demonstrated that the regenerated tissue in the MCC/PKGN + MSCs group exhibited morphology similar to that of adjacent normal cartilage tissue, with abundant cartilage lacunae (Fig. 4a) [150]. Zhao et al. combined KGN-loaded PLGA microspheres with cartilage ECM to simulate a regenerative microenvironment, thereby promoting the chondrogenic differentiation of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [152]. Dai et al. developed an injectable collagen-based microsphere hydrogel system (Col-Apt@KGN MPs) that can spatiotemporally regulate endogenous MSC recruitment and chondrogenic differentiation through the sequential release of Apt19S and KGN [153]. Additionally, Morici et al. designed a nanocrystalline-chitosan microsphere system to reduce the KGN particle size, improve solubility, and enhance the dissolution rate through wet grinding and spray drying techniques, demonstrating favorable stability and controlled release performance under simulated injection conditions [154]. To address the challenge of treating partial-thickness cartilage defects (PTCD), Han et al. engineered a GM/AN/KGN covalent adhesive hydrogel with excellent adhesion properties, minimal swelling, and strong chondrogenic potential, showing promise for long-term induction of chondrogenic differentiation in vitro [155].

Beyond KGN, other small-molecule drugs have also shown significant progress when incorporated into microsphere systems. Zhang et al. developed chitosan microspheres loaded with verteporfin to specifically target and inhibit YAP pathway activity in chondrocytes, effectively maintaining cartilage homeostasis and enhancing tissue repair [156]. Pan et al. constructed a sustained-release system combining tetramethylpyrazine (TMP)-loaded PLGA microspheres with calcium alginate hydrogel, significantly reducing vascular dysplasia and cartilage fibrosis while improving the repair microenvironment in the presence of BMSCs [157]. In the CM@pGM system developed by He et al., GA-Mg nanocarriers were loaded with paclitaxel (PA) and encapsulated within hydrogel microspheres, enabling drug release over two weeks. The synergistic effects of magnesium and PA helped alleviate inflammation and oxidative stress, effectively mitigate joint damage and osteophyte formation in OA rats, thereby promoting cartilage regeneration (Fig. 4b) [151]. To address the issue of rapid clearance and side effects associated with glucocorticoid injections such as dexamethasone (DEX), Stefani et al. designed a DEX-PLGA microsphere (DLMS)-based sustained-release system embedded in decellularized agarose hydrogel. This formulation achieved a sustained release duration of over 99 days, inhibited cartilage degradation in vitro, and promoted osteochondral repair while improving joint function in vivo [158]. Furthermore, Wang et al. fabricated an SF/COL/HA composite scaffold incorporating PAP-PLGA microspheres based on the biological activity of deer antler polypeptide (PAP). Animal experiments confirmed that this system effectively enhanced cartilage regeneration [159].

Compared with direct intra articular injection or systemic administration, microsphere-based delivery systems offer several advantages for the delivery of small molecule therapeutics. By providing sustained release and prolonged retention within the joint cavity, microspheres can reduce rapid drug clearance, decrease dosing frequency, and protect encapsulated agents from premature degradation or metabolism. In addition, surface functionalization and advanced structural design can improve drug accumulation at target sites and enhance modulation of the regenerative microenvironment. These attributes position small molecule loaded microspheres as an important intermediate stage between conventional drug depots and more sophisticated functionalized therapeutic platforms for cartilage repair and OA treatment.

Despite these advantages, most currently available systems remain dependent on preprogrammed release profiles rather than dynamic responses to pathological changes within the joint. Consequently, therapeutic efficacy may be limited by burst release, insufficient tissue retention, and poor adaptability to different stages of disease progression. Future development should therefore focus on integrating stimuli responsive elements, such as pH, ROS, enzyme, or mechanically responsive modules, to enable disease adaptive drug delivery. Such strategies may improve therapeutic precision while maintaining drug stability, biological safety, and controllable degradation.

Delivery of bioactive molecules

Cartilage repair involves the coordinated regulation of various signaling molecules. The targeted delivery of bioactive molecules enables localized intervention in cartilage injury and promotes tissue regeneration. As a key class of soluble signaling proteins, growth factors regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and synthetic activity by binding to specific receptors on the surface of target cells [[160], [161], [162]]. However, these molecules typically exhibit a short half-life in vivo and are rapidly cleared after systemic administration, thus limiting their full biological effects, such as mitogenic or anabolic activities [163,164].

The TGF-β family plays a central role in inducing chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs. Among them, TGF-β1, TGF-β2, and TGF-β3 stimulate Sox9 expression, thereby enhancing ECM production in MSCs and promoting chondrocyte synthetic activity [160,165]. To achieve stable and localized delivery, several studies have explored microsphere-based systems. Lin et al. developed a PLGA microsphere system capable of sustained TGF-β3 release for over 8 weeks [166]. Yang et al. fabricated GelMA hydrogel microspheres with excellent wettability and sustained TGF-β release properties, enabling rapid adaptation to irregular cartilage defects and promoting regeneration of the temporomandibular joint [167]. Min et al. further utilized CH-PLA/HA composite microspheres to precisely control the release kinetics of TGF-β1, meeting the dynamic growth factor demands at different stages of cartilage repair [168].

Beyond TGF-β, other bioactive molecules have also been integrated into microsphere systems to modulate the inflammatory environment. Park et al. developed responsive gelatin microspheres that release anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4, IL-10, and IL-13 in response to disease activity, enabling on-demand regulation of inflammation during arthritis and protecting chondrocytes from IL-1β- and LPS-induced damage [169]. Chen et al. encapsulated IL-1β-induced exosomes (C-Exos) in hyaluronic acid microspheres, and this not only enhanced chondrocyte function and matrix synthesis but also promoted macrophage polarization from an M1 to an M2 phenotype, thereby improving the local immune microenvironment (Fig. 5a) [170]. Yang et al. constructed a 3D-printed tissue engineering scaffold incorporating ECM, GelMA, and TGF-β3-loaded PLGA microspheres, enabling the recruitment and directional induction of endogenous stem cells [172]. Hanet al. developed a biomimetic scaffold with multi-factor gradient distribution using melt electrowriting (MEW) and inkjet printing technologies: BMP-7 was applied to the surface layer to induce PRG4 secretion and improve lubrication; Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) was used in the middle layer to enhance matrix synthesis; hyaluronic acid was incorporated into the deep layer to promote deep chondrocyte differentiation; and TGF-β1 was distributed throughout to induce MSC chondrogenesis, thereby promoting cartilage injury repair (Fig. 5b) [171]. Additionally, Li et al. employed a hyperactive platelet lysate (HPL)-loaded microsphere system enriched with TGF-β, IGF-1, and VEGF, significantly promoting chondrocyte proliferation and the expression of Sox9, Col II, and ACAN while reversing OA cartilage degeneration [173]. In another study, gelatin microspheres combined with tannic acid were used to construct a particle hydrogel system for delivering platelet-rich plasma (PRP), and this enhanced adhesion, ROS scavenging capacity, and local retention time, thereby improving the biological effects of PRP and promoting cartilage regeneration [174].

In summary, biomolecule-loaded microsphere systems provide more sophisticated biological regulation than conventional drug depots by directly influencing chondrogenic differentiation, extracellular matrix synthesis, inflammation, and cell survival. Their key advantages include localized retention, sustained bioactive presentation, enhanced molecular stability, and controlled release of growth factors, cytokines, exosomes, and other signaling molecules. Compared with traditional systemic administration or direct local injection, these systems can prolong the therapeutic activity of bioactive factors and enable more precise regulation of endogenous cellular responses, thereby supporting cell free in situ regeneration strategies. Furthermore, biomolecule-loaded microspheres can be integrated with scaffold-based constructs, exosome delivery platforms, and advanced biofabrication approaches such as 3D printing to further optimize the regenerative microenvironment and promote functional cartilage repair. As such, these systems represent an important step in the evolution from passive delivery vehicles to functionalized therapeutic platforms.

Despite these advantages, the biological effects of many signaling molecules remain highly dependent on dose, timing, and local microenvironmental conditions. Inappropriate release profiles may result in insufficient therapeutic efficacy, hypertrophic differentiation, or unintended biological responses. Future development should therefore focus on spatially confined release, sequential delivery strategies, microenvironment responsive regulation, and precise optimization of dosage and release kinetics. Such advances may facilitate the transition toward adaptive and intelligent microsphere systems capable of dynamically responding to pathological changes during cartilage repair.

Cell-laden microspheres for tissue regeneration

In tissue engineering, the repair of damaged tissues depends on a series of coordinated biological processes, including cell adhesion, survival, and proliferation [175]. Acellular endogenous regeneration strategies avoid the immune rejection and limited cell sources associated with exogenous cell transplantation. However, even with sufficient endogenous cell recruitment during repair, complete restoration of normal tissue function remains difficult [176]. As a key component of tissue engineering, stem cells have attracted considerable attention due to their self-renewal ability, multilineage differentiation potential, and notable paracrine, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory functions [177]. Among various types, MSCs stand out for their multipotent differentiation potential, self-renewal capability, and low immunogenicity. Moreover, MSCs can be readily isolated from autologous sources such as bone marrow and adipose tissue, making them a promising and clinically relevant cell source for cartilage tissue engineering in recent years [133]. However, directly transplanted cells often face challenges in the harsh intra-articular environment, characterized by high mechanical loading and friction, leading to cell damage or restricted expansion and ultimately compromising therapeutic efficacy [178]. To overcome these limitations, microspheres have been widely explored as cell delivery vehicles. Their three-dimensional architecture and excellent injectability enhance cell viability, proliferation, and differentiation. Notably, porous microspheres offer superior suitability for cell seeding and tissue regeneration owing to their high specific surface area and interconnected porosity. Furthermore, microsphere-based systems can create a biomimetic microenvironment that supports cell growth and functional maturation, demonstrating clear advantages over conventional delivery methods. Previous studies have also shown that incorporating microspheres as porogens within leaching scaffolds can introduce microporous structures, thereby improving mass transfer efficiency and providing additional space for cell attachment and proliferation [179].

Currently, microsphere-based stem cell delivery systems have demonstrated encouraging progress across multiple studies. Chen et al. employed gelatin microspheres to deliver Wharton's jelly-derived MSCs (WJMSCs), demonstrating favorable cell viability, proliferation, and cartilage matrix production in an osteoarthritis model [180]. Yin et al. developed a modular bioink that integrates GelMA/alginate microspheres with 3D printing technology to fabricate a biomimetic multiscale scaffold, and this demonstrated excellent cartilage regeneration outcomes and clinical translation potential [181]. In addition, Bai et al. developed positively charged PLGA-CS microspheres that facilitate cell adhesion to their porous interior. With a high cell-loading capacity (1 × 104 mm−3), these microspheres enable the co-delivery of MSCs and KGN, providing protection and controlled release in the osteoarthritic microenvironment. The synergistic effect of KGN and PLGA-CS promotes the chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs. In vivo experiments demonstrated that this system significantly reduces osteophyte formation and enhances the cartilage repair potential of MSCs (Fig. 6a) [77].

Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) are widely utilized due to their accessibility, minimal invasiveness, and strong chondrogenic differentiation potential. Liao et al. fabricated alginate-gelatin (Alg-Gel) microspheres using electrospray technology, effectively encapsulating ADSCs and inducing their differentiation into cartilage-like phenotypes in a rat model of knee cartilage defect [183]. To counteract the detrimental effects of intra-articular hypoxia on chondroprogenitor cell (CPC) function, Feng et al. further developed hypoxia-preconditioned GelMA microspheres (CGM). Results showed hypoxia-pretreated CGMs expressed the highest levels of classical chondrogenic genes Gdf5, Prg4, Sox6, and Sox9, compared to the normoxia-pretreated CGMs group and other groups. This system activates the NRF2/HO-1 signaling pathway, effectively mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation, promoting cell proliferation, and maintaining extracellular matrix homeostasis, thus improving cartilage repair outcomes (Fig. 6b) [182].

In summary, cell-laden microsphere systems further extend the concept of microsphere functionalization by integrating delivery functions with living regenerative components. Beyond serving as protective carriers that reduce cell loss and mechanical damage during administration, these systems function as dynamic microenvironments that support cell adhesion, survival, proliferation, differentiation, and paracrine signaling. Their injectability, biocompatibility, structural tunability, and capacity to regulate the local microenvironment make them attractive platforms for precise and minimally invasive cell-based cartilage repair. Furthermore, cell laden microspheres can be combined with drug delivery systems, 3D bioprinting technologies, and advanced biomaterial engineering strategies to enhance cell function and improve adaptation to diverse pathological conditions. Through rational control of microscale architecture and biochemical cues, these platforms can guide cell fate decisions and promote tissue specific regeneration. As such, they represent a further evolution of functionalized microspheres from biomolecule delivery platforms toward living therapeutic systems.

Despite these advantages, several challenges remain. Therapeutic outcomes are strongly influenced by cell source variability, post implantation cell survival, nutrient and oxygen transport, immune compatibility, and the long-term fate of transplanted cells. Future development should therefore focus on enhancing cell protection, regulating paracrine activity, promoting immunomodulation, facilitating adaptive matrix remodeling, and establishing standardized criteria for evaluating cell viability, functionality, and long-term stability. Such advances may enable the development of next-generation microsphere systems capable of integrating cellular therapies with intelligent and responsive regenerative functions.

Advanced functionalization strategies for in situ cartilage regeneration

Building on delivery-oriented microsphere platforms, advanced functionalization strategies aim to transform microspheres from therapeutic carriers into active regulators of the cartilage repair microenvironment. Rather than functioning solely as reservoirs for drug or biomolecule release, these systems are designed to influence key biological processes involved in tissue regeneration through microenvironment modulation, structural guidance, and adaptive therapeutic regulation. Within the hierarchical framework proposed in this review, such functionalization represents an important transition toward smart microsphere systems. Representative strategies include regeneration mimicking microenvironments, immunomodulatory regulation, metabolic intervention, integration of physical cues, and stimuli responsive delivery, all of which enable microspheres to exert increasingly sophisticated control over cartilage repair and disease progression.

Engineering regeneration-mimicking microenvironments

In cartilage repair, functionalized microspheres often facilitate tissue regeneration by mimicking a cell-free regenerative microenvironment. The primary mechanism involves regulating stem cell recruitment and directed differentiation. For example, Wu et al. employed microfluidic technology to fabricate core-shell structured PLGA microspheres, encapsulating (Stromal cell-derived factor-1) SDF-1 and KGN separately within the core and shell layers. These microspheres were then integrated into HA scaffolds, enabling coordinated regulation of cell homing and chondrogenic differentiation, thereby promoting cartilage defect repair without the need for exogenous cell transplantation [184]. Zhang et al. fabricated polydopamine-modified PLGA microspheres (PMS) loaded with KGN and functionalized with E7 peptide. The PDA coating enhanced the adhesion of microspheres, allowing them to anchor specifically to damaged cartilage. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that these multifunctional microspheres could stably adhere to the cartilage matrix, recruit synovial mesenchymal stem cells (SMSCs), and provide sustained release of KGN to promote chondrogenic differentiation, thereby enhancing cartilage repair and reducing the progression of osteoarthritis (Fig. 7a) [185].

Decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM), known for retaining abundant bioactive proteins, growth factors, and immunomodulatory components, has been widely utilized in the development of functionalized microspheres. Ghosh et al. encapsulated dECM within PLA microspheres to achieve sustained release of structural proteins, glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), and growth factors, and this significantly promoted hMSC migration, proliferation, and chondrogenic differentiation [187]. Pang et al. fabricated HA microspheres using MSC-derived ECM stimulated by IFN-γ, enhancing their targeted retention at injury sites. These microspheres also modulated the release of key signaling molecules such as CXCL12 and TGF-β, thereby closely recapitulating the natural cartilage regeneration microenvironment [188]. Li et al. developed MESS microspheres derived from hBMSC secretions, enriched with ECM components and exosomes. When combined with hydrogel scaffolds, this system effectively regulated stem cell migration, inflammatory responses, and cartilage formation (Fig. 7b) [186].

Overall, regeneration mimicking microspheres represent an important functionalization strategy that seeks to recreate key features of the native cartilage repair niche. By reconstructing cell matrix and cell signaling interactions, these systems can provide a more biomimetic microenvironment that supports cell recruitment, adhesion, differentiation, and extracellular matrix deposition. Such capabilities enable microspheres to move beyond passive delivery and actively participate in the regulation of tissue regeneration.

Despite these advantages, accurately replicating the native cartilage microenvironment remains challenging because cartilage repair is governed by dynamic biochemical, mechanical, metabolic, and immunological processes that vary across different stages of healing. Future studies should therefore identify the critical microenvironmental cues required during specific phases of repair and develop strategies for their coordinated and temporally controlled presentation. Achieving this level of regulation will be essential for advancing regeneration mimicking microspheres toward more adaptive and intelligent regenerative platforms.

Immunomodulatory and metabolic reprogramming strategies

Modulating the local inflammatory microenvironment

In the OA environment, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 significantly impair cartilage repair processes, making it essential to modulate the local inflammatory microenvironment [[189], [190], [191]]. To address this challenge, Ji et al. developed a thermosensitive controlled-release system combining KGN and DMOG to regulate the immune environment at the defect site and enhance the repair process [192]. Li et al. developed a dual-functional composite system (KM13E@PGE) that responds to the inflammatory environment in a sequential manner. This system first releases IL-10+EV to polarize macrophages toward the M2 phenotype and effectively mitigate pathological inflammation induced by OA, followed by the subsequent release of SOX9+EV to enhance cartilage matrix synthesis, enabling spatiotemporally controlled immune modulation and cartilage tissue regeneration (Fig. 8a) [193]. Furthermore, Bai et al. designed PLGA microspheres encapsulating PDGF-AB and KGN, with the addition of metformin (Met), to integrate anti-inflammatory effects with tissue repair enhancement. This strategy not only suppressed inflammation but also promoted endogenous MSC recruitment and macrophage polarization toward a reparative phenotype [195]. Qiu et al. engineered a double-layer microsphere system capable of co-delivering parecoxib and IL-4, synergistically inhibiting inflammation, inducing M2 macrophage polarization, and facilitating cartilage regeneration [196].

Most of the studies mentioned above focus on anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative strategies. However, a more promising approach involves constructing multifunctional, time-controlled integrated systems that mimic the natural stages of cartilage regeneration. The ideal biomimetic approach recapitulates the key phases of endogenous healing, beginning with an initial inflammatory phase, followed by proliferative and remodeling phases. Endogenous MSCs are recruited from the surrounding microenvironment, undergo proliferation and chondrogenic differentiation, deposit matrix, and ultimately achieve tissue maturation [79]. The GFs@μS system proposed by Li et al. integrates these biological processes through a biomimetic design. In this system, TGF-β3-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN), along with IGF-1 and Platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB), were co-encapsulated into polydopamine (pDA)-coated microspheres embedded within a chitosan hydrogel. These components were further incorporated into acellular cartilage extracellular matrix (ACECM) and PCL scaffolds to simulate the structure of cartilage lacunae. This platform enables the sequential regulation of inflammation, stem cell recruitment, differentiation, and matrix deposition, demonstrating a pronounced anti-inflammatory effect and effectively modulating macrophage phenotype, thereby exhibiting robust biological activity and therapeutic potential for AC regeneration (Fig. 8b) [194].

Collectively, immunomodulatory microspheres represent an important functionalization strategy for osteoarthritis (OA) intervention because inflammation is not merely a secondary consequence of disease progression but a key driver of cartilage degeneration and joint destruction. By reducing catabolic cytokine signaling, regulating macrophage polarization, and protecting chondrocytes from inflammatory injury, these systems can help restore a more favorable regenerative microenvironment and support cartilage homeostasis. Such functions enable microspheres to actively participate in disease modification rather than simply serving as therapeutic carriers.

Despite these advantages, effective immunoregulation remains challenging because inflammatory responses are highly dynamic and play essential roles in tissue repair and host defense. Excessive or prolonged immunosuppression may impair normal remodeling processes and increase the risk of undesirable biological effects. Future development should therefore emphasize context dependent immunoregulation, precisely controlled therapeutic windows, and coordinated transitions from inflammation resolution to matrix regeneration. Achieving these goals will be critical for the development of adaptive microsphere systems capable of balancing immune homeostasis with long term tissue repair.

Regulating cell metabolism and maintaining homeostasis

The development of OA is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Dysfunctional mitochondria generate excessive ROS, leading to oxidative stress, which in turn damages chondrocytes and the ECM, promotes the formation of an inflammatory microenvironment, and accelerates cartilage degeneration [197]. Although antioxidant interventions can mitigate oxidative stress, they are often insufficient to restore mitochondrial function fully [198]. To address this challenge, Chen et al. developed a mitochondrial quality control system based on nanoenzyme hydrogel microspheres. These microspheres integrate cerium oxide nanoparticles with metformin to reduce mitochondrial oxidative stress and activate autophagy, thereby facilitating the clearance of damaged mitochondria. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the microspheres significantly improved mitochondrial membrane potential and overall chondrocyte function. In a rat OA model, intra-articular injection of these microspheres effectively regulated mitochondrial mass, increased the joint space width (JSW), reduced cartilage matrix degradation and osteophyte formation, and significantly lowered the Mankin score (Fig. 9a) [199]. Moreover, excessive intracellular iron ions can trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and induce ferroptosis in chondrocytes, emerging as a key contributor to cartilage damage in OA. Xu et al. designed Gel@P-PTPC composite microspheres with metal-chelating properties. These microspheres release β-diketone groups that scavenge reactive iron and restore mitochondrial homeostasis under conditions of oxidative stress. By activating the Nrf2/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway, the microspheres inhibit lipid peroxidation and normalize aberrant iron metabolism, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and promoting cartilage repair (Fig. 9b) [200].

Taken together, metabolism regulating microspheres extend the scope of cartilage repair from extracellular matrix preservation and regeneration toward the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. By targeting mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, autophagy, cellular senescence, and ferroptosis, these systems have the potential to intervene in fundamental mechanisms underlying cartilage degeneration and disease progression. As a result, metabolism-based regulation represents an important step toward more mechanistically informed and disease modifying therapeutic strategies.

Despite this promise, metabolic pathways are highly interconnected and dynamically regulated, making precise intervention particularly challenging. Excessive or inappropriate modulation may disrupt normal cellular adaptation and compromise tissue homeostasis. Future studies should therefore identify optimal metabolic targets, define appropriate therapeutic windows and dose ranges, evaluate disease stage specific responses, and determine the long-term effects of metabolic regulation on chondrocyte phenotype, extracellular matrix quality, and overall tissue function. Such advances will be essential for translating metabolism regulating microspheres into adaptive regenerative platforms capable of addressing the underlying causes of cartilage degeneration.

Integrating biophysical cues and mechanical stimuli

Electrical stimulation can modulate cell membrane potential, activate intracellular signaling pathways, and regulate cell-cell interactions, thereby enhancing the synthesis of specific proteins and promoting cell differentiation. Under electrical stimulation, MSCs differentiate and function as progenitor cells within the joint environment [201]. Han et al. developed a piezoelectric hydrogel microsphere system (Piezo@CR MPs) that incorporates barium titanate nanoparticles and is functionalized with a stem cell recruitment peptide (CR) on its surface. In vivo experiments demonstrated that regenerated cartilage tissue in the defect sites of the experimental group (piezo with US activation) closely resembled native cartilage, indicating that external pressure matching the threshold of the piezoelectric microspheres can trigger electrical signal output to promote cartilage defect repair. These microspheres are capable of converting mechanical stress, such as ultrasound, into localized electrical stimulation, thereby accelerating the release of stem cell-recruiting peptides and facilitating the colonization of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, ultimately promoting cartilage formation. This approach pioneers a novel strategy for targeted mechano-electrical transduction therapy (Fig. 10a) [202].

To address the issue of high energy loss in conventional piezoelectric materials, Wang et al. employed supramolecular engineering combined with microfluidic technology to fabricate stress-electrical coupling hydrogel microspheres with a slip-ring structure. This design significantly reduced energy dissipation and enhanced electrical coupling efficiency. The electrical signals generated by these microspheres induced Ca2+ influx in stem cells, promoted chondrogenic differentiation via activation of the cAMP signaling pathway, and facilitated macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby reducing inflammation. In vivo experiments demonstrated that these microspheres significantly alleviated cartilage damage, reduced the severity of osteoarthritis, and improved functional recovery, offering a novel strategy for repairing mechanosensitive tissues (Fig. 10b) [203].

In summary, biophysical cue integrated microspheres represent a distinctive functionalization strategy that complements conventional biochemical regulation. By incorporating mechanical, electrical, magnetic, ultrasound responsive, or piezoelectric signals, these systems can influence mechanotransduction pathways, cell migration, cytoskeletal organization, macrophage polarization, and extracellular matrix alignment. As such, they expand the design space of microsphere-based therapies beyond molecular delivery and provide additional opportunities to regulate the regenerative microenvironment through physical stimulation.

Despite their potential, the therapeutic windows and mechanisms of action of many biophysical cues remain incompletely understood. Excessive or inappropriate stimulation may induce cellular stress, disrupt tissue homeostasis, or promote abnormal matrix remodeling. Future studies should therefore systematically evaluate the effects of cue intensity, stimulation duration, frequency, safety, and interactions with biochemical signaling pathways. Establishing these design principles will be essential for optimizing the therapeutic efficacy of biophysical cue integrated microspheres and for advancing the development of multifunctional regenerative platforms that combine physical and biochemical regulation.

Stimuli-responsive and targeted delivery systems

Among the various functionalization strategies discussed above, stimuli responsive and targeted delivery systems most closely embody the concept of smart microspheres. Unlike conventional delivery platforms that rely on predetermined release profiles, these systems are designed to recognize and respond to pathological cues within the cartilage microenvironment. Representative triggers include acidic pH, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), matrix degrading enzymes, inflammatory mediators, temperature fluctuations, and abnormal mechanical stimuli. By translating disease associated signals into regulated therapeutic responses, stimuli-responsive microspheres can achieve on demand release while adapting to local pathological conditions. This capability represents an important transition from functionalized delivery systems to truly responsive therapeutic platforms. Through selective activation at diseased sites, stimuli responsive microspheres may improve therapeutic precision, enhance local efficacy, and minimize unnecessary exposure of surrounding healthy tissues.

To achieve precise delivery of molecules, cells, and drugs essential for cartilage repair, researchers have increasingly utilized functionalized microspheres to enable targeted therapeutic strategies. Thomas et al. developed alginate-based Janus microspheres that encapsulate MSCs and iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) in separate compartments. By integrating an external electromagnetic control system, these magnetically responsive microspheres were directed to cartilage injury sites with high spatial precision [204]. Similarly, Yao et al. fabricated drug-loaded microspheres (MHS@PPKHF) coated with fluorescein nanoparticles and further modified with hyaluronic acid methacrylate to form a lubricating layer within the joint space, reducing friction. The positively charged nanoparticles enabled electromagnetic guidance to the deep cartilage zone, facilitating real-time fluorescence imaging of cartilage regeneration. Simultaneously, the system promoted BMSC-mediated chondrogenic differentiation and accelerated tissue repair, while also exhibiting pronounced anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 11a) [205].

In addition, Yu et al. designed ROS-responsive hydrogel microspheres (KGN/Dex-TSPBA@WHMs) functionalized with the cartilage-targeting peptide WYRGRL. These microspheres specifically bind to type II collagen in cartilage tissue, allowing stable anchoring within the cartilage matrix. Upon exposure to ROS, the microspheres trigger a dual-release mechanism of KGN and dexamethasone (Dex), effectively reducing inflammation and promoting chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs, thereby achieving long-term localized therapeutic effects [207]. Zhang et al. further advanced this concept by developing TGFβ-affinity peptide-modified gelatin methacrylate (GelMA)-based hydrogel microspheres (TRG microspheres). These microspheres were engineered to carry type I collagen antibodies that specifically recognize type I collagen present in OA cartilage lesions. This design enables precise targeting of multiple injured regions, facilitating localized hyaline cartilage regeneration and achieving both targeted delivery and regenerative efficacy (Fig. 11b) [206].

Despite their considerable promise, the smart performance of responsive microspheres should be evaluated using well defined design and performance criteria rather than descriptive labels alone. Key parameters include response threshold, trigger specificity, response reversibility, release precision, degradation behavior, local biocompatibility, and long term retention. Collectively, these factors determine whether a microsphere system can reliably translate pathological signals into controlled therapeutic responses under physiologically relevant conditions.

Particular attention should be paid to the response threshold, which directly influences activation selectivity. A threshold that is too low may lead to premature activation or off target release, whereas an excessively high threshold may prevent effective triggering within the diseased microenvironment. In addition, the biological safety of responsive components and their degradation products must be carefully evaluated, including their potential effects on synovial tissues, macrophage uptake, chronic inflammation, and systemic exposure. Establishing standardized criteria for responsiveness, safety, and therapeutic performance will therefore be essential for distinguishing truly smart microsphere systems from conventional sustained release platforms and for facilitating their clinical translation.

Integrated OA-oriented design framework for microsphere systems

The functionalization strategies discussed above support the development of an integrated OA oriented design framework based on the sequential logic of “pathological cue, responsive design, payload selection, and therapeutic objective”. Within this framework, microsphere systems are not designed solely according to material properties or delivery functions, but are instead tailored to specific disease mechanisms and therapeutic needs.

OA is increasingly recognized as a whole joint degenerative disease rather than a localized cartilage defect. Its progression involves a complex pathological network that includes inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, acidic and catabolic microenvironments, matrix degrading enzymes, abnormal mechanical loading, chondrocyte senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated autophagy, ferroptosis, synovitis, and subchondral bone remodeling. Consequently, the selection of microsphere materials, responsive mechanisms, and therapeutic cargos should be guided by the dominant pathological cues and intervention goals at different stages of disease progression.

For example, inflammatory cytokines such as IL 1β, TNF α, and IL 6 may guide the delivery of anti-inflammatory agents, cytokine modulators, or macrophage polarizing cargos to suppress catabolic signaling and restore immune homeostasis. Excessive ROS can be targeted using antioxidant therapeutics or ROS responsive delivery systems to reduce oxidative damage and preserve chondrocyte viability. Acidic microenvironments and matrix degrading enzymes may serve as endogenous triggers for pH responsive or enzyme responsive release of matrix protective factors. Abnormal mechanical loading may be addressed through mechanically adaptive, piezoelectric, or stress responsive microspheres capable of regulating mechanotransduction and matrix remodeling. In addition, pathological processes such as cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated autophagy, ferroptosis, synovitis, and subchondral bone remodeling should be considered when selecting metabolism regulating, immunomodulatory, anti-senescent, or osteochondral regenerative cargos.

Collectively, this framework highlights the transition from defect centered repair toward disease oriented microenvironmental regulation. Rather than functioning solely as structural fillers, next-generation microsphere systems should be designed to provide sustained, multi target, and disease adaptive therapeutic interventions that address the complex and evolving pathophysiology of OA (Table 3).

Functional improvement of microspheres and synergistic integration with adjunct therapies

Beyond delivery, functionalized microspheres can be further integrated with clinical procedures, tissue engineering constructs, nanomedicine platforms, and paracrine regulatory systems. This higher level of integration embodies the concept of systematization proposed in this review. While functionalization focuses on endowing microspheres with specific biological, structural, or responsive properties, systematization emphasizes the coordinated integration of these functions within broader therapeutic platforms designed to address complex regenerative challenge. Within this framework, microspheres no longer function solely as isolated drug depots or cell carriers. Instead, they serve as modular building blocks that can be combined with scaffolds, biofabrication technologies, surgical interventions, and advanced delivery systems to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects. Through such integration, structural support, biological signaling, immune regulation, cell recruitment, and spatiotemporally controlled delivery can be orchestrated in a coordinated manner to enhance cartilage regeneration and disease modification. Consequently, systematized microsphere platforms represent an important step toward multifunctional and clinically translatable regenerative strategies for cartilage repair and osteoarthritis treatment.

Integration with clinical microfracture procedures

Microfracture has become a first-line treatment for cartilage repair due to its simplicity, low cost, and rapid short-term efficacy [208,209]. However, the resulting fibrocartilage differs significantly from native hyaline cartilage and exhibits poor long-term outcomes. This limitation is primarily attributed to the lack of an appropriate in vivo microenvironment, comprising biochemical cues and mechanical support, for recruiting, anchoring, and directing BMSCs toward hyaline cartilage differentiation [210]. To address this issue, Liu et al. developed biomimetic microspheres containing decellularized cartilage extracellular matrix (BCAMM), and these microspheres effectively promote the chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs released through microfracture and significantly enhance the quality of tissue regeneration [211]. Chen et al. developed SIM@CACM microspheres with excellent injectability, strong adhesion, efficient BMSC recruitment capability, and sustained release of simvastatin and matrix signaling molecules, demonstrating a significant cartilage repair effect in vivo (Fig. 12a) [210].

Overall, the integration of microsphere systems with microfracture procedures represents a clinically relevant example of systematized cartilage repair. By combining the marrow stimulation effect of microfracture with the sustained biological regulation provided by microspheres, this strategy can partially overcome the intrinsic limitations of conventional microfracture treatment. In particular, microspheres can serve as localized reservoirs of biochemical signals and adhesive matrices that enhance BMSC recruitment, promote chondrogenic differentiation, and improve the quality of reparative tissue.

Despite these advantages, the therapeutic benefits of this approach remain largely confined to focal cartilage and osteochondral defects, whereas its efficacy in diffuse OA-associated degeneration remains uncertain. In addition, successful clinical implementation requires careful optimization of microsphere retention, degradation kinetics, mechanical stability, and integration with postoperative rehabilitation and joint loading conditions. Future studies should therefore focus on establishing procedure specific design criteria and evaluating long term functional outcomes to maximize the translational potential of microsphere assisted microfracture therapy.

Engineering tissue-like organoids via microsphere assembly

Organoids are three-dimensional microtissues derived from stem or progenitor cells that closely mimic the structure and function of natural cartilage, and their development typically requires the support of 3D scaffolds [213,214]. Shen et al. fabricated RGD-modified silk fibroin-DNA hydrogel microspheres (RSD-MS) using a combination of microfluidic systems, photopolymerization, and self-assembly techniques. These microspheres were further functionalized with Pep-RGDfKA, promoting BMSC proliferation, adhesion, and chondrogenic differentiation. The resulting organoid precursors significantly enhanced cartilage regeneration in vivo (Fig. 12b) [139]. Moreover, a strong correlation exists between cellular senescence levels and the progression of osteoarthritis in patients with cartilage defects. miR-24 is one of several miRNAs highly enriched in synovial mesenchymal stem cell (SMSC)-derived organoids and serves as a potential aging marker in inflammatory conditions. Sun et al. developed a composite hydrogel system incorporating miR-24-loaded microspheres and SMSC organoids, and this system effectively improved cartilage defects in an OA environment by enhancing miR-24/TAOK1 signaling pathways, offering a novel strategy for targeting cellular aging (Fig. 12c) [212].

Taken together, microsphere assisted organoid construction represents an advanced stage of microsphere functionalization and systematization by integrating biomaterial support with organized living cellular assemblies. Compared with conventional cell loaded microspheres, these systems can provide more tissue like regenerative units with enhanced cell-cell communication, extracellular matrix production, and paracrine regulation. As a result, they offer a promising strategy for recapitulating key features of native cartilage development and regeneration.

Despite their therapeutic potential, the increasing biological complexity of organoid based systems also introduces substantial translational challenges. Factors including cell source variability, organoid maturation control, nutrient and oxygen transport, storage stability, immunogenicity, manufacturing scalability, and quality standardization remain important barriers to clinical application. Future development should therefore focus on balancing biological sophistication with reproducibility, scalability, and regulatory feasibility. Achieving this balance will be essential for establishing next generation organoid microsphere platforms as clinically translatable regenerative systems.

Hybrid nanomedicine approaches and paracrine regulation

Feng et al. developed SponGel MS, a hybrid system combining sponge scaffolds with hydrogel microspheres capable of releasing MnO2@EGCG. This formulation effectively scavenges ROS, suppresses inflammation, releases SDF-1 to recruit stem cells, and creates a favorable microenvironment for cell differentiation, thereby significantly promoting cartilage regeneration (Fig. 13a) [215]. Xu et al. constructed DIC/Mg-PDA@HM microspheres that co-deliver diclofenac and Mg2+ ions, exerting anti-inflammatory effects, promoting chondrogenic differentiation, modulating macrophage phenotypes, and improving the overall cartilage regeneration microenvironment [217].

Paracrine signaling is a key mechanism through which MSCs facilitate tissue regeneration. However, adverse microenvironments can impair paracrine function and induce apoptosis. To overcome this challenge, Li et al. developed porous GelMA microspheres (GMs) loaded with PDGF-BB. The porous structure and mechanical strength of GMs allowed exogenous stem cells to adhere and proliferate, enhancing cell-matrix interactions and paracrine signaling. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that cell-laden GMs enhanced secretion, reduced inflammation, and slowed OA progression by engaging both exogenous and endogenous MSCs (Fig. 13b) [216].

In summary, hybrid nanomedicine and paracrine regulating microsphere systems extend the role of microspheres beyond localized therapeutic delivery toward comprehensive microenvironmental regulation. By integrating nanoparticles, bioactive molecules, extracellular vesicles, ions, or paracrine signaling mediators within a single platform, these systems can simultaneously modulate multiple pathological processes, including oxidative stress, inflammation, immune imbalance, impaired cell recruitment, and dysfunctional intercellular communication. Such multifunctional capabilities make them promising candidates for disease modifying interventions in cartilage repair and osteoarthritis treatment.

More broadly, these synergistic strategies illustrate the transition from functionalized microspheres as individual therapeutic carriers to integrated regenerative platforms. Through combination with extracellular matrix components, exosomes, microfracture procedures, organoid technologies, nanomedicine systems, or paracrine regulatory approaches, microspheres can support higher level functions, including microenvironment reconstruction, stem cell recruitment, immunomodulation, oxidative stress control, metabolic regulation, and spatiotemporally controlled therapeutic delivery. These capabilities are closely aligned with the concept of smart functionalized microspheres, particularly when multiple biological and physical signals can be coordinated in a disease adaptive manner.

However, increasing system complexity also introduces substantial translational challenges. The incorporation of diverse functional components may affect degradation behavior, release profiles, immune interactions, long term retention, and overall safety. Additional concerns include component compatibility, release sequence optimization, mechanical durability, degradation product safety, batch to batch reproducibility, sterilization, storage stability, repeated administration feasibility, and clinically relevant outcome assessment. Future development should therefore focus not only on expanding functionality but also on identifying combinations that are necessary, safe, reproducible, and clinically meaningful.

This principle of context dependent design is especially important when distinguishing focal cartilage defect repair from OA intervention. Focal cartilage defects are typically localized lesions resulting from trauma, surgery, or limited osteochondral injury, and treatment primarily aims to achieve defect filling, tissue integration, cartilage like matrix formation, and restoration of local mechanical function. In contrast, OA is a chronic whole joint disease characterized by persistent inflammation, cartilage degradation, oxidative stress, abnormal mechanical loading, subchondral bone remodeling, and progressive chondrocyte dysfunction. Consequently, microspheres designed for focal defect repair should prioritize stable defect retention, structural support, cell recruitment, chondrogenic induction, and degradation kinetics matched to tissue regeneration. By comparison, OA oriented microspheres should emphasize prolonged intra articular residence, controlled or stimuli responsive release, feasibility of repeated administration, and sustained regulation of inflammatory, catabolic, oxidative, and metabolic pathways.

Taken together, these distinctions highlight that microsphere design should be guided by disease specific pathology and therapeutic objectives rather than by material properties alone. Parameters including degradation kinetics, therapeutic cargo selection, release duration, tissue retention, dosing strategy, and efficacy endpoints should therefore be tailored to the biological requirements of each clinical setting (Table 4).

Structural innovations in microsphere design

Traditional microspheres are typically characterized by a smooth surface, dense internal structure, and low porosity, features that impose significant constraints on cell adhesion and migration, drug loading efficiency, and controlled release kinetics [23,24,218]. To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed various structural optimization strategies, including macroporous microspheres, core-shell microspheres, and nanofiber microspheres, to enhance the functional properties of these materials in cartilage repair.

The introduction of a macroporous structure can substantially increase the specific surface area of microspheres, thereby promoting cell adhesion and migration. It also provides space for cell growth and tissue formation, as well as facilitates the loading and sustained release of bioactive factors [23]. More importantly, the interconnected pore network provides essential pathways for three-dimensional cell infiltration and proliferation, while facilitating the efficient exchange of nutrients and metabolic waste. This overcomes the key limitation of traditional microspheres, where cell distribution is primarily confined to the surface. For example, Qu et al. fabricated PLGA microspheres (OPM) with open channels ranging from 7 to 18 μm through alkali treatment. These microspheres optimized the three-dimensional cellular microenvironment, enhanced cartilage matrix deposition by improving cell infiltration, and accelerated degradation. They demonstrated staged repair advantages, supporting early chondrogenesis and later maturation in animal experiments (Fig. 14a) [219].

Core-shell microspheres (CS-MS) consist of a distinct core and shell structure, enabling precise and sequential release of bioactive molecules by modulating material composition and structural design [222]. The principal advantage of this architecture lies in its capacity for spatiotemporal control over therapeutic delivery. By encapsulating functionally diverse agents, such as early-stage anti-inflammatory drugs and late-stage growth factors in the shell and core respectively, CS-MS can deliver programmed responses tailored to the biological demands at different stages of cartilage repair. For instance, Miao et al. developed inflammation-responsive core-shell hydrogel microspheres, with celecoxib in the outer layer for rapid anti-inflammatory action and slow-release ChS in the core to support long-term tissue regeneration, achieving synergistic effects between early inflammation suppression and sustained repair. In vivo experiments have demonstrated that ChsMA + CLX@Lipo@GelMA can prevent osteoarthritic joint injury, thereby reducing osteophyte formation (Fig. 14b) [220]. Min et al. further demonstrated sequential delivery of PDGF-BB and KGN via a bilayer structure, with the former promoting MSC recruitment and proliferation and the latter inducing chondrogenic differentiation and enhancing endogenous repair mechanisms [223]. Additional studies have constructed rhIGF-1 sustained-release systems [224] and bevacizumab/IGF-1 composite delivery platforms [225], further validating the broad potential of core-shell structures in controlled-release and tissue-engineering applications. Moreover, nanofiber microspheres exhibit excellent capabilities in cell and drug loading due to their high specific surface area. Chen et al. fabricated gelatin/polylactide nanofiber microspheres using electrospinning combined with electrospray technology, and further functionalized them via a metal-phenolic network (MPN). This modification endowed the microspheres with anti-inflammatory properties and enhanced their ability to secrete a cartilage matrix (Fig. 14c) [221].

In summary, structural functionalization represents a fundamental strategy for enhancing the therapeutic performance of microsphere systems in cartilage repair. Porous, macroporous, core shell, multilayered, and nanofiber architectures not only optimize microsphere morphology but also establish biomimetic microarchitectures that support cell adhesion, migration, nutrient transport, drug loading, controlled release, and extracellular matrix deposition. Among these designs, porous and macroporous microspheres facilitate cell infiltration and mass transport, core shell and multilayered structures enable staged or sequential delivery of multiple therapeutics, and nanofiber microspheres better recapitulate the fibrous features of the native extracellular matrix and promote cell matrix interactions. Beyond improving delivery performance, these structural characteristics provide a physical basis for microenvironmental regulation and advanced functionalization. By controlling spatial organization, transport behavior, and cell material interactions, structural design can influence biological responses and enhance the effectiveness of regenerative therapies. As such, structural functionalization serves as an important bridge between conventional delivery systems and more sophisticated smart microsphere platforms.

Despite these advantages, increasing structural complexity also introduces significant challenges, including fabrication reproducibility, particle size uniformity, mechanical stability, burst release control, degradation matching, pore collapse, and long-term material retention. Future studies should therefore optimize key structural parameters, including pore size, shell thickness, fiber density, stiffness, and degradation kinetics, while systematically evaluating their effects on release behavior, cell fate regulation, tissue integration, and long term safety across different cartilage related repair scenarios.

Translational insights: From articular cartilage repair to cartilaginous endplate regeneration

Although functionalized microspheres have been extensively investigated for articular cartilage repair, their application in CEP regeneration remains relatively underexplored, representing an important unmet need in current IVDD therapies [39,65]. As discussed above, the CEP is not merely another form of articular cartilage. Instead, it performs distinct functions in nutrient diffusion, metabolic waste exchange, mechanical buffering, calcification regulation, and NP-CEP crosstalk [62,63,68]. Consequently, microsphere strategies validated in articular cartilage defect models should be regarded as sources of design inspiration rather than direct evidence for CEP repair. CEP targeted microsphere systems must therefore be developed and evaluated according to the unique biological, anatomical, and mechanical characteristics of the disc endplate interface.

Several concepts derived from articular cartilage repair may nevertheless provide useful guidance for CEP regeneration, provided that they are adapted and validated in a disease specific manner. First, injectable microspheres may offer a suitable platform for minimally invasive delivery to localized endplate defects, fissures, or disc endplate regions. Compared with bulk scaffolds, microspheres can more readily conform to irregular microspaces and enable spatially confined therapeutic delivery while minimizing disruption of the native NP and annulus fibrosus architecture [23,24]. However, the delivery route, spatial distribution, and retention behavior of microspheres within the disc endplate unit differ substantially from those observed in intra articular or focal cartilage defect applications. In IVDD related models, dual antioxidant TBA@Gel&Chs hydrogel microspheres loaded with NP cells have been reported to enhance disc repair and prevent NP collapse, supporting the technical feasibility of microsphere-based delivery within the disc environment [226]. Nevertheless, these findings should be considered preliminary evidence for disc-oriented applications rather than definitive validation of CEP regeneration.

Second, CEP degeneration is closely associated with reduced permeability, calcification, pore occlusion, and impaired nutrient transport [39,66]. Accordingly, diffusion enhancing microspheres may represent a rational design strategy for CEP targeted regeneration. Porous, macroporous, or nanofiber microspheres could be engineered to mimic the porous extracellular matrix, facilitate local fluid exchange, and improve oxygen and glucose transport [219,221]. Importantly, structural features that enhance cell infiltration in articular cartilage repair may not necessarily restore CEP permeability. Within the CEP, excessive material retention, pore obstruction, or inappropriate degradation could further compromise nutrient diffusion. Therefore, future CEP oriented microsphere systems should evaluate not only matrix restoration but also permeability recovery, calcification status, and NP nutritional homeostasis.

Third the degenerative CEP microenvironment is characterized by inflammation, oxidative stress, acidic pH, matrix degrading enzymes, abnormal mineralization, and altered mechanical loading [67,227,228]. These pathological features provide opportunities for the development of stimuli responsive or mechanically adaptive microspheres. For example, pH or ROS responsive systems may selectively release antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, or anti calcification agents under degenerative conditions, whereas enzyme responsive systems may provide matrix protective release in response to elevated catabolic activity. Recent IVDD related studies support this rationale. GM@mTG-V microspheres have been shown to reconstruct disc biomechanics and modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, thereby promoting NP regeneration and preserving endplate integrity (Fig. 15a) [229]. Hydrogen ion capturing GMNP microspheres can stabilize the acidic microenvironment, inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and enhance extracellular matrix synthesis in IVDD (Fig. 15b) [230]. Similarly, IL-1ra loaded ChS functionalized microspheres have been reported to suppress inflammation and restore extracellular matrix metabolic balance (Fig. 15c) [231]. More recently, internal friction network piezoelectric hydrogel microspheres composed of piezoelectric bismuth ferrite nanoparticles and sliding ring functionalized HAMA hydrogels achieved physiological electrical adaptation under dynamic loading conditions. In IVDD models, these microspheres restored the local electrical microenvironment, activated PINK1/Parkin mediated mitophagy, reduced NP cell apoptosis, and delayed disc degeneration [232]. Collectively, these studies provide encouraging disc and IVDD related evidence, although direct CEP specific validation remains scarce.

Fourth, core shell and multilayered microspheres may offer advantages for staged CEP regeneration. During the early stages of degeneration, rapid release of anti-inflammatory or antioxidant agents may help attenuate catabolic injury. At later stages, sustained delivery of matrix restorative, anti-calcification, or chondrogenic factors may support preservation of CEP structure and maintenance of NP CEP crosstalk. Similar sequential delivery strategies have been explored in articular cartilage repair using core shell and bilayer microsphere systems [220,223]. Nevertheless, their applicability to CEP regeneration remains uncertain because the mechanical environment of the disc endplate unit, particularly axial compression and shear loading, differs substantially from that of focal articular cartilage defects. Accordingly, CEP targeted microspheres should be evaluated for mechanical stability, tissue adhesion, controlled degradation, and non-obstructive retention within the disc endplate interface.

Overall, functionalized microspheres represent a promising yet still emerging strategy for CEP regeneration. While existing IVDD related studies provide encouraging preliminary evidence, many CEP specific applications remain largely conceptual. Future investigations should establish CEP focused disease models and systematically evaluate microsphere distribution, retention, degradation behavior, permeability restoration, anti-calcification efficacy, NP CEP crosstalk, disc height preservation, Modic like changes, biomechanical performance, and long term safety. More broadly, extending microsphere technologies from articular cartilage repair to CEP regeneration requires a shift from tissue centered repair toward interface-oriented regeneration. By restoring the structural, transport, and signaling functions of the disc endplate unit, next generation microsphere systems may provide new opportunities for disease modifying IVDD therapies and expand the therapeutic scope of smart functionalized microspheres beyond conventional articular cartilage repair (Fig. 15d).

Conclusion and future perspectives

In summary, advances in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine have established functionalized microspheres as versatile platforms for cartilage related repair and intervention. Beyond serving as carriers for localized delivery and controlled release of therapeutic agents, including small molecules, growth factors, cytokines, nucleic acids, exosomes, and cells, microspheres have progressively evolved from passive delivery depots into multifunctional regenerative systems. Through the integration of biological, structural, and responsive properties, these platforms can modulate key pathological processes associated with cartilage degeneration and repair, including inflammation, oxidative stress, metabolic dysfunction, extracellular matrix breakdown, and impaired tissue regeneration. As a result, functionalized microspheres have emerged as promising tools for improving the precision and biological efficacy of cartilage regeneration and OA intervention.

The therapeutic value of microspheres depends strongly on disease context, functional design, and translational feasibility. For focal cartilage defect repair, microspheres primarily contribute to defect filling, local retention, cell recruitment, chondrogenic induction, and cartilage-like matrix formation. In contrast, microspheres for osteoarthritis intervention should provide sustained anti-inflammatory, anti-catabolic, antioxidant, and multi-target regulation of the whole-joint degenerative microenvironment. When integrated with acellular matrices, exosomes, clinical microfracture procedures, organoid systems, composite nanomedicine, or paracrine regulatory platforms, microspheres can be further developed into systematized regenerative platforms. Structural innovations, including macroporous, core-shell, multilayered, and nanofiber architectures, also provide a physical basis for enhanced cell adhesion, nutrient diffusion, drug loading, sequential release, and matrix deposition. Although these advances offer valuable design principles for underexplored applications such as cartilaginous endplate regeneration, they should be regarded as conceptual guidance rather than direct evidence for CEP repair. Therefore, future development of functionalized microspheres should focus not only on achieving smarter and more multifunctional designs, but also on addressing disease-specific validation, manufacturing standardization, preclinical evidence quality, long-term biosafety, and regulatory translation.

Emerging smart microsphere systems: Stimuli responsiveness, intelligent delivery, and multifunctional platforms

Stimuli-responsive microspheres for pathological microenvironment sensing

The next generation of functionalized microspheres is expected to evolve from passive delivery depots into smart and adaptive regenerative systems. A key direction is the development of stimuli responsive microspheres capable of sensing pathological changes within the cartilage microenvironment. Degenerative cartilage is characterized by acidic pH, ROS, inflammatory cytokines, matrix degrading enzymes, and abnormal mechanical stress [233]. These disease associated signals can serve as endogenous triggers for controlled therapeutic release. For example, pH or ROS responsive microspheres may selectively release antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, or matrix protective agents under degenerative conditions, whereas enzyme responsive systems may respond to elevated matrix metalloproteinase activity and provide localized protection against extracellular matrix degradation. Such approaches have the potential to improve therapeutic precision while minimizing unnecessary drug exposure to healthy tissues.

Intelligent delivery systems with spatiotemporal control

Another promising direction is the development of intelligent delivery systems with spatiotemporal control. Unlike conventional microspheres with predetermined release profiles, advanced systems may be designed to dynamically respond to changes in the local microenvironment and adjust therapeutic output accordingly. Such feedback regulated delivery strategies could better accommodate the sequential requirements of cartilage repair. Early-stage intervention may benefit from rapid anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, whereas later stages may require sustained chondrogenic stimulation, matrix remodeling, and functional tissue integration. Core-shell, multilayered, and multicompartment microspheres therefore provide attractive platforms for sequential or programmable delivery of multiple therapeutic agents.

Multifunctional regenerative platforms beyond simple carriers

The development of multifunctional regenerative platforms represents a further step toward systematized microsphere-based therapies. Rather than acting solely as drug or growth factor carriers, advanced microsphere systems may actively regulate the regenerative microenvironment through the coordinated integration of bioactive molecule delivery, cell recruitment, immunomodulation, metabolic regulation, mechanobiological stimulation, and structural support. In this context, microspheres may function as modular regenerative units rather than passive carriers. Furthermore, integration with hydrogels, decellularized matrices, exosomes, nanomedicine systems, organoid assemblies, or clinical microfracture procedures may further enhance therapeutic efficacy. Such multifunctional platforms could provide comprehensive solutions for complex cartilage lesions and may eventually be adapted for cartilaginous endplate regeneration in intervertebral disc degeneration.

Optimization and evaluation of advanced microsphere systems

The successful translation of these advanced microsphere systems will require rigorous optimization and evaluation. Future studies should systematically define the relationships among microsphere composition, particle size, degradation behavior, stimulus responsiveness, release kinetics, mechanical properties, and biological performance. Computational modeling, high throughput screening, and standardized in vivo assessment may facilitate the identification of optimal combinations of materials, therapeutic cargos, and delivery strategies. Through continued refinement and validation, stimuli responsive microspheres, intelligent delivery systems, and multifunctional regenerative platforms may emerge as key technologies for precise and personalized cartilage repair.

Overcoming barriers to clinical translation

Standardization, scale-up manufacturing, and quality control

Standardization and scalable manufacturing are essential for translating functionalized microspheres from laboratory prototypes into clinically applicable products. Although microfluidics, emulsion-based fabrication, spray drying, electrospray, and phase separation methods can produce microspheres with controlled structures, scale-up remains challenging. Key quality attributes, including particle size distribution, morphology, drug loading efficiency, encapsulation efficiency, burst release, release kinetics, degradation rate, mechanical stability, injectability, tissue adhesion, and residual solvent or crosslinker content, must be carefully controlled to ensure batch-to-batch reproducibility and safety. Different fabrication methods also present distinct barriers: microfluidics provides excellent monodispersity but limited throughput, emulsion methods are scalable but may yield broader size distributions and surfactant residues, and spray drying is efficient but may damage heat-sensitive bioactive molecules. For growth factor-loaded, exosome-loaded, cell-laden, or nanoparticle-containing microspheres, manufacturing becomes even more complex because biological activity, sterility, structural integrity, and release behavior must all be preserved simultaneously.

Quality-by-design strategies can help define critical material attributes, process parameters, and product specifications prior to scale-up. In addition, sterilization and storage stability should be evaluated for each microsphere system, as gamma irradiation, ethylene oxide, filtration, or aseptic processing may alter degradation behavior, bioactivity, or release profiles. From a regulatory perspective, multifunctional microspheres may be classified as combination products involving biomaterials, drugs, biologics, nanoparticles, or living cells; therefore, GMP-compatible production, sterility and endotoxin testing, stability testing, degradation-product analysis, predefined batch release criteria, and cost-effective manufacturing are required before clinical translation.

Preclinical evidence and model selection

Although many microsphere-based systems have shown encouraging in vitro and in vivo results, the quality and translational relevance of preclinical evidence vary substantially across studies. Small animal models, including mouse and rat models, are widely used for early screening because they are economical, easy to operate, and compatible with molecular and histological analysis. However, their articular cartilage is thin, joint loading conditions differ from those in humans, and surgically or chemically induced models may not fully recapitulate chronic human disease. Rabbit models provide larger cartilage surfaces and are frequently used for cartilage defect and osteochondral repair studies, but differences in cartilage thickness, spontaneous healing capacity, and joint biomechanics remain compared with humans. Large animal models, such as sheep and pig models, better approximate human joint size, cartilage architecture, and load-bearing conditions, as well as surgical feasibility. Nevertheless, they are more costly, require longer follow-up, and show greater variability in defect creation, postoperative loading, and outcome assessment. Therefore, promising microsphere systems should ideally progress from in vitro validation and small animal screening to large animal studies prior to clinical translation.

Outcome measures and cross-study comparability

Another limitation of the current evidence is the heterogeneity of outcome measures. Focal cartilage defect studies commonly evaluate defect filling, integration with native cartilage, histological repair quality, type II collagen and aggrecan deposition, and biomechanical properties. In contrast, osteoarthritis studies more often focus on OARSI scores, synovial inflammation, osteophyte formation, inflammatory cytokines, cartilage matrix degradation, pain-related behavior, and subchondral bone remodeling. Imaging modalities, including MRI, micro-CT, and gross morphology, provide structural information, whereas histological scoring systems, such as ICRS- or OARSI-related scales, remain central for evaluating repair quality and degeneration severity. However, many studies still rely mainly on short-term histology and matrix marker staining, while biomechanical testing, long-term functional assessment, and subchondral bone evaluation are less consistently reported. Future studies should adopt disease-context-specific endpoint panels and report histological, imaging, molecular, biomechanical, and safety outcomes in a more standardized manner.

Long-term biosafety and material clearance

Long-term safety remains a critical barrier to clinical translation. Most microsphere systems are evaluated over short- or intermediate-term follow-up periods, which may be insufficient to determine whether regenerated tissue remains durable or whether degradation products are fully cleared. Follow-up beyond 12 months may be particularly important for slow-degrading polymers, composite microspheres, cell-laden systems, and nanoparticle-containing formulations. Potential safety concerns include immune reactions, synovial irritation, macrophage uptake, chronic inflammation, ectopic tissue formation, altered joint homeostasis, and systemic exposure to released drugs or degradation products. These risks vary across material systems. For example, PLGA microspheres require attention to acidic degradation products and burst release, natural polymer-based microspheres require evaluation of batch variability and immunogenicity, and composite or nanoparticle-loaded microspheres require careful assessment of long-term retention and off-target distribution. Therefore, safety evaluation should extend beyond cell viability and local histology to include inflammatory markers, synovial response, macrophage infiltration, degradation-product clearance, organ distribution when applicable, and long-term joint function.

Translational candidates and regulatory considerations

At present, most regenerative microsphere systems for cartilage repair remain in the preclinical stage. In contrast, clinically used intra-articular long-acting formulations for osteoarthritis mainly aim to prolong symptomatic drug exposure rather than regenerate cartilage. This distinction is important, as regenerative microspheres often integrate biomaterials, active pharmaceutical ingredients, growth factors, nucleic acids, nanoparticles, or living cells, placing them closer to combination products than conventional drug depots. Representative late-preclinical candidates include PLGA-based sustained release systems, gelatin or GelMA microspheres for cytokine or cell delivery, hyaluronic acid-based exosome or growth factor delivery platforms, chitosan-based adhesive and immunomodulatory microspheres, silk fibroin-based organoid-supporting systems, and composite microspheres integrating ROS scavenging, macrophage regulation, and stem cell recruitment. For these systems, translational evaluation should be closely linked to specific material properties and manufacturing processes, including GMP-compatible preparation, sterilization, storage stability, batch-to-batch reproducibility, particle size control, loading efficiency, release consistency, degradation product safety, and cost-effectiveness. A clearer connection among material type, functional complexity, regulatory classification, and clinical indication will be essential for advancing microsphere-based cartilage therapies from laboratory constructs toward clinically viable products.

Expanding research horizons

Current studies primarily focus on inducing chondrogenic differentiation to promote ECM production, often overlooking the long-term structural integrity and organization of the resulting ECM in engineered cartilage tissue. In addition, although degeneration of CEP is widely recognized as a key initiating factor in IVDD, current microsphere-based repair strategies predominantly target the nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus, with relatively limited exploration of CEP regeneration. Future research should strategically leverage the injectability and localized delivery capabilities of microspheres to pioneer novel therapeutic interventions specifically targeting CEP regeneration, offering new perspectives for the prevention and treatment of IVDD through cartilage endplate repair.

In conclusion, only by systematically addressing these key challenges in basic science, manufacturing scalability, and clinical validation can functionalized microspheres transition from sophisticated laboratory constructs to clinically viable, reliable, and accessible therapies for cartilage regeneration, ultimately offering transformative benefits to patients suffering from osteoarthritis and IVDD.
