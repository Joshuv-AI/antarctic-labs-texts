Air pollution and the mystery of high household savings in China
Authors: Jingwen Ding, Dandan Fan, Yuxia Guo, Qingqing Ning
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Despite consumption being a key measure of residential welfare, the relationship of the residential savings rates, an inverse proxy for household consumption, with air pollution is unclear. This study empirically examines the impact of air pollution on household savings rates, and the underlying mechanism. The results reveal that worsening air pollution significantly increases household savings rates; this finding holds under alternative variable specifications and employing instrumental variables to address endogeneity issues. The mechanism analysis demonstrates that air pollution enhances household savings rates through health risks. Heterogeneity analysis indicates that in developed areas with more comprehensive public medical service systems, the positive effect of air pollution on household savings rates weakens in second-tier cities and above as well as in high-income areas. Furthermore, improvements in commercial insurance coverage mitigates the positive effect of air pollution on household savings rates. Overall, this study explains the high savings phenomenon among Chinese households from an environmental perspective. The Chinese government should encourage the use of renewable energy, improve air pollution control, and reduce the inhibitory effects of air pollution on residential consumption.

Introduction

Consumption is a core factor affecting residents' welfare and driving economic growth. At the micro level, happiness is each human's eternal pursuit. Consumption is a part of this pursuit. An increase in consumption often promotes an increase in residents' utility, thereby increasing residents' happiness [1]. At the macro level, residential consumption is an important driver of economic growth [2]. A theory based on demand-led innovation suggests that a decline in residential consumption demand leads firms to invest less in innovation and a subsequent slowdown in economic growth [3]. Among the world's top ten major economies, China's resident consumption rate (consumer spending/expenditure method of GDP) ranks at the bottom, declining from 48.8 % in 1995 to approximately 37.2 % in 2022, which is approximately 17 percentage points below the world's average [4]. Indeed, Chinese households' high savings rate has long been a topic of discussion [5]. Modigliani [6] called this phenomenon as the mystery of China's residential savings. Although many studies have attempted to explain the high savings in Chinese households from different perspectives, few have examined the mechanisms driving this from an ecological perspective.

The environment significantly affects physical and mental health. According to Roser, approximately seven million deaths per year worldwide are attributed to air pollution [7]. Like other BRICS countries, China's early economic growth model was characterized by high energy consumption, emissions, and pollution, resulting in environmental degradation [8], particularly declining air quality and frequent haze.

In 2013, nearly 100 cities in 25 provinces of China were seriously affected by hazy weather, triggering widespread social concern. Consequently, in the same year, the State Council of China promulgated the Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Air Pollution. As shown in Fig. 1, in 2012, PM2.5 concentrations exceeded 40 μg/m3 in most provinces in China's central and southern regions. These regions house more than 80 % of the country's population. Encouragingly, China's air pollution has decreased in recent years, with an average PM2.5 concentration of 29 μg/m3 in 339 cities across the country. However, this level is still well above the World Health Organization's air quality standard of 10 μg/m3, according to the China Ecological and Environmental Status Bulletin 2022.Fig. 1p.m.2.5 concentration by region in China (2012–2018).Fig. 1

Air pollution has significant negative effects on physical and mental health. For every 1.54 % increase in PM2.5 concentration, overweight and obesity rates rise by 0.82 and 0.27 percentage points, respectively [9]. Increased air pollution also leads to decreased life expectancy [10]. Pathological studies show that air pollution is strongly associated with respiratory infections, cardiovascular diseases, and strokes [11,12]. Scholars also provide evidence that air pollution significantly and negatively affects mental health [13,14].

Along these lines, we predict that air pollution will significantly affect consumer-saving behavior. If this effect does exist, what are the underlying mechanisms? While China is facing the problem of air pollution, its aging population and inadequate social security systems are equally prominent issue. According to the National Bureau of Statistics of China, China had 280 million people aged 60 years and older in 2022, accounting for approximately 19.8 % of the total population. The proportion of this population is expected to exceed 30 % by 2035. Meanwhile, China's health insurance coverage level is relatively low, with the actual proportions of hospitalization costs paid for in China's basic health insurance for employees, and urban and rural residents in 2019 being 75.6 % and 59.7 %, respectively, and the proportions of individual payments being 24.4 % and 40.3 %, respectively. On the one hand, the older adult population is more susceptible to air pollution, which induces various underlying diseases [15], leading to increased health risks. On the other hand, the imperfections of China's social security system often make it difficult for ordinary resident families to support the burden of high medical expenditures. Then, based on the precautionary savings theory, precautionary savings to cope with health will rise further under an imperfect social security system. Specifically, the intensification of air pollution will raise the level of residents' savings rate through health risks [16].

Rationality and novelty of the study

First, China offers an interesting research setting, especially for understanding changes in residential saving rates in developing economies. Most developing countries face problems related to environmental deterioration and imperfect social security systems. Rising air pollution is likely to increase residential savings rates through health risks and reduce their welfare. Here, we use the panel fixed effects model and instrumental variable method to explore the effect of air pollution on residential savings rates, and the underlying mechanism. We find that the intensification of air pollution significantly increases the residential savings rates, with health risk acting as a key mechanism.

Second, our research complements studies on the causes of the Chinese residents' savings mystery. Studies mainly explain the increase in the household savings rate of Chinese residents from the perspective of population aging [17], children's gender [18], and income uncertainty [19]. The closest link to our work is Li and Wang, who focused on the impact of air pollution on residents' consumption [20]. Healthcare expenditure is essentially an investment in human capital rather than consumption [21]. An increase in air pollution increases households' healthcare expenditure. If healthcare is included in consumption, the negative impact of air pollution on resident consumption may be underestimated. Indeed, we show that air pollution negatively affects Chinese households' savings rate using a panel data model and instrumental variables.

Finally, this study complements research related to the economic consequences of air pollution. The literature has focused on the perspectives of corporate human capital structure [22], corporate ESG performance [23], stock price volatility [24], and household electricity consumption [25]. Our findings further expand the field of research on the economic effects of air pollution. Furthermore, policymakers should promote an increase in resident consumption by reducing environmental pollution and promoting sustainable development.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the literature review. Section 3 outlines the data and empirical research design. Section 4 discusses the empirical results and analysis. Finally, section 5 presents the conclusion and policy recommendations of this study.

Literature review

Economic growth and environmental pollution

China's rapid economic growth has been accompanied by ecological degradation. Economic development and environmental quality are closely related. In the early stages of economic development, the level of environmental pollution gradually increases with per capita income. However, when it reaches a certain critical point, a further increase in per capita income reduces the level of environmental pollution, and the quality of the environment improves and recovers. Grossman and Krueger [26] summarized the relationship between environmental pollution and economic growth using the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC). Many studies have empirically tested this assertion. For instance, the use of fossil fuel energy exacerbates the emission of pollutants owing to an overemphasis on economic growth [22], whereas the negative externalities that economic growth produces in the ecosystem are often ignored [27].

Jahanger et al. [28] emonstrated an inverted U-shaped relationship between economic growth and carbon emissions, supporting the EKC hypothesis. An increase in environmental pollution is also related to recreational activities. For instance, tourism development increases carbon emissions to some extent [29]. In addition to economic development and increased energy demand, changes in environmental quality may also be associated with energy price trends. For example, using panel data for Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka from 1990 to 2018, Murshed and Tanha [30] identified a nonlinear relationship between crude oil prices and renewable energy use, with rising crude oil prices initially unfavorable to renewable energy consumption; however, at a later stage, higher crude oil prices are associated with higher levels of renewable energy consumption.

From a policy perspective, numerous studies have advocated using renewable energy sources to decouple economic growth from fossil energy use, thereby contributing to the reduction of environmental pollution and carbon emissions [31,32]. Focusing on Turkey, Karaaslan and Camkaya [33] empirically tested whether the use of unclean energy significantly increases the level of CO2 emissions, while the use of clean energy contributes to the restoration of environmental quality. Furthermore, nuclear energy can be an important means of reducing environmental pollution [34,35]. Saidi and Omri [36] examined the short- and long-run impacts of renewable and nuclear energy consumption on CO2 emissions in 15 Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development countries over the 1990–2018 period. The authors found that nuclear energy use can reduce total global carbon emissions by about 15 % by 2050. Based on quantile regression, Jahanger et al. [27] analyze data from 1990 to 2017 for major nuclear energy countries and found that nuclear energy is an effective alternative to traditional fossil energy sources; further, the spread of information and communication technologies plays a crucial role in improving environmental quality. However, the use of nuclear energy requires complex technical support and safety prerequisites, increasing the public's hesitancy about using it in many countries [37].

Health risks and population consumption

Health risk is a key component of background risk that cannot be optimized over time through savings or reduced through portfolio diversification [38]. CCAPM theory suggests that health significantly affects asset allocation. The related literature in the area of consumption attempts to analyze the impact of health risk on consumption [39]. As an important component of human capital, a deterioration in health leads to a decline in income and inhibits household consumer spending besides the healthcare-related impacts [40]. Exposure to health-risk shocks creates an incentive for precautionary savings to increase, prompting households to hold more secure assets to cope with future uncertainties [41]. Ren and Zheng [42] found that residential households' precautionary savings motive increases significantly to cope with future health risks and income uncertainty in the event of a new crown outbreak. Wang and He [43] empirically showed that negative health shocks have a significant dampening effect on residents' consumption; however, the Internet can mitigate the negative impact of health uncertainty on consumption by smoothing consumption.

Another strand of the literature analyzes the impact of health insurance on consumption, contributing to the understanding of the relationship between health risks and people's consumption [44]. Crainich et al. [39] empirically examined the impact of the NRCMS on people's savings rates and found that the health plan significantly reduces the savings rate in affluent and middle-income groups. Meanwhile, Yi et al. [21]empirically found that commercial health insurance allocation promotes consumer spending mainly by reducing the level of household precautionary savings and mitigating health-risk-induced precautionary savings motives. Using the difference-in-differences method, Hu et al. [40]found that the integration of urban and rural residents' health insurance in China can unleash residents' consumption potential by reducing health risk shocks.

Air pollution and household consumption

The literature on the impact of air pollution on residential consumption focuses on three levels: expenditure on medical and protective goods, external consumption, and household electricity consumption. First, deteriorating air quality not only increases the sales of supplies such as masks and air purifiers, but also raises overall healthcare costs [45]. Based on an analysis of data from China's Urban Household Survey, Yang and Zhang [46] found that for every 1 % decrease in PM2.5, residential household healthcare expenditures decreased by 2.94 %. Similarly, Barwick et al. [47] empirically analyzed air pollution and bank card transaction data, and found that an increase in PM2 by five concentrations increased healthcare expenditures.

Second, in terms of consumption outside the home, He et al. [48] used data on movie box offices in major Chinese cities from to 2012–2014 and found that air pollution reduces the number of moviegoers by an average of 3 % and box office revenues by an average of 4 %. Using data on air quality and sales at large retail shops in Korea, Kang et al. [49] found that an increase in air pollution significantly reduces sales. Within this framework, a subset of studies has explored the impact of air pollution on residents' consumption regarding eating out. Chew et al. [50] argued that poor air conditions reduce individuals' pro-sociality, suggesting that people's out-of-home socializing activities and related consumption may decline when air quality is poor. Increased air pollution may reduce the frequency of residents' outdoor activities; this can significantly dampen out-of-home dining consumption [51]. Sun et al. [52] empirically found that good air quality can increase the number of out-of-home dining and shopping trips for Beijing residents.

Many studies have shown that air pollution increases electricity consumption [53,54]. The impact of air pollution on people's electricity consumption works through two main channels: On the one hand, air pollution increases the demand for indoor ventilation in households, and thus, increases the electricity consumption of air conditioners or air purifiers [55]. On the other hand, air pollution reduces the demand for travelling and people choose to stay indoors more [56]. [57]. This increases electricity consumption from indoor use. Chen et al. [25] estimated the impact of air pollution on electricity consumption of residential households in prefecture-level cities in China using daily electricity consumption data from 2018 to 2019. The authors found that each standard deviation increase in the daily air quality index increases the average daily electricity consumption of residential households by 0.05 kW-hr.

Overall, we found the following research gaps: (1) Although an extensive literature has analyzed the costs and economic consequences of air pollution, few studies have focused on the impact of air pollution on residential savings rates. (2) Among the driving mechanisms behind the high savings among Chinese residents, studies mainly focus on population aging, the gender of children, and income uncertainty. Few have focused on the impact of ecological changes on residential savings rates. (3) Although some studies have analyzed the impact of air pollution on consumption, they have mainly studied the impact of air pollution on residents’ medical expenditure, out-of-home consumption, and household electricity consumption. The overall effect of air pollution on the savings rate remains unclear, as well as the underlying mechanisms. This study attempts to fill these research gaps, proposing the following hypotheses.H1Air pollution increases residential savings rate.H2Air pollution affects residential savings rate through health risks.

Air pollution increases residential savings rate.

Material and methods

Data

We used data from the China Household Finance Survey (CHFS) released by the Research Center for Household Finance of the Southwestern University of Finance and Economics. The survey data cover 29 provinces and municipalities directly under the Central Government in China (see Fig. 2), providing detailed micro-information on resident families' consumption and savings with broad coverage and high sample representativeness [58]. This large-scale micro survey has been conducted biennially since 2011, providing data for 2011, 2013, 2015, 2017, and 2019. Given the representativeness and scientific sampling of the dataset, this study primarily used data from four survey periods from 2013 to 2019. During data processing, samples from interviewed families with missing values, outliers, or those who refused to answer important questions were excluded. Additionally, because of the presence of many outliers in the savings rate data, we followed the treatment methods of Cai et al. [59] and Li et al. [60], and excluded samples with a savings rate of less than −2. Finally, considering the issue of sampling representativeness, we followed Yi et al. [4]and aggregated the relevant variables in the prefecture-level cities of the interviewed resident families before merging them into the panel data. Air pollution data were obtained from the United States National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA). The annual PM2.5 concentration data for China's prefecture-level cities were computed based on NASA's published data combined with the administrative divisions of China. The per capita GDP data for prefecture-level cities were sourced from the National Bureau of Statistics of the People's Republic of China.Fig. 2Chinese cities Covered by the sample.Fig. 2

Model

At the prefecture-level city scale, utilizing the aggregated data obtained in the previous section, we constructed the following econometric regression equation to test the influence of air pollution on residential savings rate:(1)savingit=α+β1pm2.5it+Xit′β2+γi+yeart+εitsavingit represents the mean savings rate of residents in region i in year t. The residential savings rates can be measured using several methods. However, if education and healthcare expenditures are considered investments in human capital rather than consumption, they should be excluded from the calculation of the savings rate [61]. This study considers this as the dependent variable, while the saving rates measured by other methods are used for robustness checks. The independent variable pm2.5it is the air pollution index. We used the regional PM2.5, concentration as a proxy for air pollution. Xit′ denotes the set of control vectors. Drawing on Gan et al. [62] and Yi et al. [4], our control variables primarily included the proportion of industrial and commercial residential households in the prefecture-level city, credit card penetration rate, average proportion of children, average proportion of older adults, commercial insurance coverage rate, average household income, and average net assets. γi and yeart represent the provincial and time fixed effects, respectively. εit is a random error term. We focused on the coefficient β1. A significantly positive coefficient indicates that air pollution increases household savings. To further examine the mechanism of the impact of air pollution on residential savings rates from a health risk perspective, we constructed the following econometric regression model:(2)savingit=α+β1pm2.5it+β2pm2.5it×healthit+β3health+Xit′β4+γi+yeart+εit

Due to the unreliability of the mediation effects for the mechanism test results [63], we constructed Model (2) using interaction terms for empirical testing. In this model, healthit was the regional residents’ health risks. If air pollution promotes an increase in residential savings rates in areas with higher health risks, then air pollution affects residential savings rates through health risks. In the subsequent empirical analysis, we divided the sample equally into two groups according to the health risks to regional residents: high and low. Mechanism analysis was performed by introducing an interaction term between the health risk dummy and original core explanatory variables.

Variables

Dependent variable. The dependent variable was the average savings rate for households in the region. In the benchmark regression results, we used the savings rate, excluding healthcare and education expenditures, as the dependent variable. This is because education and healthcare expenditures are part of human capital investments (Saving1). In the robustness test, we also considered two other measures of the savings rate: 1) one included healthcare and education expenditures to calculate the savings rate (Saving2); and for the other, we referred to Gan et al. [62], who used the logarithmic difference between the income and consumption of resident households as the savings rate (Saving3). These variables were constructed as follows:(3)saving1=incomei−consumption1iincomei×100%(4)saving2=incomei−consumptioniincomei×100%(5)saving3=[ln(incomei)−ln(consumption1i)]×100%Here, incomei, consumptioni, and consumption1i denote income, consumption expenditure, and consumption excluding the education and healthcare of household i, respectively. After obtaining the resident household savings rates, the mean household savings rate was calculated at the city level.

Independent variable. The independent variable was the level of regional air pollution, measured using the concentration of PM2.5. This is an internationally recognized air quality indicator to facilitate comparisons between different countries and regions. The data were obtained from NASA.

Control variables. Following the literature on household consumption of Chinese residents [4,62] the control variables included the proportion of households with regional industrial and commercial operations (Business), proportion of households with credit cards (Card), average educational attainment (Edu), average age (Age), proportion of children (Child_r), proportion of older people (Old_r), average number of people in a household (Family_s), coverage rate of commercial insurance (Insurance), share of rural households (Rural_r), size of social capital (Social_n), average household income (Income), average household net worth (Asset) and regional GDP per capita (GDP). Main results

This section empirically tests the effect of air pollution on residential savings rates using Equation (1). Secondly, we conduct robustness tests and endogeneity analysis through various methods. Using Equation (2), we investigate the mechanism through which air pollution affects residential savings from the perspective of health risks. Finally, it conducts heterogeneity analysis from multiple perspectives.

Among the control variables, the higher the level of education of residents, the lower the savings rate. Thus, an increase in the level of human capital helps promote consumption, consistent with previous studies [64]. An increase in commercial insurance coverage also significantly reduces residential savings rates. Commercial insurance reduces precautionary saving incentives mainly by mitigating uncertainty shocks in residents’ households [21]. The higher the proportion of rural residents, the higher the residential savings rates. Because of the lower income and higher income risk of rural residents, preventive savings incentives are also stronger [65]. The effect of social networks on savings rates is significantly negative. Social networks can alleviate household liquidity constraints by providing informal lending, thereby promoting consumption [66]. In addition, the effects of income and assets on residential savings rates are consistent with findings in the literature [62,67]. Thus, hypothesis H1 is supported.

Endogeneity analysis

Owing to endogeneity issues, the impact of air pollution on residential savings rates obtained in the previous section may merely represent a correlation rather than a causal effect. Endogeneity issues mainly stem from two aspects: First, the measurement error problem, which is relatively common in empirical analyses based on micro-survey data [4]. Second, the omitted variable problem, which arises from limitations in data availability that may result in the simultaneous omission of variables that affect both regional PM2.5, concentration and residential savings rate. Omitted variables can typically be divided into two categories: those that do not vary over time, and those with time-varying characteristics. This study employs a panel fixed-effects regression analysis for factors that do not change over time. Mechanism analysis of the impact of air pollution on savings rate

Using Model (2), we examine whether health risks acts as a mechanism through which air pollution affects residential savings rates. Conclusions

China's residential savings rate has shown an upward trend. The related literature mainly focuses on population aging, children's gender, and income uncertainty to explain the mystery of high savings in Chinese households. Although some studies have focused on the impact of air pollution on out-of-home consumption, medical expenditure, and household electricity consumption, a substantial research gap remains on the relationship between air pollution and savings rates.

This study empirically investigates the impact of air pollution on household savings rate and its underlying mechanisms by aggregating CHFS data at the prefecture-level city scale. The findings indicate that air pollution significantly increases the household savings rate. A one-unit increase in PM2.5 leads to a 0.131 percentage point increase in the savings rate. This conclusion holds even after addressing potential endogeneity issues by replacing the dependent variable and employing the instrumental variable method. Mechanism analysis reveals that, owing to the imperfect social security system in China, worsening air pollution heightens health risks for households, thereby increasing the motive for precautionary savings. Heterogeneous analysis finds that in economically developed areas with a more comprehensive public service system, the positive driving effect of air pollution on the savings rates of families in second-tier and higher cities, high-income areas, and regions with higher commercial insurance coverage is lower.

Based on our conclusions of the empirical study, we propose the policy following recommendations. First, an important reason for China's high household savings rate lies in the aggravation of environmental pollution. Policymakers should adopt more renewable energy sources, gradually reduce dependence on traditional fossil energy sources, improve environmental regulation policies, and enhance residents' welfare from the perspective of sustainable development. Second, health risks are the core mechanism by which air pollution affects residential savings rates. A key reason for this is the imperfections in China's medical insurance system. Authorities should continue to improve the medical insurance system, increase the breadth and depth of residents' medical insurance coverage, and reduce the motivation for preventive savings on health risks to weaken the impact of air pollution on residents' consumption. Third, air pollution has a weaker effect on residential savings rates in second-tier and above cities, high-income areas, and areas with high commercial insurance coverage. This suggests that residents in high-income groups and areas with high commercial insurance coverage have better consumption-smoothing abilities, and can reduce the negative impacts of health risks on the savings rate. Therefore, raising residents' income may encourage the development of commercial insurance and reduce the inhibitory effects of uncertainty shocks on consumption.

This study has some limitations, as air pollution is only one form of environmental pollution. Future work should explore whether other types of environmental pollution, such as soil and water pollution, significantly affect residential savings rates. Furthermore, the imperfections in China's health insurance system may be an important reason for the impact of air pollution on residential savings rates. Finally, scholars should empirically examine whether air pollution affects resident savings in developed economies. Moreover, cross-country panel data may provide more extensive and robust evidence.
