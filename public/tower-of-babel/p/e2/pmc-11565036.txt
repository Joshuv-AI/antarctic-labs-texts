Toxic effects of DEHP and MEHP on gut-liver axis in rats via intestinal flora and metabolomics
Authors: Jiaqi Wang, Hongwei Liu, Xiwen Kou, Yuxin Zhang, Yang Wang, Changlan Chen, Zheng Xiang, Xin Wang, Ce Su, Yangcheng Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryDi (2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) is an environmental endocrine disruptor and commonly used as a plasticizer. Exposure to DEHP and its active metabolite mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP) can lead to adverse health consequences; however, the toxic mechanism is remains unclear. In this research, male and female rats were exposed to DEHP and MEHP by oral gavage for 60 consecutive days. Pathological analysis revealed that DEHP and MEHP exposure could affect liver, heart, kidney, and testis tissues, as well as alter biochemical indicators. Metagenomics (16S rRNA gene sequencing) analysis indicated that DEHP and MEHP could reduce the diversity and alter the composition of the gut microbiota. Toxic exposure also affected the levels of short chain fatty acids (SCFAs), with noticeable variations between genders. Metabolomic analysis revealed that DEHP and MEHP could influence bile acids, amino acids, hormones, and lipids. These results demonstrate that exposure to DEHP and MEHP can induce toxicity in rats via the gut-liver axis.Subject areas: Toxic agents, Microbiome, Metabolomics

Introduction

In modern industry, the application of phthalates acid esters (PAEs) has become very widespread, and there are more and more ways of contact for humans, to the extent that their relationship with human health is inseparable.1 PAEs are a general term for a class of esters formed by phthalic acid and alcohols. PAEs, as plasticizers and modifiers, are widely used in industry, agriculture, and daily life, such as plastics, coatings, inks, insect repellents, food packaging materials, medical supplies, personal care products, etc.2 PAEs have been identified as environmental endocrine disruptors (EDCs),3 exhibiting reproductive and developmental toxicity,4 but also have toxic effects, such as teratogenicity, carcinogenicity,5,6 and mutagenicity.7,8 China, the European Union, the United States, and other countries and organizations have prioritized the control of PAEs as pollutants.9,10 Due to the semi-volatility of PAEs and their physical bonding with plastic polymer materials rather than strong chemical covalent bonding, they are prone to escape from plastic products and enter the human body, posing a threat to human health.

Pathways for the entry of PAEs into the body include the respiratory tract, digestive tract, intravenous infusion, and skin contact. As one of the exposure pathways for PAEs, air is most unavoidable exposure for people. The exposure pathways of the human body can be mainly divided into four categories, including occupational exposure, medical exposure, exposure to pregnant women and infants, and exposure to the general population. The primary routes for occupational exposure group, belonging to the high-risk exposure group, are skin contact and respiratory intake.11 Some studies have found that the concentration of PAEs in some PVC and synthetic leather production workshops could reach 1.7–66.0 mg/m3.12 The pathway for patients to come into contact with PAEs is through medical device and drug packaging. In medical devices, plastics are employed for the advantages of low cost, simple processing, high chemical inertness, good toughness, and high biological safety.13 Infants who frequently use medical devices containing PAEs have 13–14 times the amount of PAEs metabolites in their urine.14 Moreover, due to the significant increase in pathways of exposure to PAEs in modern life, multiple studies have detected the presence of PAEs in amniotic fluid and breast milk.15 The general population is exposed to PAEs via multiple pathways, including the digestive tract, respiratory tract, skin, and other common pathways. The main exposure pathway is through the ingestion of contaminated food.16 During the dietary process, the bioaccumulation of the food chain leads to the accumulation and increase of low concentrations of PAEs, which ultimately enter the human body at higher concentrations. This biological amplification effect makes higher animals, such as humans more susceptible to exposure to relatively high concentrations of PAEs.17

Di (2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) is a crucial compound in PVC products, known as PAEs. The non-covalent bonding of DEHP to plastics makes it susceptible to gradual leakage into the environment during use, resulting in human exposure through various routes including ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact.16 After exposure, DEHP undergoes metabolic transformations to produce several metabolites, of which mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP) serves as the primary metabolite. The prolonged DEHP exposure will cause adverse health effects associated with prolonged DEHP exposure.18,19,20 The co-exposure of PS-MPs and DEHP induced oxidative stress, activating the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway, which aggravated kidney pyroptosis and inflammation.21 DEHP exposure resulted in morphological changes, motor deficits, cognitive impairments, and disruption of the blood-brain barrier in the brain.22 Research indicated that DEHP exposure reduced whole-body bone mineral density (BMD), caused damage to femur microstructure, decreased femur bone formation, and increased femur bone marrow adipogenesis in C57BL/6 female mice. In vitro experiments demonstrated that DEHP treatment significantly enhanced adipogenic differentiation and inhibited osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs).23

Intestinal flora refers to the large microbial community that inhabits the human gastrointestinal tract. For example, bifidobacteria, lactobacilli, and others can synthesize several essential vitamins for human growth and development, such as B vitamins, vitamin K, niacin, pantothenic acid, etc. A complex ecosystem of intestinal flora is very important for the host metabolism, immune regulation, intestinal homeostasis, etc.24,25 Recent evidence suggests that Oscillospira spp. and Methanobrevibacter smithii are associated with suppressing obesity.26 In obese individuals, there was a significant reduction in the abundance of Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, which was negatively correlated with serum glutamate concentration. Furthermore, colonization of these bacteria in animal models has been shown to inhibit obesity.27 Epidemiological studies have indicated that patients who undergo total colectomy may have a higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes compared to those who have not undergone the procedure. This suggests a potential link between intestinal flora, hormone secretion in the distal intestine, and glucose regulation.28 Moreover, intestinal flora is also associated with liver-metabolic diseases and cardio-metabolic diseases.

Bile acids (BAs) are generated in the liver and modified by microorganisms in the intestine and have a common structure but differ in the number and spatial position of hydroxyl groups on the steroid ring.29 With the introduction and continuous improvement of the concept of gut-liver axis, research has shown that changes in BAs can affect microbial antigens and the production and metabolic process of metabolites and BAs in the host, and can regulate the metabolic and immune response of the intestine and liver, affecting the composition and efficacy of the intestinal flora.

There is also a close relationship between short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and the gut microbiota. The main function of the gut microbiota is related to the catabolism of carbohydrates and proteins. Polysaccharides are the main energy source of the Bacteroidetes, while Firmicutes also participate in polysaccharide degradation,30 while some undissociated SCFAs are absorbed by the colon through passive diffusion. After being absorbed by the colon, SCFAs enter the bloodstream, but after entering the liver through the portal vein, they are metabolized by liver cells,31 and ultimately about 5% of SCFAs are excreted from the body.32 The functions of the different SCFAs vary, with butyric acid providing energy for the growth of intestinal cells33 propionic acid being mainly absorbed and utilized by the liver as a substrate for gluconeogenesis and participating in host metabolism,34 and most acetic acid entering the peripheral circulation.

The intestinal flora can not only interact with the local gut, but also form a network of interactions with many distant organs and tissues. Researchers have proposed a series of hypotheses, including the gut-brain axis,35 gut-liver axis,36 gut-muscle axis,37 and gut-kidney axis.38 In recent years, the impact of intestinal flora on the reproductive system has received widespread attention, and the interaction between intestinal flora and sex hormones has become an emerging research hotspot. There are significant gender differences in the intestinal flora itself,39 and intestinal flora transplantation can cause significant changes in the levels of multiple sex hormones.40 Conversely, sex hormones can also have an impact on the intestinal flora, and both gonadectomy and hormone replacement therapy can cause significant changes in the species composition of the intestinal flora.41 In male animals, fecal microbiota transplantation can penetrate the blood-testis barrier and alter the state of spermatogenesis.42 In female animals, the changes in intestinal flora are correlated with hormone-related diseases.43,44,45

Based on the mutual influence relationship between the gut-liver axis and its related molecules mentioned previously, an integrated strategy combining toxicology, intestinal flora analysis, and metabolomics was used to systematically assess the toxicity caused by long-term exposure to DEHP and MEHP. Through comprehensive data analysis, we investigated the effects of their exposure on the gut-liver axis, which provides a perspective on the toxic mechanisms of pollutants in the aquatic environment.

Body weight and histopathological observation

The weights of the rats were observed and recorded weekly. The initial body weight of all rats was within the range of 200 g ± 20 g. After 8 weeks of continuous exposure, the weights of the DEHP and MEHP groups have no significant difference compared with the control group (p > 0.05).

Compared with the control group, the rat heart tissue in the MEHP groups (♂ and ♀) showed cell swelling and edema degeneration, the myocardial cells were not densely arranged, and the irregular white voids increased. HE staining of liver tissue indicated that the hepatic histology of the control group was clear, and the hepatocytes were radially arranged with rich cytoplasm and nuclei centered on the central vein. The gap between hepatocytes in the DEHP and MEHP groups (♂ and ♀) became larger and occurred vacuolar degeneration, and the male rats showed dilated congestion. These indicate that long-term exposure will cause damage to the liver. The HE staining of kidneys tissue from the control group showed clear edges of several renal corpuscles in the visual field, with the renal capsule and glomerulus being easily visible. In the DEHP group, there was diffuse infiltration of inflammatory cells observed in the visual field. In both exposed groups, some renal corpuscles could not be clearly identified, and the renal capsule and glomerulus were indistinguishable. The edges of the renal corpuscles appeared blurred and could not be clearly differentiated from the surrounding proximal and distal tubules, suggesting that DEHP and MEHP caused damage to the kidneys of the rats. HE staining of testicle tissue indicated that there was obvious atrophy of the seminiferous tubules, the gap between the seminiferous tubules became larger, the arrangement was irregular, and the Leydig cells were also relatively reduced compared with the control group (Figure 1). There was no pathological change on the uterine staining results of uterus.

Serum biochemical analysis

In the male-DEHP group, there was a significant decrease in the levels of ALT and indirect bilirubin (IBIL), compared to the male-control group. On the other hand, the male-DEHP group exhibited significantly increased levels of TG, total cholesterol (TC), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C) when compared to the control group (Figure 2). In the female-DEHP group, the levels of total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and LDL-C increased significantly (p < 0.05), while the levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), direct bilirubin (DBIL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C), creatinine (Cr), and glucose (GLU) decreased when compared to the female-control group (Figure 3). For the male-MEHP group, there was a significant decrease in the levels of TP, GLOB, ALT, total bilirubin (TBIL), and DBIL detected, while the levels of LDL-C and IBIL were significantly increased when compared to the male-control group. Similarly, the female-MEHP group demonstrated significantly increased levels of TP, GLOB, and AST, while the levels of ALP, TB, DBIL, TG, and TC were lower when compared to the female-control group. These identified parameters are strongly associated with liver and kidney health. The exposure to DEHP and MEHP appeared to influence the mentioned indicators, causing either an increase or decrease in their levels, thereby suggesting the detrimental impact of toxic exposure on the liver and kidney, as substantiated by the consistent results obtained from HE staining.

Analysis of intestinal flora

Intestinal flora diversity and composition

Based on the 16S rRNA gene data, the number of OTUs and the community diversity were evaluated by the α diversity index (Figure 4). Compared with the control group, the Chao1 and observed species indexes both decreased in the DEHP group (♂ and ♀), indicating that the influence of DEHP reduced the species diversity and changed the structure of the intestinal flora. The Good’s coverage index increased significantly (p < 0.05) in the toxic exposure groups (♂ and ♀), indicating that under the influence of DEHP, the probability of the sequence being measured in the sample increased and the species abundance decreased. This is consistent with the results of the decrease in species diversity analyzed aforementioned. In MEHP groups (♂ and ♀), there was a slight decrease in both indices (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that MEHP has a certain impact on reducing the flora species diversity and refactoring the intestinal flora. However, it is noteworthy that the effect of MEHP is less severe compared to that of DEHP. The slight decrease in Faith’s PD index and the slight increase in Good’s coverage index support the aforementioned conclusions. In conclusion, the results of α diversity index showed that DEHP exposure reduced the intestinal flora diversity and refactored the intestinal flora.

Nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) reduces search objects (samples or variables) in high-dimensional space to lower dimensions for localization, analysis, and classification while preserving the original relationship between objects. The β diversity analysis showed an inter-group difference between the DEHP group and the control group (♂ and ♀, Figure 5). Although the sample points of the MEHP group were close to the control group (♂ and ♀), there was no cross between the groups, and the group gathered well, indicating that DEHP and MEHP changed the composition of intestinal flora in rats, DEHP exposure had a more significant effect on the flora, according to the NMDS analysis results. As we all know, MEHP is the active metabolite of DEHP in the body. Analysis of serum concentrations (section 3.1) reveals that DEHP is partially metabolized into MEHP in the blood. Interestingly, levels of free MEHP are higher in the DEHP group compared to that in MEHP group. This suggests that toxic effects of DEHP exposure are due to both the converted MEHP and the residual DEHP, which could result in a greater impact on intestinal flora in the DEHP groups compared to the MEHP groups at the current dose.

Analysis of community structure and difference at the phylum level

The relative abundance (RA) of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Tenericutes, TM7, Verrucomicrobia, Cyanobacteria raised in the male-DEHP group, compared to the male-control group (Figure 6A), conversely, the RA of Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria reduced. Similarly in the female-DEHP group, there was an increase in the Tenericutes, TM7, Verrucomicrobia, Deferribacteres, while the RA of Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Cyanobacteria decreased compared to the female-control group. After MEHP exposure, the RA of Actinobacteria, Tenericutes, TM7, and Elusimicrobia increased in the male group, while it decreased in Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Cyanobacteria (Figure 6A). In the female group, the RA of Tenericutes, TM7, and Verrucomicrobia increased, while the RA of Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Cyanobacteria decreased (Figure 6B).

At the class level, the abundance of Actinobacteria, Flavobacteriia, 4C0d 2, Erysipelotrichi, Mollicutes, TM7 3, Verrucomicrobiae increased, the abundance of Betaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria decreased in the male-DEHP group and the male-MEHP group. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is highly correlated with many epidemic diseases.46 The DEHP and MEHP exposure led to significant destruction of the morphology and composition of the gut microbiota. Thereinto, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidetes are the main members of gut microbiota.47 The results of Figure 6 show that MEHP exposure has the opposite effects on the F/B ratio of different gender groups. The decreased F/B ratio in the male-MEHP group indicates metabolic disorders, which may be related to fat deposition, lipogenesis and impaired insulin sensitivity. The increase in F/B ratio in the female-MEHP group was related to the immune-inflammatory response.

Analysis of SCFAs

SCFAs contents were quantitatively analyzed by GC-MS (Figure 8A). The results revealed the contents of PA, BA, isoBA, VA, isoVA, and CAA increased significantly in the male-DEHP group (Figure 8B, p < 0.05). It obviously reduced the contents of isoBA (p < 0.05) while there was an obvious increase in the contents of BA, VA, CAA in the female-DEHP group (Figure 8C). The results revealed a significant increase in the contents of PA, BA, isoBA, VA, and isoVA in the male-MEHP group (p < 0.05). It showed an abvious decrease in the contents of PA, BA, isoBA, VA, and isoVA in the female-MEHP group (p < 0.05).

SCFAs, which are the final outcomes of the gut microbiota, contribute to preserving the integrity of the gut mucosa, enhancing glucose and lipid metabolic processes, regulating energy consumption, and controlling the immune system and inflammatory reactions. The SCFAs were increased to varying degrees in male-MEHP group, while the SCFAs in female-MEHP group were reduced. However, in the DEHP group, besides isobutyric acid and isovaleric acid, the contents of other SCFAs increased to varying degrees. It is also in agreement with the aforementioned abundance changes of Clostridium in the female and male groups. Because Clostridium is a major group of bacteria producing various SCFAs in the intestine,48 it’s upregulation in the male group led to the increase of the overall content of SCFAs in DEHP and MEHP groups, and it’s downregulation in female group leads to the decrease of the overall content of SCFAs in DEHP and MEHP groups.

Correlation heatmap analysis revealed a close relationship between the flora and the levels of BA (Figures 11 and 12). Combined with the results of intestinal flora analysis, it can be concluded that toxic exposure affects BA metabolism by affecting intestinal flora.

Amino acid metabolism

The levels of 9 amino acids in rats were determined (Figures 13 and 14). In the male-DEHP group, the levels of GABA, Arg, Lys, Phe, Thr, and Val decreased significantly compared with the male-control group. In the male-MEHP group, the levels of Arg, -Phe, Thr, and Val decreased significantly compared with the male-control group, but the levels of Orn and Pro increased significantly in the male-MEHP group. In the female group, after exposure to MEHP, the Arg levels increased significantly, and the Cit levels significantly decreased, while other amino acid levels did not change significantly. After DEHP exposure in the female group, the levels of GABA, Cit, Lys, Orn, Phe, Pro, Thr, and Val were significantly reduced.

Amino acids interact with intestinal flora, participate in microbial metabolism, and play a role in maintaining the balance of intestinal environment. Amino acids are also used as energy substrates and are oxidized in the tricarboxylic acid cycle to produce ATP. Correlation heatmap analysis was used to analyze the correlation between the intestinal flora and amino acids (Figures 15 and 16). In the male-DEHP group, the relative abundances of Paraprevotella, Blautia, and Turicibacter were positively correlated with the amino acids at the genu level. In the male-MEHP group, the relative abundances of Lactobacillus and Roseburia were positively correlated with the Thr, Val, Phe, and Arg, and they were negatively correlated with Orn and Pro at the genu level. In the female-DEHP group, the relative abundances of Blautia, Shigella, Corynebacterium, and Dorea were positively correlated with the amino acids at the genu level. In the female-MEHP group, the relative abundances of Blautia, [Ruminococcus], Corynebacterium, Sutterella, Jeotgalicoccus, and Bacteroides were positively correlated with Cit and negatively correlated with Arg. It can be seen that DEHP exposure decreased the amino acids levels in rats (♂ and ♀), while the effect of MEHP exposure on amino acids was gender-specific. In male groups, MEHP exposure resulted in a significant decrease in 4 amino acids, while in female rats, only the levels of Arg and Cit were changed. The exposure of MEHP has an effect on the ornithine cycle in the female group. The process by which carbon dioxide generates urea through ornithine, citrulline, and arginine is known as the ornithine cycle. Ammonia is the final product of amino acid metabolism in the body. A small part of ammonia can be re-synthesized into amino acids and other nitrogen-containing compounds. Most of the ammonia is synthesized into urea through the ornithine cycle, which is excreted with urine to relieve the toxic effect of ammonia.

Lipid metabolism

15 LPCs were determined to study the influence of DEHP and MEHP on lipid metabolism in rats (Figures 17 and 18). In the male group, MEHP exposure increased in the levels of LPC(14:0), LPC(15:0), LPC(16:0), LPC(16:1), LPC(17:0), LPC(18:0), LPC(18:1), and OCC compared to the control group. The DEHP exposure led to an increase in the level of LPC(16:1), but a decrease the level of LPC(17:0), LPC(19:0), LPC(20:0), LPC(20:1), MC, OCC, and PC. In the female group, MEHP exposure increased the contents of LPC(14:0), LPC(15:0), LPC(16:0), LPC(16:1), LPC(17:0), LPC(18:0), LPC(18:1), LPC(18:2), LPC(19:0), LPC(20:1), LPC(22:0), MC, and OCC, compared with the control group, while DEHP exposure would lead to increase in the contents of LPC(14:0), LPC(16:0), LPC(16:1), LPC(18:0), LPC(18:1), LPC(18:2), LPC(22:0), and OCC. In summary, the DEHP and MEHP exposure in rats of different genders will disturb the contents of LPCs. Correlation heatmap analysis was used to analyze the correlation between the intestinal flora and LPCs (Figures 19 and 20). In the male-DEHP group, the relative abundances of Paraprevotella, Blautia, Bifidobacterium, and Turicibacter were positively correlated with the LPC(20:0), LPC(20:1), LPC(17:0), LPC(19:0), PC, OCC and MC. In the male-MEHP group, the relative abundances of Allobaculum, Turicibacter, Dorea, and Jeotgalicoccus were positively correlated with the LPCs at the genu level. In the female-DEHP group, the relative abundances of Turicibacter, Coprococcus, Desulfovibrio, [Prevotella] and Roseburia were positively correlated with the LPCs at the genu level. In the female-MEHP group, the RA of Desulfovibrio was positively correlated with LPCs.

After exposure to DEHP and MEHP, there was a significant alteration in the levels of LPCs in rats. In the male-MEHP group, female-MEHP group and female-DEHP group, most of the lipid content increased, while in male-DEHP group, some of the lipids were significantly reduced. Lysophospholipids, also known as “biological second messengers”, play a crucial role in lipid signal transduction. LPC is an intermediate in the metabolism of PC, during PC turnover, and is one of the major lipid components of low-density lipoproteins.49 In the liver, LPCs upregulate genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis and downregulate genes involved in hepatic fatty acid oxidation.50 LPCs can generate fatty acids like arachidonic acid, the levels of LPCs are closely associated with metabolic disorders, such as dyslipidemia, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. The increase of lipids indicates that MEHP exposure may lead to lipid accumulation in rats, and DEHP can cause same in the female rats.

Steroid hormone metabolism

The contents of 7 sex hormones in rat serum were analyzed by LC-MS (Figures 21 and 22). Compared with the control group, the levels of androstanolone, dehydroepiandrosterone, estradiol, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, pregnenolone were decreased significantly (p < 0.05) in the DEHP group and MEHP group (♂ and ♀). The microbiota plays a major role in the reproductive endocrine system by interacting with estrogens, androgens, insulin, and other hormones. Sex hormone levels have a potential relationship with the gut microbiota.51 The abundance of Ruminococcus was related to the changes of 5α-dihydrotestosterone.52 In the male group, 5α-dihydrotestosterone showed an increasing trend in the DEHP and MEHP, which was positively correlated with Ruminococcus. In the female group, both of them showed a decreasing trend after DEHP exposure. Studies have shown that the abundance of Clostridium is related to the change of estrogen content.53 The results of this study showed that the abundance of Clostridium decreased in the female group, resulting in the decrease of estradiol, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, pregnenolone levels in rats. These trends indicate that the two EDCs regulate the hormone levels of rats by affecting the structure of intestinal flora. Correlation heatmap analysis was used to analyze the correlation between the intestinal flora and hormones (Figures 23 and 24). The results of the interaction analysis indicated that the interaction between progesterone and intestinal flora was differed from that of other hormones. The relative abundances of Sutterella and Corynebacterium were positively correlated with progesterone in the male-DEHP group. In the male-MEHP group, the relative abundances of Allobaculum, Dorea, and Jeotgalicoccus were positively correlated with progesterone. In the female-DEHP group, the RA of Coprococcus was positively correlated with progesterone. Likewise, in the female-MEHP group, the RA of Desulfovibrio was positively correlated with progesterone at the genu level. It is noteworthy that the content of progesterone raised in male group, indicating that the function of the reproductive system is affected, which will not only lead to endocrine disorders, low gonadal function, but also appear a variety of feminization characteristics, including breast development, voice feminization, and so on.

Conclusions

This study reported the effects of long-term DEHP and MEHP intake on the gut-liver axis in rats of different genders. There was pathological damage in the heart, liver, kidney, and testis tissues of rats exposed to DEHP and MEHP, and affected the contents of biochemical indicators. The exposure reduced the diversity of intestinal flora and reduced the species category of flora, and DEHP exhibited a more pronounced effect on the flora compared to MEHP. Metabolomic study showed that the contents of BAs, SCFAs, amino acids, steroid hormone, and other metabolites were also affected. The correlation analysis between the intestinal flora and metabolites showed that the intestinal flora was significantly correlated with the changes of metabolites. The toxicity of DEHP and MEHP based on gut-liver axis was evaluated systematically, which hold theoretical significance for understanding the toxic mechanism. Additionally, it has valuable research implications for studying the environmental intake, mechanism of action, and health risk level associated with DEHP and MEHP.

Limitations of the study

In our current study, the effects of DEHP and MEHP on BAs and amino acids had been investigated, however, their effects on related proteins remains to be verified, which will be the direction of our future research.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal studies

Male and female Sprague-Dawley rats aged 6 weeks (200 ± 20 g) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shenyang, China). (SCXK（LN）2020-0001). Rats were housed in controlled environmental conditions under a 12-hour-light/dark cycle with food and water provided ad libitum. After a week of adaptation at the Animal Center of Liaoning University, all rats were randomly subjected to the experiment. The study and include experimental procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of Liaoning University (Approval No. 20200723512).

The LD50 value of DEHP was 30 g/kg (NTP, 2021), and the LD50 of MEHP was 1000 mg/kg.54 1/10 of the LD50 value was used for the dosage according to “Technical Guidelines for Long-term Toxicity Testing of Chemical Drugs ([H]-GPT2-1)” issued by the State Drug Administration of China (2005).55 36 rats were divided into 6 groups (n = 6) including male-DEHP group, female-DEHP group, male-MEHP group, female-MEHP group, male-control group and female-control group. DEHP and MEHP were dissolved in olive oil, and the same volume of olive oil was given in the control group. After one-week adaptive feeding, oral gavage was performed daily for 60 consecutive days. Body weight was recorded weekly. On the 61th day, blood samples were collected via the abdominal aorta, and serum samples were obtained by centrifugation at 4°C (3000 rpm, 15 min). The heart, liver, kidney, testis and uterus were collected for histopathological analysis. Rat feces were collected from the rectum.

Experimental scheme of rat assay design

Method details

Serum concentrations of DEHP and MEHP

100 μL of methanol containing carbamazepine (IS, 10 ng/mL) and 400 μL of methanol were added into 100 μL of the serum, vortexed and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (15 min, 4°C). Thereafter, 300 μL of the supernatant was blow-dried with nitrogen. The residues were reconstituted in 100 μL of MeOH solution, and pass through a 0.22 μm filter membrane before analysis.

Serum concentration was analyzed on Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system and Agilent 6460 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent, USA) with MRM mode. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, auto-sampler temperature was kept at 4°C, and the column compartment was set to 40°C. The mobile phase was composed of Solvents A (H2O with 0.1% formic acid) and B (MeOH). The gradient conditions were as follows: 20–95% B from 0 to 6.0 min, 95% B from 6.0 to 8.0 min, 95-20% B from 8.0 to 11.0 min. The Table S1 shows the optimized MS/MS parameters and qualitative ion pairs for each analyte. MRM analyses were performed with transitions m/z 391→149 for DEHP, 279→149 for MEHP, and 237.1→194.1 for the IS.

Serum biochemical analysis

The biochemical serum indicators including total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), urea, creatinine (Cr), glucose (GLU) and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) were determined using Cobas8000 automatic analyzer (Roche, USA).

16S rRNA sequencing of colonic contents

Total genomic DNA samples were extracted using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). following the manufacturer’s instructions, and stored at −20°C prior to further analysis. The quantity and quality of extracted DNAs were measured using a NanoDrop NC2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. PCR amplification of the bacterial 16S rRNA genes V3–V4 region was performed using the forward primer 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and the reverse primer 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3'). PCR amplicons were purified with Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After the individual quantification step, amplicons were pooled in equal amounts, and pair-end 2 × 250 bp sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq platform with NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles) at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The α-diversity and β-diversity were analyzed on the online platform of Personalbio Genescloud (https://www.genescloud.cn/).

SCFA analysis

SCFAs were carried on GC-MS (Agilent 7890B–5977 B GC/MSD). Phosphoric acid solution containing 4-methylvaleric acid as internal standard (0.2%, v/v, 50 μL) was added to 100 mg feces. The above pretreatment steps of feces samples were carried out at 4°C to prevent the volatilization of SCFAs.

Metabolomics analysis

UPLC-MS/MS analysis was carried on X500R QTOF LC-MS/MS system (AB Sciex, USA) equipped with ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters, USA). The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The elution program was set as Table S2.

150 μL of methanol containing carbamazepine and mycophenolic acid as internal standards (50 ng/mL) and 600 μL of methanol were added into 150 μL of the serum, vortexed and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (15 min, 4°C). Thereafter, 700 μL of the supernatant was pipetted into centrifuge tube and blow-dried with nitrogen. The residues were reconstituted in 70 μL of 50% MeOH-H2O solution, and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min) before analysis.

All the metabolite standards were dissolved in double distilled water or methanol to prepare 2 mg/mL stock solutions, which were diluted before use.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was calculated using SPSS software and shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison tests was used for statistical significance. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01 and ∗∗∗p < 0.001 versus the control group).

Interactive correlation heatmap analysis

Interactive correlation heatmap analysis was used to visualize the correlations between the gut microbiota and different intestinal metabolites based on Spearman’s correlation coefficients. The Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) score of differential bacteria was obtained by LEfSe analysis.
