Study protocol for a cluster-randomized controlled trial of a universal home-based, multi-component intervention to promote healthy weight development in children during infancy and toddlerhood in Denmark: the Bloom study
Authors: Camilla Thørring Bonnesen, Mette Rasmussen, Lene Kierkegaard, Rikke Rothkegel Carlsson, Marie Pil Jensen, Thilde Risager Ubbesen, Katrine Rich Madsen, Lau Caspar Thygesen, Rikke Fredenslund Krølner, Louise Ayoe Sparvath Brautsch, Johanne Aviaja Rosing, Line Planck Kongstad, Mette Skovgaard Væver, Ida Egmose, Trine Pagh Pedersen

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChild overweight and obesity are major public health challenges associated with poor physical, psychological, and social health through life. This protocol describes the study design for the Bloom Study, an evaluation of a theory- and evidence-based intervention addressing infancy and toddlerhood (age 0–3 years) to promote healthy weight development among children in Denmark.MethodsThe Bloom intervention is evaluated through an open-label, two-arm, cluster-randomized wait-list control trial, with 22 municipalities randomly allocated to 11 intervention and 11 control municipalities. All first-time families within one year are invited (approximately 3200 families). The intervention was developed using the Intervention Mapping protocol and a thorough co-creation process. It is delivered through the Danish community health nursing system and includes four main components: (1) Home visits, (2) Proactive telephone consultations, (3) Parent groups for both parents, and (4) A video library. The intervention begins during pregnancy (between weeks 22–24) and continues until the child is 30 months. The primary outcome measure is the BMI z-score at 36 months of age. Secondary outcomes relate to the key determinants of child weight development targeted by the Bloom intervention, including food and meals, sleep, movement and motor skills, screen use, and family sense of security. A comprehensive mixed methods process evaluation and a health economic evaluation will also be conducted.DiscussionThe protocol outlines the evaluation of a theory- and evidence-based intervention aimed at promoting healthy weight development in infants and toddlers in Denmark. Overall, the study has the potential to inform future health promotion initiatives at the municipal level, supporting healthy weight development during early childhood.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06705400. Background

Child overweight and obesity are serious public health challenges in the 21 st century. The prevalence of overweight and obesity has reached historically high levels, affecting all age groups throughout the life course [1–3]. In Denmark, 14% of children has overweight or obesity when they start school at six years of age, with this prevalence rising to 19% by the time they leave lower secondary school at 15 years of age [2]. Notably, the prevalence is even higher among children in families from low socioeconomic position (SEP) [3] and those with a minority ethnic background [2].

Childhood overweight and obesity significantly impact physical, psychological, and social health. For example, overweight and obesity often negatively influence the child’s quality of life [4–9] by increased risk of various adverse psychosocial outcomes, including low levels of well-being, low self-esteem, feelings of sadness, loneliness, nervousness, and depression [10–13]. Moreover, children with overweight or obesity may experience decreased social participation, such as being excluded from group activities or facing difficulties in forming friendships and being more frequently exposed to bullying [14]. This aligns with a recent Danish report indicating that childhood overweight and obesity negatively impact social well-being, school performance, and educational choices [15]. The adverse physiological impacts of overweight and obesity manifest already during childhood and adolescence including elevated risk of cardiometabolic diseases (e.g., type-2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension), and asthma [16–25].

The importance of infancy and toddlerhood as a critical period for promoting healthy weight development

It is increasingly acknowledged that infancy represents a window of opportunity for promoting healthy weight development. Several factors contribute to this perspective: (1) Numerous risk factors for overweight and obesity are particularly prevalent during infancy and early childhood [3, 26, 27]; (2) Rapid growth during the first year of life, along with an early adiposity rebound, is associated with increased risk of overweight and obesity both in childhood and later in life [3, 28–32]; and (3) Increased body mass index (BMI) during infancy serves as a predictor for overweight and obesity in childhood, adolescence, and adulthood [3, 33–36]. Furthermore, early intervention strategies have the potential to mitigate social inequalities in overweight and obesity, which often emerge in infancy and become more pronounced during youth [37, 38].

Modifiable early life risk factors for childhood overweight and obesity

The causes of childhood overweight are multifaceted and not yet fully understood [39, 40]. It is widely believed that the development of overweight and obesity in children and adolescents is a consequence of interactions between genetic, behavioural, psychosocial, and environmental factors [3, 39, 40]. Several well-established risk factors can potentially be modified through interventions during infancy and childhood [3, 27, 41–44]. These include behaviours related to energy balance, such as no or short duration of breastfeeding [3, 45], early introduction of complementary foods [3, 46], the quantity and quality of the child’s diet, including high protein intake [3, 47], increased total energy intake [48], and excessive consumption of sugar sweetened beverages and semiliquid foods [49]. Other factors include low intake of fruit and vegetables [50–53], non-responsive parental feeding practices [49, 54–56], absence of shared family meals [50–53], low levels of physical activity and delayed motor development in infants [57–63]. Frequent screen use [64–70], and insufficient sleep duration and quality during infancy and early childhood [71–73] are also important factors.

Family sense of security

There is growing evidence that psychosocial aspects, such as growing up in disadvantaged socioeconomic conditions (e.g., low education, low socioeconomic position, poverty, and financial insecurity in the family) significantly contribute to obesity through the socio-emotional stress they impose on children and their families [40, 74–78]. Hemmingsson et al. recently proposed a model suggesting that prolonged social adversity within the family increases the risk of parental chronic stress, creating an insecure and disharmonious family environment [40]. This is particularly detrimental during early childhood, a sensitive developmental phase where children lack sufficient coping skills, resilience, and self-esteem [40, 79]. The model emphasizes the importance of meeting children’s social and emotional needs of the young child through sensitive parent-child interactions, thereby promoting secure child-parent attachment and emotional bonding. When parents do not adequately meet the child’s socioemotional needs, this increases the risk of insecure child-parent attachment and maladaptive emotion regulation strategies [40]. This cascade of psychological, emotional, and behavioural processes may lead to chronic stress characterized by e.g., anxiety, increased stress reactivity, allostatic load, and hormonal changes which in turn may cause disrupted energy balance homeostasis (such as excessive energy intake and reduced energy expenditure), resulting in weight gain and obesity [40].

State of the art: primary obesity prevention addressing infancy and toddlerhood

Following the growing recognition of the need for early primary prevention of overweight and obesity, as well as a better understanding of the early risk factors associated with childhood overweight and obesity, an increasing number of intervention studies have been conducted. However, at the time of the conceptualization of the Bloom Study, only a few health center- or home-based randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of multi-behavioral interventions targeting infants and their families have undergone complete evaluation. The impact of previous early interventions on child weight is limited and often not sustained, as described below.

The INFANT study in Australia focused on enhancing parenting skills to promote improved child eating and physical activity [80]. Follow-up data at the child’s age of 20 months (n = 480), three and half years (n = 361), and five years (n = 337) showed no significant difference in BMI-z scores between the intervention and control groups [81, 82]. The Healthy Beginnings study in Australia targeted breastfeeding, fruit and vegetable intake, active play, and reduced television viewing [83]. This study found a significantly lower mean BMI at age two in the intervention group compared to the control group (n = 497) [84], but the effect was not sustained at the five-year follow-up (n = 369) [85]. The NOURISH study, another Australian study, focused on establishing healthy food and meal practices [86]. This study found a significantly lower BMI-for-age z-score at 14 months in the intervention group compared to the control group (n = 598) [87]. Follow-up at ages two (n = 541) and five years (n = 424) demonstrated trends in the expected directions, but effect sizes failed to reach statistical significance [88, 89]. The New Zealand study, POI.nz, targeted breastfeeding, diet, physical activity, and sleep, evaluated in a four-arm RCT design: Control (usual care), FAB (food, physical activity, and breastfeeding), Sleep, and Combination (FAB and Sleep) [90]. No differences in BMI were observed at the two-year follow-up (n = 686). Explorative analyses showed a protective effect against obesity among infants who received the sleep intervention [91]. Follow-up at ages three and a half years (n = 616) and at five years (n = 557) revealed significantly lower BMI z-scores in the sleep group compared to the control and the FAB groups. Moreover, the FAB group had an unexpected significantly higher BMI z-score at age five compared to the control group [92]. A meta-analysis pooling data (n = 2,196) from the three Australian trials [80, 83, 86] and the New Zealand trial [90], revealed a significant difference in mean BMI z-score at 18–24 months of age between the intervention group and the control group of − 0.12 (95% CI: −0.23 to − 0.03; p = 0.012). The authors conclude that the derived mean BMI z-score difference equates to a 2% (95% CI: 0%−4%) absolute reduction in the prevalence of overweight/obesity [93].

The INSIGHT study in the US focused on responsive parenting and feeding, sleep, active play, and emotion regulation [94]. At age three, the intervention group showed a modest reduction in BMI z-score compared to the control group, but no significant difference in BMI percentile was detected (n = 232) [95]. The Swedish PRIMROSE study tested an intervention targeting children from nine to ten months to age four years. Parents were supported in promoting healthy dietary behaviors and physical activity through motivational interviewing [96]. No statistical effects were observed on child BMI, waist circumference, or prevalence of overweight (n = 1,148) [97]. A US study targeting children from birth until the age of 24 months focused on nutrition, feeding, and physical activity. At 24 months, no effects were detected on BMI, BMI z-scores, or skinfold thickness (n = 232) [98]. Another US study, targeting nutrition, feeding, physical activity, and screen time starting at age two months [99], revealed less weight gain in the intervention group compared to the control group until age 18 months, although this was not sustained at 24 months (n = 802) [100]. Analyses showed that the intervention was more effective at reducing child BMI z-score among families with limited health literacy, those for whom Spanish was the primary language, and families in both groups (n = 802) [101]. A US study targeting Hispanic or Mexican American families evaluated an intervention focusing on growth monitoring (measurement/feedback to parents at home visits), parental cue recognition, early feeding education, and promotion of activity and rest/sleep. The intervention began during pregnancy and lasted until the child reached two years of age [102]. The study showed no effect on weight-for-length z-scores or overweight/obese status at age 12 months (n = 119) [103]. The Family Spirit Nurture study, targeting Native American mothers and children from pregnancy to age two, focused on child eating, parental feeding practices, diet and physical activity routines, maternal psychosocial stressors, and household food insecurity [104]. At six and nine months, infants in the intervention group had lower z-BMI compared to those in the control group (n = 120). However, the difference did not remain statistically significant at 12 months (n = 120) [105].

One of the most recent reviews concluded that many trials showed impacts on weight-related behaviors, such as prolonging the duration of breastfeeding, improving healthy food intake and reducing intake of discretionary foods [106]. The authors call for more standardized methodologies in future trials and suggest that comprehensive, multi-faceted approaches may be most effective in addressing infant obesity prevention [106].

The most promising results among the interventions that have reached complete evaluation are seen for the high-dose and home-based programs commenced in pregnancy and extended for a minimum of two years postpartum. Consequently, there is a need for further exploring the potential of the home setting [107], for example adapting and tailoring the intervention to the specific needs and circumstances of the individual families. This is not built into the design of the previous studies in an explicit and standardized manner. Related to this, previous early obesity prevention initiatives have predominantly focused on mothers as the primary recipient of the intervention [108]. This deviates from the contemporary recognition of the integral role that fathers play in e.g., child feeding [109, 110] as they are increasingly involved in childcare and household routines and responsibilities [111].

Most previous interventions have primarily focused on nutrition and physical activity. However, recent evidence points to the importance of addressing child sleep, as well as psychosocial and emotional aspects of parent-child interactions in future interventions. SEP has generally not been a key parameter in the development or implementation of previous interventions, and only few studies have investigated differential effects of the intervention by SEP. While many interventions have adhered to a one-size-fits all approach, it is plausible that different target groups (e.g., fathers/co-parents, families of low SEP, and ethnic minorities) need different modes of delivery [112].

Many previous interventions have been implemented outside pre-existing health service structures, which may have affected implementation and contributed to the observed absence of long-term effects. More comprehensive and multicomponent interventions, co-designed with stakeholders and integrated into existing public health practice are needed to address the challenge of childhood overweight and obesity [37, 112–114]. The lack of solid effects on weight-related outcomes of previous interventions within this field calls for rethinking and a commitment to innovative strategies.

The Bloom Study

Therefore, we developed the Bloom Study, which aims to test, implement and evaluate a universal, theory- and evidence-based intervention designed to promote healthy weight development in children from infancy to toddlerhood in Denmark. The intervention is integrated into the well-established and widely accepted home-based community health nursing system. The intervention was developed based on a comprehensive co-creation process, existing evidence, and insights from previous interventions [80, 83, 86, 90, 94, 114]. The Bloom intervention incorporates promising strategies aimed at improving children’s weight development outcomes. It addresses modifiable risk factors associated with overweight and obesity during early childhood, including factors that are gaining recognition for their potential impact but have not yet been tested in large-scale trials. These factors include food and meals, movement and motor skills, screen use, sleep, and sense of security in the family. The intervention targets all first-time families, with a particular emphasis on reaching families across social diversities including families with low SEP and/or from ethnic minority background, while also encouraging the involvement of fathers or co-parents.

Design and Methods

Setting: the Danish system of community health nurses

The Bloom Study is developed to fit into the existing setting of community health nurses in Danish municipalities. Community health nurses are educated nurses with one and a half years of specialized training in child health and development. Community health nurses operate locally in all 98 municipalities in Denmark where they have the primary health promoting responsibility for supporting families in fostering optimal growth and development in infants and children from birth to six years of age. They adhere to national guidelines from the Danish Health Authority [115], which are informed by international standards and local expertise [115]. The community health nurse system is universal in Denmark, while also being characterized by significant individual tailoring to meet the needs and circumstances of each family.

Community health nurses primarily provide services through home visits during the infant’s first year of life. The Danish Health Authority recommends five to six home visits for families with newborns during the first 8–10 months, though municipalities have the flexibility to determine the exact number [115]. Additional visits may be offered to families with specific needs, such as breastfeeding support or mental health challenges. Some municipalities also conduct home visits during pregnancy, while few extend visits beyond the 8–10-month period unless additional needs are identified. In the first year of life, many municipalities offer group activities for parents and their children, including mothers’ groups, parent programs, themed events, and drop-in consultations in municipality locations.

During each visit, the community health nurse examines the child (e.g., measurement of weight, height, and head circumference) and provide counseling on various aspects of health and well-being including growth, breastfeeding, complementary food introduction, social and emotional development, sleeping practices, and overall family health and well-being [116]. The national guidelines for the community health nursing system are broad, with limited specification. Consequently, the practices of the community health nurses across municipalities are characterized by heterogeneity. Among others, this concerns the number of visits and other services offered to the families (e.g., parent groups and events, prioritization of themes covered in the dialogue with the families and type of further education of community health nurses).

Although participation in the home visiting program is voluntary, statistics indicate that 95–99% of families in Denmark receive regular home visits in the infant’s first year of life [115, 117].

The Danish community health nursing system is unique, offering comprehensive support to families and children from birth to six years of age. Its integration of health promotion, education, and support services distinguishes Denmark’s system from those found elsewhere, highlighting its innovative approach to child and family health and well-being [118].

Study design

An open label two-arm cluster-randomized wait-list controlled trial is applied to evaluate the effectiveness of the Bloom intervention. The study involves a home-based intervention targeting first-time parents and their child commencing in pregnancy and continuing until 30 months postpartum with follow-up at child age of 36 months. Alongside the cluster-randomized controlled trial (cluster-RCT), a thorough process evaluation and health economic evaluation will be conducted. The control municipalities will get access to the main intervention and implementation guide and materials following the study period.

Sampling strategy

To reach the municipalities most in need, a strategic sampling approach was applied. Danish municipalities were ranked and recruited according to the proportion of children with overweight and obesity at school entry (based on data from the Danish National Child Health Register [119, 120]).

Municipalities that participated in the intervention development phase (outlined below) were invited to participate under the same conditions as other municipalities. Initially, 20 municipalities were excluded for the following reasons: (1) too few annual births in the municipality (n = 4), or (2) the community health nurses in these municipalities were not certified in the Alarm Distress Baby Scale (ADBB) (n = 16) [121]. The ADBB is an observational tool used to assess social withdrawal in children aged two to 48 months, helping community health nurses identify children at risk. Currently, community health nurses in 82 out of the 98 Danish municipalities hold certification in ADBB. This certification is a prerequisite for obtaining a certificate in the “Understanding Your Baby” intervention [122], which is an integral component of the Bloom intervention (described below). Recruitment strategy

Prior to recruitment of municipalities, existing fora and communication channels for community health nurses were used to raise their interest in the study (e.g., articles in relevant newsletters and on websites, presentations at national conferences and political forums). The recruitment process involved the following two steps: (1) An introducing telephone conversation with the managing community health nurse, and (2) an e-mail with an invitation to participate in the project, including elaborated information on the aim of the study, the intervention program, the methods of data collection, expected use of resources, and financial incentive for participation. If further introduction to the intervention was needed, the project group visited the municipalities or online meetings were arranged. The recruitment material was disseminated by e-mail with a request to reply within one month after receipt of the materials. Municipalities that did not reply within one month were contacted using a standardized protocol to provide more information about the study and to encourage participation.

Randomization

In this study, randomization occurred at the municipal level and was performed prior to inclusion of participants (first-time parents and their child). Upon acceptance, the 22 participating municipalities were randomized into intervention (n = 11) and control (n = 11) groups. To mitigate the risk of unbalanced randomization caused by the low number of randomized units, the randomization was stratified by population size (number of annual births in the municipality based on 2023 data from Statistic Denmark) and region (five regions). The randomization procedure was carried out by an epidemiologist not involved in recruitment of municipalities or participants, and the procedure was concealed to the remaining investigators. Given the nature of the intervention, the study is open-label, with allocation not blinded to participants, community health nurses, municipalities, or research team members.

Target group

The target group of the Bloom Study consists of first-time parents and their children in Danish municipalities with the highest prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity at school entry. In the Bloom Study, first-time parents are defined as mothers having their first child(ren). Parents who already have children may have established habits and practices related to the intervention’s focus. To ensure a uniform target group, the intervention supports first-time parents, and their involvement may indirectly benefit future siblings.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All first-time parents who are in pregnancy week 20 between November 1, 2024 and October 31, 2025 are eligible for participation. Families are excluded from participation in the study if the parents are unable (1) to give informed consent, or (2) receive digital post. Additionally, families are excluded if the community health nurse evaluates that the family is too challenged to be enrolled in a research project by either somatic or psychiatric health problems, or by a high level of conflict between parents. Such families will generally be offered extended services, such as home visits, from the standard system of community health nurses.

Recruitment of families

All parents receive oral and written information about the intervention and planned study procedures from their community health nurse during a home visit. Before initiating recruitment, the community health nurses are thoroughly instructed in the process of providing oral information about the project. Written participant information is available in a total of ten languages (Danish, English, Arabic, Farsi, Polish, Romanian, Turkish, Ukrainian, Urdu, and German). Additionally, parents have access to videos in which the informed consent forms, participant information, and an information letter describing how their data will be processed during and after the research project (in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation - GDPR) are read aloud in both Danish and English. Parents who are not present when oral information is provided by the community health nurse, are asked to contact the research group for oral information.

The consent forms are administered to parents via Digital Post - a secure national platform for digital communication between public authorities and citizens. The platform provides all Danish citizens with a digital mailbox and private e-mail account linked to their Personal Identification Number (CPR number), a unique identifier assigned to all individuals with permanent residence in Denmark. Written informed consent is obtained from all custody holders before a child is enrolled in the study. Additionally, each parent submits a consent form for their own participation.

Withdrawal criteria

Parents can withdraw their child and themselves from the study at any time during the study period. In this case, no additional data will be collected, and families in the intervention group will be offered the standard community health nursing services available in the municipality. Data collected prior to withdrawal will be included in the study, as participants provide informed consent at the outset, which permits the research team to use data gathered up until the point of withdrawal.

Sample size

Power calculations were performed to assess the adequate sample size of municipalities and parent-child dyads needed to be recruited over a period of 12 months to detect a mean difference in the primary outcome, child BMI z-score at age 36 months, between children in intervention and control municipalities. We calculated the power of a two-sided t-test using the CRTSize command in R (R statistical software, 2022) to adjust for cluster-design effect. Conventional levels of statistical power (0.8) and level of significance (0.05) were used. Estimation of the mean number of first-time families by municipality was based on information from Statistics Denmark of births in the 30 municipalities with the highest prevalence of children with overweight and obesity when starting school (n = 161 per year). Data from the Child Health Database (Danish community health nurse collaboration) [117, 123], the Danish Child Health Register [119, 120], and other available information on child overweight at municipality level [2] were applied to estimate the prevalence of children with overweight and obesity when starting school. Data from the Danish Child Health Register [119, 120] were used to estimate the Intra Class Correlation (ICC = 0.017). Based on experiences from previous studies (e.g [83, 92–94])., and communication with experts, we aim at a difference in mean BMI z-score of 0.21 units at age 36 months between children in intervention and control municipalities. Finally, based on experiences from previous studies [81, 83, 85, 92–95], we anticipate an attrition rate of parent-child dyads of up to 39%. Under these assumptions, calculations showed that at least 22 municipalities should be recruited for the study (11 intervention municipalities and 11 control municipalities), equivalent to a total of 2,161 participating first-time families (parents and their child) recruited among 3,542 families (3,542 children, 3,542 mothers and 3,542 fathers/co-parents – in total 10,626 study participants) being eligible within the recruitment period of 12 months.

Timeframe of the trial

The trial runs over four and a half years (November 2024 – April 2029). Enrolment period of parents runs over one year (November 2024 to October 2025).

Development of the bloom intervention

The development of the Bloom Study was guided by the Intervention Mapping (IM) protocol [124], a systematic six-step approach for designing, implementing, and evaluating an intervention based on theory, available evidence, new empirical studies, and context knowledge [124]. The IM protocol has proven useful for developing other intervention studies within the field of childhood obesity prevention (e.g., [125, 126]) and in energy-balance-related interventions (e.g., [127, 128]). Each IM step was informed by a thorough co-creation process [129, 130], inspired and guided by co-creation approaches used in other studies [129–131].

The co-creation process involved community health nurses from five Danish municipalities, representatives from Center for Health Promotion in Practice at Local Government Denmark and University College Copenhagen (educational institution of community health nurses), parents of infants and toddlers, and national and international experts within the field of childhood overweight prevention and social epidemiology. A detailed description of the development of the Bloom intervention based on the IM protocol will be described elsewhere.

Intervention themes

The primary outcome of the Bloom Study is child BMI z-score at age 36 months. Secondary outcomes focus on the key determinants of child weight development addressed by the Bloom intervention, including food and meals, movement and motor skills, screen use, sleep, and family sense of security. The program theory (Fig. 2) outlines the causal chain that demonstrates how the intervention is anticipated to promote healthy weight development by directly and indirectly influencing these secondary outcomes.

Program theory of the Bloom Study

Food and meals

The intervention emphasizes the duration of breastfeeding, proper bottle-feeding methods, parental feeding strategies including responsive feeding, the timing of introducing complementary foods, the nutritional quality of the child’s diet, eating behaviors, and family meal patterns. All information and recommendations provided to parents align with the guidelines and recommendations from the Danish Health Authority [116], including two handbooks for health professionals on breastfeeding [132] and infant and toddler nutrition [133].

Movement and motor skills

The intervention focuses on supporting movement practices and developmentally appropriate motor skills, parental role modeling, and reducing sedentary behavior. Parents are supported in establishing a regular movement schedule with their child, starting with prone position playtime (tummy time) and progressing to floor play and outdoor activities. The community health nurse will explain and demonstrate various (playful) activities which facilitate direct interactions between the child and parents during home visits. All information and recommendations provided to parents are consistent with the guidelines from the Danish Health Authority [116].

Screen use

The intervention promotes a policy of zero screen time (e.g., television, smartphone, or tablet) without active adult interaction for children under two years of age. For children aged two years or older, the intervention recommends that screen time is limited to a maximum of one hour per day. All information and recommendations shared with parents align with the guidelines set by the Danish Health Authority [134] and the World Health Organization (WHO) [135]. To provide alternatives to screen engagement, the intervention educates parents in encouraging active play and utilizing various toys to promote exploration (see above). Additionally, parents are informed about the negative impacts of their own screen use during interactions with their child.

Sleep

In collaboration with a Danish community health nurse specialized in infant sleep we adapted the Sleep, Baby & You program [136] for the Danish community health nursing context. This part of the Bloom intervention aims to empower parents by providing information on the biological regulators of sleep, specifically the circadian rhythm and sleep pressure. It addresses parents’ expectations around infant sleep, outlines the typical development of sleep patterns, and offers guidance on aligning parents’ expectations with the realities of normal infant sleep. Furthermore, it includes concrete strategies parents may apply to support child sleep, e.g., waking up at the same time, playing and spending time outside and being active during the day. Moreover, parents have access to an online leaflet on sleep in the video library. The aim of the leaflet is to help parents to: (1) Understand the basics of sleep, (2) Determine what is important for them and their baby, (3) Discover what works for the family, and (4) Take action and enjoy their baby.

Family sense of security

Through the preceding qualitative co-creation process with community health nurses, we applied Hemmingsson et al.‘s model [40] to develop intervention components aiming at enhancing family sense of security. Through these initiatives, the goal is to support and strengthen parents’ abilities and opportunities for meeting their child’s social and emotional needs through sensitive parent-child interactions, and thereby to promote secure child-parent attachment and emotional bonding. In the co-creation process, we identified three key focus areas to be addressed by the selected intervention components and generally in the community health nurses’ continuous interactions with families: Parents’ life course (e.g., sociodemographic background, bullying, and addiction); parents’ current life situation (e.g., socioeconomic security, relational security, and emotional security), and parent-child relationship (e.g., sensitive parenting and parental self-efficacy). The three focus areas influence each other. Parents’ life courses affect their current life situation, and both life course and life situation impact the parent-child relationship as well as the parents’ experience and handling of parenthood. Based on these key focus areas concrete intervention components for enhancing family sense of security in the Bloom intervention were defined and developed. These encompass the following:

Early, long-term and regular family support through home visits and telephone consultations from pregnancy until the child is two and a half years old.

Early, long-term and regular family support through home visits and telephone consultations from pregnancy until the child is two and a half years old.

Individually tailored support and supervision based on the specific needs and life circumstances of each family: During the pregnancy home visit, the community health nurse will use a dialogue tool developed as part of the Bloom Study to gain insight into the parents’ life circumstances and previous experiences. The dialogue tool focuses on socio-demographic factors, social challenges, social support systems, expectations of parenthood and each other as parents, parental self-efficacy, thoughts on breastfeeding, family lifestyle, and the parents’ own childhood experiences.

Psychoeducation for parents, delivered by community health nurses certified in Understanding Your Baby, informs them about their infants’ socioemotional development and behavioral cues at different ages during the first year of life. Parents also receive educational support on three key themes: infant crying, screen time in the family, and ‘good enough’ parenting [122]. The aim is to support and enhance parents’ understanding of their infants’ socioemotional needs, their ability to meet those needs, and, consequently, to foster the development of secure attachment and optimal socioemotional development. Understanding Your Baby uses the routine ADBB observational situation (described above) as a setting for a standardized platform for sharing knowledge and engaging in dialogue with parents about their infants’ socioemotional cues, needs, and development across all families [122]. Intervention components

Figure 3 presents the intervention components integrated into the existing community health nursing system. The white boxes represent the usual care provided by community health nurses in Danish municipalities, while the yellow boxes depict the additional intervention components included in the Bloom Study. The intervention is universal but is designed to reach and appeal to families across social diversities, such as families with low SEP and/or from ethnic minority background. It emphasizes the involvement of fathers or co-parents and is inclusive of various family structures.

Timeline of delivery of the Bloom intervention components

In the Bloom Study, the five to six universal home visits provided from birth to 8–10 months as part of usual care are systematized and standardized across municipalities. To support systematization, community health nurses receive an implementation guide outlining the Bloom Study. In a structured format, the guide includes detailed instructions for home visits and phone consultations, specifying objectives, delivery methods, content, timing, and materials for families. Moreover, the Bloom intervention supplements usual care with (1) an extra home visit during pregnancy (week 22–24), (2) an extra home visit at child age of 18 months, (3) an extra home visit at child age of 30 months, (4) up to eight telephone consultations from birth until child age of 24 months, (5) parent group participation offered to both parents, and (6) a video library to parents. In the following section we will provide a short description of the intervention components. A full overview of the intervention will be published elsewhere.

Home visits

The five to six universal home visits are standardized across municipalities. The primary aim is to systematically strengthen the focus on food and meals, movement and motor skills, screen use, sleep, and family sense of security to promote healthy child weight development.

The overall aim of the home visit during pregnancy is to increase family sense of security through early contact and supervision. The visit aims to establish a trustful relation between the community health nurse and the expectant parents, and to identify and support families in additional needs for support (e.g., families in extra needs for breastfeeding support). The community health nurses’ communication with the families will be guided by a dialogue tool developed as part of the Bloom Study to increase the reach of the intervention across families of varying social diversities. The tool is designed to increase the community health nurses’ insight and understanding of the families’ history, experiences and circumstances, enabling them to tailor the dialogue and guidance specifically to the needs, situation and contexts of each family. The dialogue tool focuses on key elements such as socio-demographic factors, social challenges, social support systems, expectations to parenthood and each other as parents, parental self-efficacy, attitudes towards breastfeeding, family lifestyle, and the parents’ own childhood experiences. Moreover, the community health nurse will screen the expectant mother and father for symptoms of perinatal depression using the Edinburg Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [137]. When depression is identified, the community health nurses will follow up according to standard procedures in the municipality.

The home visits at child age of 18 months and 30 months aim to stimulate and support maintenance of desired changes achieved and to address new challenges which arise as the child becomes older.

Based on the existing strong competences of Danish community health nurses, supervision and guidance during home visits will be provided through an individualized, motivational, empowering, and supportive dialog adapted to the needs of the family. All home visits and telephone consultations will be conducted by the same community health nurse to increase continuity and sense of security. The home visits may be supported by an interpreter or be extended in duration to accommodate families who do not speak or understand Danish, if deemed necessary by the community health nurse. Moreover, if significant challenges are identified by the community health nurse, the family may be offered additional home visits or referred to the general practitioner or to supplementary supportive services within the municipality (standard procedure). Throughout all home visits, the community health nurse will strongly encourage the participation of both parents.

Proactive telephone consultations

Between home visits, parents will receive up to eight proactive telephone consultations (Fig. 3). The first five consultations (approximately ten days to four months after birth) aim to promote breastfeeding and introduce best practices for bottle feeding. The community health nurse will prioritize identifying and supporting families where the mother is at risk of early cessation of breastfeeding. All families will receive the first and last of these five consultations, while the remaining three consultations will be allocated to mothers identified as being at risk of early cessation of breastfeeding by the community health nurse. To accommodate families who do not speak or understand Danish, these early telephone consultations can include an interpreter, be extended in duration, or be conducted as home visits.

A consultation will be provided to all families seven months after birth and aims to support optimal complementary feeding, including nutritional content, variety, and texture in the infant diet. Moreover, all families will receive a telephone consultation when the child is 12 months. At this point in time many children start day-care in Denmark and in most families (81%) both parents return to work or education [38]. This can be a stressful time for the family where extra support and guidance may be needed for maintaining healthy habits and behaviors adopted over the preceding 12 months. Finally, all families will receive a telephone consultation when the child is 24 months, aiming to stimulate and support maintenance of desired changes achieved.

Parent groups

As part of the Bloom Study’s focus on involving and empowering both parents, intervention municipalities will ensure that parent group offerings for both parents are available and that they are encouraged to participate. The goal is to provide both parents with a supportive environment where they can share experiences and challenges of first-time parenthood, empower them, and offer social support during parental leave.

In most Danish municipalities the community health nurses already initiate the formation of mothers’ groups. The community health nurses typically compose groups of four to six newly mothers who have given birth to a child around the same time and who live in the same neighborhood. Often, the mothers self-organize the meetings and host group meetings at home. In 2022, a new Act on parental leave came into force in Denmark earmarking 11 weeks of the parental leave to each parent. The remaining 26 weeks can be freely divided between the mother and the father or co-parents. The earmarked weeks are forfeited if not used before the child turns one. Consequently, over the coming years many more fathers or co-parents are expected to take parental leave [138].

A video library to parents

The Bloom video library aims to offer reliable information and inspiration, both in breadth and depth, through a single platform. It features 62 videos in Danish organized according to the intervention topics: food and meals, movement and motor skills, screen use, sleep, and family sense of security. Additionally, it includes four videos specifically focused on fatherhood, based on the initial needs assessment indicating that fathers could benefit from targeted parenting information.

The content includes e.g., brief oral presentations of key information, visual instructions for breastfeeding, bottle-feeding and cooking, and demonstration of movement and cooking activities.

The videos include families from various ethnic backgrounds, with some edited to reflect greater cultural diversity. Recent evidence suggests that the use of illustrations and translated materials can enhance the effectiveness of intervention components targeting families from minority ethnic backgrounds or with low SEP [37]. Furthermore, interviews with parents conducted during the initial needs assessment for the development of the Bloom intervention revealed a variety of preferences regarding the types of materials they found most useful. While some parents preferred written materials, others favored video content. Implementation strategies

Several activities and structures have been developed to support the community health nurses’ delivery of the intervention. A five-day training course qualifies and prepares the community health nurses for the Bloom Study. In intervention municipalities, the course is offered to all community health nurses working with infants. The training covers specialized knowledge and additional competencies in supporting healthy weight development in children, focusing on food and meals, movement and motor skills, sleep, screen use, and family sense of security. It also guides the community health nurses in implementing the intervention components. Moreover, the course offers a half-day training focused on fatherhood. The aim is to is to upskill community health nurses in using strategies to include and engage fathers in home visits and parenthood, as well as in effective communication with them. Finally, the course includes a full day session on diversity competencies in healthcare. The aim of this session is to enhance community health nurses’ abilities to interact appropriately with families from diverse backgrounds, including different SEP, age groups, sexual orientations, ethnic backgrounds, religions, and gender.

In addition, all participating community health nurses are trained in Understanding Your Baby (described above) through a two-day course, followed by two supervisions and two brush-up sessions. The course includes lectures on infant socioemotional development, and community health nurses practice sharing age-specific, research-based knowledge with parents by reviewing video clips of ADBB screenings. The focus is on infant behavior and underlying needs, how parents can support socioemotional development, and the importance of each area for long-term development.

Throughout the project period, community health nurses may attend peer consulting meetings frequently with teachers from the training course to discuss and get feedback on cases from their daily practice and integrate acquired skills from the course [142–144]. Moreover, community health nurses receive an implementation guide [142, 144, 145] that outlines the Bloom Study, the latest research on healthy weight development, risk factors for obesity, and practical strategies for guiding families (described above).

Prior to intervention start, municipalities recruited one or two project ambassadors among their community health nurses. They are responsible for receiving and disseminating information about the Bloom Study from the research team to their colleagues and serve as local project ambassadors. The ambassadors are encouraged to regularly discuss and solve implementation challenges during staff meetings, coordinate and participate in Bloom network meetings, and ensure that the intervention aligns with local practices. Ongoing communication and meetings between the research group and the intervention municipality further support the implementation. The municipalities also receive partial financial compensation for activities additional to usual care, such as home visits and telephone consultations. Detailed descriptions of the implementation strategies are published elsewhere.

Control group

The control group of first-time parents will receive the existing publicly available postnatal care services in the 11 municipalities (previously described). Moreover, for parents in the control group there will be additional visits at age 18 months, 30 months, and 36 months for the purpose of data collection only. During the study period, the families in the control municipalities will receive several newsletters covering themes not related to the intervention focus, but still with relevance for first time parents.

Control municipalities’ participation in the Bloom Study does not restrict or limit their opportunities for implementing other changes or initiatives in the services provided by the community health nurses.

We apply a wait-list design to motivate the control municipalities to stay in the control group during the study period. When all children within the intervention group reach the age of 12 months, the community health nurses within the control municipalities will be offered free participation in the Bloom course. Also, each control municipality will be offered partial financial compensation to implement the Bloom intervention for 50 first-time families and their child.

Data collection methods

We will apply multiple data collection methods to assess intervention effects on the primary and secondary outcomes: (1) Parent questionnaires, (2) anthropometric measurements of child length/height and weight, (3) bio-electrical impedance analysis to measure child body composition, (4) information from national registers, and (5) community health nurses’ electronic records.

Data will be collected simultaneously in the intervention and control groups. The various measures and data collection procedures are described in more detail below.

Parent questionnaires

Over the study period, each participating mother and father or co-parent from the intervention and control groups will be invited to complete a total of four questionnaires at home at child age of 6, 12, 24 and 36 months.

The themes covered in the questionnaires will include background information (e.g., parental place of birth, employment, sick leave due to child illness, language spoken at home, family structure, daycare start date), child health (e.g., chronic health conditions and disabilities), child well-being (e.g., social and emotional well-being), parents’ health (e.g., height and weight, mother’s weight gain during pregnancy, mother’s smoking during pregnancy), parents’ well-being (e.g., parental stress and family functioning), food and meals (e.g., breastfeeding, bottle feeding, complementary feeding, child eating behaviour, parental feeding practices, family meal habits), movement (e.g., child motor skills, parents’ stimulation of child motor skills), screen use (child’s screen use, parents’ screen use), and sleep (e.g., child sleep patterns, parents’ practices related to child sleep, parental self-efficacy related to child sleep).

Anthropometric data including measures of weight and length/height

Data on children’s weight and length/height will be collected at multiple time points throughout the study.

Data sources at each time point: Birth weight and length is obtained from the Medical Birth Registry [157]. Information on weight and length recorded by the community health nurses during routine universal visits is retrieved from the journal system via access to the community health nurses’ records (see below) for both the intervention and control groups. Community health nurses in the intervention group will record weight and length/height in a secure online survey database (REDCap – Research Electronic Data Capture) hosted at OPEN Storage, OPEN, Open Patient data Explorative Network, Odense University Hospital, Region of Southern Denmark [158], during the additional intervention home visits when the child is 18 and 30 months old. For the control group, these measurements at 18 and 30 months will be conducted by research staff (community health nursing students, qualified nurses undergoing further education, and nursing students) during scheduled measurement visits, with the data recorded in REDCap. In both the intervention and control groups, weight and height will be measured and recorded in REDCap at 36 months by research staff during measurement visits to all participating families.

All parents will receive a gift card to a local grocery store of the total value of DKK 300 (USD 42) at the 36 months measurement visit.

Measuring tools: Weight will be measured nearest to 0.1 g using a handhold beam scale or a handhold hanging scale. Length will be measured nearest to 0.1 cm using a Mobile Measuring Mat for Infants and Toddlers Seca 210, as described in the Danish National guidelines [159]. From 24 months, height will be measured to the nearest 0.1 cm standing with the back against the wall and the child looking straight ahead. Since no measuring tools are described in the Danish National guidelines for measuring child height and weight after 24 months, the measurements at 30 and 36 months will be standardized by providing the community health nurses and nursing students with transportable scales Seca 878 and measuring tape. The community health nurses in both intervention and control municipalities will receive thorough written measurement instructions prior to the measurement rounds.

BMI z-scores will be calculated using the WHO Child Growth Standards taking into account the infant’s sex and age [160].

Bio-electrical impedance

Body composition at age 36 months will be assessed by community health nurse students and nursing students using bioimpedance analysis during the home visit. Bioimpedance analysis is feasible to use in children [161, 162]. It is simple, easy to use, and requires less co-operation from the child due to the fast measurement time in comparison to other widely used methods, such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry [161, 163]. Bioimpedance will be measured using the Impedimed SFB7 device (ImpediMed, Brisbane, Australia) to calculate fat mass (FM) and fat-free mass (FFM). Based on previous literature on young children, we will use reference values for body composition for children to identify excess adiposity [164].

National registers

Denmark has a strong tradition of collecting excellent and reliable information on health and social conditions using nationwide registers and health databases. Using CPR numbers, it is possible to link data from one or more registers with individual-level, register-based information [165].

From national health and socioeconomic registers at Statistics Denmark and the National Health Data Authority, we will obtain information on eligible children and their parents with the purposes of: (1) Comparing socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., parental education, household income, ethnic background) of participants with eligible non-participation to characterize the attrition over the study period and thereby evaluate generalizability of the study results, (2) investigating long-term effectiveness of the intervention on the primary outcome (weight status when starting school at age five to six and finalizing elementary school at age 15–16) and secondary outcomes (e.g., child well-being and eating habits when finalizing elementary school at age 15–16), (3) qualifying the evaluation of the intervention effectiveness by providing information on important co-variates (e.g., child birth weight and length, child diseases and disabilities, medication, parental somatic and psychiatric disease, use of daycare and periods of maternity and paternity leave) to be used in multiple imputation of missing data and as prognostic variables to increase precision of effect estimates, (4) lowering the burden of the parental questionnaire survey, and (5) informing the health economic evaluation of the intervention.

Community health nurses’ electronic records

In Denmark, community health nurses are obligated to keep and update health records and systematically register details about the child at all home visits [117]. Records are completed separately for the child and each parent and include the child’s and parents’ CPR-numbers which allow data to be linked with national health and sociodemographic registers with complete data from the entire population. Beyond providing information on child weight and length/height, the record data will qualify the effectiveness evaluation by providing information on important co-variates. Key information from the child’s and the parents’ records is child weight and length, duration of full and partly breastfeeding, passive smoking in the home, remarks to child’s nutrition and eating habits, social contact, sleep, motor skills, and communication – including information on relevant referrals, remarks to parents’ vulnerability and parent-child relation, family social networks, screening results on child social contact and development (ADBB) [121], and on child gross motor skills (Alberta Infant Motor Scale - AIMS) [166], and screening results on parental perinatal depression.

Statistical methods

Analysis and presentation of data will be reported according to the Consort guidelines for cluster-RCTs [167].

The primary analysis of BMI at child age of 36 months will be conducted based on an intention-to-treat analysis using multilevel multivariate regression, taking the hierarchical nature of data into account (children clustered in municipalities). The model will include intervention group (intervention group versus control group) and prognostic variables (see below) as fixed effects. The intervention variable is the main test of effect. Multiple imputations will be used to impute missing data as suggested by Graham [168]. The primary outcome will be tested with significance level of 5%, and a blinded version of the data will be used for data analyses.

General and generalized linear regression models will be used to study continuous and binary outcomes. If the model assumptions of the general linear model are not fulfilled, transformation of the outcome will be performed. To increase precision of effect estimates, analyses will be adjusted for prognostics variables [169], such as SEP at time of childbirth, ethnic background, maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, presence of gestational diabetes, birth weight, and maternal and paternal smoking during pregnancy [3]. The final prognostic variables will be identified from the literature and pre-specified in a statistical analysis plan before the analyses are carried out.

Children with chronic health problems that are likely to influence height, weight, levels of physical activity or eating habits will be excluded from the analyses.

To explore effects of the Bloom intervention on growth patterns from birth to age 36 months, sensitivity analyses will be performed using all data on weight and length/height from the entire study period. This model will be analyzed using a repeated measures mixed-effects linear model with child and municipality as random effects. Time point (few days after birth, week of returning home, within first month, 2 months, 4–6 months, 8–10 months, 18 months, 30 months, and 36 months), intervention group (intervention group versus control group) and interaction between time and intervention group (in addition to prognostic variables) will be included as fixed effects. The interaction between time point and intervention group is the term of interest.

Differential effects of the intervention on the primary outcome by SEP and ethnic background will be explored by stratification [170, 171].

Analyses of the predefined secondary outcomes will be analyzed with no p-value adjustment due to multiplicity and the interpretation of these results will be assessed in the light of multiple testing. In papers on secondary outcomes, the predefined secondary outcomes will be explicitly reported as predefined secondary outcomes. Per-protocol and complete case analyses of the primary outcome and secondary outcomes will also be carried out as part of sensitivity analyses. Also, interim analyses of selected secondary outcomes will be conducted. To avoid unblinding the analysis of the primary outcome, these analyses will not be supplemented with per protocol analyses. The trial will continue irrespectively of the results of the interim analyses of secondary outcomes (no stopping rules).

We will apply mediation analysis to test our program theory and hypothesized assumptions of whether changes in specific determinants will lead to changes in the primary outcome [172].

All analyses, which are not pre-planned in the final study statistical analysis plan published before the start of the trial, will be labelled as exploratory analyses.

Process evaluation

We will conduct a comprehensive, theory-based, mixed-methods process evaluation of the Bloom Study, which will involve collecting, analyzing, and integrating both quantitative and qualitative research [173, 174]. The overall aim of the process evaluation is to understand how and why the intervention works (or does not work), including an assessment of implementation, mechanisms of impact, and contextual factors. The process evaluation will be guided by the UK Medical Research Council (MRC) guidance [175, 176] and will incorporate key concepts from, among others, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [143], and the Implementation Outcomes Taxonomy [177]. In brief, the process evaluation data will include questionnaire data from parents, community health nurses, and managing community health nurses; field observations of home visits; interviews and focus groups with parents, community health nurses, and managing community health nurses; and usage statistics from the video library. The process evaluation includes a specific focus on how socially diverse families, such as those from low SEP backgrounds and ethnic minority families, receive and experience the intervention. Potential unintended outcomes of the Bloom Study will be explored, such as unhealthy child weight development, parental stress, stigmatization, self-blame, decreased trust in the community health nurse, and reduced level of overweight and obesity in parents. Questionnaires completed by the managing community health nurses in the control municipalities will be used to collect information on the postnatal care services provided, including any relevant changes made during the study period.

A detailed description of the process evaluation design will be published separately.

Health economic evaluation

Overweight and obesity in early childhood have been linked to lower health-related-quality-of-life and higher costs in early adolescence and adulthood. The health economic analysis will evaluate the lifetime cost-effectiveness of the Bloom intervention compared to usual care. The primary economic outcome will be the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), expressed as cost per Quality-Adjusted-Life-Year (QALY) gained. The analysis will be performed from a Danish national healthcare perspective. Direct intervention costs as well as total health care costs will be included. A micro-costing approach will be applied to assess the intervention costs and the average costs of usual care. We will develop a decision-analytic model to assess the long-term mean costs and QALYs of the Bloom intervention and usual care. The model will take the approach, that change in BMI is directly associated with costs and effects and will predict downstream costs and health outcomes in relation to BMI. The structure of the model will be informed by existing published decision models that evaluate overweight and obesity. We will use published data from systematic reviews, meta-analyses and new analyses of existing survey and register-based datasets to develop the model. The model will be refined based on inputs from experts from the Bloom Study. Appropriate sensitivity analysis will be conducted. In addition, the health economic evaluation will quantify the equity impacts of the intervention to assess potential trade-offs between health maximation and equity. The distributional equity impact of the Bloom intervention will be assessed by expanding the decision-analytic model to include input parameters (mortality, QALYs and costs (treatment and health care costs)) for different SEPs estimated based on age, sex, and BMI. The net health benefit and equity impact of the Bloom intervention and usual care will be calculated and plotted on an equity-efficiency plane.

A comprehensive description of the health economic evaluation will be published separately.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the Bloom Study is one of the first cluster-RCTs to examine whether a theory- and evidence-based intervention targeting infancy and toddlerhood can promote healthy weight in children in Denmark.

Important strengths of the Bloom Study include: (1) a transparent and systematic planning approach guided by the IM protocol, which included a thorough needs assessment and a co-creation process with community health nurses, families, and experts in childhood overweight and obesity, (2) integration of the intervention into a well-established and effective healthcare system, characterized by a high level of health promotion expertise and broad acceptance among families with young children in Denmark, (3) a strong design for evaluating effectiveness, with comprehensive process and health economic evaluations, (4) a multi-focus approach addressing food and meal habits, movement and motor skills, screen use, sleep, and family sense of security, (5) a high-dose intervention starting during pregnancy and continuing until 30 months postpartum, (6) focus on social inequality throughout all phases of intervention research, including development, implementation, and evaluation, (7) systematic integration of a range of intervention tools for implementers, increasing the likelihood of reaching families with diverse needs, circumstances, and resources, (8) a strong focus on both mothers, fathers across various family structures.

Establishing a strong foundation for the long-term sustainability of the Bloom intervention is a key objective throughout the project. Firstly, the comprehensive co-creation process at the outset was designed to guarantee a high degree of relevance, acceptability, and adaptability to existing practices, organizational structures, and values within the Danish community health nursing system. Secondly, the Bloom intervention is being integrated into a well-established and effective healthcare system, which is characterized by a high level of health promotion expertise and a broad acceptance among Danish families with young children. Thirdly, a sounding board consisting of key stakeholders in community health nursing from Danish municipalities will be established as part of the project’s organizational structure. This board will provide guidance to the implementation and scaling of the intervention. Members will include representatives from community health nursing management, municipal health promotion directors, Local Government Denmark, educational institutions, and relevant NGOs. Together, these initiatives will ensure a sustained focus on the intervention’s long-term sustainability throughout the project.

While the integration of the Bloom Study within an existing and well-functioning healthcare system supports its potential for long-term sustainability, it may also pose challenges for the planned project evaluation. Given the high quality of health promotion services already available to families with young children in Denmark, detecting significant differences between the intervention and control groups — and thus demonstrating significant effects — may prove challenging [113]. However, during the Bloom Study’s developmental phase, it became clear that community health nurses strongly desire concrete tools and initiatives to support healthy weight development in infants and toddlers. Additionally, the potential of the Danish system of community health nurses to promote healthy child weight development remains largely unexplored.

The long-term impacts of the Bloom Study include reducing childhood overweight and obesity, addressing social inequalities, and improving both psychosocial and physical health from childhood to adulthood. It is also expected to reduce societal healthcare costs related to obesity-related morbidity and enhance the quality of community health nurses’ practice, methods, and education.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for the Bloom Study was obtained from the Scientific Ethics Committee of the Capital Region (reference number H-24032126). Any subsequent protocol amendments will be submitted to the Scientific Ethics Committee of the Capital Region for evaluation and will also be updated in the registration at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06705400). The Bloom Study will be conducted in accordance with the Danish Code of Conduct for Research Integrity and the ethical principles in the current version of the Declaration of Helsinki. All custody holders of children participating in the study will provide written informed consent for their child’s and for their own participation in the study. We place special emphasis on avoiding weight-related stigmatization and negative focus on body weight. For example, all communication will carefully be prepared to ensure that children and families are not stigmatized. Additionally, the training course for community health nurses includes education on diversity competencies. Staff conducting data collection will be trained to ensure that families always feel comfortable and secure, and to know how to handle any issues that arise during measurement visits. Finally, it is crucial that the intervention at no point jeopardizes the families’ acceptance and trust in the community health nurse system. Implementation of the intervention will be based on the community health nurse’s evaluation of the family’s most urgent needs. If for example a family is experiencing severe social challenges, these will always be priority of the guidance and support provided by the community health nurse. A close continuous dialogue with community health nurses will support this ethical awareness.

Data management and data security

All collected data will be processed and stored in accordance with the Data Protection Act and comply with the GDPR (EU) 2016/679. Questionnaire data will be collected via a secure online survey database (REDCap) hosted at OPEN Storage, OPEN, Open Patient Data Explorative Network, Odense University Hospital, Region of Southern Denmark. All quantitative data applied in the study will be stored at Statistics Denmark, where analyses will be conducted.

Publication and dissemination

The trial’s pre-registered primary and secondary outcomes will be reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines and published in peer-reviewed journals. Additionally, the results will be presented at conferences to disseminate findings to the scientific community and relevant stakeholders. Lay summaries will also be made available on the project’s website for participating municipalities, local stakeholders, and parents. The authors and contributors to publications related to the project will be determined in line with the recommendations of the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICJME).

Study organization

The Bloom Study is anchored at the National Institute of Public Health, University of Southern Denmark. The study is led by a management group consisting of the the principal investigator (PI) (CTB), co-PI (MR), KRM, and TPP, and is carried out by the Bloom Study project group. The study is structured into three work packages (WPs): WP1 focuses on the effectiveness evaluation (led by MR), WP2 on the process evaluation (led by CTB), and WP3 on the health economic evaluation (led by LPK). Additionally, the study is supported and overseen by a Steering Committee, which monitors trial progress, advises on scientific credibility, reviews accumulated trial data, and makes recommendations on whether the trial should continue or if modifications to the protocol are necessary. The Steering Committee includes the Bloom Study management and project groups, along with senior researchers from the National Institute of Public Health, University of Southern Denmark; DaCHE - Danish Centre for Health Economics, University of Southern Denmark; Centre of Excellence in Early Intervention and Family Studies, Department of Psychology, Copenhagen University, Local Government Denmark, and a representative from the Danish National Organization of Leading Community Health Nurses. The study also has an International Advisory Board, consisting of prominent national and international scientists in the fields of child overweight and obesity, obesity prevention, community-based intervention research, nutrition epidemiology, and social epidemiology. In the spring 2025, a Sounding Board will be convened to discuss the study’s progress, dissemination, and potential strategies for scaling up the intervention if deemed appropriate. The project does not have an External Data Monitoring Committee due to the minimal risks associated with the intervention.
