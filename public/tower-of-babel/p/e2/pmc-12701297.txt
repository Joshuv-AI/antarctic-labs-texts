Drug Discovery Applications of Nitroso (Hetero)Arene Derivatives
Authors: Silvia Roscales, Aurelio G Csáky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrosoarenes exhibit a variety of biological and pharmacological activities. This review uncovers their utility as therapeutic agents, which extends to oxidative stress regulation, DNA damage and repair interaction, cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitors, anticancer, antiviral, antibacterial, antifungal, antiparasitic, anti‐inflammatory, and other miscellaneous effects. The synthesis of the most relevant targets is also reviewed.Keywords: antibiotics, antiproliferation, antiviral agents, nitroso compounds, ROS

Introduction

Nitroso derivatives of (hetero)aromatic compounds represent a fascinating and chemically diverse class of molecules characterized by the presence of a highly reactive nitroso functional group. This group is commonly found in biologically active molecules of medicinal interest, while nitroso compounds also play a key role as versatile precursors in organic synthesis and materials science. Among them, nitrosoarenes stand out for their exceptional reactivity, acting as both electrophiles and nucleophiles across a wide variety of transformations, which underscores their importance in modern organic chemistry. This review explores their pharmacological activities, including antiviral, antibacterial, antifungal, antiparasitic, anticancer, anti‐inflammatory, and neuroprotective applications, alongside their mechanisms of action involving oxidative stress, DNA damage, and enzyme inhibition. Synthetic strategies and structure–activity relationship (SAR) analyses reveal the importance of structural modifications in enhancing efficacy and mitigating toxicity. Specific compounds, such as NU6027 and nitrosopyrimidines, showcase promising therapeutic potential by targeting cell cycle regulators, DNA repair pathways, and infectious agents, addressing critical gaps in oncology, infectious diseases, and neurodegenerative therapies. Despite these advancements, studies underscore the genotoxic and environmental risks posed by nitroso compounds, emphasizing the need for careful evaluation. This comprehensive review balances the opportunities for drug discovery with the challenges of toxicity and ecological impact, paving the way for further research into these chemically intriguing compounds. The synthesis of these biologically relevant compounds is reviewed as well.

Benzene Derivatives

Toxicity of Nitrosobenzene (NOB)

Multiple studies have documented the toxicity of nitroso derivatives of benzene and aniline. Nitrosobenzene (NOB) is a metabolite of nitrobenzene and aniline, two widely distributed environmental pollutants. NOB is formed during the enzymatic reduction of nitrobenzene to aniline via intermediate steps involving phenylhydroxylamine. Importantly, NOB can undergo nonenzymatic reduction by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), yielding a phenylhydronitroxide radical. This radical is able to reduce Cu(II) to Cu(I), thereby initiating a redox cycle that facilitates the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide, ultimately producing highly reactive species capable of damaging DNA.[ 1 ]

Studies conducted using NOB to investigate the mechanisms underlying aniline‐induced splenic toxicity revealed that, upon administration of NOB to male Sprague‐Dawley rats, decreased erythrocyte counts and increased methemoglobin levels at higher doses were observed.[ 2 ] Total iron content in the spleens of NOB‐treated rats increased significantly in a dose‐dependent manner, with nonheme iron following a similar trend. Splenic lipid peroxidation also exhibited a dose‐dependent response. Malondialdehyde (MDA)‐protein adducts were markedly elevated across all NOB‐treated groups, and protein oxidation, measured by carbonyl content, was also increased. These findings indicate that NOB acts as a splenotoxin and may contribute to aniline‐induced splenic toxicity, with oxidative stress as a likely underlying mechanism.

While oxidative stress is a major factor, further studies have shown that NOB also promotes toxicity through protein adduction, particularly targeting hemoglobin, which provides additional insight into its molecular reactivity. Investigation of the adducts formed during the in vitro reaction between NOB and human hemoglobin (Hb) using mass spectrometry allowed for the identification of a specific adduct on Cysβ93 with high sensitivity and specificity.[ 3 ] Since the reaction between Hb and NOB appears to require the presence of oxy‐ and deoxyhemoglobin, it was speculated that in vivo exposure to even low doses of NOB may promote adduct formation. A combined mass spectrometry‐based proteomic analysis and in silico quantum mechanical calculations approach was used to investigate protein adduction by NOB.[ 4 ] It was confirmed that Cys thiol moieties, rather than His or Lys residues, are the preferred reaction sites for NOB. Key reaction conditions were also evaluated, including protein‐to‐metabolite molar ratio, exposure time, pH, and the presence of reducing agents and O2, which influence the formation of sulfinamide and sulfonamide adducts in purified Hb. While these results align with previous reports on selective β93Cys modification, two additional adduction sites were identified: β112Cys and α104Cys. These studies provide deeper insights into hemoglobin modification by aniline metabolites. NOB is the main metabolite of aniline.

Toxicity of Nitroso‐Aniline and Nitroso‐Naphthalene Derivatives

The ability of four environmental chemicals, aniline, NOB, N,N‐dimethylaniline, and N,N‐dimethyl‐4‐nitrosoaniline (DMNA), to induce free radical formation and cellular damage in HepG2 cells was performed.[ 5 ] Only DMNA‐generated free radicals triggered lactate dehydrogenase release and caused morphological changes, primarily in the cytosol. Preincubation with N‐acetylcysteine and 2,2‐dipyridyl prevented free radical formation, suggesting DMNA metabolism leads to protein radical generation, contributing to its toxicity. The study proposes that the captodative effect stabilizes DMNA radicals, enhancing protein radical formation.

Also, 2‐nitrosonaphthalene exhibits genotoxic effects,[ 6 ] as it induces DNA damage in Salmonella typhimurium and bacteriophages. It inactivates S. typhimurium repair‐deficient mutants more effectively than wild‐type strains, suggesting that it causes genome damage that can be repaired by normal bacterial mechanisms. Additionally, it triggers the induction of bacteriophage P221 from lysogens, indicating interference with genetic regulation, although it does not directly inactivate the phage. Moreover, in the presence of divalent cations and NADH, 2‐nitrosonaphthalene undergoes nonenzymatic activation, leading to bacteriophage genome damage. These findings suggest that 2‐nitrosonaphthalene is a potentially carcinogenic compound with the ability to induce genetic damage through multiple pathways.

2‐Naphthylamine, a known bladder carcinogen, is present in cigarette smoke. Smoking is a risk factor for both bladder and lung cancer. 2‐Nitroso‐1‐naphthol, a metabolite of 2‐napththylamine, induces oxidative DNA damage.[ 7 ] It likely contributes to bladder carcinogenesis through its metabolites near the bladder, although its genotoxicity may not be specific to urothelial cells. Oxidative DNA damage, alongside DNA adduct formation, may play a role in smoking‐related bladder cancer.

Antiviral Activity of 3‐Nitrosobenzamide

On the other hand, 3‐nitrosobenzamide is known for its ability to chelate zinc from the retroviral‐type zinc finger of nucleocapsid protein 7 (NCp7).[ 8 ] NCp7 is a fundamental component of the HIV virion core that plays a pivotal role in multiple stages of viral replication.[ 9 ] Its structural conservation across all HIV strains, including those from patients with prior therapy exposure, underscores its potential as a target for next‐generation antiretroviral therapies. Unlike conventional approaches that have struggled with drug resistance and pharmacological limitations, NCp7 inhibition presents a compelling alternative for broad‐spectrum antiviral intervention. Other strategies, including viral entry inhibitors like dextran sulfate and soluble CD4, as well as reverse transcriptase and protease inhibitors, have faced significant hurdles in clinical applications. These challenges highlight the urgent need for novel therapeutic agents. NCp7, characterized by its two conserved retroviral zinc finger motifs essential for replication, offers a unique and underexplored avenue for drug development. Its disruption could pave the way for innovative antiviral strategies capable of overcoming resistance barriers and enhancing treatment efficacy against HIV. In this context, 3‐nitrosobenzamide effectively inhibits acute infection of human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV‐1) in cultured human lymphocytes.[ 8 ] Notably, it does not interfere with viral attachment to lymphocytes or the enzymatic activities of key viral proteins, including reverse transcriptase, protease, and integrase. However, it completely disrupts reverse transcription, preventing the formation of proviral DNA. This indicates that 3‐nitrosobenzamide exerts its antiviral effect by impairing the essential role of p7NC in ensuring accurate proviral DNA synthesis during the infectious phase of the HIV‐1 life cycle.

Antiparasitic and Antibacterial Activities of Nitroso‐Naphthalene Derivatives

Nitroso‐naphthylamine derivatives 2 have demonstrated potent efficacy in combating schistosomiasis (Scheme 1 ).[ 10 ] Schistosomiasis, also known as bilharziasis, is the second most prevalent tropical parasitic disease after malaria. It is caused by blood‐dwelling flukes of the genus Schistosoma, with S. haematobium, S. japonicum, and S. mansoni being the most common pathogenic species in Asia, Africa, and South America.[ 11 ] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), schistosomiasis affects over 249 million people and accounts for approximately 280,000 deaths annually.[ 12 ] Despite extensive research efforts, no effective vaccine is currently available. Praziquantel, a tetrahydroisoquinoline derivative, remains the only widely used, cost‐effective treatment for all Schistosoma species. A series of N 1,N 1‐cycloalkyl‐N 3‐(4‐nitrosonaphthalen‐1‐yl)propane‐1,3‐diamines[ 10 ] 2 was prepared by nitrosation of the corresponding naphthylamines 1 and essayed against S. mansoni in mice. Several of the tested compounds demonstrated high activity. Among them, the most promising candidate, 1‐[3‐(4‐nitroso‐1‐naphthylamino)propyl]piperidine 2c, also exhibited potent therapeutic effects in rhesus monkeys.

It is worth noting that the stability of these nitroso compounds varies significantly depending on their side chains, as tested using UV spectroscopy. Side chains containing a dialkylaminoethyl group were particularly unstable, making it challenging to obtain the desired products. However, introducing a cyclic terminal amine (2a, 2c, 2e) improved stability. These findings highlight how structural modifications influence can be used to deliver analogs with improved stability.

Building on the impact of structural features on the biological activity of nitroso‐naphthylamines, related nitroso‐naphthol derivatives have been explored as ligands for tin species, yielding organotin compounds with marked antibacterial properties. Nitroso‐naphthol derivatives are able to coordinate with tin species. Organotin compounds exhibit a broad spectrum of biological activity, particularly as antifungal, antibiotic, and antibacterial agents.[ 13 , 14 ] Treatment of 1‐nitroso‐2‐naphthol 3 with a 1:1 or 1:2 molar ratio of Sn(IV) precursors permitted the synthesis of the organotin derivatives 4 (Scheme 2 ).[ 15 ] They exhibited antibacterial activity comparable to or exceeding that of streptomycin sulfate when tested against Staphylococcus aureus, B. subtillis, and Salmonella typhi. Compound 4c was found to be rather equally good for all three types of bacteria, whereas the maximum activity was observed for 4d against S. typhi.

Six‐Membered Monocylic Heterocycles

NU6027

Nitrosopyrimidine derivatives can act as inhibitors of cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs) and can provide useful therapeutic derivatives for use in treatment of tumors or other cell proliferation disorders. NU6027 (Scheme 3 ) was first discovered as a small molecule inhibitor of CDKs 1 and 2. Its development and research are still ongoing, and it has been explored in preclinical models for its potential in treating diseases where CDK plays a significant role, such as cancer and neurodegenerative diseases.

a) Structures of NU6027 and NU2058. b) Hydrogen bonds with CDKs. c) NU6027 bound to CDK2. Hydrogen bonds between the CDK2 backbone at residues Glu 81 and Leu 83 and NU6027 are illustrated by thin lines. Reproduced with permission.[ 18 ] Copyright 2025, American Chemical Society.

CDK1 and CDK2 are key enzymes in regulating the cell cycle, which is essential for cell division and growth.[ 16 , 17 ] They are part of a family of serine/threonine protein kinases that require binding to specific regulatory proteins called cyclins to become active. CDK1 controls the transition from the G2 phase to mitosis (M phase), essential for cell division, while CDK2 regulates the G1 to S phase transition, facilitating DNA replication. Both CDKs are activated by binding to cyclins (cyclin B for CDK1 and cyclins E and A for CDK2) and are tightly controlled by a network of inhibitors, such as the p21 and p27 proteins, to ensure proper cell cycle progression. Dysregulation of CDKs can lead to uncontrolled cell division, making them important targets in cancer therapies.

NU6027 is a potent and adenosine tri phosphate (ATP)‐competitive inhibitor of both CDK1 and CDK2 (Ki = 2.5 μM and 1.3 μM, respectively), developed from O 6‐cyclohexylmethylguanine (NU2058).[ 18 ] The 5‐nitroso group provides an intramolecular hydrogen bond to the amine group at the 6‐position (Scheme 3), forcing the molecule to assume a pseudopurine geometry. This ensures that the remaining hydrogen on the nitrogen adopts the correct geometry for binding to CDKs, forming a triplet of hydrogen bonds to Glu 81, to Leu 83, and to Leu 83, similarly to NU2058. In addition, against human tumor cells, NU6027 is growth inhibitory in vitro (mean GI50 values of μM), with a pattern of sensitivity distinct from alvocidib (flavopiridol) and olomoucine.

In addition, NU6027 is a potent inhibitor of cellular ataxia telangiectasia and rad3‐related (ATR) activity (IC50 = 6.7 μM) and enhances the cytotoxicity of hydroxyurea and cisplatin in an ATR‐dependent manner.[ 19 ] ATR inhibitors block the activity of ATR (ataxia‐telangiectasia and Rad3‐related), a protein kinase involved in the DNA damage response and repair, especially during replication stress.[ 20 ] By inhibiting ATR, these compounds prevent DNA repair, making cancer cells with damaged DNA more vulnerable to treatments like chemotherapy or radiation, thus holding potential for cancer therapy. NU6027 mitigates G2/M arrest following DNA damage, inhibits RAD51 focus formation, and increases the cytotoxicity of major DNA‐damaging anticancer therapies, though it does not affect the antimitotic agent paclitaxel. In A2780 cells, sensitization to cisplatin was most pronounced in cells with functional p53 and mismatch repair (MMR), while temozolomide sensitization was greatest in p53 mutant cells with functional MMR. Importantly, NU6027 exhibits synthetic lethality when DNA single‐strand break repair is compromised, either by PARP inhibition or defects in XRCC1.

NU6027 also causes transient receptor potential channel 5 (TRPC5) inhibition, a calcium‐permeable ion channel involved in processes like calcium signaling, neuronal excitability, and vascular function.[ 21 ] Inhibiting TRPC5 may have therapeutic potential for treating neurological disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer by affecting cellular processes, such as proliferation, migration, and survival. Inhibition of TRPC5 by NU6027 effectively reduces zinc accumulation and prevents neuronal cell death, indicating that NU6027 could serve as a potential therapeutic tool to protect against traumatic brain injury (TBI).[ 22 ] TBI can lead to lasting physical, cognitive, and behavioral changes, often resulting in permanent disability. Following the initial brain injury, excessive zinc accumulation in neurons contributes to secondary damage, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and cognitive impairment, all of which exacerbate the injury.

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a global health threat, with 10 million cases and 1.6 million deaths in 2017. Poor treatment adherence and long therapy durations (6–9 months) have contributed to drug resistance. Multidrug‐resistant TB (MDR‐TB) resists first‐line drugs (isoniazid, rifampin), while extensively drug‐resistant TB (XDR‐TB) also resists fluoroquinolones and second‐line injectables. TDR‐TB is nearly untreatable. Current treatments are costly, toxic, and lengthy (9–18 months). There is an urgent need for a shorter, more effective TB regimen that minimizes drug interactions and combats drug‐resistant and latent TB infections.[ 23 ] By using a phenotypic screening using a library of pharmacologically active compounds, NU6027 has been identified as a novel antimycobacterial compound. NU6027 inhibited Mycobacterium bovis bacillus calmette‐guérin growth in vitro and demonstrated cross‐reactivity with M. tuberculosis protein kinases PknD and PknG. Structural and docking analyses suggest that PknG and PknD possess a unique binding site stereochemistry that favors NU6027 binding over other Ser/Thr protein kinases. Additionally, NU6027 treatment induced proapoptotic gene expression in macrophages and inhibited M. tuberculosis growth in both macrophages and mouse tissues. These findings highlight NU6027 as a promising lead compound for developing new antimycobacterial agents.[ 24 ]

Aside from NU6027, other nitrosopyrimidines have been evaluated as CDK inhibitors. A set of compounds (Scheme 4 ) (7a‐f, 8a‐c, 10a,b) was synthesized and evaluated as potential inhibitors of CDKs 1 and 2, along with an investigation of the SARs related to NU6027.[ 25 ] However, in this study, NU6027 remained the most potent inhibitor of CDK1 and CDK2.

Similar to previous studies with O 6‐cyclohexylmethylpurines, pyrimidines with carboxamide (16a) or sulfonamide (16b) groups at the 4‐position exhibited potent inhibition, with IC50 values against CDK2 of 34.0 and 1.0 nM, respectively. Thus, the introduction of a carboxamide or sulfonamide group at the 4‐position of the pyrimidine 2‐arylamino ring resulted in a dramatic increase in activity over the parent nitrosopyrimidine NU6027 (IC50 = 2.2 μM).

The crystal structure of the 4‐carboxamide derivative 16a complexed with phosphoThr160 CDK2/cyclin A (Scheme 5) confirmed the expected binding mode and revealed an additional interaction between the carboxamide and an aspartate residue. These findings align with the known binding mode of NU6027 in the ATP‐binding site of CDK2 (Scheme 3) and with SAR observed for analogous purine compounds. Two compounds, 16c and 16d, showed excellent potency against CDK2, with IC50 values of 0.7 and 0.8 nM, respectively. In addition, excellent selectivity within the CDK family was found for 16c.

Modification of the 5‐substituent in the 2,6‐diaminopyrimidine series revealed that the 5‐nitroso group was optimal for CDK inhibition, while 5‐formyl and 5‐acetyl substituents were also well tolerated at this position.[ 28 ] Sulfonamide derivative 16e (IC50 = 1.6 nM) was also identified as a potent CDK2 inhibitor (Scheme 6 ).

PI3K and mTOR inhibitors are drugs that target the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, which is frequently overactive in cancer, promoting uncontrolled cell growth.[ 29 ] PI3K inhibitors, such as Idelalisib and Alpelisib, block specific isoforms of PI3K to slow tumor progression, while mTOR inhibitors, like Everolimus and Temsirolimus, inhibit mTOR to regulate cell growth and metabolism. Additionally, dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitors, such as BEZ235 and GDC‐0980, simultaneously target both molecules to improve effectiveness and reduce resistance. These inhibitors play a crucial role in cancer therapy by slowing tumor growth and enhancing the efficacy of other treatments. 2,6‐Diaminopyrimidines featuring an O 4‐cyclohexylmethyl substituent and a C5‐nitroso group (compounds 16b, NU6027) induced cell cycle phase alterations and exhibited growth inhibitory activity (GI50 < 20 µM).[ 30 ] Among them, compound 16b (Scheme 5) demonstrated potent CDK2 inhibition (IC50 = 0.1 nM) along with PI3K inhibition and was cytotoxic at growth‐inhibitory concentrations. Consistent with its kinase inhibition profile, compound 16b reduced phospho‐Rb and phospho‐rS6 levels at GI50 concentrations. In HCT116 and HT29 cell lines, PI3K inhibition correlated with cell growth suppression, independent of CDK2 activity, while mTOR inhibition did not significantly influence growth inhibition. Notably, compound 16b, a dual CDK2/PI3K inhibitor, effectively induced apoptosis and exhibited cytotoxicity, suggesting that simultaneous inhibition of CDK2 and PI3K may be more effective than targeting either kinase alone.

To further elucidate the SAR of 2,4‐diaminopyrimidine CDK2 inhibitors, aliphatic amino substituents were introduced at position 2 (Scheme 7 ).[ 31 ] The incorporation of linear or less sterically hindered amines resulted in compounds with slightly improved activity compared to the lead compound. In contrast, introducing bulkier amino substituents led to a decrease in activity. No significant activity improvements were achieved in terms of biological activity. The most active nitroso compound was compound 20 (IC50 = 0.16 μM).

A computational study integrating various molecular dynamics (MD) simulations was conducted to explain the significant differences in binding affinities of three structurally related nitrosopyrimidines toward CDK2 (Scheme 8 ).[ 32 ] Free energy surface exploration using well‐tempered metadynamics revealed a characteristic binding pose stabilized by a triplet of hydrogen bonds in the two most active compounds (20 and 21). Additionally, for compound 20, a stable hydroxyl‐mediated interaction with Asp86 was identified, which persisted throughout unbiased MD simulations.

Binding affinities of nitrosopyrimidines 20, 21, and 22 toward CDK2.

In contrast, compound 22 exhibited a different binding behavior, forming a strong electrostatic interaction between its protonated NH2 group and Asp86. However, this interaction induced a significant displacement of the pyrimidine core from the binding site, negatively impacting its binding affinity. Binding free energy calculations using the molecular mechanics/generalized born surface area approach confirmed these findings,[ 32 ] as did unbinding force analysis via steered molecular dynamics (SMD). Binding energy decomposition highlighted the dominant role of van der Waals interactions, while also emphasizing the crucial contribution of electrostatic interactions in properly orienting the ligands within the binding pocket. Overall, this study provides a molecular‐level explanation for the observed differences in CDK2 binding affinities among the three ligands. It also suggests that optimizing ligand activity could be achieved by enhancing electrostatic interactions with Asp86, which can be accomplished through a strategically designed side chain at the N2 position, in accordance with the geometric constraints elucidated through the comparative analysis of compounds 20 and 22.

A phenotypic drug discovery (PDD) approach[ 33 ] was used to evaluate analogs of NU6027 bearing an amino group in replacement of the alkoxy substituent and lacking the amino group in position 2 (Scheme 9 ).[ 34 ] This strategy evaluates compound effects based on changes in cells as a whole rather than individual molecular targets, opposite to targeted drug discovery (TDD), which focuses on a single target and may overlook broader activity. The new compounds were synthesized by the reduction of nitro groups under the treatment of sodium methoxide. The mechanism of this transformation (Scheme 9) starts with the cyclization of 25 to intermediate I. Attack of hydroxide to N‐oxide II provides the formation of the intermediate III and elimination of OHC–COOMe leads to the 5‐nitroso derivatives 26.

Their cytotoxic activity was investigated against the panel of representative human solid tumor cells comprised of A2780 (ovarian), HBL‐100 (breast), HeLa (colon), Ishikawa (endometrium), SW1573 (nonsmall cell lung), T‐47D (breast), and WiDr (colon). For the most active compounds 26a and 26b, cell cycle analysis and protein expression studies were conducted to gain insight into their potential biological targets. Using PDD, the biological targets of the tested compounds were narrowed down to those involved in the metaphase‐to‐anaphase transition, excluding CDK1 and CDK2 as targets.[ 33 ] This finding would have been missed with a TDD approach based solely on structural similarity to NU6027.[ 34 ] While the exact target remained unknown, potential candidates include DNA topoisomerase IIα, mitotic kinases, motor proteins, microtubules, or the mitotic spindle.

Preliminary SAR trends were identified, and a docking study on the most potent compound 34g (IC50 = 1.8 μM) revealed insights into the structural basis for its strong CDK2 inhibition. The 1‐nitro moiety of the new scaffold was found to form a hydrogen bond with the side chains of Phe82 and Leu83, resembling the interactions observed in the crystal structure of CDK2 bound to NU6027. Additionally, the NO moiety was shown to retain a hydrogen bond interaction with the side chain of Lys33, further contributing to the binding affinity and specificity.

Additionally, all synthesized compounds were assessed for cytotoxicity against human lung cancer (H522) and glioma (U87) cell lines. The results demonstrated that all target compounds exhibited potent in vitro antitumor activity. Compound 34g, the most potent candidate (H522, IC50 = 2.89 μM; U87, IC50 = 3.19 μM), was further tested against a normal cell line (L02, IC50 = 86.7 μM), showing selective toxicity toward cancer cells.

In addition to CDK inhibition, various nitrosopyrimidines have been proposed as inhibitors of human O 6‐alkylguanine‐DNA alkyltransferase (AGAT). Compounds that inhibit human AGAT are important in cancer treatment because AGAT repairs DNA damage caused by alkylating chemotherapy agents, such as temozolomide, helping cancer cells survive. Inhibiting AGAT enhances the effectiveness of these chemotherapy agents by preventing DNA repair, leading to increased cancer cell death. AGAT inhibitors could be used to overcome drug resistance in tumors and potentially allow for lower chemotherapy doses, reducing side effects. However, selective inhibitors are necessary to minimize toxicity to healthy cells. The development of AGAT inhibitors is an active research area aimed at improving cancer treatment.[ 39 ] A series of 4(6)‐(arylyloxy)−2,6(4)‐diamino‐5‐nitrosopyrimidines 37 (Scheme 12 ) was synthesized by reacting 4‐chloro‐2,6‐diaminopyrimidine (35) with a sodium arylmethyl oxide in tert‐butyl alcohol, followed by nitrosation at the 5‐position of the pyrimidine ring.[ 40 ]

Antibacterials and Antifungals

Inhibitors of E. coli dihydropteroate synthase (DHPS) target a crucial enzyme in bacterial folate biosynthesis, making them effective antimicrobials.[ 44 ] Sulfonamides, the main DHPS inhibitors, block folate production and are used to treat E. coli infections, often in combination with dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors like trimethoprim. DHPS catalyzes the condensation of PABA (p‐aminobenzoic acid) with 7,8‐dihydropterin‐pyrophosphate to form 7,8‐dihydropteroate. Sulfa drugs are close structural analogs of PABA and exhibit their antimicrobial activity by competing with PABA at the DHPS active site, forming sulfa drug–pterin adducts instead of dihydrofolic acid. However, bacterial resistance through DHPS mutations and increased PABA production has driven the search for novel inhibitors. Structural studies aid in designing next‐generation drugs that evade resistance, with future strategies focusing on nonsulfonamide inhibitors and combination therapies to enhance efficacy. A series of 5,6‐disubstituted isocytosine derivatives (Scheme 16 ) was evaluated as DHPS inhibitors, with several 6‐(alkylamino)‐5‐nitrosoisocytosines 51 exhibiting potency comparable to or greater than sulfonamides.[ 45 , 46 ] The 5‐nitrosoisocytosines were typically prepared by nitrosation of an appropriate 6‐substituted isocytosine (method A) starting from 50, or through condensation of the appropriate amine with 53 (method B), which was prepared by methylation of 6‐mercaptoisocytosine 52 followed by nitrosation.

While larger 6‐amino substituents generally impaired binding, this steric hindrance could be mitigated by a phenyl ring at the terminus of alkylamino chains, leading to positive allosteric interactions (compounds 51a–d). Further investigation into 6‐[[3‐(aryloxy)propyl]amino]‐5‐nitrosoisocytosines revealed a 30‐fold range in inhibitory potency (0.6–18 µM), though no clear correlation with electronic or hydrophobic properties was found. Additionally, compound 51 showed moderate growth inhibition (2.5–8.9 µM) but lacked exceptional potency, and kinetic studies of the synthase inhibition suggested that these inhibitors do not interact with the PABA binding site.[ 46 ] Despite the strong in vitro inhibition observed in many nitrosoisocytosines, none exhibited significant antibacterial activity, likely due to poor bacterial cell penetration or rapid metabolic inactivation.

Structurally related 4,6‐bis(alkyl or arylamino)‐5‐nitrosopyrimidines 56 and 59 (Scheme 17 ) were synthesized by a two‐step strategy consisting of a nitrosation and selective aminolysis, and evaluated for their antibacterial potential against the human pathogenic bacteria S. aureus, methicillin‐sensitive and methicillin‐resistant S. aureus, E. coli, Yersinia enterocolitica, Salmonella enteritidis, and Salmonella sp.[ 47 ] Compounds 56 were synthesized by selective monoaminolysis of 55, which was prepared through the introduction of a 5‐nitroso group into the commercial 2‐amino‐4,6‐dimethoxypyrimidine 54. Compounds 59 were prepared by the selective monoaminolysis of 58 with amines.

a) Synthesis of 5‐nitrosopyrimidines 56 and 59 by a two‐step strategy (nitrosation‐aminolysis). b) α and β rotamers for 59a.

A SAR study, supported by theoretical calculations, identified the minimal structural features required for antibacterial activity. While some compounds demonstrated potency comparable to cefotaxime, their high acute toxicity limited their therapeutic potential. However, compounds 56a, 56b, 56c, and 59b combined potent antibacterial activity (minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) = 0.5–50 μg mL−1) with relatively low acute toxicity, making them promising candidates for further development. For compound 59b, B3LYP/6‐31G(d, p) calculations predict that rotamer β is 0.04 kcal mol−1 higher in energy than rotamer α, with an energy barrier of 27.6 kcal mol−1 for conformational interconversion. These theoretical findings are in perfect agreement the experimental results, which indicate a rotamer ratio of approximately 57:43 (α/β). Moreover, these results are consistent with the theoretical data previously reported.[ 48 ]

The development of new antifungal agents is crucial due to the increasing incidence of this type of infection, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. These pathogens can cause life‐threatening systemic infections, and the rise of antifungal resistance limits current treatment options. Novel antifungals with improved efficacy, reduced toxicity, and new mechanisms of action are essential to overcoming drug resistance and ensuring effective treatment for fungal infections.[ 49 ] A set of N 4‐substituted 2,4‐diamino‐6‐methoxy‐5‐nitrosopyrimidines was synthesized and evaluated for antifungal activity against human pathogenic strains.[ 50 ] These compounds exhibited potent activity against Candida albicans, Candida tropicalis, and Cryptococcus neoformans. A detailed SAR study, supported by quantum mechanical calculations and molecular electrostatic potentials analysis using B3LYP/6‐31G(d) methods, helped identify a topographical template for antifungal activity. Several compounds demonstrated significant antifungal effects, particularly compounds 56b, 56c, and 59b. The latter was identified through molecular modeling studies and displayed the strongest antifungal activity among the series.

Lipoxygenase (LOX) Inhibition

LOX inhibitors play a crucial role in oxidative stress regulation by preventing the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), a key process in the formation of ROS.[ 51 ] LOX inhibitors exhibit antioxidant properties, reducing lipid peroxidation and protecting cellular components from oxidative damage. Their antioxidant activity helps protect cellular components, reduce inflammation, and mitigate diseases like cancer, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative disorders. By limiting proinflammatory eicosanoids like leukotrienes, LOX inhibitors serve as promising therapeutic agents for oxidative stress‐related conditions. A series of novel piperidine‐pyrimidine‐cinnamic acid amides 64 (Scheme 18 ) has been designed, synthesized, and evaluated for their antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory potential.[ 52 ] Their synthesis was achieved via N‐acylation reactions between 63 and six different carboxylic acids. Two distinct N‐acylation methods were employed: Coupling using (dimethylamino)phosphonium hexafluorophosphate (BOP) (method A) or the formation of an N‐hydroxysuccinimide active ester, which rapidly reacts with amines in the presence of 1‐ethyl‐3‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCI·HCl) (Method B).

A) Synthesis of compounds 64. B) Preferred docking pose of compound 64f bound to soybean lipoxygenase (PDB ID: 3PZW). Reproduced with permission.[ 52 ] Published under the Creative Commons Attribution License (CC BY 4.0).

The synthesized compounds were evaluated in vitro for their biological activity, including: a) interaction with the stable free radical 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) to assess antioxidant potential; b) inhibition of 2,2´‐azobis(2‐amidinopropane) dihydrochloride (AAPH)‐induced linoleic acid peroxidation, measuring their ability to prevent lipid oxidation; and (c) inhibition of soybean lipoxygenase, determining their potential as anti‐inflammatory agents. Among the newly synthesized compounds, 64a, 64b, and 64d exhibited potent lipid peroxidation inhibition. However, in the DPPH assay, most compounds demonstrated limited reducing activity (2–25%). Regarding LOX inhibition, the most potent inhibitors were compounds 64f and 64b, with IC50 values of 1.1 µM and 10.7 µM, respectively. Other derivatives showed moderate inhibitory activity. Molecular docking studies (Scheme 18) revealed that the formation of a hydrogen bond with Tyr525 plays a crucial role in LOX inhibition. Notably, compound 64f emerged as a promising LOX inhibitor, displaying an IC50 value comparable to nordihydroguaiaretic acid (NDGA), a well‐known LOX inhibitor.

Five‐Membered Monocylic Heterocycles

Antifungal Activity

Nitrosoazoles are mainly known for their fungicide and antibacterial activities. For example, several novel 4‐nitrosopyrazoles (68, 69) (Scheme 19 ) were synthesized and evaluated for their antibacterial and antifungal activities.[ 53 ] Compounds 68 and 69 were prepared by nitrosation of β‐diketones 65, followed by cyclization using suitable commercial hydrazino derivatives 67, affording a mixture of two isomers that were easily separated by silica gel column chromatography.

Synthesis of 4‐nitrosoisoxazoles 68 and 69.

While none of the compounds exhibited antibacterial properties, several displayed potent antifungal activity (C. albicans, C. parapsilosis, C. paratropicalis, C. kruzei, C. glabrata, C. laurenti, C. neoformans, A. fumigatus, T. mentagrophytes, T. capitum) at noncytotoxic concentrations. Among them, derivative 68c demonstrated exceptional efficacy, being nine times more potent than miconazole and 20 times more selective against C. neoformans. Additionally, 68c outperformed amphotericin B and fluconazole by factors of 8 and 125, respectively. SAR studies revealed that the NO group at position 4 of the pyrazole ring is crucial for antifungal activity. Moreover, the lipophilicity of the pyrazole moiety, the N‐alkyl chain length, and the planarity of the two heterocyclic rings played key roles in modulating both cytotoxicity and antifungal potency. Lipophilicity measurements pointed out constantly higher values for derivatives 68 than for corresponding isomers 69. Compound 68 was more active and less cytotoxic. The potency of these derivatives appeared to be modulated by the type of substituent and by the length of the carbon chain of the alkyl group bound to the nitrogen of the pyrazole ring: the NH (68a) and N‐phenyl (68g) derivatives were poorly active. With the increasing length of the alkylic carbon chain, the activity increases passing through a maximum with the ethyl group (68c), decreasing progressively with the propyl and butyl groups (68e, 68f).

Antibacterial Activity

Nitrosoimidazoles 71‐73 have been investigated as bactericidal compounds (Scheme 20 ).[ 54 ] Methylation of 71 results in the formation of the isomers 72 and 73. Once purified, all three nitrosoimidazoles exhibit stability in solid form but decompose gradually in aqueous solution at pH 7. However, their degradation accelerates significantly under acidic conditions, in the presence of sulfhydryl compounds, or within bacterial cultures. Like other C‐nitrosoimidazole monomers, compounds 71, 72, and 73 display a characteristic green coloration in both solid state and aqueous solution, with weak n‐π UV absorption bands around 670 nm.

a) Synthesis of nitrosoimidazoles 71‐73. b) Structures of metronidazole and nitro compound 74.

The bactericidal activity of these nitrosoimidazoles appears to be limited to bacteria that are naturally sensitive to metronidazole. For instance, E. coli strain AB1157, the wild‐type progenitor of the metronidazole‐sensitive SR58 strain, was resistant to nitrosoimidazoles at concentrations up to 0.5 mM, as well as to metronidazole and nitro compound 74. However, a notable exception was observed in a strain of Bacteroides fragilis with reduced nitroreductase activity. This strain, which exhibits decreased sensitivity to metronidazole, remained highly susceptible to nitrosoimidazole 71, similar to metronidazole‐sensitive B. fragilis. The proposed mechanism of action suggests that these compounds induce DNA damage via radical formation, similar to 5‐nitroimidazoles but with higher potency.

Similar to nitrosoimidazoles, nitrosopyrazoles have likewise been investigated as bactericidal compounds. N‐pyridazino‐pyrazole 79 (Scheme 21 ) exhibited antimicrobial activity against the Gram‐negative bacteria E. coli but was less active against the Gram‐positive bacteria S. aureus, and the fungus C. albicans.[ 55 ] The synthesis of 79 started by the condensation of benzylmonohydrazone with ethyl phenylacetate. Reaction of compound 75 with POCl3/PCl5 gave 76, which yielded 77 upon reaction with hydrazine hydrate. Fusion with ethyl benzoylacetate in an oil bath gave 78, which upon treatment with nitrous acid gave 79.

Antitubercular Agents

A series of trifluoromethyl‐pyrazoles 81 (Scheme 22 ) was synthesized via a one‐pot nitrosation of 1,3‐diketones or their lithium salts 80, followed by treatment with hydrazines.[ 56 ]

Synthesis of nitrosopyrazoles 81 via a one‐pot procedure.

The biological evaluation of these compounds included tuberculostatic, antibacterial, antimycotic, antioxidant, and cytotoxic activities in vitro, as well as analgesic and anti‐inflammatory effects in vivo, alongside acute toxicity studies. Among them, N‐unsubstituted 4‐nitrosopyrazoles (81a–d) demonstrated the strongest tuberculostatic activity (MIC as low as 0.36 µg mL−1) and exhibited potent antibacterial activity against Streptococcus pyogenes (MIC = 7.8 µg mL−1), S. aureus (including MRSA), and Neisseria gonorrhoeae (MIC = 15.6 µg mL−1). Additionally, 4‐nitroso‐1‐methyl‐5‐phenylpyrazole 81e displayed pronounced antimycotic activity against various fungal strains (Trichophyton rubrum, T. tonsurans, T. violaceum, T. interdigitale, Epidermophyton floccosum, and Microsporum canis) with MIC values ranging from 0.38 to 12.5 µg mL−1. Moreover, 4‐nitrosopyrazoles bearing a phenyl group at position 5 (81a, 81e) demonstrated pronounced analgesic effects in the hot plate test. Most of the synthesized pyrazoles displayed moderate acute toxicity.

Other 3‐trifluoromethyl‐substituted 4‐nitrosopyrazoles 81 and 4‐nitrosopyrazolines 82 (Scheme 23 ) were synthesized via a one‐pot reaction using trifluoromethyl‐containing 1,3‐diketones 80, sodium nitrite in acetic acid, and hydrazines (hydrazine hydrate or methylhydrazine).[ 57 ] Notably, 3‐trifluoromethylpyrazolines 82 could be thermally converted into the pyrazoles 81. The use of phenylhydrazine in these reactions resulted in the formation of regioisomeric 4‐hydroxyimino‐5‐(trifluoromethyl)pyrazoline 83.

Synthesis of nitrosopyrazoles 81.

The synthesized compounds were evaluated for in vitro tuberculostatic activity against the Mycobacterium tuberculosis laboratory strain H37Rv, with isoniazid (MIC = 0.15 μg mL−1) serving as a reference. Among the tested compounds, 4‐nitrosopyrazole 81a, featuring an NH group and a phenyl substituent at position 5, exhibited the highest tuberculostatic activity, inhibiting bacterial growth at 0.36 μg mL−1. Substituting the phenyl group with a methyl group resulted in reduced activity, as demonstrated by pyrazole 81hr (MIC = 1.62 μg mL−1). Further methylation at the N1 position led to an even greater decline in efficacy, with pyrazole 81i exhibiting a MIC of 6.15 μg mL−1. The pyrazoline 82i displayed lower tuberculostatic activity.

The pyrazole NSC 18,725 (Scheme 24 ) was found to be an antitubercular compound with activity comparable to isoniazid (INH), a first‐line tuberculosis drug.[ 58 ] Notably, NSC 18,725 also exhibits activity against dormant M. tuberculosis in vitro. Additionally, it enhances host defense mechanisms by inducing autophagy and inhibiting M. tuberculosis survival in macrophages. In checkerboard assays, NSC 18,725 demonstrated synergy with INH and an additive effect with other tested drugs such as rifampicin and ethambutol. Structural analysis revealed that the nitroso group is essential for its antimycobacterial activity, and para‐substitution on the phenyl ring further enhanced its potency in vitro. Pyrazole derivative with para‐chlorophenyl displayed the highest activity in the range of 0.039–0.078 μM against M. tuberculosis compared to NSC 18,725 (MIC99 = 0.3125 μM).

Fuzed Heterocycles

Anti‐Inflammatory Activity

The development of new nitric oxide (NO)‐based anti‐inflammatory drugs is crucial for advancing treatments for chronic inflammatory conditions.[ 59 ] The slow release of nitric oxide from these compounds leads to subtle changes in the profile of pharmacological activity of the parent compound. NO plays a key role in regulating immune responses and inflammation, and targeting it can provide more effective and safer treatments compared to traditional anti‐inflammatory drugs like NSAIDs. NO‐based drugs offer benefits such as reduced side effects, dual action in modulating inflammation and improving blood flow, and the potential for combination therapies. These drugs could be particularly useful in managing diseases like arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and autoimmune disorders, presenting a promising solution for treating inflammation‐related conditions. Three novel series of nitroso heterocycles (Scheme 25 ) were synthesized from hydroxyimoyl chloride 85.[ 60 ]

Synthesis of nitroso heterocycles 86–88.

The in vitro COX‐1 and COX‐2 inhibition activities of these compounds were evaluated. The results indicated that C‐nitroso pyrazoles 86–88 exhibited excellent nitric oxide donor activity. In vivo anti‐inflammatory activity was demonstrated by pyrazoles 86a and 87a, which were as effective as celecoxib. Most compounds were safe in terms of ulcer induction, and most showed excellent nitric oxide (NO) release.

Molecular docking simulations were conducted for the most active compounds, 86b and 87b, to investigate their interaction with the COX‐2 receptor. The compounds exhibited IC50 values of 1.55 µM and 1.22 µM, respectively. The docking study aimed to analyze the binding interactions of these compounds with the COX‐2 receptor. Compound 87a exhibited three hydrogen bonds: Pyrazole N2, the nitrogen of the NO group, and the oxygen of the NO group with amino acids Arg 106, Tyr 341, and Arg 499 at distances of 2.84, 2.59, and 2.40 Å, respectively. The higher number of hydrogen bonds in 87a likely accounts for its higher in vivo anti‐inflammatory activity (64%), which is comparable to celecoxib. Imidazopyrimidine 86b showed two hydrogen bonds: the nitrogen of the NO group and the oxygen of the NO group with amino acid Arg 106 at distances of 2.28 Å and 2.49 Å, respectively, with an energy score of −12.40 kcal mol−1. The in vivo result (62% inhibition) supports this hypothesis. Despite their lower energy scores, compounds 86b and 87a showed higher in vivo reactivity (62% and 65% inhibition of paw edema), though their COX selectivity was lower (4.38 and 2.03, respectively), which could be linked to their favorable absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of a substance profiles.

Antitumor, Antifungal, and Antibacterial Activities

A series of indolo[1,2‐c]benzo[ 1 – 3 ] triazine derivatives 93 (Scheme 26 ) was synthesized through the diazotization of substituted 2‐(2‐aminophenyl)indoles 90, followed by an intramolecular coupling reaction between the diazonium group and the indole nitrogen.[ 61 ] To ensure the formation of the indolobenzotriazine system and prevent undesired cyclization into the indolo[3,2‐c]cinnoline system (94), the 3‐position of the indole nucleus was substituted with a nitroso group.

Synthesis of indolo[1,2‐c]benzo[1,2,3]triazine derivatives 93.

The synthesized indolobenzotriazines (93a–g) were evaluated for their antitumor activity against a panel of leukemia‐, lymphoma‐, carcinoma‐, and neuroblastoma‐derived cell lines. Some derivatives exhibited potent antiproliferative effects on T and B cell lines at submicromolar concentrations, while their activity against solid tumor cell lines remained in the micromolar range. Additionally, their antifungal properties were assessed, revealing that compounds 93a and 93d inhibited certain fungal strains; however, their inhibitory concentrations were close to those affecting human cell proliferation, limiting their therapeutic potential. Test compounds inhibited the fungal growth at concentrations that were in the same order of magnitude as those cytotoxic for human cells (MIC = 1.6–50 μM). Conversely, all indolobenzotriazines displayed strong and selective antibacterial activity against Streptococcus and Staphylococcus species. Notably, compounds 93b and 93c were up to 80 times more potent than streptomycin, effectively inhibiting Gram‐positive bacterial growth at significantly lower concentrations than those cytotoxic to mammalian cells.

Related to compounds 93, compounds 101a−f bearing an additional nitrogen atom (Scheme 27 ) were prepared[ 62 ] by a multistep sequence starting from 3‐methylpyridine 95 and commercially available 2‐aminobenzonitriles 96a−f. Compounds 97a−f were prepared by an intramolecular Chichibabin‐type reaction in the presence of lithium diisopropylamide. The acetylation of compounds 97a−f proceeded efficiently, yielding the corresponding acetamide derivatives 98a−f in quantitative amounts. These acetamides were then subjected to nitrosation with sodium nitrite in acetic acid, affording the desired nitrosoacetamides 99a−f. Subsequent deacetylation using potassium hydroxide produced the amino derivatives 100a–f. Finally, diazotization of intermediates 100a–f resulted in the formation of compounds 101a−f.

Synthesis of compounds 101.

These compounds exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against approximately 60 human tumor cell lines, representing nine cancer types, with MG_MID values ranging from 0.74 to 1.15 μM and GI50 values reaching the nanomolar range. They demonstrated exceptional efficacy against the leukemia subpanel (GI50 = 0.73−0.0090 μM) and retained activity in cells overexpressing P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), suggesting potential use in drug‐resistant cancers. Flow cytometric analysis indicated significant cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase, while mechanistic studies confirmed that these compounds induce apoptosis in the Jurkat cell line through multiple pathways: (i) mitochondrial transmembrane potential disruption, (ii) ROS generation and cardiolipin oxidation, and (iii) activation of caspase‐3, caspase‐8, and caspase‐9. At the molecular level, they function as potent topoisomerase I inhibitors. Interestingly, the inhibition of topoisomerase I by compounds bearing the triazine scaffold is unprecedented.

Adenosine receptors (ARs) are increasingly recognized as potential targets for antitumor therapy due to their role in tumor progression, immune evasion, and the tumor microenvironment.[ 63 ] There are four subtypes of adenosine receptors: A1, A2A, A2B, and A3, all of which are G‐protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) with distinct effects on cancer cells and immune responses. Compound 105 (Scheme 28 ),[ 64 ] was prepared from inosine (102). The chloride 103 was converted to the corresponding azide with sodium azide. Hydrogenation of both the azido and the nitro functionality was accomplished using 10% Pd/C. The nitro group was reduced to hydroxylamine and was immediately oxidized to 104 with an aqueous solution of sodium periodate. Compound 105 exhibited weak affinity for the human adenosine A1, A2A, A2B, and A3 Receptors as determined in radioligand binding studies (Ki or % displacement at 10 µM: A1, 40%; A2A, 720 nM; A2B, 580 nM; A3, 14%). Extension of the nitroso group to phenylaminocarbonyltriazene‐1‐yl resulted in a compound 106 with low nanomolar affinity for the human adenosine A1 and A2A receptors, due to binding sites of N6 and C2 substituents are different.

Summary and Outlook

Nitroso derivatives of (hetero)aromatic compounds exhibit a fascinating dual nature, acting as both therapeutic agents and environmental hazards. These molecules demonstrate diverse pharmacological activities, including antiviral, antibacterial, antifungal, antiparasitic, anticancer, and anti‐inflammatory properties. Their therapeutic potential stems from innovative synthetic strategies, SAR analyses, and advanced computational approaches.

Nitrosobenzenes, including nitrosobenzene (NOB), display both therapeutic potential and toxicological concerns. Formed as metabolites of environmental pollutants like nitrobenzene and aniline, NOB induces oxidative stress, DNA damage, and protein adduction, contributing to genotoxic and splenotoxic effects. Mechanistically, NOB interacts with hemoglobin at cysteine residues and promotes Cu(II)‐mediated DNA damage via ROS. Some derivatives, such as 3‐nitrosobenzamide, exhibit antiviral activity by disrupting the HIV NCp7 zinc finger protein, while others, like 2‐nitrosonaphthalene, show genotoxicity and impair genetic regulation. These findings underscore the dual‐edged nature of nitrosobenzene derivatives as both environmental hazards and potential therapeutic agents.

Notable compounds, such as NU6027, are potent inhibitors of CDKs 1 and 2, designed to competitively target the ATP‐binding site and disrupt key cell cycle transitions, particularly from G1 to S and G2 to M phases, making it a strong candidate for cancer therapy. Its 5‐nitroso group stabilizes a pseudopurine conformation, enabling precise CDK binding. Beyond CDKs, NU6027 also inhibits ATR kinase, enhancing the efficacy of DNA‐damaging agents like hydroxyurea and cisplatin and showing synthetic lethality in cells with compromised DNA repair pathways. Remarkably, NU6027 extends its therapeutic potential to neuroprotection by inhibiting TRPC5 channels, reducing zinc accumulation, and preventing neuronal death, suggesting usefulness in TBI. Additionally, it has demonstrated antimycobacterial activity, including inhibition of M. tuberculosis and its kinases. Altogether, NU6027 emerges as a versatile lead compound with promising applications in oncology, infectious diseases, and neuroprotection.

Other six‐membered monocyclic compounds, particularly nitrosopyrimidines, exhibit a broad spectrum of biological activities with strong therapeutic potential. Notably, they serve as potent inhibitors of CDKs 1 and 2, effectively regulating cell cycle progression and offering promise in cancer treatment. Nitrosopyrimidines have shown antiviral activity by targeting viral replication enzymes, including those of HIV. Their antitumor effects are influenced by structural variations that impact key pathways like PI3K/mTOR, making them effective even against resistant cancer types. Additionally, they induce synthetic lethality in cells with impaired DNA repair mechanisms, supporting their use in precision oncology. Beyond cancer, derivatives such as nitrosoisocytosines display antimicrobial activity, although limited cell penetration remains a challenge. Overall, six‐membered monocyclic compounds represent a versatile and promising class of therapeutic agents.

Nitroso‐functionalized five‐membered heterocycles exhibit a broad range of pharmacological activities. 4‐nitroso‐pyrazoles, particularly compound 68c, have shown potent antifungal effects against C. neoformans and other fungal strains, outperforming standard drugs like fluconazole, with their activity strongly influenced by the presence of a nitroso group and the lipophilicity of the pyrazole ring. Nitrosoimidazoles demonstrated notable antibacterial activity against metronidazole‐sensitive strains such as B. fragilis, primarily through DNA damage induced by radical formation. In the antimycobacterial context, 4‐nitroso‐3‐trifluoromethylpyrazoles exhibited significant tuberculostatic activity against M. tuberculosis, with structural modifications like phenyl substitution enhancing their efficacy. Additionally, 4‐nitrosopyrazoles presented in vivo analgesic and anti‐inflammatory properties, though some derivatives showed moderate acute toxicity. These findings highlight the therapeutic versatility of these heterocycles and emphasize the critical role of SARs in optimizing their pharmacological potential.

Fuzed heterocyclic nitroso compounds exhibit a wide range of biological activities with significant therapeutic potential. Notably, nitroso pyrazoles and imidazopyrimidines show strong anti‐inflammatory effects by acting as nitric oxide (NO) donors and inhibiting COX‐2, with efficacy comparable to celecoxib and minimal ulcerogenic effects. Substituted benzotriazines demonstrate potent antitumor activity, inducing apoptosis and generating ROS while inhibiting topoisomerase I. Indolobenzotriazines exhibit selective antibacterial activity against Gram‐positive bacteria, outperforming conventional antibiotics. Additionally, phenylaminocarbonyltriazene derivatives show nanomolar affinity for adenosine receptors, suggesting their potential in cancer immunotherapy and tumor microenvironment modulation. These findings underscore the diverse applications of fuzed heterocyclic compounds in treating inflammation, cancer, and infections.

Despite their promising therapeutic potential, nitroso‐functionalized heterocyclic compounds present several challenges that must be addressed for their successful clinical translation. Genotoxic risks, oxidative stress induction, and environmental stability require careful evaluation to ensure safety, while limited bioavailability and metabolic activation necessitate structural optimization. Ongoing advancements in synthetic chemistry and proteomics, coupled with growing interest in these compounds, pave the way for their development into clinically viable therapies. However, balancing their therapeutic benefits with inherent risks remains a critical focus for future investigations. The diverse biological activities of these compounds, including their ability to modulate key pathways in cancer, infections, inflammation, and neuroprotection, underscore their potential for novel drug discovery. Yet, challenges such as optimizing cell penetration, minimizing toxicity, and enhancing in vivo efficacy persist, emphasizing the need for continued research. With further structural optimization and targeted delivery systems, these compounds could play a pivotal role in addressing cancer, drug‐resistant infections, and neurodegenerative diseases, marking them as versatile candidates for future treatments.
