Diagnostic accuracy of ultrasound and computed tomography in obstructive jaundice at emergency department: a retrospective study
Authors: Reyhan İrem Mutlu Eren, Çağdaş Yıldırım, Alp Şener, Fatih Ahmet Kahraman, Mehmet Ergin, Şervan Gökhan, Osman Ersoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstructive jaundice is a common clinical condition, often requiring timely diagnosis to guide management. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of ultrasound (USG) and computed tomography (CT) compared to endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) as a reference standard in the emergency department.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital Emergency Department from January 2022 to December 2023. Data were collected from 1011 patients who underwent ERCP and met inclusion criteria. Diagnostic accuracy of USG and CT for choledocholithiasis, biliary strictures, malignancies, and ductal dilatation was assessed.ResultsUSG showed low sensitivity (20.83%) but high specificity (85.96%) for choledocholithiasis, while CT demonstrated higher sensitivity (57.55%) and similar specificity (80.93%). CT consistently outperformed USG in sensitivity across diagnoses, whereas USG maintained higher specificity. USGs ease of use and non-invasive nature support its role as the first-line imaging, though negative findings should prompt further CT evaluation.ConclusionUSG and CT remain essential tools in the diagnostic evaluation of obstructive jaundice in the emergency department. While USG offers high specificity and initial assessment capability, CT provides superior sensitivity and is recommended when USG findings are negative but clinical suspicion remains.Keywords: Obstructive jaundice, Ultrasound, Computed tomography, Emergency medicine, Diagnostic accuracy, ERCP

Background

Jaundice is a symptom caused by an increase in the serum bilirubin level. This increase in bilirubin may be due to excessive production, impaired conjugation, hepatic inflammation, or biliary obstruction [1]. The most common causes of jaundice are hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, obstruction of the bile duct by stones or tumors, and toxic reactions. The most commonly used imaging methods in the emergency department to identify these causes are ultrasonography (USG) and computed tomography (CT) [2].

Obstructive jaundice occurs when there is a blockage in the bile ducts, either intrahepatic or extrahepatic [3]. Laboratory tests such as total and direct bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are performed for patients suspected of having obstructive jaundice in the emergency department. Imaging is performed both for hepatic evaluation and to confirm the presence of an obstructive pathology and determine the level of obstruction. The first imaging method is USG because of its ease of access, affordability, and lack of additional risk to the patient [4]. Moreover, ultrasonography has been applied successfully to a range of emergency department diagnoses [5, 6]. Contrast-enhanced CT is the second most common method. Although contrast-enhanced CT is superior to USG in clarifying the etiological cause, it is the second choice because it involves exposure to nephrotoxic contrast agents and radiation [7]. Emergency department imaging is hampered by tight time constraints, non-fasted patients (leading to bowel-gas artefacts), and the operator-dependence of USG. These factors can reduce sensitivity and consistency, making it essential to evaluate both ultrasound and CT in true emergency conditions.

There are few studies in the literature on the diagnostic value of these two imaging methods for the diagnosis of obstructive jaundice in the emergency department. The few studies that have been conducted also have small sample sizes [8]. In our study, we aimed to test the reliability of imaging methods by examining the diagnostic value of USG and CT in obstructive jaundice patients in the emergency department by expanding the sample size and comparing the results with those of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), which we accepted as a reference test, with the aim of contributing to literature and medical practice.

Methods

Study design

Our study is a retrospective, cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study. The study was conducted at the Emergency Medicine Clinic of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital. Our hospital is a third-level hospital and an education and training clinic that receives patients from both Ankara and surrounding provinces. Our hospital’s emergency department receives approximately 470,000 patient admissions annually. USG and CT scans are reported by radiologists. At our hospital, ERCP is routinely performed by gastroenterologists.

For this study, data from patients whose data were obtained between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023, and who met the inclusion criteria were analyzed.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital Medical Research Scientific and Ethical Review Board (Approval number: TABED 2/91/2024), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

After ethical committee approval was obtained, patient data was requested from the hospital statistics unit for patients who visited the emergency department and underwent ERCP with Social Security Institution Health Application Bulletin code 701.360. The patients in this file were evaluated according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and those included were retrospectively analyzed via the HICAMP hospital patient record system. The presenting complaints, comorbidities, abdominal examination findings, laboratory results, abdominal and hepatobiliary USG reports, contrast-enhanced abdominal CT reports, and ERCP reports of patients who met the inclusion criteria were reviewed. The presenting complaints were classified as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, itching, jaundice, fever and loss of appetite. The comorbidities were diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HT), hyperlipidemia (HL), coronary artery disease (CAD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), malignancy, and others. For the abdominal examination, a classification was made that included tenderness, defense, and rebound. Blood test results were examined for total bilirubin (mg/dL), direct bilirubin (mg/dL), ALP (U/L), GGT (U/L), AST (U/L), and ALT (U/L) levels. Patients’ presenting complaints, accompanying diseases, examination findings, doctor notes, and consultation notes were analyzed.

Our hospital uses a General Electric brand LOGIQTM S7 with a XDclearTM (General Electric Company, Milwaukee, WI, USA) model USG device. Hepatobiliary system and abdominal USG are performed and reported by radiologists while patients are in the supine position. For contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans, we used a General Electric Revolution™ (General Electric Company, Milwaukee, WI, USA) model device with slice thicknesses of 0.625, 1.25, and 2.5 mm. Additionally, we used Omnipaque™ (iohexol) contrast agent at our hospital. The CT images obtained were evaluated and reported by radiologists. USG and CT were classified as choledocholithiasis, stricture, malignancy, cholangitis, no pathology, or uncertain. Classification was performed according to what was written in the report. Patients in whom the common bile duct could not be clearly evaluated, the etiological cause could not be specified, or appropriate imaging could not be performed (gas superposition, patient uncooperativeness, etc.) were classified as uncertain. Additionally, common bile duct dilatation and common bile duct diameter were analyzed. For the common bile duct to be considered dilated, the report was required to indicate dilation or that the diameter exceeded the specified value ( > 6 mm). An Olympus (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) side-viewing endoscope was used for ERCP. At our hospital, ERCP procedures are performed in 48 hours per instutional protocol and reported by gastroenterologists after appropriate sedation has been administered by anesthesiologists. Similar to CT and USG, classification was also performed for ERCP. For stricture classification, cytology results were examined for patients with narrowing and blunt termination in the report. Those with malignancies detected via cytology were classified as malignant, and those with benign findings were classified as stricture.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were patients aged 18 years or older who presented to the emergency department, underwent ERCP during their visit, had total bilirubin and direct bilirubin levels meeting the jaundice criteria (total bilirubin > 1.2 mg/dL and direct bilirubin > 0.3 mg/dL, which is the accepted range for our hospital), and who underwent diagnostic USG and/or CT scans. The exclusion criteria were patients admitted directly to inpatient services without an emergency department visit, patients who did not undergo ERCP, or for whom ERCP failed to provide adequate diagnostic imaging, patients lacking both USG and CT imaging (unstable patients unable to undergo imaging), or whose both imaging studies were performed at other centers, patients whose bilirubin levels did not meet the defined jaundice thresholds (total bilirubin ≤1.2 mg/dL and direct bilirubin ≤0.3 mg/dL and patients younger than 18 years of age.

Primary outcome

The primary aim of our study was to measure the sensitivity and specificity of USG and CT for detecting choledocholithiasis, the most common cause of obstructive jaundice. Sensitivity and specificity were also evaluated for other causes of obstructive jaundice, such as malignancy, stricture, cholangitis, and choledochal dilatation.

Statistical analysis

ERCP was used as the reference test in our study [8]. Diagnoses determined by ERCP were compared with USG and CT reports and analyzed statistically. The statistical analyses in this study were performed via SPSS Statistics 20.0 for Windows. Frequency distributions were provided for categorical variables, and descriptive statistics were provided for continuous variables. The distribution analysis of numerical continuous data in the study was performed via the Shapiro‒Wilk test, and the median and interquartile range were used for data that did not conform to a normal distribution. In cases where the normality assumption was not met (p < 0.05) in dependent two-group median comparisons, the nonparametric Wilcoxon test was used. The McNemar test was used for categorical data between dependent groups. For diagnostic value assessments, the MedCalc [9] diagnostic value calculator was used after the McNemar test. The specificity, sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (+LR), negative likelihood ratio (−LR), and accuracy rate calculated by the calculator were obtained with 95% confidence intervals. For statistical significance, a p value < 0.05 was used.

A total of 83.7% of patients (N = 846) presented with abdominal pain, 21% (N = 212) with nausea, and 19.5% (N = 195) with vomiting. Among the patients included in our study, 33% (N = 334) had hypertension, 31.8% (N = 322) had other comorbid conditions, and 20% (N = 202) had diabetes mellitus. In the diagnostic comparison of USG and ERCP for choledochal pathologies, the sensitivity of USG in diagnosing choledocholithiasis was 20.83% (95% CI: 17.66–24.28), the specificity was 85.96% (95% CI: 80.77–90.20), the PPV was 79.75% (95% CI: 73.37–84.91), the NPV was 29.04% (95% CI: 27.69–30.43), the -LR was 0.92 (0.86–0.98), and the +LR was 1.48 (95% CI: 1.04–2.12). The analysis revealed that USG had low sensitivity and relatively high specificity in every diagnostic category. In the diagnostic comparison of CT and ERCP for choledochal pathologies, the sensitivity of CT for diagnosing choledocholithiasis was 57.55% (95% CI: 53.07–61.94), the specificity was 80.93% (95% CI: 75.03–85.95), the PPV was 87.46% (95% CI: 83.98–90.27), the NPV was 45.19% (95% CI: 42.21–48.21), the -LR was 0.52 (0.46–0.59) and the +LR was 3.02 (95% CI: 2.27–4.01). As a result of this analysis, CT was found to be superior to US in terms of sensitivity and NPV for all diagnoses. Similar results were found for specificity and PPV with USG. Discussion

Obstructive jaundice frequently presents in older adults and requires differentiation between intrahepatic and extrahepatic causes, most commonly choledocholithiasis and malignancy [8]. Although clinical evaluation establishes the diagnosis in most cases, imaging with USG or CT is essential to confirm obstruction and localize its level [2, 10]. In our study, the primary outcome was the diagnostic performance of USG and CT in the diagnosis of choledocholithiasis, compared with that of ERCP (gold standard). According to our findings, the sensitivity of USG in the diagnosis of choledocholithiasis was 20.83%, whereas its specificity was 85.96%. On the other hand, CT had a sensitivity of 57.55% and a specificity of 80.93% and was found to be significantly superior to USG, particularly in terms of sensitivity.

Our retrospective cohort of 1 011 consecutive ED patients with obstructive jaundice is, to our knowledge, greater than previous studies. This robust sample enhances the precision of our sensitivity and specificity estimates and lends greater generalizability to emergency-department practice.

USG is preferred for the initial imaging of patients presenting to the emergency department with abdominal pain because of its easy accessibility and lack of additional risk to the patient. Our results indicate that the low sensitivity, NPV, and LR of USG in diagnosing choledocholithiasis are insufficient for diagnostic exclusion, both statistically and clinically. In a study comparing CT and USG for the diagnosis of obstructive jaundice, Kaushal et al. reported that the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV values of USG for the diagnosis of choledocholithiasis were 68.42%, 98.77%, 92.86%, and 89.2%, respectively [8]. In a study by Alsaigh et al. comparing the diagnostic value of USG and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of USG in the evaluation of choledocholithiasis were found to be 26.6%, 100%, 100%, and 31.6%, respectively [10]. In a study by Fadahunsi et al. evaluating the accuracy of USG in obstructive jaundice, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV rates were 70.7%, 93.7, 75, and 92%, respectively [11]. In all three studies, positive and negative likelihood ratios were not examined. In both our study and the studies, we compared, the specificity of USG for detecting choledocholithiasis was found to be greater than its sensitivity. Although the specificity and PPV ratios were high in other studies, their clinical significance was not evaluated because +LR was not analyzed. The low -LR and +LR in our study showed that USG is an inadequate test for the clinical diagnosis of choledocholithiasis. We believe that this inadequacy is due to the inability to standardize physicians because the patients are emergency patients, the test being dependent on the physician performing it, and factors such as the presence of gas in the patient. The prospective nature of the other studies we compared in the literature allowed evaluations to be performed by the same person. Additionally, since the other studies were not conducted in the emergency department under emergency conditions, we believe that the optimal fasting level and more time for evaluation were provided for hepatobiliary USG.

Computed tomography is not the first-choice imaging method for the diagnosis of choledocholithiasis. However, CT scans performed on patients who present to the emergency department with nonspecific complaints and abdominal pain are used to diagnose choledocholithiasis [12]. When our data were analyzed, the low sensitivity of CT for detecting choledocholithiasis was thought to be because gallstones have a density similar to that of bile, resulting in a low detection rate on CT scans. In a comparative study of MRCP, CT, and USG conducted by Singh et al., the specificity of CT for detecting choledocholithiasis was 97,14%, with a diagnostic accuracy rate of 94.29% [13]. In a comparative study of USG and CT conducted by Adedin et al., the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of CT for detecting choledocholithiasis were found to be 75%, 100%, 100%, and 96.1%, respectively [14]. When we compared the data in the literature with the results of the current study, we found lower values for both specificity and sensitivity. When the results of USG were compared with those of CT in our study for choledocholithiasis, the sensitivity, PPV, and NPV were found to be greater for CT, whereas similar results were found for specificity. This advantage in contrast resolution explains CTs higher sensitivity in detecting biliary pathology and supports its use as a second-line modality when USG findings are negative but clinical suspicion remains high.

In our study evaluating benign strictures with USG revealed that the sensitivity was very low and that the specificity was high. However, when the LR ratios were examined, both the negative and positive values were found to be low, indicating that USG is a method of very low clinical significance in the evaluation of strictures. In a study by Swaraj et al., the sensitivity was 17%, and the specificity was 100% [15]. Similar results were observed between this study and our current study. Such limited detection reflects USGs dependency on acoustic windows and operator technique, underscoring the need for more sensitive imaging when strictures are suspected.

In a comparative study of ERCP, MRCP, CT, and endoscopic USG for biliary stricture evaluation conducted by Rösch et al., the values for CT were found to be 94, 50, 94, and 50%, respectively [16]. In an another comparative study of USG, MRCP, CT, and ERCP conducted by Ferrari et al., the sensitivity of CT in the evaluation of benign strictures was 92.3%, and the specificity was 92.85% [17]. When the results of our study and these two studies in literature are compared, different results are found. This modest sensitivity and specificity reflect CTs limited ability to resolve subtle ductal narrowing without specialized cholangiographic phases, accounting for its variable rule-in utility in an acute-care setting.

In our study evaluating malignancy, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, -LR, and +LR ratios of USG were 12.0%, 98.46%, 33.3%, 94.57%, 0.89, and 7.79, respectively. When evaluated on the basis of specificity and PPV, the values in our study showed that USG is a successful test for diagnosing malignancy. Additionally, the +LR ratio of 7.79, which reflects clinical applicability, indicates that this imaging method is a clinically moderately meaningful test for diagnosing malignancy. In a study conducted by Swaraj et al. evaluating the diagnostic performance of USG and MRCP, these rates were found to be 72.41%, 98.90%, 95.46%, and 91.48%, respectively [15]. In a study conducted by Kaushal et al., the rates of malignancy via USG were found to be 82.9%, 98.3%, 97.1%, and 89.2%, respectively [8]. In a study conducted by Hanif et al., the values for malignancy diagnosis via USG were found to be 88.89%, 93.75%, 97.56%, and 75.0%, respectively [18]. In our study, the sensitivity of USG was lower than that reported in other studies, but its specificity was similar. When both our study and the results of other studies were examined, it was concluded that although USG has high specificity for diagnosing malignancy, it has low sensitivity and should not be used for exclusion. The low sensitivity rate in our study was thought to be due to the patient’s lack of cooperation with the physician performing the test, as well as the inability to standardize the procedure, which made it dependent on the skill of the person performing it, similar to the evaluation of choledocholithiasis.

In the study conducted by Kaushal et al., the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of CT for assessing malignancy were were 90.2%, 98.3%, 97.4%, and 93.4% [8]. In a comparative study of imaging methods for malignant obstructions conducted by Heinzow et al., the sensitivity was 67%, and the specificity was 82% [19]. In the report by Rösch et al., these rates were 77% for sensitivity and 63% for specificity [16]. Differences have also been observed in literature on this subject. In our study, this difference was likely caused by the fact that imaging was performed under emergency conditions, with a focus on emergency diagnoses. We recommend an initial USG due to its accessibility, noninvasive nature and high specificity. However, given its limited sensitivity, a contrast-enhanced CT should follow if USG findings are inconclusive, or clinical suspicion persists. This tiered approach supports accurate, timely decision-making in the emergency department.

In the evaluation performed for choledochal dilatation, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were found to be 63.95%, 50.33%, 85.32%, and 23.6%, respectively, for USG and 84.36%, 36.15%, 85.54%, and 34.06%, respectively, for CT. When these values were compared, CT had better values for sensitivity, PPV, and NPV, but its specificity was lower than that of USG. This indicates that CTs high sensitivity in detecting choledochal dilatation may necessitate additional confirmation with USG or MRCP to mitigate its higher false-positive rate.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design precluded blinding of radiologists and endoscopists to patients’ clinical data, introducing potential observer and confirmation bias. Ultrasound and CT imaging were operator-dependent, with no uniform protocol or standardized training to minimize variability in acquisition and interpretation. The time interval between imaging and ERCP was not controlled or recorded uniformly, allowing for interval changes in biliary pathology that may have influenced sensitivity and specificity estimates. Finally, we did not perform an a priori sample size calculation or power analysis; although our cohort of 1,011 patients exceeds those of previous reports, the lack of formal statistical power assessment may limit the robustness of subgroup comparisons.

Future studies should expand on our work through prospective, multicenter studies that use uniform ultrasound and CT protocols to limit operator- and procedure-related variability. By implementing predefined, efficient imaging workflows, researchers can confirm and broaden our results under consistent conditions.

Conclusion

According to the ACR criteria, both USG and CT are classified as appropriate and usable for obstructive jaundice, and our study also obtained results supporting this.

CT demonstrated higher sensitivity than USG for choledocholithiasis and other biliary pathologies, whereas USG exhibited superior specificity. Given the logistical constraints of emergency department CT (scanner availability, contrast administration, patient transport), USG—with its accessibility, safety, and rapidity—remains the preferred first-line modality. Contrast-enhanced CT should be reserved for patients with inconclusive USG and persistent clinical suspicion of obstructive jaundice.
