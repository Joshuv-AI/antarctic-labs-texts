Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids and Atrial Fibrillation: Friend or Foe?
Authors: Takeo Sato
Published: 2026

See article vol. 33: 1140-1147

Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are essential fatty acids that cannot be synthesized endogenously and must therefore be obtained through dietary intake. PUFAs are broadly classified into omega-3 PUFAs, represented by eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and omega-6 PUFAs, represented by arachidonic acid (AA) and dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA). The relationship between PUFAs and atrial fibrillation (AF) has attracted considerable attention for many years.

Previous studies have suggested that omega-3 PUFAs may exert antiarrhythmic effects and reduce the risk of AF 1) . Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the anti-arrhythmic effects of omega-3 PUFAs. Experimental studies suggest that EPA and DHA may suppress AF by modifying the ionic channels in the atrial cell membrane and stabilizing atrial electrophysiology through inhibition of pulmonary vein ectopy and early afterdepolarizations, attenuating inflammatory signaling pathways, reducing atrial structural remodeling through modulation of connexins, matrix metalloproteinases, and collagen expression, and influencing autonomic regulation 2 , 3) . Collectively, these effects may contribute to the prevention of atrial fibrosis and remodeling, and the maintenance of atrial electrical stability.

However, growing evidence in recent years has raised concerns regarding a potential association between omega-3 PUFAs and an increased risk of AF. Several large randomized controlled trials evaluating high-dose omega-3 PUFA supplementation have attracted particular attention. In the STRENGTH trial (patients with various cardiovascular disease risks; EPA+DHA, 4 g/day), the REDUCE-IT trial (patients with various cardiovascular disease risks; EPA, 4 g/day), and the OMEMI trial (elderly patients with recent myocardial infarction; EPA+DHA, 1.8 g/day), the incidence of AF was significantly higher in the omega-3 PUFA treatment groups than in the control groups 4 - 6) . In contrast, the VITAL Rhythm trial, which evaluated a lower-dose intervention (patients aged 50 years or older without prior cardiovascular disease, cancer, or AF; EPA 0.46 g/day plus DHA 0.38 g/day), did not demonstrate a significant increase in AF risk 7) . A meta-analysis of these four randomized controlled trials suggested a possible dose-dependent relationship between omega-3 PUFA supplementation and the risk of incident AF 8) .

Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that the association between omega-3 PUFAs and AF may be biphasic rather than linear 9) . While low-to-moderate levels of omega-3 PUFAs appear to exert antiarrhythmic effects through anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic, and membrane-stabilizing mechanisms, higher doses have been associated with an increased risk of AF as seen in the several large randomized clinical trials mentioned above 9) . This hypothesis provides a potential mechanistic explanation for the apparent discrepancy between observational studies, which generally report lower AF risk with higher omega-3 status, and intervention trials, in which pharmacological doses of omega-3 PUFAs have been associated with an increased incidence of AF.

Compared with omega-3 PUFAs, research examining the association between omega-6 PUFAs and AF is markedly limited, and the available evidence remains inconclusive. A large pooled analysis of 11 prospective cohort studies reported a largely neutral association between circulating omega-6 fatty acids and AF risk 10) . Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that certain omega-6 PUFAs may exert protective effects against AF in specific clinical settings. In a cohort of patients with ischemic stroke, lower levels of DGLA and AA were independently associated with the presence of AF 11) . Furthermore, among ischemic stroke patients with AF, lower AA levels were associated with higher brain natriuretic peptide concentrations and a greater prevalence of large vessel occlusion, suggesting a potential cardio-cerebrovascular protective role of omega-6 PUFAs 11) . Collectively, these findings indicate that the effects of omega-6 PUFAs on AF may vary according to the underlying disease context.

In this issue, Yanagisawa et al. added important evidence to the ongoing debate regarding PUFAs and AF 12) . They reported that higher circulating levels of both omega-3 (EPA and DHA) and omega-6 (AA and DGLA) PUFAs within physiological ranges were independently associated with a lower prevalence of AF 12) . These findings suggest that the relationship between PUFAs and AF may differ according to the biological context. While physiological PUFA status appears to be associated with a lower AF burden, pharmacological high-dose omega-3 supplementation may trigger AF ( Fig.1 ) . The effects of PUFAs on AF should also be influenced by patient characteristics, including underlying cardiovascular risk, autonomic tone, and the degree of atrial remodeling. Further studies are needed to clarify how PUFA profiles and clinical backgrounds interact to influence AF susceptibility.
