Service time thresholds at barrier-operated freeway exit toll booths
Authors: Göker Aksoy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper discusses service times based on vehicle class at barrier-operated toll booths. Service times were measured using four vehicle classifications: car (C), medium goods vehicle (MGV), trucks & bus (TB), and articulated truck (AT) with two payment options: electronic (ETC) and manual toll collection (MTC). Each toll booth has both payment systems and resulting in mixed toll booth utilization in terms of payment and vehicles. The collected data was utilized to estimate logistic regression, and non-parametric statistical tests were performed. Findings indicate, when vehicle size increases, so does service time for even ETC. Furthermore, logistic regression used to compute threshold service times shifting from ETC to MTC for C, MGV, TB, and AT, which were 5.92, 7.51, 10.00, and 12.80 s, respectively. The transition time for a vehicle switch from ETC to MTC based on the logistic curve is denoted by threshold service time. Below the threshold, ETC is more favorable option and affected by barrier and vehicle class. Overall, the results indicate that operators can benefit from more dynamic operational conditions facilitated by threshold service times, enabling adaptive and efficient toll booth operations. Additionally, these thresholds can serve as valuable measure of service quality for managing freeway exits.Keywords: Toll booth, Barrier control, Service time, Toll plaza, Electronic toll collection

Introduction

Istanbul, a megacity, is geographically split into two sides by the Bosporus strait. Due to the challenging topography and the scattered dispersion of commercial and residential areas, there has always been significant daily demand for travel between the two continents. As the backbone of the Istanbul highway network, three freeways are in service to meet this high demand, with one of them being operated by a private company. The privatized freeway is operated in a different manner compared to the ones run by public enterprises. The main focus is on preventing revenue loses and the toll booths on this freeway are equipped with both electronic toll collection (ETC) and manual toll collection (MTC) systems. Additionally, barriers are used to fend off unauthorized crossings. ETC relies on a prepaid radio frequency identification (RFID) tag affixed to a vehicles' windshield for fee collection, while MTC requires on-the-spot cash transactions. In the case that a driver does not have an ETC, each exit toll booth is operated with a toll collector servant for MTC. The private operator benefits from the flexibility to adjust the number of active toll booths based on demand, aiming to reduce operating expenses, and this adjustment is typically made according to the time of day. Consequently, each toll booth is used by all types of vehicles, with two different payment methods, resulting in the mixed use of toll booths. As an exception, the heavy vehicles are required to use only the rightmost toll booths. In Istanbul, even though the rightmost toll booths are reserved for heavy vehicles, small-sized vehicles frequently use them.

Under the uninterrupted traffic flow conditions with in high-standard and high-capacity freeways like those with, the presence of toll booths causes interruptions in traffic flow, reduces speeds, and adversely affects safety. This situation also decreases freeway efficiency by increasing the average headway per lane. To address this issue, electronic toll collection systems can be utilized to improve the service time per vehicle during toll collection, and an increase in the number of operating booths may also be necessary. Regardless, the presence of toll booths on freeways will have a negative impact on the number of vehicles passing through in a unit of time, thus adversely affecting service quality, making it a significant issue that needs to be addressed. In this research, the study focused on examining the service times at barrier-operated exit toll booths on freeways. The threshold values for service times were identified by employing logistic regression, which is the transition time for a vehicle class shift from ETC to MTC, in conjunction with vehicle classifications for the toll booth section illustrated in Fig. 1. The study area encompasses a two-lane freeway exit ramp that links to four exit toll booths. Since entry toll booth service times are substantially shorter [1], exit toll booths become critical points for maintaining an acceptable level of service. It should be noted that, the service time merely denotes the amount of time spent paying the toll fee, by ignoring the queue waiting time [2]. In technical terms, there should not have service time for ETC. Due to the presence of barriers, each vehicle must slow down and pass through the toll gate. Even with ETC, a time is set out for the payment process, and some time is required.

Literature review

Toll booths are typically the weakest points of freeways in terms of service quality. Service times, easily recognizable by drivers, have a crucial impact on both freeway capacity and perceived service quality by users. Therefore, it is important to measure and analyze service times in a meaningful way. Interruptions caused by toll booths create traffic discontinuity between upstream and downstream areas. Due to limited human perception, traffic conditions may deteriorate in these areas. Delays, which are particularly common in those areas, reduce travel comfort, road safety, and efficiency [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Lane discontinuity among upstream and downstream causes obligatory bottlenecks in those areas. Although bottleneck is a substantial source of discomfort on its own, barrier-operated toll booths worsen the conditions [8]. Nevertheless, when selecting road tolling technologies, it is crucial to consider the physical scale of the project and the complexity of the fees to be imposed [9]. Navandar et al. [10] point out that service times at toll booths primarily differ depending on vehicle class and payment method. With the increasing adoption of ETC, there is potential for reduced queue lengths and delays, leading to enhanced efficiency for road users [11]. This is especially important because an increase in mean queue lengths significantly impacts the perceived level of service in these areas [12].

Numerous researchers have previously investigated freeway toll booths and service times from various perspectives [1,7,10,11,13,14]. It was observed that service times for barrier-operated toll booths tend to be considerably longer than those without a barrier, with capacities typically ranging from 600 to 750 passenger cars per hour [8]. When cash payments are used by 50 percent or more of vehicles and the demand reaches to 4000 vehicles per hour, the required number of barrier-controlled toll booths can increase to as high as 14–18 to reduce delays [15]. Bari et al. [16] noted that the service headway, which encompasses charge processing and vehicle clearance time, is shorter for small-sized vehicles and longer for trailers. Jun-long, C [17]. divides the service time into two components: the first is the time from when the vehicle decelerates until the payment is complete, and the second is the time it takes for the vehicle to exit the payment area.

Another drawback associated with toll booths is the occurrence of vehicle delays and queues, as noted in various studies [3,5,14,15,[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Microscopic simulation has proven to be an effective tool for evaluating such performance metrics and distinguishing between ETC and MTC [14,15,19,21,[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Aksoy et al. [5], utilizing VISSIM, demonstrated that as the number of toll booths increases, delays also escalate. Bari et al. [18] introduced a delay model for MTC, considering a mix of traffic compositions, which takes into account deceleration/acceleration delay, queue waiting time, and service time across seven different vehicle classes. A similar delay model was proposed by Aycin et al. [19], based on the demand/capacity ratio and payment method. Al-Deek et al. [3] revealed that using only ETC reduces service times by 5 s per vehicle, though there is no significant change at mixed toll booths. Klodzinski and Al-Deek [20] assessed effectiveness by considering queue waiting time, vehicle service time, and the 85th percentile cumulative individual vehicle delays. Pompigna and Mauro [28] evaluated waiting times and queues using a time-dependent queuing model to assess the level of service at toll booths. Spiliopoulou et al. [26] considered a ramp metering control algorithm to manage toll booth traffic flow when exiting traffic exceeds the downstream road capacity. Weng et al. [29] demonstrated that ETC led to a reduction in vehicle emissions and energy savings. They achieved this by considering vehicle classes, speed, volume, and average queue length in MTC lanes. Bari et al. [13] conducted an investigation into MTC service times in mixed traffic conditions, employing a follower-leader pair, taking into account vehicle class and toll rates. They discovered that service times varied depending on the vehicle class and traffic composition. Lima et al. [7] found that the payment type and gender of the toll booth employee had the most significant impact on MTC service times, along with vehicle class. Navandar et al. [10] concluded that as the proportion of small-sized vehicles in the traffic stream increased, service times for those vehicles decreased. Mahdi et al. [1] observed that the percentage of heavy vehicles in the traffic flow had a substantial influence on queue lengths. Deshmukh et al. [30] evaluated the statistical distribution of clearance times for MTC in mixed traffic, considering seven different vehicle classes and sequential leader-follower vehicle pairs. They found that the general extreme value distribution provided the best fit for clearance times.

This literature review clearly indicates that the time-based operational characteristics and thresholds between ETC and MTC, taking into account vehicle class, have not been adequately addressed. Most of the previous studies have focused on queue lengths, delays, safety, the impact of vehicle classes, and payment options at toll booths. However, these studies have not comprehensively examined the individual or combined effects of these external factors to establish a payment service time threshold. This study aims to bridge this gap in the literature by introducing a methodology for determining and computing threshold service times for ETC and MTC, considering different vehicle classes. This approach has the potential to serve as a valuable tool for both practitioners and academicians involved in the design and analysis of toll booths.

Materials and methods

In this study, service times were measured by using video recordings for each unique payment process to establish threshold service times distinguishing between ETC and MTC. By considering vehicle class, the threshold service time can serve as an indicator of toll booth efficiency. Therefore, the primary research objective of this study is to identify the threshold service time at barrier operated toll booths based on vehicle composition.

At the exit toll booths operated with barriers (as given in Fig. 1), a total of 3264 individual measurements were collected. The data collection excluded instances of adverse weather conditions. Service times were recorded exclusively on sunny days, both on weekdays and weekends, and at various times throughout the day.

This study defines service time as the amount of time spent in the toll booth for the transaction. In reality, the entire time spent for the payment process can be influenced by the arrival rate, queue waiting times, and a variety of other external factors. In general, queue lengths mostly depend on the arrival rate. As a consequence, this study uses service time as the fee transaction time of a vehicle, whether it is ETC or MTC, while queue duration and arrival rate are ignored. Based on the most common vehicle classes, four unique vehicle categories were established Car (C, passenger car, pick-up, jeep, 1–8 passenger capacity), Medium Goods Vehicle (MGV, 8–25 passenger capacity and light trucks with 3.5–5.0 tones), Truck & Bus (TB, high load and passenger transport capability, 8–12 m), and Articulated Trucks (AT, higher load capability with 10–18 m). Out of 3264 observations, 70.82 percent were C, 11.85 percent were MGV, 8.24 percent were TB, and 9.09 percent were AT. Overall, 86.73 percent of the observations preferred ETC, while the remaining 13.27 percent used MTC.

Service times at toll booths are influenced by various factors, including the payment amount, the experience of toll booth employees, the payment method, and the vehicle class [11]. Notably, significant service time differences exist between cars and trucks [8]. On the other hand, there is neither a unique definition nor usage of service time in the literature. For instance, Mahdi et al. [1] defined service times for MTC as the time interval between a vehicle coming to a stop and then resuming movement. Karim et al. [11] characterized service times as the duration between a vehicle coming to a stop and subsequently passing through a barrier for MTC, while ETC payment was assumed to take 5 s. Lima et al. [7] described service times for MTC as the time between the initiation of a transaction and the vehicle's complete departure, whereas Navandar et al. [10] defined service time as the transaction time plus the time required for the vehicle to travel its own length. In the present study, MTC service times were assessed as the duration between when a vehicle stops for a transaction and when it completely clears the barrier. In contrast, as ETC payments do not require vehicles to come to a stop, service times were measured based on the time it takes for the vehicle to traveled the own length during a payment section. It's important to note that even vehicles equipped with ETC systems experience loss of time during the payment process in contrast to open-toll designs. Hence, there's a need to define a specific service time metric that should also be measured for ETC users.

Service time distribution analysis

As mentioned above, any discrepancy between the designated test groups must be statistically confirmed. Therefore, before deciding whether to employ a parametric or non-parametric statistical test, the data should be thoroughly analyzed. First of all, the statistical distribution of the data must be determined since, parametric tests are affected by whether or not the data is normally distributed. The Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is the primary technique for examining any potential differences between groups. For this test, data must be normally distributed and population variances must be homogeneous. Consequently, before initiating this analysis, the normality of each group was confirmed.

To determine group normality, Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were used. Although both test findings are presented in Table 2, Shapiro-Wilk that was designed for small sample sizes (<50) is not suitable for the existing data set. The interpretation of findings in Table 2 as follows: if the significance is larger than 0.05, the data do not significantly differ from normal distribution, and the alternative hypothesis (sample data differ significantly from a normal population) is rejected. As the results of both tests are only similar for AT-ETC, TB-MTC, and AT-MTC, these groups were found to be normally distributed.

Tests of Normality for independent samples.

Even though the ANOVA's normality criteria were not met, variance homogeneity was evaluated and found to be significant (Levene's statistic: 860.005, df1: 7, df2: 3256, Sig: 0.000), and the H0 null hypothesis (independent groups having equal population variances) was rejected. This implies that the ANOVA assumption of variance homogeneity among groups has been violated. Accordingly, the analysis was decided to be carried out using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test.

The Kruskal-Wallis test does not require the data to be normally distributed [31]. For this test, the samples should be independent and random, and the populations in each group should have the same distributional shape [31]. Kruskal-Wallis can be assumed to be the same as parametric one-way ANOVA, with the exception that the data are replaced by their ranks. In this study, the following hypotheses have been developed: H0: All eight populations have the same median and H1: At least one median differs. The Kruskal-Wallis test resulted in a score of 2158.238 (df: 7), 0.000 asymptotic significance and the test statistic was significant (<0.05), indicating that the null hypothesis (mean rankings of the groups are the same) is rejected. This outcome indicates that the median of all group samples differs. However, this test cannot indicate which group or groups are responsible for this difference. Here, pair-wise comparisons are required to differentiate the distinct groupings. For this aim, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare independent groups pairs having non-normal distributions.

When doing pair-wise post-hoc comparisons to evaluate differences within groups, the significance level should be adjusted using the Bonferroni method [32]. Keeping the significance level at 0.05 for pairwise comparisons, on the other hand, may lead to misleading findings. Due to the increased possibility of a type I error when performing multiple statistical tests, the Bonferroni correction must be employed to change the probability (p) values. The Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni's adjustment was used to compare groups in pairs. Since there are 28 possible comparisons between the eight groups, the significance threshold was set at 0.05/28 = 0.002. According to pair-wise testing, the only difference between the C-MTC and MGV-MTC was statistically significant among the 28 groups, as shown in Table 3, since the p-value was found to be larger than 0.002.

Methodology

In this study, a logistic regression model was estimated for the payment method (ETC or MTC) as the outcome (dependent) variable, and service times and vehicle classes as explanatory (independent) variables. Therefore, the threshold service time between ETC to MTC has been clarified by taking into account vehicle classes. Even though there is no payment choice for a driver who has an ETC system on his/her vehicle, the effect of mixed payment at a toll booth can be exposed by using logistic regression. Previous studies on service time have focused on estimating service time based on vehicle class and payment type. It is well established in the literature that smaller vehicles require less service time for the payment process. Additionally, delay modeling and queue length have been addressed to determine the level of service and estimate toll booth performance. However, none of the previous studies have considered service time as a performance indicator or attempted to estimate thresholds between ETC and MTC, taking into account vehicle classes as well as logistic regression.

The logistic regression model estimates the probability (p) the occurrence of an event. Accordingly, the dependent variable π(x) can only take two values: π(x) = 1 for ETC and π(x) = 0 for MTC. The probability of an event is constrained from 0 to 1, but odds ratios can be between zero to infinity. Higher odd ratios mean a lower probability and vice versa.

Development of the logistic regression model

In this study, a logistic regression model was estimated for the payment method (ETC or MTC) as the outcome (dependent) variable, and service times and vehicle classes as explanatory (independent) variables by selecting Car (C) vehicle class as a reference. Therefore, the threshold service time between ETC to MTC has been clarified by taking into account vehicle classes. Even though there is no payment choice for a driver who has an ETC system on his/her vehicle, the effect of mixed payment at a toll booth can be exposed by using logistic regression.

During the model estimation process, it was determined that omnibus testing for model coefficients was statistically significant (Chi-square 2514.515, df: 4 and Sig: 0.000). This is the likelihood ratio of a chi-square test comparing the model coefficients with the null model, and it implies that statistically significant model coefficients are determined and model fit improves.

Unlike the traditional least squares regression model, logistic regression does not provide a strong indication of the quality of fit. Especially, metrics such as R-square, should be used with caution. In the evaluation of the model, the following performance indicators were determined: −2 Log-likelihood: 40,616, Cox & Snell R-Square: 0.537, and Nagelkerke R-Square: 0.989. In logistic regression, Cox & Snell and Nagelkerke measures are regarded as pseudo R-squares. Although there isn't much information on how to interpret the pseudo R-squares, they do provide information on how well the model fits. According to the Cox & Snell and Nagelkerke R-square values, the independent variables explained 53.7 percent to 98.9 percent of the variance in the de-pendent variable. Furthermore, the Hosmer and Lemeshow test was found to be non-significant (Chi-square: 0.258, sig: 1.00), indicating that it is a good predictor of model fit. It is certain that, under the same conditions, AT required greater service times, lowering the toll booth's capacity.

The precise nature of these thresholds needs further clarification. These thresholds have been calculated based on the data collected in this study, which involved on-site observations conducted at different times and with varying vehicle compositions. However, this approach yields valuable insights, particularly for operators. Neither average nor minimum service times prove useful in determining active toll booths from the operator's perspective. For instance, referring to Table 1, the vehicle class C exhibits an average service time of 2.416 s for ETC and 22.075 s for MTC. Consequently, it is not feasible to decide number of active toll booths exclusively for either ETC or MTC users based solely on these averages.

Moreover, these thresholds are subject to variation throughout the day due to various human-related factors, changes in vehicle composition, and traffic volume. Hence, the calculated threshold of 5.92 s for the vehicle class C encompasses a wide array of human-related factors, making it an informative metric for operators. Consequently, the determination of available toll booths could be achievable even in real-time for operators, as outlined in this paper. Furthermore, the real-time adjustment of this threshold can offer a reliable measure of service quality and assist in determining the number of available toll booths or evaluating the individual performance of toll gates.

As explained, the logistic curve proves to be highly valuable for assessing service quality and guiding decision-making processes, particularly in real-time scenarios. Moreover, it offers a clear understanding of how various factors, such as vehicle types, vehicle compositions, and, in this study, barrier operation, influence service times. Even when ETC is utilized, larger vehicles require longer periods at toll booth sections.

Furthermore, it could be beneficial to determine whether to temporarily suspend barrier operation when high thresholds are encountered, and operational conditions cannot be altered to reduce them. Naturally, implementing such a decision may increase the workload for operators, as they would need to manually verify payments in cases of violations. Although with the offered approach, it may not be possible to estimate number of only MTC or only ETC toll booths, threshold service times in real time could enhance service quality during periods of excessive demand. At a freeway toll booth where both ETC and MTC are in mixed usage, the operator cannot simply operate only MTC for revenue maximization. Instead, there is a need to employ staff at each booth to collect cash payments from the drivers without an ETC system while preserving a desired level of service for road users. In order to prevent excessive delay, mixed usage of MTC and ETC is preferred by the operator. However, to make informed decisions about when to employ this last-minute strategy, temporarily suspend barrier operation, the logistic curve proves to be a valuable source of information for existing service quality to operator.

Toll booths equipped with barriers initially seemed to be an effective method for reducing toll payment violations and preventing revenue losses for private operators. However, as service times are expected to increase, a greater number of barrier-controlled toll booths will be necessary to accommodate the demand. This decision is likely to lead to increased operational costs and contribute to the negative environmental effects of stop-and-go traffic caused by these toll booths. For these reasons, the methodology proposed in this study proves to be valuable, especially for operators, as it addresses various aspects discussed above.

Conclusions

Freeways offer a fast and convenient means of time saving by providing high travel comfort and reliability. However, toll collection is an inevitable part of any freeway journey. The method of manually collecting toll fees has been in use since the early days of modern freeways but has evolved with advancing technology. However, private operators typically employ both Electronic Toll Collection (ETC) and Manual Toll Collection (MTC) simultaneously for financial reasons, allowing mixed traffic in conjunction with barrier control.

Regardless of how much a vehicle's service time is extended by barrier-controlled toll booths, significant delays are incurred when combined with mixed payment methods and mixed traffic. To address this issue, this study investigates service times while considering payment methods and vehicle classes. A logistic regression model was developed based on service times and vehicle class to predict the probability of ETC usage. Consequently, service time thresholds have been established for four distinct vehicle classes differentiating between MTC and ETC.

The results indicate that even for ETC, the service time increases as the size of the vehicle increases. Utilizing the developed logistic regression model, the threshold service time for MTC for the AT vehicle class was determined to be 12.8 s. Transactions with toll charges below this threshold are more likely to be processed through ETC. However, longer service times require a higher number of toll booths, each requiring an MTC attendant, leading to increased operating expenses. Additionally, beyond the cost considerations, the stop-and-go traffic resulting from longer service times can potentially lead to increased greenhouse gas emissions from internal combustion engine vehicles.

The logistic regression model establishes threshold service times between MTC and ETC for each vehicle class. Even for the smallest vehicle category (C), the service time threshold was determined to be 5.92 s. The thresholds for MGV and TB were found to be 7.51 and 10.00 s, respectively. These thresholds serve as valuable indicators of the existing service quality and can aid in determining the required number of active toll booths in real time.

Private operators often aim to deploy an appropriate number of toll booths, even if they have more available on-site. However, the number of active toll booths should vary based on the specific characteristics of the toll booth, the vehicle composition, and the traffic volume. Particularly for real-time applications, making adaptive decisions about the number of active toll booths can be achievable by utilizing the logistic regression curve that has been developed and explained in this study.

Even if ETC payments are made below calculated thresholds, the passage through a toll booth is still significantly time-consuming due to mixed payment methods and barrier operation. These times certainly vary with traffic volume and vehicle composition, further emphasizing the importance of this study from the perspective of toll booth operators. When these thresholds are combined with high demand, it results in either a large number of toll booths or long queues with extended service times.

Policy Recommendations

Toll booths located in urbanized areas play a vital role in traffic management, revenue generation, and the efficiency of transportation systems. However, traditional barrier-operated toll booths have demonstrated various drawbacks that affect service times, lead to delays, harm the environment, and worsen congestion during peak periods. Operating them is another research topic aimed at improving service quality. From the perspective of toll booth operators, potential toll losses can be prevented, and operating costs can be reduced, as indicated by the findings of this study. Instead of using a fixed number of toll booths, making the system more dynamic and adaptable to environmental factors can result in time savings for users and cost savings for operators simultaneously. Clearly, barrier operation increases vehicle interactions, and with changes in demand, the operation of toll gates should be well-planned. Therefore, the methodology presented in this paper serves as an excellent tool for both determining the required number of active toll booths and measuring service quality.

Suggestions for future research

Future research endeavors should integrate a blend of threshold service times and the delay attributed to toll booths. Moreover, analyzing deceleration/acceleration patterns and considering the physical conditions across various toll booth areas would offer insights into drivers' toll booth behavior patterns. Factors such as the total and operational toll booth numbers, lane configurations in both downstream and upstream directions, weaving area length, ramp width, and lane numbers are essential in understanding drivers' decisions regarding toll booth selection. Such investigations hold the potential to hasten the transition from traditional toll booth setups to barrier-free tolling systems integrated with connected vehicle technology. This strategic shift not only aids in quantifying hazardous emissions but also facilitates the proposal of short-term solutions. As vehicle and payment technologies continue to evolve, it becomes imperative to adapt operational conditions accordingly. The growing prevalence of connected vehicle technology and the increasing adoption of electric vehicles could potentially accelerate changes in toll booth operation conditions, making it a pivotal research focus in the near future.
