Recovery from COVID-19 may take more than just time
Authors: Ella Victoria Brockwell-Mole, Kylie Hill, John D Blakey
Published: 2025

Next time you are on the train, or in long queue at the supermarket, ask the person next to you what they think of the local hospital. It is likely their opinion will be heavily coloured by an experience of acute illness or injury. However, we take it for granted that most people are soon out of danger from their initial vascular event, infection, trauma or other significant episode. Hospitals spend relatively little time on the unstable patient, in comparison with the considerable time progressing the unwell patient towards being ‘fit for discharge’. This new label may imply a level of functionality substantially lower than that on admission. Acute severe respiratory tract infections are a prime example of the abrupt changes in function that can occur, with life-changing consequences driven by damage to multiple organs and deconditioning. An admission with a viral respiratory illness decreases independence and carries a likelihood of the need for increased support.1 2 New disability, psychological disturbance and readmissions are common after bacterial pneumonia.3 4 Hospitals may not support individuals through the challenges of recovery, and such arrangements are given little consideration in guidelines.4 The paucity of services and evidence-based standards following discharge with acute lower respiratory tract infection5 contrasts with the wealth of high-quality information on pulmonary rehabilitation and widespread availability of at least some aspects of such a service.

The COVID-19 pandemic rapidly created a cohort of individuals with severe acute respiratory illness and, therefore, its longer term sequelae. Some of these features were very familiar, consistent with other consequences of critical respiratory illness. However, it also appeared that COVID-19 might be a special case, with the emergence of a prevalent ‘long COVID’ syndrome across social media and in primary care. The efforts of 45 UK centres to collaborate on the PHOSP-COVID cohort to generate information on post-COVID sequelae are remarkable, especially given the burden of disease at the time.6 The latest paper from Briggs et al in this issue of BMJ Open Respiratory Research provides valuable insights into the postdischarge services provided by hospitals assessing their clinical and cost-effectiveness. The study has leveraged a unique natural experiment created by concurrent rollout of a diverse range of healthcare pathways across the UK. The study followed >1000 participants who were offered a variety of postacute care support. Key outcomes assessed were participant self-reported recovery, number of newly diagnosed conditions and health-related quality of life. The cost-effectiveness of postdischarge interventions was then estimated.

The findings of the study are striking and may challenge clinicians’ preconceptions. They underscore the widespread and lasting impact of severe and acute severe respiratory illness on patient health, with only 29% of participants reporting feeling fully recovered. Crucially, the study demonstrates that more structured and comprehensive postdischarge care can significantly improve outcomes, particularly if they include physical rehabilitation. The most extensive services that offered comprehensive assessment, rehabilitation and mental healthcare to all patients were associated with better clinical outcomes at a cost of £1 700 per quality adjusted life year (QALY), well within accepted thresholds. As with any observational study, its ‘real-world’ strengths are also accompanied by limitations. These include selection bias, imprecision and incompleteness in data collection, confounding by indication and variation in the ecology of care over the study’s duration. It is also unclear how well this unvaccinated cohort provides a generalisable model for other situations, and the postdischarge care is not sufficiently well described in nature and uptake to directly inform practice.

As health services plan for the long-term management of COVID-19 and other acute severe respiratory ailments, the message is clear: offering a more comprehensive, coordinated postdischarge care package is not only clinically beneficial, but economically justified. The study’s insights may serve as a blueprint for future pandemic preparedness to ensure that our systems are equipped to deliver impactful, systematic and cost-effective care.
