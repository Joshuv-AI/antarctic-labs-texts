Nutritional status and clinical severity in children with sickle cell disease and confirmed asthma: a multicentre observational study
Authors: Gabriel Bafunyembaka, Mathieu Nacher, Ariel Makembi, Chimène Maniassom, Narcisse Elenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveChildren with sickle cell disease (SCD) frequently experience chronic inflammation, increased metabolic demands, and recurrent acute complications. Asthma is a recognised comorbidity associated with increased morbidity in SCD. However, the contribution of nutritional status, particularly body mass index (BMI), to clinical severity among children with SCD and confirmed asthma remains poorly documented, especially in tropical settings.The objective of this study was to describe the nutritional status of children with SCD and spirometry-confirmed asthma and to assess the association between BMI-for-age z-scores and clinical severity.MethodsWe conducted a multicentre observational study including children aged 5–17 years with SCD and spirometry-confirmed asthma. Nutritional status was assessed using WHO 2007 BMI-for-age z-scores calculated from measured weight and height. Undernutrition was defined as a BMI-for-age z-score < − 2. Clinical severity was defined as the occurrence of at least two hospitalisations for vaso-occlusive crises and/or acute chest syndrome in the preceding 12 months. Associations between nutritional indicators and clinical severity were evaluated using bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for relevant clinical covariates.ResultsA total of 138 children were included (median age 8.0 years). Overall, 17.4% presented undernutrition, while 12.3% were overweight or obese. In bivariate analyses, undernutrition was more frequent among children with severe disease than among those with non-severe disease (24.5% vs. 13.5%). When BMI-for-age z-score was analysed as a continuous variable, lower values were associated with increased odds of severe disease (OR per 1-SD decrease = 1.34; 95% CI 1.01–1.78). After multivariable adjustment, the association between BMI-for-age z-score and severity was attenuated and did not reach statistical significance (adjusted OR 1.29; 95% CI 0.96–1.74; p = 0.09).ConclusionThese findings highlight the contribution of clinical and inflammatory factors to disease severity in children with sickle cell disease and confirmed asthma, including prior ACS, which showed a borderline association. Clinical severity appeared to be more closely related to respiratory burden than to nutritional status alone. Lower BMI-for-age z-scores reflected global systemic vulnerability rather than an independent risk factor. Routine nutritional assessment may therefore support risk stratification and help identify children who could benefit from targeted multidisciplinary care.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is one of the most common inherited haemoglobin disorders worldwide and represents a major contributor to childhood morbidity and mortality. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 estimates, more than 7 million people were living with SCD globally, with a substantial proportion of deaths occurring during childhood, particularly in high-burden and resource-limited settings [1, 2]. Importantly, recent analyses have highlighted that the mortality and disability burden attributable to SCD is underestimated when relying solely on single underlying cause-of-death coding, underscoring the need for improved risk stratification and comprehensive care in paediatric populations [1, 2].

Among acute complications, acute chest syndrome (ACS) remains a leading cause of hospitalisation and death in children with SCD. ACS is a complex and multifactorial condition involving infectious, inflammatory, thrombotic, and ventilatory mechanisms, and it often presents with rapid clinical deterioration [3, 4]. Recent paediatric reviews emphasise that early identification of children at risk of ACS and recurrent hospitalisations is essential to reduce morbidity and improve outcomes [3, 4]. These issues are particularly relevant in tropical and low-resource settings, where environmental exposures, infectious burden, and limited access to specialised care may further exacerbate respiratory complications.

Asthma has emerged as a clinically important comorbidity in children with SCD. Multiple studies have shown that children with SCD and asthma experience higher rates of ACS, vaso-occlusive crises (VOC), and hospital admissions compared with their non-asthmatic counterparts [5–7]. Asthma in this population is associated with increased respiratory symptoms and healthcare utilisation, and its diagnosis may be challenging because airway obstruction and wheezing can also occur as part of SCD-related lung disease [5–7]. Nevertheless, functionally confirmed asthma, particularly when assessed using spirometry, has been consistently linked to worse respiratory and clinical outcomes in paediatric SCD [5–7].

Beyond asthma alone, respiratory morbidity in children with SCD is increasingly recognised as heterogeneous and context-dependent. Recent studies from sub-Saharan Africa have reported a high prevalence of abnormal lung function patterns, including obstructive, restrictive, and mixed phenotypes, often associated with disease severity and cumulative organ damage [8, 9]. These findings suggest that respiratory vulnerability in SCD reflects a complex interaction between disease biology, environmental exposures, and comorbid conditions, reinforcing the importance of studying respiratory outcomes within specific epidemiological contexts.

Nutritional status represents another critical, yet often underappreciated, dimension of vulnerability in paediatric SCD. Children with SCD are exposed to chronic inflammation, increased metabolic demands, recurrent infections, and frequent hospitalisations, all of which may impair growth and lead to low body mass index (BMI) or undernutrition [10–13]. Contemporary data continue to document growth faltering and nutritional compromise in children with SCD, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [10–13]. Importantly, nutritional impairment is not merely a descriptive finding; recent cohort studies have demonstrated that underweight status in school-aged children with SCD is associated with an increased risk of early mortality, highlighting its potential prognostic significance [11, 14, 15].

At the same time, a growing body of literature describes a “double burden” of malnutrition in SCD populations. While undernutrition remains prevalent, overweight and obesity are increasingly observed, particularly in settings undergoing nutritional and epidemiological transition [16]. Excess body weight may adversely affect respiratory mechanics, increase airway resistance, and exacerbate respiratory symptoms, potentially compounding the risk of acute respiratory events in children with SCD [17].

Mechanistically, nutritional status may interact with SCD severity through multiple pathways. Undernutrition may reflect reduced physiological reserve, impaired immune function, and heightened susceptibility to infections, thereby increasing the likelihood of ACS and recurrent hospitalisations [11–13]. Conversely, higher BMI has been associated with altered airway physiology in children with SCD, suggesting that body composition can modulate lung function and respiratory symptoms [17].

Despite these advances, there is a notable lack of data examining the combined impact of nutritional status and asthma particularly when asthma is confirmed by spirometry on disease severity in children with SCD. This gap is especially pronounced in tropical settings, where environmental exposures, infectious diseases, and socioeconomic factors may further modify the relationship between nutrition, respiratory disease, and SCD morbidity. Standardised nutritional assessment using the World Health Organization (WHO) 2007 BMI-for-age growth reference for children and adolescents aged 5–19 years provides a robust and internationally comparable framework to address this gap [18].

In this context, the present study aimed to describe the nutritional status of children with SCD and spirometry-confirmed asthma using WHO BMI-for-age z-scores, and to assess whether undernutrition and lower BMI-for-age z-scores are associated with increased clinical severity, defined by recurrent hospitalisations for VOC and/or ACS.

By focusing on a functionally confirmed asthma phenotype and standardised nutritional indicators, this study seeks to contribute to a more integrated understanding of respiratory and nutritional vulnerability in paediatric SCD and to inform targeted, multidisciplinary care strategies in tropical settings. Nutritional status may influence clinical outcomes through multiple interconnected pathways. Low BMI may reflect chronic inflammation, increased metabolic demands, recurrent hospitalisations, and reduced physiological reserve. In children with asthma, impaired nutritional status may contribute to increased respiratory vulnerability and susceptibility to acute chest syndrome. In this context, BMI should be interpreted as a marker of systemic vulnerability rather than a direct causal determinant of clinical severity. This study was conducted in a tropical setting, where sickle cell disease prevalence is high and environmental exposures may influence both respiratory and nutritional status.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a multicentre retrospective observational study involving children followed for sickle cell disease in referral paediatric centres across three public hospitals in French Guiana: Cayenne Hospital (Centre Hospitalier Andrée-Rosemon), Kourou Hospital (Centre Médico-Chirurgical de Kourou), and Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni Hospital (Centre Hospitalier de l’Ouest Guyanais). Data were collected retrospectively from medical records between January 2018 and December 2024. This study was based on retrospective data collected as part of routine clinical care. According to French regulations, it falls under the category of Research not involving human subjects (Recherche n’impliquant pas la personne humaine, RnIPH), as defined by Article L1121-1 of the French Public Health Code.

Study population

Children aged 5 to 17 years with a confirmed diagnosis of SCD (all genotypes) and spirometry-confirmed asthma were eligible. Eligible patients were included using consecutive sampling of all children meeting inclusion criteria during the study period. Place of residence was categorized as urban or rural. Urban residence was defined as living within the central urban areas of Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, Kourou, or Cayenne. Rural residence referred to living in peripheral or outlying areas surrounding these urban centres.

Asthma diagnosis was established according to standardised criteria detailed in the Respiratory assessment and spirometry section. Children were included if complete anthropometric data (weight and height) were available. Patients with missing nutritional measurements or uninterpretable spirometry were excluded from the present analysis.

Respiratory assessment and spirometry

Asthma diagnosis was confirmed based on physician diagnosis supported by bronchodilator reversibility, defined as an increase in FEV₁ of ≥ 12% following bronchodilator administration, according to ATS/ERS recommendations.

Spirometry was performed in clinically stable children aged 5 years and older across the three participating hospitals, in accordance with American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) paediatric spirometry standards. Predicted spirometry values were calculated using Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) reference equations.

Spirometric values were expressed as percentages of predicted values based on age, sex, height, and ethnicity using reference equations. Measurements included forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), and the FEV₁/FVC ratio.

At least three acceptable and reproducible manoeuvres were required for interpretation.

Asthma diagnosis followed Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) recommendations.

Nutritional assessment

Weight and height were measured during routine clinical visits using standardised procedures. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Nutritional status was assessed using WHO 2007 BMI-for-age z-scores (5–19 years), calculated from measured values using the AnthroPlus reference dataset. BMI-for-age z-scores were calculated using WHO AnthroPlus software version 1.0.4.

Clinical severity definition

Clinical severity was defined a priori as the occurrence of two or more hospitalisations for vaso-occlusive crises (VOC) and/or acute chest syndrome (ACS) within the preceding 12 months. This threshold has previously been used in paediatric sickle cell disease studies as a clinically meaningful indicator of recurrent morbidity and severe disease burden [19, 20].

Hospitalisations were ascertained from hospital electronic medical records and verified using discharge summaries from the participating centres.

Acute chest syndrome was defined as a new pulmonary infiltrate on chest radiograph associated with fever and/or respiratory symptoms. Vaso-occlusive crisis was defined as an acute painful episode requiring medical evaluation or hospitalisation, in accordance with standard paediatric sickle cell disease definitions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic, nutritional, and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were summarised using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Model goodness-of-fit was assessed using standard diagnostic procedures.

Associations between nutritional status and clinical severity were first explored using bivariate logistic regression analyses. BMI-for-age z-scores were examined both as a categorical variable (undernutrition vs. no undernutrition) and as a continuous variable to assess potential dose–response relationships.

Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for predefined covariates. An extended multivariable model was used to explore the independent contribution of nutritional status alongside key clinical factors. Covariates included in the multivariable models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and previously published literature on predictors of clinical severity in paediatric sickle cell disease. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethics statement

In accordance with Article L1121-1 of the French Public Health Code and national regulations governing RnIPH studies, the requirement for individual informed consent to participate was waived. Patients and/or their legal guardians were informed of the possible use of anonymized routine-care data for research purposes, and no opposition was recorded. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 138 children with sickle cell disease (SCD) and confirmed asthma were included in the analysis (Fig. 2). Baseline demographic, clinical, lung function, and nutritional characteristics of the study population are summarised in Table 1. The median age was 8.0 years (IQR 6.0–11.0), and 63 children (45.7%) were male. Most children lived in rural areas (103, 74.6%). The distribution of SCD genotypes included HbSS (50.7%), HbSC (31.9%), and HbSβ (17.4%). Hydroxyurea treatment was reported in 105 children (76.1%), and 47 children (34.1%) had a history of acute chest syndrome.

The mean body mass index (BMI) was 16.7 ± 2.3 kg/m², corresponding to a mean BMI-for-age z-score of − 0.58 ± 1.21. Nutritional status was heterogeneous, with 24 children (17.4%) classified as undernourished (BMI-for-age z-score < − 2), including 6 children (4.3%) with severe undernutrition. In contrast, 17 children (12.3%) had a BMI-for-age z-score > + 1, indicating the presence of a double burden of malnutrition in this population.

In bivariate analyses, undernutrition was more frequent among children with severe clinical disease than among those with non-severe disease (24.5% vs. 13.5%), although this association did not reach statistical significance (OR = 2.06; 95% CI 0.86–4.92; p = 0.10) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this cohort of children with sickle cell disease (SCD) and confirmed asthma, we observed patterns suggesting an association between nutritional status and clinical severity. First, WHO-defined undernutrition (BMI-for-age z-score < − 2) was more frequent among children with severe clinical forms in bivariate analyses. Second, when BMI-for-age was analysed as a continuous variable, lower z-scores were associated with increased odds of severe disease. However, these associations were attenuated after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that nutritional status may reflect broader clinical vulnerability rather than acting as an independent determinant of severity.

Undernutrition and impaired growth are well-described features of paediatric SCD, likely driven by hypermetabolism, chronic haemolysis, persistent inflammation, recurrent infections, and inadequate intake [10–13]. This background is consistent with the heterogeneous nutritional profile observed in our cohort, and aligns with reports from resource-limited settings where underweight status and growth failure remain frequent in children with SCD [13, 14]. More recently, African and international cohorts using WHO-based metrics have shown that underweight status is not only common but also prognostically relevant, being associated with adverse outcomes including increased mortality in school-aged children with SCD [15, 16]. Taken together, these data support the interpretation that low BMI may represent a marker of systemic vulnerability and reduced physiological reserve rather than a purely descriptive anthropometric characteristic [10–13, 15, 16].

At the same time, an emerging “double burden of malnutrition” is increasingly described in SCD populations, particularly in higher-income contexts, where overweight and obesity are becoming more prevalent alongside persistent undernutrition [17]. Excess adiposity may worsen respiratory mechanics, increase airway resistance, and intensify dyspnea and symptoms, potentially complicating the clinical course in children with SCD [18]. Although high BMI-for-age values were less frequent in our cohort, their presence reinforces the need for routine and standardised anthropometric assessment using WHO references, particularly in children with SCD and asthma, where body habitus may influence respiratory physiology and symptom perception [15, 18].

The respiratory dimension is central because asthma is a recognised comorbidity that increases SCD morbidity, including higher risks of acute chest syndrome (ACS), pain episodes, and hospitalisations [5–7]. Foundational studies and subsequent reviews have framed the asthma ACS interaction as a critical clinical and physiopathological nexus rather than a simple coexistence of two conditions, with overlapping triggers and shared pathways of airway inflammation, hypoxemia, and acute respiratory deterioration [6, 7]. More recent work further emphasises that pulmonary phenotypes in paediatric SCD are heterogeneous (obstructive, restrictive, mixed) and that lung function may decline longitudinally, sometimes independently of classical atopic markers, highlighting the complexity of respiratory vulnerability in this population [11, 12, 21].

In this broader context, our results support a multifactorial model of severity in children with SCD and confirmed asthma, in which nutritional vulnerability, respiratory burden, and contextual factors (including prior ACS and rural residence) may jointly shape hospitalisation risk and clinical trajectories [5–7, 11, 12, 21].

Rural residence showed a tendency toward increased clinical severity, possibly reflecting differences in access to healthcare services, environmental exposures, or delayed treatment seeking in peripheral areas. These contextual factors highlight the importance of considering social and environmental determinants of health when interpreting clinical severity in children with sickle cell disease.

The attenuation of nutritional associations after multivariable adjustment is therefore not unexpected and may reflect several complementary mechanisms. Confounding by intrinsic disease severity, particularly a history of ACS, could influence both nutritional status (through inflammation, anorexia, and repeated hospitalisations) and the likelihood of subsequent severe events [19, 20]. In addition, nutritional status may operate partly through mediating pathways rather than as an independent determinant: undernutrition may increase susceptibility to infections and respiratory complications and reduce recovery capacity, thereby raising the risk of ACS and recurrent hospitalisations without necessarily retaining an independent effect once closely correlated covariates are included [10–13]. Finally, limited statistical power may reduce the ability to detect modest independent effects in adjusted models when several clinically relevant variables are correlated and included simultaneously [19, 20].

From a clinical and public health perspective, these findings support an integrated approach to paediatric SCD care in which systematic nutritional screening using WHO BMI-for-age z-scores is incorporated alongside respiratory assessment for children with confirmed asthma [15]. Identifying children with low BMI, prior ACS, and contextual vulnerabilities may help prioritise intensified follow-up, tailored therapeutic education, optimisation of asthma control, and prevention strategies aimed at reducing acute morbidity. While nutritional supplementation and nutrition-focused interventions are increasingly considered in SCD, recent evidence syntheses highlight the need for robust studies assessing clinically meaningful outcomes, supporting the rationale for future prospective and interventional work in well-characterised paediatric subgroups such as those with confirmed asthma [22].

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study include the focus on children with spirometry-confirmed asthma and the use of standardised WHO nutritional indicators, allowing international comparability. Limitations include the observational design, moderate sample size, and the possibility of residual confounding related to overall disease severity and socioeconomic factors. Nutritional associations should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation in larger, prospective, and longitudinal studies. Due to its retrospective design based on routine care data, this study did not require formal ethics committee approval under French regulations.

Important potential confounders, including haemolysis markers, infection burden indicators, and asthma treatment variables, were not consistently available and could not be included in multivariable models. Data on dietary intake and pubertal status were not available in the present dataset. These factors may influence BMI interpretation, particularly during adolescence, and their absence represents a limitation of this study.

Additional indicators of disease severity, including transfusion history, detailed lung function indices beyond basic spirometry, and outpatient vaso-occlusive crisis frequency, were not consistently available in the medical records and therefore could not be included in the present analysis. These missing variables may have contributed to residual confounding.

In addition, potential centre effects may have influenced clinical management and hospitalisation practices across participating hospitals. Selection bias may also have occurred because inclusion required interpretable spirometry, potentially excluding younger or more severely ill children unable to perform reliable testing.

Conclusion

In children with sickle cell disease and confirmed asthma, clinical severity appeared to be associated with respiratory burden, particularly prior acute chest syndrome, while lower BMI-for-age z-scores reflected global systemic vulnerability rather than an independent risk factor. Routine nutritional assessment may therefore support clinical risk stratification.
