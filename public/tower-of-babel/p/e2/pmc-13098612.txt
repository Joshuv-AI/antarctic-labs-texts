Pregnancy outcomes of early pregnancy with chorionic bump: Case series
Authors: Yu Feng, Siman Liu, Xiaoling Qin, Zhong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDA chorionic bump is an uncommon and localized irregular protrusion of the chorion into the gestational sac on the decidual basalis side, typically identified via ultrasound during early pregnancy. The clinical significance and its association with pregnancy outcomes remain uncertain.OBJECTIVETo retrospectively analyze 5 cases of chorionic bumps detected by ultrasound in early pregnancy and explore potential associations with embryonic development issues or fetal chromosomal abnormalities.METHODSClinical data from 5 patients with chorionic bumps were reviewed. Ultrasound findings, embryonic development, and pregnancy outcomes were examined.RESULTSThe study provides a detailed analysis of the identified cases, with a focus on early pregnancy ultrasound findings and clinical management strategies.CONCLUSIONThe presence of chorionic bumps in early pregnancy is a rare occurrence, and further research is needed to determine their impact on pregnancy outcomes, including their potential correlation with fetal developmental abnormalities.

Introduction

A chorionic bump is an irregular protrusion of the chorionic membrane into the gestational sac on the decidual basalis side. It is rare and usually detected by ultrasound. The relationship between chorionic bumps in early pregnancy and pregnancy outcomes is still unclear, but it may be linked to abnormal embryonic development and fetal chromosomal abnormalities. Data in the literature concerning chorionic bumps are limited, and the only previous report estimated the prevalence of a chorionic bump to be 0.7% with a live birth rate of 47%.1 This study reports 5 cases of chorionic bumps detected by ultrasound in early pregnancy, offering insights into their management.

Case presentation

This retrospective analysis reviewed the clinical data of 5 patients diagnosed with a chorionic bump by ultrasound during early pregnancy at our center between August 2024 and August 2025 (Table 1). Informed consent for the publication of clinical data was obtained from all patients, and the project has been approved by the ethics committee. Case 1

A 33-year-old woman (G2P0) presented with a chorionic bump. Her last menstrual period was on September 25, 2024. A transvaginal ultrasound on day 39 of amenorrhea showed an intrauterine pregnancy (gestational sac 11 mm × 23 mm × 9 mm3, with a 2.4 mm yolk sac,5 weeks gestation) with a chorionic protrusion into the gestational sac, measuring 9 mm × 6 mm × 4 mm3 (Figure 1A). A follow-up ultrasound 1 week later showed resolution of the bump. Routine ultrasounds indicated normal embryonic development, with no recurrence of the chorionic bump. Nuchal translucency (NT) measured 1.3 mm, and whole-genome sequencing of amniotic fluid obtained via amniocentesis showed no abnormalities. A 3D ultrasound showed no fetal structural abnormalities. At 39 weeks, she delivered a healthy 3.5 kg infant.Fig. 1Ultrasonic image of chorionic bump (A:Ultrasonic image of chorionic bump in case 1; B:Ultrasonic image of chorionic bump in case 2; C:Ultrasonic image of chorionic bump in case 3; D:Ultrasonic image of chorionic bump in case 4; E:Ultrasonic image of chorionic bump in case 5).Fig 1 dummy alt text

Case 2

A 34-year-old woman (G2P0) had her last menstrual period on June 24, 2024. She conceived through assisted reproductive technology at our hospital using an artificial cycle. A transvaginal ultrasound 26 days after embryo transfer showed an intrauterine pregnancy (gestational sac 21 mm × 19 mm × 15 mm3, with a fetal bud length of 2.2 mm,6 weeks gestation) with a slightly hyperechoic mass measuring 13 mm × 12 mm × 11 mm3 on one side of the chorion within the gestational sac. Follow-up ultrasounds showed progressive enlargement of the chorionic bump, reaching a maximum size of 25 mm × 20 mm × 10 mm3 (Figure 1B). At 9± weeks gestation, the bump had resolved. Subsequent ultrasounds showed normal embryonic development with no recurrence. The nuchal translucency (NT) measurement was 1.4 mm, and noninvasive DNA testing indicated a low-risk result. A 3D ultrasound revealed no fetal structural abnormalities. The patient underwent a cesarean section at 34+ weeks for fetal distress and delivered a healthy live infant.

Case 3

A 29-year-old woman (G1P0) presented with a chorionic bump. Her last menstrual period was on October 5, 2024. She conceived through assisted reproductive technology at our hospital using an artificial cycle. An ultrasound performed 22 days after embryo transfer showed an intrauterine pregnancy (gestational sac 17 mm × 31 mm × 15 mm3, with a 3.3 mm yolk sac,5 weeks gestation) with a slightly hyperechoic mass measuring 17 mm × 15 mm × 13 mm3 on one side of the chorion within the gestational sac (Figure 1C). Subsequent monitoring showed good embryonic development, with a slight decrease in the size of the chorionic bump. At 7± weeks gestation, the bump had resolved completely. Routine ultrasounds showed normal embryonic development with no recurrence of the chorionic bump. The nuchal translucency (NT) measurement was 1.5 mm. An anechoic area measuring 7 mm × 4 mm2 adjacent to the umbilical cord was noted, suspected to be an umbilical cord cyst. Chromosomal microarray analysis of amniotic fluid obtained via amniocentesis showed no abnormalities. A 3D ultrasound revealed no fetal structural abnormalities. The patient delivered a healthy 3.4 kg infant at 39 weeks.

Case 4

A 40-year-old woman (G4P1) visited our clinic after a chorionic bump was detected on ultrasound. Her last menstrual period was on October 4, 2024. She conceived through assisted reproductive technology with an artificial cycle at our hospital. An ultrasound performed 36 days post-transfer showed an intrauterine pregnancy (gestational sac 40 mm × 36 mm × 29 mm3, with a fetal pole of 13 mm,7+ weeks’ gestation) with a 16 mm × 9 mm2 heterogeneous echogenic area on one side of the chorion, suspected to be a chorionic bump (Figure 1D). A follow-up ultrasound at 8± weeks confirmed resolution of the bump. Routine ultrasounds showed normal embryonic growth with no recurrence. The nuchal translucency (NT) measurement was 1.4 mm, and noninvasive DNA testing indicated a low-risk result. A 3D ultrasound ruled out fetal structural abnormalities. She delivered a healthy 3.85 kg infant at 39 weeks of gestation.

Case 5

A 34-year-old woman (G0P0) visited our clinic after a chorionic bump was detected on ultrasound. Her last menstrual period was on November 4, 2024. She conceived through assisted reproductive technology with an artificial cycle at our hospital. An ultrasound performed 25 days post-transfer showed an intrauterine pregnancy (gestational sac 25 mm × 23 mm × 17 mm3, with a fetal pole of 4.6 mm,6 weeks’ gestation) with a 10 mm × 9 mm2 slightly hyperechoic heterogeneous area within the gestational sac, suspected to be a chorionic bump (Figure 1E). Subsequent monitoring showed satisfactory embryonic development, with the bump gradually decreasing in size. A follow-up ultrasound at 12 weeks confirmed resolution of the bump. The nuchal translucency (NT) measurement was 0.9 mm. Karyotype analysis of amniotic fluid obtained via amniocentesis revealed fetal trisomy X (47, XXY). She underwent induction of labor in the second trimester.

Discussion

The incidence of chorionic bump is approximately 1.5 to 7 per 1000 pregnancies, with the majority occurring in early pregnancy.2 Most chorionic bumps resolve spontaneously and rarely persist into the second or third trimester.3 Currently, research on chorionic bumps is limited, mainly consisting of case-control studies and case reports. Nowadays, chorionic bumps are mainly evaluated clinically using Doppler ultrasound. MRI is less frequently used due to its high cost. Most studies have shown a significant association between chorionic bumps and adverse pregnancy outcomes or fetal chromosomal abnormalities.4, 5, 6 However, a few studies suggest that a chorionic bump detected on ultrasound during early pregnancy does not necessarily predict a poor pregnancy outcome.7

The etiology of chorionic bump remains unclear. Most theories suggest it represents a hematoma or localized hemorrhage,8 while others propose it may be related to widespread necrosis of the decidualized endometrium.9 The correlation between chorionic bumps and pregnancy outcomes is still debated. Most studies focus on assessing the number, size, and timing of chorionic bumps via ultrasound and comparing outcomes between affected and healthy control groups. Many studies suggest that a chorionic bump detected early in pregnancy is associated with embryonic demise or fetal chromosomal abnormalities. However, a few studies argue that a chorionic bump does not necessarily predict adverse pregnancy outcomes.10 The timing of the bump’s appearance may also be linked to pregnancy outcomes. Studies suggest that bumps appearing before 8 weeks of gestation may be more predictive of embryonic abnormalities or demise,5,11 while those appearing after 11 to 13 weeks or persisting beyond that time may have less clinical significance.12, 13 Assisted reproductive technology (ART) refers to medical procedures used to treat infertility by manipulating eggs, sperm, or embryos outside the human body to achieve pregnancy. The ART process itself (eg, artificial cycle preparation, hormonal support or the underlying infertility) might influence the prevalence or nature of the chorionic bump compared to natural conception.

This study reviewed 5 hospitalized cases where chorionic bumps were detected by ultrasound during early pregnancy at our center. All patients had singleton pregnancies. None of the 5 patients in this study had a history of major diseases. All 5 patients were admitted for tocolytic therapy due to abdominal pain and/or vaginal bleeding. Case 1 conceived naturally, while the other 4 (Cases 2 and 3 due to tubal factors; Case 4 due to male factor; Case 5 due to ovulatory disorders) conceived via assisted reproductive technology. In all 5 cases, the chorionic bump appeared before 8 weeks of gestation and resolved spontaneously before the second trimester. Ultrasound monitoring showed no progressive enlargement of the bumps, with the largest measuring 25 mm × 20 mm × 10 mm3. The bumps resolved naturally as gestation progressed. Ultrasound evaluations were performed every 1 to 2 weeks to monitor embryonic development. The gestational sac size and crown-rump length remained within normal ranges, and follow-up showed normal nuchal translucency (NT) measurements in all 5 cases. In Case 3, a suspected umbilical cord cyst (7 mm × 4 mm2) was noted on NT ultrasound, which resolved by 18 weeks of gestation. Genetic testing showed no abnormalities in Cases 1 and 3, who underwent amniocentesis with whole-genome sequencing or chromosomal microarray analysis. Noninvasive DNA testing showed low-risk results for Cases 2 and 4. Karyotype analysis of amniotic fluid in Case 5 revealed a fetal chromosomal abnormality (47, XXY). No sonographically detectable fetal malformations were observed on 3-dimensional ultrasound in Cases 1 through 4. All cases showed normal embryonic development.

In summary, all 5 cases with chorionic bumps in early pregnancy showed embryonic development consistent with gestational age. Four out of 5 cases resulted in the delivery of healthy infants, while only 1 case had a chromosomal abnormality.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that a chorionic bump detected by ultrasound in early pregnancy does not necessarily predict an adverse pregnancy outcome. This was a retrospective study with a small sample size (n=5). Our findings provide additional evidence that chorionic protrusion does not necessarily predict adverse pregnancy outcomes, though further large-sample, multicenter studies are needed for validation. Close monitoring with regular prenatal screening or diagnostic evaluation is recommended for patients with chorionic bumps to enable early detection of potential fetal anomalies.
