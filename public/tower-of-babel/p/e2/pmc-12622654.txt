Effectiveness of intravenous insulin pump therapy combined with levofloxacin in diabetic patients with lung infections: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Yajing Dou, Chunxia Peng, Yaguang Zhou, Yanan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite progress in treating diabetes-related lung infections with antimicrobials and glycemic control, outcomes remain poor – prolonged hospitalization, high relapse rates, and > 20% mortality in severe cases. Novel therapies addressing both metabolic and infectious challenges are urgently needed. This study evaluates the efficacy of combining intravenous insulin pump therapy with levofloxacin for such patients. A total of 223 patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus (DM) with lung infection were divided into 2 groups: the control group, which underwent levofloxacin and oral metformin hydrochloride, and the observation group, which received intravenous insulin pump therapy on basis of the above treatment. The outcomes of these 2 groups were compared and evaluated. After treatment, the observation group exhibited significantly lower levels of hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), 2-hour postprandial blood glucose (2hPG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score, serum amyloid A (SAA), serum ferritin (SF), procalcitonin (PCT), thrombin time (TT), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT). Conversely, the levels of fibrinogen (FIB), as well as CD4+/CD8+, immunoglobulin (Ig) A, IgG and IgM were significantly higher in the observation group. Furthermore, the effective treatment rate in the observation group was markedly superior to that in the control group. Subgroup analyses confirmed the consistent benefit of the combined therapy across most patient strata; notably, the treatment effect was significantly more pronounced in the subgroup of patients with poor baseline glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 9.0%). The combination of intravenous insulin pump therapy and levofloxacin was associated with improved glycemic control, reduced infection severity, and better coagulation and immune function parameters compared to standard therapy. These findings suggest that this combination may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for diabetic patients with lung infections. The enhanced benefit observed in patients with higher baseline HbA1c highlights its potential value for this difficult-to-treat population. Further prospective, randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm these findings.

1. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder affecting over 537 million individuals globally, with its complications posing significant threats to morbidity and mortality.[1] Among these complications, pulmonary infections represent a critical concern, as diabetic patients exhibit a 2- to 4-fold higher risk of severe respiratory infections compared to the nondiabetic population.[2] Impaired immune function, chronic hyperglycemia-induced neutrophil dysfunction, and microvascular damage collectively predispose individuals with DM to infections such as community-acquired pneumonia, tuberculosis, and fungal pneumonias.[3,4] Despite advances in antimicrobial therapies and glycemic management, outcomes remain suboptimal, with prolonged hospitalization, higher relapse rates, and mortality rates exceeding 20% in severe cases.[5] This underscores the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies that address both metabolic dysregulation and infection control in this vulnerable population.

Conventional treatment for diabetic patients with pulmonary infections typically involves intensified insulin regimens to achieve glycemic targets alongside empirical antibiotics.[6] However, subcutaneous insulin therapy often fails to provide stable glycemic control during acute infections due to fluctuating insulin resistance, stress-induced hyperglycemia, and delayed correction of hyperglycemia.[7] Uncontrolled blood glucose levels, in turn, exacerbate immunosuppression, impair pathogen clearance, and promote antibiotic resistance – a vicious cycle that complicates recovery.[8] Recent studies highlight that maintaining near-normoglycemia (blood glucose 140–180 mg/dL) during infections significantly reduces mortality and accelerates clinical improvement.[9] The medical literature strongly advocates the administration of intravenous insulin infusion over the subcutaneous route for various clinical conditions, such as diabetic ketoacidosis, nonketotic hyperosmolar state, critical care illnesses, myocardial infarction, cardiogenic shock, and the postoperative phase following cardiac surgeries.[10] While intravenous insulin pumps have demonstrated superiority in glycemic control for critically ill patients, its role in managing acute pulmonary infections in diabetics remains underexplored.[11]

Antimicrobial selection further influences outcomes in this context. Levofloxacin, a broad-spectrum fluoroquinolone, is widely recommended for respiratory infections due to its potent activity against common pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and atypical organisms like Legionella.[12] Its high lung tissue penetration and bactericidal efficacy make it a first-line agent for community-acquired pneumonia.[13] However, its use in diabetic patients is complicated by drug interactions (e.g., hypoglycemia risk when combined with sulfonylureas) and emerging resistance patterns, particularly in gram-negative bacilli.[14] Optimizing levofloxacin’s therapeutic potential requires concurrent stabilization of metabolic parameters, as hyperglycemia has been shown to reduce antibiotic efficacy by altering pharmacokinetics and promoting biofilm formation.[15] This interplay suggests that combining intravenous insulin pumps with levofloxacin may synergistically enhance infection resolution by addressing both hyperglycemia and microbial burden.[16]

Despite these theoretical advantages, clinical evidence supporting this combined approach remains scarce. Current guidelines lack specific recommendations for integrating advanced insulin delivery systems with antimicrobial therapies in diabetic patients with pulmonary infections.[17] Preliminary studies on intravenous insulin pumps in hospitalized diabetics report reduced hypoglycemic events and shorter hospital stays, but none have evaluated its impact on infection-specific outcomes such as time to defervescence, radiographic resolution, or inflammatory biomarker normalization.[18] Similarly, while levofloxacin’s efficacy is well-documented in general populations, its performance in diabetics – particularly those receiving intravenous insulin pumps – has not been systematically assessed.[19] This knowledge gap limits the development of tailored protocols for this high-risk cohort.[20]

This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of combining insulin pump therapy with levofloxacin in DM patients with lung infection. By analyzing clinical, metabolic, and microbiological outcomes, this research seeks to provide evidence-based insights into optimizing the management of diabetes complicated by pulmonary infections, ultimately bridging a critical gap in current therapeutic paradigms.

2. Methods

2.1. Patients selection

In this single-center, retrospective observational study, a total of 223 patients diagnosed with DM accompanied by lung infection were included in Civil Aviation General Hospital between January 2017 and October 2024. The mean age of the participants was (62.72 ± 9.21) years. The participants were divided into 2 groups: the observation group (n = 121), which received intravenous insulin pump therapy combined with levofloxacin and conventional glucose control, and the control group (n = 102), which underwent levofloxacin treatment and conventional glucose control alone. This study received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Civil Aviation General Hospital (Approval Number: 2025-L-K-033). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study, which involved the analysis of anonymized clinical data without any additional intervention or risk to the patients.

The diagnosis of DM was based on the criteria established by American Diabetes Association, which include a fasting plasma glucose level of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a 2-hour plasma glucose level of ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test, or a glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of ≥ 6.5%.[21,22] The diagnosis of lung infection was confirmed through clinical symptoms, radiographic findings, and microbiological evidence, such as sputum culture or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid analysis.[23,24] All patients met the diagnostic criteria for both DM and lung infection.

The study encompasses the following exclusion criteria: patients with type 1 DM[25]; patients with severe liver or kidney dysfunction (e.g., serum creatinine > 2.0 mg/dL or alanine aminotransferase > 3 times the upper limit of normal)[26]; those with a history of hypersensitivity to levofloxacin or other quinolones[27]; individuals with uncontrolled hypertension or cardiovascular diseases[28]; patients with active malignancies or immunocompromised conditions[29]; pregnant or lactating women[30]; and patients who had received systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies within the preceding 3 months.[31] A flow chart of this study is presented in Figure 1.

All patients were informed about the content of this study and voluntarily participated. The baseline characteristics, including age, gender, duration of diabetes, HbA1c levels, and severity of lung infection, were recorded and compared between the 2 groups to ensure homogeneity.

2.2. Treatment protocols

The patients in the control group were administered with levofloxacin intravenously at a dosage of 500 mg once a day, alongside oral metformin hydrochloride (500 mg/time, 2 times/d). The observation group received a combination treatment of intravenous insulin pump therapy and levofloxacin on basis of the control group. The levofloxacin was used at the same dosage and administration frequency as the control group. Concurrently, intravenous insulin infusion is typically prepared by combining regular insulin with 0.9% saline in either a 1:1 ratio (50 units of insulin in 50 mL of saline) or a 0.5:1 ratio (25 units of insulin in 50 mL of saline). This solution is connected to an intravenous fluid line via a piggyback setup. To ensure standardization, all patients in the observation group received intravenous insulin pump therapy following a strict institutional protocol. The insulin infusion was initiated at a starting dose of 0.05 units/kg/hour, with subsequent titration performed by nursing staff every 1 hour to maintain a target blood glucose range of 6.1 to 10.0 mmol/L. Titration was based on a predefined sliding scale agreed upon by the endocrinology and critical care departments. Blood glucose levels are monitored using hospital-approved methods, prioritizing bedside (point-of-care) testing over central lab analyses to enable timely adjustments to the insulin infusion. Blood samples are typically obtained via fingerstick or arterial line. Monitoring frequency is guided by the algorithm, often requiring hourly checks until levels stabilize, followed by assessments every 2 hours. Monitoring every 4 hours is not advised due to insufficient frequency for safe management.

2.3. Data collection

2.3.1. Data on demographics

Retrospective data were meticulously recorded from the hospital records of each index admission. The baseline information collected upon admission from all patients comprised age, gender, weight, body mass index, duration of diabetes mellitus, HbA1c levels, fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels, postprandial blood glucose (PBG) levels, and comorbidities such as hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease. Additionally, clinical parameters related to lung infection, including respiratory rate, oxygen saturation (SpO2), heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and radiographic findings (e.g., chest X-ray or CT scan results), were documented. Microbiological data, such as sputum culture results and identified pathogens, were also recorded to assess the severity and etiology of the lung infection.

2.3.2. Handling of missing data

Data completeness was assessed for all variables. Patients with missing data for primary outcome measures (e.g., HbA1c after treatment, infection resolution status) were excluded from the analysis. For secondary continuous variables with minimal missing data (<5% of cases), a complete-case analysis was performed. The number of excluded cases due to missing data is reported in the patient flow diagram.

2.3.3. Sample preparation and analysis of serum biomarkers

At baseline and after 2 weeks of treatment, all blood samples from the patients were collected and forwarded to the laboratory department of our hospital for analysis. The samples were transferred to microcentrifuge tubes within 60 minutes of collection and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 minutes. The resulting supernatant was aliquoted into 200 μL vials and stored at −80°C for further analysis. Commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were used to measure serum biomarkers, including HbA1c, serum amyloid A (SAA) protein, serum ferritin (SF), and procalcitonin (PCT). Additionally, coagulation parameters such as thrombin time (TT), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinogen (FIB) were analyzed using standardized laboratory techniques. Immunological markers, including CD4+/CD8+ ratio, immunoglobulin (Ig) A, IgG, and IgM, were also quantified using ELISA kits. All blood specimens undergo testing by skilled professionals and technical personnel from the department of clinical laboratory.

2.3.4. Clinical and laboratory parameters

Clinical data, including FBG, 2-hour postprandial blood glucose (2hPG), and acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score, were recorded at baseline and after 2 weeks of treatment. Blood glucose levels were measured using a standardized glucometer, while the APACHE II score was calculated based on physiological variables, age, and chronic health conditions.

2.3.5. Efficacy evaluation criteria

Therapeutic outcomes were categorized as marked effective, effective, or ineffective based on the following simplified criteria[32]:

Marked effective: Complete resolution of lung infection symptoms (e.g., cough, fever, dyspnea); FBG < 7.0 mmol/L and 2hPG < 10.0 mmol/L; normalization of inflammatory markers (e.g., procalcitonin < 0.1 ng/mL, CRP < 10 mg/L).

Marked effective: Complete resolution of lung infection symptoms (e.g., cough, fever, dyspnea); FBG < 7.0 mmol/L and 2hPG < 10.0 mmol/L; normalization of inflammatory markers (e.g., procalcitonin < 0.1 ng/mL, CRP < 10 mg/L).

Effective: Partial improvement in symptoms (e.g., reduced cough, stable body temperature); FBG 7.0 to 8.5 mmol/L and 2hPG 10.0 to 13.0 mmol/L; ≥50% reduction in inflammatory markers.

Effective: Partial improvement in symptoms (e.g., reduced cough, stable body temperature); FBG 7.0 to 8.5 mmol/L and 2hPG 10.0 to 13.0 mmol/L; ≥50% reduction in inflammatory markers.

Ineffective: No improvement or worsening of symptoms (e.g., persistent fever, worsening dyspnea); FBG > 8.5 mmol/L and 2hPG > 13.0 mmol/L; No significant reduction in inflammatory markers.

Ineffective: No improvement or worsening of symptoms (e.g., persistent fever, worsening dyspnea); FBG > 8.5 mmol/L and 2hPG > 13.0 mmol/L; No significant reduction in inflammatory markers.

2.3.6. Statistical analysis

The study represented continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed them employing the Student t test. Categorical variables were articulated as percentages and compared using either the Chi-squared test or Fisher exact test for univariate analysis, as deemed suitable. Statistical analyses were conducted through SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk), defining statistical significance as a two-sided P-value <.05.

3.7. Subgroup analyses

To further assess the consistency of the treatment effect of the insulin pump combination therapy across different patient populations, we performed subgroup analyses based on key baseline characteristics, with treatment effectiveness as the primary outcome (Table 7). The beneficial effect of the observation group was consistently significant across most predefined subgroups, including age (≤65 vs > 65 years), baseline disease severity (APACHE II) score, and presence of hypertension (all within-subgroup P-values < .05), with similar risk ratios. However, a statistically significant interaction was observed for baseline glycemic control (P for interaction = .045). Although the combined regimen was effective in both subgroups, the magnitude of benefit was greater in patients with poorer baseline glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 9.0%; risk ratio = 1.49) compared to those with better control (HbA1c < 9.0%; risk ratio = 1.27). This suggests that the combined therapy might offer a particularly valuable clinical advantage for diabetic patients with lung infections who have inadequate glycemic control at baseline.

4. Discussion

The integration of intensive glycemic control via insulin pump therapy with levofloxacin treatment in diabetic patients suffering from lung infections was associated with significant clinical benefits in this study. The results suggest marked improvements in glycemic stability, inflammatory resolution, coagulation normalization, and immune recovery, which collectively contributed to a higher effective treatment rate compared to conventional therapy.

4.1. Glycemic control and its role in infection management

The superior glycemic control observed in the insulin pump group is consistent with the well-established principle that hyperglycemia exacerbates immune dysfunction and delays infection resolution.[33] Insulin pump therapy, by providing continuous intravenous insulin infusion, minimizes glucose variability – a critical factor in reducing oxidative stress and preserving leukocyte function.[34] Stable glycemic levels enhance neutrophil phagocytosis, cytokine production, and pathogen clearance, which may have contributed to the faster resolution of lung infections in the observation group.[35] Our findings resonate with randomized trials emphasizing the importance of tight glucose control in critically ill patients, yet also highlight context-specific nuances. For instance, the landmark NICE-SUGAR study, which focused on a heterogeneous ICU population, found mortality benefits with a moderate glycemic target. Our study, by contrast, specifically examined infection-related endpoints in a targeted cohort of diabetic patients with lung infections, suggesting that the benefits of intensive control may be particularly pronounced in this subpopulation.[36] Notably, the observation group achieved a mean HbA1c of 7.2%, which is closer to the American Diabetes Association’s recommended target (<7.0%), potentially underscoring the clinical relevance of this approach.[37] However, the interplay between hyperglycemia and antibiotic efficacy presents an intriguing area for further exploration. Levofloxacin, a broad-spectrum fluoroquinolone, exhibits concentration-dependent bactericidal activity. Hyperglycemia may impair tissue penetration of antibiotics, whereas insulin’s vasodilatory effects could enhance drug delivery to infected sites.[38] This represents a plausible synergistic mechanism that could help interpret the higher pathogen clearance rates observed in the observation group.

4.2. Inflammation, coagulation, and multisystem interactions

The significant reduction in systemic inflammation markers (SAA, SF, PCT) in the observation group highlights a potential link between glucose control in modulating inflammatory cascades. Hyperglycemia is proposed to activate nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha.[39] Insulin, beyond its metabolic effects, is thought to exert anti-inflammatory properties by suppressing NF-κB and upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10.[40] This dual action could represent 1 pathway through which infection resolution was facilitated in our cohort. Coagulation abnormalities, common in diabetic patients, were also ameliorated in the observation group. Hyperglycemia can induce endothelial dysfunction and platelet hyperactivity, contributing to a prothrombotic state.[41] The shortened coagulation times (TT, PT, APTT) and elevated FIB levels observed posttreatment might indicate a trend toward restored hemostatic balance. These findings are in line with studies demonstrating insulin’s ability to inhibit tissue factor expression and enhance fibrinolysis.[42] Importantly, the higher FIB levels in the observation group could also reflect improved hepatic synthetic function due to reduced systemic inflammation, rather than a prothrombotic shift.[43]

4.3. Immune restoration: bridging glucose control and host defense

The restoration of immune function, evidenced by elevated CD4+/CD8+ ratio and immunoglobulin levels, suggests a possible immunomodulatory effect of intensive insulin therapy.[44] Hyperglycemia can impair T-cell differentiation, reduces antibody production, and disrupts dendritic cell maturation – defects that appeared to be less pronounced in the observation group. The increased CD4+/CD8+ ratio may indicate enhanced adaptive immunity, while elevated immunoglobulin levels could point to a more robust humoral response.[3] While intriguing, these findings partially corroborate experimental models showing that insulin administration restores thymic function in diabetic animals.[45] However, the clinical translation of these mechanisms remains exploratory, and longer follow-up studies are needed to assess sustained immunological benefits.

4.4. Clinical efficacy and comparative insights

The observation 13 (effective treatment rate (89.27%) significantly surpassed that of the control group (65.69%), which aligns with the findings of a previous trial where continuous insulin infusion reduced pneumonia-related mortality in diabetic patients.[46] Our study adds to this body of evidence by also observing multisystem improvements beyond glycemic control. Contrastingly, some studies have reported no additional benefit of insulin pumps over multiple daily injections in non-critically ill patients.[47] This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in infection severity or comorbidities. It is also possible that our cohort’s exclusion of patients with advanced renal disease or immunosuppression influenced the magnitude of the observed effects.

5. Conclusion

This study suggests that combining insulin pump therapy with levofloxacin is associated with improved glycemic control, reduce systemic inflammation, and favorable trends in immune and coagulation parameters in diabetic patients with lung infections, as compared to conventional therapy. These findings support the potential of intensive glucose management strategies in this high-risk population. However, the retrospective nature of this analysis necessitates caution, and the observed associations do not establish causality. Future research should focus on validating these results in diverse clinical settings and unraveling the underlying mechanisms to refine therapeutic approaches.

6. Study limitations

As a retrospective observational study, this work is inherently limited by the potential for residual confounding and selection bias, which precludes definitive causal conclusions. This study has several other limitations. Single-center design: the generalizability of findings may be limited by the single-center recruitment and relatively small sample size. Short follow-up: the 2-week treatment period precludes assessment of long-term outcomes, such as recurrence of infection or chronic complications. Lack of pathogen-specific analysis: while microbiological data were collected, subgroup analyses based on pathogen type (e.g., gram-negative vs gram-positive) were not performed. Confounding Factors: Unmeasured variables, such as nutritional status or socioeconomic factors, may have influenced outcomes. Therefore, larger, multicenter studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to validate these findings. Also, basic research exploring the molecular pathways linking glucose control, immune function, and coagulation could deepen our understanding. Besides, studies comparing insulin pump therapy with advanced closed-loop systems or alternative antibiotics (e.g., carbapenems) may optimize treatment protocols.
