Topical administration of testosterone ameliorates atopic dermatitis of KFRS4 rats
Authors: Kenta Hayashi, Kana Nagasaka, Haruki Kitazume, Mao Urasaki, Hoang Trung Hieu, Shinichiro Nakamura, Takashi Kuramoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder influenced by genetic, environmental, and immunological factors, and its prevalence shows notable sex-associated differences. KFRS4 rats spontaneously develop AD-like dermatitis with higher severity in females, suggesting a role for sex hormones in disease modulation. This study investigated the contribution of gonadal hormones to dermatitis development in KFRS4 rats by examining the effects of gonadectomy and topical testosterone treatment. Ovariectomy did not alter dermatitis onset or severity in female rats, whereas orchiectomy markedly exacerbated dermatitis in males. Orchiectomized males developed severe lesions characterized by crusting and erosion, and exhibited significantly higher total skin lesion severity scores (TSLSS) compared with sham controls, indicating a protective role of testicular factors. To further evaluate testosterone’s contribution, we applied testosterone ointment to ovariectomized females and orchiectomized males. Testosterone treatment significantly reduced TSLSS in both sexes and prevented lesion formation in the treated dorsal neck region. Histological analysis revealed increased sebaceous gland number and epidermal thickening in testosterone-treated skin, accompanied by robust induction of androgen receptor (AR)-positive cells within sebaceous glands and across epidermal layers. In contrast, dermatitis-affected skin lacked AR-positive cells, likely due to architectural disruption. In addition, testosterone treatment significantly increased the expression of filaggrin and decreased the expression of Th2 (Il4 and Il5) and Th17 (Il17a) cytokine genes. Together, these findings suggest that testosterone ameliorates dermatitis by enhancing skin barrier function and modulating type 2 and Th17 immune responses. These findings identify testosterone as a key regulator of dermatitis in KFRS4 rats and highlight androgen-mediated barrier enhancement as a potential therapeutic strategy for AD.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a relapsing chronic inflammatory disease characterized by eczematous skin lesions and intense pruritus. Patients with AD often exhibit skin barrier dysfunction and predispose to elevation of plasma IgE. They also have a personal or family history of type I hypersensitivity reactions, such as allergic rhinitis and asthma [1].

Globally, AD affects approximately 9.6% of adults aged 16 and older, although the prevalence in children is even higher, 20% to 25% [2]. In Japan, the prevalence of AD is estimated to be approximately 12% to 15% of children with lower rates around 5% to 8% among adults [3].

Although the etiology of AD is complex, several lines of evidence demonstrate that genetic predisposition, immune system dysregulation, and environmental factors are involved in the development of AD. For example, mutations in the filaggrin (Flg) gene affect skin barrier function [4]. Skewed Th2 immune response plays a role in AD development [5, 6]. Allergens derived from mites, animals, pollen, and dusts cause AD [7, 8]. Pollutants and climate such as sunlight and humidity also provoke AD [9, 10].

Clinical and epidemiologic studies have indicated gender differences in the prevalence of AD. Female gender was associated with an increased prevalence of AD [2, 11]. In a Scotland survey, AD presentations were predominant in female during adolescence and were approximately twice as common as those seen in adult males. The female predominance emerged from the age of 15 years and persisted until ages 45–49 years, which suggested the sex hormones affected the AD presentations [12]. Such female predominance in adolescents with AD has been also demonstrated in a South Africa and Taiwan [13, 14] as well as in adults [3, 15].

In the case of adult AD, the Th2 shift is more prevalent in women than in men, but skin barrier impairments are more likely to occur in men with exposure to high levels of androgens that perturb skin permeability barrier. In women, skin barrier is restored by estrogen but are disturbed by progesterone. Female adults might be slightly more protected from skin barrier impairment compared to male adults. Thus, it is hypothesized that female adults with higher Th2 activity but slightly more protection from skin barrier impairment might show a slightly higher prevalence of AD compared to male adults.

KFRS4 rats spontaneously develop dermatitis that accompanies scratching behavior. Dermatitis initially appeared after puberty, around 4 months of age. Extensive infiltration of eosinophils, mast cells and lymphocytes was histologically observed. KFRS4 rats also exhibit skin barrier dysfunction, elevated plasma IgE level, and increased levels of Th2 cytokine transcripts in lymph nodes. Betamethasone, generally used to treat human AD, improved the symptoms of dermatitis. Thus, KFRS4 rats have been thought to be a good animal model of AD [16].

Dermatitis is more prevalent in female than in male KFRS4 rats. The skin lesions are observed in 100% of female but only 50% of 8-month-old KFRS4 rats. Total skin lesion severity score (TSLSS) was significantly higher in females than in the age-matched males. Therefore, KFRS4 rats can serve as an animal model to elucidate the relationship between AD and sex differences.

In the present study, to clarify the relationship of sex with the dermatitis development in KFRS4 rats, we focused on sex hormones. As gonads are dominant sources of sex hormones, we performed gonadectomy in both male and female KFRS4 rats and observed development of the skin lesions. We topically administrated testosterone to KFRS4 rats and found attenuation of the skin lesions. Additionally, we found induction of androgen receptor (AR)-positive cells in the epidermal layer of the KFRS4 rats.

Materials and Methods

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Research Committee of Tokyo University of Agriculture (TUA) (Approval number: 2020124) and were conducted in accordance with the Regulations on Animal Experimentation of TUA.

Rats

KFRS4/Kyo rats were obtained from the National BioResource Project for the Rat (NBRP-Rat) [17]. Animals were housed in the animal facility of the Laboratory of Animal Nutrition, Department of Animal Science, Faculty of Agriculture, Tokyo University of Agriculture. Rats were provided standard chow (CE-2; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and tap water ad libitum. Environmental conditions were maintained at 23 ± 2°C with 55 ± 10% relative humidity.

Ovariectomy

Six-week-old female rats (n=24) were anesthetized using a mixture of medetomidine (MED), midazolam (MID), and butorphanol (BUT) (0.5 ml/100 g body weight) and placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. The anesthetic mixture contained 0.375 mg MED, 2 mg MID, and 2.5 mg/kg BUT, diluted with distilled water to a final volume of 0.5 ml/100 g [18]. After shaving the flank, the skin was disinfected with 70% ethanol. A small incision was made, and the ovary was exteriorized. Ovarian blood vessels were ligated, and the ovary was removed. The procedure was repeated on the contralateral side. Sham-operated animals underwent the same procedure without ovary removal.

Orchiectomy

Six-week-old male rats (n=20) were anesthetized with mixture of MED, MID, and BUT (0.5 ml/100 g) and placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. The scrotal skin was incised after sterilization with 70%. The testis and epididymis were pulled out together and the vas deferens and blood vessels were ligated. The testis and epididymis were removed by cutting at the distal side of ligation. For sham animals, the same procedure was repeated without the removal of testis.

Testosterone treatment

A commercial testosterone ointment (Prisma hormone; Harasawa Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) containing 10 mg/g testosterone was used. A total of 0.1 g ointment was applied to the shaved dorsal cervical region three times per week from 8 to 35 weeks of age.

Skin score observation

AD severity was evaluated using the TSLSS [16]. Lesions were graded as 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), or 3 (severe) for erythema, eczema, crust, edema, and erosion across six body regions: nose/upper lip, lower lip, neck, forelegs, hind legs, and dorsal region. The TSLSS represents the average of these individual scores.

Histology

For histological assessment, major organs including skin were harvested, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE).

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), 3-µm paraffin sections from 35-week-old rats were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100–70%). Sections were treated with antigen retrieval solution (Nichirei Biosciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and 3% H2O2 for 30 min, blocked with Block Ace (KAC Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) for 30 min, and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-AR antibody (ab133273, 1:500; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After rinsing, slides were incubated with a secondary antibody (Nichirei Biosciences Inc.,) for 30 min, followed by 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DOJINDO LABORATORIES, Kumamoto, Japan) and counterstaining with hematoxylin.

Sebaceous gland counts were obtained from four randomly selected fields per section. Epidermal thickness was measured at five locations per section. AR-positive cells were quantified from eight fields per section.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the FastGene RNA Premium Kit (NIPPON Genetics Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). cDNA synthesis was performed using FastGene Scriptase II (NIPPON Genetics Co., Ltd.). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using the Thermal Cycler Dice Real-Time System (Takara Bio, Inc., Shiga, Japan) with the KAPA SYBR Fast qPCR Kit (Roche Diagnostics K.K., Tokyo, Japan). Primer sequences for androgen receptor (Ar), interleukin 1b (Il1b), Il4, Il5, Il6, Il10, Il13, Il17a, Il22, Il23a, and Flg genes are listed in the Supplementary Table 1.

The number of target molecules in each sample was determined by an absolute quantification method. Reference standards containing 101–108 molecules were prepared by serial dilution of synthetic double-stranded DNA (gBlocks; Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) and used to generate standard curves. Target molecule numbers were normalized to those of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) as an internal control.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using Welch’s one-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni and Games-Howell tests for post hoc comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed with EZR [19]. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ovariectomy did not influence AD in female KFRS4 rats

To determine whether the absence of ovarian hormones affects dermatitis development, we performed ovariectomy in female KFRS4 rats. Dermatitis first appeared as erythema on the lips or forelegs at 14 weeks of age in ovariectomized rats, whereas onset occurred at 18 weeks of age in sham-operated controls. As the disease progressed, both groups developed eczema and crust formation, followed by severe edema and erosion, particularly in the skin of the head and neck.

Although the sham-operated controls exhibited consistently elevated TSLSS values throughout the experimental period, the ovariectomized rats showed a different pattern, namely a biphasic curve: TSLSS values declined from 26 weeks of age and increased again from 28 weeks of age. This pattern may be explained by the humane euthanasia protocol, in which rats with severe dermatitis (TSLSS exceeding 20) were euthanized. Following euthanasia, the TSLSS decreased because only rats with lower dermatitis scores remained. Subsequently, the TSLSS increased again as dermatitis in the remaining animals progressively worsened.

Orchiectomy worsened AD in male KFRS4 rats

To determine whether removal of the testes affects dermatitis development, we performed orchiectomy in male KFRS4 rats. Sham-operated males, which retained their testes, developed only mild dermatitis characterized by erythema confined to the lips. In contrast, orchiectomized males exhibited markedly more severe dermatitis, presenting with crust formation and erosion on the neck and dorsal trunk.

The TSLSSs of orchiectomized rats were significantly higher than those of sham-operated controls at 22, 26, 30, 32, 34, and 35 weeks of age (Fig. 1B). These findings demonstrate that orchiectomy exacerbates dermatitis in male KFRS4 rats and suggest that the testes—or testis-derived sex hormones—normally exert a suppressive effect on dermatitis development in this strain.

Testosterone ameliorated AD in both male and female KFRS4 rats

To evaluate the role of testosterone—a hormone predominantly secreted by the testis—in suppressing dermatitis, we applied testosterone ointment percutaneously to KFRS4 rats. To eliminate the influence of endogenous gonadal hormones, ovariectomized females and orchiectomized males were used.

In ovariectomized females treated with testosterone, dermatitis developed only after 26 weeks of age. Although lesions gradually worsened with age, they remained largely restricted to the lips and forelimbs, and no lesions were observed on the dorsal neck, where testosterone was applied. Consequently, the TSLSSs of testosterone-treated ovariectomized rats were significantly lower than those of untreated controls at 24 and 35 weeks of age (Fig. 2AFig. 2.Topical administration of testosterone ointment suppresses the development of dermatitis in KFRS4 rats. B, TSLSSs observed in the testosterone-treated orchiectomized male KFRS4 rats, which were significantly lower than those in the non-treated after 22 weeks of age. *; P<0.05.). Similarly, testosterone-treated orchiectomized males showed significantly reduced TSLSSs compared with untreated orchiectomized controls after 22 weeks of age (Fig. 2B). As seen in females, no dermatitis was detected on the testosterone-applied dorsal neck region. These findings indicate that topically administered testosterone effectively ameliorates dermatitis in gonadectomized KFRS4 rats, suggesting a direct protective role of testosterone in modulating AD-like skin lesions.

Cytokine gene expressions in the skin

Dermatitis in KFRS4 rats is characterized by elevated plasma IgE levels and increased expression of Th2 and Th17 cytokines in skin-draining lymph nodes [16]. To assess whether testosterone attenuates dermatitis through modulation of Th2 and Th17 cytokines, we evaluated the expression of Il4, Il5, Il6, Il13, Il1b, Il17a, Il22a, and Il23a in skin samples.

The expression levels of Th2 cytokines, such as Il4 and Il5, were significantly higher in dermatitis-affected skin in females and males, respectively. In contrast, the expression of these cytokines was significantly decreased following testosterone treatment. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that ovariectomy did not affect dermatitis development in female KFRS4 rats, whereas orchiectomy markedly exacerbated dermatitis in males. These findings suggest that testis-derived factors—most likely testosterone—play a critical role in suppressing dermatitis in this strain. Consistent with this notion, topical testosterone treatment significantly ameliorated dermatitis in both ovariectomized females and orchiectomized males. This improvement was accompanied by increased AR expression, epidermal thickening, and downregulation of Th2 and Th17 cytokine genes, suggesting that testosterone primarily modulates dermatitis through mechanisms related to skin barrier enhancement as well as immune suppression.

Histological examination revealed that testosterone treatment increased the number of sebaceous glands in unaffected skin, coinciding with enhanced AR expression (Figs. 3 and 5). In humans, disrupted sebaceous gland (SG) function is implicated in AD pathogenesis [24,25,26]. For examples, SG number and volume are reduced in the clinically unaffected skin of AD patients [27], and diminished sebum secretion correlates negatively with barrier integrity, hydration, and disease severity [28]. These observations support the concept that “oil-poor” skin predisposes individuals to AD. Although we did not directly quantify sebum production in testosterone-treated KFRS4 rats, the observed increase in SGs likely contributes to the amelioration of dermatitis through increased sebum output.

Testosterone treatment also resulted in marked epidermal thickening, accompanied by robust induction of AR-positive cells within the basal, spinous, and granular layers (Figs. 3 and 5). Because keratinocytes constitute the majority of epidermal cells and express high levels of AR, they are the most plausible mediators of this effect. Testosterone is known to stimulate keratinocyte proliferation during wound healing and epidermal barrier formation [29]. Keratinocytes form the primary physical barrier through stratum corneum regeneration [30], establishment of tight junctions [31], and production of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that orchestrate immune responses [32]. The integrity of the epidermal barrier is essential for preventing allergen penetration [33, 34], and barrier dysfunction is a well-established hallmark of AD. Furthermore, the expression levels of Flg were significantly increased in the testosterone-treated skin regions of both male and female KFRS4 rats (Figs. 3I and J), suggesting that skin barrier function is improved by testosterone treatment.

Thus, testosterone-induced keratinocyte proliferation and upregulation of Flg expression likely strengthen the physical barrier of KFRS4 skin, contributing substantially to dermatitis suppression.

The epidermis and dermis also constitute an immunological barrier that includes dendritic cells, mast cells, and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs). These cells respond to environmental stimuli by secreting cytokines that activate effector pathways. Harmless agents such as pollen, food antigens, or drugs can sometimes elicit inappropriate adaptive immune responses, leading to allergic diseases. Among cytokines, IL-4 plays a central role in B-cell activation, IgE class switching, and Th2 differentiation. Th2-dominant immunity is a major driver of allergic disorders including seasonal rhinoconjunctivitis, asthma, food allergy, and AD. Il5, secreted from Th2 cells, induces proliferation of differentiation of eosinophils. In addition, IL-17A, which is associated with Th17 cells, plays a pivotal role in the development of inflammatory symptoms in allergic diseases by recruiting neutrophils and eosinophils [35]. We previously reported upregulation of Il4 in skin-draining lymph nodes of affected female KFRS4 rats [16].

In the present study, Il4 expression increased in dermatitis-affected skin and decreased following testosterone treatment in females. Moreover, transcriptions of Il5 and Il17a increased in dermatitis-affected skin and decreased following testosterone treatment in males. These findings indicate that the testosterone treatment suppressed the type 2 and Th17 immune responses in the dermatitis of KFRS4 rats and suggest that the type 2 and Th17 immunities are likely involved in the pathogenesis of AD in this model.

Together, testosterone’s protective effect observed in our gonadectomized model are thought to result from two primary mechanisms: suppression of type 2 and Th17 immunities and enhancement of the epidermal barrier function.

Our findings also have potential therapeutic implications. Testosterone, widely used to treat hypogonadism, could theoretically be repurposed for AD management. However, testosterone therapy is associated with adverse effects such as skin redness and acne [36], as well as systemic hormonal effects including mood changes, breast tenderness, and prostate enlargement [37]. Future studies are needed to identify treatment regimens that retain the dermatological benefits of testosterone while minimizing undesirable side effects. Nonetheless, the present study suggests that testosterone—or downstream pathways activated by androgen signaling—may serve as promising targets for developing novel AD therapies.

In summary, while ovariectomy did not affect the severity of dermatitis in female KFRS4 rats, orchiectomy exacerbated dermatitis in males, suggesting that testis-derived factors are involved in suppressing the disease. In both sexes following gonadectomy, testosterone administration alleviated dermatitis, accompanied by increased expression of AR and Flg, marked epidermal thickening, and decreased expression of Th2 and Th17 cytokine genes. Taken together, these findings suggest that testosterone plays a crucial role in alleviating dermatitis in KFRS4 rats, likely through the suppression of type 2 and Th17 immune responses and the enhancement of skin barrier function.
