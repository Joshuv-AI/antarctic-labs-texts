From classroom to clinic: Evaluating the educational utility of two Thiel embalming methods
Authors: Veronica Antipova, Martin Siwetz, Simone Manhal, Niels Hammer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver three decades since its introduction, the Thiel embalming method remains a widely used and praised technique in anatomical education and clinical training. This study compared for the first time the Thiel original protocol (TO) and a Modified version (TM) across user groups—undergraduate medical students, student demonstrators in anatomy, postgraduate trainees, and expert anatomists—to evaluate tissue quality and educational suitability. A total of 520 participants assessed both methods based on tissue preservation, color, pliability, and achievement of learning objectives. Undergraduate medical students and demonstrators rated TO significantly higher than TM in all domains (p < 0.01), highlighting its lifelike appearance, clear anatomical definition, and superior dissection properties. Anatomists supported these findings, citing better tissue realism and structural integrity in TO specimens. Postgraduate users, mainly orthopedic surgeons, rated both methods similarly for musculoskeletal and nervous tissues, but preferred TO for the clarity of visceral organs (p < 0.01). While TM offered improvements for musculoskeletal training, users reported challenges including overly soft viscera, liquefied fat, and reduced structural clarity, making it less suitable for beginners. Both TO and TM were rated superior to formaldehyde‐ or ethanol‐based techniques in terms of educational utility and realism. Qualitative feedback confirmed TO as better suited for undergraduate anatomy education, particularly for dissection. TM, though valuable in specialized postgraduate training, was seen as less appropriate for novice learners. The findings highlight the need to align embalming protocols with educational goals and experience levels, with TO remaining the preferred method for foundational anatomical training.Keywords: anatomists, clinical course, dissection, embalming, student demonstrators, Thiel embalming, undergraduate medical students

INTRODUCTION

More than 30 years have passed since Walter Thiel from Graz, Austria, first described his embalming method. 1 Thiel's method was introduced with the claim that it renders human postmortem tissue lifelike in appearance. 1 , 2 Thiel embalming is based on embalming experiments involving almost 1000 donors over a course of 30 years. 3 The embalming process consists of an initial perfusion followed by at least two months of immersion in embalming fluid. Harmful components such as formaldehyde, p‐chlorocresol, and morpholine are only used in very low concentrations, making the handling of embalmed bodies presumably safe. 4 Over the years, additional advantages have been recognized, including tissue flexibility, enhanced joint mobility, and less odor when compared to formaldehyde embalming. 3 , 5 The benefits have contributed to the growing popularity of the Thiel method, particularly as a substitute for fresh frozen tissues in medical training. 3

The Thiel technique has been adopted worldwide across Europe, Asia, North America, and Oceania and is widely used in medical education and research. 3 , 6 , 7 In the central European DACH countries, six anatomy institutions are known to use Thiel embalming exclusively for postgraduate training, 8 , 9 except for dental medical training in Graz 10 and at Dundee (personal reference). The use of Thiel‐embalmed specimens has been reported in various clinical training settings including regional anesthesia, interventional radiology, surgery, as well as dental and maxillofacial surgery. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

Thiel embalming is a complex procedure and has a shortened time of useful life of the specimens when compared to other embalming methods. It involves various chemicals and several demanding steps, requiring a highly skilled and experienced team as well as explosion‐proof facilities. 1 , 2 , 4 , 7 , 18 , 19

While the Thiel method has been widely adopted for medical education and research, 20 it is less well known to most users that two types of Thiel embalming exist: the Thiel original embalming (TO, 1992) and a Thiel modified embalming protocol (TM, 2002). The Modified method differs primarily by the addition of ethanol, 3 , 19 replacing isopropanol. Literature suggests that larger centers providing postmortem tissue for research predominantly use the 2002 ethanol‐enhanced protocol. 3 The later protocol 19 aimed at enhancing the embalmings' suitability for the musculoskeletal system, especially for postgraduate trauma and orthopedic training, while the fixation of visceral organs was less prioritized.

To date, no studies have compared the performance of the two Thiel embalming protocols for both the undergraduate as well as postgraduate education environment. This study aimed to substantiate both strengths and weaknesses of the Thiel protocols across different user groups from a broad background, including undergraduate medical students and student demonstrators in anatomy, experienced anatomists, and postgraduate users. Additionally, tissue characteristics at both gross anatomical and histological levels were assessed. It was hypothesized that the two Thiel embalming protocols offer different benefits depending on the user group.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Between 2022 and 2025, second‐year undergraduate medical students of the Medical University of Graz actively enrolled in an elective dissection course for advanced anatomy, student demonstrators in anatomy, experienced anatomists from various parts of central Europe and orthopedic surgeons participating in postgraduate training courses in Graz from 2023 to 2024 were included in the given study. All participants gave their written and informed consent prior to their voluntary participation in the respective evaluations. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethical committee of the Medical University of Graz, Austria (32‐568 ex 19/20 and 36‐048 ex 23/24).

Demographic information

A total of 520 participants were surveyed regarding their opinions on the TO and the TM protocols. 157 undergraduate medical students, 102 student demonstrators and 58 anatomists completed a questionnaire assessing the suitability of tissues embalmed according to both TO and TM. From 203 orthopedic surgeons, 69 completed the questionnaire for TO and 134 for TM (Table 1).

Embalming procedure used in the study

For TO, the protocol published by Thiel, 1 and for the TM embalming, the protocol developed by Friedrich Anderhuber and published by Walter Thiel 19 were utilized. All specimens used for the given study were bequeathed to the Division of Macroscopic and Clinical Anatomy, Medical University of Graz, Austria as part of an ongoing body donation program and in accordance with the Austrian legislation concerning body donations. While alive, all body donors had given their informed consent for the donation of their postmortem tissues for research and teaching purposes (https://anatomie.medunigraz.at/en/bodydonation), fulfilling the criteria of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Histology

The integrity of skeletal muscle, peripheral nerve, duodenum, liver, and lung tissue was examined using hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining. Tissue samples from both TO and TM protocols were obtained from a 76‐year‐old female and an 85‐year‐old male donor. After removing the tissues, they were fixed in phosphate‐buffered formaldehyde (4 vol%, pH = 7.0), dehydrated, embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned at 5 and 20 μm, and finally stained using H&E according to standard protocol. 21

Surveys

The selection of the items for the surveys was conducted by experienced anatomists and one psychologist. For this purpose, a 5‐point Likert scale was adopted (1 = most suitable/strongly agree, 5 = most unsuitable/strongly disagree). 22 All participants were surveyed on the suitability of TO and TM embalming concerning tissue preservation, colorfastness, tissue pliability, and perceptions on tissue suitability to achieve the anatomy learning objectives. The survey items were piloted both internally within the study team, including experienced anatomists and a psychologist, and in two prior studies 10 , 23 to ensure clarity, relevance, and feasibility.

The cohorts of students, student demonstrators, and anatomists had access to tissues embalmed using both TO and TM. Therefore, each of these participants was surveyed on tissues embalmed with the two protocols. The questionnaire for students, student demonstrators and anatomists was divided in 3 sections: (1) general information, including experience in macroscopic teaching and research and body donation; (2) macroscopic assessment including tissue preservation, colorfastness, pliability, and suitability for students' dissection courses; and (3) other comments (Data S2 and S3).

For postgraduate participants, either TO‐ or TM‐embalmed tissues formed part of their study. Consequently, each participant was asked on their assessment on either TO or TM, dependent on what they had used. Postgraduate participants completed a different survey, adapted for their use cases (Data S1). The questionnaire for postgraduate users was divided into 3 sections: (1) general information, including the experience in using human tissue for undergraduate and postgraduate training; (2) macroscopic assessment based on embalming, including assessment of the tissue preservation, colorfastness, pliability, and suitability for the students' dissection courses in relation to the musculoskeletal system, visceral organs, and nervous system; and (3) additional macroscopic assessment for the self‐prepared regions (Data S1).

Statistical evaluation

Data on the examination scores and personal data were analyzed using Prism v10 (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, MA, USA) and Microsoft Excel version 16.95.1 (Microsoft Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normal distribution was assessed using the D'Agostino and Pearson test. A Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post‐hoc comparisons was used for between‐group comparisons comparing TO to the TM method. To control for type I error inflation across multiple comparisons, p values from Dunn's tests were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Adjusted p values are reported in the results section. Effect sizes are reported as Cliff's delta (δ) with 95%‐confidence intervals (CI) estimated via percentile bootstrap resampling, computed as: δ = (U 1–U 2)/(n 1 × n 2). Here, U represents the Mann–Whitney U statistic. Effect sizes were interpreted as: trivial (<0.10), small (0.10–0.30), medium (0.30–0.50), or large (>0.50). Internal consistency reliability of all assessment instruments was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha coefficient, with values ≥0.70 considered acceptable, ≥0.80 good, and ≥0.90 excellent.

RESULTS

Cronbach's α analyses demonstrated excellent internal consistency across all assessment instruments, with the detailed 24‐item regional assessment (α = 0.94) and the 26‐item physician objectives and suitability evaluation (α = 0.93) showing the highest reliability, while the aggregate measures across different expertise levels ranged from acceptable to good (α = 0.71–0.85).

Thiel original embalming was rated consistently higher than Thiel modified across various applications in anatomy, but not for postgraduate users

Assessments of undergraduate medical students participating in an advanced placement rated TO higher when compared to TM regarding tissue preservation (1.50 ± 0.66 vs. 2.14 ± 0.86, δ = −0.21, CI [−0.35, 0.06]), pliability (1.69 ± 0.89 vs. 2.13 ± 1.08, δ = −0.17, CI [−0.31, −0.02]), and suitability to achieve their learning objectives (1.79 ± 0.81 vs. 2.61 ± 1.11, δ = −0.23, CI [−0.37, −0.08]) (all p ≤ 0.01) (Figure 1). Assessment of color was not significantly different (1.85 ± 0.85 vs. 2.13 ± 0.95, δ = −0.21, CI [−0.33, −0.04]).

This cohort rated both TO and TM good to excellent in all domains (preservation 1.54 ± 0.62 vs. 1.68 ± 0.73, δ = −0.11, CI [−0.24, 0.02]; color 1.57 ± 0.64 vs. 1.7 ± 0.74, δ = −0.09, CI [−0.22, 0.04]; pliability 1.59 ± 0.69 vs. 1.71 ± 0.72, δ = −0.08, CI [−0.21, 0.05]; learning objectives 1.38 ± 0.53 vs. 1.50 ± 0.68, δ = −0.10, CI [−0.23, 0.03]) (all p > 0.05) (Figure 1).

Among postgraduate users, quantitative ratings show comparable results but qualitative feedback consistently favors Thiel original

The TO method was consistently praised across all four user groups for its superior dissection quality, realistic anatomical structures, and excellent tissue preservation. Users highlighted its suitability for teaching, especially for novices, due to clearly distinguishable anatomical features, realistic haptic feedback, and preservation of organ shape and color. It was rated particularly effective for muscle, nerve, and vascular structures and was frequently regarded qualitatively as the most appropriate embalming method when compared to the TM or other embalming techniques, making the TO method a preferred choice for educational and surgical training purposes.

The TM embalming received mixed qualitative feedback. While it was occasionally recognized as superior in terms of the preservation quality of joints and muscles in advanced or specialist training, it was generally viewed as less suitable for undergraduate education. Common criticisms regarding TM included overly soft organs, liquefied and disruptive adipose connective tissues, unrealistic haptic feedback, and inferior color contrast when compared to TO. All this complicated dissection and reduced morphological clarity.

Above all, the morphology of the viscera was critically assessed in TM. Viscera were often reported as soft and dark, to collapse or blend together, making it difficult for users to identify key features. Overall, while TM may offer some value in niche applications, it was judged as less effective and less suitable for beginners compared with the Original method. Histological limitations of Thiel‐embalmed tissues

Both TO and TM embalming methods rendered tissues unsuitable for detailed histology. Fixatives caused extensive washout, leading to blurry structural borders.

At low magnification, overall tissue architecture was still distinguishable, allowing basic identification (Figure 9A–J). However, higher magnification revealed severe cellular degradation—loss of nuclei and membranes—though some cytoplasmic eosinophilia remained (e.g., muscle fibers, peripheral nerves, duodenal epithelium, liver cytoplasm, and erythrocytes) (Figure 9A–J). Despite cellular damage, collagen fibers were well‐preserved, maintaining the structural framework of all tissues examined.

DISCUSSION

On Thiel embalming method and its variants

The Thiel embalming method has been widely used for medical education and research over the past 30 years. 8 , 9 Two variants exist: TO (Thiel [1]) and TM (Thiel [19]). Despite their widespread application, direct comparisons between these two protocols are still lacking. This study compared both TO and TM across multiple user groups—advanced medical students, student demonstrators in anatomy, experienced anatomists, and postgraduate users—to evaluate their suitability for anatomical education and surgical training.

Thiel original was generally rated better than Thiel modified by most user groups

Medical students, student demonstrators, anatomists, and postgraduate users rated TO higher than TM in terms of tissue preservation, pliability, and overall suitability for learning—except for color features. TO specimens were considered easier to dissect and preserved internal organs more effectively, maintaining shape and structural integrity (Table 2). While TM tissues were rated more pliable, TO was considered to offer a better overall balance of lifelike appearance, haptics, and dissection quality.

TO specimens to some extent exhibited effects similar to those of ethanol‐glycerin embalming, including moderate organ induration and bleaching, which facilitated structure identification. This has in particular been the case in the liver, spleen, kidneys, neuromuscular tissue, and vessels. 2 , 24 , 25 These findings align with Balta et al., 26 who found that students had an easier time identifying thoracic, abdominal, and pelvic structures in firmer, formaldehyde‐embalmed tissues compared with TM. However, TM was noted to outperform ethanol‐glycerin embalming in pliability. 5 , 7 , 27 , 28

Postgraduate users rated Thiel original and modified equally well

Only postgraduate users rated TO and TM equally in terms of color, pliability, and overall tissue quality. Their assessments emphasized that both methods met the standards required for surgical simulation and postgraduate anatomical training. 2 , 3 , 12 , 29 , 30 , 31 Since postgraduate education focuses on replicating lifelike anatomical conditions for surgical practice, both TO and TM were considered suitable. Conversely, bleached and hardened tissues—undesirable in surgery—can benefit students by making anatomical structures more easily identifiable. Accordingly, trauma and orthopedic surgeons regarded both TO and TM as effective embalming methods for educational purposes. Notably, TO was initially developed to enhance musculoskeletal dissection for postgraduate training. 3 , 19

Key differences between Thiel original and modified embalming are in the viscera

Region‐specific assessment by postgraduate users found no difference between TO and TM in the preservation of musculoskeletal tissues and nerves. However, TM was clearly inferior in preserving visceral organs. TM‐embalmed viscera tended to collapse, making structure identification more difficult. This may explain why orthopedic and trauma surgeons, whose focus is musculoskeletal anatomy, rated TM as more suitable than TO, whereas other groups with broader anatomical interests (e.g., anatomists and students) found TO superior. Eisma et al. 32 similarly noted that TM‐embalmed organs were more challenging to dissect, although some aspects, such as skin and superficial fascia, were easier to handle due to the preservation of tissue layers.

Postgraduate users rated Thiel original and modified equally good

Postgraduate users specializing in orthopedics and traumatology rated both TO and TM equally effective for achieving training objectives. No difference was noted in tissue pliability nor preservation of musculoskeletal structures and nerves. Both embalming types met expectations for visual and tactile properties necessary in clinical workshops and surgical simulation, especially in the context of orthopedic surgery. 24 , 25 , 33 , 34 The focus of postgraduate training appears to shift attention away from minor embalming drawbacks and toward functional training goals, including the haptic feedback and pliability, which both TO and TM support equally well. 2 , 3 , 29

Most groups rated color and colorfastness better in Thiel original embalming

Although Thiel embalming was originally described as a “color preservation method”, 1 the visual benefits degrade over time. Once exposed to air, both TO and TM tissues turn brown and take on a Bakelite‐like appearance, impairing structure identification during prolonged dissection. 2 , 10 This decline in color quality was consistently noted by students, demonstrators, and anatomists. However, postgraduate users, who typically engage with embalmed tissues for shorter durations, rated the color quality of TO and TM more favorably. Based on our own observations, TO preserves colorfastness longer than TM.

Group‐specific advantages and disadvantages of Thiel embalming compared with other embalming procedures

Postgraduate users consistently rated both TO and TM as superior when compared to fresh, ethanol‐, or formaldehyde‐embalmed tissues. Educational needs differ by learner group: Undergraduate students often benefit from firmer, more bleached tissues for better visual discrimination, while postgraduate training benefits from lifelike pliability and appearance. 24 , 25 , 33 , 34 The softer viscera resulting from TM are less useful in undergraduate settings due to organ collapse and reduced structure visibility. 2 , 10 Therefore, advantages reported for TM in surgical settings do not translate well to novice‐level education.

Both Thiel original‐ and Thiel modified‐embalmed specimens are unsuitable for histological examinations

We re‐evaluated the suitability of both embalming protocols for histology here; as most studies in the literature have exclusively examined and evaluated the 2002 protocol only; TM. 2 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Histological analysis aligned with earlier reports on these embalming methods in showing that both TO‐ and TM‐embalmed tissues were poorly preserved at a microscopic level, including hydropic swelling, indistinct cell borders, absent nuclei, and epithelial and endothelial degradation, while extracellular structures, such as liver interlobular fibers and the perineurium, remained largely intact. Thus, both protocols are equally unsuitable for histological examination.

Future directions

TO was consistently rated more favorably than TM by undergraduate students, student demonstrators, and anatomists. However, both embalming methods were highly valued for musculoskeletal training in postgraduate orthopedic and trauma surgery education. Future studies should evaluate TO and TM across a wider range of postgraduate specialties, including abdominal surgery, ENT, plastic surgery, and maxillofacial surgery. 20 , 40 , 41 Furthermore, since Thiel embalming is used for undergraduate dental education in Graz, it would be valuable to assess which method better supports learning outcomes in dental anatomy.

Limitations of the study

This study had several limitations. The sample size for undergraduate students, student demonstrators, and anatomists was limited. In addition, only postgraduate users from orthopedic and trauma surgery specialties were included and only evaluated the used protocol, limiting within‐subject comparison for this group. Broader inclusion across medical and surgical specialties would improve generalizability. While our instruments demonstrated good to excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.71–0.94), we did not assess other forms of validity such as content, construct, or criterion validity, which should be addressed in future research to further strengthen the psychometric properties of these assessment tools.

CONCLUSIONS

TO offers superior dissection quality, anatomical realism, and tissue preservation, making it the preferred choice for undergraduate anatomy education and for anatomists teaching medical students. By contrast, TO and TM performed equally well in postgraduate settings, particularly in orthopedic and trauma surgery, where lifelike musculoskeletal properties are prioritized. Neither embalming method is suitable for histological studies. Selection of an embalming protocol should be guided by the educational objectives and the anatomical regions of interest for the learner group.
