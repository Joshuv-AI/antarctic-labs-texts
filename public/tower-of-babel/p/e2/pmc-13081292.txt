Perinatal outcomes of pregnant adolescents in rural Japan: a retrospective cohort study at a comprehensive perinatal center
Authors: Masamitsu Kurakazu, Tomohito Kobiyama, Eriko Iito, Naho Tokunaga, Koki Yagi, Mao Sekimata, Ryota Tsuge, Naoki Abe, Sachino Kira, Sotaro Hayashi, Chiho Kadota, Lifa Lee, Satoshi Nishiyama, Hiroshi Tsujioka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, even within developed nations, specific regions exhibit disproportionately high rates of adolescent pregnancy that require comprehensive perinatal medical care, including social interventions. We aimed to compare perinatal outcomes between pregnant adolescents and pregnant adults in rural Japan.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of pregnant women between 2014 and 2025, categorizing participants into two groups based on maternal age: pregnant adolescents (< 20 years) and adults (20–34 years). Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were calculated using logistic regression analysis.ResultsOf the 3157 singleton pregnant women identified, 142 (4.5%) were adolescents and 3015 (95.5%) were adults. Adolescent pregnancy was significantly associated with nulliparity (p < .001), shorter stature (p < .001), lower pre-pregnancy body weight (p < .001), lower body weight at delivery (p < .001), and lower pre-pregnancy body mass index (p = .0065). Adolescents were also more likely to smoke during pregnancy (p = .041), and to undergo pregnancy termination before 22 weeks of gestation (p < .001). Multivariable analysis revealed that the adolescent group had significantly higher risks for fetal growth restriction (aOR, 1.82; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.00–3.30), preterm delivery (aOR, 1.86; 95% CI, 1.02–3.37), low birth weight (aOR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.04–2.19), low Apgar score at 5 min (aOR, 4.21; 95% CI, 2.09–8.48), and admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (aOR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.02–2.08). Conversely, the incidences of gestational diabetes mellitus (aOR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.30–0.86), and cesarean delivery (aOR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.33–0.69) were significantly lower in the adolescent group than those in the adult group.ConclusionWe found that adolescent pregnancy in rural Japan was associated with important adverse perinatal outcomes, including preterm delivery, low birth weight, low Apgar scores at 5 min, and neonatal intensive care unit admission. The present findings underscore the importance of providing targeted perinatal support and specialized obstetric care for pregnant adolescents to mitigate these risks.Keywords: Fetal growth restriction, Gestational diabetes mellitus, Low birth weight, Multivariable analysis, Pregnant adolescents, Preterm birth

Background

Adolescent pregnancy has been reported to be closely linked to socioeconomic challenges, including financial hardship and inadequate familial support [1, 2]. These pregnancies are frequently unintended and often complicated by psychological stressors, such as poor mental health [3–5], and environmental factors including smoking, alcohol use, and strained interpersonal relationships [6]. Pregnant adolescents are not only physically and psychologically immature but they also tend to lack social support, making their perinatal care high-risk.

In Japan, as in other developed Western countries, the birth rate has been in persistent decline, and correspondingly, the incidence of adolescent pregnancy has been exhibiting a long-term downward trend [7–10]. However, some regions have disproportionately high rates of adolescent pregnancy in which comprehensive perinatal medical care is needed, including social interventions [11]. Domestic research on perinatal outcomes among adolescents in Japan remains limited [12, 13], despite adolescent pregnancy being a serious perinatal challenge requiring continued clinical investigation.

In this study, we aimed to investigate perinatal outcomes among pregnant adolescents using data from a comprehensive perinatal center. A distinctive feature of this research is the inclusion of maternal and partner smoking as analyzed risk factors, in addition to established clinical variables. By comparing these outcomes with those of pregnant adults, we sought to clarify the specific perinatal risks associated with adolescent pregnancy.

Methods

We conducted this retrospective observational cohort study at Iizuka Hospital, a comprehensive perinatal maternal and child center in Iizuka City, Fukuoka, Japan. Our hospital serves as the sole comprehensive center in the Chikuho region of Fukuoka Prefecture and it is the only comprehensive perinatal maternal and child medical center. The institutional review board of our institution approved this study (Ref: 17070, approved June 1, 2023).

We reviewed the records of all pregnant women who received perinatal care at our hospital between April 2014 and March 2025. Inclusion criteria were singleton pregnancies in women under 35 years of age. We excluded multiple pregnancies, women aged 35 years or older at the time of delivery, and women who declined to participate in this study.

The following clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical records: maternal age, nulliparity, height, maternal body weight pre-pregnancy and at delivery, and body mass index (BMI) pre-pregnancy and at delivery. Maternal weight gain during pregnancy was also calculated. Participant characteristics included smoking status during pregnancy and partner smoking status, both defined as ≥ 1 cigarette per day. Additionally, we recorded cases of pregnancy termination before 22 weeks of gestation.

Assessed pregnancy complications included the incidence of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (including gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, and eclampsia [14]), placental abruption, abnormal placental position, and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM; including overt diabetes mellitus [14]). Fetal growth restriction (FGR) was defined as an established fetal weight less than − 1.5 standard deviations, based on the Japanese Society of Ultrasound in Medicine formula [15]. Preterm delivery was defined as delivery before 34 weeks of gestation; this threshold is considered clinically significant regarding neonatal morbidity and mortality, as it represents the standard window for the administration of antenatal corticosteroids. Delivery-related outcomes included the use of oxytocin augmentation, cesarean delivery rates, and blood transfusion therapy. For vaginal delivery, we specifically analyzed post-partum hemorrhage (defined as blood loss volume more than 500 mL) and the surgical evacuation of vaginal and/or vulvar hematomas. Neonatal outcomes included the incidence of low birth weight (LBW; < 2500 g), small for gestational age, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, and admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Low Apgar score was defined as < 7 at 1 or 5 min, and low umbilical arterial blood gas pH was defined as < 7.20. Early neonatal death was defined as death within 7 days of birth.

Participants were stratified into two groups based on maternal age: the adolescent group (aged < 20 years) and the adult group (aged 20–34 years). Background characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequency and percentages for categorical variables. We compared pregnancy, delivery and neonatal outcomes between the two groups. To assess the effect of maternal age on outcomes, we excluded cases of pregnancy termination before 22 weeks of gestation and used logistic regression models to control for potential confounders. The multivariable model included height, pre-pregnancy body weight, body weight at delivery, and smoking status (both maternal and partner) during pregnancy. For outcomes with a small number of events (fewer than 10 events per group), we applied Firth’s penalized likelihood method to the logistic regression analysis. This approach was employed to mitigate potential bias and ensure stable estimates, as conventional maximum likelihood estimation can be unreliable in sparse data settings. Crude odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical analyses were conducted using JMP software, version 17.0.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Values p < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The initial study population included 3359 pregnant women, comprising 161 adolescents (aged < 20 years) and 3198 adults (aged 20–34 years). We excluded 19 adolescents (11.8%) and 183 adults (5.7%) who underwent pregnancy termination before 22 weeks of gestation. The final analysis included 3157 singleton pregnant women: 142 adolescents (4.5%) and 3015 adults (95.5%).

Maternal characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Significant differences were observed between the adolescent and adult groups for the incidence of nulliparity (p < .001), maternal height (p < .001), pre-pregnancy body weight (p < .001), body weight at delivery (p < .001), pre-pregnancy BMI (p = .0065), maternal smoking status (p = .041), and the rate of pregnancy termination before 22 weeks of gestation (p < .001). No significant differences were found between the two groups regarding BMI at delivery (p = .0833), body weight gain during pregnancy (p = .621), or partner’s smoking status (p = .441).

Regarding delivery outcomes, the incidence of cesarean delivery (aOR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.33–0.69) was significantly lower in the adolescent group than that in the adult group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups for the incidence of oxytocin augmentation, post-partum hemorrhage during vaginal delivery, surgical evacuation of vaginal or vulvar hematomas, or blood transfusion therapy.

Regarding neonatal outcomes, the incidences of LBW (aOR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.04–2.19), low Apgar scores at 5 min (aOR, 4.21; 95% CI, 2.09–8.48), and NICU admission (aOR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.02–2.08) were significantly higher in the adolescent group than those in the adult group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in the incidence of small for gestational age, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, low Apgar score at 1 min, or low umbilical arterial blood gas pH. Notably, there were no cases of early neonatal death in the adolescent group.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study, involving over 3000 singleton pregnancies at a regional comprehensive perinatal center in Japan, we investigated the clinical characteristics and perinatal outcomes of pregnant adolescents in comparison with pregnant adults. A novel aspect of this study incorporated both maternal and partner smoking as analyzed risk factors, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of environmental influences on the perinatal outcome of pregnant adolescents. Our findings demonstrated that adolescent pregnancy was associated with a significantly decreased incidence of cesarean delivery and GDM. However, pregnancy in adolescence was also associated with a risk of adverse outcomes, including FGR, preterm birth before 34 weeks of gestation, LBW, low Apgar scores at 5 min, and NICU admission.

In the present study, pregnant adolescents had a lower incidence of cesarean delivery and GDM compared with pregnant adults. A comparison of maternal characteristics revealed that adolescents had significantly lower height and pre-pregnancy body weight, likely reflecting physical immaturity (Table 1). However, adolescents gained weight during pregnancy at a rate similar to that of adults, and their BMI at delivery did not differ significantly from that in adults, indicating that adolescents achieved a comparable body size by the time of delivery. Furthermore, the significantly higher incidence of LBW among adolescents likely contributed to the higher rate of successful vaginal delivery in this group. Previous research suggests that a higher incidence of LBW and a lower incidence of macrosomia may contribute to the relative ease of vaginal delivery in adolescents [16], which supports our findings.

Regarding the low rate of GDM, the incidence of this condition typically increases with age due to rising insulin resistance, obesity, and metabolic disorders [17]. Therefore, the inherent age-related risk is relatively low in adolescents, contributing to the lower incidence of GDM. A meta-analysis evaluating maternal age and GDM reported that pregnant adolescents have a lower incidence of the disease and that the risk increases with age, further validating our results [18].

In the current study, pregnant adolescent women had a higher incidence of FGR, preterm delivery before 34 weeks of gestation, LBW, low Apgar scores at 5 min, and NICU admission. The physical and reproductive immaturity observed in the adolescent group (Table 1) may result in insufficient physical preparation for fetal development and labor maintenance, potentially leading to increased rates of preterm birth, FGR, and LBW. Previous research has identified physical limitations in adolescents, such as short stature [12] and an underdeveloped pelvis [19], as factors contributing to these outcomes, in line with our findings. Furthermore, the significant increase in preterm birth and LBW among adolescents, coupled with low Apgar scores and more frequent NICU admission, may result from a combination of factors that worsen neonatal outcomes [20–25]. Given these risks, it is essential to develop a management system for adolescent deliveries that strengthens cooperation with neonatology departments and ensures readiness for neonatal resuscitation.

We accounted for both maternal smoking during pregnancy and secondhand exposure via partner smoking, using these data as adjustment factors in our multivariable logistic regression analysis. High rates of smoking remain a significant social issue among pregnant adolescents [13, 26]. Tobacco use during pregnancy is known to significantly increase the risk of various adverse outcomes for the mother, fetus, and neonate [27–30], with strong associations to LBW [31], preterm birth, respiratory diseases such as asthma, and sudden infant death syndrome. Furthermore, smoking may predispose the offspring to future obesity and long-term developmental or cognitive decline [26]. Research indicates that even light smoking (1–9 cigarettes per day) affects outcomes, demonstrating a clear dose–response relationship [32] that increase the risk of miscarriage, stillbirth, and neonatal mortality [28, 32]. By adjusting for both maternal and partner smoking, we demonstrated that adolescent age is an independent risk factor for adverse perinatal outcomes, which is a notable strength of this study.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the retrospective observational design means that not all confounding factors affecting outcomes could be eliminated; however, multivariable statistical methods were used to minimize their influence. Second, because this study relied on perinatal data from a single institution, the findings may be subject to selection bias and may not be fully generalizable to the broader population. Future research using large-scale national databases or multicenter designs is necessary to validate our findings. In particular, in a clinical research setting, a comparison between rural and urban adolescent pregnancies would clarify setting-specific social environments and be useful for the perinatal management strategies for adolescent pregnancies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that although pregnant adolescents have a lower rate of cesarean delivery and a lower incidence of GDM, they have significant risks for FGR, preterm birth before 34 weeks of gestation, LBW, low Apgar scores at 5 min, and NICU admission. These finding underscore the importance of providing targeted perinatal medical support to pregnant adolescents that addresses the specific risk factors identified in this population.

To improve outcomes, it is essential to establish an evidence-based support system based on the scientific findings revealed by this study. Strengthening cooperation between medical facilities and government agencies is necessary to provide comprehensive care. The present results can help to facilitate the development of a community-wide network that ensures appropriate medical and social support for pregnant adolescents to promote better health outcomes for both mothers and their infants.
