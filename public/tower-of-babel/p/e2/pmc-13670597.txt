Establishing a standardized endoscopic-assisted transoral approach for submandibular gland pleomorphic adenomas
Authors: Wei Wang, Jie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscervical excision is the conventional treatment for submandibular gland pleomorphic adenomas (SMG PA), but it may result in visible cervical scarring and marginal mandibular nerve injury. This study aimed to establish a standardized endoscopic-assisted transoral surgical protocol and evaluate its feasibility and early outcomes.MethodsThis study included 120 consecutive patients with cytologically diagnosed SMG PA who underwent standardized endoscopic-assisted transoral resection between February 2023 and May 2025. The primary outcome was procedural feasibility, defined as successful completion of gland and tumor resection through the transoral route without conversion to a transcervical approach. Introduction

Pleomorphic adenomas (PA) account for approximately 57% of tumors arising from the submandibular gland (SMG) [1]. Standard surgical management requires en bloc excision of both the tumor and the involved gland. The transcervical approach remains the conventional technique but is associated with visible cervical scarring and a reported 1%–7% risk of injury to the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve [2, 3]. These adverse outcomes may impose both functional and psychological burdens, particularly in younger patients with high aesthetic expectations.

To improve cosmetic outcomes, several alternative surgical approaches have been proposed, including postauricular, facelift, submental, transaxillary, and hairline incisions [4–7]. These approaches may make the scar less conspicuous; however, they do not eliminate external scarring and may still be associated with nerve-related complications.

The transoral approach, first described in 1960, offers the potential advantage of avoiding a visible cervical scar [8]. Nevertheless, limited illumination, restricted visualization, and unfamiliar surgical anatomy historically hindered its widespread adoption. Consequently, this approach has beenused mainly for inflammatory conditions, such as sialadenitis and sialolithiasis, with relatively limited experience reported for tumor resection [9–11].

Advances in endoscopic technology have improved illumination and magnified visualization, facilitating the identification of critical anatomical structures during transoral surgery [12, 13]. Although endoscopic techniques have been widely adopted in thyroid surgery, their application in submandibular gland tumor management remains exploratory [14, 15]. Therefore, this study aimed to establish a standardized endoscopic-assisted transoral technique for the resection of submandibular gland pleomorphic adenomas and to evaluate its feasibility, perioperative outcomes, complications, and short-term recurrence-free status.

Patients and methods

Patients

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology(IRB No. PKUSSIRB-2024106231) and retrospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500096609). All patients provided written informed consent(patients under 16 informed consent was obtained from their parents), and relevant documentation was properly archived.

Between February 2023 and May 2025, consecutive patients undergoing endoscopic-assisted transoral resection of the SM were prospectively enrolled. All patients completed a minimum postoperative follow-up of 6 months. Demographic and clinical data collected included age, sex, tumor laterality, and tumor size.

Patients were considered for the endoscopic-assisted transoral approach after comprehensive clinical and radiological assessment, including fine-needle aspiration cytology, contrast-enhanced computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, mouth opening, tumor mobility, oral floor exposure, and patient preference. Imaging findings favoring transoral surgery included a well-circumscribed lesion suggestive of pleomorphic adenoma, without infiltrative margins, invasion of the mandible, floor-of-mouth musculature, skin, or adjacent soft tissues, or suspicious cervical lymphadenopathy. The relationship of the tumor to the glandular hilum, submandibular duct, and facial vasculature was evaluated preoperatively. Transoral surgery was selected only when complete en bloc gland–tumor excision and secure vascular control were considered feasible through the oral cavity.

Patients were excluded if they had clinical or radiological findings suspicious for malignancy, recurrent tumors, prior cervical surgery or radiotherapy, tumor fixation, poor tumor mobility, or trismus. Tumors encasing the facial vessels or lesions in which the vascular hilum could not be safely accessed through the oral cavity were also considered unsuitable for this approach.

The primary endpoint was procedural feasibility, defined as successful completion of gland resection via the transoral route without conversion to a transcervical approach. Secondary outcomes included operative time, length of hospital stay, postoperative complications, and patient-reported cosmetic satisfaction. Cosmetic satisfaction was assessed at 3 months postoperatively using a 10-point Likert scale (0 = complete dissatisfaction; 10 = maximal satisfaction).The score was self-reported by the patient, no blinded evaluator assessment was performed. Functional satisfaction, including oral discomfort, taste disturbance, speech, swallowing, and salivary function, was not systematically assessed using validated instruments.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed according to a standardized stepwise protocol (Fig. 1).

Standardized workflow of endoscopic-assisted transoral submandibular gland resection

Step 1: anesthesia and preparation

General anesthesia was administered via transnasal endotracheal intubation. The oral cavity was prepared with chlorhexidine followed by saline irrigation. Surgical instruments included a stereoscopic mouth gag, tongue retractor, mylohyoid retractor, vascular clip applier, and a 4-mm 30° rigid endoscope (Fig. 2).

Instruments for transoral resection of the submandibular gland

Step 2: incision design

After local infiltration with diluted epinephrine (1:100,000), a mucosal incision was made along the floor of the mouth, extending from the mesial second premolar to the distal second molar (Fig. 3A), however, it could be adjusted when necessary to permit safe mobilization and delivery of the intact gland–tumor specimen. A 1-cm mucosal margin was preserved to reduce closure tension. Endoscopic assistance provided enhanced illumination and magnified visualization of deep anatomical structures.

Key steps of endoscopic-assisted transoral resection of the submandibular gland. A Mucosal incision in the floor of the mouth. B Identification of the submandibular duct (arrow) and its accompanying vein (asterisk). C Identification of the lingual nerve (arrow) and submandibular ganglion (asterisk). D Exposure of the posterior border of the mylohyoid muscle (arrow). E Identification and control of a branch of the facial artery (asterisk). F En bloc removal of the tumor and submandibular gland

Step 3: management of the lingual nerve and submandibular duct

The accompanying vein of the submandibular duct served as an important anatomical landmark to distinguish the submandibular duct from the lingual nerve during dissection.The duct was ligated and transected near the mandibular border (Fig. 3B). The submandibular ganglion was sharply dissected from the deep lobe of the gland, constituting the first key surgical step (Fig. 3C). The sublingual gland was preserved, and adjacent tissues were retracted medially to improve exposure.

Step 4: mylohyoid retraction and gland exposure

External digital pressure facilitated identification of the posterior border of the mylohyoid muscle, which was subsequently retracted anterolaterally, representing the second key surgical step (Fig. 3D). Blunt dissection was performed to expose the anterior, medial, and posterior aspects of the gland while minimizing neurovascular injury.

Step 5: vascular control

At the vascular pole, branches of the facial artery and vein supplying the gland were carefully identified and secured using vascular clips (Fig. 3E). This maneuver constituted the third key surgical step and was critical for intraoperative hemostasis and prevention of postoperative hematoma formation.

Step 6: closure and postoperative care

After complete mobilization, the submandibular gland and tumor were removed en bloc (Fig. 3F). Hemostasis was confirmed by saline irrigation.The wound was closed with interrupted absorbable sutures. Routine drains were omitted with meticulous haemostasis and external compression. All patients received perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis covering oral aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. Postoperative oral hygiene included regular saline or chlorhexidine mouth rinsing, soft diet instructions, and repeated inspection of the intraoral incision. A mandibular compression dressing was applied for 3 days.

Pathological assessment

All resected specimens underwent routine histopathological examination. Final diagnosis, margin status, capsular integrity, tumor spillage, specimen fragmentation, and completeness of gland excision were reviewed. Operative records were also checked for intraoperative tumor violation.

Follow-up and complication assessment

Postoperative follow-up included examination of the oral floor and submandibular region, assessment of lingual and hypoglossal nerve function, wound evaluation, and surveillance for recurrence. Ultrasonography was performed when clinically indicated, and contrast-enhanced CT or MRI was obtained for suspicious clinical or sonographic findings. Follow-up duration was calculated from surgery to the most recent clinical assessment.

Transient lingual numbness was defined as new-onset sensory disturbance of the ipsilateral anterior two-thirds of the tongue or floor of the mouth. Assessment was based on patient self-report and clinical examination. Complete recovery was defined as patient-reported resolution without persistent sensory complaints. Formal quantitative sensory testing and systematic taste assessment were not routinely performed.

Postoperative abscess was defined as an infected localized fluid collection with swelling, tenderness, fluctuation, purulent aspiration, fever, or elevated inflammatory markers. Ultrasonography was used for suspected collections, and CT was performed when deep-space infection required exclusion. Treatment included aspiration, intensified antibiotics, compression, and oral hygiene care.

Learning curve analysis

All procedures were performed by the same senior surgeon, W.W., who had prior endoscopic surgical experience. Operative time was defined as the interval from mucosal incision to wound closure.

The learning curve was assessed using cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis of operative time. The CUSUM value was calculated as CUSUMn = Σ(Xi − Xmean), where Xi represents the operative time of each case and Xmean represents the overall mean operative time of the cohort. A positive slope indicated operative times longer than the overall mean, whereas a negative slope indicated shorter operative times. The transition point between the learning and proficiency phases was defined as the maximum CUSUM value followed by a sustained downward trend.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 120 patients were included in this study, comprising 19 males and 101 females, with a mean age of 29.7 ± 8.7 years. Tumors were unilateral in all cases. Tumor size ranged from 0.9 to 5.0 cm, with a mean diameter of 2.2 ± 0.7 cm. All patients completed a minimum postoperative follow-up period of 6 months (Table 1).

Surgical feasibility and operative outcomes

Endoscopic-assisted transoral resection of the SM was successfully completed in all patients without conversion to a transcervical approach. Operative time ranged from 16 to 180 min, with a mean duration of 57.6 ± 28.1 min.The length of hospital stay ranged from 2 to 7 days, with a mean duration of 4.5 ± 1.2 days.

Pathological findings

Final histopathological examination confirmed pleomorphic adenoma in all 120 patients. No cases of carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma or other salivary gland tumors were identified. All tumors were removed en bloc together with the involved submandibular gland. No intraoperative tumor violation, capsular rupture, tumor spillage, or specimen fragmentation was documented. Complete gland excision was confirmed pathologically in all cases, and all resection margins were negative.

Oncologic and cosmetic outcomes

Follow-up duration ranged from 6 to 34 months, with a mean follow-up of 17.9 ± 7.9 months. No local tumor recurrence was observed during the follow-up period. Patient-reported cosmetic satisfaction scores assessed at 3 months postoperatively were uniformly high, reflecting the absence of visible cervical scarring (Table 1).

Postoperative complications

Postoperative complications were predominantly transient and self-limited. Transient lingual nerve numbness occurred in 112 patients (93.3%) and resolved spontaneously within 0.3 to 3 months, with a median recovery time of 1months. Temporary limitation of tongue movement was observed in 5 patients (4.2%) and resolved within 2 weeks without intervention. Postoperative abscess formation occurred in 9 patients (7.5%) and was successfully managed with needle aspiration, followed by intensified antibiotic therapy and local compression. One patient (0.8%) developed a postoperative hematoma, which resolved following aspiration and compression. No cases of marginal mandibular nerve injury or hypoglossal nerve injury were observed (Table 2).

Learning curve and comparison between phases

CUSUM analysis identified an inflection point at case 34 (Fig. 4), dividing the cohort into the learning phase (cases 1–34) and proficiency phase (cases 35–120). Operative time decreased significantly in the proficiency phase (45.91 ± 14.53 min vs. 87.09 ± 32.83 min, P < .001), while tumor size was similar between phases (2.12 ± 0.59 cm vs. 2.36 ± 0.94 cm, P = .167). No significant differences were observed in postoperative abscess, transient lingual numbness, temporary tongue movement limitation, hematoma, or other complications. No conversion, marginal mandibular nerve injury, hypoglossal nerve injury, or local recurrence occurred in either phase (Table 3).

Discussion

This study represents one of the largest reported cohorts evaluating endoscopic-assisted transoral resection for SMG PA. Using a standardized surgical protocol, complete tumor resection was achieved while preserving the sublingual gland. No visible cervical scarring or marginal mandibular nerve injury was observed during the current follow-up period.

Tumor-specific reports of transoral excision for SMG PA remain limited [9–11, 16–19]. Previous applications of this approach have mainly involved inflammatory diseases, such as sialadenitis and sialolithiasis, and normal sublingual gland tissue was often sacrificed during tumor resection [10, 11, 19]. In contrast, the present technique preserves the sublingual gland, which may help maintain salivary function and is consistent with the principles of function-preserving surgery.In this cohort, tumors up to 5 cm were successfully treated; however, tumor size alone should not determine eligibility. Larger tumors may increase the difficulty of exposure, manipulation, vascular control, and intact specimen removal. Therefore, tumors approaching or exceeding 5 cm should be treated cautiously, and a transcervical approach may be more appropriate when oral exposure is inadequate or capsular violation is a concern. Tumors close to the glandular hilum or facial vessels also require careful preoperative assessment, as inadequate vascular control may increase the risk of bleeding or conversion.

Because no transcervical control group was included, a literature-based comparison table was added to contextualize our findings (Table 4) [11, 16, 19–22]. Previous transcervical series have shown that conventional submandibular gland excision is reliable but may be associated with visible cervical scarring and variable rates of marginal mandibular, lingual, or hypoglossal nerve dysfunction. In contrast, transoral series emphasize avoidance of a cervical scar and a low risk of marginal mandibular nerve injury, but temporary lingual nerve-related symptoms are relatively common. In the long-term intraoral series by Hong et al., temporary lingual sensory disturbance and tongue movement limitation occurred in 75% and 54% of patients, respectively, and two recurrences were associated with intraoperative tumor rupture [19]. In the present cohort, transient lingual numbness occurred in 93.3% of patients. Potential measures to reduce this complication include dissecting close to the glandular capsule rather than along the lingual nerve, avoiding excessive or prolonged nerve traction, intermittently releasing retractors, performing sharp dissection around the submandibular ganglion, and maintaining a moist operative field to minimize thermal and mechanical trauma. Thus, the cosmetic benefit and avoidance of marginal mandibular nerve injury should be carefully balanced against the high frequency of transient lingual nerve-related symptoms. Given differences in study design, pathology, surgical indications, and follow-up duration, this literature-based comparison should be interpreted as contextual rather than as evidence of superiority over the transcervical approach.

Learning curve analysis suggested improved operative efficiency after approximately 34 cases. This may reflect increasing familiarity with oral floor exposure, endoscopic visualization, identification of the lingual nerve and submandibular ganglion, mylohyoid retraction, and vascular control. However, operative time is only a surrogate marker of technical proficiency and may be affected by tumor size, location, anatomical variation, vascular anatomy, and case complexity. Therefore, tumor size and postoperative outcomes were compared between the learning and proficiency phases.Procedural proficiency should not be assessed solely by operative time. Completeness of en bloc excision, absence of tumor violation, complication rates, postoperative recovery, and patient-reported outcomes are also clinically important. Thus, the present CUSUM analysis should be regarded as an exploratory assessment of the technical learning process rather than definitive evidence of surgical mastery. Further multicenter studies involving different surgeons are needed to determine the reproducibility of this learning curve.

The clinical outcomes of this study suggest that the proposed technique is feasible and potentially safe in selected patients. The absence of marginal mandibular nerve injury and visible cervical scarring addresses two important concerns associated with the conventional transcervical approach [2, 23, 24]. However, the high cosmetic satisfaction score should be interpreted cautiously, as it was assessed using a simple 10-point Likert scale at 3 months rather than a validated patient-reported outcome measure. No blinded assessment or systematic evaluation of functional satisfaction was performed. Therefore, cosmetic satisfaction should not be interpreted as evidence of overall superiority in quality of life. Postoperative abscess formation may be associated with the transoral route, oral bacterial contamination, salivary leakage, residual dead space, and lack of routine drainage. Although all cases resolved with conservative management, this complication should be considered during patient counseling and postoperative care. Selective drainage may be useful in patients with large dead space, difficult hemostasis, extensive dissection, prolonged operative time, or high infection risk. Further studies are needed to evaluate whether selective drainage can reduce abscess or hematoma formation while preserving the cosmetic benefits of the transoral approach.

Several limitations should be noted. First, this was a single-center prospective study, which may limit generalizability. Although consecutive patients were enrolled, the findings may reflect institutional experience, and validation in other clinical settings is needed. Second, the absence of a control group precludes direct comparison with conventional transcervical approaches. Third, the follow-up duration was relatively short. Given the potential for late recurrence and malignant transformation in pleomorphic adenoma, longer follow-up is required to adequately assess long-term clinical recurrence-free outcome. Fourth, the study population was highly selected and included only patients with cytologically confirmed PA and favorable clinical and anatomical conditions.Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with SMG tumors. Another methodological limitation is the retrospective nature of trial registration. Despite these limitations, the present study provides prospective evidence supporting the safety, functional preservation, and early surgical excision adequacy of a standardized endoscopic-assisted transoral approach in appropriately selected patients.

Conclusion

A standardized endoscopic-assisted transoral approach appears feasible and potentially safe for selected patients with SMG PA. This technique avoids visible cervical scarring and may reduce the risk of marginal mandibular nerve injury. However, the high frequency of transient lingual numbness should be carefully considered and discussed with patients. Comparative studies with transcervical control groups and longer follow-up are needed to clarify the relative benefits and limitations of this approach.
