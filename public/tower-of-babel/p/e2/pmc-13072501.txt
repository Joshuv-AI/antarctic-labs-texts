Observation of the progression of tension in intracranial arachnoid cyst: a case report and review of literature
Authors: Zhixiong Lin, Jintao Chen, Hanrong Dong, Bei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe intracranial arachnoid cyst (IAC) are common benign intracranial space-occupying lesions in children, which can be categorized into tension and non-tension IAC based on imaging features. As observed cases of tension progression in IAC are extremely rare, the relationship between the two is currently unclear. Here, we present a rare case that demonstrates the transition from a non-tension IAC to a tension cyst by complete imaging evidence. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the brain performed 6 days after birth revealed the presence of a small non-tension IAC at the left cerebellopontine angle, with no significant compression of adjacent brain tissue and clear boundaries of the cerebral gyrus and sulcus. At 15 months, MRI revealed a significant enlargement of IAC, displacement of the brainstem, loss of the cerebral gyrus in the neighboring brain tissue, and tension-arc changes at the cyst-brain tissue interfaced with obstructive hydrocephalus. Through fenestration, the IAC were reduced, exhibiting non-tension changes, significantly alleviating pressure on adjacent brain tissue, and hydrocephalus significantly improved. Subsequent follow-up showed no obvious abnormalities. In conclusion, the degree of tension seen in IAC imaging was the result of IAC enlargement, which provides a basis for surgical intervention and postoperative efficacy evaluation.Keywords: Arachnoid cyst, Radiographic, MRI, Treatment, Endoscopic fenestration

Introduction

Intracranial arachnoid cyst (IAC) are common benign intracranial space-occupying lesions in children. They are pouch-like intra-arachnoid masses containing cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), primarily located in the CSF cisterna and the main cerebral fissure. The IAC constitute 1% of all intracranial space-occupying lesions, affecting 2.6% of the population under 18 years of age, of which more than 90% are asymptomatic [1–3]. It is believed that congenital IAC may form in the early stages of subarachnoid space development, stemming from abnormal arachnoid replication and division during the embryonic period. This formation is associated with irregular CSF flow [2, 4].The majority of congenital IAC are clinically silent and remain static in size; however, some IACs may gradually increase in size. Presently, research data from different centers indicate a wide variation in the proportion of enlarged IAC within the total IAC population, ranging from 0.73% to 19.7% [1, 5–8]. These suggested that enlargement of IAC in adults is rare and should be distinguished from that in children, especially those under 3 years of age. Although most IACs remain static throughout their lifespan, some IACs may enlarge under pressure, exerting a space-occupying effect on adjacent neural structures [9]. Among these cases, age stands out as the sole definitive effective factor.

Based on imaging features, IAC were divided into non-tensional and tensional [5], the latter is defined as a CSF accumulation encapsulated by the arachnoid membrane that exhibits expansibility with a space-occupying effect; and imaging demonstrates that the lesion exerts compressive effects in adjacent structures. This classification system focuses on the role of tension in IAC determining the appropriate time for surgical intervention. Nevertheless, due to the inability to clinically observe the progression of tension, the relationship between non-tensional and tensional is currently unclear. In this report, we present a rare case with complete imaging evidence of transformation from a non-tension IAC to a tension IAC.

Case presentation

A 15-month-old child was admitted to the hospital due to a progressive increase in head circumference over the past year. Brain MRI performed 6 days after birth revealed a small IAC in the left pontocerebellar region, without obstructive hydrocephalus at that time (Fig. 1A, B, C). At the age of 15 months, the child exhibited a progressive increase in head circumference, measuring 49.7 cm. Physical examination revealed retarded development, an abnormal head shape with a dilated and unclosed anterior fontanelle, measuring about 5*5 cm, no fundus oedema and an inability to walk. Brain MRI showed a giant subarachnoid IAC in the left pontocerebellar region, constricting the brain stem and left cerebellum. At the same time, CSF film demonstrated a weak CSF flow velocity signal in the midbrain aqueduct, accompanied by ventricle and fontanelle enlargement (Figs. 1D, E, F, 3A). The IAC fenestration was performed under general anesthesia on July 12, 2023. During the operation, upon opening the dura mater, the IAC wall exhibited high tension (Fig. 2A). Upon piercing the IAC wall, colorless clear liquid was ejected. Following the slow release of CSF, the IAC wall collapsed. Upon full opening of the lateral IAC wall, the IAC wall of the cerebellum and the inner side of the brain stem appeared to bulge outward. Recognizing the reduced pressure in the IAC cavity after opening, CSF accumulated outside the IAC wall, forming an inverse bulge into the IAC like a unidirectional valve (Fig. 2B).

To establish communication with the cisterna ambiens, the walls of the IAC were further opened at the medial and ventral parts of the cerebellar brainstem (Fig. 2C, D). Brain MRI 2 months after surgery indicated a reduction in the IAC, alleviation of the mass effect, and a significant decrease in the child's head circumference (Fig. 1G, H, I, and Fig. 3C). At follow-up, there were no differences in psychological and motor development to children of the same age. At follow-up, the psychological and motor development of the study group showed no differences among children of the same age.

Discussion

IACS is classified by intracerebral locus as middle cranial fossa, suprasellar cistern, cerebellopontine angle cistern, quadrigeminal cistern, dorsolateral surface, posterior cranial fossa, lateral ventricle, vermiform process of cerebellum [5]. This case report clearly demonstrates the progression of non-tensional IAC to tensional IAC and elucidates that the tensional nature of IACs is caused by enlargement in IACs. In order to understand the documented relationship between enlargement and tension enlargement of IAC in the literature, we searched the English literature in PubMed and Web of Science databases, using the key words "growing", "Space-Occupying", "Increasing Arachnoid Cysts", "Increasing Arachnoid Cyst" and "Cystic Lesions".

The search of the English databases was concluded in February 2024 with an initially unrestricted scope. Abstracts of relevant literature were searched and screened by two independent reviewers, followed by a full-text review of relevant studies, and data were extracted and verified.

It turns out that, out of the 15 articles reviewed, only 7 were considered eligible for inclusion as they discussed the relationship between tension and enlargement of IAC [10–16]; the remaining 8 articles mentioned tension, but did not explore the relationship between tension and enlargement of IAC, so they were excluded (see Table 1). Of the 7 articles, only 1 reported on hydrocephalus caused by a voluminous left frontotemporal IACs with an important mass effect on the midline and lateral ventricles [11]; however none of the studies demonstrated the whole process.

Although the mechanism driving IACs enlargement remains unclear, several postulated mechanisms include: (1) The ball valve hypothesis: There may be an anatomic communication that acts functionally as a one-way valve between the cyst and the subarachnoid space. (2) Osmotic gradient between cystic content and CSF: This theory is not supported by evidence because the cystic content is similar in composition to CSF. Congenital IAC are dysdevelopmetal anomalies and contain clear CSF-like fluid. In acquired cysts, the intracystic fluid may be hemosiderin-stained or may contain inflammatory cells with a possible gradient. (3) Fluid secretion by cyst wall cells: morphological and ultracellular cytochemical evidence supports the secretory nature of the wall of IAC. The argument against continuous secretion is that the IAC often remain static in size and sometimes disappear, thereby demonstrating that secretion is neither universal nor, likely, the only mechanism involved [17–20]. Generally, if an IAC enlarges, it can induce symptoms such as raised intracranial pressure, nausea and vomiting, focal neurologic deficits, or hydrocephalus [9, 21, 22]. Surgical intervention is typically recommended for symptomatic patients, depending on the effect that is often determined by the tension of the IAC. However, due to the infrequent observation of the progression of tensional IAC, controversies persist regarding when and how to perform surgery. In this case, we posit that tension in the IAC results from its progression and is associated with a unidirectional valve within the subarachnoid space.

It can be hypothesed that a small section of the AC wall forms a one-way valve, when intracranial pressure increases, which may be caused by crying, exertion, or the Valsalva maneuver, the responsiveness of the subarachnoid space to changes in venous pressure might also lead to increased filling of the cyst and contribute to enlargement, which prevents its outward flow due to the valve's unidirectional nature (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the brain tissue outside the cyst exerts pressure on the AC wall, which results in a pressure balance between the inside and outside of the IAC, ultimately forming a stable tensional cyst (Fig. 4B). Consequently, IAC is often incidentally diagnosed during brain computed tomography or MRI scans conducted for unrelated symptoms. The stable appearance of the IAC on imaging suggests that their internal and external pressures have equilibrated. In the case of slit valves, pulsations of CSF flowing from caudad to cephalad in the prepontine cistern during the cardiac cycle lift the wall of the cyst, increasing the opening of the slit valve and thereby resulting in net flow of CSF into the IAC. Normal backflow of CSF into the spinal compartment is relatively blocked during the cardiac cycle, due to apposition of the slit valve against the tension exerted by the IAC way and adjacent brain tissue. Thus, this imbalance in oscillatory flow of CSF between the craniospinal compartments further supports the slit valve is responsible for the increase in size of suprasellar prepontine IAC.

The model of progression of tensional IAC: A Early IAC with the pressure in the IAC cavity being lower than dynamic pulsatile ICP pressure; B Tension IAC with a pressure balance between the inside and outside of the IAC; C After treatment, with the pressure in the IAC cavity being lower than the pressure around the IAC and the retraction of the IAC wall. a: Dynamic pulsatile ICP pressure; b: pressure of the surrounding tissue on the IAC; c: IAC wall tension; d: pressure of IAC cavity on IAC wall; e: pressure of IAC flow outlet. When a > d > b+c, the IAC enlarges; when a = d = b+c, the IAC remains stable; when a = (d-e) < b+c, the IAC shrinks; when a = d = e = b+c, the IAC becomes stable again

Clinical data have shown that tensional IAC tend to become smaller after fenestration or shunt surgery, leading to the alleviation of corresponding clinical symptoms. If there is bidirectional communication in the IAC, it should present non-tension characteristics; whereas the presence of a one-way valve would lead to gradual enlargement in the early stage, demonstrating tensional changes. Studies have demonstrated the existence of only a few small capillaries on the IAC wall, lacking cells capable of clear fluid secretion. Moreover, the properties of IAC cavity fluid and CSF proteins are roughly similar [23, 24]. Additionally, the majority of IAC do not increase in size. Therefore, this case does not support a mechanism of gradual IAC enlargement caused by fluid secretion from the IAC wall or gradient differences. Under normal circumstances, high-pressure liquid will flow to a low-pressure area. Following the principles of mechanics, generally enlarged IAC are considered tension IAC.

The primary surgical methods for IAC intervention include craniotomy with IAC resection or fenestration, endoscopic cystocisternostomy, and cyst-peritoneal shunt (CPS) [5, 25]. A recent meta-analysis revealed no significant difference in the impact of these surgical methods on the quality of life of patients after 5 years [26]. However, there were variations in efficacy among the three surgical methods. The reduction rate of IAC after craniotomy was 92%, with a near-total disappearance rate of 51%. For endoscopic fenestration, the reduction rate was 75%, and the near-total disappearance rate was 39%. CPS demonstrated the highest reduction rate of 100%, with a near-total disappearance rate of 89% [27]. However, this conclusion is not universally applicable and the information in this case is more supportive of opening through a cyst.

Serious complications, such as shunt dependency syndrome (SDS), can occur after CPS [28–30]. Shim et al. [31] reported that among 19 patients undergoing CPS, 8 developed SDS, with an average onset time of 6.1 years post-operation, reaching a proportion as high as 42%. In 11 cases with a complete disappearance of the IAC, attempting shunt removal by ligation was successful in 8 cases. Ventriculoperitoneal shunt not only effectively alleviates SDS through the reconstruction of the CSF circulation pathway but also prevents the enlarged IAC from increasing in size simultaneously. These conditions further suggest that the IAC fluid primarily originates from extracapsular CSF through a one-directional pathway, contradicting the theories of intracystic fluid production by secreting cells or fluid movement following an osmotic gradient. Therefore, IAC can be comprehended as a special type of hydrocephalus-local hydrocephalus.

In this case, the IAC cavity gradually reduced in size and exhibited non-tensional changes after the subarachnoid fistula of the IAC (Fig. 4C). This reduction was attributed to the pressure within the IAC cavity being lower than the pressure surrounding the IAC, leading to the retraction of the IAC wall.

Conclusion

The IAC is characterized by an abnormal accumulation of CSF in the CSF circulation pathway, similar to limited hydrocephalus. The tensional features observed in IAC imaging reflect the progression of the IAC. The severity of tension serves as the basis for surgical intervention and a critical index for assessing postoperative efficacy. IAC cystostomy has almost become the gold standard for cysts around cisterns and intraventricular, a procedure that not only restores the physiological flow and circulation of CSF within the capsule but also addresses the space-occupying effect of the IAC, thereby avoiding the development of long-term SDS.
