Cause-specific mortality in a cohort of paediatric cerebral malaria patients
Authors: Hunter J. Wynkoop, Ruth Bvalani-Kaunda, Karl B. Seydel, Christopher Alan Moxon, Yamikani Chimalizeni, Terrie E. Taylor, Gretchen L. Birbeck, Douglas G. Postels, Nicole F. O’Brien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPaediatric cerebral malaria (CM) kills thousands of children globally each year. Despite multiple interventional trials, no adjunctive therapy has demonstrated a survival benefit. As CM is known to cause multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), failure to consider the immediate cause of death or cause-specific mortality may partially explain these negative findings, particularly for targeted, organ specific therapies.MethodsA retrospective review of data from children with CM enrolled in interventional clinical trials at the Blantyre Malaria Project/Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital between 2018 and 2025 was performed. For participants who died during their hospitalization, the immediate cause of death was categorized into one of seven groups using a priori definitions.ResultsA total of 393 children were enrolled in the study period, and 52 (13%) died. Cerebral herniation was the most common immediate cause of death (46%) followed by status epilepticus with apnea (17%). Non-neurologic immediate causes of death occurred in 37% and included: cardiac failure/shock (13%), respiratory failure (12%), renal failure (6%), hepatic failure (4%), and other/indeterminant (2%). Admission coma score was significantly lower in children who died of cerebral herniation or hepatic failure (p = 0.001). Admission oxygen saturation was lowest in patients who died of respiratory failure (p = 0.04) and admission lactate was highest in those that died of cardiac failure/shock, hepatic failure, or other/indeterminant causes (p = 0.01). MODS was prevalent across the cohort regardless of the immediate cause of death.ConclusionsMultiple different immediate causes of death were identified in children with CM. This may dilute the measured impact of brain-focused therapies aimed at reducing central nervous system injury. Modifications in sample size estimates may need to be made in future clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05619-9.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated there were 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 deaths globally in 2023 [1]. African cases accounted for 95% of the world’s malaria-related deaths, the majority of whom were children. Cerebral malaria (CM) is a severe manifestation of the disease associated with high mortality rates and significant long-term sequelae in many survivors [2–4]. Despite the significant public health burden of CM, clinical trials have failed to find definitive adjunctive treatments that improve outcomes for children with the disease [5].

“All-cause” mortality is commonly used as the primary endpoint in paediatric CM trials, but this approach may be problematic. Neurologic injury resulting in a cerebral herniation event has historically been considered the primary cause of death in this population. However, recent work has demonstrated that multiple organ dysfunction (MODS) is ubiquitous in these patients [6, 7]. Thus, if a significant proportion of patients in a clinical trial have non-neurologic malaria-induced organ failure that leads to death, clinical trials of brain-based therapies may result in type II error, showing no advantage of the intervention over placebo.

To decrease the likelihood of inaccurate inferences about the therapeutic effects of new drugs or interventions, clinical trials in disease states where MODS is common frequently report both all cause-mortality and the cause-specific mortality (otherwise known as the immediate cause of death) [8–10]. This approach is particularly relevant in studies with small sample sizes. A comprehensive evaluation of the types and frequency of various immediate causes of death for children with CM has not been done. Therefore, the immediate cause of death for each paediatric CM patient who died while participating in recent interventional trials at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital in Malawi was established.

Methods

Patient population

A retrospective chart review of patients aged 6 months to 12 years who were admitted with a diagnosis of CM (Blantyre coma score ((BCS)) ≤ 2, Plasmodium falciparum parasitaemia, and no other known coma aetiology) to the Blantyre Malaria Project Paediatric Research Ward at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital in Blantyre, Malawi between 2018 and 2025 was completed. To be included in the presented study, participants had to have died while enrolled in an interventional clinical trial that occurred during the study period (Treating Brain Swelling in Paediatric Cerebral Malaria ((TBS study); NCT03300648), Aggressive Antipyretics in CNS Malaria ((FEVER study); NCT03399318), or DON (6-diazo-5-oxo-L-norleucine) in Paediatric Cerebral Malaria: A Phase I/II Dose-Escalation Safety Study ((DON study); NCT05478720) [11, 12].

All enrolled participants received routine standard of care. At admission, following vital sign measurement, a detailed physical examination assessing signs of respiratory distress, capillary refill time and warmth of extremities, and neurologic signs including brainstem reflexes, tone, and posturing was performed. Blantyre Coma Score (BCS) was determined. Admission blood glucose (Aviva Accucheck, Switzerland), lactate (Arkray Lactate Pro2, Japan), packed cell volume (PCV), creatinine (StatSensor, Nova Analytical Systems, Austria), and electrolytes (Abbott iStat, USA) were measured using point of care testing methods.

As soon as was clinically feasible but generally within 4 h of admission, a baseline Transcranial Doppler Ultrasound (TCD) was acquired using a non-imaging unit (NovaSignal, United States of America). TCD was interpreted by a certified practitioner. Terminal intracranial hypertension was determined to be present when systolic spikes with reversal of diastolic flow was identified [13]. A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study was then completed using a General Electric 0.35 T scanner (General Electric Corporation, USA) for those enrolled in 2018 or 2020, and a Hyperfine 0.0625 T portable MRI (Hyperfine Corporation, Guilford, USA) for those enrolled between 2021 and 2025. MRIs were systematically reviewed by radiologists experienced in radiographic findings of children with CM and a brain volume score (BVS) was determined. A BVS of 3 represents a normal brain volume whereas scores of 4–8 signify progressive increases in brain volume [14]. A BVS of 8 is consistent with cerebral herniation. An admission electroencephalogram (EEG) (Ceegraph digital machine, BioLogic, Natus Medical Incorporated, USA) with a modified 10–20 system was performed to evaluate for non-convulsive status epilepticus. Duration of EEG was 30 min for the TBS and FEVER studies and 12 h for the DON study. EEGs were reviewed by a neurologist with fellowship training in EEG.

Following admission, participants underwent vital sign assessment and BCS scoring every two hours. Blood glucose, lactate, and PCV evaluation were performed every six hours. Twice daily physical examination including a detailed neurologic assessment was completed by a consultant level physician. TCD and EEG were performed daily as long as BCS was < 4. Brain MRI was performed daily when clinically stability allowed.

Determining the immediate cause of death

Severe malaria was assumed to be the underlying cause of death for all participants. Seven categories of immediate causes of death were established: (1) neurologic failure- cerebral herniation, (2) neurologic failure- status epilepticus with apnea, (3) cardiovascular failure, (4) respiratory failure, (5) renal failure, (6) hepatic failure, and (7) other. Classification scheme to determine immediate cause of death

Electronic databases were accessed to determine the total number of enrolled participants during the study period. Patients with the primary outcome of death were flagged, their paper case report forms were reviewed, and a synopsis of the relevant clinical course, laboratory data, and imaging findings was developed. Three physicians (HW, RK, NO), unaware of primary interventional trial assignment, independently reviewed the clinical charts of all patients who died. On the basis of these reviews, each patient was placed into one of the seven immediate cause of death categories described above. All three reviewers had to agree on the underlying cause of death. If there was an initial disagreement between reviewers on the underlying cause, an adjudication process was used to reach consensus.

Additionally, utilizing a modified Paediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction-2 Score that has been previously described in this patient population, reviewers noted evidence of other organ dysfunction that was present outside the immediate cause of death [6, 7]. In addition to cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, and hepatic dysfunction, reviewers also identified haematologic dysfunction/disseminated intravascular coagulation in patients that had clinically significant bleeding in the absence of hepatic failure. Asymptomatic anaemia and thrombocytopenia were not categorized as haematologic dysfunction since these are known complications of malaria.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Following assessment of normality, other variables are presented as medians with interquartile range or mean with standard deviation where appropriate. Kruskal Wallis was performed to assess differences between demographics, vital signs, and laboratory values between immediate cause of death groups. Fleiss kappa was used to estimate agreement between reviewers.

Results

Discussion

Cerebral malaria is a devastating disease associated with high mortality rates in children globally. Death in CM is typically attributed to cerebral herniation/brain stem compression [14]. Many interventional trials of adjunctive therapies targeting mechanisms that contribute to brain swelling have been undertaken over the last 25 years [5]. These studies have largely yielded disappointing results, with no single adjunctive therapy demonstrating unequivocal benefit in survival rates. This study identified that while cerebral herniation was the most common immediate cause of death (46% of our cohort), many paediatric CM patients had an alternate specific cause of death. Thus, using all-cause mortality as a primary endpoint for paediatric CM trials may dilute treatment effect and be a reason that some previous interventional studies have failed to show benefit. This is likely to be particularly true for treatments that were focused on reducing cerebral edema.

To effectively use all-cause mortality as a primary end point, an investigational therapy must either possess an exceptionally strong treatment effect or the trial must enroll a large enough sample size to overcome the weight of patients who do not stand to benefit from the therapy. These are difficult obstacles to overcome in paediatric CM trials. Most treatments would not be expected to have such a significant treatment effect and studies would likely be stopped for futility before including enough participants to establish a benefit of a therapy on a subset of the studied population.

Distinguishing between all-cause and cause-specific mortality/immediate cause of death is an approach that may aid in interpretation of interventional trial results in paediatric severe malaria [15, 16]. If using immediate cause of death as a trial endpoint, it is imperative to eliminate bias. Some interventional trials rely on clinical event review committees to provide a blinded, objective, and systematic mechanism for evaluating and adjudicating specific causes of death [8, 10]. Alternatively, in critical care research, a standardized, validated, classification system for immediate cause of death has been developed (ICU-DECLARE) [17]. In the future, one of these approaches could be developed for use in severe malaria interventional trials.

Outside of the impact on trial design and interpretation, it remains unclear if the findings of this study can assist in making acute clinical care decisions. Several variables at the time of hospital admission differed significantly between children that went on to have different immediate causes of death (i.e. BCS was lower for those dying of cerebral herniation and hepatic failure than other causes, oxygen saturation was lower in those dying of respiratory failure, and lactate was highest in those dying of cardiac or hepatic failure). Thus, it may be possible to identify a child at high risk of progressive organ failure that would benefit from organ specific supportive therapy. However, based on small sample sizes in each immediate cause of death group, the identified associations cannot be overinterpreted. Future trials with larger numbers of participants may allow for a more powerful statistical analytic approach, such as logistic regression, to more clearly determine relationships between admission variables and later immediate cause of death. If these relationships are confirmed, future interventional trials could test the impact of specific organ support on mortality for each different cause of death. However, several issues should be considered that would likely limit the success as well generalizability of this approach. A total of 27% of the cohort died within the first six hours of hospitalization, a time frame that is generally considered to represent non-modifiable mortality. Further, given the intensive monitoring of clinical, laboratory, and neuroimaging that was required to identify organ dysfunction and assign immediate cause of death in this study, use in clinical care in resource limited settings outside of highly protocolized research centres will be difficult.

Limitations

The World Health Organization (WHO) definition of CM provides a standardized framework for its diagnosis that is easily utilized in resource-limited settings where the disease occurs. While the definition has a high sensitivity to detect cases, it lacks specificity. For example, it doesn’t account for other conditions such as viral encephalitis, metabolic disorders, or toxic exposures that can cause coma in a patient with incidental malaria parasitaemia. All patients included in this manuscript were enrolled in a clinical trial that used the WHO case definition for CM. While the patients were extensively characterized using detailed physical examinations and several laboratory measurements not widely available for the care of CM patients, without autopsy data it is possible that some participants had an alternate cause of coma, and this resulted in higher rates of non-cerebral immediate causes of death than would be identified if a more specific case definition for the disease were available.

Further, CM has complex underlying pathophysiology with multiple interacting factors. This has several implications for the interpretation of the findings. First, while there was excellent agreement between reviewers on the immediate cause of death, determination can be complex, and some patients may have been misclassified. This is further complicated by the retrospective nature of the study where some data points that may have influenced decision-making were absent. Additionally, classification of participants into an immediate cause of death category by single organ failure etiology may be overly simplistic. It is clear from both Table 4 and Fig. 2 that multiple organ dysfunction is at play in nearly all patients; key interactions between failing organs may, therefore, be a significant factor to consider. Prospective work in another cohort of children with severe malaria evaluating the utility of multiorgan dysfunction phenotyping (single vs several overlapping combinations) and the relationship with immediate cause of death could be performed to validate and improve this study’s findings.

Last, it remains unclear if the mechanisms leading to neurologic failure in CM are on the same causal pathway as those that lead to non-neurologic organ failure. If they are, then some of the assumptions made around the impact of various targeted treatments on all-cause mortality may be incorrect. Future trials of the impact of various targeted interventions on both cause-specific and all-cause mortality could be undertaken.

Conclusions

In clinical trials of cerebral malaria, relying solely on all-cause mortality risks overlooking critical insights. Determination of cause-specific mortality may be considered when interpreting the efficacy of interventions being evaluated, particularly for those with a specific targeted mechanism.
