Artificial intelligence system for predicting areal bone mineral density from plain X-rays
Authors: Huy Gia Nguyen, Dinh-Tan Nguyen, Thach Son Tran, Sai Ho Ling, Lan Thuc Ho-Pham, Tuan Van Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is the standard method for assessing areal bone mineral density (aBMD), diagnosing osteoporosis, and predicting fracture risk. However, DXA’s availability is limited in resource-poor areas. This study aimed to develop an artificial intelligence (AI) system capable of estimating aBMD from standard radiographs.MethodsThe study was part of the Vietnam Osteoporosis Study, a prospective population–based research involving 3783 participants aged 18 years and older. A total of 7060 digital radiographs of the frontal pelvis and lateral spine were taken using the FCR Capsula XLII system (Fujifilm Corp., Tokyo, Japan). aBMD at the femoral neck and lumbar spine was measured with DXA (Hologic Horizon, Hologic Corp., Bedford, MA, USA). An ensemble of seven deep-learning models was used to analyze the X-rays and predict bone mineral density, termed “xBMD”.ResultsThe correlation between xBMD and aBMD was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. The correlation between xBMD and aBMD at the femoral neck was strong (r = 0.90; 95% CI, 0.88–0.91), and similarly high at the lumbar spine (r = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.85–0.88). This correlation remained consistent across different age groups and genders. The AI system demonstrated excellent performance in identifying individuals at high risk for hip fractures, with area under the ROC curve (AUC) values of 0.96 (95% CI, 0.95–0.98) at the femoral neck and 0.97 (95% CI, 0.96–0.99) at the lumbar spine.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that AI can accurately predict aBMD and identify individuals at high risk of fractures. This AI system could provide an efficient alternative to DXA for osteoporosis screening in settings with limited resources and high patient demand.SummaryAn AI system developed to predict aBMD from X-rays showed strong correlations with DXA (r = 0.90 at femoral neck; = r0.87 at lumbar spine) and high accuracy in identifying individuals at high risk for fractures (AUC = 0.96 at femoral neck; AUC = 0.97 at lumbar spine).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00198-025-07634-7.Keywords: Artificial intelligence; bone mineral density, Fracture, Osteoporosis, Plain radiography, XBMD

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a skeletal condition characterized by the reduction in bone mineral density (BMD) and deterioration in bone structure, rendering individuals more susceptible to fragility fractures. Patients with a fracture have an increased risk of mortality and significant healthcare costs [1, 2]. The disease poses a significant threat to global health. According to the World Health Organization, an estimated 200 million people worldwide have osteoporosis, with a new fragility fracture occurring every 3 s [3, 4]. The economic burden is also considerable, with fracture-related costs expected to reach an estimated USD 131.5 billion annually by 2050 [5].

Identifying individuals at high risk for fractures is critical for early intervention and the prevention of fractures and their related complications [6, 7]. Achieving this, however, depends on having accessible and reliable methods for diagnosing osteoporosis. Currently, the gold standard for measuring bone mineral density is dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). DXA uses two X-ray beams with different energy levels to distinguish between bone mineral content, soft tissue, and fat, providing accurate measurements of areal BMD (aBMD) at specific skeletal sites. These measurements allow clinicians to diagnose osteoporosis and estimate fracture risk based on established diagnostic thresholds.

While DXA provides accurate BMD measurements, its accessibility presents a significant challenge. DXA scanners are relatively expensive, require trained personnel, and may not be readily available in resource-constrained settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [8–14]. Even in developed countries, DXA scans can be time-consuming, with the average wait time stretching to months [12]. Existing risk assessment tools such as FRAX [15] and Garvan [16] use aBMD (or body weight if aBMD is unavailable) as a key parameter for estimating fracture risk. This lack of accessibility creates a critical gap in early diagnosis and intervention for osteoporosis in these regions [11, 14, 17].

X-ray radiography is widely available and has been a cornerstone of medical diagnosis for decades, offering a rapid, non-invasive, and relatively inexpensive way to visualize bones and identify fractures. However, the potential of X-ray technology extends beyond fracture diagnosis. X-ray images contain valuable information encoded within the bone structure and density variations.

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning techniques, have opened new opportunities for utilizing X-rays for BMD prediction [18–20]. Deep learning models, trained on big datasets of medical images and corresponding clinical data, can learn to extract complex features and patterns within the images. By analyzing the subtle variations in bone texture and density visible on X-ray images, AI models can potentially be trained to predict BMD values with high accuracy. Although older models were utilized in earlier research due to their historical significance [18–22], newer models have not been extensively studied. Furthermore, previous studies primarily involved hospital-based patients, which may limit their applicability to population-based screening.

The present study sought to harness the potential of AI for predicting BMD from plain X-rays. We present a novel approach using a large population-based dataset of X-ray images and corresponding DXA-measured BMD values to train and evaluate a new deep learning model for BMD prediction and osteoporosis diagnosis.

Methods and materials

Vietnam osteoporosis study

This study is part of the Vietnam Osteoporosis Study (VOS) project, in which the protocol and details have been published elsewhere [23]. In summary, VOS is a study conducted in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, involving 4159 participants from the community who were randomly recruited via either advertisement at the universities or computer-based random selection from the database of community organizations. The study includes men and women aged 18 years and older who give their written consent and are mentally and physically able to take the clinical tests. All research protocols and procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the People’s Hospital 115 (approval number: 297/BV-NCKH) and conducted according to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Anthropometric measurement

Anthropometric parameters such as age, weight and height were obtained. Height and weight were measured with an electronic portable wall-mounted Seca Model 769 (Seca Corp, CA, USA) without shoes, accessories and heavy layers of clothing. Body mass index (BMI) was derived as weight in kilograms over the square of height in meters. We categorized participants into four groups: underweight (if BMI < 17 kg/m2), normal (BMI between 17 and 22 kg/m2), overweight (BMI between 23 and 27.4 kg/m2) and obese (BMI > 27.4 kg/m2).

Bone mineral density measurement

The aBMD measurements were performed using a Hologic Horizon machine (Hologic Corp., Bedford, MA, USA). The DXA machine utilized the APEX system version 5.6.0.5 for data acquisition and analysis. To ensure standardized densitometry, qualified radiologists conducted phantom calibrations before each measurement. During the DXA scan, participants were instructed to wear light gowns and remove any accessories or external objects that could interfere with the scan and affect the accuracy of the measurement. Reading of aBMD was obtained at the left femoral neck and the lumbar spine. The short-term in vivo precision, expressed as the coefficient of variation, was 1.7% for the femoral neck and 1.5% for the lumbar spine based on 20 individuals [23].

Plain radiograph acquisition

The digital X-ray of the pelvis and spine was obtained from each participant using an FCR Capsula XLII (Fujifilm Corp., Tokyo, Japan). Standard lateral X-rays of the lumbar spine were taken with a tube-to-film distance of 101.6 cm centered at L2. Frontal X-rays of the pelvis were taken angled to the femoral neck from the same distance. X-rays were stored as 12-bit with a resolution of 0.15 mm × 0.15 mm pixel spacing in digital imaging and communications in medicine format. The X-rays were taken at the same time with DXA measurements.

Data preprocessing

The data were partitioned into a hip dataset, which included frontal pelvis X-rays with corresponding femoral neck aBMD, and a spine dataset, comprising lateral spine X-rays with lumbar spine aBMD. We retrieved both the X-rays and participant information whenever possible and selected if the corresponding aBMD fell within the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the distribution. This restriction prevents the AI from learning irrelevant noise and ensures it learns from a more consistent range, minimizing the influence of extreme outliers that may not be clinically relevant. Specifically, the ranges were as follows: femoral neck aBMD from 0.45 to 1.05 g/cm2 and lumbar spine aBMD from 0.6 to 1.2 g/cm2.

Each dataset was randomly divided into a development set and a testing set (Table A. 1). The testing set consisted of approximately one thousand randomly chosen participants who were excluded from the development phase. The development set, comprising the remaining participants, was further randomly split into a training set (80%) and a validation set (20%) [24].

During the development phase, the AI was trained on the training set to maximize performance on the validation set. In the testing phase, the AI was evaluated using the testing set to ensure its performance on unseen data. The results from the testing phase were then reported.

Artificial intelligence design

The AI system processed two X-ray views to estimate AI-predicted BMD, i.e., frontal pelvis X-rays for predicting the femoral neck aBMD and lateral spine X-rays for predicting the lumbar spine aBMD (see Appendix B for details). The AI model comprises seven modules (six convolutional neural networks [VGG16, VGG19, ResNet34, ResNet50, ConvNeXt-B and RegNetY-16GF] and one vision transformer [ViT-B/16]), developed and tested on 7060 X-ray images using the Stochastic Gradient Descent optimizer with the learning rate set to decrease on a plateau (learning rate: 1e-3), and mean squared error loss up to 300 epochs, with predictions ensembled to enhance accuracy. The AI system consists of two stages: (1) to detect the region of interest and (2) to predict aBMD (Fig. 1). While VGG [25] and ResNet [26], known for their simplicity and historical impact, were commonly used in earlier studies [18–22], newer models offer significant improvements. These include better network functions, enhanced detail capture, and a better balance between complexity and performance. Our AI uses seven advanced models, including new convolutional neural networks and vision transformers, which excel in visual recognition by effectively handling detailed and spatial information [27]. Each model predicts a value, and these predictions are weighted to output a single value, maximizing accuracy. The AI was developed with Pytorch [28], and computational processes were carried out on the high-performance supercomputer infrastructure of the University of Technology Sydney. To enhance the training process, we augmented the training X-rays with affine transformation to account for potential positioning variations, including vertical and horizontal shifting within 10%, rotation within the range of − 10–10° and zooming in and out up to 20%.

The deep-learning AI system extracted the regions of interest and then predicted bone mineral density from the extracted regions using an ensemble of seven deep-learning models

Statistical analysis

The concordance between AI-predicted BMD (xBMD) and aBMD and the performance of xBMD in classifying individuals with osteoporosis and high risk of fracture were conducted for the whole study population. Predefined genders and age groups-specific analyses were also considered. Firstly, we computed the Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) to quantify the concordance between xBMD and aBMD, with an r over 0.8 indicating a strong correlation. To visualize the agreement between the two measurements, we constructed a Bland–Altman plot, which plots the mean of each pair of aBMD and xBMD values against their difference. We also reported the Bland–Altman bias, a measure used to assess the agreement between two measurements, calculated as the mean difference between the two BMD values. Additionally, we reported the mean absolute error (MAE) between the predicted and actual values.

Secondly, we calculated 10-year fracture risk for all participants using the Garvan Fracture Risk Calculator [16]. Participants with a high risk of any fracture ≥ 20% or of hip fracture ≥ 3% are considered high risk. We examined the correlation in the estimated risk and the concordance in discriminating high-risk individuals between aBMD and xBMD. The data management and analysis were conducted using the R statistical environment (version 4.2.1) [29].

Results

Among the 3783 participants with radiographs and BMD data in the VOS, we collected a total of 7060 plain X-rays, with 5134 used for development and 1926 for testing. Specifically, we obtained 3677 pairs of X-rays and corresponding aBMD measurements for the hip dataset and 3383 pairs for the spine dataset. The hip dataset was divided into a development set with 2743 pairs and a testing set of approximately 934 pairs. Similarly, the spine dataset was split into a development set of around 2391 pairs and a testing set of about 992 pairs.

The average age was 47 years old, and approximately 51% of women and 45% of men were over 50 years (Table 1). The mean BMI of the participants was 22.8 kg/m2 (standard deviation, 3.3) in women and 23.5 kg/m2 (9.5) in men. Approximately 9% of participants were obese, and 36% were overweight. Among those aged 50 years and older, osteoporosis prevalence among women was 28%, twice that of men (~ 14%).

Concordance between xBMD and aBMD

A strong correlation between xBMD and aBMD was noted (Table 2). For example, there was a high correlation at the femoral neck (r = 0.90 [95% CI, 0.88 to 0.91]) and the lumbar spine (r = 0.87 [95% CI, 0.85 to 0.88]) (Fig. 2a). No significant bias was observed between xBMD and aBMD at both the femoral neck (bias = 0.00 [95% CI, − 0.10 to 0.11]) and the lumbar spine (bias = 0.00 [95% CI, − 0.11 to 0.12]). The limits of agreement were narrow, and while the difference between BMD values increased with their mean, the variability remained consistent across the range of mean values (see Fig. A. 1).

Concordance between AI-based and DXA-based fracture risk

There was a high level of concordance between DXA-predicted and AI-predicted 10-year hip fracture risk at the femoral neck (r = 0.93 [95% CI, 0.91 to 0.94]) and lumbar spine (r = 0.92 [95% CI, 0.90 to 0.93]) (Fig. 3a). The concordance for overall fracture risk was stronger, with correlations of r = 0.97 [95% CI, 0.96 to 0.97] at the femoral neck and r = 0.95 [95% CI, 0.94 to 0.96] at the lumbar spine. The AI demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability in identifying individuals at high risk for hip fracture (AUC = 0.96 [95% CI, 0.95 to 0.98] at the femoral neck, AUC = 0.97 [95% CI, 0.96 to 0.99] at the lumbar spine) and for any fracture (AUC = 0.99 [95% CI, 0.99 to 1.00] at the femoral neck, AUC = 1.00 [95% CI, 0.99 to 1.00] at the lumbar spine) (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry remains the gold standard for measuring BMD and predicting fracture risk. However, in resource-constrained settings, especially in low-income countries, access to DXA is severely limited, posing significant challenges for osteoporosis management. In this study, we have developed an AI system capable of predicting BMD from plain radiographs, which are more readily available in most healthcare settings. Our findings demonstrate that the BMD predicted by the AI system aligns closely with BMD measured by DXA, suggesting that osteoporosis diagnosis and fracture prediction can be effectively conducted in low-resource settings.

Our findings are unique in that the model we developed is the first to use an AI approach for predicting BMD from plain X-rays obtained from a general community sample. Our results are generally in line with a previous study [18], which reported a high correlation (r = 0.92) between AI-predicted BMD (from pelvis/lumbar spine radiographs) and BMD measured by Hologic DXA. However, we were unable to replicate the reported correlation using their method in our sample. An explanation for the discrepancy could not be determined.

The ensemble model in their study showed stronger correlations compared to single-model approaches [19], with excellent discrimination of osteoporosis, consistent with our findings. Notably, predictions were more accurate for the hip than for the spine. Utilizing xBMD for osteoporosis discrimination proved more effective than direct classification methods [20, 22]. In line with prior studies [19], we found that background removal hindered the backbone learning process, indicating that the AI leveraged differences in radiation between bone and non-bone regions.

Early research employed AI to analyze computed tomography (CT) images for predicting BMD and found a strong correlation with BMD measured by DXA [30–32]. In contrast, the uses of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in predicting BMD were not explored. Nonetheless, AI was able to discriminate osteoporosis from MRI [33, 34] and CT [31] without relying on BMD prediction. However, both CT and MRI were not readily accessible in resource-limited settings, whereas X-ray was common and often the primary diagnostic imaging. Studies had reported that the performance of AI in classifying osteoporosis from X-rays was lower than classification based on AI-predicted BMD [22, 35, 36]. This discrepancy may be attributed to information loss compounded by small sample sizes.

The advancement of AI-powered approaches for predicting BMD from routine X-rays has the potential to transform osteoporosis screening, particularly in resource-limited settings. Conventional DXA scans, the current gold standard, are often cost-prohibitive and require specialized equipment, limiting access in developing countries. AI, however, can analyze readily available X-rays, significantly reducing the economic and logistical barriers to osteoporosis detection. This could lead to earlier diagnoses, enabling timely interventions like medication and lifestyle modifications to prevent debilitating fractures, a major healthcare concern for aging populations.

The benefits extend beyond low-resource environments. In well-equipped settings, AI analysis of X-rays can serve as a first-line screening tool. By efficiently identifying patients at high risk for osteoporosis, AI can optimize healthcare resource allocation. Doctors can prioritize DXA scans for those most likely to require them, streamlining the diagnostic process and reducing unnecessary scans. This not only improves patient experience but also frees up valuable resources for those who need them most.

Our study’s findings must be interpreted within the context of strengths and weaknesses. One notable strength of our study is that it was a representative community-based sample rather than a hospital-based sample, which could be affected by selection bias and indication bias. Our study has a large sample size with enough X-rays taken at the same time with DXA measurements, therefore eliminating the error of time difference, which can be up to 1 year in the previous studies [18, 19, 21]. This study focused on novel architectures that had not been previously researched, such as vision transformers. However, our study used standardized acquisition techniques, which could slightly lower the performance in settings with poor image quality. Our study was conducted in a single geographical location, which may limit the generalizability to other populations. It is important to acknowledge that AI technology is still under development in this domain. Rigorous validation and clinical trials are essential to ensure accuracy and reliability before widespread adoption. Additionally, integrating AI seamlessly into existing healthcare workflows and ensuring proper training for healthcare providers are vital steps for successful implementation.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed an AI system for predicting BMD from readily available plain X-rays. Our AI model achieved a very high correlation with measured BMD, paving the way for a paradigm shift in osteoporosis screening, particularly in resource-limited settings. By leveraging existing X-ray infrastructure and eliminating the need for expensive DXA scans, AI-based BMD prediction can significantly improve access to early diagnosis and risk assessment for osteoporosis. This can empower timely interventions and preventative measures, ultimately leading to improved bone health outcomes and a reduction in fracture risk on a global scale. Further research should focus on implementing this technology within clinical workflows and exploring its integration with existing diagnostic tools for a comprehensive osteoporosis management strategy.
