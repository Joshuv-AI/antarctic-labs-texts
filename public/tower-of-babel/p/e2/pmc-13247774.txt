Sex disparity in liver cancer recurrence—from bench to bedside
Authors: Takeshi Yamada, Kay Uehara, Hiroshi Yoshida
Published: 2026

Liver cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies globally, with postoperative recurrence occurring in up to 70% of patients within 5 years after curative resection (1). A striking and consistent clinical observation is that male patients experience significantly higher recurrence rates compared to females, even after adjusting for tumor stage and liver function (2). Traditional explanations focused on sex hormones, yet clinical trials of hormone-modulating therapies have uniformly failed to improve survival, revealing a critical gap in mechanistic understanding (3).

The perioperative period represents a unique immunological vulnerability, as surgical ischemia-reperfusion injury can create a permissive microenvironment for tumor seeding. Zhang et al. focused on the sex disparity in postoperative recurrence of liver cancer [hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC)], demonstrating that the male-specific Sex-determining Region Y (SRY) gene promotes the formation of an immunosuppressive liver microenvironment, thereby facilitating tumor recurrence and metastasis (4). Through analyses of clinical data and experiments using mouse models, they revealed that SRY induces the secretion of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) via the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, which recruits polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) and suppresses CD8+ T cells, ultimately increasing the risk of cancer recurrence. Mechanistic innovation beyond hormonal paradigms

What distinguishes this work from previous correlative studies is the mechanistic depth. The authors have identified the SRY gene as a molecular driver of this sex disparity, moving beyond the traditional focus on sex hormones that has dominated the field for decades. This is particularly noteworthy given the disappointing clinical outcomes of hormone-targeted therapies in liver cancer (5).

Previous mechanistic studies have established SRY’s oncogenic role in HCC. Liu et al. demonstrated in liver-specific transgenic mice that SRY overexpression significantly promoted diethylnitrosamine-induced hepatocarcinogenesis, with elevated SRY expression found in approximately 84% of male patient HCC samples. SRY activation drives hepatocarcinogenesis through SOX9 and PDGFRα/PI3K/Akt and c-Myc/CyclinD1 pathways (6). Xue et al. provided clinical evidence that high SRY expression correlates with poor patient survival and enhanced HCC cell migration and invasion, establishing SRY as a prognostic biomarker (7). The current study by Zhang et al. extends these findings by elucidating a distinct mechanism—SRY-mediated recruitment of immunosuppressive MDSCs—specifically relevant to perioperative tumor recurrence.

The immunological landscape: SRY-orchestrated immunosuppression

The elegance of this study lies in its systematic dissection of the immunological landscape. Using hepatocyte-specific SRY transgenic and knockout mouse models, the authors demonstrate that SRY expression orchestrates an immunosuppressive microenvironment specifically during ischemia-reperfusion injury—a clinically relevant scenario that inevitably accompanies liver resection and transplantation.

The identified mechanism is both sophisticated and clinically relevant: SRY promotes NF-κB-dependent secretion of CXCL1, recruiting polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs, also known as granulocytic MDSCs) to the liver (8). These immunosuppressive cells subsequently inhibit CD8+ T cell function through mechanisms including arginase-1 and reactive oxygen species production, creating a permissive niche for circulating tumor cells to seed and establish micrometastases (9). This represents a surgical pre-metastatic niche concept specifically applicable to the perioperative setting.

Translational validation in human liver grafts

The study’s strength lies in its translational approach, validating murine findings in human liver transplant donor samples. Ten paired liver tissue samples were obtained from healthy living liver transplantation donors between January and December 2023. Liver biopsies were collected before transplantation (baseline control) and at 2 hours after portal vein reperfusion. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated that SRY expression was significantly upregulated in male donor livers after reperfusion compared with pre-reperfusion baseline. The correlation between SRY expression, PMN-MDSC infiltration (CD11b+ cells), and CD8+ T cell reduction in human liver grafts after reperfusion provides crucial clinical validation of the proposed mechanism. This paired-sample design and human data strengthen the translational potential of targeting the SRY-CXCL1-MDSC axis in clinical trials.

Critical considerations and limitations

Several important considerations warrant careful evaluation. First, while the study focuses on early post-ischemia/reperfusion changes, tumor recurrence is a prolonged process, and investigating whether SRY-induced immunosuppression persists throughout the critical metastatic seeding window would enhance clinical applicability. Second, given the heterogeneity of HCC and ICC, stratified analyses based on etiology (viral vs. non-viral HCC, perihilar vs. intrahepatic ICC) could identify key subgroup differences for patient stratification. Third, although hormonal mechanisms are de-emphasized, SRY expression and androgen receptor signaling likely interact synergistically, particularly through convergent NF-κB activation pathways and testosterone's known effects on MDSC function (10). Additionally, while propensity score matching addresses many confounders, unmeasured surgical variables—procedural complexity, Pringle maneuver duration, blood loss, and immunosuppressive therapy in transplant settings—may still influence outcomes. Finally, identifying female-specific protective mechanisms could open new therapeutic avenues. Future research should explore estrogen’s influence on MDSC recruitment, X-chromosomal immunoregulatory genes, and sex-based differences in adaptive immunity that may contribute to differential recurrence risks.

Clinical applications and future directions

This study supports the integration of sex-stratified surveillance and treatment strategies into hepatobiliary oncology—a principle increasingly recognized across cancer disciplines but still underutilized in liver surgery. Several clinical implications emerge from the findings. First, surgical techniques aimed at minimizing ischemia-reperfusion injury—such as ischemic preconditioning, intermittent clamping, or selective vascular occlusion—may confer particular benefit to male patients by reducing myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) recruitment. Prospective evaluations of sex-specific outcomes based on surgical approach are warranted. Second, the elucidation of the CXCL1-MDSC axis offers a promising therapeutic target. Agents that inhibit the CXCL1/C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CXCR2) pathway (e.g., SX-682, AZD5069) or deplete MDSCs (e.g., all-trans retinoic acid, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors) could be trialed perioperatively in high-risk male patients (8,11). However, perioperative CXCR2 inhibition requires careful consideration of safety concerns, including potential impairment of neutrophil-mediated wound healing and increased infection risk. Optimal timing of administration—whether preoperative loading, intraoperative initiation, or early postoperative dosing during the critical 48–72-hour ischemia-reperfusion window—remains to be determined through dose-escalation studies. Early-phase clinical trials should prioritize establishing safety profiles and pharmacokinetic parameters before evaluating efficacy endpoints.

Unanswered questions and research priorities

While this study provides crucial mechanistic insights, several questions remain that should guide future research. First, it remains to be determined whether pharmacological inhibition of SRY or blockade of CXCL1 can effectively prevent tumor recurrence in preclinical models. Demonstrating therapeutic efficacy in intervention studies, including dose-response relationships, would enhance translational relevance and support clinical trial design. Although the authors demonstrated MDSC-mediated T cell suppression in murine models, the specific molecular mechanisms—such as arginase-1 activity, inducible nitric oxide synthase, or PD-L1 expression—remain to be elucidated. Furthermore, functional validation in human systems is essential to confirm the translational relevance of the SRY-CXCL1-MDSC axis in clinical settings. Moreover, the role of the Y chromosome in cancer biology is complex; recent studies have highlighted contributions from other Y-linked genes such as KDM5D and UTY, as well as age-related mosaic loss of the Y chromosome (12). How SRY interacts with these elements in modulating immune function and tumor recurrence remains unknown. It is also important to consider whether the SRY-MDSC axis contributes to sex disparities in other solid organ malignancies, especially those influenced by surgical ischemia/reperfusion injury, such as pancreatic cancer, colorectal liver metastases, or renal cell carcinoma. Comparative cross-cancer studies could reveal whether this represents a broader, conserved mechanism. Finally, potential ethnic and genetic variations in SRY expression, CXCL1/CXCR2 polymorphisms, or MDSC biology may affect the extent of sex-based differences in recurrence risk. These variables could be pivotal for informing future precision medicine strategies.

Conclusions: toward precision hepatobiliary surgery

Zhang et al. have identified the SRY-NF-κB-CXCL1-MDSC axis as the key mechanism driving sex disparities in postoperative liver cancer recurrence. For surgeons and medical oncologists, surgical techniques minimizing ischemia-reperfusion injury may preferentially benefit males, and perioperative CXCR2 inhibitors or MDSC-depleting agents represent actionable therapeutic targets. For researchers, priorities include therapeutic validation in preclinical intervention studies, mechanistic confirmation in human systems, assessment of applicability to other solid tumors, and biomarker development for risk stratification. For policy makers, this work demonstrates why sex-stratified analyses should be mandated in oncology trials and why research funding must prioritize mechanistic studies of chromosomal sex differences beyond hormonal paradigms. Sex is not merely a covariate but a fundamental biological variable requiring distinct therapeutic approaches in precision hepatobiliary oncology.
