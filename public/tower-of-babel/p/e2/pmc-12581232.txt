Interpretable machine learning model for predicting low birth weight in singleton pregnancies: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaojuan Wu, Qingxiang Zhao, Yong Gao, Yiyu Zhang, Linrui Xu, Xianzhu Cong, Na Sun, Fuyan Shi, Suzhen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow birth weight (LBW), defined as a newborn weighing less than 2500 g, is an increasingly significant public health concern. Exploring the risk and protective factors for LBW is getting more and more important. This study aimed to utilize predictive models to identify the critical factors associated with LBW in singleton pregnancies.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Binzhou Medical University Hospital, China, from 2022 to 2023. Singleton pregnancies with gestational age exceeding 27 weeks were included, while multiple pregnancies and fetal anomalies were excluded. Logistic regression (LR) model and four machine learning (ML) algorithms were tested(random forest, light gradient boosting machine, support vector machine, and extreme gradient boosting). The LR model was interpreted through odds ratio analysis and clinical nomogram visualization. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis was used to interpret the importance and impact of individual features on ML model.ResultsIn this cohort of 10,227 deliveries, 237 cases were classified as LBW. The XGBoost model exhibited superior performance in predicting LBW, with an AUC of 0.786 (training set) and 0.741 (test set, ) AUPRC of 0.350 and 0.186, respectively. Both LR and XGBoost model identified maternal age, gestational age, BMI, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), fetal distress as the critical factor. Additionally, a follow-up study on LBW found that LBW infants prone to significant health challenges, such as a high rate of hospitalization and the complex conditions including congenital anomalies, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) and neonatal hyperbilirubinemia.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that the LR and XGBoost model exhibited clinically meaningful predictive performance in identifying factors associated with LBW in singleton pregnancies. Pregnant women with a gestational age of less than 37 weeks, a gestational BMI below 18 kg/m², maternal age under than 25 years, and maternal comorbidities such as HDP or fetal distress are at higher risk of delivering LBW infants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08318-0.

Background

Birth weight (BW) is a critical determinant of neonatal health. Newborns weights less than 2500 g are classified as low birth weight (LBW) [1, 2]. LBW is associated with increased infant mortality and a range of adverse events [3], contributing significantly to the global burden of disease. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), epidemiological studies have demonstrated that infants with a birth weight below 2500 g face approximately a 20 times greater risk of death than those with normal birth weights [4]. Notably, LBW is more prevalent in developing countries than in developed ones and is linked to a wide range of health complications [5, 6]. Consequently, predicting LBW delivery is a crucial preventive action in the preconception or early stages of pregnancy.

Previous studies have identified several factors associated with LBW, including maternal age [7, 8], education level, and socioeconomic status, etc [9, 10]. However, LBW is a multi factorial issue influenced by a wide range of determinants that exhibit regional variations due to disparities in socioeconomic conditions, healthcare access, and lifestyle habits, etc. Moreover, while these studies have highlighted key factors affecting birth weight, there is a notable lack of follow-up investigations examining the outcomes of LBW infants. LBW infants frequently experience a range of complications that can adversely affect their health and development.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop the predictive model, and to identify key predictors through Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis and nomogram from a large single-center retrospective cohort dataset from China. Additionally, a follow-up study was conducted to investigate the clinical characteristics of LBW infants.

Methods

Study design

We performed a retrospective cohort study and collected data from pregnant women admitted to Binzhou Medical University Hospital in China from 2022 to 2023. The dataset comprehensively includes detailed maternal and neonatal outcomes for each delivery, systematically documented on a daily basis by hospital midwives. Participants with singleton pregnancies at a gestational age of at least 28 weeks were included in the study. The key exclusion steps were as follows: (1) multiple births (n = 458), (2) extreme prematurity (< 28 weeks gestation) or missing gestational age data (n = 37), (3) incomplete records regarding newborn weight, pregnancy history, or maternal anthropometric measurements (n = 467), and (4) macrosomic infants (n = 674). After applying these exclusions, the final analytic cohort consisted of 10,227 singleton births, including 237 low birth weight (LBW) cases (2.3%). Among the LBW infants, 101 (42.6%) required hospitalization within 28 days after birth, as shown in Fig. 1.

Definition of variables

Candidate predictors for LBW were selected based on previous studies suggesting significant factors influencing birth weight [11, 12]. The predictor variables encompassed the following categories: (1)maternal demographic characteristics, including maternal age, occupation, parity, gestational age, and gestational body mass index (BMI); (2) maternal comorbidities, such as gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), cesarean scar pregnancy (CSP), premature rupture of membranes (PROM), cervical insufficiency during pregnancy (CIC), fetal distress, uterine rupture, and hypoproteinemia; (3) newborn information, including newborn gender. According to previous researches [1, 13–16], we considered 14 potential predictor variables, with definitions and coding schemes provided in Supplementary Table S1. Binary variables (e.g., HDP, GDM, PROM) were coded as presence = 1 and absence = 0. Newborn weight was classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) standards for birth weight categories: Low birth weight (LBW < 2500 g; coded = 1), Normal birth weight (NBW: 2500–4000 g; coded = 0). Maternal age was stratified into three categories according to established reproductive risk classifications [17]: ≤25 years (young adult; code = 1), 26–35 years (peak reproductive age; code = 2), ≥ 36 years (advanced maternal age; code = 3). Parity was dichotomized according to World Health Organization (WHO) maternal health reporting standards: primiparous (code = 1) and multiparous (code = 2). Gestational age was categorized based on standard obstetric definitions: preterm (28–36+6 weeks; code = 1) and term (37–41 weeks; code = 2). Maternal BMI was categorized according to Chinese-specific gestational weight gain guidelines [18]: underweight (< 18 kg/m², code = 1), normal weight (18–28 kg/m², code = 2), and overweight (>28 kg/m², code = 3).

Data Preparation

Prior to model development, we evaluated potential multicollinearity among predictor variables by analyzing the correlation matrix (Supplementary Fig. S1), which revealed no strong correlations between any of the variables. To ensure model robustness, we employed the Generalized Variance Inflation Factor (GVIF) to assess multicollinearity among the model’s independent variables [19]. The results revealed that all GVIF values were below 2.0, suggesting that multicollinearity did not pose a significant issue in the model (Supplementary Table S2).

The comprehensive dataset, comprising 10,227 instances, was partitioned into train and test datasets. The train dataset comprised 80% of the total data, whereas the test dataset constituted the remaining 20%. To optimize model performance and mitigate overfitting, a 5-fold cross-validation strategy [20, 21] was implemented to assess model performance during the tuning process, with the training dataset partitioned using the caret package in R. The training set was randomly divided into five mutually exclusive subsets of equal size, preserving data distribution. In each fold, four subsets were used for model training, while the remaining subset served as the validation set [20]. This process was repeated five times, with each subset serving as the validation set exactly once. Model performance metrics were averaged across all folds to ensure robustness against variability in partitioning, as.

detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

Model development and assessment

Logistic regression (LR) model and four machine learning (ML) algorithms— Random Forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) were employed to predict risk factors for LBW in the singleton pregnancies. To complement this, we developed a nomogram based on a basic LR model using the selected predictors to measure overall risk [22, 23]. For the best ML model, feature significance and contribution were interpreted using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), based on the absolute values and signs of SHAP values [24–26]. In addition, we applied SHAP analysis to provide detailed, individualized explanations of how the model predicts LBW risk for each specific newborn.

Model performance was evaluated using metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC), balanced accuracy and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). All metrics ranged from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better performance [19]. A calibration plot was employed to assess the consistency between predicted and actual birth weight categories, thereby evaluating the reliability of the reported prediction rates.

Statistical analysis

In this study, maternal and neonatal characteristics were compared using Chi-square tests for categorical variables. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (percentages), while continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD). Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05. Data were extracted using Microsoft structured query language (SQL) server management studio version 2008, and all statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results of logistic regression model and nomogram

We employed logistic regression model to predict risk factors for LBW births in the training dataset. In the LR Model, LBW was found to be significantly associated with six factors: maternal age, gestational age, BMI, HDP, CIC, fetal distress. The results identified HDP, CIC, fetal distress etc as the risk factors and maternal age (≥ 25 years), gestational age (≥ 37 weeks), overweight BMI as the protective factors against LBW births.

Specifically, maternal HDP, CIC, and fetal distress were linked to increase odds of LBW, with ORs of 2.652 (95% CI: 1.544–4.358), 4.386 (95% CI: 1.486–11.153), and 2.555 (95% CI: 1.542–4.068), respectively. In contrast, maternal age between 25 and 35 years, maternal age between 35 and 53 years, gestational age between 37 and 42 weeks, overweight BMI were statistically significantly linked to reduced odds of LBW, with ORs of 0.451 (95% CI: 0.294–0.710), 0.599 (95% CI: 0.367–0.990), 0.019 (95% CI: 0.012–0.030), and 0.468 (95% CI: 0.267–0.845), respectively (Fig. 2, Table2).Fig. 2Forest plot of LR analysis showing odds ratios (ORs) for LBW risk factors. Model interpretation

In addition, we applied SHAP analysis to provide detailed, individualized explanations of how the XGBoost model predicts LBW risk. Figure 8 A illustrate the feature importance for the XGBoost models. SHAP summary plot of XGBoost model presents the feature importance, providing an intuitive illustration of feature significance. We identified the top eight significant features with gestational age emerging as the most critical factor. Additionally, other notable factors were also fitted in the model, including BMI, maternal age, newborn gender, occupation, HDP, fetal distress, and GDM. The key predictors identified through SHAP analysis were consistent across the XGBoost and LightGBM models (Supplementary Fig. S3). The Y-axis of the figure represents individual features, while the X-axis represents the SHAP values. Each point on the graph corresponds to an observation, with its position along the X-axis reflecting the feature’s influence on the model’s output for a specific patient [29]. When multiple data points converge at the same X-axis position, they aggregate to form a denser region. A positive SHAP value indicates a positive influence to the predicted outcome, while a negative SHAP value suggests a negative contribution. The SHAP explanations for the XGBoost model allowed us to systematically to rank predictors of population-based LBW risk. The color gradient visualizes represents the SHAP values, where purple indicates lower SHAP values and yellow indicates higher SHAP values. Specifically, purple dots denote that the feature values had a negative influence on the predicted outcome, thereby reducing the predicted value with its growth, whereas yellow dots signify a positive influence, thereby increasing the predicted value with its growth [30].Fig. 8SHAP summary plot of the XGBoost model for predicting LBW. Discussion

Our study conducted a retrospective cohort study of singleton pregnancies and newborns in China, to utilize predictive models to identify the critical factors associated with LBW. The XGBoost model showed the best overall performance with high AUC of 0.786 in training sets, along with favorable balanced accuracy and AUPRC. The study identified that strongly influence birth weight outcomes included maternal demographic characteristics and maternal comorbidities, which was in accordance with previous findings [31, 32]. Nomogram and SHAP analysis identified maternal age, gestational age, BMI, HDP and fetal distress as critical factors associated with LBW. In addition, we performed a short-term follow-up study of LBW infants that the main neonatal complications included congenital anomalies, NRDS and neonatal hyperbilirubinemia.

According to the AUPRC curves, the RF model showed higher performance in the training set but lower performance in terms of AUC and balanced accuracy. In contrast, the XGBoost model exhibited stable and efficient performance across all datasets, owing to its integration of weighted decision trees and a gradient boosting framework refined through iterative optimization [19]. This outcome aligns with findings of prior studies [33, 34]. Although all models performed well in in terms of AUC values, their performance in terms of AUPRC was notably lower, ranging from 0.182 to 0.390. The AUPRC indicates limited generalizability in identifying the minority class (LBW), which consistent with established challenges in imbalanced learning scenarios [28, 30], and reflects the extreme class imbalance between NBW and LBW cases (1:42 ratio). The models generally perform better on the majority class (NBW) compared to the minority class (LBW). While this level of accuracy may not be adequate for standalone diagnostic use, it represents a 15-fold improvement over random guessing.

This study underscored that gestational age was the most significant predictor, which was consistent to previous studies, highlighting the critical role of gestational age in fetal growth and development [14, 35]. Specifically, gestational age ≥ 37 weeks was linked to reduced odds of LBW. It highlights the critical importance of interventions aimed at extending gestation and preventing preterm births. Although gestational age and birth weight are highly correlated, gestational age was selected as a predictive variable. This selection aligns with the methodology used by Amene Ranjbar et al. [13], who showed the predictive significance of gestational age in their model for LBW. To ensure reliable statistical interpretation, we conducted multicollinearity diagnostics and stratified analyses to account for potential confounding effects, as supported by the following key observations. On the other side, we performed stratified analyses separately for the preterm (28–36 weeks) and term (37–41 weeks) cohorts using XGBoost (Supplementary Table S4). The stratified models showed moderate discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.582 (95% CI: 0.484–0.680) for preterm births and 0.560 (95% CI: 0.514–0.607) for term births. Notably, the non-stratified model showed significantly improved discrimination (AUC = 0.786, 95% CI: 0.747–0.824), indicating the important role of gestational age in predicting BW. These findings suggest that predicting outcomes remains a significant challenge across various gestational age strata.This study’s methodological refinements — including stratified analyses and multicollinearity assessment — improved the statistical robustness and clinical relevance of our findings. Future large-scale validation studies will include expanded stratified analyses to further validate these findings across diverse obstetric populations.

Meanwhile, the study highlights that pregnancy at a younger age (< 25 years) is associated with an increased risk of LBW. Maternal age of 25–35 years was linked to reduced odds of LBW, relative to the younger pregnant women (< 25 years). The study elucidated that mothers under the age of 20 are more prone to immaturity in uterine vascularization, which can result in premature delivery or placental insufficiency [7, 8]. The younger pregnant women increases the risk of delivering LBW infants compared to mothers of similar nutritional status in older age groups [7, 8]. Our results showed that gestational underweight BMI (< 18 kg/m2) was associated with an increased risk of delivering infants weighing less than 2500 g. Specifically, gestational normal weight BMI and overweight BMI was associated with lower odds of LBW, compared with underweight BMI. It is imperative to concentrate on precise prenatal management, enhance nutritional interventions, and provide comprehensive fertility education for young pregnant women in order to decrease the incidence of early pregnancies [36].

These findings align with established evidence demonstrating that HDP and fetal distress contribute to aberrant fetal growth trajectories, likely through placental insufficiency and chronic hypoxia [13]. The observed associations between HDP, fetal distress, and LBW further underscore the multifactorial etiology of fetal growth restriction, implicating both maternal vascular pathology and fetal adaptive responses to adverse intrauterine environments [37]. The study highlighted the clinical imperative for enhanced antenatal surveillance in complicated pregnancies [37]. Early detection of HDP through systematic and serial blood pressure monitoring, targeted cervical length assessments in high-risk populations [38], and continuous fetal heart rate monitoring in cases of suspected fetal distress could facilitate timely interventions to mitigate the risk of LBW [15, 39].

The short-term follow-up study of LBW infants identified that LBW infants encounter significant health challenges, including a high rate of hospitalization and neonatal complications. Notably, 83.17% of LBW infants were admitted on the day of birth, highlighting the urgent health challenges faced by these infants. The observation that 82.18% of admitted newborns exhibited congenital anomalies highlights the complex interplay between gestational factors and fetal development [14, 40]. The most commonly identified congenital anomalies were atrial septal defects and patent ductus arteriosus. The detection rate of ventricular septal defects has seen a significant increase, potentially attributable to advancements in medical imaging and neonatal screening [41, 42].

The comparative analysis of clinical characteristics between VLBW and LBW neonates revealed significant differences in the composition ratios of NRDS, neonatal hypoproteinemia, neonatal anemia, pulmonary hypertension, and gestational age. The increased incidence of NRDS and pulmonary hypertension correlates with the immaturity of pulmonary surfactant synthesis and vascular development observed in neonates with LBW and reduced gestational age [43]. Notably, the predominance of preterm births (< 37 weeks) in the VLBW group likely exacerbates these risks, as organ immaturity intensifies metabolic and respiratory challenges [14, 44].

Consequently, during pregnancy, it is imperative to focus on the risk factors associated with LBW, such as maternal age, gestational age, BMI, HDP, fetal distress. Recognizing these risk factors and implementing proactive measures before conception are essential. This study underscores the critical importance of optimizing perinatal care through a comprehensive, multifaceted approach that includes accurate gestational age assessment and preterm birth prevention via enhanced antenatal monitoring. Essential interventions should include systematic blood pressure monitoring to enable early detection of hypertensive disorders, targeted cervical length assessments for high-risk populations, and continuous fetal heart rate monitoring in cases of suspected fetal distress. Furthermore, the implementation of structured prenatal management protocols, nutritional optimization programs, and comprehensive reproductive health education for young pregnant women is critical to reducing early pregnancy rates and improving neonatal outcomes. Collectively, these strategies address modifiable risk factors via timely clinical interventions and population-level preventive initiatives.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, this was conducted at a single center, retrospective study. The absence of data from multiple centers reduces the ability to account for the variability in healthcare practices and outcomes. Second, the study was a cross-sectional analysis, limiting the capacity to perform longitudinal analyses that explore the changing trends between birth weight and parental predictors. We recognize that the dataset used in this research lacked some socioeconomic [45] and paternal factors [24], which could be relevant to assessing birth weight outcomes. Third, the pronounced class imbalance in the dataset highlights the inherent challenges in predicting LBW. To derive more robust and reliable conclusions, it is essential to conduct multi-center studies across diverse populations, incorporate a broader range of relevant factors, and explore advanced analytical approaches designed specifically for rare outcomes. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes should be undertaken to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the factors involved.

Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed multiple factors to identify their impact on the birth weight of newborns. To achieve this objective, we employed four ML models and LR models to develop predictive frameworks incorporating maternal demographic characteristics and maternal comorbidities. Our analysis revealed that maternal age, gestational age, BMI, HDP, and fetal distress are critical factors associated with LBW. These findings highlight the necessity for targeted interventions addressing these complexities and underscore the importance of future research aimed at developing strategies to mitigate modifiable risk factors such as gestational age and maternal health conditions, thereby improving outcomes for LBW newborns
