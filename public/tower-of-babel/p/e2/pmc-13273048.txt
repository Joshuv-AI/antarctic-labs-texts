Digitalised phenotyping of pepper (Capsicum spp.) using effective RGB imaging and optimised camera positioning
Authors: Eric Opoku Mensah, Jiseon Song, Hyeonseok Oh, Alebel Mekuriaw Abebe, Yong Suk Chung, Jeongho Baek, Sheikh Mansoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent technological advancements employ imaging techniques to examine the morphological, physiological, and genetic differences among plant accessions, enhancing precision and productivity. High-throughput phenotyping serves as an essential method for selecting traits and reducing errors tied to manual data collection. However, the effects of camera-to-object distance in imaging acquisition for plant phenomics have received less attention. We analyzed the imaging parameters that define well the morphological characteristics of pepper and the effects of camera-to-object distance on the imaging of plant growth, leaf dimensions, fruit, and seed characteristics. Three camera-to-object distances (0.8, 1.0, and 1.2 m) were studied for the vegetative stage, and six camera-to-object distances (0.35–0.85 m) were used for the reproductive stages. The results demonstrated that imaging parameters such as Major (the longest line that can be drawn within the leaf) and Minor (the shortest line perpendicular to the major axis) are more effective for assessing canopy spread, while imaging Height provides a strong correlation (r = 0.9) for actual plant height measurement. An optimal camera-to-object distance of 0.8 m yielded better correlations for vegetative traits across all pepper genotypes, likely due to resolution factors at different growth stages. For fruit and seed traits, shorter distances of 0.55 m and 0.65 m were suitable. Additionally, the weights of fresh and dry fruit correlated highly with image area (r = 0.94 and 0.89, respectively, at 0.55 m). The studied pepper genotypes exhibited distinct seed characteristics, including variations in Roundness, Solidity, and Circularity. The imaging approach can accurately capture various plant characteristics and has the potential to replace traditional methods for assessing plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46601-y.

Introduction

Pepper (Capsicum spp.), one of the Solanaceae family, is diverse, including domesticated, semi-domesticated, and wild relatives1. The diversity comprises diploid genotypes with 12-chromosome number (2n = 2x = 24), 13 chromosome number (2n = 2x = 26), and tetraploid species (2n = 4x = 48), a wild form of the cultivated species2. About 31 species of the genus are documented, including 26 wild and 5 cultivated3. The cultivated species include C. annuum L., C. frutescens L., C. chinense Jacq., C. baccatum L., and C. pubescens Ruiz & Pav., along with other related complex genotypes, which arise from varying levels of cross-pollination among the species4–6.

Pepper domestication and selection have enhanced yield and resistance to biotic and abiotic stress factors over the years7. Traits such as vegetative (including growth habit, leaf architecture, and canopy spread) and fruit characteristics (fruit shape, size, color, pungency, seeds per fruit, and wall thickness) have been used for selection for breeding or production2. Trait selections have, over the years, been through visual assessments, conventional breeding programs, or, in a detailed method, at the DNA level using molecular markers8–10. However, the lack of efficient methods to identify and quantify some specific traits, such as growth and yield-related traits, is one of the major gaps in pepper breeding3,11. The gaps are associated with challenges involving climate change-related scenarios, pests and disease, the demands and preferences of consumers, and food security12. Nevertheless, pepper production continues to support many households, especially in Africa and Asia13,14. To ensure the sustainability and biological diversity of pepper, new and modern breeding programs are important to meet climate-related challenges and consumer demands.

Pepper imaging can be a way out11,15–17. The technique, known as high-throughput phenotyping, plays an important role in trait selection and reduces errors in manual measurements18,19. Imaging is non-destructive and combines a high-throughput algorithm software to detect individual plant parts for analysis20,21. For example, the imaging approach has been used to study the performances of modelled plants such as Arabidopsis, Oryza, and Triticum17,18,22, plant diseases23, or to identify the water and temperature relations between some crops and their environment16,24 and diversity among some vegetables20,25. In view, different imaging software such as ImageJ for whole plant analysis, Tomato Analyzer for tomato image analysis, LeafAnalser and LAMINA for leaf shapes, and SmartGrain for seed analysis, are developed to enhance automated phenotyping of plants26–30.

Though efforts are made31,32, pepper imaging is at the basic stage3,11, as some challenges, such as plant architecture and overlapping of fruits and flowers, are yet to be solved33. Again, challenges with the imaging of some fruit traits, such as size, shape, surface appearance, and color, are not fully solved. Over the years, a group of researchers33–35 did extensive work to bridge the gap, but rather used the Tomato Analyzer software, which may have calibration problems for pepper.

Furthermore, many factors, such as sharpness, focus, illumination influence, image angle, and distance between the object and sensor, influence the image acquisition and analysis, especially for RGB imaging of pepper36,37. More especially, spatial resolution is important for RGB-camera sensors to provide accurate information on the plants, and the distance between the object and the camera plays a major role38. Different camera distances have been used and recommended. For example, for a high-resolution image of ground maize at three-to-five leaf stages with faster-RCNN object detection algorithms, 0.3 cm and 0.6 cm image ground sampling distances were tested at an above-ground altitude ranging between 15.0 and 22.0 m using three different RGB cameras, Sony Alpha, DJI X7, and DJI Mavic 2 pro39. In another development, an enhanced sensor resolution and precision were achieved at a closed target using an RGB-D test at different light and distance levels40. For research on the use of smart sprayers for agricultural herbicide applications, the effects of camera selection (Canon T6 DSLR, LG G6 smartphone, and Logitech C920 webcam) and target distance (0.57 m, 0.98 m, and 1.29 m) on detection accuracy were tested on RGB images, and significant differences existed based on the camera selection and camera distances41. Other camera-to-object-distances, such as 1.0 m to study the effects of camera-shooting angle for 3D planting modelling with a multi-camera photography system42, 1.0 m with an RGB camera, and 0.8 m with a thermal camera43 have been reported. Furthermore, as low as 0. 27 m camera-to-object distance for leaf wetness measurements using laser-induced light reflection and RGB imaging44 and then between 0. 25–0.40 cm for Time of Flight (active sensors) cameras and 0.75–0.85 cm for stereo vision cameras (passive sensors)45 have also been used in other projects.

Those plant imaging studies do not show optimal or standard camera-to-object distances for a robust data acquisition for specific plant traits such as size, color and shape, especially for pepper, and how variations in distance influence plant imaging during growth. Therefore, studies on valid protocols are needed to standardize the camera-to-object distance in plant phenomics to increase accuracy, consistency and reproducibility of plant traits. These attempts will increase precision in image acquisition over the traditional system, particularly in addressing the variability in the imaging environment and ensuring consistency in the imaging process. For example, the introduction of automated focus adjustment, dynamic distance calibration, and 3D spatial mapping could maintain uniform image scale and clarity to enhance accurate quantitative traits extraction and interpretation across time points of plant phenology. Again, the various imaging parameters (For example, Feret, Major, Minor, and other imaging parameters) may have limitations to project specific actual plant traits (including growth in height, leaf area, fruit, and seed characteristics). However, these gaps are not well studied. This work provides a novel contribution by assessing the impacts of camera-to-object distance on imaging outputs, thereby identifying distance-sensitive biases and proposing a more reliable imaging protocol. This primary information will advance the methodological rigor in RGB imaging for a robust data acquisition in plant phenomics.

The study aims to determine the optimal camera-to-object distance for high-throughput RGB imaging of pepper plants at various growth stages, considering factors such as resolution and camera angle. By conducting a simple experiment, we intend to establish the best imaging setup to accurately analyze plant development, leaf morphology, fruits, and seed traits across four pepper accessions, three Capsicum annuum with different colors, and one Capsicum frutescens. Finally, our goal is to provide foundational insights that support advanced research into plant growth mechanisms and traits using high-throughput imaging systems, advancing precision and digital agriculture practices in open-field environments.

Materials and methods

Plant management

To maintain uniform color during crop image acquisition, the experiment was conducted in a greenhouse where temperature and light conditions were kept consistent. Four genotypes of pepper, including Kori, Caball, Oranzela (Capsicum annuum L.), and Red Pepper (Capsicum frutescens L.), were collected from the National Gene Bank, South Korea. The selection of the genotypes was only based on availability. The seeds were sown in a 50-cell seedling tray with one seed per cell. Ten seeds were sown per genotype. After two weeks of sowing, the seedlings were transferred into a 19 cm round seed pot filled with commercial soil (Bio-Sangto No.1 Premium, Farm Han Nong Co., Ltd., Seoul, The Republic of Korea). Five plants were kept for each genotype in a greenhouse at 25 ± 3 °C air temperature and 78% relative humidity. The length of photoperiod was automated and was adjustable between 8 and 16 h (approximately 16 h during the experimental period), providing an average of 300 µmol m-2 s− 1 photosynthetic photon flux density. After four and eight weeks of transplanting, NPK 15.15.15 at 0.45 g per plant was applied. During the fruit set, thrips (Frankliniella occindentalis (Pergande), order Thysanoptera) were spotted on the plants. The insects were checked with a 1:1 formulation of Captain (Fluxanethamide emulsion, Captain Insecticides, Gujarat, India) and bullet (Pleomethorine Lighuid, IndiaMart) every three days for two weeks.

Image acquisition

Imaging of the vegetative stage of the plants started two weeks after planting and was repeated weekly until the 6th week. Two imaging views were used, the top and the side views (Fig. 1), using a Sony camera (Sony ILCE-6000 with Sony E3.5/30 Macro lens, AVCHD 24.3 megapixels) at a 30 mm camera focal length and on an average of 23.5 × 15.6 sensor size. The camera settings included shutter speed at 1/8, aperture at 8.0, and light sensitivity at 500, but these settings were adjusted depending on the climatic conditions of the day. The camera tripod was fixed during measurements, and its position was adjusted according to plant growth for comparison. All the plants were used for the imaging and the exposure settings of the camera were held constant throughout the imaging process to minimize variability.

For the top view, the camera position above the plants was set at 0.6 m during the first three weeks and was later raised step-wise to 1.0 m as the plants grew. The imaging system utilized a Sony ILCE-6000 equipped with an APS-C sensor (23.5 × 15.6 mm) with an effective resolution of approximately 24.3 megapixels, resulting in images of 6000 × 4000 pixels and an estimated pixel size of approximately 3.9 μm. The camera-to-object distance was measured from the optical center of the camera lens to the central plane of the target object (e.g., leaves, fruits, and seeds), ensuring consistency across samples and clearly defining the imaging geometry. The camera was mounted on a stable support to preserve alignment and viewing angle, while the plant object positions were marked and maintained throughout the imaging process. A fixed white balance setting was used to prevent color shifts between images. Camera exposure parameters (shutter speed, aperture, and ISO) were kept constant during imaging to ensure exposure consistency. No post-acquisition brightness normalization was applied, as all images were captured under controlled lighting conditions. Imaging was conducted under controlled illumination to minimize shadows and reflections, using both top and side light-emitting diode (LED) grow lights with stable intensity and fixed positioning. This lighting setup ensured uniform brightness and color consistency across all images. In total, 100 images were acquired for the top view (4 cultivars × 5 plants × 6 weeks).

For the side view, three different distances between the plants and the camera (0.8 m, 1.0 m, and 1.2 m) were maintained for two angled images, 90 ° and 180 °. The three camera-to-object distances were selected based on the background information from Elsayed et al.42, Lu et al.43, and Xia et al.46. The camera, positioned at 1.0 m from the ground, was aligned parallel to the plant, forming a 90-degree angle with both the ground and the plant. A color checker with various color patches was included, and through color correction, identical color values were maintained across the images. The color checker also helped to convert image units from pixels to millimeters and later, to centimeters for further analysis. Six hundred side-view images were taken throughout the experimental time (4 cultivars x 5 plants x 2 imaging views x 5 weeks x 3 object-camera distance). A light-blue background was used for all the imaging as it is shown to provide better contrast for a segmentation process47.

After five weeks, three leaves per plant were harvested and imaged at four different camera-to-object distances, 0.55, 0.65, 0.75, and 0.85 m. This was repeated after the first fruit harvest. In total, 160 leaf images were taken (4 cultivars x 5 plants x 2 imaging times x 4 imaging distances). Each leaf image had three leaf samples, making a total of 480 individual leaf images. At the reproductive stage, between 2 and 3 fruits were harvested per plant and imaged at five different camera distances, 0.45, 0.55, 0.65, 0.75 and 0.85 m. About 60 individual fruit images were taken. After the whole fruit imaging, the seeds per fruit were dried at room temperature for 5 days and also imaged. About sixty seed images from the individual fruits were also targeted, and 1720 individual seed data points were collected. On average, 105 ± 50 seeds were extracted per fruit from Caball, 58 ± 31 from Kori, 142 ± 63 from Oranzela, and 38 ± 20 from Red pepper genotypes.

The image format was collected in RAW and calibrated using the X-Rite Color Checker as a color reference. The calibrated images were converted to JPEG for image processing.

Image processing

The processing of the images was based on the algorithms from the ImageJ program using an extension script (macron created locally). Two folders, named input and output, were created and used for the analysis. Images for analysis were saved in the input folder. When the image software was launched, the script and the images of the plant were dragged into ImageJ, and the image segmentations followed. The background noise was separated using the YUV model in the Color Threshold window in the Adjust command of the Image window (Fig. 2). On average, the Y was set at 0 and 255, U at 0 and 132 (which would vary across the plant organs, but we focused on background color rather than adjusting individual object thresholds), and then V at 0 and 255 to separate the background noise. A line picker was used to draw a line from one end to the other end (a 10 mm mark) of one of the color checkers of the scale bar to calibrate the units from pixels to millimeters. On average, the distances in pixel to SI unit scale calibration were maintained at 94.02 pixels/10 mm for 0.8 m, 77.35 pixels/10 mm for the 1.0 m and 67.01 pixels/10 mm for the 1.2 m camera-to-object distance for the side view imaging. For the top view imaging of all the plant parts, the scale calibration ranged from 324.00 pixels/10 mm for the 0. 45 m to 164 pixels/10 mm for the 0. 85 m camera-to-object distances. The color checker on the scale bar which was closer to the plant object of interest was always selected for the scale setting. After the calibrations, a measurement area was selected using the rectangular or oval picker, and the background color was adjusted to blue. A limited object size of 300 mm for plant analysis and 1 mm for seeds and fruits was set to exclude other background particles that may be wrongly picked for analysis. After these changes, the script was run on the data. A similar application was used for the side views and the reproductive parts.

Image analysis processes of the plants using ImageJ software.

Output from the image J program, including Height, Feret, Major, Minor, Width and Area, was further analyzed using the R statistical software version 4.4.0. A correlation was established between manual measurements and imaging data to identify which of the traits from the imaging data would explain the manual measurements well.

Manual measurements

During every week of image acquisition, plant height, stem diameter, and canopy spread were measured manually (Fig. 1). Plant height was measured from the soil surface to the highest part of the plant. Stem diameter was measured 2 cm from the soil surface, while canopy spread was measured from the widest part of the top-view of the plant. Again, leaf area was measured with a leaf area meter (Licor, Model LI-3100 Area Meter) while leaf length and width were measured with a meter rule. Fruit width was measured with a vernier caliper, while fruit length was measured with a meter rule. After imaging, the seeds were extracted at full ripening and dried for 5 days at room temperature and weighed. The individual fruits were also cut into pieces and then oven dried at 70 °C to a constant weight and weighed.

Data analysis

For the vegetative studies of top view and side view, a correlation analysis was used to identify the best image parameter to study plant height and canopy spread. For the distance effect of the side view, a repeated-measure analysis was employed using the linear mixed effects model in R version 4.4.048. Genotype and distance were used as the fixed factors for leaves and fruits, while the number of plants and leaves or fruits were used as the random effects. For which parameter to measure, and based on which actual measurements, a correlation analysis was performed using specific distances, thereby separating the data into respective distances and correlating the actual measurements with the image parameters. For seed analysis, the responses of different genotypes were studied through the analysis of variances using the Agricolae package49. The normality of the data was checked using the normal distribution plots, quantile-quantile plots, and histogram distribution plots. For fruit analysis, Area, Minor, and fruit height were power transformed, while for seeds analysis, Solidity was also power transformed. Circularity and Roundness were square-root transformed, while the Aspect Ratio was log-transformed to determine significant differences. The mean separation was done using the Tukey Honest Significant Differences (Tukey HSD) at the 0.05 probability level.

Results

Vegetative—top view

We compared Height, Feret, Major, Minor, Width, and Area from the imaging analysis with the canopy diameter measured with a meter rule, a correlation coefficient ranging from 0.79 to 0.84 and RMSE between 6.50 and 73.51 were identified (Fig. 3). The Major (R = 84, RMSE = 10.37) and the Feret parameters (R = 0.82, RMSE = 6.50) showed the highest correlation and the least RMSE values while Perimeter (R = 79, RSME = 73.51) showed the least correlation and highest RMSE with the actual canopy diameter.

Vegetative—side view

For the side-view imaging, plant height from imaging was similar to the actual height measurement, with a 0.90 correlation and 10.64 RMSE. Unlike the top view, the side view canopy spread correlated with Minor than Major (Fig. 5). Generally, the correlation of actual plant height with the imaging parameters ranged from 0.7 to 0.9. For canopy spread, the correlation coefficient was between 0.7 and 0.87.

Vegetative—leaf parameters

Highly significant differences existed (P < 0.001) using the selected distances (0.55–0.85) to image the leaves of the pepper genotypes (Supplementary Table S1). The 0.55 m and the 0.65 m showed better performances than the 0.75 m and 0.85 m (Table 2). Leaf length was better measured by Height, Feret, and Major with a 0.95 correlation. Similar observations were shown on leaf diameter with a correlation coefficient of 0.89. Leaf area was rather reflected by Feret and Major with a 0.86 correlation, while the correlation value appeared 0.81 for image Area.

Reproductive—fruits

The camera-to-object distances of 0.55 m and 0.65 m are suitable for fruit measurements based on correlation. On the other hand, the analysis of variance did not show significant differences in distance effects on the parameters (Supplementary Table S1). Fruit length was improved with Feret by 0.87 correlations at a 0.65 m distance. Fruit diameter, on the other hand, correlated strongly with Minor with a 0.90 correlation at 0.55 m. Discussion

This study examined the effects of imaging parameters and camera-to-object distances for plant growth characterization, emphasizing non-destructive monitoring of plant phenology. Major parameters proved most effective for measuring canopy spread in top-view imaging, while Minor parameters were better suited for side-view assessments. Similar findings by Igathinathane et al.50 showed that Major and Minor parameters effectively determine particle dimensions, especially for shapes approximating circles and ellipses. Polder et al.51 also reported strong correlations between Major and Minor axes and seed dimensions, illustrating their utility across different views. Both top and side-view imaging capture canopy spread within a curvilinear framework, with these parameters representing the primary and secondary axes of the best-fitting ellipse. Notably, the secondary axis may be more prominent in side views, whereas the primary axis dominates in top views. Further research is necessary to understand the reasons behind these differences, optimize imaging approaches for plant trait measurement.

The imaging parameters had different interpretations from the actual plant measurements. As we have already explained, Major and Minor measured well the canopy diameter, and Height measured well plant height with side view imaging. Plant height and stem diameter are important traits to determine plant growth. The two parameters determine the ability of plants to compete for light and to facilitate photosynthesis52,53; therefore, an efficient and accurate way to measure the parameters is important. Different imaging methods have been employed to measure height54, and these findings provide baseline information on the imaging parameters that can be used to represent height with precision. Measurement of plant height using imaging has also been confirmed by Treder et al.55 on pear trees, Polter et al.56 on aquatic plants, and Brunes et al.57 on wheat seedlings.

For leaf characteristics, leaf length, width, and area were better measured by Major, Minor, Feret, and Area than the other parameters. The orientation of the leaf might be a contributing factor for the measurements as Major (the longest line that can be drawn within the leaf), Minor (the shortest line perpendicular to the major axis), and Feret diameter (the longest distance between any two selected points along the leaf perimeter) better account for irregular shapes and variations in particle orientations50. The leaves are important organs for plant activities, which are involved in light capture, gas exchange, photosynthesis, and other important functions58. The Major, Minor, and Feret would offer a more adaptable approach to capture varying shapes and orientations of different leaf species. Measuring leaf dimensions, especially the leaf area, is mostly done through the destructive method using a leaf area meter or other devices59, but the imaging approach can be easy, more accurate, and more precise than the traditional approach, aside from being non-destructive. Further research in the imaging approach can provide a non-destructive method with precision and accuracy, and the leaves can be studied over a long time.

More so, actual measurement of fruit length correlated well with Feret, while Minor and Height well-calibrated fruit diameter. A good relationship existed between fruit fresh and dry weights with image Area than all the other parameters. These observations provide information on what imaging parameter is needed for a particular trait of the plants. Few attentions are given to these observations in the phenotypic analysis of plants. Fruit segmentation of the different pepper cultivars needed some levels of care and precision. More especially, the red pepper has some dark brown spots around the point of attachment to the pedicel. Much attention was needed for the masking and the segmentation. Moreover, weak correlations were identified among some of the traits, which could be due to the trait differences among the genotypes that were not considered in some of the analyses. Further research is needed here.

The experiment provided information on the effect of distance on the imaging of the plants. Generally, the short camera-to-object distances provided better correlations than the long distances. The camera-to-object distance plays a crucial role in providing accurate data for RGB-camera sensors38. The camera-to-object distance can affect the sharpness and the resolution of the images, which can cause wrong image interpretations. As the plant grows, image resolutions change and thus affect the camera-to-object distance. For the side-view imaging of the vegetative growth of the plants, the 0.8 m camera-to-object distance was identified to produce a higher correlation between the image parameters and the actual measurements compared with the 1.0 and 1.2 m. The observation could result from spatial resolution, pixel density, and object scale relative to the camera’s field of view. Decreasing or increasing camera-to-object distance affects pixel density on the object and the texture complexity of the plant organs (such as leaves, flowers, and fruits). Beyond 0.8 m, a few pixels might have been occupied for traits extraction and segmentation, but measurement noise might have increased. Other factors might result from perspective distortion and increased occlusion among the plant organs. At 0.8, plant architecture might have balanced the frame to maintain enough pixels for trait extraction. On the other hand, handling the short distance is difficult when the plants are fully grown, as the camera coverage reduces, and therefore misses some parts of the plant. In this way, increasing the camera distance step-wise may be appropriate to study the overall life span of the plant.

For leaves, fruits, and seeds, imaging at 0.55 m and 0.65 m object-to-camera distances was better than the very short distances of 0.35 and 0.45 m, and the long distances of 0.75 and 0.85 m. However, some of the parameters depended on a particular distance selection. For example, Fruit length measured manually was improved with Feret by 0.87 correlations at 0.65 m camera-to-object distance, while fruit diameter measured manually correlated strongly with Minor (0.90 correlation coefficient) at 0.55 m camera-to-object distance. Hennessy et al.41 demonstrated significant differences in the camera-to-object distances in the RGB detection accuracy of agricultural herbicide applications. Different camera distances, mostly ranging between 0.25 and 1.0 m, have been employed for plant image analysis. Frogeye leaf spots on soybeans were studied with a 0.75 m camera-to-object distance60, while as low as 0.27 m camera-to-object distance was used for leaf wetness studies44. This work provides primary information on the need for optimal and standard camera-to-object distances for controlled and open field plant imaging.

The seeds of the four pepper genotypes studied showed different characteristics based on the imaging parameters. Oranzela seeds had better imaging parameters, such as Height, Feret, Major, Minor, Width, and Area, while red pepper had the least. In real terms, Oranzela seeds were bigger while red pepper seeds were smaller. Caball, Kori, and Oranzela seeds showed better Circularity and Roundness than red pepper, which also had the maximum Aspect Ratio. Thus, Red pepper seeds adjust better to an ellipse than a circle, as reported by Martin-Gomez et al.61 in Castor Beans. Circularity values range from 0.0 to 1.0, and the closer the value is to 1, the perfect the circle shape of the seeds62. Roundness, on the other hand, measures how the shape of the seed is close to a perfect circle63, and it is the inverse of the Aspect Ratio. Aspect Ratio is the proportion relationship between the maximum and minimum Feret Diameter (the caliper diameter)64, which provides information on the degree to which the pepper seeds are stretched. Roundness can exist in more than one plane, as Circularity exists in one plane. Also, Solidity provides information about the smoothness of the curves of the seeds, and it describes the degree to which the projected contour of the pepper seeds is concave65,66.

The closer the Solidity is to 1, the more the projected contour is to its convex hull67, and the fewer the projections, depressions, and rough corners on the seed surface. This means the Red Pepper seeds may comparatively be small, flat, stretched, and with surface projections than the other genotypes. Arredondo-Valdez et al.63 utilized Roundness, Circularity, and Aspect Ratio to assess the quality of Opuntia ficus-indica seeds and confirmed the accuracy of using these parameters in seed studies. Seed characterization through imaging provides numerous characteristics for comparison, which can aid in seed differentiation, a process that wouldn’t be possible with manual methods. Also, the precision of using images is higher than the manual methods64. This observation is based on studies done in a controlled environment.

The imaging approach of phenotyping can support the traditional method and can help speed up the selection of different plant traits for further improvements. Imaging is less laborious, non-destructive, can capture a wider range of traits at a plant growth period, and reduces human error in data acquisition. There are more recent reviews on phenotyping standards and best practices to guide the use of automated methods for plant phenotyping68,69. This experiment has shown that imaging can be used at different stages of the plant.

It is noted that some challenges, such as occlusions, light effects, and robust growth of different varieties, occur in plant imaging70. Furthermore, the 2D RGB images reduce detailed information on a three-dimensional plant organ, such as fruits, due to a single viewpoint. In view, only the projected area of the visible organs is captured while discriminating against the hidden traits. This leads to reduced pixel intensity and causes underestimation of the plant traits, thus affecting the correlation between projected and actual measurements. Additional imaging experiments could help increase segmentation accuracy while reducing noise in shape- and texture-based metrics. The comparison of the analysis tool with the real measurements needs expansion to involve different plant data to ensure its reliability and validity71. Further work in this research dimension will provide the overall picture of using imaging tools for plant analysis to provide a broader picture of plants’ genetic traits and the definition of the traits by their environmental conditions. The imaging approach affords an efficient and possible way to link the genotype to the phenotype to select high-yielding and stress-tolerant plants72,73. RGB imaging is continuously used in plant studies, including seed morphology, germination rates, seedling vigor, root architecture, shoot biomass, and yield traits73–75.

Conclusion

RGB imaging could offer a reliable, non-destructive alternative for the practical measurement and characterization of plant traits. In this study, imaging parameters such as Major, Minor, and Feret diameter from ImageJ effectively captured traits including plant height, canopy spread, and organ dimensions, while seed traits like Roundness, Circularity, and Solidity highlighted metabolic diversity among pepper genotypes. Camera distance significantly influences image resolution and accuracy especially in a controlled environment; a distance of 0.8 m is optimal for vegetative stages, whereas 0.55–0.65 m is recommended for fruit, leaf, and seed imaging to achieve high correlation with actual measurements. The advantages of RGB imaging over traditional methods include its adaptability to irregular plant shapes and its non-destructive nature. The imaging could, however, not solve the challenges of some plant organs overlapping the others, especially for vegetative imaging due to the complex architecture of the plants. Again, using the two-dimensional imaging to study fruit characteristics (a three-dimensional object) was not very efficient and needs improvement. Further research is necessary to refine imaging parameters for specific plant traits and developmental stages, enhancing the application of high-throughput imaging in precision agriculture.
