Hormone responses to buserelin in polycystic ovary syndrome and in eumenorrheic women with hyperandrogenism/hyperandrogenemia, and the relationship of these responses with insulin resistance
Authors: Salvatore Benvenga, Michele Russo, Fausto Famà, Gianpiero Forte, Vittorio Unfer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe compared 37 women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) with 24 women with eumenorrhea plus hyperandrogenism and/or hyperandrogenemia without ultrasound evidence of PCO morphology (EuHyperA) to assess their hormone responses to a GnRH-agonist (buserelin). Following our recent paper on PCOS and EuHyperA, we selected patients who performed the 2 h-oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and stratified them according to presence/absence of insulin resistance (IR), viz. HOMA-index ≥ 2.5.IR impacted on the PCOS group since IR-yes-PCOS women had significantly higher body weight, BMI, total testosterone (TT), free androgen index (FAI), and 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP), borderline higher delta-4 androstenedione (Δ4-ASD) and ovarian volume, and significantly lower sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) vs IR-no-PCOS. IR-no-EuHyperA had significantly higher follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), borderline higher dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) and borderline lower 17-OHP vs IR-no-PCOS. IR-yes-EuHyperA had significantly higher DHEAS, borderline lower TT and FAI vs IR-yes-PCOS.The insulin curve was significantly higher in the IR-yes vs IR-no-PCOS, and IR-yes vs IR-no-EuHyperA. Compared to PCOS, EuHyperA had insignificantly lower glucose and insulin responses regardless of IR status.After steroidogenic ovarian stimulation (24 h-buserelin test), IR presence vs IR absence impacted on 4 curves in PCOS (significantly higher TT, borderline higher 17-OHP, significantly lower Δ4-ASD and DHEAS), and only one curve in EuHyperA (significantly higher TT). IR-no-EuHyperA had two curves significantly lower than IR-no-PCOS (Δ4-ASD and TT). Instead, IR-yes-EuHyperA had significantly lower Δ4-ASD, TT and 17-OHP curves, and significantly higher DHEAS curve vs IR-yes-PCOS.In conclusion, of 35 parameters (baseline, OGTT-related, buserelin-related), 28 (80%) were statistically similar in EuHyperA vs PCOS regardless of IR status. However, IR presence impacted on more parameters in PCOS than EuHyperA. Given the known ovary sparing by IR in PCOS, it appears that IR exacerbates androgen production of PCOS women more markedly than EuHyperA women.Keywords: Hyperandrogenism, Hyperandrogenemia, Eumenorrhea, Polycystic ovary syndrome, Insulin resistance, Gonadotropin-releasing hormone-agonist test, 17-OH-progesterone

Introduction

In a recent retrospective monocentric study [1] on 92 women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) per Rotterdam criteria [2] and 41 women with eumenorrhea with hyperandrogenism and/or hyperandrogenemia without ultrasound evidence of PCO morphology (EuHyperA) having a complete set of data available for analysis, we showed that most of the anthropometric and biochemical parameters evaluated were statistically similar. Yet, some differences existed, with EuHyperA having values lower than PCOS women. We concluded that EuHyperA can be considered a milder form of PCOS [1]. The biochemical parameters evaluated included two tests: the 2 h-oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and the 24 h-response of luteinizing hormone (LH) and 5 steroid hormones to the stimulation by a GnRH-agonist (buserelin). These hormones were dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS), 17-hydroxy-progesterone (17-OHP), Δ4-androstenedione (Δ4-ASD), total testosterone (TT) and estradiol (E2). Because a previous study on 148 PCOS women per National Institutes of Health (NIH) criteria [3] had found that the buserelin-induced responses of LH, Δ4-ASD, TT and E2 were significantly greater in the PCOS subgroup with functional ovarian hyperandrogenism (FOH + ve) as compared to the subgroup without it (FOH −ve), we did the same stratification for the PCOS group and EuHyperA group [1].

FOH is defined by hyper-response of serum 17-OHP to the stimulation by GnRH-agonists (usually a peak of ≥ 2.5 ng/ml, 18–24 h after injection of the agonist) [4,5]. FOH reflects an increased efficiency of the 17α-hydroxylase activity but less efficiently increased 17,20 lyase activity of the enzyme cytochromeP450c17(CYP17) in the ovaries [4,5]. CYP17 is upregulated by the hyperinsulinemia that accompanies the PCOS-associated insulin resistance (IR) because, as known, the ovaries are spared by IR [4,5] (see also Discussion). In agreement with the knowledge that FOH occurs in approximately half of PCOS women [4,5], we found a rate of 53.3 % in PCOS vs 31.7 % in EuHyperA (p = 0.021) [1]. Importantly, using serum levels at the last point of the test (24 h) for homogeneous comparison with Pasquali et al. [3], we confirmed that buserelin-induced response of LH, Δ4-ASD, TT and E2 were significantly higher in FOH + ve PCOS compared to FOH −ve PCOS [1]. However, comparing the whole curve, the higher response in FOH + ve PCOS vs FOH −ve PCOS remained for LH, Δ4-ASD and TT, but not for E2 [1]. Even more importantly, also in the EuHyperA women the whole curves of LH, Δ4-ASD and TT were significantly higher in FOH + ve subgroup compared to the FOH −ve subgroup [1]. Statistically lower in EuHyperA vs PCOS were curves of DHEAS, Δ4-ASD and TT (both in FOH + ve and FOH −ve subgroups) and curves of 17-OHP (only in the FOH −ve subgroup) [1]. These findings supported the conclusion of EuHyperA being a milder form of PCOS [1].

What we wished to do subsequently was to analyze hormone responses after stratification of both the 92 PCOS and 41 EuHyperA women based on the presence/absence of IR, a comparison that is novel and important. We also wished to look for correlations of baseline indices and buserelin-stimulated hormone responses with IR. Again, the lack of statistical differences between the two groups for most of the indices evaluated would have supported further the conclusion of EuHyperA being a milder form of PCOS.

Materials and methods

Study groups

Details on the retrospective collection of the study groups (EuHyperA and PCOS) as well as on definitions and methods were provided in our previous paper published in this journal [1].

In the present paper, the two study groups consist of 24 EuHyperA women and 37 PCOS women who (i.) performed the 2 h-oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT, 75 g) at our medical center in the morning between the 2nd and 7th day of the menstrual cycle; (ii.) had both glycemia and insulinemia measured at all time points (0, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min) to obtain a reliable curve and a reliable calculation of the area under the curve (AUC 0-120min) by the classic trapezoidal method; (iii.) completed two regimens of adrenal suppression before the GnRH agonist stimulation test (with full details also given in our previous paper [1]; (iv.) performed this test of acute stimulation of ovarian steroidogenesis at all six time points [-0.5 h, 0, 1, 4, 20 and 24 h]), again to obtain a reliable curve and a reliable calculation of the AUC. In brief, the two groups had a complete dataset for each tested parameter, including those at first observation. Making reference to the study by Pasquali et al [3], with only three time points of their OGTT test (0, 60, 120 min) missing glucose and insulin peaks at two time points (30 and 90 min), and with only 4 time points of their buserelin test (0, 0.5, 1 and 24 h) missing the LH peak at 4 h and the steroid hormone peak at 20 h instead of 24 h, we have a truly complete set of data for the two tests.

Insulin resistance (IR)

IR was evaluated using HOMA-IR (also called HOMA-IR index), which is calculated by this formula: glycemia at 0 min [(mg/dl) × insulin at 0 min (μU/ml)]/405. In correlation analysis (see Statistics), we also used the OGTT-insulinemia (whole curve and AUC0-120min) as a proxy for IR, since hyperinsulinemia is a compensatory response to IR. Per our previous paper [1], IR was defined by HOMA-index ≥ 2.5. Based on this criterion, both the EuHyperA group and PCOS group were subdivided into the IR-yes and IR-no subgroups (the latter also termed insulin-sensitive).

Stratification into HR and NR subgroups, and categorization into phenotypes

Regarding the classification into HR (hyper-response of circulating 17-OHP to a GnRH-agonist) or NR (normal response of circulating 17-OHP to a GnRH-agonist) subgroups of our previous paper [1] and the classification into phenotypes [6,7], the distribution of the 61 women was the following. Within the EuHyperA group (n = 24; 7 HR and 17 NR), of the 16 IR-no women, 4 were HR and 12 NR; of the 8 IR-yes women 3 were HR and 5 NR. Within the PCOS group (n = 37; 15 HR and 22 NR), of the 15 IR-no women, 4 were HR and 11 NR; of the 22 IR-yes women, 11 were HR and 11 NR. Concerning phenotypes in the 24 EuHyperA group, these were K (n = 12; 50 %), N (n = 10; 41.7 %), O (n = 2; 8.3 %). Per Androgen Excess Society guideline [6], phenotype K has only hyperandrogenism and hyperandrogenemia, phenotype N has only hyperandrogenism, while phenotype O has only hyperandrogenemia. In the 37 PCOS group, phenotypes were A (n = 24; 56.8 %), B (n = 6; 16.2 %), C (n = 6; 16.2 %) and D (n = 4; 10.8 %). As well-known [6,7], phenotype A is characterized by hyperandrogenism and/or hyperandrogenemia + ovary disfunction (OD) + ultrasound characteristics for polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM); phenotype B by hyperandrogenism and/or hyperandrogenemia + OD; phenotype C by hyperandrogenism and/or hyperandrogenemia + PCOM; Phenotype D by OD + PCOM. Given the limited size of phenotypes in either study group, we refrained from further stratification based on phenotypes.

Hyperandrogenism was defined as either hirsutism, acne, and/or androgenic alopecia. Hirsutism was diagnosed according to a Ferriman- Gallwey score ≥ 8. Hyperandrogenemia was defined as the increased serum levels of any of TT, free testosterone (FT), free androgen index (FAI), Δ4-ASD, DHEAS.

Statistics

Data are reported as mean ± SD. Normal (Gaussian) distribution of continuous variables was evaluated by the one sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Differences between means were handled by ANOVA or the Wilcoxon signed rank test, depending on the Gaussian or non-Gaussian distribution of the continuous variables. Differences between proportions were evaluated by the Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test, as appropriate. Though Pearson’s correlation can be performed when at least one of the two variables has normal distribution [8], we preferred the strict criterion of both variables having normal distribution. Hence, we used the Spearman rank correlation when one or both variables had non-normal distribution. To maximize chances of finding correlations, we considered both HOMA-IR and OGTT-insulinemia as the independent variable, and handled variables in several ways. For each hormone response to buserelin, two AUCs were considered: AUC-0.5-to-24h (which encompasses all 6 time points, with two baseline values [-0.5 and 0 h]), and AUC0-24h (which considers 5 time points and only one baseline value).

In all statistical comparisons, a p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while a p value between 0.05 and 0.10 was considered borderline significant.

For both the OGTT and buserelin test, use of the whole curve of a given index (blood glucose, insulin, hormone responses to buserelin) is more valuable than the corresponding AUC, since it enhances statistical robustness. For instance, in the comparison of OGTT-insulinemia between the IR-yes EuHyperA (n = 8 women) vs the IR-no EuhyperA (n = 16 women), use of AUC implies to handle 8 values vs 16 values, both values being summarized in each subgroup by a mean ± SD. In contrast, by pooling the 5 insulin levels (0, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min) in the said two subgroups, one handles 40 values vs 80 values, both values being summarized in each subgroup by a mean ± SD. In the buserelin test, with 6 hormone levels (−05, 0, 1, 4, 20 and 24 h), comparison of the two whole curves of a given hormone between the two subgroups implies contrasting one group with n = 48 vs a second group with n = 96.

Results

HOMA-IR values were similar in the IR-no EuHyperA women vs IR-no PCOS women (1.47 ± 0.62 vs 1.33 ± 0.67, p = 0.57), and in the IR-yes EuHyperA vs the IR-yes PCOS women (3.33 ± 0.84 vs 3.79 ± 1.38, p = 0.39) (Table 1).

Baseline data with intra-group comparisons

Data at first observation of patients are summarized in Table 1. Obviously, rates of abnormal HOMA-IR were excluded from all comparisons, and m ± SD of HOMA-IR were excluded from the IR-yes vs IR-no comparisons. Compared to IR-no EuHyperA, IR-yes EuHyperA women had significantly higher 17-OHP (p = 0.020) and borderline lower FSH (p = 0.087). Compared to IR-no PCOS, IR-yes PCOS women had significantly greater body weight (p = 0.034), BMI (p = 0.048), TT (p = 0.043), FAI (p = 0.0013), significantly lower SHBG (p = 0.0017), and borderline higher ovarian volume (p = 0.10), 17-OHP (p = 0.053) and Δ4-ASD levels (p = 0.099).

Baseline data with inter-group comparisons

Compared to IR-no EuHyperA, IR-no PCOS women had only one index significantly different and two indices borderline different. Indeed, the PCOS subgroup had significantly lower FSH (p = 0.044), borderline lower DHEAS (p = 0.098), and borderline higher 17-OHP (p = 0.093) (Table 1). Compared to IR-yes EuHyperA, IR-yes PCOS women also had one index significantly different and two indices borderline different. Indeed, the PCOS subgroup had significantly lower DHEAS (p = 0.0049) and borderline higher both TT and FAI (p = 0.097 and 0.10) (Table 1).

Discussion

We are unaware of studies in PCOS women and EuHyperA women that have compared responses of hormones to a GnRH-agonist injection taking into account their insulin sensitivity/resistance status. This important comparison confers novelty to our study. As well-known, there is no agreement on the threshold of HOMA-index that indicates IR. Per recent example literature, previous PCOS studies used our cut-off of ≥ 2.5 [[9], [10], [11]] or ≥ 2.6 to ≥ 2.8 [[12], [13], [14]]. The prevalence of IR found in PCOS women (n = 90 to 119) by the cited papers [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]] was 31 %-74.4 %, with our 59.4 % prevalence matching the 59.0 % prevalence reported by others [14]. The most and least represented PCOS phenotypes in our cohort were A (56.8 %) and D (10.8 %). These rates agree with those reported by others in PCOS women across the world [9,14,15,16], such as 53.9 % and 8.4 % in 382 Italian patients [16], 51.9 % and 11.9 % in 160 Iranian patients [9], 60.9 % and 11.9 % in 92 Ecuadorian patients [14]. In addition to Guastella et al. [16], other two Italian studies reported frequencies of 58.0 % and 6.9 % (n = 448) [17], and 72.7 % and 9.6 % (n = 187) [15].

In our PCOS women, the presence of IR is associated with significantly higher body weight, BMI, TT, FAI, borderline higher 17-OHP, borderline greater ovarian volume, borderline lower DHEAS and significantly lower SHBG compared to the absence of IR. Though double, the rate of FOH was insignificantly higher (50 % [11/22] vs 26.7 % [4/15], p = 0.19), most likely due to the size of the cohorts. Indeed, with a size twice greater, the same rates would have resulted from 22/44 vs 8/30, which is a significant difference (p = 0.045). In our EuHyperA women, the presence of IR was associated only with significantly higher levels of 17-OHP and borderline lower levels of FSH compared to IR absence. Upon comparing the two groups for the same IR status, regardless of IR status, most of the indices evaluated were statistically similar in PCOS women compared to EuHyperA women (Table 1). Overall, of 35 indices that can be contrasted in EuHyperA vs PCOS women (17 at first observation, 6 OGTT-related [fasting glucose, fasting insulin, 2 curves and 2 AUCs] and 12 buserelin-related [6 curves and 6 AUCs]), in the setting of IR absence only 7 indices (20 %) differed, of which 4 (11 %) significantly (FSH, curve and AUC of buserelin-stimulated Δ4-ASD, curve of buserelin-stimulated TT) and 3 borderline different (17-OHP, DHEAS, AUC of buserelin-stimulated TT). In the setting of IR presence, differences were also restricted to 7 indices, with 5 (14 %) being significant (DHEAS, buserelin-stimulated curve of DHEAS, 17-OHP, Δ4-ASD and TT) and 2 borderline (TT and FAI). In short, regardless of IR status, EuHyperA and PCOS women have statistically similar values for 80 % of the parameters evaluated.

In PCOS, correlation analysis showed the direct significant association of testosterone (TT, FT, FAI) and 17-OHP with IR. In EuHyperA, there was a direct significant correlation of 17-OHP and DHEAS with both HOMA-IR and OGTT-insulinemia; the direct significant relationship with testosterone (FT only) appeared using HOMA-IR as the independent variable.

IR status made a difference in hormone responses to buserelin particularly in PCOS. Indeed, in PCOS women the presence of IR was associated with significantly higher TT response, borderline higher 17-OHP response and significantly lower both Δ4-ASD and DHEAS responses compared to the absence of IR. Instead, in EuHyperA women, the presence of IR correlated with significantly higher response of TT solely. Only 17-OHP response was correlated with HOMA-IR, and consistently at very high levels of probability, in PCOS but borderline levels in EuHyperA. When HOMA-IR was replaced by OGTT-insulinemia as the independent variable, this relationship of 17-OHP response held, though at a lower level of probability in PCOS, while becoming significant in EuHyperA. Furthermore, two additional and inverse relationships appeared: one concerning E2 in EuHyperA, and one concerning LH in PCOS.

The hormone responses to buserelin and their correlations with HOMA-IR or OGTT-insulinemia contribute to explain baseline hormone levels. Since PCOS is characterized by increased frequency and amplitude of GnRH-driven LH pulses [5], it can be assumed that women with PCOS would undergo repeatedly a “self-administered GnRH-stimulation test of ovarian steroidogenesis” and a “self-administered OGTT” each time they consume a high carbohydrate diet. Thus, the higher TT and 17-OHP baseline levels in IR-yes PCOS compared to IR-no PCOS women match the corresponding higher TT and 17-OHP response to buserelin.

As known, IR in PCOS spares the ovaries, so that they become hyper-responsive to the compensatory increased plasma (and tissue) insulin levels; it is also known that the paracrine action of insulin in the ovaries sensitizes them to LH [5]. Particularly, in the ovaries insulin upregulates the LH receptors and cytochromeP450c17(CYP17) and 17βHSD5, two enzymes that require the presence of LH for their expression just like other enzymes involved in gonadal steroidogenesis [5]. This knowledge is consistent with the direct relationships we found in PCOS women between insulinemia (and HOMA-IR) and both 17-OHP and TT. Indeed, the 17α-hydroxylase activity of CYP17 is responsible for the synthesis of 17-OHP, while the 17,20-lyase activity of the same enzyme is responsible for the synthesis of Δ4-ASD from 17-OHP, with in turn Δ4-ASD being converted into TT by 17βHSD5 [5]. Because of the diverse degree of efficiency of the two activities of the insulin-upregulated CYP17 enzyme in PCOS (highly efficient 17α-hydroxylase activity but relatively inefficient 17,20 lyase activity) [4,5], over-secretion of 17-OHP and direct correlation of 17-OHP with insulinemia ensue. Noteworthy, our finding of a direct but borderline significant correlation between OGTT-insulinemia and 17-OHP supports the concept of EuHyperA being a milder, possibly initial form of PCOS. Yet, EuHyperA features an already present but moderate dysregulation of the two CYP17 activities. On the other hand, the consistently significant correlation of testosterone (TT, FT and FAI) with either HOMA-IR or OGTT-insulinemia in PCOS but not in EuHyperA (FT only) suggests that full insulin-driven hyperactivity of 17βHSD5 has yet to come in EuHyperA.

As a medical practice corollary of the concept of considering EuHyperA an initial, paucisymptomatic form that may advance to full-blow phenotypes of PCOS, there would be a rationale to intervene in EuHyperA women and counteract the said consequences of hyperinsulinemia on ovarian steroidogenesis.

In addition to the limited size of the two study groups, limitations of our study are the monocentric and retrospective nature of the study. These limitations are counterbalanced by the consistency of some data of the PCOS group with the literature (including the dysfunctional activities of CYP17), and the important novelty of having explored the influence of absence/presence of IR on the GnRH-LH-driven ovarian steroidogenesis not only in PCOS but also in EuHyperA women.

Conclusions

This study provides novel insights into the impact of IR on GnRH-agonist-stimulated ovarian steroidogenesis in both PCOS and EuHyperA women. By stratifying hormonal responses according to insulin sensitivity status, we demonstrated that IR is associated with distinct endocrine alterations, particularly in women with PCOS. These alterations include increased baseline and stimulated levels of testosterone and 17-OHP, reflecting the insulin-driven upregulation of ovarian steroidogenic enzymes such as CYP17 and 17βHSD5.

Importantly, despite phenotypic and diagnostic differences, PCOS and EuHyperA women shared largely similar endocrine profiles when matched for IR status, with 80 % of evaluated parameters showing no significant differences. The correlations between insulin metrics and androgen levels in PCOS and to a lesser extent in EuHyperA underscore the role of hyperinsulinemia in amplifying ovarian androgen production. These novel pathophysiological insights highlight the potential value of early intervention in EuHyperA to prevent progression toward more overt PCOS phenotypes.

The retrospective and monocentric nature of our study, along with limited sample sizes, pose certain limitations. However, our findings are consistent with existing literature and offer a new perspective on the metabolic-endocrine interplay in hyperandrogenic disorders. Future prospective studies are warranted to confirm our data and to explore the clinical benefit of targeting IR in the early stages of ovarian hyperandrogenism.
