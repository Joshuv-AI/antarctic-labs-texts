A pan-cancer single-cell analysis reveals the effect of PD-1 blockades on tumor angiogenesis by inhibiting the endothelial CXCL12-CXCR4 axis
Authors: Mairedan Muhetarijiang, Pengwei Zhu, Han Zhang, Xiangjie Sun, Yakui Wu, Qianwen Xu, Ryan Justin, Chenxi Li, Dongchen Zhou, Biqi Zhang, Jun Liu, Xiaosheng Hu, Ting Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockades, have redefined oncology in the last decade. Previous studies on PD-1 blockades mostly concentrate on their interactions with immune cells. This study aims to investigate how PD-1 blockades affect endothelial cell (EC) heterogeneity in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and to explore potential targets for enhancing the anti-tumor effects of PD-1 blockades. Here, we established a pan-cancer EC atlas from the public database and revealed that PD-1 blockades repress the angiogenic population in ECs and inhibit the CXCL12-CXCR4 signaling derived from ECs. Using a murine tumor model built with Lewis Lung Carcinoma cell line, we further validated our findings that a PD-1 blockade, as well as a CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100, inhibited EC population in tumors and their CXCL12 expression. In addition, the combo therapy of the PD-1 blockade and AMD3100 showed superior anti-tumor effects to monotherapy. Moreover, we predicted MYC to be the potential regulator through which PD-1 blockades affect ECs. Together, our results suggest that PD-1 blockades have an anti-angiogenic effect besides boosting T cell immunity, and the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway is a potential target for enhancing the effectiveness of PD-1 blockades.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04188-9.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those targeting the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis have redefined oncology in the last decade. However, clinical outcomes remain heterogeneous, with low response rates, and acquired resistance emerging in some malignancies and individuals [1]. Therefore, abundant studies have been conducted to enhance the efficacy of PD-1 blockades by exploring deeper mechanisms.

The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been pivotal in mapping the tumor microenvironment (TME) by dissecting the major cell clusters and depicting the intercellular communication. Previous single-cell studies on PD-1 blockades mostly focused on immune cells, especially T cell, which is the original functioning site [2–4]. Nevertheless, the critical roles of stromal components within the TME during immunotherapy, particularly endothelial cells (ECs), have received comparatively less attention, despite their multifaceted participation in tumor progression [5]. Current studies have identified roles for distinct EC subpopulations within the TME and their associations with ICIs. High endothelial venule (HEV) ECs facilitate T-cell entry into tumor regions, a process that can be enhanced by ICB [6, 7]. Lymphatic ECs mediate lymphocyte trans-endothelial migration, regulated by the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway [4]. Tip cells in tumor vasculature serve not only as critical initiators of angiogenesis but also suppress immune responses within the TME; additionally, they interact with pericytes to promote tumor progression [8]. Therefore, an in-depth investigation of ECs may open new avenues for enhancing ICI efficacy.

CXCL12 (chemokine C-X-C ligand 12), which encodes SDF-1 (stromal-derived factor 1), and its canonical receptor CXCR4 (chemokine C-X-C receptor 4), have been proven to play crucial roles in vasculogenesis, endothelial repair, energy metabolism, coagulation, hematopoiesis, cancer development and metastasis, and anti-cancer immunity [9–11]. The CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling pathway mediates communication between stromal cells or between stromal cells and immune cells. Paracrine signaling between CXCL12-expressing tumor arterial ECs and tip cells has been proven to participate in tumor angiogenesis [12]. The CXCL12/CXCR4 axis recruits an immunosuppressive neutrophil subpopulation in the tumor microenvironment, enhancing tumor cell tolerance to CD8+ T cells [13, 14]. Besides, the egression of CXCR4+CD8+ T cells from tumors is promoted by lymphatic EC-derived SDF-1 [15]. Since CD8+ T cells are activated by PD-1 blockades, the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis theoretically attenuates the anti-tumor efficacy of PD-1 blockades. This was validated by Chen et al. who showed that an increased CXCL12 level led to cancer progression and resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy [16]. Meanwhile, the combination of CXCR4 antagonist and PD-1 blockade was proven to reshape the tumor microenvironment and enhance anti-tumor immunity [17, 18]. Thus, the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis has become a promising target in tumor therapy, although the only listed CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 has not received FDA approval for anti-tumor application in any malignancy so far [19]. Nevertheless, despite the abundant studies on combo therapy of CXCR4 antagonist and PD-1 blockade, few focused on whether PD-1 blockade affects CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling. In addition, the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway is related to MYC, a classic proto-oncogene, in immune response, cell migration, and energy metabolism [20, 21]. However, most literature focuses on the regulation of MYC by the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, with limited research addressing the inverse question: whether MYC regulates CXCL12/CXCR4, despite its role as a transcription factor controlling numerous genes.

In this study, we characterized the impact of PD-1 blockades on EC heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment using a pan-cancer single-cell atlas and by animal experiment, illustrating their potential effect on endothelial CXCL12 expression via MYC regulation.

Method

Quality control of scRNA-seq data

For the original scRNA-seq data collected from GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus), cells with less than 1000 UMIs and 500 detected genes were filtered out. The mitochondrial RNA percentage was calculated using the PercentageFeatureSet function of R package Seurat 5.0.2, and cells with more than 20% mtRNA were filtered out. Doublets in the datasets were identified and eliminated using the R package DoubletFinder 2.0.4.

scRNA data normalization and visualization

After quality control, the scRNA-seq data were normalized using the NormalizeData function with parameter “ LogNormalize.” The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. The expression matrix was scaled for all genes using the ScaleData function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the RunPCA function. The top 30 principal components were used for the FindNeighbors function. Graph-based clustering was performed using the FindClusters function at a resolution of 0.5. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was performed for dimensionality reduction using the RunUMAP function. All functions above were implemented in the R package Seurat.

Identification of differentially expressed genes and cluster annotation

Differentially expressed genes (DEG) among the original clusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers(min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25, only.pos = TRUE) function. The top 5 DEGs were selected to annotate functional clusters (seen in Table S2).

Cytotrace analysis

Cytotrace analysis was performed on the integrated endothelial dataset using R package CytoTRACE2 1.1.0. CytoTRACE 2 is a deep learning-dependent tool that predicts cellular developmental potential using scRNA-seq data. It categorizes cells into discrete potency classes—from totipotent/pluripotent cells with broad differentiation capacity to lineage-restricted and terminally differentiated states—while also providing continuous developmental scores (0–1 scale). The system employs an interpretable deep learning model trained on multiple human and murine tissues, capturing multi-gene expression patterns characteristic of each developmental stage. Calibrating outputs across biological systems enables the direct comparison of cellular maturity levels between different experiments. This framework identifies conserved molecular signatures of cellular potency while maintaining biological interpretability, offering a standardized metric to assess developmental trajectories and cellular plasticity across diverse tissues and species.

Trajectory analysis

Imputed trajectories in the UMAP were defined using the R package Slingshot version. Cycling_ECs were considered as root state cells among all. Pseudotime analysis was performed applying the R package Monocle2 2.34.0. In the process of Monocle2, CellDataSet objects were created using the newCellDataSet function. Size factor for each cell was calculated using the estimateSizeFactor function. Dispersions of gene expressions were estimated using the estimateDispersions function. Differential genes were calculated using the differentialGeneTest function, and the parameter “reduceModelFormulaStr = ‘ ~ sample’” was employed to minimize the batch effect. Genes used for ordering were set using the setOrderingFilters function. Dimensionalities were reduced using the reduceDimensions function, and the orderCells function was used to locate cells in the pseudotime.

Gene enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis were performed using the R package ClusterProfiler 4.15.1.002.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Cell–cell communication analysis of the single-cell data was performed using the R package CellChat 1.6.1. CellChat is a computational method based on R that is designed for analysis and visualization of interactions among cells in scRNA-seq data. We employed “CellChatDB.human” as a reference database. In this section, we performed CellChat on the entire integrated dataset to acquire a general concept of the cell–cell interaction. Then we ran CellChat separately on the untreated and anti-PD-1-treated cells, and used the mergeCellChat function to perform the subsequent comparative analysis. The netVisual_diffInteraction, netVisual_heatmap, netVisual_bubble, and netAnalysis_signalingRole_network functions were used for visualization.

Scenic analysis

We used the R package Scenic version to describe the dynamic changes in the regulon activities caused by anti-PD-1 agents. Scenic is a computational method based on R and Python that can depict the regulon network in a scRNA-seq dataset. The access to the reference human regulon motif database is https://resources.aertslab.org/cistarget/databases/homo_sapiens/hg19/refseq_r45/mc9nr/gene_based/. The R package GENIE3 1.20.0 was employed to establish a gene regulation network and calculate the potential regulators of target genes.

Survival analysis

Gene differential analysis of bulk RNA-seq data was performed using DEseq2 version. R packages survival 3.8–3 and surminer were applied to perform survival analysis on the bulk sequence data. Patients in each cohort were divided into CXCL12-high and CXCL12-low groups based on the CXCL12 expression. A Kaplan–Meier plot was generated for each cancer type.

Animal model

LLC2 (Lewis lung carcinoma 2, Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd) cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Biosharp, BL304A) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, CORNING, 35-081-CV) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Solarbio, P1400-100), and maintained in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. C57BL/6 mice (male, 6 week-old, SPF level, weighted 18-20 g) were acclimatized for one week, and then injected subcutaneously with LLC2 cells (1 × 106 cells in 100 μL PBS) into the right flank. Treatment started 1 week after tumor cell implantation. Mice were treated with 100 μg control isotype IgG (BioXCell, BP0089), 100 μg anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (BioXCell, BP0146), 2.5 mg/kg CXCR4 antagonist (MedChemExpress, HY-10046), or anti-PD-1 + CXCR4 antagonist treatment once every 3 days, and 4 times in total. All drugs were injected intraperitoneally. One week after the last injection, the mice were killed, and the tissues were obtained. The tumor volume was measured by V = 0.5 × length × width2.

H&E staining

Appropriately sized tissue samples were placed in cassettes and processed through a standard dehydration series using an automated tissue processor. Dehydrated tissues were then embedded in paraffin wax with the cutting surface oriented downward; after cooling and solidification, the blocks were removed from molds and chilled on an ice plate. Sections of 4–7 μm thickness were cut using a microtome, mounted onto glass slides, and baked at 65 °C for 30–45 min. For dewaxing and rehydration, slides were sequentially immersed in xylene I, II, and III (10 min each), followed by a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%; 5 min each), then rinsed under running tap water for 10 min. Hematoxylin (LEAGENE, DH0004) staining proceeded for 20 min, differentiated briefly in 1% acid-alcohol (LEAGENE, DH0085) until optimal nuclear staining was achieved, and rinsed for 10 min. Counterstaining in eosin (Solarbio, G1108) lasted 5 min, after which slides were dehydrated through absolute ethanol (2–3 changes, 5 min each). Finally, sections were air-dried, cleared in xylene, mounted with neutral balsam (Solarbio, G8590), and examined under a light microscope for image acquisition and histological analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Fresh tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (equivalent to 10% formalin) for 48 h. After fixation, tissues underwent running water rinsing to remove residual fixative, followed by graded ethanol dehydration (50%, 70%, 85%, 95%, and absolute ethanol; 2 h per concentration). Clearing was achieved through immersion in ethanol-xylene (1:1) for 2 h, then pure xylene twice (2 h each). Paraffin infiltration was conducted in a thermostat-controlled oven before embedding tissues in molten wax. Sections of 4–7 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome. For immunofluorescence, sections were baked at 65 °C for 45 min and dewaxed through xylene I, II, and III (10 min each). Rehydration employed graded ethanols (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%; 5 min each) with subsequent 10-min water rinsing. Antigen retrieval was utilized with 40 × citrate buffer (Beyotime, P0086) by heating the slides in the solution for 20 min. After cooling, sections were washed in 1 × PBS (3 × 3 min). The slides were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma) for 20 min and blocked with 3% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies (1:100 dilution) were applied overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber, followed by PBS washes (3 × 3 min). Fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with subsequent PBS washes. Sections were mounted using antifade medium containing DAPI (Servicebio, G1012, 1:500), stored at − 20 °C, and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss LSM 900 Airyscan2). The details of the antibodies used in this study are seen in Table S4.

Flow cytometry

Under sterile conditions, tumor tissue (including core and invasive margin) and cardiac tissue were excised, minced with ophthalmic scissors, and digested in 1% collagenase I/trypsin solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Enzyme activity was terminated by adding 5 mL RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The digestate was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and resuspended in 3 ml PBS. This filtration-centrifugation cycle was repeated to obtain purified single-cell suspensions. For CD31 + cell quantification, cell density was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/ml. Aliquots (1 ml/tube) were stained with anti-mouse CD31-FITC antibody (test group, 1 µg/test) or isotype control (blank group, 1 µg/test), with all procedures performed under light-protected conditions at 4 °C. Samples were analyzed on a flow cytometer using the following gating strategy: CD31 + populations were identified as vascular ECs based on established surface marker expression profiles. T-lymphocyte detection used parallel staining protocols with appropriate lymphoid markers. The details of the antibodies used in this study are seen in Table S4.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Centrifuged cell pellets were immediately lysed in TRIzol (1 mL per 5 × 106 cells). Visible clumps were dispersed by vigorous pipetting or vortexing before 5 min room temperature incubation, followed by storage at − 80 °C. For phase separation, 0.2 mL chloroform per 1 mL TRIzol was added. Samples were vortexed vigorously for 15 s and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. Centrifugation at 12,000 × g (4 °C, 10 min) separated phases. The aqueous layer was carefully transferred to new tubes, mixed with 0.5 mL isopropanol per 1 mL initial TRIzol volume, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. RNA pellets were obtained by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min), then washed with 75% ethanol (volume equal to initial TRIzol). Pellets were dislodged by vortexing and tube-tapping before centrifugation (7500 × g, 4 °C, 5 min). Supernatant was decanted without disturbing pellets. RNA was air-dried for 8 min, dissolved in 30–100 μL RNase-free water, and aliquoted for quality assessment prior to storage at − 80 °C. The primer sequences for qPCR are detailed in the Supplementary Material (Table S5).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Murin SDF-1 ELISA kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd) was used to measure SDF-1 level in tissues. Tissue samples (shipped on dry ice) were homogenized in ice-cold saline (9:1 volume/tissue weight ratio) at 10,000 rpm, centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min), with supernatants analyzed post-protein quantification (BCA assay). Plasma samples (dry ice shipment) used EDTA/heparin anticoagulants, centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min) within 30 min post-collection, stored at − 80 °C, and re-centrifuged before analysis. The ELISA process followed the standard protocol provided by the ELISA kit.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in this research were all performed using R and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Bar plots are expressed as mean ± SD. Wilcoxon, one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, and log-rank tests were applied in the analysis and specified in the figure captions. Statistical significance was defined as (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant).

Result

A pan-cancer EC atlas revealed the impact of PD-1 blockade on EC heterogeneity

We collected single-cell RNA sequencing data from 10 human malignancies from the GEO database, including treatment-naive or anti-PD-1 treated samples from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), colorectal cancer (CRC), Melanoma (MEL), head and neck squamous cancer (HNSC), bladder cancer (BC), basal cell carcinoma (BCC), gastric cancer (GC), ovarian cancer (OC), prostate cancer (PC), renal cell carcinoma (RCC) (Fig. 1A, B). ScRNA-seq data from tumor-adjacent normal tissues were also collected for comparison with tumor tissues. Due to the lack of relevant research and data, no treated tumor samples from BC, GC, OC, and PC were integrated. To have a brief view of the TME, we sampled each dataset and integrated them into a single dataset encompassing all major cell populations, annotating cells based on classical markers (Fig. S1A, B). Analysis revealed that cells derived from tumors contained a higher proportion of tumor cells, whereas the proportion of tumor cells was significantly reduced in cells obtained from PD-1 blockade-treated tumors (Fig. S1C). Then ECs were sorted out from the original datasets using typical endothelial markers (VWF, PECAM1) (Fig. S1D). The ECs from each dataset were integrated using harmony integration[22] into a pan-cancer EC atlas containing 18,439 ECs in total of tumor (n = 13,737) and adjacent normal tissues (4702) obtained from untreated (n = 12,531) and PD-1 blockade-treated (n = 5908) (Fig. S2A). Eight original clusters were identified in the integrated dataset, and according to the marker genes, they were annotated into 8 subtypes (Fig. 1C) including high endothelial venule ECs (HEV_ECs) expressing SELE and SOD2 (n = 3195), arterial ECs (Art_ECs) expressing GJA4 and FBLN5 (n = 1943), tip cells (Tip_ECs) expressing ESM1 and NID2 (n = 2758), Capillary ECs (Cap_ECs) expressing CA4 and CD36 (n = 2144), Venous ECs (Ven_ECs) expressing ACKR1 and SELP (n = 3585), Lymphatic ECs (LECs) expressing CCL21 and COLEC12 (n = 1005), immature ECs (Immature_ECs) expressing MALAT1 and RPL31 (n = 3291), and cycling ECs (Cyc_ECs) expressing MKI67 and ASPM (n = 518) (Fig. 1D and E). HEV ECs and venous ECs share similar marker genes, both highly expressing ACKR1 and SELP. Consistent with previous studies, HEV ECs also highly express SELE and immune-related genes like IL6 and ICAM4, suggesting their role in immune cell migration and immune reactions. Tip cells and capillary ECs both have a high expression of RGCC. The EC composition in tumor tissues is markedly different from that in normal tissue, with higher HEV and tip cell percentages and lower capillary EC and LEC percentages (Fig. 1F). Statistical analysis showed that the proportions of tip cell (p < 0.0001) and cycling cell (p = 0.004) are significantly higher in ECs from tumor tissues, while the proportion of LEC is lower (p = 0.006) (Fig. S2B). The percentages of venous, arterial, immature, HEV, and capillary ECs remained similar in the two tissues. The composition of ECs also varies across different tumor types (Fig. S2C). In basal cell carcinoma, bladder cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer, there is a considerable amount of HEV ECs, which show a lower percentage in gastric cancer and ovarian cancer. In renal cell carcinoma, immature ECs occupy the majority of the EC population, with no appearance of HEV ECs. Gastric cancer has the largest proportion of capillary ECs, while renal cell carcinoma has the lowest. Cycling ECs and LECs occupy lower fraction among all tumors, and they were both absent in prostate cancer. Non-small cell lung cancer and prostate cancer exhibit low tip cell percentage. In the cells from PD-1 blockade-treated samples, the proportion of tip, cycling, and capillary ECs decreased, while venous and LECs increased. HEV and immature EC also exhibited a trend to increase after treatment. We further examined the changes in the composition ratio of ECs in different malignancies between untreated and treated tumors. Due to sample imbalance in certain tumors, we can only perform a qualitative evaluation. We found that the proportion of EC subtypes exhibited particularly notable changes after therapy in CRC (Fig. 1G). The proportions of HEV, LEC, and venous EC increased, and tip cell and cycling EC decreased. This finding may suggest that PD-1 blockades exhibit favorable efficacy against CRC, as the HEV and LECs promote lymphocyte infiltration, and the reduction in tip cells indicates impaired angiogenesis in the tumor. In NSCLC, the proportions of HEVs and LECs increased, whereas the proportion of tip cells was inherently low. In MEL and HNSC, unlike CRC and NSCLC, we observed a reduction in HEV and LEC percentage. Due to the limited number of ECs in BC, BCC, and RCC, the significance of the alterations may be diminished. We hypothesize that the baseline proportions of EC subtypes and their reaction to PD-1 may reflect the tumor type’s sensitivity to PD-1 blockades. To conduct more statistically meaningful analyses, we integrated the untreated and treated samples of all tumor types and compared the proportions of each EC subtype across the two groups (Fig. 1H). The results revealed statistically significant declines in the proportions of tip cells (p = 0.0003), cycling (p = 0.017), and arterial ECs(p = 0.034) in the treated samples, with tip cell exhibiting the most pronounced reduction. LECs showed a significant increase (p = 0.026) in treated samples, and HEV and immature ECs exhibited notable upward trends. These results collectively suggest that PD-1 blockades can attenuate angiogenesis in TME and enhance immune response by increasing HEV and LECs.

PD-1 blockades suppressed the angiogenic EC subpopulation in tumors

Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed to explore the functions of each subcluster. Tip cells are enriched in epithelial and endothelial cell migration, angiogenesis, and endothelial proliferation and differentiation (Fig. 2A). The Cycling cells, which are cells that are going through proliferation, have an enrichment in nuclear division and chromosome segregation (Fig. S3A). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis shows that tip cells are active in VEGF signaling, which proves their ability to initiate angiogenesis by sprouting (Fig. S3B). Immature EC functioning might be related to the p53 class mediator. Moreover, comparing untreated and treated group shows that untreated ECs are enriched in epithelial, blood vessel endothelial, and tissue migration, while ECs in treated tumors are enriched in MHC II protein assembly, leukocyte adhesion and activation (Fig. 2B). The expressions of VEGFR family genes were compared across EC subtypes and tip cells exhibited the highest expression of FLT1 (encoding VEGFR1) and KDR (encoding VEGFR2), along with considerable FLT4 (encoding VEGFR3) expression (Fig. 2C), suggesting this cell type is closely associated with angiogenesis. To validate the angiogenic potential of the annotated tip cell cluster, we conducted Cytotrace analysis on the integrated dataset, which rates the cell’s potential for differentiation according to the reference database. Higher Cytotrace scores (max = 1) given to the cells represent a higher potential to differentiate into other cells. Our analysis indicates that the cycling ECs have the highest average score and tip cells have the second, while immature ECs, arterial ECs, and capillary ECs are equally low (Fig. 2D, E, and F). We plotted the differentiation trajectory of ECs using Slingshot, which generated trajectories from tip cells to HEV, arterial, and cycling ECs (Fig. 2G). To clarify the relationship between tip cells in tumors and tumor prognosis, we performed survival analyses of the tip cell markers ESM1 and NID2 using bulk sequencing data from various malignancies in the TCGA database (Fig. S4). High expression of ESM1 and NID2 is related to a poorer outcome in bladder cancer (p = 0.0038) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (p = 0.0005), while the correlation is insignificant in other tumors. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that tip cells represent a distinct endothelial subpopulation with relatively high differentiation and proliferative potential, playing a pivotal role in angiogenesis initiation. Therapeutically, PD-1 blockade suppresses tumor angiogenesis by reducing the tip cell compartment within the EC population.

PD-1 blockades inhibited EC-derived CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling

We performed cell–cell communication analysis to reveal the potential mechanism by which PD-1 blockade affects EC heterogeneity. Generally, ECs have relatively stronger interactions with myeloid cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and mural cells (Fig. 3A). Comparison between the untreated and treated groups shows that epithelial cell-derived communication was generally weakened in treated tumors, and T/NK cell-derived interaction was stronger, which can be attributed to the decrease in epithelial cells and enhanced T cell immunity (Fig. 3B). Communications received by ECs from myeloid cells and T/NK cells are enhanced in the treated group, while signals sent by ECs are weakened. Since T/NK cells are the major functioning site of PD-1 blockade, we made further investigation into the interaction between ECs and T/NK cells. We examined the typical cytokines secreted by T cells and found that CCL5 exhibited a significant decrease (p < 0.0001) after treatment (Fig. S4B). But CCR5 is barely expressed in ECs of our dataset, so we turned to explore the signaling derived from ECs. The main sources of CXCL signaling are myeloid cells, ECs, and fibroblasts, which can also act as receivers (Fig. 3C). Besides, B/plasma and T/NK cells receive CXCL signaling at a high level, especially T/NK cells. CXCL12 is mainly expressed in ECs and fibroblasts, and the primary receptor for CXCL12, CXCR4, demonstrates its highest expression levels in T/NK cells, followed by B/plasma cells and myeloid cells. Ligand-receptor network revealed a reduction in the CXCL12-CXCR4 signaling between ECs and T/NK cells (Fig. 3D). We observed that CXCL12 is mainly expressed on ECs and fibroblasts, and CXCR4 is expressed on T/NK, B/plasma and Macro_mono cells (Fig. 3E). ACKR3, another CXCL12 receptor, exhibits a negligible level of expression across virtually all characterized cell lineages. We compared the CXCL12 expression of ECs and fibroblasts in two conditions, and the result shows that CXCL12 was significantly reduced in ECs (p = 0.00004) in treated tumors, while the expression only showed a trend to increase in fibroblasts (Fig. 3F). However, the CXCR4 level did not change significantly in T/NK, myeloid cells and B/plasma cells (Fig. 3G). Subsequently, we systematically profiled dynamic changes in CXCL12 expression across EC subpopulations (Fig. 3H). In treated tumors, tip cells (p = 0.003), capillary (p = 0.0005), HEV (p = 0.00003), LECs (p = 0.0005), and venous ECs (p = 0.00008) exhibited significantly decreased CXCL12 levels. Arterial ECs have the highest average expression levels, while the LECs have the lowest. Taken together, PD-1 blockade can make a strong impact on the cell–cell communication in the TME. Combined treatment of PD-1 blockade and CXCR4 antagonist significantly inhibited tumor growth

To further investigate whether a declined CXCL12 signaling affects tumor growth, we established a murine tumor model using LLC (Lewis lung carcinoma) cell line, and intervened with anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody and CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 (Fig. 4A). Four groups of mice were administered the following regimens for 4 times: (i) isotype IgG antibody (IgG), (ii) anti-PD-1 antibody monotherapy (anti-PD-1), (iii) CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 monotherapy (anti-CXCR4), and (iv) combo therapy with anti-PD-1 and CXCR4 antagonist (combo). The mice were killed 1 week after the last dose, and tissues were collected for subsequent analysis. The results demonstrated that the combo therapy group exhibited statistically significant reductions in tumor weight compared to both the control IgG group and the two monotherapy groups (combo vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-PD-1, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-CXCR4, p = 0.0004) (Fig. 4B). Both monotherapy groups significantly reduced tumor weight, with the anti-CXCR4 group exhibiting a stronger effect (anti-PD-1 vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; anti-CXCR4 vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; anti-PD-1 vs. anti-CXCR4, p = 0.001). Comparison of the tumor volume revealed a similar result to the tumor weight. The combo therapy group demonstrated the smallest tumor volumes and the slowest growth speed across the four groups (combo vs. IgG, p < 0.001; combo vs. anti-PD-1, p < 0.01; combo vs. anti-CXCR4, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). The two monotherapy groups also showed significantly reduced tumor volumes than the control group (anti-PD-1 vs. IgG, p < 0.001; anti-CXCR4 vs. IgG, p < 0.001). In summary, PD-1 and CXCR4 blockade can both suppress tumor growth, and the combination of these two agents can potentiate their efficacy. We also harvested tumor-adjacent tissues, heart tissues, and blood samples to evaluate the systemic effects of PD-1 and CXCR4 blockades. HE staining revealed an aggregated lymphocyte infiltration and necrosis in the combo therapy-treated tumor tissues (Fig. 4G). However, no obvious lymphocyte infiltration was observed in the other three tissues of the monotherapy or combo therapy group. Combined treatment of PD-1 blockade and CXCR4 antagonist repressed endothelial CXCL12 expression

To verify our findings that PD-1 blockades affect CXCL12 levels, the SDF-1 level was measured in various tissues. ELISA results showed that the combo therapy group had the lowest SDF-1 average level in tumor tissues and plasma, and the two monotherapy groups both presented significantly reduced SDF-1 levels compared to the control group (combo vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-PD-1, p = 0.0001; combo vs. anti-CXCR4, p = 0.002; anti-PD-1 vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; anti-CXCR4 vs. IgG, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4D). In tumor adjacent tissues, the combo therapy group also showed a trend to decrease (combo vs. IgG, p = 0.027), but no significant changes were observed in heart tissues. The result observed in the plasma was similar to that in tumor tissues (combo vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-PD-1, p = 0.0004; combo vs. anti-CXCR4, p = 0.017; anti-PD-1 vs. IgG, p = 0.003; anti-CXCR4 vs. IgG, p < 0.0001). To examine the CXCL12 expression dynamics in ECs, we employed fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate phenotypically defined endothelial populations (CD31 +) across distinct tissues (Fig. S5). In tumor tissues, a significant reduction in CD31+ cell rate was observed in the combo therapy group, and the percentages also decreased in the two monotherapy groups (combo vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-PD-1, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-CXCR4, p = 0.025; anti-PD-1 vs. IgG, p = 0.0001; anti-CXCR4 vs. IgG, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4E). CD31+ cell rate also reduced significantly in peritumoral tissue after combo therapy (combo vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-PD-1, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-CXCR4, p < 0.0001). Then we performed quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) on the collected ECs, measuring the mRNA expression of CXCL12. The results are consistent with those given by ELISA. In the tumor tissues, CXCL12 mRNA level showed a gradual decline in the anti-PD-1, anti-CXCR4 and combo therapy group (combo vs. IgG, p < 0.0001; combo vs. anti-PD-1, p = 0.0006; combo vs. anti-CXCR4, p = 0.003; anti-PD-1 vs. IgG, p = 0.0006; anti-CXCR4 vs. IgG, p = 0.0001) (Fig. 4F). When treated with combo therapy, the mRNA expression also showed a decrease in the adjacent normal tissues (combo vs. IgG, p = 0.007), and yet no significant difference was observed in the cardiac tissues. Immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissues for CD31 and CXCL12 showed a marked reduction in CXCL12 signal and decreased CD31–CXCL12 co-localization in the combo group versus the control group (Fig. 4H), which further validated our findings above. In conclusion, the administration of an anti-PD-1 agent can inhibit CXCL12 expression in ECs in tumor tissues and probably in adjacent tissues as well, and reduce the SDF-1 level in TME and plasma. A combination of PD-1 and CXCR4 blockades can significantly enhance this effect in mice. However, there is not enough evidence to prove that PD-1 or CXCR4 blockade, with dosages used in this research, can cause a systemic change in SDF-1 levels.

PD-1 blockade potentially regulates endothelial CXCL12 expression by transcription factor MYC

To investigate the specific pathways through which PD-1 blockades affect CXCL12 expression in ECs, we performed transcriptional regulation analysis on integrated scRNA-seq data using the R package Dorothea, which calculates transcription factor (TF) activity based on the expression levels of their target genes. Our results revealed that TF activity varies across EC subpopulations.

Cycling ECs exhibited substantially higher activity of E2F1, E2F2, E2F3, and E2F4 compared to other subclusters (Fig. 5A). These TFs play crucial roles in cell cycle regulation and DNA replication. Tip cells showed a TF profile similar to cycling ECs but displayed the highest activity of EGR1, ETS1, and HIF1A among all subclusters. Given established roles of EGR1 and HIF1A in endothelial responses to hypoxia, we infer that vascular sprouting mediated by tip cells correlates with hypoxia-induced activation of these TFs. In contrast, venous ECs, LECs, and immature ECs demonstrated low HIF1A activity, potentially reflecting their microenvironmental oxygen concentrations and insensitivity to hypoxia. Most TF activities were lower in immature ECs and LECs compared to other subclusters. We next identified TFs with significantly altered activity following PD-1 blockade treatment (Fig. 5B). HIF1A, EGR1, and ETS1 activity decreased post-treatment. Since hypoxia is a key driver of tumor angiogenesis, reduced activity of these factors suggests that PD-1 blockade may suppress tumor growth through this pathway. We also observed decreased activity of the proto-oncogene MYC, which is known to be hyperactive in tumors versus normal tissues. This reduction further demonstrates the anti-tumor effects of PD-1 blockade.

To identify which TF regulates CXCL12 downregulation in ECs, we cross-referenced significantly changed TFs (168 genes) against TF lists that potentially regulate CXCL12 from four public databases: ENCODE (68 TFs), ChEA (36 TFs), hTFtarget (76 TFs), and knockTF (40 TFs) (Fig. 5C). MYC emerged as the most likely regulator of CXCL12 expression. Consistent with previous studies, MYC activity was higher in tumor ECs versus normal ECs, and decreased significantly after anti-PD-1 treatment in both tissues (normal, p < 0.0001; tumor, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5D). This reduction was observed in all EC subpopulations except immature ECs (Fig. 5E).

To validate these findings, we examined expression of MYC-cooperating genes, all of which showed significant downregulation in the treated group (MAX, p < 0.0001; MLX, p = 0.002; MGA, p < 0.0001; CDK9, p < 0.0001; BRD2, p < 0.0001; CDKN2A, p < 0.0001) (Fig. S6A). We built MYC-overexpressing adenovirus, and qPCR results indicated successful model establishment (p = 0.0002), with CXCL12 mRNA expression significantly elevated in the MYC-overexpressing group compared to the control group (p = 0.0004) (Fig. 5F). Moreover, consistent with CXCL12, MYC staining in the tumor tissues was attenuated in the combo group (Fig. 5G), so was the co-staining of MYC and CD31, which supports that endothelial MYC expression was inhibited. Taken together, these results indicate that PD-1 blockades reduce endothelial CXCL12 expression, likely by inhibiting MYC activity, though further validation through animal or cellular experiments remains necessary.

CXCL12 expression is related to a poor outcome in multiple malignancies

To explore the effect of CXCL12 expression on tumor outcomes, we performed survival analysis using bulk RNA sequencing data of several cancers from the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) database. The CXCL12-high group exhibited a poor outcome in several malignancies, including colon adenocarcinoma (COAD, p = 0.020), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD, p = 0.038), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC, p = 0.013), and bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA, p = 0.0002) (Fig. 5H). Moreover, in an anti-PD-1 treatment cohort from TCGA, the CXCL12-high group showed a tendency toward poorer prognosis, but this association did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.27). However, in head and neck squamous cancer, prostate adenocarcinoma, skin cutaneous melanoma, and rectum adenocarcinoma, a high CXCL12 level is related to a better outcome. We also performed survival analysis on MYC expression (Fig. S6B), and the result showed that high MYC expression predicts a significantly poorer outcome in lung squamous cell carcinoma (p = 0.001), ovarian cancer (p = 0.012), and skin cutaneous cell carcinoma (p = 0.018). In addition, we analyzed the correlation between MYC and CXCL12 expression in tumors using TCGA datasets. However, their correlation varied across tumor types: weak–to–moderate positive in bladder cancer (r = 0.227, p < 0.0001), negative in colon cancer (r = − 0.264, p < 0.0001), and negligible in head–and–neck squamous cell carcinoma (r = − 0.084, p = 0.047) and stomach adenocarcinoma (r = 0.016, p = 0.739) (Fig. 5I). This indicates that CXCL12 may play a more complex role in tumor development and the body’s immune response. Since bulk sequencing results cannot clearly determine gene expression levels in ECs, more in-depth animal or cellular experiments are required to investigate the regulatory relationship between MYC and CXCL12 in ECs.

Discussion

The efficacy of PD-1 blockade varies across different malignancies and individuals, which remains a major obstacle limiting its clinical application. Therefore, strategies to enhance the effectiveness of PD-1 blockade represent a longstanding research focus. Blood vessels and vascular ECs constitute critical components of the tumor microenvironment, playing a pivotal role in tumor growth and development [5, 23]. This has led to the emergence of combining anti-angiogenic interventions with immunotherapy as a promising strategy [24]. Substantial clinical and preclinical evidence demonstrates that combining PD-1 blockades with anti-angiogenic agents, such as bevacizumab, significantly improves anti-tumor efficacy compared to monotherapy [25–30].

In this study, we established a comprehensive EC atlas of untreated and PD-1 blockade-treated tumors through integrated single-cell analysis. The atlas reveals considerable heterogeneity in ECs between tumor and normal tissues and across different tumor types. Tip cells drive vascular sprouting, cycling ECs represent actively dividing populations, and HEV ECs and LECs are related to lymphocyte infiltration into the tumor sites. Variations in these EC subpopulations across tumors may correlate with responsiveness to PD-1 blockades. As we observed in the data of CRC, which is from the mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) type, the tip cell percentage decreased, and the HEV and LEC increased in the treated group. This indicates that PD-1 blockades, by altering vascular endothelial heterogeneity, enhanced anti-tumor immunity and inhibited tumor angiogenesis at the same time in dMMR CRC. The dMMR is known to predict a better outcome in immunotherapy, with a response rate of 43.8% in a dMMR colorectal cancer cohort treated with pembrolizumab [31], which is much higher than in other malignancies [32, 33]. This high response rate is usually attributed to the higher mutation burden and extensive immunogenicity. However, our result suggests a new perspective that a decreased proportion of tip cells and increased HEV and LECs might be related to a higher responsiveness to PD-1 blockade. However, larger-scale single-cell studies integrating more samples are needed to validate this hypothesis.

Our analysis demonstrates a statistically significant reduction in the proportions of tip cells and cycling ECs within tumor endothelia following anti-PD-1 treatment. Supporting this, flow cytometry experiments in this study confirmed a decrease in the overall proportion of ECs in tumor tissues following anti-PD-1 therapy. These results indicate that PD-1 blockade can suppress angiogenesis activity within tumors. Beyond altering EC composition, PD-1 blockade also induces functional changes. Functional enrichment analysis reveals that PD-1 blockades shift EC function away from tumor migration and vascular proliferation toward antigen presentation and modulation of immune cell activity. This functional reprogramming likely contributes to PD-1 blockade-induced T cell activation, enhanced immune cell infiltration, and improved anti-tumor effects.

These findings raise a fundamental question about how PD-1 blockades influence ECs. Given that PD-1 expression is predominantly confined to T cells and virtually absent on stromal cells, this influence must necessarily occur through indirect mechanisms. Thus, we sought to identify a signaling pathway connecting angiogenesis, ECs, and T cells. The CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway fulfills this role. Heidegger et al. identified CXCL12 as a marker for arterial ECs within TECs through scRNA-seq analysis of human prostate cancer tissues [12]. They claimed CXCL12 interacts with CXCR4 on tip cells, thereby promoting neovascularization and regulating prostate cancer cell migration. Furthermore, they demonstrated via cell proliferation and scratch assays that the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 inhibits tumor cell proliferation and migration. Regrettably, this study did not validate the effect of AMD3100 in animal tumor models. Steele et al. discovered that functional tumor-specific CD8⁺ T cells are expelled from tumors via lymphatic vessels [15]. Using genetically modified mouse models and scRNA-seq, they revealed that this phenomenon is closely associated with CXCL12/CXCR4 interactions between LECs and effector T cells. Moreover, AMD3100 enhanced anti-tumor efficacy by inhibiting the efflux of functional CD8⁺ T cells from tumor sites. Similar to our study, Steele et al. also achieved superior anti-tumor effects by combining AMD3100 with a PD-L1 blockade compared to either agent alone. Intriguingly, although the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis is recognized for promoting tumor growth and metastasis, certain studies report that inhibiting or silencing CXCL12/CXCR4 fails to yield the expected antitumor effects but instead induces tumor cell immune tolerance [34, 35]. This indicates a dual role for CXCL12/CXCR4 within the TME. Building on this concept, a recent study demonstrated that a CXCR4 partial agonist reduces immunosuppressive neutrophils in the TME [36]. When combined with PD-1 blockade in mouse gastric cancer models, this dual therapy showed significantly superior efficacy compared to monotherapy. These findings suggest that CXCL12 may promote tumor growth and migration through two mechanisms: fostering new blood vessel formation and facilitating effector T cell tolerance and efflux from the tumor site.

However, our results diverge from the studies mentioned above in several aspects. While arterial ECs and tip cells within our integrated scRNA-seq dataset did exhibit higher expression of CXCL12 and CXCR4 compared to other endothelial subtypes, few tip cells were identified in the prostate cancer ECs in this dataset. Additionally, contrary to Steele et al.’s findings, CXCL12 expression in LECs in our dataset was the lowest among all EC subpopulations. Therefore, the interaction between LECs and T cells may not play a predominant role in the mechanism mediated by the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway. We also observed alterations in certain cytokines secreted by T cells, specifically CCL5, IFN-γ, and TNF-α. Since PD-1 blockades act on T cells, these changes may serve as initiating factors for the altered CXCL12 expression in ECs, whereas further experimental validation is required to confirm this.

Through animal experiments, we validated the hypothesis derived from bioinformatic analysis: anti-PD-1 treatment reduces SDF-1 levels within tumor tissues and suppresses CXCL12 expression in ECs. The combination of PD-1 blockade and AMD3100 inhibited tumor growth, with an effect significantly superior to monotherapy. Furthermore, we observed that the reduction in SDF-1 levels induced by PD-1 blockade was not only evident in tumor tissues but also occurred systemically in plasma and showed a similar trend in peri-tumoral normal tissues. However, this phenomenon was not observed in myocardial tissue, necessitating further investigation by measuring SDF-1 levels in more organs. Consistent with Steele et al.’s conclusions, PD-1 blockade or AMD3100 treatment enhanced immune cell infiltration within tumor tissues and augmented tumor cell necrosis, while no changes were observed in surrounding normal tissues or myocardial tissue via H&E staining.

We further investigated how the PD-1 blockade modulates CXCL12 expression in ECs. Transcriptional regulatory analysis identified MYC as the transcription factor likely regulating CXCL12 expression among the highly variable genes. MYC is a unique proto-oncogene because its oncogenicity typically relies on elevated activity rather than sequence mutations [37, 38]. During tumor progression, MYC plays a pivotal role in cell proliferation, neovascularization, and immune evasion by repressing or promoting a cascade of gene expressions [39]. Our study revealed a significant decrease in MYC activity in tumors following PD-1 blockade treatment, accompanied by reduced expression of multiple cooperating molecules. Thus, a plausible pathway is that the PD-1 blockade reduces CXCL12 secretion via suppression of endothelial MYC activity and thereby inhibits neovascularization. We verified the effect of MYC using adenovirus overexpression experiments and immunofluorescence assays. The above results and our survival analysis suggest the significance and feasibility of targeting CXCL12 for tumor therapy. However, our correlation analysis using TCGA data did not reveal a clear connection between MYC and CXCL12, likely because bulk sequencing cannot analyze specific cell types, and we still need animal or cellular overexpression experiments to explore.

In summary, our study shows that PD-1 blockade can affect tumor EC heterogeneity and suppress tumor angiogenesis by inhibiting the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway, potentially through suppressing MYC activity. PD-1 blockades and CXCR4 antagonists synergistically enhance anti-tumor efficacy. The CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway is a potential target for enhancing the effectiveness of PD-1 blockade.
