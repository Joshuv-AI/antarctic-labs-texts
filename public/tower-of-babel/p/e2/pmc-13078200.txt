COP1 regulates the abundance and stability of the Arabidopsis retromer protein AtVPS29 via the proteasome pathway
Authors: Wang Ki Min, Seung Ju Lee, Jong Tae Song, Hak Soo Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVacuolar protein sorting 29 (VPS29) is a pivotal component of the retromer complex, which plays a central role in endosomal trafficking. Numerous studies have investigated its functions in various eukaryotic systems; however, research in plants is still at an early stage. Moreover, the mechanisms controlling its abundance and stability remain largely unknown. In this study, we elucidated the role of constitutive photomorphogenic 1 (COP1) in regulating the abundance of Arabidopsis VPS29 (AtVPS29), a component of the trimeric retromer complex. Degradation of AtVPS29 is delayed by treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132, resulting in the accumulation of ubiquitinated AtVPS29 in MG132-treated plants. The AtVPS29 level is higher in cop1-6 mutant seedlings than in wild-type seedlings under both continuous light and dark conditions. In addition, AtVPS29 abundance is reduced in COP1-overexpressing plants compared with wild-type plants. Furthermore, COP1 directly interacts with AtVPS29, and AtVPS29 is ubiquitinated by the E3 ligase activity of COP1. Moreover, genes encoding the trimeric retromer complex components AtVPS35a and AtVPS26a are upregulated in cop1-6 mutants. Together, our data indicate that COP1 negatively regulates the abundance and stability of AtVPS29 through its E3 ligase activity and suggest that COP1 is involved in regulating the function of the trimeric retromer complex.Keywords: AtVPS29, COP1, E3 ubiquitin ligase, retromer, ubiquitination

Introduction

Ubiquitination is a cellular process in which a small protein called ubiquitin is attached to a target protein. Three enzymes – an activating enzyme (E1), a conjugating enzyme (E2), and an E3 ligase – act sequentially to attach ubiquitin to the target protein.1,2 There are two major types of ubiquitination: monoubiquitination and polyubiquitination.3 Monoubiquitination serves as a signal for processes such as DNA repair, endocytosis, and nuclear export. In contrast, K48-linked polyubiquitination typically targets proteins for degradation by the 26S proteasome complex. K63-linked polyubiquitination, however, is known to be involved in DNA repair, inflammation, and signaling. Ubiquitination is reversible through the action of a large family of deubiquitinating enzymes that remove ubiquitin from target proteins.4

COP1 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase containing a RING-finger motif, a coiled-coil domain, and WD40 repeats, and it functions as a dimer.5,6 COP1 polyubiquitinates various target proteins, including transcription factors and photoreceptors, leading to their degradation by the 26S proteasome.7,8 COP1 regulates multiple aspects of plant growth and development, including photomorphogenic responses to far-red, red, blue, and ultraviolet-B light, organ development, stress responses, flowering, and crosstalk between light and hormone signaling.9–13 In recent years, many studies have revealed various novel interactors of COP1, expanding and elucidating the multilayered role of COP1 on regulating plant growth and development.14–18

VPS29 is a component of the retromer complex, together with VPS26 and VPS35, in both animals and plants.19,20 The retromer is a conserved complex localized to the cytosolic face of endosomes and is responsible for transporting membrane proteins from late endosomes to the trans-Golgi network.21–24 In plants, VPS26, VPS29, and VPS35 form complexes that participate in processes such as programmed cell death,25 oil body biogenesis and degradation during vegetative growth,26 and root hair growth.27 AtVPS29 specifically contributes to the maturation of storage proteins 12S globulin and 2S albumin28 and to the establishment of cell polarity during plant organogenesis.29 Recently, AtVPS29 has also been reported to be involved in activation of the sumoylation system through upregulation of the E3 SUMO ligase AtSIZ1.30 However, the mechanisms governing AtVPS29 stability and abundance remain unknown.

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms controlling AtVPS29 abundance and stability, we examined AtVPS29 modification following proteasome inhibitor treatment and found that AtVPS29 is ubiquitinated and that its degradation is suppressed by proteasome inhibition. Functional analyses demonstrated that AtVPS29 is ubiquitinated in a COP1-dependent manner through direct interaction, and that AtVPS29 abundance and stability depend on COP1 activity, suggesting that COP1 participates in the control of AtVPS29-mediated protein retrograde transport. To our knowledge, this study provides the first evidence, throughout both plant and animal systems, describing the regulation of VPS29 stability and abundance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia-0 was used as the wild-type (WT). To analyze the effects of COP1 on AtVPS29 expression and protein levels, the cop1-6 mutant allele was used. Seeds were sterilized with a 5% sodium hypochlorite solution. The plants were grown on plates containing Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 0.8% agar. All seedlings and plants were grown at 22 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle in a growth chamber.

Production of transgenic Arabidopsis plants

To overexpress AtVPS29 and COP1 in plants, we used previously reported 35S-AtVPS29-HA3 and XVE-COP1-Myc6 transgenic lines.30 Briefly, to generate 35S-AtVPS29-HA3 transgenic plants, full-length AtVPS29 complementary DNA (cDNA) was amplified by PCR, digested with appropriate restriction enzymes, and then ligated with restricted plant expression vector pBA002.31 Resulting plasmid was transformed into the Agrobacterium strain GV3101, and then introduced into WT via floral dipping method.32 To generate XVE-COP1-Myc6 transgenic plants, full-length COP1 cDNA was amplified by PCR, digested with appropriate restriction enzymes, and then ligated with the restricted plant expression vector pER8.33 The resulting plasmid was transformed into the Agrobacterium strain GV3101, and then introduced into WT via the floral dipping method. In XVE-COP1-Myc6 transgenic plants, COP1-Myc6 expression was induced by treatment with 10 µM β-estradiol. The 35S-AtVPS29-HA3/cop1-6 transgenic plants were generated by crossing cop1-6 mutants with 35S-AtVPS29-HA3 transgenic plants. The primers used for cloning are listed in Table 1.

Effects of MG132 on AtVPS29 level

The effect of MG132 treatment on AtVPS29 levels was examined according to a previously reported method.30 Briefly, 35S-AtVPS29-HA3-expressing plants were treated with 50 μM MG132 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 h, and AtVPS29-HA3 levels were then estimated by western blotting using an anti-HA antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). An anti-Actin antibody (Abcam) was used as a loading control.

Detection of ubiquitinated AtVPS29 was also performed according to a previously reported method.30 Briefly, total proteins from WT and 35S-AtVPS29-HA3-expressing plants were extracted with IP buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Nonidet P-40 (NP-40), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The extracts were immunoprecipitated with an anti-HA antibody, and the immunoprecipitates were analyzed by western blotting using either an anti-HA antibody or an anti-ubiquitin antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Cell-free degradation assay

Fifteen-day-old WT plants were incubated in liquid MS medium containing 50 µM MG132 for 15 h. The samples were harvested and ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Total proteins were then extracted using a degradation buffer consisting of 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl₂, 4 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, and 10 mM ATP, as previously described.34–36 Protein concentrations were determined using the Bio-Rad protein assay and normalized to equal concentrations with degradation buffer. MG132 was then added to the in vitro degradation mixtures containing 500 µg of total protein, to a final concentration of 50 µM. Subsequently, 100 ng of GST-AtVPS29 protein was added to each reaction. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 22 °C for 4 h, and samples were collected at the indicated time points. GST-AtVPS29 levels were analyzed by western blotting using an anti-GST antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). An anti-Actin antibody was used as a loading control. Quantification of the band intensities of GST-AtVPS29 was performed using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/).

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay

The cDNAs of COP1 and AtVPS29 were cloned into the Gateway entry vector pENTR3C (Invitrogen). The cDNAs were introduced into the binary Gateway destination vectors pBA3136 and pBA3132,37 respectively, using LR clonase (Invitrogen). The resulting plasmids were subsequently transformed into the Agrobacterium strain GV3101, and then co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. After 4 d, YFP signals were detected using SP8 X Confocal Microscope (Leica); excitation at 514 nm and emission at 550–600 nm. The images were taken at ZT 8.

Preparation of recombinant proteins

To produce full-length COP1 or its deletion mutants in vitro, the cDNAs encoding full-length COP1 and its deletion mutants were cloned into the pMAL-c2x vector. Detailed information on the COP1 deletion mutants was described in a previous study10 (R-CC, amino acids 1–255; CC-WD40, amino acids 93–675; WD40, amino acids 237–675). To produce full-length AtVPS29 in vitro, the cDNA encoding full-length AtVPS29 was cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector. Maltose-binding protein (MBP)-COP1 and glutathione S-transferase (GST)-fused AtVPS29 proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli strain BL21 and purified as previously described.38 Purified recombinant proteins were separated by 8% SDS–PAGE, and then stained with the staining buffer containing 0.25% Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250.

In vitro binding assay

To examine the in vitro binding of AtVPS29 to full-length COP1 or its deletion mutants, 2 µg of full-length MBP-COP1 or deletion-mutant MBP-COP1 bait proteins were added to reaction mixtures containing 2 µg of full-length GST-AtVPS29 prey protein, and the reactions were then performed as previously described.30 After incubation with amylose resin, bound proteins were separated by 11% SDS–PAGE and detected by western blotting using an anti-GST antibody. Inputs were detected by western blotting using an anti-GST and an anti-MBP antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Examination of the AtVPS29 level in cop1-6 mutants

To examine the effect of COP1 loss on AtVPS29 levels, WT and cop1-6 mutant plants were grown on MS medium under light or dark conditions. After 4 d, total proteins were extracted from the samples, separated by 11% SDS‒PAGE, and AtVPS29 levels were then determined by western blotting using an anti-AtVPS29 antibody, which was generated as previously described.30

Effects of COP1 overexpression on AtVPS29 levels in vivo

To examine the effects of COP1 overexpression on AtVPS29 levels, XVE-COP1-Myc6 transgenic plants were grown on MS medium for 15 d and then treated with 10 µM β-estradiol for 15 h. Total proteins were extracted from the leaves, and AtVPS29 and COP1-Myc6 were detected by western blotting using either an anti-AtVPS29 antibody or an anti-Myc antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Effects of COP1 loss on AtVPS29 concentration in vivo

To investigate the effect of the proteasome pathway on AtVPS29 levels in 35S-AtVPS29-HA3/WT and 35S-AtVPS29-HA3/cop1-6 transgenic plants, 15-day-old WT, cop1-6, 35S-AtVPS29-HA3/WT, and 35S-AtVPS29-HA3/cop1-6 plants were treated with or without 50 μM MG132 for 16 h. Total proteins were extracted from the samples, and AtVPS29-HA3 levels were analyzed by western blotting using an anti-HA antibody. An anti-actin antibody was used as a loading control. Quantification of the band intensities of AtVPS29-HA3was performed using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/).

In vitro ubiquitination assays

In vitro ubiquitination assays were performed as previously described.38 Reaction mixtures (30 µL) contained 500 ng MBP-COP1 (E3), 100 ng each of rhUBE1 (E1) and UbcH5b (E2) (both from R&D Systems), 50 ng GST-AtVPS29, and His₆-ubiquitin (R&D Systems) in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 2 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl₂, and 2 mM DTT. After incubation for 2 h at 30 °C, the reactions were terminated by adding SDS–PAGE sample buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol and heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated on 8% SDS–PAGE gels, and ubiquitinated GST-AtVPS29 was detected by western blotting using an anti-GST antibody.

Transcript levels of COP1, AtVPS29, AtVPS35a, and AtVPS26a genes

WT, cop1-6 mutant, and transgenic plants expressing XVE-COP1-Myc6 were grown on MS medium plates for 4 or 15 d under light or dark conditions. The plant samples were thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using the FavorPrep™ Plant Total RNA Mini Kit (Favorgen). The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the ReverTra Ace® qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (Toyobo). qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler® 480 instrument using the KAPA SYBR® FAST qPCR Master Mix (2×) (Kapa Biosystems) with gene-specific primers. Actin 2 (ACT2) was used as the internal reference gene. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis about cell-free degradation assays and qRT–PCR were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (https://www.r-project.org/). Differences in the means of all data were determined by Student's t-test.

Results

AtVPS29 is degraded by ubiquitin-proteasome pathway

VPS29 functions as a component of the retromer complex that mediates the retrograde transport of membrane proteins,19,20 and Arabidopsis VPS29 (AtVPS29) has also been reported to regulate the sumoylation system.30 However, because the regulatory mechanisms governing AtVPS29 stability and degradation remain unknown, we investigated whether AtVPS29 is subject to ubiquitin-mediated degradation. To this end, we generated transgenic plants overexpressing AtVPS29 under the control of the 35S promoter. These 35S-AtVPS29-HA₃ transgenic plants were treated with the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132, and AtVPS29-HA₃ levels were analyzed by western blotting. MG132 treatment resulted in increased AtVPS29 levels in the transgenic plants (Figure 1A).

After a 15 h incubation, AtVPS29-HA₃ levels were assessed by western blotting using an anti-HA antibody. Actin was used as a loading control. (B) Immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-HA antibody, and AtVPS29-HA₃ was detected by western blotting with an anti-HA antibody. (C) Immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-HA antibody, and the ubiquitinated AtVPS29-HA₃ was detected by western blotting of immunoprecipitated proteins, using an anti-ubiquitin antibody. The asterisk (*) indicates a non-specific band. (D) Cell-free degradation assay. Total proteins were extracted from fifteen-day-old WT plants treated with or without MG132 and mixed with purified GST-AtVPS29. After incubation for the indicated times, the GST-AtVPS29 levels were analyzed by western blotting using an anti-GST antibody. An anti-actin antibody was used as a loading control. (E) The band intensities for the cell-free degradation assays were shown as a graph. The relative band intensities for GST-AtVPS29 were normalized to numerical values based on a value of 1.0 for the protein levels at 0 hr. The results are expressed as the means ± SD (n = 3). Asterisks indicate statistically significant differences in the relative protein levels of GST-AtVPS29 (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; Student's t-test) between MG132-treated and untreated samples.

To detect ubiquitinated AtVPS29, we performed immunoprecipitation with an anti-HA antibody using total protein extracts from MG132-treated transgenic plants. Transgenic plants expressing 35S-AtVPS29-HA3 were treated with MG132, immunoprecipitated with an anti-HA antibody, and then immunoblotted with an anti-HA antibody. The results clearly showed the presence of multiple additional protein bands besides the band for AtVPS29-HA3 (Figure 1B). We speculated that these bands were ubiquitinated AtVPS29-HA3, and to confirm this, we conducted additional immunoprecipitation. Transgenic plants expressing 35S-AtVPS29-HA3 were treated with or without MG132, immunoprecipitated with an anti-HA antibody, and then immunoblotted with an anti-ubiquitin antibody. The results revealed that ubiquitinated AtVPS29-HA3 were detected in the samples treated with MG132, while the samples without MG132 showed no difference compared to WT samples (Figure 1C). Taken together, these findings indicate that AtVPS29 is ubiquitinated in vivo.

Furthermore, we performed a cell-free degradation assay using total protein extracts from WT plants treated with or without MG132. Following incubation with purified GST-AtVPS29, AtVPS29 levels were analyzed by western blotting. The results showed that AtVPS29 degradation was delayed in MG132-treated samples compared with untreated samples (Figure 1D,E). Collectively, these observations indicate that AtVPS29 is degraded by the 26S proteasome following ubiquitination.

COP1 physically interacts with AtVPS29

It has been reported that target proteins whose stability and abundance are regulated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of COP1 often contain a conserved amino acid motif, xEExxxVPD/E.39–43 Interestingly, AtVPS29 also contains a similar conserved sequence, xDExxxVPE (Figure 2A), suggesting that AtVPS29 may directly interact with COP1. We therefore examined the potential interaction between AtVPS29 and COP1 using both BiFC and in vitro pull-down assays.

For the BiFC assay, COP1 was fused to the N-terminal fragment of EYFP, whereas AtVPS29 was fused to the C-terminal fragment of EYFP. The fusion proteins were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana by bacterial infiltration, and fluorescence signals were visualized using CLSM. Fluorescence was observed at the cytoplasmic membrane region (Figure 2B), indicating an in vivo interaction between COP1 and AtVPS29.

For the in vitro pull-down assay, recombinant MBP-COP1, MBP-RING-CC, MBP-CC-WD40, and MBP-WD40 (Figure 2C) were expressed in E. coli and purified using affinity columns (Figure 2D). The purified proteins were incubated with GST-AtVPS29. The results showed that GST-AtVPS29 interacted with MBP-COP1 and MBP-CC-WD40, but not with MBP-RING-CC or MBP-WD40 (Figure 2E), indicating that the WD40 domain is important for interaction with AtVPS29. However, deletion of the coiled-coil domain, which mediates COP1 dimerization, abolished the interaction with AtVPS29 (Figure 2E). These results suggest that both the WD40 domain and the dimerization domain are required for the interaction with AtVPS29. COP1 dimerization via the coiled-coil domain has been reported previously.5 Collectively, these findings support a direct interaction between COP1 and AtVPS29 in vivo.

AtVPS29 level is regulated by COP1 in vivo

Based on these data, we next examined the effect of COP1 on AtVPS29 levels. It is well-known that light attenuates the activity of COP1.44–46 To this end, to determine whether the stability of AtVPS29 is affected by light and COP1, AtVPS29 protein levels were analyzed by western blotting using total protein extracts from WT and cop1-6 seedlings grown under continuous light or dark conditions. The results showed that AtVPS29 levels were higher in cop1-6 mutants than in WT plants under both conditions (Figure 3A). In addition, AtVPS29 levels were quantified in transgenic plants expressing the XVE-COP1-Myc6 construct. Following β-estradiol-induced expression of COP1-Myc6, total proteins were extracted and AtVPS29 levels were assessed by western blotting. As expected, AtVPS29 levels decreased after β-estradiol-induced COP1 expression (Figure 3C).

To determine whether changes in AtVPS29 protein levels were associated with transcriptional regulation, we measured AtVPS29 transcript levels by qRT-PCR in cop1-6 mutants and COP1-overexpressing XVE-COP1-Myc6 transgenic plants. AtVPS29 transcript levels were higher in the cop1-6 mutant than in the WT under both light and dark conditions (Figure 3B). In contrast, AtVPS29 transcript levels in β-estradiol-treated transgenic plants were similar to those in untreated transgenic plants (Figure 3D).

The effect of COP1 on AtVPS29 protein levels was further analyzed in transgenic WT and cop1-6 plants expressing the 35S-AtVPS29-HA3 construct. Total proteins were extracted from samples treated with or without 50 μM MG132, and AtVPS29-HA3 levels were examined by western blotting. AtVPS29-HA3 levels were higher in 35S-AtVPS29-HA3/cop1-6 plants than in 35S-AtVPS29-HA3/WT plants, and these levels increased after MG132 treatment in both transgenic lines (Figure 3E).

Collectively, these findings suggest that COP1 regulates AtVPS29 abundance as well as the stability of the AtVPS29 protein, and that AtVPS29 is destabilized by the 26S proteasome following ubiquitination.

AtVPS291 is ubiquitinated by COP1 in vitro

The direct interaction between AtVPS29 and COP1 provides preliminary evidence that AtVPS29 may serve as a substrate of COP1. To further investigate this possibility, recombinant GST-AtVPS29 and MBP-COP1 proteins were expressed in E. coli, purified using glutathione or amylose affinity columns, and subjected to in vitro ubiquitination assays. GST-AtVPS29 was ubiquitinated in the presence of MBP-COP1 but not in the presence of MBP alone (Figure 4), indicating that COP1 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase for AtVPS29.

AtVPS29 is ubiquitinated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of COP1. In vitro ubiquitination assays were performed in the presence or absence of recombinant rhUBE1 (E1), UbcH5b (E2), MBP-COP1 (E3), GST-AtVPS29, and His₆-ubiquitin. After incubation at 30 °C for 2 h, ubiquitinated GST-AtVPS29 was detected by western blotting using an anti-GST antibody.

Expression of AtVPS35a and AtVPS26a genes is negatively regulated by COP1

In plants, VPS26, VPS29, and VPS35 are localized to the prevacuolar compartment and function as a retromer trimeric complex through interactions with each other,24,29 suggesting that they may exhibit similar expression patterns. Therefore, we also examined the expression of the AtVPS35a and AtVPS26a genes. qRT-PCR analysis using total RNA extracted from seedlings grown under light or dark conditions showed that the transcript levels of both AtVPS35a and AtVPS26a were higher in the cop1-6 mutants than in the WT under both conditions (Figure 5), a pattern similar to that observed for AtVPS29 (Figure 3B). These results indicate that COP1 negatively regulates the expression of AtVPS26, AtVPS29, and AtVPS35, and suggest that COP1 participates in regulating retromer complex function.

Discussion

In this study, we present evidence that COP1 plays a role in regulating the level and stability of AtVPS29. Because the mechanism underlying AtVPS29 protein stability has not yet been elucidated, and because numerous studies have shown that the stability and degradation of many proteins are controlled by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway,2,3,8 we first examined whether AtVPS29 stability is regulated through the ubiquitin-proteasome system. Analysis of AtVPS29 protein levels in 35S-AtVPS29-HA₃-overexpressing transgenic plants treated with MG132 showed that AtVPS29-HA₃ levels increased upon MG132 treatment (Figure 1A). Moreover, MG132 treatment led to the accumulation of ubiquitinated AtVPS29 (Figure 1B,C) and the delay of degradation of GST-AtVPS29 (Figure 1D,E). These data strongly suggest that AtVPS29 is degraded by the 26S proteasome following ubiquitination.

Previous studies reported that COP1 protein accumulates in the vps29-3 mutant,30 raising the possibility that AtVPS29 directly interacts with COP1. It has been established that Val-Pro (VP) peptide motifs with a core sequence V-P-E/D-Φ-G, where Φ denotes a hydrophobic residue, can bind to the WD40 domain of COP1.40,43 Deletion of regions containing VP peptide motifs disrupts the interaction between COP1 substrates and the COP1 WD40 domain.40,42,47 Because AtVPS29 also contains a VP peptide motif implicated in COP1 binding (Figure 2A), we examined the direct interaction between AtVPS29 and COP1 using BiFC and pull-down assays. Consistent with this hypothesis, AtVPS29 directly interacted with COP1 (Figure 2B,E). Furthermore, we found that AtVPS29 binds to the WD40 domain of COP1 and that COP1 dimerization is required for its interaction with AtVPS29 (Figure 2E).

Because this direct interaction suggests that AtVPS29 stability may be regulated by COP1, we analyzed AtVPS29 protein levels in the cop1-6 mutant and in β-estradiol-inducible XVE-COP1-Myc6 transgenic lines. The results showed that AtVPS29 levels were higher in the cop1-6 mutant than in the WT, whereas β-estradiol-induced COP1-Myc6 expression led to a reduction in AtVPS29 levels (Figure 3A,C). However, AtVPS29 transcript levels did not change despite the reduction in AtVPS29 protein levels in XVE-COP1-Myc6 plants following COP1-Myc6 induction (Figure 3D). Therefore, these data indicate that AtVPS29 abundance is regulated by COP1 at the post-translational level. It is notable that the protein levels of AtVPS29 were higher than WT at both continuous light and dark conditions (Figure 3A). Light interferes with the activity of COP1 in the nucleus, characterized by the nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling of COP1, light-dependent degradation of SPAs, and interruption of the interaction between COP1 and SPAs.44–46 Meanwhile, the interaction between COP1 and AtVPS29 occurred in the cytoplasmic regions (Figure 2B), making the accumulation of AtVPS29 in cop1-6 mutants under dark conditions confusing. We speculate that the accumulation of AtVPS29 in cop1-6 mutants, regardless of light conditions is due to the possible nuclear localization of AtVPS29. Recently, we suggested that AtVPS29 localizes not only to the cytoplasmic regions but also to the nucleus.30 Based on this, we consider that COP1-mediated degradation of AtVPS29 may also occur in the nucleus, yet the exact molecular mechanisms regulating the subcellular localization of AtVPS29 and the specific role of AtVPS29 in the nucleus are largely unknown.

The direct interaction between COP1 and AtVPS29, the increase in AtVPS29 levels upon MG132 treatment, and the COP1-dependent regulation of AtVPS29 protein levels collectively suggest that AtVPS29 is ubiquitinated by COP1. COP1 is well-known to possess E3 ubiquitin ligase activity toward various substrates.7,8 An in vitro ubiquitination assay showed that AtVPS29 was ubiquitinated in reactions containing COP1 (Figure 4). These results further support the conclusion that the stability and abundance of AtVPS29 are regulated post-translationally by the E3 ligase activity of COP1.

It is widely recognized that VPS29 functions as part of the trimeric retromer complex.20,30 Consequently, we examined the expression of genes encoding AtVPS35a and AtVPS26a, which are also components of the retromer complex.20 As expected, their transcript levels were higher in cop1-6 mutants than in the WT under both light and dark conditions (Figure 5). These findings suggest that COP1 participates in regulating retromer complex function by modulating retromer component levels at both the transcriptional and post-translational levels. Yet it is largely unknown whether light conditions affect the localization, abundance, and stability of retromer complex components. However, we suspect that light may regulate the localization or activity of retromer proteins, as auxin efflux carrier PIN-FORMED 1/2 (PIN1/2) undergo endocytosis, vacuolar degradation, and recycling responding to light conditions, which processes that retromer complex is engaged in Laxmi et al. and Sassi et al.48,49. Examination of light-regulation of retromer components can broaden our understanding about light-dependent reconstruction of plasma membrane (PM) proteins and subsequent regulation of plant growth and development.

In plants, the retromer complex regulates vacuolar targeting and recycling back to the PM of PM proteins.29,50,51 Retromer subunits AtVPS35, AtVPS29, and SORTING NEXIN 1 (SNX1) are essential for vacuolar degradation and relocalization throughout the PM of auxin efflux carrier PIN proteins.50 Especially, loss of AtVPS29 results in reduced PM localization and ectopic accumulation at enlarged compartments of PIN1/2.29,50,51 Meanwhile, it was reported that PIN2 degradation, which is important for building root gravitropism,52 is compromised in cop1 mutants upon gravitropic force, indicating that COP1 also participates in intracellular trafficking and degradation of PIN2.49 Yet how COP1 mediates these processes is largely unknown, and COP1-AtVPS29 axis may explain the dynamic distribution of PINs upon external stimuli. Moreover, as many other PM-localized channel or receptor proteins are subjected to endocytosis, vacuolar targeting, and recycling back to the PM,53 this study provides insight into various types of signal transduction and hormonal regulation to fine-tune plant growth and development.

COP1, as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, regulates various layers of the plant life cycle, including germination, photomorphogenesis, phytohormone-regulated growth and development, flowering, and stress responses.35,38,54–57 The main mechanism underlying these roles is to mediate the polyubiquitination and 26S proteasome-dependent degradation of nuclear-localized transcription factors, which regulate gene expression for various cellular processes.35,38,42,58 Substrates for COP1 besides transcription factors have also been characterized.55,57,59,60 Besides the well-known regulation of photomorphogenesis by COP1, it is worth noting that COP1 plays a pivotal role in developing abiotic stress responses. COP1 regulates abiotic stress responses, including cold, drought, and salt stress, by modulating the amount and SUMO-conjugating activity of the E3 SUMO ligase AtSIZ1.10 Moreover, COP1 controls stomatal aperture and subsequent leaf temperature to affect tolerance-related gene expressions,61 and regulates internal sucrose levels to build tolerance towards salt stress.62 Meanwhile, PM proteins and lipids are essential for the perception of abiotic stress, and their dynamic reconstruction and trafficking are considered as important cellular mechanisms to cope with various stress conditions.63,64 This study focused on the role of COP1 in regulating AtVPS29, a subunit of the retromer complex required for intracellular trafficking, thereby suggesting a different route of COP1 in regulating abiotic stress responses. In conclusion, we demonstrate that AtVPS29 abundance is regulated by the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway through the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of COP1. To our knowledge, this study provides the first evidence that the abundance and stability of the retromer protein VPS29 are controlled by an E3 ubiquitin ligase. Given the diverse functional roles of VPS29 in various eukaryotic systems, including plants and animals, our findings provide a foundation for further investigation into VPS29 function as a retromer complex component at the endosomal membrane. In addition, future studies will help elucidate the role of COP1 in the retrograde transport of membrane proteins through endocytic recycling and subsequent regulation of various plant growth and developmental processes.
