Structure-based functionality of a multi-domain periplasmic chitodextrinase from marine bacterium Vibrio cholerae
Authors: Kanokbhorn Khumnonkhro, Thitipat Chongrungreang, Shohei Sakuda, Robert Chales Robinson, Tamo Fukamizo, Wipa Suginta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriplasmic chitodextrinase (Chdx) is involved in chitin metabolism in Vibrio species. Chdxs from Vibrio cholerae (VcChdx) and Vibrio harveyi consist of six domains: an N-terminal family 5/12 carbohydrate-binding module (N-CBM), two immunoglobulin-like domains (IgL-1 and IgL-2), a GH18 catalytic domain (CatD), a chitin-binding domain (ChBD), and a C-terminal family 5/12 carbohydrate-binding module (C-CBM). We produced recombinant VcChdx, which exhibited a single band at 116 kDa on SDS-PAGE. VcChdx fwas successfully crystallized in the ligand-free and bound state with the inhibitor allosamidin, providing insights into the catalytic center and substrate-binding groove of CatD, which tightly interacts with ChBD. However, no electron density was observed for two CBMs and two IgLs, probably due to their high conformational mobility. Size-exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering and small-angle X-ray scattering revealed the overall architecture of multi-modular VcChdx, in which the individual folded domains are extended in solution. Isothermal titration calorimetry analysis indicated that the interaction between VcChdx and allosamidin was enthalpy-driven with moderate affinity (Kd = 1.58 ± 0.48 μm). HPLC analysis of the reaction products from the substrates, chitooligosaccharides, (GlcNAc)n (n = 2–6), indicated that VcChdx hydrolyzes (GlcNAc)n in an endo-splitting manner. Among tested substrates, the specific activity was highest toward (GlcNAc)6. Taken together, VcChdx is a GH18 enzyme with an elongated, multi-modular structure and endo-splitting activity toward soluble (GlcNAc)n. It likely acts cooperatively with chitoporin localized to the outer membrane, which preferentially translocates (GlcNAc)6 into the periplasm.Keywords: Vibrio cholerae, chitodextrinase, crystal structure, SAXS, allosamidin, chitooligosaccharides, ITC

Chitin, a β-1,4-linked homopolymer of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, is widely distributed in crustacean shells, insect cuticles, and fungal cell walls (1, 2, 3). It is a highly recalcitrant material due to its crystalline structure, which limits chitin utilization by other organisms or by industry (4). Chitinase is a well-known hydrolytic enzyme that degrades chitin, often in cooperation with chitin-active lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases, in the chitin-utilizing pathways. It converts insoluble chitin into soluble chitooligosaccharides, (GlcNAc)n, through a series of enzymatic cleavages and is involved in diverse physiological and ecological processes (3, 5). In chitin-utilizing bacteria, three types of chitinases belonging to glycoside hydrolase family 18 (GH18) (6) have been identified: chitinase A, B, and C (ChiA, ChiB, and ChiC). These enzymes are proposed to act synergistically in chitin degradation. The classification of these chitinases depends on their domain organization and mode of action (7). ChiA and ChiB degrade chitin polysaccharide chains via a processive mechanisms but with a opposite directionality. ChiA produces (GlcNAc)2 acting at the reducing end (8, 9), while ChiB releases (GlcNAc)2 from the non-reducing end (GlcNAc)2. The contiguous binding subsites of ChiA or ChiB extend from the catalytic center of the GH18 domain towards a subdomain located on the glycone side or on the aglycone side, respectively (7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14). By contrast, ChiC hydrolyzes chitin in a non-processive, endo-splitting manner. These conclusions were derived from comparative experiments using the chitinase degradation system from Serratia marcescens, including SmChiA, SmChiB, and SmChiC (15).

Vibrio spp. are flagellated Gram-negative bacteria, which include species that cause pathogenesis in marine animals and in humans (16). Chitin degradation in Vibrio cholerae results from the coordinated action of an extracellular endo-chitinase, a periplasmic chitodextrinase (Chdx), and a periplasmic β-N-acetylglucosaminidase (GlcNAcase) (17, 18). The extracellular endo-chitinase cleaves insoluble chitin into soluble chitooligosaccharides, (GlcNAc)n, as the primary products. Subsequently, Chdx hydrolyzes soluble (GlcNAc)n, predominantly producing (GlcNAc)2, while an exo-splitting GlcNAcase degrades (GlcNAc)n by removing GlcNAc units from the non-reducing end. The enzymatic properties of recombinant Chdx and GlcNAcase from Vibrio furnissii were reported (19, 20). In the periplasm, soluble (GlcNAc)n are further hydrolyzed by Chdx or GlcNAcase into (GlcNAc)2 or GlcNAc, respectively. These are captured by (GlcNAc)2/GlcNAc-specific transporters (21, 22) for translocation into the cytoplasm. The discovery of Chdx was reported for the first time in Vibrio funissii (19), providing insight into the degradation of longer-chain (GlcNAc)n into (GlcNAc)2 within the periplasm. However, the structure of Chdx has not yet been elucidated, and its structure-based functionality remains unclear.

In this study, we examined the structure-function relationship of Chdx from V. cholerae (VcChdx), to gain insights into its role in chitin metabolism. The enzyme structure was analyzed by X-ray crystallography in the ligand-free and inhibitor-bound states, together with small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) to elucidate the behavior of the multi-domain enzyme in solution. Enzyme kinetics, ITC binding studies, and TLC/HPLC product analyses were also conducted to understand its functionality, in the context of the structural information.

Results

Sequence alignment of Chdx enzymes from Vibrio species

The genes encoding Chdx were identified in the genomes of Vibrio campbellii (formerly Vibrio harveyi) ATCC BAA 1116 (VhChdx) and V. cholerae O1 EI Tor N16961 (VcChdx). The corresponding full-length protein sequences were retrieved from GenBank under accession numbers WP_061066403.1 (VhChdx) and WP_001264253.1 (VcChdx). Amino acid sequence alignment of the VhChdx (1024 residues) and VcChdx (1021 residues) together with the first sequenced Chdx from V. furnissii, VfChdx (1019 residues) (19), is shown in Figure 1, in which the signal sequences were excluded. These enzymes consist of six domains arranged from the N-terminus as follows: an N-terminal CBM5/12 domain (N-CBM), two contiguous immunoglobulin-like β-sandwich domains (IgL-1 and IgL-2), a GH18 catalytic domain (CatD), a chitin-binding domain (ChBD), and a C-terminal CBM5/12 domain (C-CBM), where “CBM5/12” represents a combined group of carbohydrate-binding module (CBM) families 5 and 12, which are not reliably distinguishable at the sequence level. The catalytic domain contains a conserved DxDxE catalytic motif for GH18 enzymes (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/entry/InterPro/IPR001223), which is highlighted with a red background.

The sequence identity of VhChdx/VfChdx and VcChdx/VfChdx are 74% and 81%, respectively, while that of VhChdx/VcChdx is 77%. Notably, in the first sequenced VfChdx (19), the conserved glutamic acid (E) within the catalytic DxDxE motif was reported to be replaced by arginine (R), generating a DxDxR sequence that should be catalytically incompetent. Based on the amino acid sequence of VcChdx, we predicted the three-dimensional structure using AlphaFold2. The predicted structure and the predicted aligned error (PAE) map are shown in Figure 2, A and B, respectively. Both N-CBM and C-CBM adopt a three-stranded antiparallel β-sheet fold, with two loops containing aromatic residues suitable for interacting with pyranose rings. The two IgL domains adopt a typical β-sandwich fold, whereas CatD adopts a (β/α)8 barrel fold, consistent with reported GH18 structures. The ChBD was predicted to interact tightly with CatD, forming a stable interdomain interface. The PAE plot clearly indicates that the individual domains are well folded with high confidence, whereas the linker regions display low confidence, suggesting substantial interdomain flexibility.

Size exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS)

Purified protein of recombinant VcChdx was analyzed by SEC-MALS. The elution profile was narrow and symmetric, and the ultraviolet (UV), light-scattering (LS), and differential refractive index (dRI) signals were fully overlapped, indicating that VcChdx is monodisperse in solution (Fig. 3). The molecular mass calculated from the amino acid sequence is 108,548.26 Da, while that determined from SEC-MALS was 103.50 kDa, showing good agreement between the theoretical and experimental values. Furthermore, the plot of log10(molecular mass) vs elution time across the main elution peak (∼9.1–9.4 min) was flat, indicating that the protein did not change in size during elution. This behavior confirms that VcChdx exists as a monomer, with no detectable degradation or association–dissociation events.

SEC-MALS analysis of VcChdx. Ultraviolet (UV, red) and light-scattering (LS, blue) profiles are overlapping. The blackline at 8 to 12 min indicates the plot of log10(molecular mass) vs elution time.

SEC-MALS analysis also provided information on the molecular shape. The shape factor (ρ) was calculated using the following equation, ρ = Rg/Rh, where Rg is the radius of gyration, and Rh is the hydrodynamic radius, describing the static mass distribution and dynamic frictional behavior, respectively (24). The values obtained were Rg = 6.0 ± 5.0 nm and Rh = 4.2 ± 0.12 nm, resulting in ρ = 1.43. This value is higher than expected for a globular protein (ρ = 0.77∼0.78) (25, 26), indicating that full-length VcChdx adopts an extended, non-globular conformation in solution. However, the large error in the estimated Rg prompted us to investigate the structure using higher resolution techniques.

Crystal structures of ligand-free and allosamidin-bound VcChdx

Crystals of the ligand-free VcChdx were obtained in 1.0 M sodium acetate, 0.1 M sodium HEPES, pH 7.5, and 0.05 M cadmium sulfate after incubation for 292 days at 19 °C. Crystals of the VcChdx–allosamidin complex were prepared by soaking ligand-free crystals in 20 mM allosamidin for 10 min. Both single crystals belonged to the P 3221 space group and yielded diffraction data suitable for structure determination. The ligand-free structure was refined to Rwork/Rfree values of 22.6%/26.2%, whereas the allosamidin-bound structure was refined to 20.6%/25.8%. In both structures, clear electron density was observed for the CatD and ChBD domains, whereas density for the two CBM5/12 domains and the two IgL domains was absent, likely due to their high flexibility (27) and the tight packing of CatD and ChBD, or cleavage during the incubation period for crystal growth. The ligand-free VcChdx structure (Fig. 4A, cyan) superimposes well with a chitinase from Chromobacterium violaceum (PDB ID: 4TXG, gray), with an RMSD of 0.753 Å. The main-chain conformation of the allosamidin-bound structure (Fig. 4B) is similar to that of the ligand-free form (RMSD = 0.804 Å), indicating that inhibitor binding does not induce major conformational changes. CatD (green) adopts a (β/α)8 barrel fold, and ChBD (orange) adopts a β-sandwich fold. The allosamizoline moiety of allosamidin binds within a deep hydrophobic pocket at subsites −3 to −1 of the catalytic center (Fig. 4C). The conserved DxDxE motif (Asp442, Asp444, and Glu446) is located adjacent to the bound inhibitor. Glu446 is positioned to act as a proton donor, facilitating cleavage of the β-1,4-glycosidic bond and formation of an oxazolinium ion intermediate, whose positive charge is stabilized by Asp444.

At subsite −1 (Fig. 4D), the side-chain carboxylates of Asp444, Glu446, and Asp530 form hydrogen bonds with the nitrogen atom and the C6-hydroxyl oxygen of the allosamizoline moiety. The phenolic hydroxyl of Tyr529 and the main-chain amide of Trp387 also contribute hydrogen bonds. A hydrophobic cavity formed by Tyr294, Ala501, Met527, and Met739 accommodates the dimethylamino group of allosamizoline, while the aromatic ring of Trp741 stacks against the allosamizoline ring.

At subsite −2, the main-chain amide of Ala388 forms a hydrogen bond with the N-acetylallosamine C6-hydroxyl, and Tyr596 forms a CH-π interaction with the N-acetyl methyl group. At subsite −3, two hydrogen bonds are formed between the side chain carboxyl of Asp647 and the pyranose-ring oxygen/C6-hydroxyl, respectively, while the Trp298 aromatic ring stacks with the pyranose ring of N-acetylallosamine. Taken together, hydrogen bonding interactions dominate in forming the VcChdx-allosamidin complex (Table S1). The aglycone-binding subsites +1, +2, and +3 are located within the CatD domain (Fig. 4B).

Multi-domain structure of VcChdx analyzed by SAXS

SAXS experiments were performed to further characterize the solution structure of VcChdx. The Kratky plot (Fig. 5A), calculated from the low angle X-ray scattering data for the VcChdx solutions, exhibits a bell-shaped curve at low q values and a plateau at higher q values, indicating the coexistence of folded globular domains and extended regions. Pair-distribution function analysis (Fig. 5B) yielded an Rg of 4.1 nm and a Dmax of 17.4 nm. Scattering collected at a protein concentration of 5 mg mL−1 were analyzed with the ensemble optimization method (EOM) (Fig. 5C), which indicated that VcChdx exists exclusively as a monomer. The error-weighted residual plot (ΔI/σ vs q) shows a uniform distribution around zero, supporting the reliability of the fit (lower panel, Fig. 5C). The molecular mass estimated from the particle volume (Vp) was ∼107.2 kDa, consistent with values derived from sequence analysis (108.5 kDa) and SEC–MALS (105.7 kDa). Ab initio modeling using DAMMIF and DAMAVER produced molecular envelopes that accommodated the crystal structures of CatD/ChBD and the AlphaFold2-predicted models of the remaining domains (Fig. 5D). The SAXS data and atomic models have been deposited in the Small Scattering Biological Data Bank (SASBDB) (ID: SASDXQ9).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) of the VcChdx interaction with allosamidin

ITC experiments were performed by titrating allosamidin into VcChdx. Representative ITC thermograms and corresponding binding isotherms are shown in Figure 6. Each injection resulted in heat release, indicating binding. The dissociation constant (Kd) was calculated to be 1.59 ± 0.48 μM. Binding was exothermic and enthalpy-driven, with ΔH° = −6.57 ± 0.74 kcal mol−1 and a smaller entropic contribution (−TΔS° = −1.37 ± 0.81 kcal mol−1) (Table 2). The binding affinity (ΔG°) of VcChdx (−7.9 kcal/mol) is lower than that of most reported GH18 chitinases (−9.4 ∼ −9.6 kcal/mol) but similar to Serratia chitinase C (28, 29). Notably, the binding of VcChdx is dominated by enthalpic contributions, whereas entropy dominates in Serratia chitinase C.

HPLC analysis of hydrolysis products

Hydrolysis of soluble (GlcNAc)n (n = 2–6) was analyzed by HPLC and TLC. Reactions were initiated by mixing 0.9 μM VcChdx with 2.5 mM substrate. The HPLC profiles of the products obtained from the substrates (GlcNAc)2, (GlcNAc)3, (GlcNAc)4, (GlcNAc)5, and (GlcNAc)6 at 0 min (upper panel) and 2 min of the reaction time (lower panel) are shown in Figure 7, A–E, respectively. (GlcNAc)2 was not hydrolyzed. Hydrolysis of (GlcNAc)3 yielded GlcNAc and (GlcNAc)2, with β-anomer enrichment in (GlcNAc)2, indicating preferential cleavage at the first glycosidic bond from the reducing end. For (GlcNAc)4, the predominant product was (GlcNAc)2, suggesting preferential binding across subsites −2 to +2. Minor amounts of (GlcNAc)3 and GlcNAc were also detected. Hydrolysis of (GlcNAc)5 produced (GlcNAc)2 and (GlcNAc)3, with stronger β-enrichment in (GlcNAc)3, consistent with binding across subsites −3 to +2. Hydrolysis of (GlcNAc)6 generated comparable amounts of (GlcNAc)2 and (GlcNAc)3, consistent with single and double cleavage events. Time-course analysis (Fig. 7F) showed low accumulation of (GlcNAc)4, suggesting that this intermediate is retained and translocated within the binding groove of the enzyme, indicative of a processive mechanism (31).

Finally, hydrolysis of insoluble substrates (crystalline and colloidal α- and β-chitins) was examined (Fig. S2). VcChdx predominantly produced (GlcNAc)2, with smaller amounts of (GlcNAc)3 and trace GlcNAc from colloidal chitin after 2 min. The colloidal chitin preparations may include some soluble forms of polysaccharides, resulting from the harsh acid condition employed in the preparation process, which may explain the initial activity. No detectable products were observed from crystalline chitin after 12 h, consistent with previous observations for VfChdx (19), which hydrolyzes chemically modified (acetylated) chitin but not intact crystalline chitin.

Discussion

The first functional characterization of a Chdx was conducted by Keyhani and Roseman (19) using a recombinant VfChdx enzyme. They reported that VfChdx hydrolyzes (GlcNAc)n in an endo-splitting manner and that the kinetic behavior toward the substrates (GlcNAc)n was characterized by strong substrate inhibition. VfChdx was inactive toward intact chitin but active toward chemically reacetylated chitin. The amino acid sequence of VfChdx was deduced from the cloned gene and exhibiting significant similarities to several GH18 chitinases, including Bacillus circulans chitinase A1, S. marcescens chitinase A and chitinase B. Notably, however, the GH18 catalytic motif (DxDxE) in VfChdx consists of DIDLR, in which the glutamic acid is substituted with arginine (Fig. 1). It is well-known that this glutamic acid acts as a proton donor in the acid-catalyzed cleavage of the β-1,4-glycosidic bond (14). Hence, VfChdx may be classified as a “pseudo”-GH18 Chdx. Thus, the enzymatic properties observed in VfChdx may differ from those of DxDxE GH18 enzymes. Here, we report the structure and function of VcChdx, which possesses a complete DxDxE catalytic motif.

Catalytic motif of VfChdx

Based on the mechanism widely accepted for the catalytic reaction of enzymes belonging to the GH18 family (14), an enzyme without a glutamic acid in the DxDxE motif should be inactive. However, Keyhani and Roseman (19) observed full chitinolytic activity in VfChdx with a reported arginine (R) at the “E” catalytic site. Here, we investigated the substrate specificity, substrate-splitting mode, anomer specificity, and allosamidin-binding mode of VcChdx, which has the classical DxDxE motif. The enzymatic properties of VcChdx are typical of the ChiC-type GH18 enzymes, similar to those reported for VfChdx (19). It is likely that the originally reported substitution (E → R) at the catalytic site of VfChdx was an error in sequence determination, due to the immature DNA sequencing technology available in the 1990s, and that VfChdx may contain a standard DxDxE. During this study, we noticed that the amino acid sequence for VfChdx has been updated on March 21, 2026 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/WP_158108554.1), where the DxDxE motif correctly exists at the corresponding site. Based on these findings, it is concluded that DxDxR originally reported for VfChdx (19) was due to an error in sequence determination.

Crystal structure and mode of action

Although the structure of multi-modular VcChdx was predicted using AlphaFold2 (Fig. 2), we analyzed its crystal structure by X-ray diffraction method, which only yielded the structure of CatD and ChBD domains of the recombinant protein. No electron density was obtained for the remaining domains, including N-CBM, IgL-1, IgL-2, and C-CBM, likely due to their higher flexibility or cleavage. The CatD and ChBD domains strongly interact with each other, and both domains appear to be involved in polysaccharide binding (Fig. 4). In GH18 chitinases with a processive mode of action, such as SmChiB, several aromatic side chains are often aligned along a single binding groove to interact hydrophobically with the pyranose rings of a polysaccharide chain. Thus, we examined the distribution of the aromatic side chains in CatD and ChBD (Fig. S3).

Aromatic residues were densely distributed in the active-site region of CatD, spanning subsites −3 to +3, but were sparsely distributed in ChBD. Furthermore, the putative binding surface of ChBD, inferred from the superimposition with a ChBD from Thermococcus kodakaraensis (PDB code, 5DHE; RMSD, 3.01 Å), is bent almost perpendicularly to the binding groove of CatD (shaded in cyan in Fig. S3). It is therefore unlikely that a linear polysaccharide chain binds to VcChdx spanning both CatD and ChBD. In this respect, VcChdx is fundamentally different from SmChiB, which processively hydrolyzes polysaccharide chitin from the non-reducing end using the contiguous binding subsites extending from CatD to ChBD. The HPLC data shown in Figure 7 suggests an endo-splitting, non-processive mode of action, similar to that of bacterial ChiC-type enzymes, indicating that the oligosaccharide hydrolysis takes place exclusively within the substrate-binding groove of CatD, despite the presence of ChBD (Fig. 4, A and B). However, the time-course of (GlcNAc)6 degradation (Fig. 7F) suggests a partly processive mode of action, as the amount of (GlcNAc)2 produced was much higher than that of (GlcNAc)4. This limited processivity may occur within the CatD binding groove when (GlcNAc)6 initially binds to −2 to +4. Thus, VcChdx is a unique GH18 chitinase possessing an elongated multi-modular structure with endo-splitting, partly processive mode of action.

VcChdx-allosamidin interaction

ITC analysis of the allosamidin interaction revealed a binding affinity (ΔG° = −7.9 kcal mol−1) that was lower than those reported for SmChiA/SmChiB (ΔG° = −9.4 kcal mol−1, (29)) and plant GH18 chitinases (ΔG° = −9.4 ∼ −9.6 kcal mol−1 (28), but similar to that of S. marcescens ChiC (ΔG° = −7.9 kcal mol−1). The interaction of allosamidin with VcChdx was enthalpy-driven, whereas that with SmChiC was entropy-driven. The binding affinity of VcChdx for allosamidin occurs mainly by direct hydrogen bonding rather than hydrophobic interactions. This is supported by the crystal structure of VcChdx-allosamidin complex, in which eight direct-hydrogen bonds are formed between the enzyme and allosamidin (Fig. 4D and Table S1). The binding geometry supports that Glu446 of the DxDxE motif functions as the catalytic acid, while Asp444 stabilizes the oxazolium ion intermediate formed during catalysis. In SmChiB, the middle Asp of the DxDxE motif is oriented toward the first Asp in the ligand-free state and rotates toward the catalytic acid Glu upon substrate binding (14). In SmChiA, the orientation of this Asp is equally distributed toward the first Asp or the catalytic acid Glu in the ligand-free state (32). In contrast, in VcChdx the carboxylate of Asp444 is oriented toward Glu446 in the ligand-free state, and this orientation does not change significantly upon allosamidin binding (Fig. S4).

Catalytic mechanism

The fixed orientation of Asp444 appears to be disadvantageous for “pKa cycling” of the Glu446 carboxylate (33). In GH18 chitinases, the catalytic acid, Glu, of the DxDxE motif donates a proton to the glycosyl oxygen of the substrate as well as enhances the nucleophilicity of water molecules as the catalytic base in the subsequent step. This dual functionality of Glu is explained by "pKa cycling”, which is derived from the rotation of the middle-Asp side chain of the DxDxE motif during a single catalytic turnover. Since the kcat value obtained for VcChdx using pNP-(GlcNAc)2 (Table 3) is approximately twice of that obtained for SmChiB (17∼18 s−1 (34)), indicating that the catalytic reaction is properly conducted by the catalytic triad, Asp442-Asp444-Glu446. It appears that the stability of Asp444 interacting with Glu446 via the side chains as well as that of Asp442 hydrogen-bonded with Tyr294, Asn322, and Thr499 (Fig. S4), may destabilize the interaction between Asp444 and Asp442, which may only exist for a short lifetime in the catalytic turnover. This may be the reason why the Asp444 side chain is only oriented toward Glu446 in the crystal structures.

SAXS-based full-length model

To gain insight into the structure of the remaining regions of VcChdx, SAXS analysis was conducted to determine the full structure of the six-domain enzyme in solution. The resulting model (Fig. 5D) shows that the N-CBM, IgL-1, IgL-2, and C-CBM are extended and flexible. These domains are unlikely to contribute directly to substrate binding during catalysis since the substrate-binding surface resides on CatD. For periplasmic enzymes, however, additional factors inherent to this environment, such as molecular crowding, must be considered. Under such conditions, the four domains outside CatD and ChBD may exhibit additional functionality, potentially assisting catalysis through substrate or product anchoring effects.

Enzymatic and physiological functionality

Marine bacteria of the genus Vibrio utilize chitin as a nutrient source. Extracellular chitinases secreted by these bacteria hydrolyze polysaccharide chitin into oligosaccharides with various chain lengths (9, 35). Chitoporins (ChiPs) localized in the outer membrane then translocate the chitin oligosaccharides (GlcNAc)n (n = 3–6) into periplasm (36). VcChdx is an enzyme acting specifically in periplasm. This is consistent with the kcat value toward the low molecular weight substrate, pNP-(GlcNAc)2, was higher in VcChdx than in extracellular chitinase A, which acts toward insoluble polysaccharide chitin (Table 3). Furthermore, VcChdx does not hydrolyze insoluble crystalline chitins (Fig. S2), but hydrolyze the soluble (GlcNAc)n (n = 3–6) (Figs. 7 and 8), in line with its localization. Meanwhile, as to ChiP, time-resolved single-channel recordings and liposome swelling assays demonstrated that the ChiP channel from V. harveyi (VhChiP) responds specifically to (GlcNAc)n, with interaction strength increasing with chain length. Detailed kinetic analyses indicated that VhChiP is most active toward (GlcNAc)6, exhibiting a binding constant of K = 5 × 105 M−1, which is several orders of magnitude higher than those of porins specific for sucrose, maltose, glucose, or cyclodextrin (37, 38). These findings on ChiP indicate that (GlcNAc)6 produced by an extracellular chitinases are efficiently translocated through ChiP into the periplasm, resulting in a high periplasmic abundance of this oligosaccharide. As determined here, VcChdx hydrolyzes (GlcNAc)6 most efficiently into (GlcNAc)2 with smaller amounts of (GlcNAc)3, which is further hydrolyzed into (GlcNAc)2 and GlcNAc. (Table 4 and Fig. 7). Thus, we propose that VcChdx and ChiP are functionally coordinated with respect to their chain-length specificities toward (GlcNAc)n, supporting a cooperative action of ChiP and Chdx in the chitin utilization system of Vibrio species. Taken together, VcChdx is a periplasmic enzyme designed to efficiently convert chitinous nutrients in cooperation with surrounding chitin-related proteins.

Experimental procedures

Materials

Chitooligosaccaharides, (GlcNAc)n (n = 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6), and pNP-(GlcNAc)2 were purchased from Megazyme Ltd (Wicklow, Ireland). Chromatography resins for the enzyme purification, including Ni-NTA affinity resin, HiTrap Q HP, and Superdex-200, were obtained from Cytiva. All other reagents were of analytical grade commercially available. Allosamidin, a GH18 chitinase inhibitor, was isolated from the mycelial extract of Streptomyces sp. No.1713 (39).

Expression and purification of full-length recombinant VcChdx

The full-length VcChdx gene including the region encoding the original signal peptide (28 amino acids, MRVLEGKAGKSVFTLSTLASCMAFSSYA), codon optimization for Escherichia coli, was synthesized and cloned into the pET-23a(+) expression vector using NdeI (CATATG) at the 5′ site and XhoI (CTCGAG) at the 3′ site. The resulting recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli SHuffle T7 expression host (New England Biolabs). Expression of the recombinant VcChdx was conducted according to the manufacture’s protocol. After overproducing VcChdx, cell pellets were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 20 min under 4 °C. 10g of cell pellet was resuspended with lysis buffer (100 ml of 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 8.0 containing 0.1 M sodium chloride, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1 mg DNaseI, and 1 ml 100 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) and disrupted using a Vibra cell Ultrasonic Processor, converter type CV33, fitted with a 6 mm probe (Sonics). The lysed cell suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 45 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. Then, the supernatant was loaded into a 5 ml of Ni-NTA affinity column (GenScript) pre-equilibrated with equilibration buffer (20 mm Tris-HCl, pH 8.0 and 100 mM NaCl) for five column volumes (CVs). The column was washed with a wash buffer (equilibration buffer with 5 mM imidazole) to remove unbound protein contaminants for 10 CVs, and the bound proteins were eluted with a linear gradient from 20 mM to 50 mM imidazole in the same buffer. The fractions containing VcChdx were pooled, dialyzed against the equilibration buffer used for the following anion-exchange chromatography. The dialyzed sample was applied into an anion-exchange HiTrap Q HP anion-exchange column (5 ml) with a flow rate of 2 ml min−1, pre-equilibrated for five CVs with the equilibration buffer, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4. Bound VcChdx was eluted with a linear gradient of 0 to 1 M NaCl in the equilibration buffer. The fractions containing VcChdx were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a 16/60 Superdex S-200 pre-packed column (Cytiva) with a flow rate of 0.5 ml min−1. Eluted fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE to assess purity. After confirming a single band on SDS-PAGE (40), purified VcChdx was concentrated for storage at −80 °C. Protein concentration was determined by UV absorbance at 280 nm using a calculated extinction coefficient of 217,095 M−1 cm−1 for VcChdx (41).

X-ray crystallography of the ligand-free and allosamidin-bound VcChdx

VcChdx purified by size-exclusion chromatography was freshly prepared in 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4 with 100 mM NaCl and concentrated to 5 mg mL−1. The sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (Eppendorf 5418R centrifuges) for 10 min at 4 °C to remove protein aggregates. Sitting-drop vapor diffusion crystallization screening was conducted using a Swiss 96-Well (2 drops) MRC Crystallization plates (Molecular Dimensions) by the aid of a Mosquito crystallization robot (TTP Labtech). Drops were prepared by mixing protein solution and precipitant solutions (150:200 or 200:200 in nl). Commercial screening kit structural screening kit from Molecular dimensions, Sheffield, UK were used, and plates were incubated at 19 °C. Ligand-free crystals were obtained from the solution, 1.0 M sodium acetate trihydrate, 0.1 M sodium HEPES at pH 7.5, and 0.05 M cadmium sulfate hydrate, after incubation for 292 days. For preparation of the allosamidin complex, a single crystal of VcChdx was soaked with a 20 mM allosamidin solution for 10 min at 25 °C. Crystals were harvested with cryo-loops and flash-cooled directly in liquid nitrogen without cryoprotectant. X-ray diffraction data were collected at the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC, Hsinchu, Taiwan) on beamline TPS-05A1 using a wavelength of 0.99987 Å and an EIGER2 X 9M detector (Dectris). Diffraction data were processed using STARANISO (42, 43). The data were highly isotropic, showing maximum diffraction of 2.6 Å and 5.0 Å in b∗ and c∗, respectively. Data to a maximum of 3.5 Å were used in the refinement. Structure factors were obtained and phased by molecular replacement using Phaser in the CCP4 suite v7.0 (44, 45) with an AlphaFold2-predicted structure of VcChdx as the search model. Model building and refinement were performed using WinCoot v0.9.8.93 (46, 47), phenix.refine (48, 49, 50) in Phenix v1.9 (51) and Buster via CCP4 cloud v9.0.003 (52). Due to the limited resolution of the diffraction data, iterative model building was performed against the initial molecular replacement solution rather than against intermediate refined models. As a result, the final structure was obtained after multiple rounds of manual model building followed by a single round of refinement. This process resulted clear electron density for the bound ligand. Unexplained sphere-shaped electron densities close to histidine, aspartic acid or glutamic acid residues were assigned to be cadmium ions, which was present in the crystallization conditions. These densities were present in omit maps and Fo-Fc maps prior to the addition of cadmium and showed robust density in the 2Fo-Fc maps after the addition of cadmium (Figs. S5 and S6). However, the final refined B-factors for the cadmium ions and allosamidin were elevated relative to those of the surrounding protein atoms, suggesting partial occupancy or high mobility. Furthermore, CheckMyMetal analysis showed incomplete coordination for the cadmium ions (53, 54). Thus, the cadmium ions assignments should be regarded as tentative. Structure figures were generated using PyMOL v2.5.4.

Size-exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS)

The molecular mass of full-length VcChdx was confirmed using SEC-MALS using an HPLC system (Agilent 1260 Infinity II) coupled to a multi-angle light scattering detector. A Superdex 200 Increase column (Tricorn 5/150 Gl; 5 × 150 mm) was equilibrated at 20 °C using a protein buffer consisting of 20 mM HEPES at pH 7.5 and 150 mm NaCl. The buffer was filtered and degassed prior to use. The system was operated at a flow rate of 2.0 ml min−1. Data were collected using ASTRA software v7.3.1.9 (Wyatt Technology) with a light scattering (LS) wavelength of 660.8 nm, a differential refractive index wavelength of 658.0 nm, and UV absorbance at 280 nm. Measurement parameters included an extinction coefficient of 1.994 × 10−3 ml (mg cm)−1, protein concentration = 5 mg mL−1, an injection volume of 50 μl, and a dn/dc = 0.185.

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

Purified VcChdx at 5 mg mL-1 in 100 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl and 10%(v/v) glycerol (to protect protein from radiation damage) was applied to a Superdex S-200 size-exclusion column (Cytiva) to remove aggregate protein and to isolate the dominant oligomeric protein species prior to SAXS analysis. SAXS data were collected at a wavelength of 0.0827 nm, with 240 successive frames of 2 s exposure to assess the radiation damage and concentration-dependent intermolecular interactions. Scattering vectors were defined as q = 4π sinθ/λ, where 2θ represents the scattering angle. 2D images were processed and radially averaged using 13A_SAXSDRK_4.89.vi (developed at the TPS synchrotron) (54) and BioXTAS RAW v2.1.0 (25) to obtain 1D scattering curves. Guinier and Kratky analyses were performed using BioXTAS RAW. The pair distribution functions, P(r), were calculated from the scattering curves using GNOM from the ATSAS package v4.0.1 (55). The Ensemble Optimization Method (EOM) was used to evaluate flexibility, generating 50,000 models of the five flexible linkers with RANCH and calculating scattering profiles with FFmaker. The most probable ensembles were then selected using GAJOE (56). Rigid-body models obtained from EOM were superimposed onto the SAXS envelope using CIFSUP. A. initio envelopes were generated using DAMMIF (57) and averaged with DAMAVER (58) from ten independent reconstructions without symmetry constraints (P1). The resulting SAXS envelopes were superimposed with the CatD + C-term crystal structure and with the AlphaFold2-predicted models of the CBM and IgL domains.

VcChdx-allosamidin interaction analyzed by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

ITC experiments were carried out using a MicroCal-PEAQ ITC system (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. VcChdx was freshly prepared at a concentration of 25 μM in 20 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4. Allosamidin (250 μM) in the same buffer was titrated into the enzyme solution using the instrument control (Origin, Northampton, USA). The initial injection was 0.4 μl followed by 18 injections of 2.0 μl each, with a stirring speed of 500 rpm, a reference power of 5.0 μcal s−1, an injection duration of 4 s, and spacing of 60 s. Binding isotherms were fitted using a one-site binding model using a nonlinear least-squares fitting algorithm, and the thermodynamic parameters, N, Kd, ΔH°, ΔG°, and −TΔS°, were calculated using the MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software v1.41.

Kinetic studies with 4-nitrophenyl N, N′-diacetyl-β-D-chitobioside

Kinetic analysis of VcChdx was conducted using a colorimetric assay with pNP-(GlcNAc)2 as the substrate. Release of p-nitrophenolate ions from the hydrolysis of the pNP-glycoside substrate was monitored by measuring absorbance of 410 nm. A standard calibration curve was generated using a pNP concentration range of 0 to 500 μM. Reactions were performed in 100 μl total volume containing 0.093 μM VcChdx, 0 to 750 μM of pNP-(GlcNAc)2 in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH 7.0. Sample were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min with shaking at 300 rpm and quenched by addition of 100 μl of 3 M Na2CO3. Absorbance at 410 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and product concentrations were calculated from the calibration curve. Kinetic parameters, Km, Vmax, and kcat, were obtained by a non-linear fitting to the Michaelis-Menten equation (9).

TLC and HPLC analysis of the enzymatic products

Enzymatic reactions were carried out in the reaction mixture (450 μl) containing 0.93 μM VcChdx and the individual chitin oligosaccharides, (GlcNAc)n (n = 2–6, 2.5 mm) in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.0 at 37 °C using a 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes. Reactions were terminated after 1, 5, 10, 15, 30 min, and 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h by heating at 100 °C for 10 min. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove the denatured protein. Supernatants were applied to silica gel 60 F254 plates (10 × 13 cm, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The mobile phase consisted of n-butanol: methanol:28% ammonia solution: water (ratio 10:8:4:2 by volume). After the TLC separation, the TLC plate was sprayed with freshly prepared solution containing 0.5g diphenylamine, 500 μl aniline, and 3.75 ml orthophosphoric acid in 25 ml 85%(v/v) acetone. Then, the hydrolytic products were visualized by baking at 180 °C for 30 s. For HPLC analysis, reaction products were separated on a TSKgel Amide 80 column (4.6 mm I.D. × 25 cm length, 5 μm spherical silica particles, Tosoh, Tokyo, Japan) (59) connected to an HPLC system (Shimadzu i-series, LC-2050C 3D, Genzo Shimadzu). The separation of the individual products, including α- and β-anomers was conducted with 70% (v/v) acetonitrile, a flow rate of 0.7 ml min−1, at 20 °C. Sugars were detected by UV absorbance at 200 nm.

To determine anomer composition, reaction mixtures (50 μl) containing VcChdx (9.3 μM) and (GlcNAc)n (n = 2–6, 2.5 mM) were initially mixed on ice, followed by incubation at 15 °C for 2 min to minimize α/β mutarotation. The reaction was terminated by immersing the sample tubes in liquid N2, followed by HPLC analysis described above. α/β ratios were calculated from peak areas to determine the preferred cleavage sites (60).

When insoluble substrates (crystalline α-chitin, colloidal α-chitin, crystalline β-chitin, or colloidal β-chitin) were used as the substrate instead of soluble (GlcNAc)n, enzymatic reactions were carried out by mixing the enzyme (0.93 μM) with individual chitins (2.5 mg/ml) in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer at pH 7.0 at 37 °C in a 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes (total volume 450 μl).
