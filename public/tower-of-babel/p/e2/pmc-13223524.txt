Bacterial warfare at the molecular level: Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 disrupts Vibrio splendidus membrane integrity via ClpP-CheW2 regulation
Authors: Ya Li, Fa Dai, Weibo Shi, Zihao Sun, Huili Hong, Yuanyuan Huang, Qingxi Han, Lin Zhang, Weiwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CheW2 negatively regulated the expression of outer membrane proteins in V. splendidus.•ClpP degraded CheW2 based on the recognization of its AA motif at the N terminal.•Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 attenuated the expression of clpP gene of V. splendidus AJ01.Keywords: Pseudoalteromonas sp., Vibrio splendidus, Antagonism, Coupling protein CheW2, Serine protease ClpP, Membrane integrity

Introduction

Vibrio spp. are important pathogenic bacteria in aquatic ecosystems, with a pathogenic spectrum encompassing numerous economically important aquaculture species (Zhang et al., 2016). Vibrio splendidus, an opportunistic pathogen in marine environments, is the predominant species within the Splendidus clade and has broad infectivity across diverse aquaculture species. The pathogenic potential of V. splendidus differs across hosts. For example, V. splendidus 10/068 1T1 exhibited a mortality rate of 90% in mussels (Mytilus edulis), while biovar III of this strain exhibited complete lethality in the Pacific oyster (Crassostrea gigas) (Li et al., 2024). Recent studies have confirmed that V. splendidus AJ01 employs multiple virulence factors to modulate host immune defense pathways (Zhang and Li, 2021), facilitating infection and inducing skin ulceration syndrome in Apostichopus japonicus (Li et al., 2023).

Considering the increasing consumer demands for seafood quality and safety along with the increasing public health risks posed by antibiotic-resistant pathogens, the development of novel eco-friendly control strategies has become an urgent priority in aquatic disease management (Hicks, 2016). The use of nonpathogenic bacteria with antagonistic properties as potential biocontrol alternatives has emerged as a promising strategy to combat bacterial diseases in aquaculture (Kumar, 2021). Antagonistic bacteria such as Pseudomonas spp. (Chythanya, 2002; Pai, 2009), Lactobacillus spp. (Ma, 2009), Pediococcus spp. (Yan, et al. 2019), Bacillus spp. (Adel, et al. 2017), and Pseudoalteromonas spp. (Wang et al., 2019), which exhibit activity against Vibrio spp., offer sustainable alternatives to antimicrobial chemicals in mariculture. Pseudoalteromonas is a genus of gram-negative bacteria widely distributed in marine environments and is recognized for its significant probiotic potential because of its broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties (Wang et al., 2019). Studies indicate that bacteria belonging to this genus produce diverse antimicrobial metabolites, including alkaloids, polyketides, and peptide compounds (Offret et al., 2016). Pseudoalteromonas flavipulchra was found to exhibit dual functionality by increasing microalgal biomass while suppressing the growth of pathogenic Vibrio spp. (Wu et al., 2024). The cell-free supernatant (CFS) of Pseudoalteromonas sp. SW-1 was found to attenuate the pathogenicity of Vibrio alginolyticus MP-1 in clams (Sun et al., 2024). The antagonistic activity of Pseudomonas piscicida WCPW15003 against Vibrio harveyi is increased via the modulation of metabolic pathways (Ren et al., 2022). These findings collectively suggest that Pseudoalteromonas spp. exhibit substantial potential as probiotics for preventing diseases in aquaculture (Aiyar et al., 2017).

In addition to guiding bacterial movement in response to environmental cues, bacterial chemotaxis also regulates gene expression and virulence factor production (Matilla and Krell, 2018). In Vibrio cholerae, the expression of certain chemotaxis proteins, such as CheA2, CheY3, and CheZ mutants, attenuates the expression of toxT, the major transcriptional regulator of virulence genes (Lee et al., 2001). In Acinetobacter baumannii, the direct interaction between the CheW-like protein and RecA is crucial for surface-associated motility, chemotactic behavior, and overall virulence (Corral et al., 2020). Furthermore, the degradation of chemotaxis proteins is tightly regulated to facilitate rapid energy conversion and maintain cellular homeostasis (Arapov et al., 2020). ClpP, a serine protease, plays a crucial role in protein homeostasis in both prokaryotic cells and eukaryotic organelles by regulating the degradation of specific proteins (Moreno-Cinos et al., 2019). In the plant pathogen Ralstonia solanacearum, a proteomics study revealed that the substrates of ClpP include proteins associated with bacterial chemotaxis and flagellar assembly, such as CheA, CheB, CheR, CheW, CheY, and CheZ (Zheng et al., 2020). Additionally, the candidate substrates of ClpP in Caulobacter crescentus include several chemotaxis proteins, such as CheR, CheW, CheD, and McpJ (Bhat et al., 2013). However, direct evidence for ClpP-mediated degradation of chemotaxis proteins is lacking.

In this study, we investigated the antagonistic effect of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 against V. splendidus AJ01 and revealed that CheW2 of V. splendidus AJ01 partially regulated the antagonistic effect of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 against V. splendidus AJ01. The functional role of CheW2 in the antagonistic process was subsequently elucidated through integrated RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT‒PCR), and membrane integrity assays. Importantly, the results of in vitro and in vivo degradation assays demonstrated that the serine protease ClpP mediated CheW2 proteolysis and that the expression of the clpP gene in V. splendidus AJ01 was downregulated under coculture conditions. Integrating these results, we propose that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 suppresses the expression of the clpP gene in V. splendidus AJ01, thus stabilizing CheW2 to increase membrane integrity through an “antagonistic bacterium–ClpP–CheW2–membrane integrity” regulatory axis.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

V. splendidus AJ01 is an important opportunistic pathogen isolated from A. japonicus affected by skin ulcer syndrome, and it is maintained at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection (CGMCC) with the strain designation 7.242. The genetic mutant strains ΔcheW1 and ΔcheW2 and the double genetic mutant strain ΔcheW12 were constructed as described in our previous study (Li et al., 2024). Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 was isolated from healthy cuttlefish in our previous study (Xu et al., 2024).

Escherichia coli strains BL21(DE3) and S17-1 λpir were used for protein expression and bacterial conjugation, respectively. Luria–Bertani (LB) medium was used for the propagation of E. coli strains at 37°C with the appropriate concentration of a specific antibiotic. V. splendidus AJ01 and Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 were cultured separately in 2216E medium (5 g/L tryptone, 1 g/L yeast extract, and 0.01 g/L FePO4 in seawater) at 28°C. The medium was supplemented with antibiotics at the appropriate concentrations, as follows: 50 µg/mL kanamycin and 100 µg/mL ampicillin.

Plasmid construction

To construct protein expression plasmids, the DNA fragments of the cheW2, clpP, and clpX genes were separately amplified from the genomic DNA of V. splendidus AJ01 using the primer pairs cheW2-F/cheW2-R, His-clpX-F/His-clpX-R, and GST-clpP-F/GST-clpP-R, and they were inserted into the pGEX-4T-2 or pET-28a plasmid double digested with BamH I and Xho I to construct the pGEX-cheW2, pET-clpX, and pGEX-clpP plasmids. To generate the site mutant CheW2 protein, the Ala-Ala (AA) motif-encoding fragment of the cheW2 gene was substituted with a sequence encoding an Asp-Asp (DD) motif using the Fast Mutagenesis System (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and the primers cheW2-DDF and cheW2-DDR, which were specifically designed for the mutant. To construct the pET-GFP-CheW2 plasmid for the overexpression of the rGFP-CheW2 fusion protein, the pET-GFP plasmid constructed by Dai et al. (2020) was modified by inserting the amplified cheW2 gene sequence at the Xho I site. The pET-GFP-CheW1 plasmid for rGFP-CheW1 overexpression was constructed as described above. To measure clpP promoter activity using a GFP reporter, the promoter of the clpP gene was amplified using the primers PclpP900F and PclpP900R, and the 900 bp DNA fragment was inserted into a modified pET-28a-based plasmid designed in a previous study to generate the pET-PclpP-GFP plasmid (Li et al., 2023). The plasmids pET-PclpP-GFP, pET-GFP-CheW2, and pET-GFP-CheW1 were introduced separately into V. splendidus AJ01 by bacterial conjugation. All the primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Construction of gene knockout mutants

Single deletion of the clpP gene and double deletion of the clpP and cheW2 genes in V. splendidus AJ01 were performed via allelic exchange utilizing the suicide plasmid pK18mobSacB (Li et al., 2024). The upstream and downstream homologous DNA fragments of the clpP gene were amplified and ligated into the BamH I-digested suicide plasmid pk18mobSacB to generate the pK18-clpP plasmid. The pK18-clpP plasmid was introduced into both wild-type (WT) V. splendidus AJ01 and the ΔcheW2 strain via conjugation with double allelic exchange as described previously. Successful construction of the knockout strains ΔclpP and ΔcheW2clpP was verified using PCR amplification and sequencing.

Antagonistic activity of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2

Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 and the target strains (V. splendidus AJ01 or its mutants) were cultured overnight in 2216E liquid medium. To trace the components involved in the antagonistic activity of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2, extracellular products and total protein were extracted as previously described (Xu et al., 2024), and different components were tested for antagonistic activity by measuring both the inhibition zone diameter (Tang et al., 2020) and the growth inhibition area (Bisaro et al., 2023). For the inhibition zone diameter method, 50 µL of bacterial suspension (OD600 ≈ 0.8) of each target strain was evenly spread onto 2216E agar plates, and 20 µL of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 bacterial suspension (OD600 ≈ 0.8) was then added into Oxford cups. The plates were incubated at 28°C, after which the diameter of the inhibition zones was measured. For the growth inhibition area method, 5 µL of the Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 and target strain suspensions were spotted adjacent to each other on agar plates. The plates were incubated at 28°C, and the growth inhibition zones of the target strain were observed.

Noncontact interactions between Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 and V. splendidus AJ01 were evaluated using a Transwell® permeable support system (Tang et al., 2020). One milliliter of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 suspension with OD600 ≈ 0.2 was added to the upper chamber, while 1 mL of V. splendidus AJ01 suspension (OD600 ≈ 0.5) was added to the lower chamber. The system was incubated at 28°C with horizontal shaking at 100 rpm. Samples from both the upper and lower chambers were taken and serially diluted 10-fold, and 10 µL of the cell suspension from each dilution was added dropwise to 2216E agar plates for colony counting.

The growth of monocultures and cocultures in liquid medium was measured. Two milliliters of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 suspension with OD600 ≈ 0.2 and 2 mL of V. splendidus AJ01 suspension with OD600 ≈ 0.5 were cocultured in a 6-well plate at 28°C with shaking at 100 rpm. At the designated time intervals, samples of the cultures were taken, and 100 µL of the diluted samples was spread onto 2216E agar plates. The colonies of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 and V. splendidus AJ01 were distinguished on the basis of their distinct colony morphologies. Pure cultures of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 and V. splendidus AJ01 under the same conditions were used as controls. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times using separate bacterial cultures.

Microscopy

Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 (OD600 ≈ 0.2) was cocultured with V. splendidus AJ01 (OD600 ≈ 0.5) for 3 h. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), cells were collected and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h. The fixed samples were subsequently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% NaCl (PBS-2%NaCl) and subjected to gradient dehydration using 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% ethanol. The dehydrated samples were subjected to critical point drying and gold sputtering and were finally observed by SEM (Hitachi SU8600-Quorum, Japan). For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), the samples were fixed with 5% glutaraldehyde and were then washed with PBS-2% NaCl. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, and 10 µL aliquots were adsorbed onto 200-mesh carbon-coated copper grids. The grids were blotted and air-dried under infrared light and observed under a transmission electron microscope (HT-7800, Japan). All microscopy experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates, with similar results observed across replicates. Representative images are shown.

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) analysis

To investigate the potential functional roles of CheW2, RNA-seq analysis was performed using cells collected from the WT and ΔcheW2 strains. Both strains were cultured in 2216E medium at 28°C and harvested at an OD600 of 0.5. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in V. splendidus AJ01 and Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 during the antagonistic process, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on samples obtained from cocultures and monocultures incubated for 3 h. The experiment was performed with three biological replicates per sample.

After the cell pellets were collected by centrifugation, total RNA was extracted using an RNA Extraction Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Strand-specific libraries were constructed by Novogene Biotech (Beijing, China). The clean reads were compared with the V. splendidus genome database ASM2281204v1 using HISAT2. Gene counts were normalized using DESeq2 software (version 1.20.0). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler software (version 3.8.1), and genes meeting the threshold of padj < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription‒PCR (qRT‒PCR)

qRT‒PCR was performed to analyze the mRNA expression levels of genes. To validate the regulatory role of CheW2 in the expression of β-barrel assembly machinery (BAM) complex components, the WT and ΔcheW2 strains were separately grown in 2216E medium at 28°C to an OD600 of 0.5, after which the cell pellets were collected by centrifugation. Total RNA was extracted and reverse transcribed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). One microgram of cDNA was used as a template for qRT‒PCR with a ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) in an ABI7500 instrument (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). 16S rDNA was used as the internal reference gene to normalize the mRNA levels of the functional genes. The amplification efficiencies of the primers were confirmed to range from 90% to 110%. The primers used are listed in Table S1. qRT‒PCR performed with three independent biological replicates, each measured in triplicate (technical replicates).

To measure the mRNA level of the clpP gene in V. splendidus AJ01 under stress conditions, V. splendidus AJ01 was cultured in medium supplemented with 1 µM mitomycin C for 4 h to the logarithmic growth phase. Cell pellet collection, RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and qRT‒PCR were carried out as described above.

Measurement of hemolytic activity

The hemolytic activity of V. splendidus AJ01 was determined as described previously (Liang et al., 2016). Briefly, cells from sterile defibrinated sheep blood were washed in sterilized Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and resuspended in sterilized DMEM at a concentration of 5% (v/v). V. splendidus AJ01 cells at an OD600 of approximately 0.5 were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in sterilized DMEM. Five hundred microliters of a sheep blood–cell suspension was mixed with the bacterial suspension, and 2216E medium was added to the bacterial suspension as a control. After incubation for 2 h, the supernatants were obtained by centrifugation, and the released protoheme was quantified by measuring the OD570 with a Nanodrop 2000 UV‒Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The hemolytic activity assay was performed using three independent biological replicates.

Expression and purification of recombinant proteins

Protein expression was induced in E. coli BL21 cells harboring pGEX-cheW2, pET-clpX, or pGEX-clpP plasmids by treatment with isopropyl β-d-thiogalactopyranoside at a concentration of 0.4 mM, after which the cell pellets were collected and subjected to sonication. The His- and GST-tagged proteins were purified utilizing Ni-NTA resin (Sangon, Shanghai, China) and GST-agarose (Sigma‒Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany), respectively, according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentrations of the obtained proteins were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China).

In vitro protein degradation assay

All degradation reactions were performed in buffer containing 100 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 10% glycerol in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5). Total protein of V. splendidus AJ01 was extracted as previously described (Dai et al., 2023), and 5 μM purified rGST-CheW2 protein was mixed with the total protein of V. splendidus AJ01. To screen for the types of enzymes with the ability to degrade CheW2, serine protease inhibitor, cysteine protease inhibitor, aspartic protease inhibitor, phosphatase inhibitor, and metalloproteinase inhibitor were separately added to the reaction mixture at ratios of 1:100 and 1:20. The same volume of solvent was added for use as a control. Samples were collected at different time points and subjected to electrophoresis on 12% SDS‒PAGE gels. The gels were observed with an imaging system (Hangzhou Shenhua Co., China).

To determine whether the rClpXP complex participates in the degradation of rCheW2, 0.3 μM rClpX, 0.8 μM rClpP, 4 mM ATP, and an ATP regeneration system (50 μg/mL creatine kinase and 2.5 mM creatine phosphate) were mixed in PD buffer as described previously (Flynn et al., 2001) and incubated with 5 μM rGST-CheW2 protein. Samples were collected at specific time points, and proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE. The proteins were subsequently transferred to 0.45 μm-pore nitrocellulose membranes for western blotting analysis. After blocking with 5% skim milk in TBST (20 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20) overnight at 4°C, the membranes were incubated with a 1:10,000 dilution of a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated mouse anti-GST antibody (Zenbio, Chengdu, China) at 4°C for 4 h. After being washed three times with TBST, the membranes were immersed in Western Lightning-ECL substrate solution (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) and were then exposed in a Luminescent Imaging System (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

In vivo protein degradation assay

To analyze the protein stability of CheW2 in vivo, total protein from the mutant strains ΔcheW2::cheW2 and ΔclpP::cheW2 overexpressing rGFP-CheW2 was extracted at different growth stages. Fifty micrograms of protein from each sample was separated by SDS‒PAGE prior to transfer to a nitrocellulose membrane. After blocking with 5% skim milk, the membranes were incubated with a mouse anti-GFP primary antibody at a dilution of 1:10,000 (Sangon, Shanghai, China) or a mouse anti-RNAP subunit beta primary antibody at a dilution of 1:10,000 (BioLegend, San Diego, California, USA). The membranes were then washed and immersed in a solution containing an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:10,000 (Zenbio, Chengdu, China). After the membranes were washed with TBST three times, chemiluminescence signals were detected with the Luminescent Imaging System.

Measurement of bacterial membrane integrity

The cell membrane integrity assay was performed based on a previously described method (Zhang et al., 2021). Two hundred microliters of bacterial suspension was added to each well of a black 96-well microplate along with propidium iodide (PI; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at a final concentration of 5.5 μg/mL. The plates were incubated in a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, Singapore) at 28°C. The fluorescence intensity was measured at 10 min intervals using an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 515 nm.

Furthermore, the integrity of the cell membrane was visualized as described previously with minor modifications (Zhang et al., 2021). Bacterial cells cultured overnight on slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After being washed twice with PBS-2% NaCl, the slides were incubated with 5.5 μg/mL PI and 10 μg/mL 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in the dark at 28°C for 30 min. The slides were subsequently washed, dried, and sealed with antifade mounting medium (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Afterward, the slides were imaged under a Ni-E fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). All membrane integrity experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Mass spectrometry

To analyze the differentially expressed proteins between the WT and ΔclpP strains, 50 μg of total protein from the WT and ΔclpP strains was separated by SDS‒PAGE. The bands corresponding to the differentially expressed proteins were excised and analyzed by mass spectrometry. Briefly, after the bands were subjected to enzymatic digestion, the supernatants were collected and lyophilized. Liquid chromatography was performed on an Easy nLC 1200 nL liquid chromatography system (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and mass spectrometry was performed on a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive system (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). The primary mass spectrometry scan resolution was 70,000 with a scan range of 350–2000 m/z, and the maximum injection time was 100 ms. For secondary mass spectrometry, ions were injected over a maximum injection time of 50 ms. The identity of the peptides was determined by UniProt database searches.

Promoter activity measurement

To analyze the expression of the clpP gene in V. splendidus AJ01 in the presence of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2, the PclpP-GFP plasmid was introduced into V. splendidus AJ01 via conjugation to generate a reporter strain for determination of clpP promoter activity. During the growth inhibition zone assay, a Ni-E fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to evaluate the green fluorescence intensity at the edge of the reporter strain colony in the presence of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2. Promoter activity assays were performed using three independent biological replicates, and representative fluorescence images are shown.

Database search and statistical analysis

To assess the prevalence and distribution of the AA motif within the Vibrio genus, 100 representative CheW protein sequences were retrieved from the UniProt database (www.uniprot.org) using the keywords "Vibrio" and "CheW". To evaluate spatial distribution preferences, each sequence was bisected into N-terminal and C-terminal regions based on its total length. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. All the data are representative of three independent experiments and are presented as the mean±SD values. For western blotting, band densities were analyzed with ImageJ, and protein expression levels were normalized to those of the RNAP protein. A statistically significant difference was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, or ***P < 0.001. The original transcriptome sequencing data have been archived in the NCBI Short Read Archive (SRA) under project identifiers PRJNA1247283 and PRJNA1247365.

Results

Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 antagonized V. splendidus AJ01 through contact-dependent growth inhibition

An inhibition zone assay demonstrated that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 strongly antagonized V. splendidus AJ01. To investigate the components of the antagonistic active substances of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2, the cell-free extracellular products and total proteins of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 were extracted. The results revealed that only intact Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 cells exhibited significant antagonistic activity (Fig. 1A). To determine whether Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 inhibited the growth of V. splendidus AJ01 through direct contact or secretion products or through predatory behavior, a Transwell assay was employed. The Transwell device features a specialized permeable membrane with 0.4-μm pores, which allow the passage of small molecules while preventing direct contact between bacterial cells (Fig. 1B). When cells were separately cultured in two spaces in cell noncontact mode, the cell count of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 increased over time, and the number of V. splendidus AJ01 cells in the lower chamber also increased (Fig. 1C, D). These results indicated that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 inhibited the growth of V. splendidus AJ01 through direct contact rather than via an extracellular product (ECP).

To gain deeper insights into the interactions between Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 and V. splendidus AJ01, equal numbers of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 and V. splendidus AJ01 cells were cocultured in 2216E medium, and the cell counts of both strains were quantified at different time points. The results revealed a significant decrease in the cell number of V. splendidus AJ01 over time, whereas the cell number of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 gradually increased (Fig. 1E). SEM revealed that V. splendidus AJ01 cells cocultured with Pseudoalteromonas sp. strain H2 exhibited a loss of structural integrity and appeared fragmented (Fig. 1F). Additionally, TEM revealed rupture of the cellular membrane in cocultured V. splendidus AJ01 cells (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these results indicated that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 disrupts the cellular architecture of V. splendidus AJ01 through contact-dependent antagonism.

CheW2 of V. splendidus AJ01 negatively regulated membrane integrity and affected bacterial antagonism

Our previous studies revealed that the coupling protein CheW1 but not CheW2 was the key gene product responsible for chemotaxis in V. splendidus AJ01. Subcellular localization analysis of E. coli revealed that CheW2 exhibited predominantly polar localization, whereas CheW1 was localized to both the polar region and the cytoplasmic compartment (Fig. S1A). This distinct partitioning suggests a functional difference between CheW1 and CheW2. To further investigate the biological processes associated with CheW2, we performed transcriptomic analysis to identify DEGs between the ΔcheW2 and WT strains. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 160 upregulated genes and 179 downregulated genes in the ΔcheW2 strain (Fig. 2A). Notably, GO analysis revealed significant enrichment of outer membrane terms with the upregulated genes (Fig. 2B). The expression of four genes encoding components of the BAM complex, which is responsible for assembling β-barrel outer membrane proteins (OMPs) essential for maintaining the selective permeability of the outer membrane (OM) (Hart et al., 2019)—namely, bamA, bamB, bamC, and bamD—was confirmed to be upregulated in the ΔcheW2 strain by qRT‒PCR (Fig. 2C). In addition, integrated analyses of transcriptomic data and phenotypic validation were conducted to identify the novel regulatory functions of CheW2. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that the deletion of cheW2 was associated with altered expression levels of htpG (log2FoldChange = −4.75)and thermolabile hemolysin (tlh, log2FoldChange = −2.6). Subsequently, phenotypic assays confirmed that the ΔcheW2 exhibited significantly reduced ampicillin resistance and hemolytic activity (Fig. S1B and S1C), which are functional traits known to be associated with HtpG (Qin et al., 2024) and TLH (Wang et al., 2023), respectively. Phenotypic characterization of the ΔcheW1 and ΔcheA strains revealed no significant differences compared with the WT strain, confirming that CheW2 was specifically involved in regulating these phenotypes (Fig. S1B and S1C). Together, these data suggest that the phenotypic changes resulting from ΔcheW2 may be mediated, at least in part, through its impact on the expression or function of these two genes.

Considering that the disruption of membrane integrity in target bacteria is among the key bactericidal mechanisms (Le et al., 2022), the negative regulation of OMPs by CheW2 led us to wonder whether CheW2 is involved in bacterial antagonism. The antagonistic activity of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 against the ΔcheW2 and ΔcheW12 strains was significantly reduced (Fig. S2). Both the diameter of the inhibition zones and the area of growth inhibition for the complemented strain were restored to levels similar to those observed for the WT strain (Fig. 2D–F). These results suggested that CheW2 expression is positively correlated with the antagonistic activity of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 toward V. splendidus AJ01.

Coculture-induced species-specific metabolic and functional transcriptional shifts in competing marine bacteria

To reveal changes in gene expression in Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 and V. splendidus AJ01 under antagonistic conditions, dual RNA-seq was performed on both monocultures and cocultures grown in 2216E medium for 3 h. Under coculture conditions, the gene expression profile of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 was significantly altered, with 122 upregulated genes and 196 downregulated genes identified (Fig. 3A). GO analysis further demonstrated that in terms of biological processes (BPs), the upregulated genes exhibited enrichment predominantly in transmembrane transport, translocation, and localization processes. In terms of molecular function (MF), the upregulated genes exhibited enrichment in transmembrane transporter activity and nuclease activity (Fig. 3B). In terms of BPs, the downregulated genes exhibited enrichment in amino acids, organic acids, carboxylic acids, and carbohydrates, whereas in terms of MFs, they exhibited enrichment in enzymatic activities such as transferase, hydrolase, and ligase functions (Fig. 3C). These findings suggested that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 underwent metabolic adjustments to adapt to the new nutritional environment and competitive pressures under coculture conditions. Notably, both the upregulated and downregulated genes were minimally involved in cellular components (CCs), further indicating that adaptive regulation in Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 occurs primarily at the functional level rather than at the structural level. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the upregulated genes exhibited enrichment primarily in pathways related to transmembrane transport, membrane components, and localization (Fig. 3D), suggesting that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 may adapt to the coculture environment by increasing transmembrane substance exchange and cell membrane stability. The downregulated genes exhibited enrichment in metabolic pathways, including β-lactam resistance, cationic antimicrobial peptide resistance, amino acid biosynthesis, purine metabolism, carbon metabolism, and nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism (Fig. 3E), indicating the occurrence of metabolic reprogramming in Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 in response to changes in energy and nutrient demands under coculture conditions.

Transcriptomic analysis of V. splendidus AJ01 in the coculture system revealed differential expression of 158 genes, namely, 49 upregulated genes and 109 downregulated genes (Fig. 4A and B). To further analyze the functions of the DEGs, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated genes did not exhibit significant enrichment in any specific BP but were primarily associated with intracellular metabolic processes, such as RNA metabolism and the regulation of gene expression (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the downregulated genes exhibited significant enrichment in ribosome assembly in the BP category, cytoplasmic components in the CC category, and enzymatic activities in the MF category (Fig. 4D). KEGG analysis revealed that the upregulated genes were predominantly involved in metabolic pathways, such as carbon metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and pyrimidine metabolism (Fig. 4E). The downregulated genes exhibited the most significant enrichment in the ribosome pathway, followed by the RNA degradation pathway (Fig. 4F). These results demonstrated that the gene expression profile of V. splendidus AJ01 changed significantly in terms of biosynthesis and metabolic pathways. Notably, the serine protease ClpP was identified among the downregulated genes (log2FoldChange = −3.2). It was selected for further investigation due to its central role in protein homeostasis (Gersch et al., 2015), which is critically relevant to our observed phenotypes. This finding is particularly interesting because ClpP has typically been reported to be upregulated under stress conditions as a response to environmental perturbations (Lowry and Mitchell, 2025).

ClpP-mediated degradation of CheW2 and its impact on membrane integrity

To further investigate how CheW2 regulates membrane integrity, pull-down assays were performed, but unexpectedly, CheW2 was degraded by certain proteases in the whole-cell lysates (Fig. S3A). To identify the types of proteases responsible for CheW2 degradation, various types of enzyme inhibitors were added to the reaction system. The results revealed that serine protease inhibitors significantly attenuated the degradation of rGST-CheW2 (Fig. S3B), suggesting that certain serine proteases might be involved in the degradation of CheW2. Given that the serine protease ClpP is known to form protease complexes with AAA+ partners (ClpX) to degrade a wide range of substrates (Gersch et al., 2015) and that the ClpXP complex has been reported to degrade chemotactic components in multiple bacterial species, for example, CtrA, McpA, and McpB in C. crescentus (Alexander and Zhulin, 2007), CheW2 was postulated to be the substrate of the serine protease ClpP (Fig. S3C).

Recombinant ClpP and ClpX proteins were separately purified and they were simultaneously incubated with rGST-CheW2 in vitro, after which the CheW2 protein levels were analyzed by SDS‒PAGE and western blotting. The results revealed a time-dependent decrease in the intensity of each rGST-CheW2 band, indicating that ClpP can directly degrade CheW2 (Fig. 5A and B). To further explore the role of ClpP in regulating the protein level of CheW2 in vivo, plasmids expressing the rGFP-CheW2 fusion protein were transformed into ΔclpP and ΔcheW2. The protein levels of CheW2 at different growth stages were analyzed by western blotting. In the ΔcheW2::gfp-cheW2 strain, the expression of the fusion protein increased during the early growth phase but then progressively decreased as bacterial growth progressed, ultimately stabilizing at the basal level (Fig. 5C and D). In contrast, in the ΔclpP::gfp-cheW2 strain, the protein level of CheW2 remained stable throughout all the growth stages, with no observable protein degradation (Fig. 5E and F). These results, combined with those of the in vitro degradation assays, demonstrated that CheW2 is the target substrate of the protease ClpP.

ClpP degradation is conserved in widely divergent organisms, and many of these substrates generally have short AA peptide sequences (recognition motifs) at the extreme C-terminus (Bhat et al., 2013). Interestingly, in CheW2, a putative recognition motif (AA) was identified at the N-terminus rather than at the C-terminus (Fig. S4). To investigate the functional significance of this motif, the AA dipeptide of CheW2 at the N-terminus was mutated to DD, and the corresponding mutant protein was obtained through recombinant expression and purification. The results of in vitro degradation assays revealed that the AA-to-DD mutation completely abolished CheW2 degradation by ClpXP (Fig. 5G and H), demonstrating that the N-terminal AA dipeptide in CheW2 is essential for its specific recognition and proteolysis by ClpXP. Bioinformatic analysis revealed that 23% of the CheW has AA motif in the 100 CheW sequences of Vibrio with N-terminal distribution preference; specifically, in 18 CheW, the AA motif occurred in the N-terminal region compared to 11 CheW in the C-terminal region (a 1.6-fold difference; data not shown).

Bacterial membrane integrity was assessed by measuring the uptake of the membrane-impermeable dye PI by bacteria (Zhang et al., 2021). There were no significant differences in the fluorescence values between the WT and ΔcheW2 strains after incubation with PI (Fig. 5I). In contrast, compared with both the WT and ΔcheW2 strains, the ΔclpP strain exhibited significantly greater fluorescence values over time, indicating greater membrane damage in the ΔclpP strain (Fig. 5I). Fluorescence microscopy further confirmed the presence of membrane damage in the ΔclpP strain, as evidenced by the increased PI staining (Fig. 5J). To investigate the role of the ClpP-CheW2 axis in maintaining membrane integrity, a ΔcheW2-clpP double mutant was constructed. Compared with the ΔclpP strain, the ΔcheW2-clpP strain showed reduced PI uptake and partial restoration of membrane integrity (Fig. 5I and J). Mass spectrometry analysis of the differentially expressed protein bands corresponding to the differentially expressed proteins between the ΔclpP and WT strains identified three outer membrane proteins: porin, TolC, and LpdA (Fig. S5A). qRT‒PCR validation confirmed that the expression of these corresponding genes (porin, tolC, and lpdA) was downregulated in the ΔclpP strain but upregulated in the ΔcheW2 strain (Fig. S5B). These results confirm that the ClpP-CheW2 axis plays a critical role in maintaining bacterial membrane integrity.

Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 antagonized V. splendidus AJ01 partially through the ClpP-CheW2 axis

The sensitivity of the ΔclpP strain to Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 was further examined. No significant difference in susceptibility to Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 was found between the ΔclpP and WT strains upon growth inhibition (Fig. 6A). However, inhibition zone measurements demonstrated that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 formed significantly larger inhibition zones, indicating stronger antagonism, against the ΔclpP strain than against the WT strain (Fig. 6B and C). The observed lack of phenotypic difference may stem from the lower biomass of the ΔclpP strain in the growth inhibition assay, potentially obscuring its phenotypic divergence from the WT strain. The opposing trends in ΔclpP and ΔcheW2 antagonism by Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 indicate that the antagonistic mechanism may be partially mediated through changes in membrane integrity regulated by the ClpP-CheW2 axis.

Under stress conditions, i.e., in the presence of mitomycin C, the expression of clpP was significantly upregulated (Fig. 6D), demonstrating that ClpP responds to external environmental changes by increasing its corresponding mRNA expression under stress conditions. In the present study, transcriptomic analysis revealed significant downregulation of the clpP gene in V. splendidus AJ01 (log2FoldChange = −3.20, P < 0.001), corresponding to a 9.1-fold reduction in expression. Due to the inherent difficulty in genetically transforming the H2 strain with fluorescent plasmids, the transcriptional response of clpP in V. splendidus AJ01was specifically monitored using a promoter-reporter system introduced into V. splendidus AJ01 (Fig. 6E). The fluorescence of GFP was significantly decreased in the presence of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 (Fig. 6F). These results confirmed that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 suppressed the expression of the clpP gene, thus compromising ClpP-CheW2 axis-mediated membrane integrity and ultimately leading to growth inhibition.

Discussion

Vibriosis, a prevalent bacterial infection in aquaculture, is effectively prevented through probiotic deployment of Vibrio-antagonistic bacteria, an emerging biocontrol strategy (Wu et al., 2024). This study revealed that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 exhibited significant antagonistic activity against the pathogen V. splendidus AJ01 and demonstrated that ClpP-CheW2-regulated membrane integrity in V. splendidus AJ01 played a critical role in this antagonistic effect (Fig. 7).

Schematic representation of antagonism mediated by Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 via targeting of the ClpP-CheW2 membrane integrity regulatory axis in V. splendidus AJ01. Under normal physiological conditions, the protease ClpP constitutively degrades the CheW2 protein in V. splendidus AJ01, thereby maintaining low cellular levels of CheW2 and preventing its negative regulation of membrane integrity. Upon interaction with Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2, an unknown signal from the antagonist transcriptionally suppresses the expression of the clpP gene. This leads to the accumulation of CheW2, which in turn damages the integrity of the membrane. This membrane compromise ultimately facilitates the lethal activity of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2, leading to the death of V. splendidus AJ01.

Previous studies have shown that Pseudoalteromonas spp. effectively inhibit the growth of marine pathogens such as Vibrio vulnificus, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Vibrio harveyi, Photobacterium damselae, and Shewanella algae (Kesarcodi-Watson et al., 2012). Typical antagonism by this genus involves ECPs, such as the 280-kDa vibriostatic protein of P. piscicida X153 (Longeon et al., 2004), with broad-spectrum activity; the β-carboline alkaloid norharman (Zheng et al., 2005); the antimicrobial protein PfaP; and small molecule compounds produced by Pseudoalteromonas luteoviolacea (Yu et al., 2012). Other inhibitory systems require direct cell-to-cell contact between competing bacteria. Following the initial discovery of contact-dependent growth inhibition (CDI) mediated by the two-partner secretion family CdiAB (Ruhe et al., 2013), research revealed that bacterial type I, IV, VI (T6SS), and VII secretion systems also export antibacterial effectors, mediating contact-dependent interbacterial antagonism (Klein et al., 2020). Recent studies have shown that P. piscicida DE1-A employs contact-dependent antagonism by transferring digestive vesicles to competing bacteria (Richards et al., 2017). Considering that there were no vesicle-like structures on the extracellular surface of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 (Fig. 1G) and that the upregulated genes in Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 exhibited enrichment predominantly in pathways related to transmembrane transport and membrane components (Fig. 3D), Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 may export antibacterial effector proteins via direct contact secretion systems. Subsequent studies demonstrated that Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 downregulated the expression of the clpP gene in V. splendidus AJ01. The gene expression regulation in target cells has been previously reported in Burkholderia thailandensis, in which BcpA-CT, with DNase activity, is delivered to alter target cell gene expression (Anderson et al., 2012). Although the mechanism by which T6SS effectors reach the target cell compartment remains incompletely understood, several families of nuclease toxins that target nucleic acids and nucleotides have been identified (Ho et al., 2017). Moreover, many toxin proteins also possess tRNase activity, which can affect translation rates by modifying RNA levels in target cells (Morse et al., 2012).

Several membrane-targeting T6SS effectors, such as phosphatases and pore-forming toxins, have been shown to exhibit toxicity in the periplasmic space of target bacterial cells (Klein et al., 2020). For instance, the Ssp6 effector of Serratia marcescens has been demonstrated to inhibit bacterial growth by forming cation-selective pores in the cell membrane (LaCourse et al., 2018). In addition to the potential direct impact of effectors on the cell membrane, our study revealed a novel antagonistic mechanism mediated by the ClpP-CheW2 membrane integrity regulatory axis in V. splendidus AJ01 (Fig. 7). In bacterial chemosensory systems, the coupling protein CheW provides a critical link between chemoreceptors and the histidine kinase CheA (Alvarado et al., 2017). Recent research has demonstrated that the functions of CheW extend beyond traditional chemotaxis signal transduction. CheW has been demonstrated to participate in diverse physiological processes, including biofilm formation (Tram et al., 2020), c-di-GMP level modulation (Tao et al., 2024), and immune evasion (Sze and Li, 2023). Our research on CheW2 in V. splendidus AJ01 further supplemented and confirmed its diverse function. Specifically, CheW2 was found to be implicated in hemolytic activity, antibiotic resistance, and the regulation of the expression of genes encoding outer membrane proteins, which has not been reported previously. The positvie regulation of hemolytic activity but negatively regulated certain OMP expression by CheW2 suggest that CheW2 is closely associated with bacterial virulence and its survival in the host. Therefore, the ClpP-CheW2 axis is an way of bacteria to adjust between its viruelnce and survival, responding to the external stressors. Thus, CheW2 acts as a regulatory hub that coordinates transcriptional output, simultaneously tuning membrane architecture, resistance determinants, and virulence factors to adapt to competitive pressures (e.g., from Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2). Critically, we provide the first direct evidence that ClpP posttranslationally regulates the level of the coupling protein CheW2. The appropriate protein levels of CheWs are crucial for bacterial survival (Cardozo et al., 2010). In Agrobacterium tumefaciens, the ratio of the two CheW proteins varies significantly across growth stages (Huang et al., 2018). Previous studies have reported evidence indicating that ClpP regulates chemoreceptor homeostasis via programmed proteolysis in Sinorhizobium meliloti (Arapov et al., 2020) and C. crescentus (Bhat et al., 2013). Research on CheW in relation to ClpP has been limited to its identification as a putative substrate in E. coli (Flynn et al., 2003) and Bacillus subtilis (Gerth et al., 2008). Specifically, while previous literature predicted that the N-terminal sequence of E. coli CheW exhibits binding activity with ClpX, the role of this motif in mediating degradation remained unverified (Flynn et al., 2003). In this study, we reveal a unique recognition mechanism where ClpP specifically targets CheW2 via an N-terminal AA motif, contrasting with the canonical C-terminal recognition of substrates (Bhat et al., 2013).

The current study establishes this axis in a specific host-antagonist pair (V. splendidus AJ01/Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2). Its evolutionary conservation across other Pseudoalteromonas-Vibrio pairs remains an open question. Notably, CheW2 homologs are integral components of complex chemosensory systems widespread in Vibrios (Alvarado et al., 2017), and ClpP is a conserved stress regulator (Moreno-Cinos et al., 2019). This provides a plausible genetic and functional basis for the axis's potential existence in other competitive interactions. However, the present study still has certain limitations. The specific effector delivered by Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 that initiates ClpP suppression remains to be identified. Future work on the inhibition effect of Pseudoalteromonas sp. H2 on more diverse types of bacteria under varied environmental conditions, e.g., salinity and temperature, will be performed to confirm the universality of this mechanism. Addressing these points will not only provide deeper insights into the antagonistic mechanism of bacteria, but also offer novel strategies for disease prevention.
