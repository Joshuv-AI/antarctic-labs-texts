An analysis of bundled care funding for total hip and knee arthroplasty in Ontario, Canada: a population-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jhase Sniderman, Jesse Wolfstadt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Rising health care expenditures and dissatisfaction with traditional models of reimbursement have driven an interest in alternative payment model (APM) initiatives. Bundled funding, an APM, was implemented province-wide for elective total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) in Ontario in 2019. In this study, we explored whether procedure volume, quality of care, and cost were affected by the program’s introduction.Methods:In this retrospective cohort study, we developed pre- and postimplementation patient cohorts with aggregate data collected from the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) and Canadian Joint Replacement Registry. We assessed quality via length of stay, 30-day readmissions, emergency department visits, and revision surgeries. We assessed costs using methodology and data provided by CIHI. We performed statistical analysis by comparing patient cohorts via χ2 and Student t tests.Results:After the introduction of the bundle, case volume increased, length of stay decreased, and more patients were discharged directly home following surgery (p ≤ 0.001). Patients with THA were less likely to be readmitted or visit the emergency department in the postbundled cohort (p ≤ 0.009). Despite a reduced length of stay, the cost of THA and TKA increased, with $106 more being spent per patient (p ≤ 0.001).Conclusion:The introduction of bundled funding for THA and TKA in Ontario was associated with preserved quality of care despite shorter lengths of stay in hospital and reduced use of inpatient rehabilitation. Although cost containment is often a goal of bundled funding, Ontario’s model saw a rise in inpatient surgical costs. A shift to outpatient arthroplasty could yield significant cost savings under the current bundle design.

Health systems throughout the developed world continue to face similar challenges as cost of care and health care expenditures grow. Many countries are now spending a significant portion of their gross domestic product on health care, with Canada spending more than $250 billion annually.1 As a result, governments and policy-makers have been forced to explore alternatives to traditional reimbursement, moving from fee-for-service models to alternative payment models (APMs).2 Bundled funding, a form of APM, is centred on a lump-sum payment that covers all services delivered by a health care team for a particular episode of patient care. This offers the potential to better integrate health teams, allow for global budgeting, and improve the quality of care patients receive.3 Pairing bundled funding with high-volume procedures that have established evidence-based care pathways is ideal, as it creates an optimal model for predicting the cost of care.4 Bundled funding for elective total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has worked as a successful model in both the United States and Sweden as part of health care reform, with the goal of improving costs and value.5,6

Ontario has been at the forefront of health payment innovation in Canada and had previously introduced Quality-Based Procedures (QBPs) for orthopedic surgery in 2013.7 These procedures are a variant of funding in which a price is set for a prespecified diagnosis or procedure.8 In an attempt to better integrate patient care and tie outcomes to quality, an initial pilot model for bundled funding was developed and implemented in 2017 for elective THA and TKA.9 This subsequently became mandatory in 2019 and covers the spectrum of care from initial clinic visit to 90 days after surgery.9 The bundle price was set at $9630.84 for THA and $8626.69 for TKA for the introductory year.9 Although every patient is eligible for inpatient postoperative rehabilitation, this significantly increases costs and requires an additional $5590 to $6184 of funding.9 Quality was initially designed to be monitored via patient-reported outcome measures, although this was not mandatory and has yet to be implemented. There is similarly no reward or penalty for providing higher- or lower-than-average care.9 Like the QBP program that came before it, funding is carved out of each hospital’s global budget and allocated based on a fixed volume of targeted care for these procedures.10 This effectively allows the government to budget for joint replacement surgery more accurately (i.e., volume of joints × bundle price = total annual expenditure) and promotes intrahospital competition to budget more effectively. In theory, hospitals that budget effectively and reduce expenditures would reap savings, allowing for more joint replacements to be performed. To date, the impact of hospital funding reform via bundled care payments for elective THA and TKA has not been assessed in Ontario. Our aim was to determine whether the implementation of bundled funding was associated with changes in procedure volume, patient selection, quality of care, and cost.

Methods

Study design and setting

We performed an aggregate, retrospective, population-based analysis of administrative health data from Ontario, Canada, where all medically necessary services are publicly funded by a single provincial health insurance program. We developed patient cohorts for before and after bundled care funding was implemented. The pre-implementation cohort consisted of THA and TKAs performed between April 2017 and February 2018, and the postimplementation cohort spanned from April 2019 to February 2020. This time interval was chosen to account for the COVID-19 pandemic, which began to affect Canadian hospitals in March 2020.

Data sources and case identification

We used multiple population-wide administrative health databases via repositories accessed by the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) and Canadian Joint Replacement Registry. Elective primary THA and TKA procedures were identified using Canadian Classification of Health Intervention codes and confirmed to have a main diagnosis code of arthritis. We used the Discharge Abstract Database and National Ambulatory Care Reporting Services databases to calculate volume of procedures, age, gender, length of stay, Charlson Comorbidity Index score, and discharge disposition. We then linked these cases with revision surgeries, readmissions, and emergency department visits within 30 days from Ontario-based facilities.

Covariates

We collected and reported age, sex, and comorbidity status; we ascertained comorbidity status using the Charlson Comorbidity Index.

Quality outcomes

We measured the mean (± standard deviation [SD]) length of patient stay; percentage of patients discharged directly home; and volume of revision surgeries, readmissions, and emergency department visits within 30 days.

Cost outcome

We calculated inpatient costs (± SD) by multiplying the average Resource Intensity Weight for THA and TKA procedures by the provincial Cost of a Standard Hospital Stay.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed patient cohorts with χ2 and Student t tests with respect to demographic, quality, and cost data.

Discussion

As health care spending continues to increase, payment innovation in the form of APMs remains an attractive option to drive quality improvement and cost containment. A mandatory bundled funding model for THA and TKA care was rolled out in 2019 in Ontario, Canada. This health policy change was associated with preserved quality of care in the face of significantly shorter lengths of patient stay, reduced inpatient rehabilitation use, and efforts to contain costs. Despite these efforts, the cost of performing an inpatient joint replacement increased in the year after the introduction of the bundle. Our findings have important implications for policy-makers and health systems experimenting with new funding models.

The annual volume of joint replacement surgeries continues to grow and is expected to persist as the population ages.11 When we remove the impact of COVID-19, the annual 5-year volume of joint replacements in Canada has grown by nearly 20%.11 At the same time, wait times have increased, backlogs exist, and there is an ongoing need for better access.12 This continues to be felt in Ontario, as evidenced by the 6596 more procedures performed in the year after the introduction of the bundle. With the bundle price fixed and annual volume increasing, the provincial cost of joint replacement surgery will continue to rise exponentially. The continued allocation of significant financial resources to joint replacement is thus needed, even if the bundle is able to drive value-based changes. To that effect, there are lessons to be learned from our peers who have introduced similar bundled payment models for joint replacement.

Bundled payment models have been the focus of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services innovation platform to reduce health care expenditures and improve quality in the US since 2008.13 Multiple iterations of these models have been applied to THA and TKA, with varying degrees of success.14 Whereas these models are more complex in their bundle design, their implementation has resulted in similar findings with preserved quality of care when measured by hospital readmissions rates, emergency department presentations, revision surgeries, and mortality.15 These US-based models were able to save about 4.5% costs per episode of care.16 Even greater cost savings were found upon the introduction of a bundled funding model for total joint arthroplasty (TJA) in Sweden.17 However, the year-after-year pressure to provide more value has created some disatisfaction with physicians and hospital systems who feel they are in a “race to the bottom” to provide the lowest cost of care before quality suffers.18,19 This similarly disincentivizes caring for the most complex and at-risk patients who also need care.19 Although cost containment was not the sole intent of Ontario’s bundle design, it is worth exploring hurdles to future cost savings within the current bundles design.

The annual cost of performing a joint replacement in Canada has increased steadily despite great strides in reducing the length of stay in hospital.20 As evidenced by previous data and the results of this study, the push to reduced length of stay can yield only a finite amount of cost savings on a per-case basis.11,21 The length of stay decreased by a mean of 0.5 days after the introduction of the bundle and even more so following the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, with the average length of stay now about 1.8 days.12 An annual average inflation rate of about 3% since 2018 and persistent supply chain disruptions owing to the COVID-19 pandemic are likely responsible for growing day-of-surgery costs identified in this study.22,23 Additionally, the increased use of disposables and the push to integrate technology into joint replacement and implant innovation have certainly contributed to expenditures at some centres.24,25 It has yet to be seen whether the return on this investment will provide considerable value for patients.26,27 Standardizing protocols and instrumentation at the intrahospital level may help to yield cost savings in this regard, as this was a successful component of Sweden’s bundle design.28 Postdischarge health care utilization continues to be a major driver of episode-of-care costs.29 The use of inpatient rehabilitation in Ontario can add nearly $5590 to $6184 of cost per patient.9 With only 10% of bundle funding allocated for inpatient use, there is an emphasis on getting patients home. The bundle has been a success in this regard, with significant improvements seen province wide, as evidenced here. A common concern with reduced lengths of stay and less inpatient rehabilitation use has been whether patients are ready for this. If they’re not, the cost of a patient returning to the emergency department or having to be readmitted is substantial.30 In this study, 30-day emergency department visits and readmissions actually decreased within the THA cohort, potentially indicating a greater emphasis being placed on patient education, multimodal analgesia, and access to telemedicine.31 The success of early discharge home after inpatient THA and TKA has played a role in the emergence of outpatient arthroplasty as a viable option for a proportion of patients. The absolute need for this was accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, but was likely heading in this direction with substantial improvements in accelerated rehabilitation, medical optimization, and multimodal pain control.32,33 Outpatient THA and TKA can be provided in either the outpatient hospital setting or through an ambulatory surgical centre, which is becoming increasingly common in the US and is now incorporated into its most recent bundled funding model.34 Institutions in Ontario have similarly been moving in this direction and have had success in transitioning TJA to an outpatient procedure.33,35,36 As the bundle is designed currently, a shift to outpatient joint replacement could result in considerable savings to the hospital and would help free up valuable inpatient beds, which is essential in our current health care landscape. Ambulatory surgical centres are becoming increasingly common and may provide an avenue to further reduce costs.37

Costs aside, patient care and quality must be preserved with any new innovation. With previous iterations of bundled payments, there has been concern about “cherry-picking” or choosing only the healthiest patients for surgery.38,39 Complex patients have been found to have longer lengths of stay, more often require inpatient rehabilitation, and are at risk of postdischarge health care utilization.19,40 In our series there was no difference in patient age, gender, or Charlson Comorbidity Index score, indicating no detectable change in patient selection. This issue may lie in the design of the bundle, with bundles that penalize providers for overspending being more at risk of this. As currently designed, there are no penalties or rewards for how hospitals perform within Ontario’s bundle. Patient care and quality were not sacrificed despite external pressures to perform an increased volume of cases and get patients home sooner. The quality of care was maintained and, in some aspects, improved when measured by 30-day emergency department visits, readmissions, and revision surgeries. This is in line with US- and Swedish-based bundles, which cite the importance of teamwork, planning, and coordination of care within a bundled model.41,42

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the impact of bundled funding on surgical care in Ontario. Although the introduction of a QBP-based model was not successful in standardizing care and improving quality for patients with hip fracture, this bundled funding model has shown some promise in driving quality improvement.10 The future success of the model will, however, depend on its continued evolution. With continued pressure for cost certainty, the incorporation of physician fees into the bundle would further standardize the cost of care. Similarly, rewards and penalties for institutions based on performance could be introduced. These changes from the status quo would surely require significant physician, hospital, and policy-maker compromise. The collection of patient-reported outcome measures as the initial bundle proposed would be beneficial to ensuring ongoing quality of care. The cost and resources required for collecting these is substantial, however, and likely not feasible for many hospitals, unless additional financial resources are allocated.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Our analysis was confined to health administrative data, which lack clinical detail and depend on accurate coding of procedures. The rollout of Ontario’s bundled funding model occurred in the year preceding the COVID-19 pandemic, precluding longer-term data collection. The cost analysis performed was limited to the inpatient stay only and does not reflect the entire duration of the patient episode of care, which could be captured only by estimating costs of urgent care or emergency department visits, readmissions, and complications. These limitations, as well as further statistical analysis with an interrupted time-series analysis, could be addressed as part of future studies on payment reform in Ontario.

Conclusion

The introduction of bundled funding for THA and TKA in Ontario was associated with preserved quality of care despite shorter lengths of stay in hospital and reduced use of inpatient rehabilitation. Although cost containment is often a goal of bundled funding, Ontario’s model saw a rise in inpatient surgical costs. A shift to outpatient arthroplasty and ambulatory surgical centres could yield significant cost savings under the current bundle design.
