Bacteria of the lung microbiome and health biomarkers in chronic airway disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Lucia Grassi, Febe Heye, Kristiaan Proesmans, Emmanuel Abatih, Axelle Van Daele, Lies Lahousse, Aurélie Crabbé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe lung microbiome is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to the development and progression of chronic airway diseases. While these conditions are typically associated with reduced microbial diversity and pathogen overgrowth, emerging evidence suggests that non-pathogenic bacteria may influence clinical outcomes. However, inconsistent findings across studies have made it difficult to determine their exact role in disease pathophysiology. To identify potentially beneficial members of the lung microbiome, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical studies investigating the association between non-pathogenic bacterial genera or species and clinico-pathological features in individuals with asthma, bronchiectasis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cystic fibrosis. For the meta-analysis, data from different diseases were combined. Our analysis revealed that several bacteria in the lung microbiome were significantly associated with improved lung function and/or reduced airway inflammation across diseases. Although causal relationships cannot be established due to the absence of interventional studies, our findings highlight promising candidates for functional characterization and therapeutic exploration. Considerable heterogeneity in study design and reporting underscores the need for standardized methods and validation in relevant experimental models to advance our understanding of the lung microbiome in chronic airway diseases and inform the development of effective microbiome-based interventions.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

Growing evidence indicates a connection between the lung microbiome and several chronic airway diseases (CADs), including asthma, bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis (CF) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Alterations in the diversity and composition of the lung microbiome in these diseases are increasingly linked to lung pathogenesis1,2. Individuals with CADs commonly exhibit reduced microbial diversity compared to healthy airways and a shift toward pathogen-dominated communities3,4. Expansion of pathogenic bacteria in the lung microbiome has been consistently associated with worse clinical outcomes, often leading to impaired lung function, increased exacerbation rates, and decreased survival5–8. Distinct microbiome profiles have also been reported depending on disease severity and inflammatory phenotypes, with more severe cases typically linked to higher abundance of respiratory pathogens9,10.

While traditional pathogens have been widely studied in various CADs, recent research has shifted attention to bacteria commonly considered non-pathogenic11. Many of these bacteria have been proposed to influence the course of respiratory diseases by modulating host immune responses, susceptibility to infection and/or treatment responsiveness12,13. A growing number of studies have found positive associations between certain members of the lung microbiome and favourable clinical features in individuals with CADs, including lower airway inflammation and fewer respiratory symptoms14–17. These observations suggest that bacteria with potentially beneficial properties may be present in diseased airways. This could have potential for the development of novel microbiome-based therapeutic interventions18,19. Nonetheless, there remain inconsistencies across studies regarding the specific bacteria associated with clinically relevant biomarkers, making it difficult to determine their impact on the pathophysiology of CADs3.

To address the variability in current evidence, we conducted a systematic review of clinical randomized and observational studies investigating the association between individual lung microbiome members and clinico-pathological features (i.e., lung function, inflammatory markers, symptom severity, disease progression indicators). We aimed to identify any concordances in bacteria-biomarker associations in different CADs. When sufficient studies were available, we performed a meta-analysis to detect significant trends across diseases. In vitro and in vivo studies were also discussed to provide insights into potential mechanisms by which lung microbiome bacteria may influence disease. This review could provide a basis for the strategic selection of bacterial genera/species for further investigation (e.g., mechanistic studies), potentially uncovering new therapeutic avenues for respiratory diseases.

Methods

This systematic review was performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)20. The review was not registered in a database and no protocol was prepared before conducting the study. The PRISMA checklist is provided in Supplementary Table 7.

Eligibility criteria

Research question and eligibility criteria were determined using the PECO framework (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome). The objective of the review was to identify bacteria in the lung microbiome associated with improved clinical biomarkers in individuals with CADs. Only original research published as a full-text article in peer-reviewed scientific journals was included in this review. Inclusion criteria were: (1) clinical randomized and observational studies with the abundance of microbiome members as intervention and clinical parameters (e.g., lung function, inflammatory markers, disease severity indicators) as biomarkers; (2) individuals with a clinical diagnosis of asthma, bronchiectasis, CF or COPD; (3) focus on bacteria of the lower respiratory tract that are not commonly recognized as pathogens; (4) reporting on the association of individual bacterial genera and/or species with quantitative changes in clinical parameters. Exclusion criteria were: (1) patient groups vulnerable towards opportunistic infections (e.g., HIV) and transplant recipients; (2) use of methods other than next-generation sequencing (NGS) for microbiome analysis; (3) microbiome composition metrics other than relative abundance of individual bacteria (e.g., diversity and dominance); (4) bacterial identification at phylum and family level, and sequence-based taxonomic units (i.e., operational taxonomic units and amplicon sequence variants); (5) reporting on the association between lung microbiome composition and pharmacological interventions.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in MEDLINE (PubMed) for full-text articles published in English between January 1, 2008, and March 31, 2025. A start year of 2008 was selected to ensure all lung microbiome studies were captured. Indeed, the first culture-independent study identifying microorganisms in the lungs dates back to 201021. The selection of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords was optimized through an iterative process based on the inclusion of reference studies (Supplementary Table 1). Reviews were excluded from the search by using the term “NOT ‘review’ [publication type]”. We also searched the reference list of publications eligible for full-text screening to identify other potentially relevant studies.

Study selection and data collection

Screening of the retrieved reports (title, abstract and full text) and data extraction from eligible studies were conducted independently by two authors (FH and LG). Corresponding authors were contacted when the data provided in the published articles was unclear or incomplete. When results were reported as heatmaps, three independent reviewers (AVD, FH, and LG) performed a manual extraction of data from the original figures with the aid of the eyedropper tool of Microsoft PowerPoint.

Data items

The following information was collected from the selected studies: publication details (name of first author and publication year), study design, country where the study was conducted, characteristics of participants (age, sex, disease status, and use of medications), sample size, type of respiratory samples and lung microbiome sequencing method. As primary outcomes of interest, we extracted measures of association between the relative abundance of bacteria and clinical parameters of individuals with CADs. These included the following effect measures: odds ratio, risk ratio, regression coefficient, and Spearman correlation coefficient. We focused exclusively on bacteria identified at the genus and species level. A comprehensive description of the clinical parameters is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Data synthesis and analysis

A qualitative synthesis of the measures of association was performed for bacterial genera (or species) and clinical parameters that were examined in at least three studies. Results of individual studies were summarized in tables indicating the presence and direction of associations between the relative abundance of individual bacteria and clinical biomarkers in each disease. Meta-analysis was conducted for bacterial genera and clinical biomarkers with sufficient data (≥3 studies per genus-biomarker combination). For each genus, subgroup analyses examined different clinical parameters separately. Data were pooled across disease categories (asthma, COPD, CF, bronchiectasis) to maximize statistical power. We explored disease-specific analyses where feasible; however, only three genus-biomarker combinations (Gemella-IL-6 in COPD, Prevotella-lung function in COPD, and Rothia-lung function in CF) contained ≥3 studies within a single disease. As no genus-biomarker combination contained multiple disease subgroups with ≥3 studies each, formal statistical testing for between-disease interactions was not performed. Our cross-disease analytical approach was necessitated by the limited number of available studies and represents a hypothesis-generating framework to identify candidate bacteria for future investigation.

To evaluate potential confounding by antibiotics, corticosteroids, and smoking, we performed sensitivity analyses stratified by exposure prevalence at the study level. For each potential confounder, studies were classified as <50% prevalence or ≥50% prevalence based on reported participant characteristics. Studies without reported or unclear metadata were excluded from sensitivity analyses. Meta-analyses were re-run when ≥3 studies remained for a given genus-biomarker combination. Heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics (Hunter-Smidt method), with I² < 50%, 50-75%, and >75% representing low, moderate, and considerable heterogeneity, respectively22, which was listed for all analyses in Supplementary Table 6. When data were reported as a range of values, the averages were used for the meta-analysis to preserve statistical independence. Effect measures were converted to Spearman correlation coefficients using an online calculator (www.escal.site). Risk ratios were manually converted to odds ratios before being entered into the online tool.

The bare-bones estimate of the overall Spearman rank correlations was calculated. This was done by computing a composite Spearman ranked correlation represented by the average of the values from the three reviewers or simply using the presented values. In addition, adjustments were made for differences in sample sizes and variances by weighting. The ma-r function in the psychmeta package of R was used for the meta-analysis (optional parameters were: collapse_method = “average” and wt_type = “inv_var_sample”)23.

Certainty of the evidence was assessed qualitatively based on multiple factors, such as quality and risk of bias of the included studies, consistency of the results, precision of effect estimates, sample size, study design and potential for reporting bias.

Quality and risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality of the selected studies was assessed using the National Institute for Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for observational cohort and cross-sectional studies24. The tool consists of 14 questions regarding study participant selection, outcome measurement, and search for confounding variables. Depending on the number of questions receiving affirmative answers, the overall quality of the selected studies is rated as ‘poor’ (0 to 4), ‘fair’ (5 to 10) or ‘good’ (11 to 14)25,26. We identified age, gender, smoking and medications (e.g., antimicrobials, bronchodilators, corticosteroids) as critical confounders.

Results

Study selection

A total of 462 potentially relevant studies were retrieved using our search strategy. According to the eligibility criteria, 325 studies were excluded after title and abstract screening, and additional 106 studies were removed after full-text screening. Three additional studies were identified through citation search. Finally, 34 studies were selected in this systematic review (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection.

Characteristics of included studies

Among the 34 selected studies (Table 1), 8 focused on asthma, 6 on bronchiectasis, 7 on CF, and 10 on COPD. Two studies investigated bronchiectasis along with asthma or COPD27,28, and another included individuals with asthma-COPD overlap syndrome (ACO)29. Most studies were cross-sectional (n = 26), while the remainder were longitudinal studies exploring the association between lung microbiome and disease progression (n = 8). Fifteen studies were conducted in East Asia (China, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan) or South-East Asia (Singapore, Malaysia) and 19 in Westernized populations (Australia, Europe, UK, USA). The median number of individuals included in these studies was 81, the mean was 114 (range: 8–877), with a total of 4119 participants. Most studies enrolled only adult participants (n = 28), while some also included paediatric subjects (i.e., < 16 years old) (n = 6). The medication usage varied greatly among participants, with only one study conducted in the absence of treatment30. The most common medications were antibiotics (n = 10), corticosteroids (n = 23), and bronchodilators (n = 12).

Different types of respiratory samples were collected for the analysis of microbiome composition, including sputum [spontaneously expectorated (n = 7), induced (n = 14), both spontaneously and expectorated sputum (n = 5), collection method not specified (n = 3)] (n = 29), bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) (n = 4) and endobronchial brush (EB) (n = 1)]. The most widely used method for microbiome analysis was amplicon sequencing targeting different variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene (n = 30). One study performed full-length sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene31, while three studies conducted whole-genome sequencing32–34. Most studies used Illumina sequencing platforms (n = 24), followed by Roche 454 (n = 4), Ion Torrent (n = 2), Nanopore (n = 1), and PacBio (n = 1). Regarding the taxonomic classification of microbiome sequences, 24 studies identified bacteria at the genus level, 9 achieved species-level identification, and one provided both levels of identification31.

Most studies focused on inflammatory markers (n = 21) and lung function (n = 19) as primary outcome(s). A more limited number of studies evaluated disease severity (e.g., symptom severity) (n = 5) and progression (e.g., exacerbation and lung function decline) (n = 7).

Significant heterogeneity was found in the criteria used for the selection of bacterial genera/species to be included in microbiome-disease association studies (Supplementary Table 3). For instance, some studies focused on the most abundant bacteria (n = 8) or established a threshold value in relative abundance to account for their inclusion in the analysis (n = 7). Other studies identified representative bacteria (n = 7) or selected bacteria with different relative abundance in healthy and diseased individuals and/or in different disease groups (n = 5).

Synthesis of main results

Association of lung microbiome bacteria with lung function

Most studies used forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) (n = 17) and FEV1/FVC (forced vital capacity) ratio (n = 7) as primary measures of lung function. At the genus level, several bacteria were positively linked to lung function across various CADs (Table 2). At least two studies demonstrated a positive association between the relative abundance of Actinomyces, Prevotella and Streptococcus and FEV1 in both CF and COPD16,17,30,35,36. In COPD, Prevotella and Veillonella were positively associated with FEV1/FVC ratio35,37. In contrast to these reports, two studies documented a reduction in FEV1 in the presence of Prevotella and Streptococcus in asthma and ACO, respectively29,38. Other genera (i.e., Granulicatella, Peptostreptococcus, Porphyromonas, Rothia) were suggested to be positively associated with lung function in only one study29,35,36,38. We explored disease-specific meta-analyses for all genus-biomarker combinations. Only three combinations contained ≥3 studies within a single disease category: Prevotella-FEV1 in COPD (n = 3 studies), Rothia- FEV1 in CF (n = 3 studies), and Gemella-IL-6 in COPD (n = 3 studies) (Supplementary Figure 1). Disease-specific meta-analysis for Prevotella in COPD confirmed a statistically significant positive association with lung function. In contrast, Rothia in CF and Gemella-IL-6 in COPD did not show statistically significant associations in disease-specific analyses. No other genus-biomarker combination contained sufficient studies (≥3) within a single disease to permit disease-specific meta-analysis.

Association of lung microbiome bacteria with inflammatory markers

Neutrophil and eosinophil levels in respiratory samples served as primary biomarkers for airway inflammation severity (n = 14). A few studies (n = 4) also measured the activity of neutrophil elastase (NE), a key protease released by activated neutrophils and associated with disease severity, incl. lung damage, in different chronic airway diseases40–42. Given the broad spectrum of secreted inflammatory mediators examined in the included studies, we focused on a panel of 12 cytokines most commonly assessed across diseases (Supplementary Table 2). It is worth noting that some of these cytokines are predominantly associated with eosinophilic (e.g., IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) or neutrophilic (e.g., IL-1β, IL-8, IL-17) inflammation, thus being differentially relevant to distinct CADs43–45.

Eosinophilic inflammation was evaluated in studies of asthma, COPD, and ACO. A negative association with eosinophil percentage was reported only for one genus (i.e., Capnocytophaga)16 and one species (i.e., Prevotella nigrescens)33,39. Conversely, elevated eosinophil levels were associated with the presence of multiple genera, including Fusobacterium, Gemella, Granulicatella, Neisseria, Rothia, and Streptococcus7,10,46. At the species level, similar results were obtained for Porphyromonas pasteri and Veillonella rogosae47. However, none of these associations were significant across diseases based on the conducted meta-analysis (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Unlike eosinophilic inflammation, neutrophil levels were measured in all CADs. In asthma, neutrophil percentage in sputum samples was positively associated with Leptotrichia and negatively associated with Gemella and Lautropia10,46. Both positive and negative associations were identified for Neisseria and Porphyromonas10,38,46,48. Nevertheless, analysis at the species level revealed lower neutrophil levels to be associated with Neisseria flavescens, Neisseria sicca, and Porphyromonas endodontalis33. In COPD, lower neutrophil levels were detected in association with Megasphaera and Prevotella (including P. melaninogenica)48. Interestingly, different studies indicated a negative impact of Streptococcus (n = 3) and Veillonella (n = 2) on neutrophilic infiltration in multiple CADs10,48,49. Data obtained for different Streptococcus species (3 out of 6 species evaluated) aligned with this observation33,47. Associations involving the genus Streptococcus were also significant across CADs according to subgroup meta-analysis (Fig. 2E).

Data on NE activity were collected primarily in CF studies and mirrored the observations on neutrophil levels. Indeed, NE activity exhibited a negative association with both Streptococcus and Veillonella17,36. Studies conducted at the species level showed that Neisseria perflava and Streptococcus salivarius were negatively associated with NE activity in asthma32,47. According to the meta-analysis, statistically significant associations were found for Prevotella, Rothia, Streptococcus, and Veillonella (Fig. 2C-F).

Most studies quantified pro-inflammatory cytokines in respiratory samples, while only three examined the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-1031,32,50. A few genera (i.e., Fusobacterium, Granulicatella, Neisseria, Solobacterium) were shown to be negatively associated with multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines (n ≥ 4) in samples from various disease cohorts (Supplementary Table 4). Interestingly, Leptotrichia, Neisseria and Rothia were negatively associated with neutrophil-derived cytokines (i.e., IL-1β and IL-8), but positively associated with eosinophil-derived cytokines (i.e., IL-4 and IL-5)16,17,31. A decrease in both eosinophil- and neutrophil-derived cytokines was detected with increased relative abundance of Granulicatella, Solobacterium, and Streptococcus17,31,46. Contradictory outcomes were reported for Actinomyces and Veillonella, which exhibited both positive and negative associations with eosinophil-derived cytokines in asthma and COPD16,31,46. Subgroup meta-analysis indicated that Neisseria, Prevotella, and Rothia were linked to significantly lower concentrations of both IL-1β and IL-8 across diseases (Fig. 2B–D), and decreased levels of IL-1β were observed in the presence of Streptococcus, and Veillonella (Fig. 2E, F). Several genera were negatively associated with TNF-α levels based on the meta-analysis, including Actinomyces, Granulicatella, Lautropia, Prevotella and Veillonella (Fig. 2A, C, F and Supplementary Table 3). No significant associations were observed for the other cytokines analysed (i.e., IL-4, IL-5, IL-6) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

At the species level, a negative association with IL-1β, IL-8 and/or TNF-α was reported for different members of the genera included in the meta-analysis (i.e., R. mucilaginosa, Streptococcus parasanguinis, S. salivarius, Veillonella dispar) (Table 3).

Association of lung microbiome bacteria with disease severity and progression

The modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea scale was the most widely used parameter to assess the severity of respiratory symptoms. A decrease in the mMRC score was observed in association with Actinobacillus, Fusobacterium, Gemella, and Prevotella in the context of COPD35,37.

Regarding disease progression, most studies measured the annual decline in FEV1 and/or the occurrence of exacerbations. Among the tested genera, a significant (negative) correlation with FEV1 decline was reported only for Gemella14,51,52. However, no significant correlations emerged from the subgroup meta-analysis (Supplementary Figure 2). The abundance of Streptococcus and Neisseria was associated with increased exacerbation frequency in individuals with ACO and bronchiectasis, respectively29,53. Conversely, no significant association was found for Porphyromonas, Prevotella, Rothia, and Veillonella15,29. At the species level, higher levels of Prevotella intermedia (but not other Prevotella species) were linked to a lower number of exacerbations in asthma33.

Sensitivity analysis for potential confounders

To assess potential confounding by antibiotics, corticosteroids, and smoking, we explored whether sensitivity analyses stratified by exposure prevalence could be performed. Studies were stratified into <50% prevalence and ≥50% prevalence for each confounder, and studies without reported or unclear metadata were excluded. We then assessed whether sufficient studies (≥3) remained in each group to re-run meta-analyses for each genus-biomarker combination. For antibiotics and corticosteroids, no genus-biomarker combination retained ≥3 studies in either stratified group. For smoking, only Prevotella-FEV₁ had sufficient studies with reported smoking data after stratification. However, all four studies had <50% current smoking prevalence16,35,37,38, and the meta-analysis on this group of studies yielded a pooled estimate of r = 0.3 (non-significant) (Supplementary Figure 4). A substantial heterogeneity was observed for the stratified meta-analysis (I² = 93.6%). This heterogeneity seems to reflect disease-specific differences (COPD vs. asthma) rather than smoking confounding. Specifically, the study by Liu et al. was the only study examining stable asthma patients (demonstrating a negative correlation between Prevotella abundance and FEV₁), while all other studies examined COPD populations. When examining the pattern of the results, the three COPD studies with smoking data showed positive or neutral associations between Prevotella abundance and lung function despite varying current smoking prevalence (0%, 24%, 34%). Most notably, the largest study (Ramsheh et al., n = 339, 0% current smokers) demonstrated strong positive associations between Prevotella and multiple lung function parameters. This pattern - with Prevotella showing opposite associations in asthma versus COPD - suggests disease-specific biological mechanisms rather than smoking confounding and aligns with disease-specific meta-analysis finding that Prevotella is significantly associated with higher FEV1 in COPD (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Quality and risk of bias of the selected studies

All included studies were categorised as ‘fair’, with the exception of the study by Einarsson et al. (2024), which was rated as ‘good’ (Supplementary Table 5)15. Common sources of potential bias were identified in the included studies. The definition of study populations was often limited, particularly regarding comorbidities (n = 7) and pharmacological treatments (n = 9) (Table 1). Several studies (n = 15) had unclear and/or incomplete reporting on participant selection criteria (Supplementary Table 5), raising concerns about selection bias and limiting the generalizability of results. Most studies (n = 30) also failed to adjust for important confounders, such as age, gender, smoking and use of medication(s). Only 4 studies accounted for at least one of the key confounding variables in the statistical analysis15,31,35,54.

While a formal assessment of reporting bias was not possible due to the small number of studies per outcome (n < 10), no major concerns emerged from the quality assessment. Indeed, most of the included studies (n = 25) reported data clearly and consistently (Supplementary Table 5). When reporting bias was identified, the main sources were selective outcome reporting (e.g., only reporting statistically significant associations)10,34,49 and/or inaccurate definitions of outcome measures37,46.

Given the small sample sizes for each outcome and the limited control over confounding factors observed in the majority of studies, the overall certainty of the evidence is reduced. In addition, we occasionally observed inconsistencies in the direction of effect estimates, with some studies reporting positive associations and others negative or null associations. Nevertheless, the fair quality of studies suggests that the synthesis provides valuable insights into the association between microbial genera and clinical outcomes. These considerations led to a moderate rating of evidence certainty. Therefore, while the results of the synthesis should be interpreted with caution (particularly with regard to the effect estimates), they offer a solid foundation for future research.

Discussion

Although the lung microbiome is increasingly recognised as a key player in the pathophysiology of CADs, the precise impact of non-pathogenic microorganisms remains poorly defined1. Some bacterial genera or species have been proposed to exert potentially beneficial effects, but there is no consensus on which are consistently associated with improved disease outcomes. In light of their potential therapeutic relevance, we performed a systematic review on the current evidence to enhance understanding of the topic and uncover consistent patterns across studies. For the most abundant and/or representative bacterial genera, we conducted a meta-analysis to identify statistically significant associations across different CADs. Although the results should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size, we found potentially beneficial bacteria in the lung microbiome. This includes Prevotella and Veillonella, which were positively associated with lung function, although effect sizes were modest (r < 0.3) and clinical significance uncertain. Several other genera were linked to lower levels of various inflammatory markers, including neutrophil-related cytokines (i.e., Actinomyces, Granulicatella, Lautropia, Neisseria, Prevotella, Rothia, Streptococcus, Veillonella) and NE activity (i.e., Prevotella, Rothia, Streptococcus, and Veillonella).

Comparative studies on lung microbiome composition have shown that many of these genera are typically more abundant in the airways of healthy individuals than in those with CADs, thus supporting hypotheses of protective effects, though causation cannot be established31,38,55–58. Significant differences in their relative abundance have also been observed according to disease status, with higher levels commonly found in mild compared to severe cases56,59–61. Similar shifts were evident between stable disease and exacerbation in different CADs, further implying their possible contribution to mitigating disease progression37,50,62. In vitro and in vivo studies have provided additional evidence in support of the potentially beneficial effects of these microbiome members, while also offering insights into their putative mechanism(s) of action. For instance, different Prevotella, Rothia, and Streptococcus species have been reported to attenuate inflammatory responses triggered by respiratory pathogens and/or other pro-inflammatory stimuli both in airway epithelial cell cultures and mouse models63–67. Additionally, R. mucilaginosa and S. salivarius have been shown to protect infected mice against airway damage induced by viral and bacterial pathogens68,69. In addition to their host-directed effect(s), Prevotella and Streptococcus species have demonstrated the ability to inhibit growth and/or biofilm formation by the respiratory pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which may help explain their positive association with lung health63,69–71. In contrast, in vitro and in vivo observations diverged from the meta-analysis results for the genus Neisseria, with different species promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine production and/or epithelial damage (e.g., Neisseria subflava)53,65,67. The latter in vitro and in vivo studies bring forward the possibility that some bacteria of the lung microbiome that are conventionally considered as non-pathogenic in the context of airway disease may function as pathobionts - which are organisms that are typically benign but can drive pathology under specific conditions. Such effects are likely to be dependent on, among others, the species, strain, disease state, microbial load, host immunity and co-occurring microorganisms. Importantly, genus-level associations in clinical datasets may obscure this underlying heterogeneity. Thus, there is currently insufficient evidence to classify non-pathogenic bacteria of the lung microbiome as pathobionts. Species- and strain-resolved, longitudinal, and mechanistic studies in relevant model systems are needed to resolve these discrepancies and establish causality.

The marked heterogeneity observed between studies should be carefully considered when interpreting the results of this review. Indeed, differences in study populations and microbiome analysis methodologies may have influenced the results of our synthesis. Variations in age, sex and geographical distribution of study participants can have a significant impact on the composition of the lung microbiome in respiratory diseases49,72. Pharmacological interventions (e.g., antibiotics and corticosteroids) and smoking habits are also known to change airway microbial communities73–75. Nevertheless, the majority of the selected studies did not adjust for these important confounders, which introduces potential bias and represents a major limitation. We attempted to evaluate the role of potential confounders (antibiotic use, corticosteroid use, smoking status) in the outcomes of the present study, where feasible, using a stratification approach. While stratification could not be performed for the potential confounders antibiotic- and corticosteroid use for any of the bacterium-clinical biomarker combinations, the combination Prevotella-FEV₁ had sufficient studies with reported meta-data on current smoking. Analysis of the group of four studies with reported smoking status provides evidence against smoking as the primary driver of the observed positive association between Prevotella and FEV₁. Specifically: (1) the three COPD studies with smoker meta-data showed consistent positive or neutral associations across a range of current smoking prevalence (0-34%)16,35,37, (2) the largest study consisting exclusively of current non-smokers35 demonstrated the strongest positive associations, and (3) only the asthma study showed a negative association38, suggesting disease-specific effects rather than smoking confounding.

Another possible confounder includes the microbiome sampling technique – which varied between studies and may have led to methodological variability and affected data comparability76. Indeed, included studies used diverse collection methods (induced sputum, spontaneous sputum, BAL, endobronchial brush) that sample different airway compartments with distinct microbial communities. This methodological heterogeneity may affect the comparability of microbiome-biomarker associations across studies. Future research should consider sampling-method-stratified analyses or standardized protocols to minimize this variability. In addition, some studies focused exclusively on a priori defined members of the lung microbiome for association analyses. This targeted approach may have disregarded other important bacteria potentially relevant to disease biomarkers. Considering these observations, we emphasize the need for greater harmonization in microbiome research to enhance methodological consistency and reliability of results across studies. Future efforts should also prioritize detailed reporting of potential confounders (incl. current smoking status, smoking history, medication exposure, sample type and collection method) and rigorously control for these confounders (incl. stratified analyses), to minimize bias and enhance the generalizability of findings across diverse populations and conditions77.

An important consideration is that several identified genera (Prevotella, Veillonella, Streptococcus, Rothia, Neisseria) are dominant oral commensals that may represent sample contamination, in particular for expectorated sputum samples. However, studies using protected bronchial brushings35 or bronchoalveolar lavage17,30 – methods that minimize contamination from the upper airways - demonstrated similar positive associations for multiple clinical outcomes with these genera, suggesting the relationships are likely not artifacts of oral contamination.

This systematic review has some inherent limitations. A first limitation is that the association between the load (absolute quantification) of non-pathogenic bacteria in the lung microbiome and clinical biomarkers has merited limited study thus far. While studies associating total bacterial density or abundance of specific pathogens (e.g., Haemophilus in COPD, Pseudomonas in bronchiectasis and CF) with clinical read-outs have been reported (refs. 78–82), quantitative data for non-pathogenic bacteria in chronic airway diseases are rare66. Hence, current knowledge captured in this review relies on relative abundance data, which gives insights into compositional shifts but cannot determine whether observed increases reflect true expansion in biomass or proportional changes due to pathogen overgrowth. Without absolute quantification (e.g., qPCR, ddPCR, CFU-based methods), it remains unclear whether non-pathogenic bacteria of the lung microbiome contribute directly to clinical biomarkers, or whether they function primarily as markers of microbiome imbalance. Secondly, subgroup meta-analysis was generally based on a limited number of studies, often with considerable differences in sample size. While we acknowledge the distinct inflammatory endotypes and clinical trajectories across asthma, COPD, CF, and bronchiectasis, our cross-disease approach was necessitated by the available evidence base. Hence, the meta-analysis combined data from studies investigating different CADs and/or involving participants with varying disease status, which may have introduced clinical heterogeneity and obscured disease-specific microbial associations. Indeed, the low number of studies within individual disease categories precluded comprehensive disease-specific meta-analyses and interaction testing. We explored disease-specific analyses where feasible, yet this was only possible for three genus-biomarker combinations. Of these, Prevotella showed statistical significance in the disease-specific COPD analysis, confirming a positive association with lung function. Notably, our sensitivity analysis for smoking confounding revealed that Prevotella showed opposite associations with lung function in asthma (negative) versus COPD (positive), providing preliminary evidence that disease-specific effects may exist. These findings underscore the need for future studies with sufficient sample sizes within each disease category to formally characterize disease-specific microbiome-biomarker relationships and test for between-disease interactions. In addition, our meta-analysis was limited to bacterial genera, thus neglecting the fact that distinct species within the same genus may have different effects on lung health33,39,54,60. A clear example of this is the Streptococcus genus, which includes both potentially beneficial species and recognized pathogens (i.e., Streptococcus pneumoniae)83. As more evidence becomes available, studies conducted at the species and strain level will be crucial to enhance our understanding of the (beneficial) properties of lung microbiome members66,84.

Another limitation is that several studies selectively reported raw effect measures, focusing only on statistically significant results and/or specific bacterial genera or species (e.g., those most strongly associated with clinical features). This may have led to an overestimation of effect sizes and prevented the analysis of potentially relevant (yet underreported) lung microbiome members. The omission of negative findings due to publication bias may have further contributed to the overestimation of some associations presented in this review. It is therefore essential to increase data availability in published articles and/or establish online repositories to ensure better comparison and synthesis of microbiome studies85.

Finally, this review primarily consists of cross-sectional studies (26/34), which preclude causal inference and the establishment of temporal relationships between bacterial abundance and clinical outcomes. The direction of causation cannot be determined: for instance, it is unclear whether Prevotella abundance improves lung function or whether better lung function creates conditions favourable for Prevotella, as reverse causation is equally plausible. More longitudinal clinical studies with repeated measurements and validation of findings in interventional experimental models are required to establish directionality and understand the precise function of non-pathogenic bacteria in CADs. It is expected that integrated omics approaches and mechanistic studies will provide insights into their causal contribution to disease outcomes19,86.

Despite limited study availability and substantial heterogeneity, our analysis identified potentially beneficial bacterial genera in the lung microbiome, which were significantly associated with lung function and/or lower airway inflammation across different CADs. While these findings do not provide evidence of causality, they represent a valuable foundation for future research aimed at elucidating the functional roles of individual members of the lung microbiome. A better understanding of the mechanism(s) that underlie their beneficial properties has the potential to drive the development of preventive and therapeutic interventions for CADs.
