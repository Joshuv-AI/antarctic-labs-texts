Graphene-based waveguide modulator for independent control of amplitude and phase
Authors: Mohammadreza Rahimi, Mina Noori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a highly efficient graphene-based electro-optical phase and amplitude modulator operating at 1550 nm, specifically designed for the TE fundamental mode. A phase difference of π is achieved at a minimum propagation length of 105 μm by applying 7.5 volts, with a Vπ·L coefficient of approximately 0.0787 V·cm. By leveraging the unique optical and electrical properties of graphene integrated within a waveguide structure, the device attains a very low insertion loss of only 0.016 dB over 105 μm, enabling effective phase modulation and amplitude control. Additionally, the shortest length at which a 3 dB extinction ratio is reached for amplitude modulation is 13 μm. Circuit model analysis indicates a modulation bandwidth of f3dB=6.5 GHz and a modulation depth of 99.6% (0.228 dB/µm) for a compact device with a 105 μm waveguide length. Dynamic performance is further validated through eye-diagram simulations at this length, which show an extinction ratio of 11.02 dB at a bit rate of 20 Gbits/s. This graphene-based electro-optic modulator offers a single structure for both phase and amplitude modulation, allowing separate control of these functions via bias voltage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47013-8.

Introduction

The growing demand for high-throughput optical communication has made electro-optic modulators essential components, enabling efficient information encoding onto optical carriers1. Beyond telecommunications, their uses include advanced sensing and integrated photonic circuits, attracting notable research interest from both academia and industry. While silicon photonics has been central to optical device development2, its inherent limitations in achieving optimal optical performance have prompted exploration of alternative material integrations. Notably, incorporating graphene, known for its exceptional properties, has become a promising strategy to address these challenges, providing improvements in bandwidth (BW), modulation speed, and device miniaturization3–5. Graphene’s unique two-dimensional structure and gapless electronic band facilitate broadband optical modulation. Its tunable chemical potential allows precise control over both the amplitude and phase of the optical signal. Additionally, the material’s extraordinary carrier mobility, reaching up to 100,000 cm²V⁻¹s⁻¹ in high-quality samples, makes it an attractive candidate for high-frequency silicon-integrated photonic systems operating in the multi-gigahertz range6–8. The integration of graphene with hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) is indeed of significant importance in the design of advanced graphene-based optical and optoelectronic devices. This substrate is characterized by an atomically smooth surface morphology, which is notably free from dangling bonds and charge-trapping centers. Furthermore, its lattice constant closely approximates that of graphite, and it exhibits a significant optical phonon energy in conjunction with a wide band gap. h-BN demonstrates potential fluctuations that are one to two orders of magnitude lower than those observed in SiO2, primarily due to charged impurities. Consequently, h-BN is firmly established as an optimal platform (or suitable substrate) for advanced graphene-based electronic devices9.

Early investigations into graphene-based waveguide electro-absorption modulators, exemplified by pioneering work in 2011, demonstrated performance metrics including 0.1 dB/µm of modulation depth, a modulation speed of 1.2 GHz, and a compact footprint of 25 μm²3. Subsequent studies have focused on optimizing device performance through structural modifications, such as increasing the number of graphene layers to enhance light-graphene interaction and incorporating dielectric materials like Al₂O₃10,11, SiO₂12, hBN13,14, LiNbO₃15, and SiN16 to form capacitor-like structures for efficient chemical potential modulation17,18. Despite these advancements, realizing the full potential of graphene modulators for next-generation communication systems requires addressing critical challenges, including minimizing insertion losses, reducing energy consumption, and achieving wider operational bandwidths19.

In 2011, Liu et al. proposed an electro-optic modulator based on a graphene waveguide. This initial design incorporated a single graphene layer, operated in the wavelength range of 1.35 μm to 1.6 μm, and possessed a 3 dB bandwidth of approximately 1 GHz with a modulation depth of 0.1 dB/µm. They subsequently improved the structure by adding two graphene layers, increasing the modulation depth to 0.16 dB/µm3,10. In 2017, Peng et al. presented a modulator achieving a modulation efficiency of up to 1.23 dB/µm and a relatively low insertion loss of 0.32 dB/µm20. Sorianello et al. proposed a phase modulator operating at 1550 nm, featuring a modulation efficiency (Vπ.L) of 280 V·µm, an insertion loss of 0.0236 dB/µm, and an extinction ratio of 35 dB over a 300 μm propagation length. They experimentally determined the structure’s bandwidth using an electrical vector network analyzer, obtaining a value of 5 GHz. Their design was a Mach-Zehnder amplitude modulator incorporating two phase modulators in its arms5. While coherent modulators, typically configured as nested Mach-Zehnder interferometers, offer simultaneous amplitude and phase control6,21,22, simpler waveguide or micro-ring designs23 primarily focus on modulating either amplitude24 or phase25.

This study presents a graphene-based electro-optical modulator capable of selective amplitude or phase modulation. The modulator’s low insertion loss enables its operation as a dedicated phase modulator within a specific bias voltage range, in addition to its conventional amplitude modulation functionality.

The paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 details the geometric dimensions of the proposed structure and the material properties of its components. It then examines the optical field profile and the electrically induced changes in the chemical potential of the integrated graphene. The subsequent discussion focuses on the real and imaginary components of the effective refractive index (neff) affected by the bias voltage. Sections 3 and 4 propose a phase and amplitude modulator, respectively. Section 5 presents the modulator’s circuit model, and the device bandwidth is derived according to the internal resistances and capacitances. For the amplitude modulator, the circuit’s time response to a 0.15 ns square pulse is simulated, and the eye diagrams are generated at 10, 15, and 20 Gbits/s data rates.

The proposed modulator’s structure

Figure 1(a) and (b) show the 2D cross-section and 3D schematic view of the proposed modulator, respectively. The proposed graphene-based electro-optic modulator operates at λ = 1550 nm, and it is a slab waveguide on an SiO₂ substrate with a refractive index of (RI) of 1.45. The waveguide core comprises silicon with an RI of 3.47, and a graphene sheet is embedded at the core’s midplane. Modeled as a two-dimensional material, the graphene’s surface conductivity follows the Random Phase Approximation (RPA) formalism26(see Appendix A). A 5 nm thick hexagonal boron nitride layer (hBN, n = 1.98) encapsulates the graphene layer, providing electrical isolation from the silicon waveguide while establishing capacitive coupling. The height and width of the core are 120 nm and 540 nm, respectively. Gold electrodes are placed on both sides of the structure to apply gate voltage to the graphene layers. The gold electrodes are distant from the core region (600 nm away from the waveguide core) in order not to affect the core’s mode profile. The graphene sheet on the left side establishes the connection between the electrode and the silicon core. Applying an electrical bias to the electrodes establishes a capacitor between the graphene sheet and the silicon waveguide. Charge accumulation induced by the bias voltage modulates the graphene’s chemical potential (µc), thereby altering the effective refractive index of the propagating mode28. Figures 3(a) and (b) illustrate the variations in graphene’s chemical potential and TE-mode effective refractive index as a function of gate voltage. Changes in µc concurrently modify both the real (Re(neff)) and imaginary (Im(neff)) components of the effective refractive index.

Figure 3(b) indicates that for bias voltages exceeding 5.5 V, the imaginary component of the effective refractive index reaches its minimum value (approaching zero), corresponding to the lowest optical loss. This regime provides optimal conditions for phase modulation, as the transmitted light’s phase exhibits a relatively linear dependence on driving voltage (Fig. 3b). Conversely, within the 2.5–5.5 V bias range, the imaginary component of the effective refractive index and the associated loss vary sharply, satisfying the essential requirement for amplitude modulation. Thus, the proposed structure functions as both a phase and amplitude modulator within these distinct bias ranges.

The design procedure of the amplitude modulator

To design an amplitude modulator according to Fig. 3(b), the bias voltage range exhibiting an abrupt loss transition is selected. Within the 2.5–5.5 V driving voltage range, corresponding to the “off” and “on” states, respectively, the normalized output difference (NOD)27, extinction ratio (ER)29, and insertion loss (IL)30 are evaluated as functions of device length. Figures 5(a), (b), and (c) illustrate the aforementioned parameters for both voltage states. Analysis reveals that a minimum device length of 13 μm is essential to achieve a 50% optical power difference between the “off” and “on” states, enabling switching functionality. At this length (dashed line in Fig. 5), the extinction ratio reaches 3 dB with an insertion loss of 0.002 dB. For a 105 μm structure length, the extinction ratio and insertion loss equal 24.19 dB and 0.016 dB, respectively.

Bandwidth analysis

Analyzing the time response and bandwidth of the proposed modulator requires its equivalent circuit model. Figure 7 presents the equivalent circuit model for the proposed modulator, incorporating the following components with assumed values: source resistance (Rsource=50 Ω)26, substrate-induced pad capacitance (Cpad=1.2 fF), electrode-graphene interface capacitance (Ccontact=0.12 pF)31, graphene-electrode interface resistivity (ρcontact = 300 Ω·µm)16, and silicon waveguide resistivity (ρSi = 400 Ω·µm)32.

Fig. 7The equivalent circuit model of the proposed modulator structure.

The equivalent circuit model of the proposed modulator structure.

Given that the graphene contact resistance and Si waveguide resistance depend on the structure’s length, their values have been calculated for different lengths (Appendix C). In this study, graphene is modeled as a two-dimensional material; thus, its surface conductivity and resistivity have been determined according to Appendix A. The graphene sheet exhibits maximum DC resistivity (~ 597 Ω/sq) near zero bias voltage, corresponding to the Dirac point (Supplementary File, Fig. S1). Positioned within the waveguide core, the graphene sheet is conformally coated with a 5 nm-thick hBN dielectric layer, forming the core capacitance (Ccore). Beyond dielectric capacitances, the intrinsic quantum capacitance (Cq) of graphene is critical. This capacitance reaches its minimum near the Dirac point (~ 1.05 µF/cm², Appendix B); its length-dependent values are provided in the Supplementary File (Appendix C).

Using the equivalent circuit model parameters, the circuit model’s bandwidth has been simulated (Fig. 7). The results for the sampled structure lengths are presented in Fig. 8.

Conclusion

In this study, an electro-optical graphene-based phase and amplitude modulator with independent control is proposed. The determining performance parameters of extension ratio, insertion loss, bandwidth, and eye diagram are investigated. The circuit model of the structure is presented, and the internal capacitors and resistors of the structure are calculated. The proposed structure shows a bandwidth of 6.5 GHz, a modulation depth of 99.6% (0.228dB/µm), an insertion loss of 0.016 dB, and VπL ≈ 0.0787 V.cm over a structure length of 105 μm. The extension ratio at this length is obtained as 11.2 dB for an input pulse with a bit rate of 20 Gbits/s. This modulator is capable of operating either as an amplitude or phase modulator and serves as a foundational step toward the development of waveguide-based coherent modulators for photonic applications.
