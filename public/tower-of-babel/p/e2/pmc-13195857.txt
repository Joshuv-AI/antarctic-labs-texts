Alexithymia and self-harm in depressed adolescents: the mediating role of resting-state functional connectivity of the ventral hippocampal-fusiform gyrus
Authors: Ying Ma, Yongjie Zhou, Huihang Fu, Xiaoyi Lin, Mengxin Huang, Yi Zhang, Yahan Li, Yu Liu, Weiju Zhou, Ruoling Chen, Yaojing Chen, Jieling Wu, Jie Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Alexithymia is implicated in self-harm behaviors, including nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI), suicide attempt (SA). However,, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying this association remain poorly understood, especially among clinically referred adolescents with depressive symptoms.MethodsA total of 129 adolescents with depressive symptoms were recruited from Shenzhen Mental Health Center. NSSI, SA, alexithymia and its dimensions were measured using validated scales. Resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) was measured using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) within a predefined set of emotion regulation (ER)-related regions of interest (ROIs).ResultsOf the analytic sample, 37 adolescents met the criteria for NSSI and 59 met the criteria for SA. The externally oriented thinking (EOT) subscale scores were positively associated with SA (adjusted OR = 1.21, 95% CI, 1.03–1.43). Adolescents with SA exhibited heightened connectivity within ER regions compared to controls, including between the left orbitofrontal cortex and right medial orbital superior frontal gyrus (z score = 0.19, P < 0.001), left medial orbitofrontal cortex and left inferior occipital gyrus (z score = 0.16; P < 0.001), left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and right medial orbital of the superior frontal gyrus (z score = 0.17; P < 0.001), right parahippocampal gyrus and right fusiform gyrus (z score = 0.17; P < 0.001), and between the right ventral hippocampus (vHC) and the right fusiform gyrus (FG) (z = 0.17; P < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed that the association between EOT and SA was fully mediated by right vHC-right FG connectivity (indirect effect β = 0.096, 95% CI, 0.027–0.190). No significant associations between alexithymia or rs-FC within ER regions and NSSI were found.ConclusionsIn clinically referred adolescents with depressive symptoms, EOT-related alexithymia is associated with SA, and this association appears to be fully mediated by enhanced vHC-FG connectivity. Background

Self-harm behaviors, including nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) and suicide attempts (SA), represent a persistent and critical public health concern [1]. Suicide remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, with over 720,000 deaths attributed to it annually [2]. Notably, NSSI has garnered increasing attention due to its robust predictive value for future suicidal behaviors, often surpassing even a prior history of SA [3]. Understanding the risk factors associated with SA and NSSI is essential for developing effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Self-harm is a complex behavior often driven by emotional dysregulation, and its etiology is frequently examined through the lens of emotion regulation (ER) frameworks [4]. Population-based and clinical studies have consistently identified stable personality traits related to emotion, such as emotional reactivity and emotional expression ability to express emotions, as significant correlates of long-term self-harm risk [5, 6]. Alexithymia, a personality construct characterized by difficulties in identifying, describing, and differentiating emotions [7], has emerged as a potential risk factor for NSSI and SA. While several studies have investigated the association of alexithymia with NSSI and SA [8–10], the findings have been inconsistent, largely due to variations in the definition and operationalization of self-harm, and the failure to differentiate between these distinct behaviors [7]. Furthermore, there is a notable lack of research examining the association of alexithymia with self-harm behaviors across diverse clinical populations, where comprehensive assessments of both alexithymia and self-harm behaviors are feasible [11, 12]. To date, no study has simultaneously explored the associations of alexithymia with NSSI and SA among clinical referred adolescents with depressive symptoms, a population at heightened risk for self-harm behaviors. In addition, much of this existing literature has treated alexithymia as a categorical construct [13], despite its inherently dimensional nature. This methodological limitation complicates efforts to fully elucidate the association of alexithymia with NSSI and SA.

Prior neurobiological research has highlighted the role of emotional processing deficits in alexithymia, as evidenced by differential activation in brain regions associated with ER, such as the amygdala, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), insula, hippocampus, and angular gyrus [14–19], which are key components of the ER network. These regions are also implicated in self-harm behaviors [17, 18], suggesting potential shared neurobiological pathways. However, to our knowledge, no study has examined whether functional connectivity within the core ER-related regions mediates the association between alexithymia and self-harm behaviors.

To bridge these research gaps, we focused on the associations of alexithymia and resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) with self-harm behaviors among adolescents with depressive symptoms. We hypothesized that alexithymia or its dimensions would be associated with NSSI and/or SA, and that rs-FC within ER regions would mediate these associations. Guided by this framework, the study was designed to (1) confirm the behavioral associations, (2) delineate the aberrant rs-FC profiles, and (3) examine the mediating role of such connectivity patterns. Through this approach, we aim to advance a mechanistic understanding of how alexithymia and its neurobiological underpinnings influence self-harm behaviors, yielding clinically actionable insights for risk stratification and intervention.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study recruited adolescents aged 12 − 18 years seeking health care at Shenzhen Mental Health Center (Shenzhen Kangning Hospital) between March 1, 2021, and October 30, 2023. Of 162 initially recruited participants, 33 were excluded based on predefined criteria: (1) absence of depressive symptoms regardless of psychiatric diagnoses (n = 11); (2) major medical conditions, substance use disorders, or pervasive developmental disorders (n = 7); (3) left-handedness or IQ < 80 (n = 4); (4) MRI contraindications (n = 4); (5) excessive head motion during imaging (mean framewise displacement (FD) > 0.2 mm, translation ≥ 2 mm, or rotation ≥ 2°; n = 6); and (6) a singular occurrence of suicidal ideation (SI) precluding group comparisons (n = 1). The final analytic sample comprised 129 adolescents with depressive symptoms. The study protocol adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Shenzhen Mental Health Center and Guangzhou Medical University (NO. 2021010002). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [20].

Clinical and behavioral assessments

Study participants were interviewed by trained psychiatrists using the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia–Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL) [3], a semi-structured diagnostic interview, to collect demographic and clinical information and the interviews were performed on the same day as neuroimaging.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9-items Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 36. In accordance with standard clinical guidelines, a total score of 5 or greater was used as the threshold to indicate the presence of depressive symptoms [21].

Lifetime self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs), including NSSI, SI, and SA, were evaluated using the K-SADS-PL suicide screen [22]. NSSI was defined as engaging in self-injurious behavior more than 5 times and/or engaged in the behavior with significant injury to self, which was consistent with the DSM-5 criteria for NSSI [23]. SI was defined as having recurrent thoughts of suicide, but without any self-injurious act [24]. SA was operationally defined as self-injurious behavior with any suicidal intent or suicidal thoughts [22]. Due to insufficient sample size (n = 1), participants with SI alone were excluded, and were not merged with other SITB groups, as it is considered a clinically and conceptually distinct construct from NSSI and SA [25]. The remaining participants were divided into three groups: NSSI group, SA group, and control group (no lifetime NSSI or SA).

The Chinese version of the 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20) was used to measure the total and three dimensions of alexithymia: difficulty identifying feelings (DIF), difficulty describing feelings (DDF), and externally oriented thinking (EOT) [6]. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree; 2 = disagree; 3 = neutral; 4 = agree; 5 = completely agree), with higher scores indicating greater alexithymia. The Chinese version of TAS-20 and its dimensions has been demonstrated to have good reliability and validity in Chinese adolescents [26]. In the current study, the Cronbach α coefficient of the scale and its dimensions were 0.84, 0.78, 0.66, and 0.73, respectively.

Neuroimaging acquisition and analysis

Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data were acquired using a 3 T scanner (see Supplement eMethod for acquisition parameters). Preprocessing and seed-based functional connectivity (FC) analysis were conducted in SPM 12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and DPABI (version 3.0, http://rfmri.org/dpabi) [27]. We selected a whole-brain seed-to-voxel analysis approach to test a priori hypotheses involving specific neural networks, and to facilitate comparisons with the existing rs-fMRI literature on self-harm behaviors among adolescents [28–30]. We generated whole-brain seed-to-voxel correlation maps by extracting the residual blood oxygen level–dependent (BOLD) signal time course from a priori regions of interest (ROIs), which focused on ER-related regions. In total, 19 ROIs within ER-related regions were selected for each participant based on prior neuroimaging evidence implicating these areas in emotion regulation (coordinates in Supplement Table S1), including bilateral amygdala (Amy), angular gyrus (AG), subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC), parahippocampal gyrus (PHG), ventral hippocampus (vHC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) [28–30]. Seed regions were 8 mm in diameter and created based on peak coordinates from the literature [28–30]. We calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between the time course of each seed and the time course of all other voxels in the brain. Correlation coefficients were converted to z-scores using Fisher transformation. At the individual level, seed-to-voxel connectivity maps were generated within the general linear model (GLM) framework. At the group level, these maps were analyzed using a second-level GLM configured as a one-way ANOVA. This model tested the main effect of group (NSSI vs. SA vs. controls), with voxel-wise F-statistics computed to identify significant connectivity differences across the three groups. White matter, cerebrospinal fluid and whole-brain gray matter signal, age at scan, sex, residence, annual family income, clinical diagnosis, and motion parameters were demeaned and included as covariates in the analyses comparing group differences. For all connectivity analyses, the significance threshold correction was conducted according to Gaussian random field (GRF) theory with a voxel level of P < 0.001 and a cluster level of P < 0.05 [31]. Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied (P < 0.01) for pairwise post-hoc tests. The mean z values were extracted when statistically significant group differences were observed in rs-fMRI FC.

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was performed from January 1, 2024, to March 15, 2025. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics, version 25.0 (IBM Corp), and significance level was set at two-sided P < 0.05 for demographic, clinical and behavioral data. For comparing TAS scores (Total, DIF, DDF, and EOT) across 3 groups, a one-way Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) was employed, and post hoc Tukey tests were utilized for pairwise group comparisons. Other continuous variables were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Chi-square tests were used to examine group differences for categorical variables, and Fisher’s exact probability test was used to test the group difference when the smallest expected number was less than 5. Binomial logistic regression model was employed to examine the association between scores of TAS or its dimensions with NSSI or SA among the study participants, adjusting for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), annual family income, educational level, residence area, smoking status and alcohol consumption. We used Pearson correlations to examine the association between rs-FC involved in ER regions and TAS-EOT score within each self-harm behavior group (NSSI group or SA group), age, sex, BMI, annual family income, educational level, residence area, smoking status, alcohol consumption, clinical diagnosis, and motion parameters were adjusted as covariates. We then employed a structural equation model (SEM) to estimate mediation effects of rs-FCs within ER regions in the association between EOT score and SA. Given that only one connectivity in ER regions was significantly correlated with TAS-EOT scores, we focused our mediation analysis on a single pathway. In SEM, we adjusted for the covariates as we did in the correlations analysis. The SEM analyses were performed using Mplus 8.0.

Overall, 37 participants (28.6%) met the criteria for NSSI and 59 participants (45.8%) met the criteria for SA. The 3 groups did not differ in age at the time of the scan, sex, educational level, residence, annual family income, BMI, smoking status, or alcohol consumption. Between-group difference in alexithymia

Scores on the TAS and its dimensions are summarized in Table 1. The MANOVA indicated a significant overall difference in the average scores of the TAS (Total, DIF, DDF, and EOT) among NSSI group, the SA group and the control group (Pillai’s Trace = 0.100, p = 0.045), subsequent post-hoc Tukey tests for pairwise comparisons revealed a significant difference (t = −2.133, p = 0.035) in EOT scores between SA group and control group. No other significant associations of TAS and its dimensions with NSSI and SA were found.

Mediation analysis

After controlling for those potential confounders, there were significant total and indirect effects (β = 0.236, 95CI, 0.025 – 0.418) and indirect effect (β = 0.096, 95% CI, 0.027 – 0.190) of TAS-EOT on SA. Discussion

Adolescence represents a critical period of neurodevelopment characterized by heightened experience-dependent plasticity within the ER-related network, which may influence vulnerability to neuropsychological problems [32]. While previous research has primarily focused on the psychosocial relationships between alexithymia and self-harm behaviors [8–10], this study is the first to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying this association among clinical adolescents with depressive symptoms, particularly within the ER regions. We compared TAS scores and rs-FC within ER regions across the NSSI, SA, and control groups, which allowed us to explore the potential mediating role of rs-FC in the association between alexithymia and self-harm behaviors.

Our findings revealed a positive association between TAS-EOT and SA. Neuroimaging suggested that adolescents with SA exhibited distinct rs-FC within ER regions, including increased connectivity between the left OFC and right SFGmed, left mOFC and left IOG, left dlPFC and right ORBsupmed, right PHG and right FG, and between the right vHC and right FG. Notably, right vHC-right FG connectivity mediated the association between TAS-EOT and SA, highlighting a potential neural pathway linking alexithymia to SA. These insights into the brain circuitry involved in this association may inform more effective prevention and intervention strategies for SA among clinical adolescents with depressive symptoms.

Association between alexithymia and self-harm behaviors

While several studies have examined the association between alexithymia and self-harm behaviors [8–10], this is the first to investigate its link with NSSI and SA simultaneously. Contrary to prior research [7], we found that alexithymia was associated with SA but not with NSSI. This discrepancy may stem from differences in study design, sample characteristics, and the definitions and assessments of NSSI and SA. By including both NSSI and SA in the same sample, our study provides a more accurate and comparable framework for evaluating the sensitivity of these behaviors related to alexithymia.

We found a specific association between the EOT (but not DIF or DDF) facet and SA, which was consistent with prior literature. For instance, Ghorbani et al. similarly found that EOT, but not DIF or DDF, was significantly associated with SA in a clinical population [33]. This pattern is further supported by a study linking EOT to altered neural reactivity to emotional stimuli in adolescents who self-harm [14]. This suggests that EOT, a cognitive style of avoiding internal emotional states, may confer risk for SA through its influence on the neurocognitive processing of emotional information, potentially impairing adaptive emotional regulation.

Between-group differences in rs-FC involving in ER regions

Our results align with prior studies showing elevated rs-FC in the OFC among the SA population [34–36], with a novel focus on adolescents. The OFC, particularly the mOFC, is critically involved in motivation, affect interpretation, and emotion regulation [37, 38]. Enhanced connectivity between the OFC and SFGmed, a region implicated in self-referential processing and cognitive control [39], or the IOG, which is crucial for face processing [40], may signify emotion dysregulation in SA adolescents. Specifically, hyperconnectivity between mOFC and IOG could indicate an over-reliance on external visual cues, such as facial cues processed by the IOG, to inform emotionally ambiguous decisions mediated by mOFC, mirroring EOT’s cognitive style of emotion avoidance [41]. Similarly, increased connectivity between the dlPFC and ORBsupmed, both regions integral to top-down regulation and executive function [28, 42], suggests cognitive inefficiency in SA adolescents.

The observed functional connectivity between hippocampal/parahippocampal (vHC/PHG) and fusiform gyrus (FG) represents a novel finding, highlighting a critical neural circuit involved in both facial emotion processing and cognitive functions such as language comprehension and semantic integration [28, 43, 44]. Aberrant connectivity within vHC/PHG has been well-documented across several neuropsychiatric conditions, such as major depressive disorder (MDD) [45], generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) [46], and aphasia [47], suggesting its role in emotional and cognitive deficits. Our findings implicate the vHC/PHG-FG connectivity as a core neural substrate underpinning the integration of facial and linguistic cognition, with its dysregulation potentially contributing to emotion dysregulation in SA adolescents.

The progressive increase in connectivity in ER regions from control to NSSI and SA groups may represent a neuro-functional pattern associated with the severity of self-harm behaviors. This pattern could suggest increased neural recruitment in the SA group, potentially reflecting a compensatory process to achieve task performance similar to that of other groups. Notably, we did not observe significant rs-FC differences within ER regions between NSSI and control groups, which contrasts with prior research [18]. This discrepancy may stem from variations in participants’ characteristics and NSSI assessment tools across studies. Given the functional differences between NSSI (transient emotional relief and even an anti-suicide function in acute crises) [48] and SA (intent to die), abnormal connectivity within ER regions may serve as a neural marker associated with suicidal behavior and an early warning for NSSI escalation.

Mediating effect of vHC-right FG connectivity on the association between TAS-EOT and SA

Our study identified a mediating effect of right vHC-right FG connectivity on the association between TAS-EOT and SA, which was aligned with the facial feedback model [49], positing that impaired integration of facial emotional signals (via FG) with contextual memory (via vHC/parahippocampal gyrus) may perpetuate emotional detachment, a precursor to SA in adolescents with EOT. Crucially, this pattern was absent in the NSSI groups, suggesting SA represents a distinct neurophenotype characterized by heightened connectivity in emotion-cognition integration networks. This distinction echoes Joiner’s interpersonal theory [50], framing SA as a “cry of pain” requiring heightened cognitive effort to override self-preservation instincts which drives individuals to “end the pain,” contrasting with NSSI’s emotion-regulation or anti-suicide function to “regulate or escape pain” [51]. Our findings also align with previous research linking alexithymia to dysfunction in cognitive-emotional regions such as the dlPFC, anterior cingulate cortex, and OFC [14, 15].

The EOT subscale, which reflects a cognitive focus on external reality rather than internal emotions, is associated with reduced interoceptive awareness and impaired emotion processing [14]. The observed association between EOT and vHC-FG connectivity underscores the role of facial cognitive and emotion processing inefficiency in SA vulnerability. Monitoring alexithymia, particularly the EOT subscale, and targeting the ER-related regions in interventions may be a promising strategy for preventing and treating SA in adolescents with depressive symptoms. Addressing the inefficiency in cognitive–emotion regulation networks could mitigate the risk of suicidal behaviors. Future longitudinal studies are needed to elucidate the causal relationships between alexithymia, ER network dysfunction, and SA.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to explore the neuroimaging mechanisms linking alexithymia and its dimensions to NSSI and SA among clinical adolescents with depressive symptoms. However, certain limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any definitive causal inferences, which necessitate future replication in longitudinal cohorts. Second, the TAS-20, as a self-report measure, may introduce inaccuracies in assessing emotional experiences. Nonetheless, prior meta-analyses support its validity [52]. Third, the TAS-EOT subscale exhibited suboptimal reliability, which may attenuate the association and limit validity. However, the reliability of EOT in the present study was consistent with previous findings in adolescent samples [53, 54]. Future studies should consider alternative measures to enhance reliability. Fourth, the ROIs within ER regions were selected based on prior studies, potentially overlooking other relevant regions. Fifth, the heterogeneous clinical status of participants (e.g., depression, bipolar disorder) limited subgroup analyses. Larger sample sizes are needed to explore these differences in future studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the EOT dimension of alexithymia is positively associated with SA, with the association fully mediated by aberrant functional connectivity between the right vHC and right FG within ER networks. These findings delineate a distinct neurobiological pathway linking alexithymia, impaired cognitive ER, and SA, identifying the vHC-FG circuit as a potential novel target for intervention in SA. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm this mechanistic pathway and elucidate its causal role in the prevention of SA among adolescents with depressive symptoms.
