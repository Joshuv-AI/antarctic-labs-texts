Pioneering ultrastructural texture analysis of dendrites in the cerebellar dentate nucleus: an experimental rat model of hyperthermia-induced seizures
Authors: Joanna Maria Łotowska, Marta Borowska, Milena Żochowska-Sobaniec, Maria Elżbieta Sobaniec-Łotowska, Piotr Sobaniec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study objective was descriptive, qualitative and quantitative texture analysis of ultrastructural research of the dendritic domain, especially dendrites, within the cerebellar dentate nucleus (DN) in an experimental model of hyperthermia-induced seizures (HS) developed at our Center, conducted on young Wistar rats. Specimens (1 mm³) collected from the DN in both experimental and control groups, and fixed via transcardial perfusion with paraformaldehyde and glutaraldehyde solutions, were routinely processed for transmission electron microscopy. Ultrastructural studies, including morphometric assessment of dendrites across the DN in the experimental model of hyperthermia-induced seizures (HS), revealed distinctive and easily recognizable morphological signs of aponecrosis, manifested as “dark” dendroplasm degeneration (“dark dendrites”), resulting in significant disturbances in the cerebro-cerebellar function in animals subjected to hyperthermic stress. The aponecrotic lesions affecting the dendritic tree were characterized by shrunken, dark, nearly black, homogeneous dendroplasm exhibiting intense degenerative changes up to and including necrosis, which fully correlated with the quantitative analysis. Lethally damaged large dendrites displayed clear signs of cytoskeletal disintegration within the dendrites, particularly affecting microtubules, which are critical structures involved in numerous cellular processes, as well as cytoplasmic organelles. Our current and previous pioneering qualitative and quantitative ultrastructural investigations into the cellular mechanisms underlying the experimental model of HS indicate that damage to the somatodendritic domain of DN, including dendritic arborization and neuronal somata, characterized by aponecrosis, may play a major role in the morphogenesis of heat stress-induced convulsions.

Introduction

The pediatric neurology literature reports that fever-associated seizures, also known as febrile seizures (FS) or hyperthermia-induced seizures (HS), represent the most common neurological disorder affecting 2% to 12% of children. These seizures result from a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors1–3. It is generally accepted that FS typically occur in children aged between six months and five years and are not associated with the CNS infections, metabolic disorders, or a prior history of epilepsy1,3–10.

Importantly, as numerous authors—including those from our center—have long noted, despite simple febrile seizures generally being benign, children experiencing non-simple (i.e., complex or febrile status epilepticus) febrile seizures require closer clinical monitoring than the vast majority of children with simple febrile seizures. This is due to their increased risk of subsequent epilepsy later in life, including, for example, drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) associated with mesial temporal sclerosis and hippocampal sclerosis3,11–18. The literature emphasizes that TLE and mesial temporal sclerosis often develop following early life febrile seizures, particularly after febrile status epilepticus11,15,17–19.

Unfortunately, to date, the cellular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of fever-associated seizures remain poorly known.

This study builds upon our previous morphological investigations conducted at the ultrastructural level, which aimed to explore the experimental model of hyperthermia-induced convulsions and their impact on various regions of the central nervous system (CNS). Prior research has focused on injuries to the hippocampal cortex20–22 and the neocortex of the temporal lobe23,24. Recently, however, the cerebellar dentate nucleus (DN) in rats has been examined25, which could potentially serve as the most comparable or significant reference point for the neuropathological pattern observed in children with febrile seizures.

In recent pioneering electron-microscopic qualitative and quantitative studies specifically focusing on the DN, we observed exciting changes, namely significant damage to large neuronal perikarya in this region of the cerebellum, which was accompanied by pronounced destruction of the dendritic tree. The most prominent neuronal lesions exhibited specific morphological signs of aponecrosis, characterized by the phenomenon known as dark cell degeneration (also referred to as “dark neurons”). Near these “dark” neurons, we frequently observed the presence of shrunken, dark, and thinner distal dendritic processes showing substantial degeneration of the dendroplasm. It is worth mentioning that the phenomenon of “dark” (compact) neurons is broader than just “cell degeneration” and is not always associated with cell death. This relates to certain properties of dark neurons that, after 2 days, have recovered normal structure in the rat cerebral cortex, produced by hypoglycemic seizures26. Furthermore, some “dark” neurons are known to appear as artifacts of sample preparation or as the result of physiological processes in the brain.

Interestingly, in contrast to the cerebellar cortex, which has become an exceptionally well-studied region of the CNS over the last two decades, with a relatively comprehensive understanding of its structure, morphology, and function, the deep cerebellar nuclei—including the dentate nucleus - particularly their dendritic domain, have not yet received adequate exploratory attention or equivalent investigative focus.

An exception is represented by the pioneering work of Chan-Palay, published in 197727, along with a few studies by other authors, including our recent aforementioned research into DN morphology that takes into account the dendritic domain25,28–33. Importantly, dendrites are extensions of the neuronal perikarya that have specialized for receiving and processing the vast majority of excitatory synaptic inputs. Therefore, the dendritic tree constitutes the essential cellular substrate for receiving and integrating neuronal information34.

It is also worth noting that dendritic trees come in many different shapes and sizes; therefore, based on these variations, it is challenging to classify the types of neurons to which they belong.

It is widely accepted that neurons are independent entities. Numerous neuronal dendrites receive electrical impulses from the axons of other neurons and conduct this activity to the neuron’s axon for transmission to other cells. This fundamental principle is often referred to as the neuron doctrine34. Thus, dendrites are the site where structural features interact with informational signals within the neuron35.

Considering, on one hand, the crucial role that dendrites play in neuronal processing, and on the other hand, the apparent lack of comprehensive research in the neuropathological literature regarding the morphology of dendritic structures in febrile seizures, particularly at the transmission electron-microscopic (TEM) level, our current study aimed to conduct descriptive qualitative ultrastructural research of the dendritic domain, especially the dendrites, of the DN within an experimental model of hyperthermia-induced convulsions in young rats.

Furthermore, we aimed to extend our research by incorporating submicroscopic computational parameters based on texture analysis to objectify the obtained morphological ultrastructural qualitative observations.

We would like to note that in recent years, texture analysis has gained considerable importance in the morphological studies of biological tissues at the microscopic level36–39. Several tissues and conditions were characterized using texture analysis37.

Wide-field second-harmonic generation (SHG) microscopy, combined with texture analysis and unsupervised machine learning, has been employed to identify collagen structures associated with the progression of papillary thyroid carcinoma40. Texture analysis has also proven helpful in identifying phase separation in phosphate-buffered saline and the birefringence ambiguity of thermotropic liquid crystals41. Images of brain tissue samples immunostained for β-amyloid (Aβ), used in Alzheimer’s disease diagnostics, have demonstrated the ability to differentiate various types of amyloid plaques based on their morphology, potentially serving as a valuable tool in neuropathological dementia research42. High sensitivity of these methods in detecting subtle changes in the granule cell layers following sensorimotor cortex ablation (SCA) on the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus has been demonstrated43. These studies highlight the vast potential of texture analysis methods for identifying various morphological observations.

We would also like to draw particular attention to a comprehensive, detailed, and uniquely valuable contribution in the neuroanatomic literature on the intracellular structure of dendrites by John C. Fiala and Kristen M. Harris34. Their work served as an inspiration for expanding our previous electron-microscopic investigations into the injured large neurons in hyperthermia-induced seizures within the dentate nucleus, with the assessment of the dendritic profile of this cerebellar region in the same experimental model.

Materials and methods

Animals

The retrospective electron-microscopic analysis of the dendrites in the cerebellar dentate nucleus of young rats, conducted in the Department of Medical Pathomorphology at the Medical University of Białystok, focused on both the experimental model of hyperthermia-induced seizures (HS) and the control group. The study involved 12 male Wistar rats, aged 22–30 days, average weight 88.5 +/- 9.3 g, divided into two groups: seven in the experimental group and five in the control group. The animals were pre-selected based on standard pharmacological screening tests. The male Wistar rats used in this study were sourced from the Department of Pharmacology’s own laboratory animal breeding programme at our University. All procedures adhered strictly to the guidelines for the care, protection and use of laboratory animals for scientific purposes. The study received approval from the Local Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments at the Medical University of Białystok.

Model of febrile seizures

The HS group consisted of rats with induced febrile seizures. Hyperthermic stress was evoked by placing the animals in a warm water bath maintained at 45 °C for 4 min over four consecutive days. The water temperature was maintained at a constant level throughout the procedure. Control animals and the HS group received only normal saline.

Most rats subjected to warm water hyperthermia developed rapid myoclonic jerks, followed by generalized seizures characterized by vigorous shaking of the head, ears, and limbs, as well as particularly intense tail tremors.

A detailed description of the methodology concerning the experimental model of hyperthermic seizures developed in our center has been provided in our previous publication44.

Preparation for transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Seventy-two hours after the last convulsion episode (the 72 h period was chosen to avoid recovering “dark” compacted neurons), the rats were deep anesthetized with Nembutal (25 mg/kg body weight, i.p.). Subsequently, they were subjected to intravital, transcardial perfusion with a fixative solution containing 2% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) and 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Serva) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (Serva), pH 7.4, maintained at 4 °C, under a pressure of 80–100 mmHg.

Following perfusion, the brains were carefully removed, and the cerebellar hemispheres were dissected out and sectioned. Small tissue blocks (~ 1 mm³) containing the structure of the dentate nucleus were collected and fixed in the same fixative solution for 24 h. Post-fixation was performed with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4, Serva) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4, for 1 h.

After dehydration in ethanol and propylene oxide (f.Serva), the tissue blocks were routinely processed for embedding in Epon 812 (f.Serva) and serially sectioned on a Reichert ultramicrotome (Reichert Ultracut S) to obtain semithin sections. The semithin sections were stained with 1% methylene blue (f. POCH) in 1% sodium borate (f. POCH) and preliminarily examined under a light microscope to select Epon blocks that contained dendrites of the DN.

From the selected blocks, ultrathin Sects. (70–80 nm) were cut, contrasted with uranyl acetate (f.Serva) and lead citrate (f.Serva), and examined using a transmission electron microscope (Opton EM 900, Germany). Photographs were taken with a TRS camera (CCD Camera for TEM 2 K inside).

The material obtained from the cerebellar dentate nucleus of the control group was processed using the same techniques as for the experimental group. Further methodological details regarding the preparation for transmission electron microscopy can be found in our previous publications25,45.

Texture analysis of the dendrites in the dentate nucleus (DN)

The images obtained with a transmission electron microscope, showing dendritic processes at a magnification of 12,000x, were randomly selected from each study group: control dendrite group (DC, n = 16), and dark dendrite group (DD, n = 15). The masks representing the dendrites were manually annotated. The annotations were performed using the ImageJ software (Wayne Rasband, National Institutes of Health, USA). First-order statistics (FOS)46 were used to extract 18 texture features from each of the annotated regions of interest (ROIs) in the electron-microscopic image, separately. Texture features were calculated using PyRadiomics, an open-source Python package for extracting features47.

Statistical analysis

The obtained numerical data representing the FOS features were presented for the control dendrite group (DC), and dark dendrite group (DD, i.e., including “dark” (compacted) dendrites). Data series were tested for their distribution using a Shapiro–Wilk normality test. As at least one data series for each extracted texture measure was not normally distributed, the numerical data were presented on plots using Tukey boxplots. In these plots, the boxes represent the interquartile range (IQR; 25th and 75th percentiles), the line within each box indicates the median, and the whiskers extend to the most extreme data points that lie within 1.5 × IQR. Data points exceeding this threshold are displayed individually as outliers. The comparisons between each FOS feature representing DC and DD groups were assessed using the t–test for data with normal distribution and the U Mann–Whitney test for data that did not have normal distribution. The significance level was established as p < 0.05.

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6 software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA), with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

Our recent pioneering studies conducted at the electron-microscopic level on the dendritic domain of the cerebellar dentate nucleus (DN) in the experimental model of HS revealed, in comparison to the control (Figs. 1a and b and 2), a series of interesting morphological changes, ranging from subtle alterations to lethal damage (Figs. 3a and b, 4 and 5a and b).

The descriptive qualitative ultrastructural abnormalities observed within the larger-caliber dendritic projections, particularly the large dendrites of the DN, varied in their expression, from relatively mild alterations (Fig. 3a, b), including minor background neuropathological changes, to predominantly intense specific lesions characterized by advanced aponecrotic changes, with distinct ultrastructural features of aponecrosis (Fig. 4).

Relatively mild morphological alterations affecting the large dendritic branches were characterized by a slightly increased electron density of the dendroplasm, minor shrinkage, and a reduced number of cytoskeletal elements (Fig. 3a, b). The submicroscopic details illustrating early neurodegenerative changes occurring within the cytoplasm of the large dendrite are well documented by a set of two electron micrographs (Fig. 3a, b).

In contrast, lethally damaged dendrites, exhibiting highly advanced aponecrotic degenerative changes, demonstrated pronounced ultrastructural features characteristic of dark dendroplasm degeneration (i.e., “dark dendrites”/“dark” (compacted) dendrites) (Fig. 4).

The large dendrites are characterized by dark, sometimes very dark, almost black appearance, with markedly increased electron density, and exhibit a shrunken, homogeneous structure that appears as a uniformly dense dendroplasm. The extensively damaged regions, filled with dark, homogeneous, microgranular material, probably derived from disintegrated polyribosomes, showed an exceptionally pronounced reduction in the number of intracellular elements, progressing to nearly complete disintegration (Figs. 4 and 5a, b). This affected both cytoskeletal structures, i.e., microtubules/microfilaments and cytoplasmic organelles within the large dendrites. The remaining cytoplasmic organelles predominantly exhibited features of very much advanced aponecrotic changes. Frequently were observed contours of very dark, nearly black degenerated mitochondria with residual cristae at their periphery, demonstrating a markedly condensed configuration, as well as disintegrating elements of the Golgi apparatus and very small vacuolar structures, probably derived from disintegrated elements of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum (Figs. 4 and 5a, b).

The smaller dendritic processes generally exhibited ultrastructural compaction - they were shrunken and contained dark or very dark, degenerated dendroplasm with markedly increased electron density. This pathology, referring to thinner dendritic processes, were well documented in our previous study on the ultrastructure of large neurons in the DN in experimental convulsions induced by heat stress25.

Repeatedly within the neuropil of the dentate nucleus, we observed alterations involving synaptic endings, primarily characterized by distinct swelling of both pre- and post-synaptic axodendritic terminals. However, a comprehensive analysis of these changes requires further detailed investigation.

The ultrastructural changes of a qualitative nature in the large dendrites correlated with the evaluation of the texture parameters of these structures. Discussion

The ultrastructural assessment, conducted at the ultrastructural level with texture analysis of the dendrites across the dentate nucleus in the experimental model of HS, revealed significant, intriguing, and well-defined morphological signs of the phenomenon of aponecrosis. These signs manifested as “dark” dendroplasm degeneration (i.e., “dark dendrites”).

The most pronounced lesions of the aponecrotic type within the dendritic processes were characterized by a shrunken, dark, nearly black, homogeneous dendroplasm showing advanced degenerative changes, including necrosis.

Lethally damaged dendritic arbors exhibited, within their dendritic cytoplasm, evident signs of disintegration of intracellular structures - both cytoskeletal components, primarily microtubules, and cellular organelles.

The ultrastructure of morphological changes caused by heat-stress-induced HS within the large dendrites and their smaller branches across the DN directly correspond to the features of dark cell degeneration, which we previously also identified at the TEM level within the cytoplasm of the large DN neurons in the same experimental model of hyperthermia-induced convulsions25.

A noteworthy finding is that the most characteristic quantitative ultrastructural changes were observed in such parameters as 10th percentile, 90th percentile, Mean, Median, Minimum, and Maximum. Lower values in the DD group confirm that the images are darker compared to those from the DC or DOE groups. Variability and dispersion measures, such as Variance, RMS, IR, MAD, and rMAD, indicate greater intensity diversity in the DC and DOE groups compared to the DD group. Positive Skewness values in the DD group indicate a greater prevalence of dark pixels and a long tail toward brighter values. In contrast, the negative values in the DC and DOE groups indicate a predominance of light pixels and a long tail extending toward darker values.

The higher entropy in the DC and DOE groups reflects a broad range of intensity values, indicating greater complexity. In contrast, the more homogeneous images in the DD group have fewer unique grayscale levels. Statistically significant differences were observed only between the DC and DOE groups for the Kurtosis parameter. Higher kurtosis values in the DOE group suggest a sharply peaked histogram, while lower values in the DC group indicate a more dispersed histogram. The absence of significant statistical differences in the remaining parameters between the DC and DOE groups suggests that there is similarity in pixel brightness across the images.

The conducted quantitative analysis corroborates the qualitative observations, providing strong empirical support for the initial findings.

Interestingly, in our current microscopic investigations on dendrites, the most prominent disorder was significant disruption of microtubule organization in dendrites, leading to disintegration of these cytoskeletal structures, including their complete breakdown.

The literature emphasizes that dendrites have a unique microtubular organization. It is precisely these microtubules that are the cytoskeletal components responsible for playing essential roles, serving as critical structures in numerous cellular processes, such as motility, migration, differentiation, intracellular transport, and organelle positioning48.

In neuroanatomy, microtubules are regarded as the “railroad tracks” of the cell, playing an essential role in the transport of mitochondria and other organelles49. This finding is also supported by the ultrastructural observations obtained in our current study.

Microtubules are composed of two proteins, alpha- and beta-tubulin, commonly referred to as microtubule-associated proteins, which form heterodimers and organize themselves in a head-to-tail manner. Interestingly, microtubules are dynamic and can rapidly switch between phases of growth and shrinkage. This behavior is known as dynamic instability48,50.

It has been reported that abnormalities in dendrites and dendritic spine morphology, arising from faulty regulation of the cytoskeleton, have been linked to several neuropsychiatric diseases51–53.

In our opinion, the degenerative changes observed in the current study, concerning large dendrites and smaller branches of dendritic domain of the DN, including aponectotic damage of the large DN neurons (i.e. “dark” (compacted) dendrites and “dark neurons”), as well as the submicroscopic lesions demonstrated in our previous research, under analogous experimental conditions, within other regions of the CNS, especially the hippocampal CA1 and the CA3 sectors and the temporal lobe neocortex resulted in marked disturbances in the cerebro-cerebellar function in the animals subjected to warm water hyperthermia20–25.

It is worth noting that in the literature on neuroanatomy and neurophysiology, the dentate nucleus, the largest and phylogenetically most recent of the cerebellar nuclei, is emphasized as a key relay center between the cortex and other parts of the brain. Indeed, the dentate nucleus receives afferent fibers from the premotor cortex and supplementary motor cortex via the pontocerebellar pathway. At the same time, its efferent projections traverse the superior cerebellar peduncle through the red nucleus to reach the ventrolateral thalamus, crossing over at the pontomesencephalic junction29,32,33,54,55.

Therefore, this region of the cerebellum is attributed with specific neurophysiological properties, particularly its responsibility for the planning, initiation, and also the control of volitional movements54,56–59.

Unfortunately, so far, little research has been dedicated to morphological studies in neuropathology, especially those correlated with texture assessment of the dendrites of the cerebellar dentate nucleus, conducted at the electron microscopic level. Therefore, special attention should be drawn to an interesting electron microscopic study conducted by Gallyas et al.26 on the phenomenon of “dark” cell degeneration in another model of experimentally induced seizures, i.e., those produced in the rat brain by hypoglycemic convulsions. The authors observed very intense submicroscopic changes characterized by dark compaction in the somatodendritic domain (i.e., in the soma and dendritic profile) of the frontoparietal cortex, particularly in the cytoplasm of the primary dendrites, similar to those demonstrated in our current study across the dendrites in the dentate nucleus.

Considering that the cerebellum, especially the cerebellar cortex and the cerebellar dentate nucleus, has an inhibitory effect on the motor component of epileptic seizures54,60,61, marked damage to the dendrites in this region of the CNS, manifested by extensive and intense aponecrotic changes characterized by “dark” dendroplasm degeneration, may lead to substantial disturbances in the receiving, processing, and integrating of neuronal information.

It should be assumed that similar neurophysiological disturbances at the cellular level also occur during hyperthermic convulsions, as evidenced by the profound ultrastructural changes (both qualitative and quantitative) of the dark cell degeneration type observed in current and previous studies.

Since these changes affect both the dendritic domain and the large neuronal perikarya in the DN, their consequence could be deprivation of the body’s natural protection in states of “seizure readiness”25,54,61.

For that reason, we believe that further experimental investigation of the same topic could provide additional insights into the neuronal organization of the dentate nucleus in heat stress-induced febrile seizures.

The main limitation of this study is the inability to compare the obtained ultrastructural texture parameters due to a clear lack of studies in the literature on hyperthermia-induced dendritic pathology in the cerebellar dentate nucleus. The lack of comparable reference data limits the ability to place the observed pre-necrotic changes in a broader context.

Future studies could address this limitation by applying quantitative texture-analysis-based methods to other brain regions, including the CA1 and CA3 sectors of the hippocampus and the temporal lobe neocortex[21–24], which we have so far examined on the morphology at the level of the ultrastructure only qualitatively in an experimental model of febrile seizures induced by warm water. A re-evaluation of existing datasets could provide a valuable comparative framework and significantly broaden the interpretation of the current results.

Conclusions

The currently conducted, first in the literature, qualitative and quantitative research at the level of electron microscopy concerning the cellular mechanisms within the dendrites of the cerebellar dentate nucleus in the experimental model of febrile seizures has revealed interesting, specific, and easily recognizable morphological signs of the phenomenon of aponecrosis, manifested by “dark” dendroplasm degeneration. Our current and previous submicroscopic assessment of the DN documents a dramatic extent of compaction of all ultrastructural elements in the somatodendritic domain of this nucleus, i.e., in the dendritic processes as well as in the previously studied large neuronal somata. The obtained results, expanded by texture analysis, clearly demonstrate that damage to the cerebellar nucleus, morphologically expressed by dark cell degeneration of the somatodendritic domain, may play an important role, or at least significantly co-occur, alongside changes in other regions of the CNS, in the morphogenesis of heat stress-induced convulsions.

In the future, this could have significant implications for the prevention and treatment of these types of neuronal disorders in pediatric patients.
