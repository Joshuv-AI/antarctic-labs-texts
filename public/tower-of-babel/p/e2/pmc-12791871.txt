Efficacy of Coronary Sinus Aspiration in Reducing Contrast-Induced Nephropathy in High-Risk Patients
Authors: Somil Verma, Anwar Hussain Ansari, Rajesh Kumar, Ankit Jain, Abhinav Aggarwal, Sandeep Bansal, Hermohinder Singh Isser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContrast-induced nephropathy (CIN) is a complication of angiographic procedures. Coronary sinus aspiration (CSA) is a novel technique aimed at reducing renal exposure to contrast and mitigating the risk of CIN.ObjectivesThe aim of the study was to evaluate the rates of CIN in high-risk patients with and without CSA at the time of coronary angioplasty.MethodsIn this single-center, prospective, single-blind randomized controlled trial, 70 high-risk patients undergoing coronary angioplasty were randomized into 2 groups: the intervention group (coronary angioplasty with CSA) and the control group (coronary angioplasty). The primary outcome was the proportion of patients developing CIN (defined as an increase in serum creatinine of at least 25% or 0.5 mg/dL within 3 days following the procedure).ResultsIn total, 70 patients with diabetes and chronic kidney disease 3 (mean age 60.6 ± 8.7 years) were included. Of the 37 participants initially allocated to CSA, 4 were excluded (3 due to difficulties in coronary sinus cannulation and 1 due to a procedural complication). The mean volume of injected contrast was 65.2 ± 23 mL and the estimated volume of aspirated contrast was 18.7 ± 7.1 mL. Among the 66 remaining patients, the rate of CIN was lower in the intervention group (5/33, 15.2%) compared to the control group (13/33, 39.4%) (P = 0.027). There were no differences in postprocedural serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate.ConclusionsWhile CSA was associated with lower rates of CIN in high-risk patients undergoing coronary angioplasty, procedural complications can occur. Larger studies are needed to understand the role of CSA for high-risk patients undergoing coronary angioplasty.

Cardiac angiography and angioplasty are critical interventions for the treatment of coronary artery disease; however, they involve the administration of iodinated contrast media (CM), which poses a notable risk of contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN).1 Also termed as contrast-induced acute kidney injury, CIN is a leading cause of hospital-acquired acute kidney injuries, contributing to approximately 12% of such cases.2 The incidence of CIN varies significantly, affecting 14.5% of the general population and up to 50% of high-risk groups which includes individuals with heart failure, diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic kidney disease (CKD), or advance age.3, 4, 5, 6 Patients with elevated risk, particularly those with CKD and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, face heightened susceptibility, which is further aggravated by the use of larger volumes of CM.7,8 CIN presents as a rapid decline in renal function occurring within 48 to 72 hours following exposure to CM. It is defined by an increase in serum creatinine (SCr) of at least 0.5 mg/dL or a rise of 25% from baseline levels within 48 to 72 hours of contrast exposure.9 The underlying pathophysiology involves direct nephrotoxicity, micro-emboli-induced kidney damage, and CM-mediated intrarenal vasoconstriction.10 Despite advancements in interventional cardiology, there is no definitive treatment for CIN, making prevention paramount. Current preventive measures include periprocedural hydration with isotonic saline, minimizing CM doses, and utilizing nonionic, low osmolar or iso-osmolar CM.11 However, the effectiveness of other strategies, such as the use of N-acetylcysteine and intravenous furosemide, remains inconclusive, and approaches like prophylactic dialysis are reserved for specific cases with CKD stage 4 or 5.12 Given the direct correlation between contrast volume and CIN risk, novel techniques to reduce renal exposure to CM are under investigation. Coronary sinus aspiration (CSA) is one such innovative approach, aiming to remove CM from circulation and thereby minimizing renal toxicity.13,14 While CSA has shown promise, particularly in high-risk populations, it remains understudied, especially in the Indian context, with lack of randomized clinical trials. Addressing this gap is crucial for improving outcomes in high-risk patients undergoing coronary angioplasty. The objective of current study was to evaluate the efficacy of CSA in mitigating the risk of CIN, with the goal of advancing preventive strategies for this vulnerable patient group.

Methodology

Study design

This study was a single-blinded, randomized controlled trial conducted over an 18-month period from October 2022 to April 2024, in the Departments of Cardiology and Nephrology, Vardhman Mahavir Medical College and Safdarjung Hospital, New Delhi. Participants were randomized into 2 groups: the control group (undergoing standard coronary angioplasty) and the intervention group (undergoing coronary angioplasty with CSA). Blinding was implemented at the participant level to ensure unbiased reporting of outcomes. Clinical trial was registered in Clinical Trial Registry India (CTRI) with registration number, CTRI/2022/10/046710.

Study participants

The study enrolled adult participants aged 18 to 75 years with stage 3 CKD (eGFR 30-60 mL/min/1.73 m2) and type 2 DM who were scheduled for coronary angioplasty. Exclusion criteria included a history of acute coronary syndrome within the past 3 months, angiography done within past 2 weeks, hemodynamic instability, mechanical complications, heart failure, and right coronary artery (RCA) interventions. Patients were also excluded if they had hemoglobin levels below 8 g/dL, hyperkalemia (serum potassium >5.3 mEq/L), or obstructive uropathy. Participants were recruited from hospitalized patients meeting these criteria, and they were subjected to clinical evaluations, laboratory testing, and imaging studies.

Sample size

Sample size calculation was based on prior data from Diab et al,15 which reported a 36% incidence of CIN in the control group and 5.5% in the intervention group. Using a CI of 95%, power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated to be 24 participants per group. To account for potential dropouts and maintain robustness, the sample size was increased to 33 participants in each group.

Randomization and allocation

Participants were assigned to either the control or intervention group in a 1:1 ratio through computer-generated randomization. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, sealed, and opaque envelopes. Blinding was restricted to participants, who remained unaware of their group assignment, while proceduralists and other clinical staff were not blinded due to the intervention's inherent nature.

Intervention

Both the groups received periprocedural hydration using isotonic saline at a rate of 1 to 1.5 mL/kg per hour. For hospitalized patients, hydration commenced 6 to 12 hours before the procedure, while for outpatients, it began 3 to 6 hours prior. This hydration regimen continued for 24 hours postprocedure. All nephrotoxic medications, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, were discontinued at least 24 hours before the procedure. For diabetic patients, metformin was withheld on the day of the procedure and was resumed 48 hours postprocedure, provided renal function remained stable. Coronary angioplasty was performed using standard techniques via either the radial or femoral approach. A 100% (undiluted) nonionic low-osmolar contrast medium, specifically iobitridol, was manually administered, with efforts to minimize usage. The contrast dose was restricted to 5 mL per kilogram of body weight, capped at 300 mL, and adjusted according to SCr levels (mg/dL) as described in prior protocols.13 The total volume of injected contrast (VIC) was meticulously documented. For participants in the control group, standard coronary angioplasty was conducted without incorporating CSA. In the CSA group, coronary sinus (CS) cannulation was performed following the insertion of a radial or femoral arterial sheath and prior to coronary angioplasty. Access to the CS was achieved via the right or left femoral vein. First, an introducer sheath was inserted, and a 0.032-inch J-wire was advanced into the right atrium (Figure 1A). Subsequently, the sheath was removed and an Abbott Swartz Braided Transseptal Guiding Introducer SL0 (8.0-F) sheath with a dilator was advanced over the wire and positioned at the junction of the inferior vena cava and the right atrium. Upon removal of the dilator, a steerable decapolar catheter was inserted through the SL0 sheath, and CS cannulation was achieved in a 40° left anterior oblique projection (Figure 1B). The SL0 sheath was subsequently advanced over the decapolar catheter and positioned deeply within the CS (Figure 1C). The decapolar catheter was removed, and CS positioning was confirmed by injecting 1 to 2 mL of contrast dye into the CS under fluoroscopy (Figure 1D). The time required for CS cannulation was recorded from the moment of venous puncture to the completion of CS angiography, along with the fluoroscopy duration during the procedure. The SL0 sheath was positioned within the CS and engaged at its proximal or middle third to facilitate direct aspiration. For each contrast injection, CSA was conducted simultaneously using a 20 mL syringe, with fluoroscopic guidance to monitor the contrast's entry into the CS and its subsequent removal through aspiration (Figure 2A). CSA was not routinely performed during RCA angioplasty as the volume of contrast used in RCA angioplasty is relatively small. The time required for contrast clearance from the CS was measured from the start of the injection until the contrast completely disappeared under fluoroscopy. To confirm the presence of contrast in the CS aspirate, the aspirate-filled syringe was compared under fluoroscopy to a syringe containing pure CS blood (Figure 2B). The volume of CS aspirate (VCSA) per injection was calculated by dividing the total CS aspirate by the number of aspirations, while the total volume of collected CS aspirate was also recorded. A sample of the CS aspirate was analyzed to determine its hematocrit (Hct) value. Volume of aspirated contrast was calculated by comparing the Hct value of the patient’s blood with that of the CS aspirate. At the conclusion of the procedure, the venous sheath was removed, followed by gentle compression and dressing to ensure hemostasis. If CSA proved unfeasible due to technical difficulties, such as challenges in cannulating the CS, the procedure was abandoned to maintain patient safety. Procedural complications, such as CS dissection or perforation, were carefully recorded to assess the intervention's safety profile. In all patients, SCr, potassium, and hemoglobin were measured daily till the third day postprocedure and eGFR was calculated.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was the proportion of patients developing contrast-induced acute kidney injury, which was defined as an increase in SCr of at least 25% or 0.5 mg/dL within 3 days following the procedure. The secondary outcomes included absolute and percentage change in SCr in both the groups.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. For continuous variables, differences between groups were analyzed using the unpaired t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, while differences within groups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. For categorical variables, the chi-square test was used. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 16.0).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Committee of Vardhman Mahavir Medical College and Safdarjung Hospital, New Delhi. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and national ethical guidelines for biomedical research. Participants were informed about their right to withdraw at any stage without repercussions.

Confidentiality of data was maintained throughout the study.

Results

A total of 70 participants with type 2 DM and stage 3 CKD (eGFR 30-60 mL/min/1.73 m2) undergoing coronary angioplasty were screened for eligibility. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria and obtaining written informed consent, participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio into 2 groups: the control group (undergoing standard coronary angioplasty) and the study group (undergoing coronary angioplasty with CSA). Of the 37 participants initially allocated to the study group, 4 were excluded—3 due to technical difficulties in CS cannulation and 1 due to a procedural complication involving CS perforation. Discussion

This study suggests that CSA might be one strategy for reducing CIN in high-risk patients undergoing coronary angioplasty. In this small study, CIN was less frequent in the intervention group (15.2%) compared to the control group (39.4%), yielding a relative risk reduction of 61% and an absolute risk reduction of 24.2% (P = 0.027). However, postprocedural creatinine and eGFR did not differ between groups, possibly due to the study’s sample size.

This technique has been reported by other groups. In comparisons, femoral vein access was used exclusively for CS cannulation in our study, with right femoral access utilized in 72.7% of cases. This contrasts with findings by Diab et al, who used subclavian access in 44% of cases.15 Femoral access was chosen for its procedural simplicity and to avoid complications such as pneumothorax associated with subclavian access.16,17 The majority of patients in both the case group (78.8%) and the control group (72.7%) underwent single-vessel PCI, and most received a single drug-eluting stent—78.8% in the case group and 69.7% in the control group. These findings align with the commonly adopted strategy of staged PCI in patients with CKD, where single-vessel intervention is preferred per session to minimize contrast exposure and reduce the risk of CIN. CSA was unsuccessful in 3 out of 37 patients (8%) due to difficulties in cannulating the CS, mainly because of anatomical issues like a narrowed CS ostium, challenging angulation, or catheter instability within the CS. Additionally, one patient (2.8%) experienced a procedure-related complication. In contrast, Danenberg et al and Duffy et al reported higher procedural failure rates of 55% and 25%, respectively. On the other hand, Diab et al reported no failures; however, their findings were based on a limited sample size (n = 18).13, 14, 15 Our study achieved shorter mean cannulation times (9.9 ± 5.7 minutes) compared to Diab et al (19.27 ± 3.54 minutes), likely due to the use of a decapolar catheter, which streamlined the process.18 The VIC in our intervention group (65.2 ± 23 mL) was lower compared to both the intervention and control groups in study by Diab et al (82.9 ± 40.16 mL).15 This difference is likely attributable to operator bias, as awareness of group allocation may have led interventionalists to adopt a more cautious approach in administering contrast. Additionally, the majority of procedures involved single-vessel PCI with the deployment of a single stent, further contributing to the reduced contrast volume. The mean VCSA in this study (102.2 ± 24.9 mL) and FAC (28.9% ± 5.1%) were lower compared to previous studies employing balloon occlusion techniques, such as by Diab et al (FAC 39.35% ± 10.47%).15 In comparing findings with Diab et al, their study achieved a higher absolute risk reduction of 30.5%, likely due to methodological differences. Diab et al included both angiography and angioplasty patients in their analysis, with angiography requiring less contrast and contributing to fewer CIN events.15 These findings suggest that while direct sheath aspiration simplifies the procedure, incorporating occlusive techniques may enhance contrast removal efficiency and improve outcomes.19 These differences underscore the importance of optimizing procedural techniques to enhance CSA efficacy.

Despite difference in CIN between groups, there were no statistically significant differences in postprocedure eGFR or SCr. The higher baseline potassium levels noted in the intervention group may be incidental and likely influenced by the small sample size. Following the procedure, potassium levels declined in the intervention group but rose slightly in the control group, suggesting that CSA may help reduce renal stress and support electrolyte stability. However, these changes were not statistically significant, underscoring the need for larger, adequately powered studies to validate these findings, especially since this study was not specifically designed to assess changes in serum potassium21 Additionally, a more pronounced drop in postprocedure hemoglobin was observed in the intervention group, possibly due to procedural blood loss, though the difference was not statistically significant, suggesting limited long-term clinical relevance.20

A single procedure-related complication occurred in our study, whereas Diab et al reported no such events, likely reflecting the low frequency of these complications and the need of a larger sample size to detect them reliably.15 Cortese et al reported a 1% incidence of CS perforation (15 out of 1,465 patients) during cardiac resynchronization therapy-defibrillator implantation, with most cases managed conservatively and only a few requiring surgical intervention.22

Other methods to decrease incidence of CIN include concept of ultra-low contrast PCI, which relies on meticulous procedural planning, hemodynamic monitoring, and imaging to limit contrast volume. Several different modalities employ different mechanism to mitigate CIN. Intravascular ultrasound and saline/dextran-based optical coherence tomography provide high-resolution intravascular imaging that enables precision PCI with minimal or no contrast use, but they require specific expertise, equipment availability, and additional procedural time. CSA provides a novel adjunctive approach by actively removing contrast from the coronary venous circulation, thereby reducing systemic exposure even in procedures where contrast use cannot be avoided. It may be particularly useful in anatomically complex cases or in centers where advanced imaging modalities are not readily available. Future comparative or integrative studies involving CSA and other contrast-sparing methods would be valuable to assess relative efficacy, feasibility, and outcomes, particularly in high-risk cohorts such as CKD patients.

STUDY Limitations

This study contributes several novel insights. It introduces a CSA technique that does not require advance equipment, making it more accessible in resource-limited settings. If CSA is validated in larger studies, it may offer high-risk patients, many of whom are excluded from life-saving procedures due to fears of CIN, an option for a safer angioplasty. However, the study has limitations. The relatively small sample size limits the ability to detect significant differences in some outcomes, such as postprocedural eGFR and creatinine and in safety outcomes. Being a single-center study, its findings may not be generalizable to all health care settings. Operator bias, due to awareness of group allocation, may have influenced procedural decisions, such as minimizing contrast in intervention cases. CS cannulation using decapolar catheter and swartz left 0 sheath require additional expertise and its availability and experience may not be present in centers lacking an electrophysiology lab. The procedure requires 2 operators, one performing the angioplasty and the other carrying out simultaneous CSA. CSA also increases the total procedure time and radiation exposure. The risk of procedure-related complication, including CS dissection or perforation, is present. Lastly the omission of balloon occlusion, while simplifying the technique, may have reduced aspirate efficiency. Future research should address these limitations. In particular, larger, multicenter randomized controlled trials are essential to validate findings. Refinements to the procedure, such as the incorporation of balloon occlusion or other advanced techniques, could enhance efficacy. Investigations into cost-effectiveness and applicability in various clinical scenarios, including primary PCI, are also warranted. As operator experience and proficiency improve, the rates of procedural failure and complications could decline further. By overcoming these challenges, CSA might be a strategy in nephroprotection for high-risk patients.

Conclusions

CIN is an increasing concern with the growing number of angiographic procedures worldwide, particularly in patients with DM and CKD, who are at high risk. In this study of high-risk patients undergoing coronary angioplasty, CSA in addition to standard preventive measures like periprocedural hydration was associated with lower contrast volume exposure and lower rates of CIN, although one patient developed a procedure-related complication.
