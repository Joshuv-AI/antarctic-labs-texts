Zebrafish neurofibromatosis type 1 mutants show disruption of sleep but not of circadian rhythms
Authors: Daniel A Lee, Grigorios Oikonomou, Yun Chiu, Alexis D Cho, Jin Xu, Tasha Cammidge, Chanpreet Singh, David A Prober
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder that is associated with several developmental and behavioral phenotypes, including reduced and fragmented sleep at night. Here, we describe the role of NF1 in sleep in zebrafish, a diurnal vertebrate whose mechanisms of sleep control are broadly conserved with those of mammals. The zebrafish genome encodes two neurofibromin 1 (nf1) paralogs: nf1a and nf1b, which function in a partially redundant manner. We found that zebrafish with reduced nf1 function exhibit decreased and delayed sleep at night, similar to humans with the NF1 disorder, in a dose-dependent manner. Zebrafish nf1 mutants also exhibit shorter sleep bouts, decreased arousal threshold, and decreased sleep depth, consistent with disrupted sleep, and show increased sleep in the morning. Finally, we found that zebrafish nf1 mutants have intact behavioral circadian rhythms, and the nf1 mutant sleep phenotype does not require entrained circadian rhythms. These results establish a vertebrate nf1 model that recapitulates key aspects of human NF1-associated sleep disturbances and provides a basis to explore mechanisms through which NF1 regulates sleep. This study also suggests that disrupted sleep in the human NF1 disorder is directly due to reduced NF1 function, rather than a secondary symptom of the disorder.Keywords: neurofibromatosis type 1, NF1, sleep, circadian, zebrafish

Individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are frequently at increased risk for disrupted sleep [1, 2]. Understanding which NDDs present with a primary sleep pathology, as well as their underlying etiology, is essential for identifying therapeutic strategies. One of the most common NDDs is neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), an autosomal dominant genetic disorder that is characterized by pigmented skin patches, a high rate of specific types of tumors, cardiovascular abnormalities, neurodevelopmental defects, and cognitive and behavioral deficits that include learning disorders, attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder, and disrupted sleep (reviewed in [3–7]). Individuals with NF1 commonly report deficits in initiating and maintaining sleep, reduced sleep duration at night, and drowsiness during the day [8–13]. The basis for these sleep defects is unknown, but they may be due to other symptoms associated with the NF1 disorder, such as neurofibromas in the airway that cause sleep-disordered breathing, seizures, pain, psychiatric disorders, or medications used to treat these symptoms [4–7, 11, 14]. Alternatively, disrupted sleep may be a primary phenotype of NF1 mutations.

NF1 results from heterozygous mutations in the neurofibromin 1 (nf1) gene [3–6, 15]. Homozygous mutations likely result in embryonic lethality in humans, as occurs in Nf1−/− mice [16, 17]. The nf1 gene encodes neurofibromin, a protein that contains a GTPase-activating protein (GAP)-related domain (GRD) that stimulates the GTPase activity of the Ras protein, and thus promotes conversion of Ras from a GTP-bound active state to a GDP-bound inactive state. NF1 can therefore act as a negative regulator of Ras/MAPK signaling [18]. Some defects associated with the NF1 disorder have been attributed to the GAP function of NF1. For example, mice with a point mutation that abolishes the GAP function of NF1 exhibit many of the features observed in Nf1−/− mouse embryos [19]. However, NF1 is a large protein that contains several other domains that can interact with different proteins. Missense mutations in humans with NF1 are present throughout the gene [15], suggesting that domains other than the GRD, and interactions with proteins other than Ras, may play roles in the disorder.

Most reports of human sleep disturbances associated with the NF1 disorder are anecdotal or use subjective sleep questionnaires [7–11, 13]. One study that used polysomnography to characterize sleep in children with NF1 mutations observed reduced and fragmented sleep compared to controls [12]. The NF1 gene is evolutionarily conserved [20], presenting an opportunity to explore the role of NF1 in regulating sleep in animal models. Drosophila nf1 mutants were first reported to lack behavioral circadian rhythms [21], but subsequent studies also revealed reduced and fragmented sleep, as well as increased sleep early in the morning [22–27], consistent with the human polysomnography study. The only study to examine the role of NF1 in rodent sleep used mice with a homozygous Nf1 mutation in neurons, astrocytes, and neuroglial precursors [28]. An increase in sleep fragmentation was observed, similar to humans with the NF1 disorder [12], but the amount of time spent in wake, NREM sleep, and REM sleep was unaffected. A noteworthy difference between these animal models and humans is that the human NF1 disorder is caused by heterozygous NF1 mutations [3–6, 15], whereas the fly sleep studies used homozygous nf1 mutants, and the mouse studies used animals with homozygous loss of Nf1 in neurons, astrocytes, and neuroglial precursors. Due to these differences and the useful features of zebrafish for modeling human diseases, we explored whether zebrafish are a good model for investigating the function of NF1 in sleep.

Zebrafish have two nf1 paralogs: nf1a and nf1b, which are 94% similar to each other and ~90% similar to human NF1 [20]. Loss-of-function mutations were made in both nf1a and nf1b (Figure 1), which were found to function redundantly in development and tumor suppression [29]. nf1a−/−; nf1b−/− zebrafish have phenotypes that resemble aspects of the human disease and die at 7–10 days postfertilization (dpf) [29], and thus cannot be used for sleep studies. However, zebrafish with one wild-type (WT) nf1 allele (out of four total nf1a and nf1b alleles) are viable, fertile, and lack obvious developmental defects [29, 30]. We therefore used this zebrafish nf1 partial loss-of-function model to test whether nf1 plays a role in regulating zebrafish sleep.

Sleep in zebrafish and other nonmammalian animal models is defined by broadly accepted behavioral criteria that include behavioral quiescence that is rapidly reversible, increased arousal threshold, and homeostatic regulation [31]. Several groups have used behavioral criteria to define sleep in a variety of organisms, including humans [32–35], rodents [36–38], and zebrafish [39–42]. In larval zebrafish, one or more minutes of inactivity is associated with an increased arousal threshold and can thus be defined as a sleep state [39, 42]. Sleep in larval zebrafish has genetic, pharmacological, and neuroanatomical similarities to mammalian sleep [43–45], suggesting that most findings in larval zebrafish will translate to mammals. Using zebrafish nf1 mutants, we show that nf1 is necessary for normal sleep initiation, maintenance, and duration in a dose-dependent manner. We also found that zebrafish nf1 mutants have a decreased arousal threshold and are more easily aroused from sleep, consistent with disrupted sleep. However, unlike Drosophila nf1 mutants [21], behavioral circadian rhythms appear to be intact in zebrafish nf1 in mutants, and these mutants affect sleep in a circadian-independent manner. These results establish zebrafish as a useful model to study the role of nf1 in sleep that complements previously established models for understanding NF1-associated human sleep defects.

Materials and Methods

Experimental model and subject details

All experimental procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with the California Institute of Technology Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and by the Office of Laboratory Animal Resources at the California Institute of Technology (animal protocol 1836). All experiments used larval zebrafish between four and seven dpf. Sex is not yet defined at this stage of development. Larval zebrafish were housed in Petri dishes with 50 animals per dish. E3 medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2, 0.33 mM MgSO4) was used for housing and experiments. Experiments were performed using siblings with the same genetic background. All experiments used animals from a TLAB hybrid strain. Unless otherwise noted, fish were raised and behaviorally tested in a 14:10 h light:dark (LD) cycle, with white lights on from 9:00 am to 11:00 pm.

Mutant zebrafish

The nf1a-8 and nf1b+10 mutants have been described [29]. These mutants were genotyped by PCR using the following primer pairs and PCR conditions: nf1a F genotyping primer, 5′- GGTGTGTATGTAAATGGGCTCAATG-3′; nf1a R genotyping primer, 5′-TACAGTTTCCATAAAACCTGACATTTC-3′; nf1b F genotyping primer, 5′-TGCTACCTGCCGGCAGGCTCAG-3′; and nf1b R genotyping primer, 5′-ACCTGTGACCATCATGTTACTGACA-3′. PCR conditions for nf1a were 94°C for 2 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 54.8°C for 30 s and 72°C for 40 s; and 72°C for 5 min. PCR conditions for nf1b were 94°C for 2 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 62°C for 30 s and 72°C for 40 s; and 72°C for 5 min. For both nf1a and nf1b, a 223-bp PCR product was generated. PCR products of mutant alleles are resistant to digestion with DdeI (nf1a) and EcoNI (nf1b), whereas the WT alleles are completely digested.

Behavioral assays

Sleep/wake assay

Sleep/wake analysis was performed as previously described [39] with small modifications [46, 47]. Fish were raised on a 14:10 h LD cycle at 28.5°C with lights on at 9:00 am and off at 11:00 pm. Individual fish were placed into each well of a 96-well plate (7701–1651, Whatman) containing 650 μL of E3 embryo medium. Fish were blindly assigned a position in the plate and were genotyped after the behavioral experiment was complete. Locomotor activity was monitored using a ZebraBox videotracking system (Viewpoint Life Sciences) fitted with a variable-focus lens and infrared filter. The movement of each fish was recorded using the quantization mode of the Viewpoint software. The 96-well plate and camera were housed inside a custom-modified ZebraBox (Viewpoint Life Sciences) that was continuously illuminated with infrared light. The 96-well plate was housed in a chamber filled with recirculating water to maintain a constant temperature of 28.5°C. Data were analyzed using custom Perl and MATLAB (Mathworks) scripts [48].

A one-minute period with no movement was defined as 1 min of sleep based on arousal threshold changes [39]. A sleep bout was defined as a continuous string of sleep minutes. Sleep latency was defined as the length of time from lights on in the morning or lights off at night to the start of the first sleep bout. Activity was defined as the average amount of locomotor activity in s/h, including sleep bouts. Waking activity was defined as the average amount of activity in seconds/active minute, ie, excluding sleep bouts.

Arousal threshold assay

We modified the videotracking system by adding an Arduino-based automated driver to control two solenoids (28P-I-12, Guardian Electric) that deliver a tap to a 96-well plate containing zebrafish larvae [49]. This setup allowed us to drive the solenoids with a voltage ranging from 0 V to 20 V over a range of 4095 settings (from 0.01 to 40.95). We used taps ranging from power settings of 1–36.31 a.u. Taps of 15 different intensities were applied in a random order with an inter-trial interval of 1 min during the night, from 12:00 am to 7:00 am. Previous studies showed that a 15 s interval between repetitive stimuli is sufficient to prevent behavioral habituation [50]. An animal was considered to respond to the stimulus if it showed any locomotor activity during the 1 s immediately following stimulus presentation. The background probability of movement was calculated by identifying for each genotype the fraction of animals that moved 5 s prior to all stimuli delivered during an experiment (15 different tap powers × 30 trials per experiment = 450 data points per animal). This value was subtracted from the average response fraction value for each tap event. The response of fish to the stimuli was monitored using the videotracking software and was analyzed using MATLAB (Mathworks) and Excel (Microsoft). Statistical analysis was performed using the Variable Slope log(dose) response curve fitting module of Prism (GraphPad).

Sleep depth assay

Using the same hardware described in the arousal threshold assay, taps were delivered every 5 min at powers low enough to allow discrimination between awake and sleeping fish [51]. A tap intensity of 2.29 a.u. was strong enough to elicit a response from awake control animals, while having a reduced effect on sleeping controls, and was used to determine sleep depth. Responses to stimuli were monitored using the videotracking software and analyzed using MATLAB (Mathworks), Excel (Microsoft), and Prism (GraphPad).

Behavioral data analysis

sort_fish_sttime_192.pl is a Perl script [39] that converts data acquired by the Viewpoint system to a format that is useful for MATLAB analysis and removes notations that are not relevant to behavioral analysis. perl_batch_192well.m is a MATLAB script that allows the sort_fish_sttime_192.pl script to run. TapAnalysis.m is a MATLAB script that analyzes tapping assay data and generates a table that lists the number of animals that moved following each tap. VT_analysis.m is a MATLAB script (modified from [39]) that analyzes data collected by the Viewpoint system to quantify several metrics, including locomotor activity, waking activity, sleep, sleep architecture, and sleep latency. These scripts are available online [48] and detailed instructions on their use will be provided upon request.

Hybridization chain reaction and immunohistochemistry

We performed hybridization chain reaction (HCR) using probes specific for c-fos as previously described [52], and pERK/tERK immunohistochemistry (also known as mitogen-activated protein kinase [MAP]-mapping) and data analysis as previously described [53]. For both types of experiments, fish were raised on a 14:10 h LD cycle at 28.5°C with white lights on from 9:00 am to 11:00 pm (normal circadian cycle for daytime points), or on from 9:00 pm to 11:00 am (reverse circadian cycle for nighttime points). All samples were fixed at 3:00 pm at 6-dpf. For MAP-mapping, we performed immunohistochemistry using antibodies that are specific for the phosphorylated form of ERK (pERK), and for both phosphorylated and unphosphorylated ERK (total ERK, tERK) [53]. Labeling with both antibodies, each in a different color, and taking the ratio of pERK/tERK signal reveals ERK phosphorylation levels normalized to the amount of ERK in each cell. The images shown are the ratio of pERK/tERK in mutants compared to pERK/tERK in controls. Cells with higher pERK/tERK levels in mutants are shown in green, while cells with higher pERK/tERK levels in controls would be shown in magenta if such cells were present. To evaluate c-fos expression in each group, confocal stacks of c-fos HCR images were registered to a template c-fos channel from a single fish for each time point using the Computational Morphometry Toolkit [54]. Standard deviation projections of transformed stacks were merged and averaged to create an expression map for each genotype and condition.

Statistical analysis

For all behavioral experiments, the unit of analysis for statistics is a single animal during the day or night. Normality tests (D’Agostino & Pearson omnibus normality test; Shapiro–Wilk normality test) demonstrated that most behavioral parameters were not normally distributed. We therefore used non-parametric tests for statistical analyses (Mann–Whitney test for two unpaired groups; Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s correction for multiple comparisons for more than two unpaired groups). Line graphs in Figures 1, 5, 6, and 7 show 1 h moving averages of mean values in 10 min intervals. For box plots, the box includes data from the 25th to 75th percentiles with the median marked by a horizontal line through the box. In Figure 3A, Tukey whiskers are shown. The lower whisker extends to the smallest datum that is larger than 25th percentile − 1.5*interquartile range (IQR). The upper whisker extends to the largest datum that is smaller than 75th percentile + 1.5*IQR. For all other box plots, the whiskers are drawn down to the 10th percentile and up to the 90th percentile. Data points outside the Tukey or 10th–90th percentile range are not shown in the graphs to facilitate data presentation but were included in statistical analyses. In Figure 3B, statistical significance was evaluated using simple linear regression, which tests whether the slopes of the linear regression lines for mutants compared to controls are significantly different from each other. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9 (GraphPad). Differences between groups were considered to be statistically significant if p < .05.

Results

Loss of zebrafish nf1 decreases sleep in a dose-dependent manner

A previous study used zinc finger nucleases to induce targeted mutations in the zebrafish nf1a and nf1b genes, which generated alleles with premature stop codons [29] (Figure 1A and B). The proteins encoded by these mutated genes retain their cysteine-serine-rich domain (CSRD) but lack their GRD, Sec14 and pleckstrin homology domain (SecPH), and C-terminal domain (CTD) (Figure 1B). These mutant proteins should fail to interact with Ras due to their lack of a GRD, which results in elevated Ras/MAPK signaling [29]. These mutant fish exhibit several hallmarks of the NF1 disorder, including defects in nervous system development, pigmentation, and learning and memory, as well as increased incidence of tumors in fish that also lack p53 [29]. nf1a−/−; nf1b−/− fish die between 7 and 10 dpf, similar to Nf1−/− mice [16, 17], which die early in life due to cardiovascular and neural crest defects. In contrast, zebrafish with only one WT allele of either nf1a or nf1b are viable, fertile, and lack gross developmental defects [29]. Thus, the NF1 gene dosage effect observed in the human NF1 disorder can be modeled in zebrafish to study sleep.

To test the hypothesis that NF1 regulates sleep in zebrafish, we analyzed sleep using the previously described zebrafish nf1a and nf1b mutants. Similar to a prior study [29], we found that nf1a; nf1b double homozygous mutants (ie, 0 WT nf1 alleles) die at 7–10 dpf. We were concerned that these fish may be unhealthy and exhibit abnormal behaviors when we perform sleep behavioral assays at 5-dpf. In contrast, nf1a−/−; nf1b+/- mutants, which have 1 WT nf1 allele, are viable and fertile, and lack obvious developmental or behavioral defects. As a result, we used nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish, which have 1 WT nf1 allele, as mutants, and compared them to siblings that have more than 1 WT nf1 allele. We found that nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish were significantly more active and slept less at night, with no significant differences during the day (Figure 1C, D, E and H), compared to nf1a+/−; nf1b+/+ sibling controls, which contain three WT nf1 alleles. Surprisingly, we found that the locomotor activity and sleep phenotypes were dose-dependent. nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− mutants (one WT nf1 allele) were 40%, 31%, and 16% more active, and slept 35%, 30%, and 22% less, than their nf1a+/−; nf1b+/+ (three WT nf1 alleles), nf1a+/−; nf1b+/− (two WT nf1 alleles), and nf1a−/−; nf1b+/+ (two WT nf1 alleles) sibling controls at night, respectively (Figure 1E and H). These effects were due to fewer and shorter sleep bouts (Figure 1I and J), again in an nf1 dose-dependent manner. nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish also had an increase in sleep latency (time to first sleep bout after lights off at night) and in waking activity (locomotor activity while awake). These results indicate that zebrafish nf1 mutants sleep less at night due to defects in both initiating and maintaining sleep, and are hyperactive when awake at night. Reduced and delayed sleep, as well as shorter sleep bouts, are also observed in NF1 patients [8–13], indicating that loss of NF1 has similar effects on sleep in zebrafish and humans.

Zebrafish nf1 mutants have a decreased arousal threshold and decreased sleep depth

Because nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− mutants sleep less at night, we asked whether they also exhibit changes in arousal levels. To assess arousal levels, we monitored behavioral responses to a mechano-acoustic stimulus over a range of 15 stimulus intensities at night [49]. We determined the fraction of animals that responded to each stimulus, constructed stimulus–response curves, and calculated the stimulus power that resulted in half-maximal responses (effective power 50 [EP50]) (Figure 2A). The EP50 of nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish (one WT nf1 allele) was 40% lower than that of nf1a+/−; nf1b+/− sibling controls (two WT nf1 alleles), demonstrating that nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish have a decreased arousal threshold. This observation is consistent with the decreased sleep at night in nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish (Figure 1).

We next assayed arousal levels when the animals were either awake or asleep. To do so, we applied a mechano-acoustic stimulus at a moderate intensity with an inter-stimulus interval of 5 min, which is a sufficient amount of time to allow the animals to enter the sleep state between stimulus presentations [51]. We found that nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish that were awake responded to the stimulus at the same frequency as nf1a+/−; nf1b+/− controls (Figure 2B, left), suggesting that nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish have normal levels of arousal when awake. In contrast, sleeping nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish responded to the stimulus at a significantly higher rate than sibling controls (Figure 2B, right), suggesting that nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish have a reduction in sleep depth.

Zebrafish nf1 mutants exhibit increased sleep in the morning

Another sleep disturbance often observed in NF1 patients is difficulty with sleep–wake transitions [8–13]. To determine whether this might also be the case for zebrafish nf1 mutants, we examined sleep during the first hour of the morning. We observed that while nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish awoke similarly to their nf1a+/−; nf1b+/− sibling controls when the lights turned on in the morning, nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish went back to sleep 32% more quickly (Figure 3A). In addition, nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish slept significantly more than their nf1a+/−; nf1b+/− sibling controls during the first hour of the morning (Figure 3B), but not at later times during the day (data not shown). These results suggest that the decreased nighttime sleep observed in nf1a−/−; nf1b+/− fish leads to increased sleep in the morning.

Zebrafish nf1 mutants have increased ERK phosphorylation at night

Given the role of NF1 as an inhibitor of Ras function, we expected to observe elevated Ras/MAPK signaling, and thus elevated levels of phosphorylated MAPK, in nf1 mutants compared to sibling controls. The level of MAPK (also known as ERK) phosphorylation can be assayed by immunohistochemistry using antibodies that are specific for the phosphorylated form of ERK (pERK), and for both phosphorylated and unphosphorylated ERK (total ERK, tERK) [53]. Using both antibodies together and taking the ratio of pERK/tERK signal allows visualization of ERK phosphorylation levels normalized to the amount of ERK protein in each cell. In addition, by comparing pERK/tERK in mutants to controls, one can identify cells with more or less normalized pERK levels in mutants relative to controls. Since zebrafish nf1 mutants have a sleep defect at night but not during the day, we assayed pERK levels at 3:00 pm, and at the equivalent of 3:00 am using fish raised on a reversed day:night light cycle (see Materials and Methods section). We observed higher levels of pERK/tERK throughout the brain of zebrafish with one WT nf1 allele compared to control fish with two WT nf1 alleles at night (Figure 4A′), consistent with previous observations of increased pERK levels in nf1 mutant zebrafish [20, 29]. However, during the day we observed less intense and more spatially restricted pERK/tERK labeling in fish with one WT nf1 allele compared to controls with two WT nf1 alleles (Figure 4A). Since pERK levels can indicate levels of neuronal activity [53], we hypothesized that this day/night difference in pERK levels might be due to higher levels of neuronal activity at night compared to the day in nf1 mutants compared to controls. We tested this hypothesis by performing HCR using probes specific for c-fos, an alternative indicator of neuronal activity [55]. We did not observe significant differences in the level or spatial pattern of c-fos expression in nf1 mutants compared to sibling controls during either the day (Figure 4B and B′) or night (Figure 4C and C′). Based on these observations, we conclude that there is widespread activation of Ras/MAPK in the brain of nf1 mutants compared to controls at night, which is reduced and more restricted during the day, without apparent differences in neuronal activity. These results suggest that circadian and/or sleep cues may affect Ras/MAPK activity, and are consistent with the restriction of the nf1 mutant sleep phenotype to the night.

NF1 signaling is not required for circadian regulation of behavior

The role of NF1 signaling in the circadian regulation of sleep is controversial. In Drosophila, loss of nf1 results in loss of behavioral circadian rhythms without altering the circadian oscillations of the clock genes period and timeless [21]. In contrast, sleep abnormalities in NF1 patients have not been associated with alterations in the circadian system [8–13], although human sleep studies performed in free-running conditions have not been reported, so this question has not been fully addressed. To determine whether the zebrafish nf1 mutant sleep phenotype is associated with defects in circadian rhythms, we entrained nf1 mutants and their sibling controls in 14:10 h LD cycles until 5-dpf, and then monitored their behavior in either constant dark (DD) (Figure 5) or constant light (LL) (Figure 6) free-running conditions that avoid the masking effects of light and dark on behavior. We observed no gross differences in circadian period length or phase of locomotor activity or sleep in either DD (Figure 5A and B) or LL (Figure 6A and B) in fish with one WT nf1 allele compared to siblings with two or three WT nf1 alleles, although we cannot rule out the presence of small differences. These observations suggest that NF1 signaling is not necessary for circadian regulation of sleep in zebrafish, consistent with human NF1 patients [8–13] but not with Drosophila nf1 mutants [21, 23, 26, 56].

These experiments also revealed a surprising effect of lighting conditions on nf1 mutant sleep phenotypes. While the zebrafish nf1 mutant sleep phenotype is restricted to the night under LD conditions (Figure 1C and D), we observed this phenotype during both subjective day and subjective night when fish were entrained in LD and then shifted to either DD or LL (Figures 5 and 6). We further examined the dependency of the nf1 mutant phenotype on circadian and lighting conditions by raising zebrafish in DD, which results in animals that lack overt behavioral or molecular circadian rhythms [57, 58]. We then tested fish with one, two, or three WT nf1 alleles in DD. We found that fish with 1 WT nf1 allele were continuously more active and slept less than sibling controls (Figure 7). The magnitude of the locomotor activity and sleep phenotypes was similar to that observed at night in LD (Figure 1), and was again due to fewer and shorter sleep bouts (Figure 7F, G, K and L), and hyperactivity while awake (Figure 7D and I). These results suggest that NF1 signaling regulates sleep in a manner that is independent of the circadian system, and that day:night cycles of light and darkness are required to restrict the nf1 mutant sleep phenotype to the night.

Discussion

This study establishes zebrafish as a model to explore the role of NF1 in regulating sleep. We found that zebrafish with a reduced number of WT nf1 alleles exhibit reduced and delayed sleep, as well as shorter sleep bouts, at night, similar to human NF1 patients [8–13] and Drosophila nf1 mutants [22–27]. There are two significant phenotypic differences between the zebrafish nf1 model and previously published animal models. First, while Drosophila nf1 mutants lack behavioral circadian rhythms [21], we found that behavioral circadian rhythms are intact in nf1 mutant zebrafish, similar to Nf1 mutant rodents [28], although our experiments do not rule out the possibility of subtle changes. One study observed blunted circadian variation of blood pressure in NF1 patients [59], but further studies are needed to determine whether the human NF1 disorder is associated with abnormal circadian rhythms. One possible explanation for the discrepancy between Drosophila and other models could relate to nf1 gene dosage. Approximately 90% of NF1 patients have a heterozygous mutation in NF1 [15, 60], and homozygous mutation of NF1 is likely lethal during human development, similar to mice [16, 17] and zebrafish [29]. Unlike these vertebrate systems, Drosophila nf1 homozygous mutants are viable and exhibit both sleep and circadian phenotypes, whereas heterozygous mutants lack these phenotypes [21, 22]. It is possible that defects in circadian rhythms require the complete loss of nf1 function, and are therefore not observed in humans or zebrafish that have one WT nf1 allele. Alternatively, a function for nf1 in regulating circadian rhythms in Drosophila may be absent in vertebrates. Second, rodent nf1 mutants have fragmented sleep, but the amount of time spent in NREM and REM sleep is unchanged from controls. In contrast, zebrafish nf1 mutants have both reduced sleep and shorter sleep bouts, although unlike the Drosophila and rodent models, zebrafish nf1 mutants also have fewer sleep bouts. However, the rodent study used mice whose neurons, astrocytes, and neuroglial precursors contained a homozygous Nf1 mutation, which is different from the whole-body heterozygous NF1 mutations in human NF1 patients, and the reduced number of WT nf1 alleles in the zebrafish model. This difference in genotype may account for the difference in phenotype, and it will be interesting to determine if sleep is reduced in Nf1 heterozygous mutant mice, similar to humans and zebrafish. Taken together, our observations suggest that zebrafish nf1 mutants are a useful model that faithfully recapitulates the gene dosage and sleep phenotypes observed in humans with the NF1 disorder. The similarity of nf1 mutant sleep phenotypes in Drosophila, zebrafish, mice, and humans suggests that NF1 participates in an ancient sleep regulatory mechanism.

Our study also helps to clarify a longstanding question in the etiology of the NF1 disorder. Since the disorder is associated with the development of tumors, cardiovascular abnormalities, neurodevelopmental defects, sleep-disordered breathing, seizures, pain, psychiatric disorders, and medications used to treat these symptoms [3–7, 11, 14], all of which may impact sleep, it has been unclear whether the disrupted sleep observed in NF1 is a secondary consequence of these factors, or is a direct result of reduced NF1 levels. Zebrafish with one WT allele of nf1 show disrupted sleep at 5-dpf, a developmental stage prior to the establishment of complex behaviors [61], and lack significant developmental defects or tumors [29]. These observations, together with Drosophila [22–27] and rodent studies [28], suggest that disrupted sleep in NF1 patients directly results from reduced NF1 function, rather than being an indirect result of other deficits.

With the establishment of a zebrafish nf1 model that recapitulates the sleep defects observed in humans with the NF1 disorder, zebrafish can now be used to determine the mechanisms through which nf1 regulates sleep. NF1 is a large protein that contains several domains that can interact with other proteins, including heparan sulfate proteoglycans [62], caveolin-1 [63], tubulin [64], kinesin-1 [65], protein kinase A (PKA) [66], protein kinase C [67], and amyloid precursor protein [68]. Perhaps the best-characterized domain is the GRD, which stimulates the GTPase activity of the Ras protein, and thus inhibits Ras-mediated signaling. Loss-of-function mutations in the Ras/MAPK pathway rescue the circadian and sleep phenotypes in Drosophila nf1 mutants [21, 22, 24], suggesting that nf1 regulates circadian rhythms and sleep via Ras/MAPK signaling. However, Ras/MAPK signaling has been shown to promote sleep in C. elegans [69, 70], Drosophila [71, 72], zebrafish [46], and mice [73, 74]. If nf1 regulates sleep by inhibiting Ras, then nf1 mutants should sleep more, similar to activation of Ras/MAPK signaling. However, nf1 mutants sleep less, similar to loss of Ras/MAPK signaling, suggesting that the nf1 mutant sleep phenotype is not due to loss of its ability to inhibit Ras. It has been proposed that GAP proteins such as NF1 might simultaneously inhibit Ras and act as a Ras effector that links Ras to downstream signaling proteins [75]. Indeed, an effector function for p120-RasGAP that promotes Ras signaling has been demonstrated [76, 77]. This possibility is consistent with the observation that loss of either nf1 function or Ras signaling results in decreased sleep. Alternatively, NF1 could affect sleep via other pathways. Consistent with this possibility, missense mutations in NF1 patients are present throughout the gene [3–6, 15, 60], suggesting that domains other than the GRD, and interactions with proteins other than Ras, may play roles in the disorder. For example, Drosophila, mouse, and NF1 patient-derived neural cell models indicate that nf1 regulates cAMP signaling both via its effects on Ras [78, 79] and by linking GPCR activation to stimulation of adenylyl cyclase in a Ras-independent manner [79, 80]. An example of the latter case is that Drosophila nf1 mutants have reduced body size, which is rescued by stimulating cAMP/PKA signaling but not by perturbation of Ras [81]. Similarly, increasing cAMP/PKA signaling rescues learning deficits, but not memory deficits, in nf1 mutant zebrafish [30]. Decreased cAMP has been hypothesized to underlie NDDs in NF1 patients [80] and might also underlie NF1-associated sleep phenotypes. However, cAMP/PKA signaling has been shown to suppress sleep in Drosophila [56, 82, 83], zebrafish [84], and rodents [85–87], which is inconsistent with the hypothesis that NF1 promotes sleep by stimulating cAMP/PKA signaling.

NF1 may also affect sleep via neuromodulators that regulate sleep. For example, several lines of evidence indicate that mutation of nf1 results in impaired dopamine signaling. NF1 patient-derived neural progenitor cells have reduced dopamine levels, and a dose-dependent relationship between NF1 level, dopamine signaling, and cognitive deficits was observed in Nf1 mutant mice [88]. In addition, Nf1 mutant mice have reduced striatal dopamine and attention defects that are normalized by treatment with drugs that promote dopamine signaling (eg, methylphenidate) [89], and nf1 mutant Drosophila show hyperactivity at night due to impaired dopaminergic signaling that is rescued by treatment with methylphenidate [90]. Since dopamine has well-established effects on sleep [91], NF1 may affect sleep via perturbation of dopaminergic systems. Another possibility is that nf1 mutants sleep less at night because nf1 is required for serotonin (5HT) signaling. In support of this hypothesis, loss of the serotonergic raphe in both zebrafish and mice results in reduced sleep [51], similar to nf1 mutants. In addition, the ability of 5HT receptors to activate adenylyl cyclase, and thus increase cAMP levels, requires nf1 [79], 5HT receptor 6 (5HTR6) can directly bind to NF1 [92], and NF1 patient mutations in the NF1 PH domain disrupt this interaction and prevent activation of adenylyl cyclase by 5HTR6 [92]. Additional studies are needed to determine the mechanisms through which NF1 regulates sleep, and the amenability of zebrafish to genetic and pharmacological perturbations makes it a useful model for this purpose.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations to this study. First, like any animal model, zebrafish cannot perfectly model a human disorder. One limitation of larval zebrafish is that they are too small to perform polysomnography, and behavioral measures are instead used to identify sleep and wake states [43–45]. However, behavioral measures are increasingly used to quantify sleep in mammals [32–38], with a high level of concordance with EEG/EMG measures of sleep. Zebrafish also lack some brain structures that have roles in mammalian sleep, such as the cerebral cortex [93]. However, neuropeptidergic and neuromodulatory systems in the hypothalamus and brainstem that have important roles in sleep are well-conserved in larval zebrafish [43–45]. It is also possible that sleep in the young animals used in our study differs from sleep in adults. However, larval zebrafish are fully independent, have anatomical and neurochemical features found in adults, and genetic and pharmacological perturbations that affect sleep in adult mammals have similar effects on larval zebrafish [43–45], suggesting that most observations in larval zebrafish will be relevant to adult mammals. Second, nf1 genetic experiments are more complicated in zebrafish than in mammals since there are two zebrafish paralogs for the single mammalian NF1 gene [29]. However, the presence of two orthologs in zebrafish allowed us to uncover a dose-dependent relationship between the level of NF1 function and sleep. Third, there is significant phenotypic variability among NF1 patients, likely due to different mutations in the NF1 gene that have different effects on protein function [15, 60]. The zebrafish nf1a and nf1b mutants used in our study encode for truncated proteins that lack most of the known NF1 protein functional domains [29], and the use of only these mutant alleles does not allow exploration of NF1 phenotypic diversity using zebrafish. However, using mutants with a single, defined mutation facilitates detailed analysis of gene function, and modeling the effects of specific mutations found in NF1 patients can be pursued in future studies. Finally, the mechanisms through which NF1 regulates sleep in vertebrates remain unclear and require further study.
