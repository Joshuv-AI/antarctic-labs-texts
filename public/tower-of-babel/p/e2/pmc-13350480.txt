Impact of fortifiers on donor milk nutrient composition: An experimental study
Authors: Kimberly Mansen, Hannah Sanders, Sarah N. Taylor, Raven Dunstan, Kiersten Israel‐Ballard, Maryanne T. Perrin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm infants frequently require human milk fortification to meet their unique nutrients requirements. How commercial fortifiers change essential macronutrients and micronutrients in donor human milk has not been well‐studied.MethodsOur sampling frame included milk from approved United States milk bank donors (n = 400), measured for macronutrients, vitamins, and minerals, and used to produce 1000 pools of donor milk. Fortification ratios were computed to describe the extent that fortifiers changed donor milk composition (<100% indicates decreased; >100% indicates increased). Intake ratios were computed to evaluate how unfortified and fortified donor milk at 160 mL/kg/day compared to upper and lower preterm nutrient recommendations.ResultsFortifiers varied greatly in their impact on individual donor milk nutrients, with final macronutrient concentrations between 75% and 399% of unfortified values, while vitamin and mineral concentrations were between 50% and 800+% of unfortified values. Unfortified donor milk was able to meet the lower intake recommendations for three nutrients—lactose, fat, and pantothenic acid—highlighting the importance of utilizing multi‐nutrient fortifiers. Bovine‐derived fortifiers frequently did not meet lower targets for protein, choline, and several minerals, while human‐derived fortifiers did not meet lower targets for most B‐vitamins and choline, and exceeded some targets including protein, fat, calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium.ConclusionFortification products available in the United States, when used with donor milk, frequently lead to both deficient and excessive nutrient intake compared to preterm enteral nutrition recommendations. Development of fortifier products based on robust nutritional reference values for donor milk is warranted to improve the nutritional care of preterm infants.

INTRODUCTION

Human milk is recommended over infant formula for feeding preterm and low birth weight infants, due to evidence that it protects against necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Fortification of human milk—including mother's milk and donor human milk (DHM) —may be needed to prevent extrauterine growth faltering in some infants including those born very preterm (<32 weeks) or very low birth weight (<1500 g). 1 , 5 , 6 Historically, preterm infants fed DHM versus mother's milk or preterm infant formula have shown slower growth 7 , 8 , 9 ; however, more recently a large randomized controlled trial showed adequate growth with DHM when it was fortified assuming a low baseline protein concentration of 0.8–0.9 g/dL protein. 10 , 11

Contemporary research on human milk fortification has focused on the practical aspects of implementing fortification in a clinical setting including when to begin fortification (e.g., early or delayed), what type of fortifier to use (e.g., bovine milk‐derived vs. human milk‐derived), and whether fortification protocols should be standardized or individualized. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Limited research has evaluated the nutritional adequacy of commercially available fortifiers at meeting nutrient recommendations for the preterm infant, with explicit consideration to the underlying type of milk being used (mother's milk vs. DHM). For example, a study on unfortified and fortified DHM (n = 300) evaluated only sodium concentrations and reported that only one fortifier regularly met the lower recommendations from European Society of Paediatric Gastroenterology Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) for sodium, and no fortifiers met the upper sodium recommendations. 16

Manufacturers' nutritional estimates for commercial fortifiers are often based on mixing with the assumed nutrient composition of preterm human milk. 17 The final concentration of essential macronutrients and micronutrients in fortified DHM has not been well‐studied. Therefore, the primary aims of this study were (1) to broadly characterize how DHM nutritional composition (macronutrients, vitamins, minerals) is altered with common commercial fortification products available in the United States and (2) to evaluate the effectiveness of fortification products at achieving enteral nutrient recommendations for preterm infants when used with DHM.

METHODS

We conducted a simulated experimental study that computed the theoretical nutritional values of fortified donor human milk using a cross‐sectional set of donor human milk samples that were collected as part of the Global Donor Human Milk Study, 18 and nutritional information about commercial human milk fortifier products currently available in clinical settings in the US. This study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at the University of North Carolina Greensboro and classified as non‐human subjects research (protocol IRB‐FY21‐68).

Donor human milk samples

The nutrient profiles of milk from approved donors (n = 400) from four milk banks in the Human Milk Banking Association of North America (HMBANA) network served as the underlying sampling frame for this study: Mothers' Milk Bank of the Western Great Lakes (Elk Grove Village, Illinois, USA; n = 100); Northwest Mothers Milk Bank (Tigard, Oregon, USA; n = 100); Mid‐Atlantic Mothers' Milk Bank provided through the Human Milk Science Institute and Biobank (Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA; n = 100); and Mothers' Milk Bank of North Texas (Benbrook, Texas, USA; n = 100). 19 Each sample represented milk from a unique, single donor pool that had been collected over at least 3 days, which is reflective of production processes for DHM. Most donors (91%) had given birth at term and had donated mature milk (average 15–21 weeks postpartum). 19 HMBANA milk banks typically pool milk between multiple donors to help create more consistent nutrient profiles; therefore, we used the nutritional profile of 1000 theoretical pools of DHM created by simulating the random pooling of five donors and subject to Holder pasteurization (Table 1). Details of this simulation have been published elsewhere, 20 as have the analytical techniques for measuring macronutrients, vitamins, and minerals. 19

Identification of fortification products

The selection of fortification products was guided by informal discussions with clinicians who work in US neonatal intensive care settings and through an internet search of human milk fortifier products. We identified 19 fortification protocols to include in our analysis, with each protocol involving a specific product mixed at a specific volume with human milk. Our analysis included 14 protocols using liquid multi‐nutrient fortifiers derived from either bovine milk or human milk, three protocols using powdered multi‐nutrient fortifiers derived from bovine milk, and one protocol using a single nutrient fortifier (fat) derived from human milk. When a manufacturer included multiple mixing protocols to achieve different energy densities, all protocols were included. Because powdered infant formula may be used to fortify human milk in low‐resource settings during periods of shortages and post‐discharge, 21 , 22 , 23 we also included an infant formula product which was selected based on its availability through the Women, Infants and Children program, 24 and mixed at 1/2 teaspoon infant formula powder to three ounces of DHM. A list of fortification protocols included in the analysis can be found in Figure 1.

Computing how fortification alters donor human milk

To characterize how fortification protocols (product + mixing proportions) altered DHM, the nutrient composition of fortifier products, including macronutrients, minerals and vitamins, were recorded in Excel per manufacturer labels and serving size (as of August 2024). Nutrient values that were labeled as “not a significant source” or not listed were assumed to be zero. For values reported as less than a given number, that number was used in the calculations as an estimate since the actual value was unknown. Values were then standardized to 1 g for powdered products, and 5 mL for liquid products. Energy values were calculated using the Atwater formula for metabolizable energy rather than using the manufacturer label. 25

To determine the final nutrient values in DHM after each fortification protocol, we used the manufacturers' mixing instructions and the recorded nutritional values of the fortifier to compute the blended nutrient composition of average DHM (Table 1) plus fortifier in a standardized total of 100 mL. We then compared the nutrient values of fortified DHM to that of unfortified DHM by computing a fortification ratio (fortified DHM/unfortified DHM × 100) for each nutrient. Thus, a fortification ratio >100% indicated an increase in nutrient concentration after fortification while a fortification ratio <100% indicated a decrease in nutrient concentration after fortification. Fortification ratios are intended to describe how much fortifiers change “average” DHM to allow for an initial comparison between fortifiers. They are not to be interpreted as reflecting nutritional adequacy, deficiency, or toxicity, but simply the degree of change. Calculations were reviewed by two researchers to ensure accuracy. Fortification ratios by nutrient are presented visually as a heat map.

Computing if fortified DHM meets preterm recommendations

There are no globally agreed upon nutrient recommendations for the preterm infant; therefore, we included recommendations from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), 26 ESPGHAN, 27 and the Nutritional Care of Preterm Infants (2nd edition). 28 Table 2 provides a summary of the low and high recommendation for each nutrient from each source. Most of the recommended nutrient ranges did not differ across the three sources. When they did, we used the minimum low value and the maximum high value to reflect the full spectrum for our analysis.

The total daily nutrient intake was computed for each DHM fortification protocol, assuming an intake of 160 mL/kg/day, and using the average, 10th percentile, and 90th percentile values for DHM nutrient composition to present a more robust distribution of expected DHM nutrient values (Table 1). An intake ratio was computed as a ratio of actual intake to the lower recommendation, such that a ratio of <1.0 would indicate that the fortification protocol did not meet the lower recommendation value. A ratio of the high recommendation to the low recommendation was also calculated and reflected the maximum intake considered safe. Fortified DHM nutrient intake ratios were plotted against the composite lower and higher ranges of recommendations for each nutrient and assessed visually. All analyses and visualization were completed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation).

RESULTS

How fortification alters donor human milk

Figure 1 provides information on the percentage of DHM used in each fortification protocol and a heat map of the fortification ratios which reflect how energy and nutrients change after fortification. Thus, Figure 1 visually describes how a wide range of nutrients change within a fortifier (across a row of the heat map) and between fortifiers (down a column of the heat map). For example, between fortifiers sodium values range from 100% to 149% to 600% to 799% of unfortified values. Fortification protocols contained between 50% to 98% DHM by volume. Overall, energy and fat increased in DHM using all fortification protocols, with most fortified calories and fat reaching 100%–150% of unfortified values. Protein also increased with all fortification protocols, with most fortifiers enhancing protein to 200%–299% of unfortified values, though some fortifiers were above and below this range. Lactose declined with some fortification protocols (75%–99%) and increased with others (100%–149%) compared to unfortified values. Overall, the fortification ratios in vitamins and minerals were much more varied than the changes in macronutrients and energy. For example, the iron concentration after fortification ranged from 50% to 99% to >800% of unfortified values. Most B‐vitamins declined (<100%) with the human milk‐derived fortifiers, while B‐vitamins increased from 100% to 149% to >800% of unfortified values with the bovine milk‐derived fortifiers. Overall, while most nutrients increased to some degree with fortification, there were nutrients that decreased in some fortification protocols including carbohydrates, iron, selenium, B‐vitamins, choline, and vitamin E (Figure 1).

Bovine‐derived fortifiers mixed at less concentrated volumes (50 mL of DHM) were able to regularly meet lower energy recommendations but were not able to achieve lower protein recommendations (Figure 2B,D). At the more concentrated volumes (25 mL of DHM), bovine‐derived fortifiers met lower true protein recommendations 50% of the time or less; were regularly below lower recommendations for sodium, zinc, copper, selenium, and choline; and were above upper recommendations for magnesium (Figure 2C,E). While most vitamins fell in the recommended ranges with the bovine‐derived fortifiers, there were exceptions including concentrations of niacin above upper recommendations, and concentrations of vitamin A below lower recommendations with some fortifiers (Figure 2E).

Human‐derived fortifiers met lower energy requirements (Figure 2F,G) and exceeded them at more concentrated protocols (Figure 2H). Protein targets were not met with the least concentrated human‐derived fortifier (Figure 2F), met with moderate fortifier (Figure 2G) and exceeded with the most concentrated fortifier (Figure 2H). The concentrated human‐derived fortifier also exceeded upper recommendations for fat, sodium, calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium (Figure 2H). Choline, vitamin A and E, and many B‐vitamins were less than 50% of the lower recommendations with the human‐derived fortifier protocols (Figure 2F–H).

DISCUSSION

In this comprehensive analysis of nutrients in fortified DHM, we found that fortification products commonly used in the US have a wide range of impact on DHM nutrients highlighting major differences in fortifier formulations. We observed more variability in the degree of change to final micronutrient concentrations than to final macronutrient concentrations. Of the 22 nutrients we assessed (Figure 2), fortifiers were able to reach target recommendations for 10–15 of the nutrients and exceeded target recommendations for 0–7 of the nutrients. These nutrient deficits and excesses may contribute to poor growth or metabolic outcomes, particularly in the smallest preterm infants who receive the majority of feeds from fortified DHM.

Most research into multi‐nutrient fortification of human milk has focused on infant health outcomes, with a Cochrane review reporting better in‐hospital growth, but no evidence of long‐term improvements. 29 Few studies have considered how commercial fortifier products alter the nutrients in human milk. Koo et al. evaluated five fortification protocols (one human‐derived fortifier, two bovine‐derived fortifiers, and two preterm formulas) compared to AAP's preterm recommendations for macronutrients, vitamin, and minerals and reported deficient and excessive intake of several nutrients with all fortifiers, which agrees with our findings. 30 Their analysis used full feeds of 150 mL/kg/day and the manufacturer's nutrient estimates for human milk, which is often assumed to be preterm mother's milk and may not be generalizable to DHM. Adamkin presented six fortification protocols (two human‐derived and four bovine‐derived) using published values for preterm mother's milk and feeding volumes of 160 mL/kg/day for a limited number of nutrients (fat, protein, energy, calcium, phosphorus, sodium, and iron). 31 Variations between fortifiers were described, with most not meeting lower targets for sodium and iron, which agrees with our findings. Importantly, Koo and Adamkin described fortification protocols as typically meeting protein requirements when used with assumed nutritional values for preterm mother's milk. This is in sharp contrast with our findings of both inadequate and excess protein when commercial fortification protocols are used with DHM, emphasizing the importance of evaluating fortification approaches based on different underlying milk types.

Prior studies on nutrients in fortified DHM have mostly focused on single nutrients. John et al. evaluated three high‐energy fortification protocols (resulting in +4 to +10 kcal/ounce) with milk from approved milk bank donors (n = 443) to a single milk bank and reported that fortifiers frequently did not meet protein targets, with some fortifiers providing excess protein and others inadequate protein at goal feeding volumes of 150–200 mL/kg/day. 32 Gates et al. evaluated three fortification protocols (one human‐derived fortifier and two bovine‐derived fortifiers mixed using higher energy protocols) for vitamin A in pooled DHM (n = 15). 33 They reported that vitamin A intake at 150 mL/kg/day would vary between 200 and 1600 IU/kg/day, with two of the fortifier protocols unable to meet ESPGHAN's minimum requirements for vitamin A. Finally, a study evaluating three fortification protocols (one human‐derived fortifier and two bovine‐derived fortifiers) with HMBANA DHM (n = 300) reported that the bovine‐derived fortifiers were not able to reach lower sodium goals. 16

The prior DHM fortification studies discussed above are all in agreement with current findings from our study that the nutrients in fortified DHM vary greatly by fortifier product and do not fully align with preterm recommendations, creating a compelling case for development of a fortifier based on robust nutritional values for DHM. In addition, recent evidence of metabolic abnormalities when utilizing different fortifiers 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 highlights the need for further research to understand how commercial fortification products alter the nutrient intake of preterm infants differentially by base milk type.

There were several strengths to our study. Notably, and as previously emphasized, findings are highly generalizable to DHM use in the US because we used a large dataset of human milk from approved milk bank donors from four HMBANA milk banks (n = 400) that took into account the impact of pooling and pasteurization. Given the lack of global standards for preterm infant nutrition, our study combined three sets of nutrient recommendations when evaluating DHM. Finally, we assessed a broad number of nutrients including macronutrients, vitamins, and minerals, where prior DHM studies had focused on single nutrients. There were several limitations to this study. Fortifiers were identified through internet search and informal discussions and might not reflect all fortifiers currently available. The final fortified values were calculated based on manufacturer's nutrient profiles of each fortifier product and not actually measured. Additionally, given the lack of standardized global nutrient references, our target comparisons are only as good as the broad range nutrient references for preterm infants. The nutrient recommendations used in this study were for infants weighing less than 1500–1800 g, so these findings may not be generalizable to larger preterm infants. There were a small number of essential nutrients that we did not assess including folate, vitamin C, vitamin K, and vitamin D. Finally, our findings might not be generalizable to DHM produced using milk banking models that differ from US non‐profit milk banks where donations reflect predominantly mature milk from term donors, and the milk of multiple donors is pooled together before Holder pasteurization.

CONCLUSION

When used with donor human milk, bovine‐derived fortifiers available in the US frequently did not meet lower targets for protein, choline, and several minerals, while human‐derived fortifiers did not meet lower enteral nutrition targets for most B‐vitamins and choline, and exceeded some targets including protein, fat, calcium, phosphorus and magnesium. This could potentially contribute to poor growth or metabolic outcomes, particularly in preterm infants who receive the majority of feeds from fortified DHM. Development of fortifier products based on robust nutritional reference values for DHM is warranted to optimize the nutritional care of preterm infants.
