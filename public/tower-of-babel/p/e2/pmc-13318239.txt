Biphasic Extraction of Selected Heavy Metals From Corn With the Aid of Kosmotropic Salts
Authors: Vhuhwavho Masindi, Shirley Selahle, Tebogo Mokgehle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study investigated the use of aqueous two‐phase extraction (ATPE) with kosmotropic salts for the extraction of toxic heavy metals from maize. Heavy metals, such as cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr) and iron (Fe), are harmful to human health because they accumulate in the food chain and may cause kidney damage, cancer and nervous system problems. This study tested copper sulphate (CuSO4) and magnesium sulphate (MgSO4) as extracting agents under different conditions of salt concentration, solvent volume and maize mass. The extracted metals were analysed using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS). Results showed that CuSO4 was significantly more effective than MgSO4, with the highest extraction achieved for cadmium, followed by chromium, and the least for iron. Response surface methodology (RSM) analysis confirmed that medium salt concentrations (30%–35%) and solvent volumes of 7–8 mL gave the best results. Fitting central composite design response model to the data generated a quadratic model with fits of R 2 = 0.90, R 2 = 0.90 and R 2 = 0.92 for Fe(I), Cr(III), and Cd, respectively. As we use environmental method (ATPE) to minimize those heavy metals (Cr(III), Cd(II) and Fe(I)) from corn, then corn can still be edible after exposure to ATPE because only water and ethanol are used. Comparison with international food safety standards revealed that Fe and Cr levels in maize were well within World Health Organization (WHO) limits. However, Cd(II) was found at 0.328 mg kg−1, which is higher than both the WHO limit (0.2 mg kg−1) and the stricter South African standard (0.05 mg kg−1). This indicates a serious food safety risk. The study concludes that ATPE with CuSO4 is a promising, eco‐friendly and low‐cost method for extracting heavy metals from maize. However, further optimization and the use of complementary agents are recommended to improve cadmium extraction and ensure safe consumption.

Introduction

The contamination of crops with heavy toxic metals has become a major concern for environmental safety and public health [1]. Industrial activities such as urban runoff, wastewater irrigation and the excessive use of chemical fertilizers have contributed significantly to the accumulation of heavy metals in soil and water systems [2]. These metals, including Fe(I), Cd(II) and Cr(III), are persistent in the environment and non‐biodegradable, which means they tend to accumulate over time [3]. The persistence of these heavy metals facilitates their adsorption by crops, posing severe health risks to both humans and animals. These health risks include, amongst others, kidney damage, nervous system disorders and even cancer with prolonged exposure [4, 5]. Therefore, the presence of heavy metals in edible crops presents a critical challenge for food safety and environmental sustainability.

Corn, a widely grown crop in backyards near roads and industrial sites, is vulnerable to heavy metal pollution. Additionally, its extensive high surface area root system facilitates effortless absorption of nutrients and heavy metals from contaminated soils [6, 7]. Various extraction methods have been studied to minimize heavy metal pollution. Existing methods for extracting and analysing heavy metals from plant materials, including wet digestion [8], microwave acid extraction [9] and other chemical‐based techniques, often involve the use of strong acids or organic solvents [10]. Although these methods can be effective in breaking down plant matrices and releasing metal ions, they are associated with several drawbacks. First, the use of harsh chemicals poses safety and environmental concerns due to the generation of hazardous waste [11]. Second, the aggressive nature of these methods can damage the plant sample structure or cause the loss of target metals through volatilization or complexation [8]. This can lead to inaccurate quantification and unreliable results. Moreover, such methods are often time‐consuming and labour‐intensive, making them less suitable for routine analysis in resource‐limited settings.

To address these challenges, there is a growing interest in developing safer, greener and more selective extraction techniques for heavy metal analysis in food and agricultural samples. One promising alternative is the use of aqueous two‐phase extraction (ATPE) systems, which offer a milder and environmentally friendly approach enabling analytes to be extracted at room temperature minimizing structural damage to samples. ATPE applies two liquid layers comprised of a hydrophobic and hydrophilic layer in conjunction with polymers that enable selective partitioning of analytes and enhance extraction efficiency through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions [12, 13]. ATPE offers several advantages over other conventional methods, including low toxicity, biodegradability, operational simplicity and the potential for metal ion pre‐concentration.

Despite the potential benefits, the application of kosmotropic salt‐assisted aqueous two‐phase systems for extracting heavy metals from corn samples remains underexplored. Most studies on ATPE have focused on bio‐separation of proteins, enzymes or pharmaceutical compounds, with relatively limited research on its use for metal ion extraction in complex plant matrices. A few studies looked at extraction of metals using polyethylene glycol ATPE systems [14, 15]. Corn presents a particularly challenging matrix due to its fibrous structure and the presence of other organic compounds that can interfere with extraction efficiency. Therefore, it is essential to investigate whether kosmotropic salts can effectively extract heavy metals from corn and to what extent variables, such as salt concentration, extraction solvent volume and sample mass, influence the extraction process. Therefore, this work aims to develop and evaluate an ATPE method using green solvents (water and ethanol) in the presence of kosmotropic salts for the extraction of toxic heavy metals (Fe(I), Cr(III) and Cd(II)) from corn. This work is also directed at determining which salts between MgSO4 and CuSO4 will be a better extractor of Fe(I), Cr(III) and Cd(II) from corn in an ATPE system.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals Reagents and Equipment

All salts, MgSO4 (analytical grade ≥99%) purity and CuSO4 (analytical grade ≥99% purity), were bought from Glassworld (Johannesburg, South Africa). An organic solvent, ethanol (99% CP), was purchased from Associated Chemical Enterprises (Burlington, Germany). Water was used for the preparation of the salt's solution. Corn seeds were obtained from Muledane (South Africa) and ground into a fine powder using a traditional grinder (Mutuli), and the powder was accurately weighed using a digital balance (model: PS 4500/C2 and serial number 392435/13).

Analytical Methods

The extraction process took place in centrifuge tubes using kosmotropic salts: magnesium sulphate (MgSO4) and copper sulphate (CuSO4) combined with ethanol as the organic solvent and water as the aqueous phase. The required volumes of water, ethanol and salts were measured using measuring cylinders (50 mL), and the mixtures were vigorously shaken using a mini orbital shaker, Stuart (serial number: 210001229), to enhance the interaction between the extraction agents and the corn samples. After settling, phase separation occurred, and the upper layer (ethanol‐rich phase), which contained the extracted heavy metals, was carefully collected into clean vials. These vials were used to hold the metal‐containing solutions before being analysed. The concentrations of Cd(II), Cr(III) and Fe(I) in the samples were then determined using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), employing 220 series models (serial number: 20CAD91) for accurate and reliable metal detection. Beakers and watch glasses were also used during handling and transfer processes throughout the procedure.

Quantitative Parameters

Once the corn was ground, it was passed through a homemade sieve to obtain a fine, uniform corn powder. In this study parameters such as percentage salt of MgSO4 and CuSO4, mass of corn and volume of ethanol were measured. Salt percentages of 30, 35 and 40 were prepared in 50 mL centrifuge tubes which translated to concentrations of 15 g/50 mL, 18 g/50 mL and 20 g/50 mL, respectively. The salt solutions were then mixed with the corn powder, where masses of 0.25, 0.625 and 1 g were used to create a suspension mixture where solid particles were dispersed in an aqueous solution containing either MgSO4 or CuSO4. This mixture was then placed on a mechanical shaker for 60 min. After shaking, an organic solvent (ethanol) was added to the aqueous solution, forming two phases, where the volumes of ethanol added were 5, 7 and 10 mL. Thereafter, the solution was left undisturbed for 18 h. The ethanol layer was removed and transferred into small, labelled vials which were then analysed using the AAS.

Results and Discussion

Evaluating Significant and Insignificant Parameters in the Extraction of Heavy Metals

Figure 1 Pareto charts show the parameters studied during the extraction of chromium (Cr), iron (Fe) and cadmium (Cd) in the presence of CuSO4 and MgSO4 as an extraction agent. From the trends, it is evident that CuSO4‐based extraction Figure 1a,c,e generally shows a stronger influence of % salt (A) and extraction volume (B) compared to corn mass (C), particularly in the case of Fe(I) and Cd(II) where factor A dominates the process. For Fe(I) extraction with CuSO4 (a), factor A has the largest effect, whereas B and C appear less influential. In contrast, MgSO4‐based extractions (b, d, and f) display more balanced effects, with combinations of factors (such as C 2, B 2 and A 2) contributing significantly to the outcomes. For example, in MgSO4–Cr(III) (d) and MgSO4–Cd(II) (f), both extraction volume (B) and corn mass (C) exhibit substantial influence alongside salt concentration. This highlights that although CuSO4 acts more strongly through salt concentration as the dominant factor, MgSO4 shows a broader sensitivity to multiple parameters, suggesting it provides a more distributed but less sharply dominant effect on extraction efficiency. Overall, the comparative analysis indicated that CuSO4 was more influential in driving extraction primarily through salt concentration. This also indicated that larger salt concentrations enhanced phase separation and metal partitioning as the more hydrophilic salt‐rich phase facilitated the transfer of metal species into the organic ethanol layer [16].

Pareto charts show the influence of the parameters (A, B and C) on extraction of Fe (a) and (b), Cr (c) and (d), and Cd (e) and (f) in the presence of CuSO4 and MgSO4 being applied as extracting agents.

The concentration gradient between the aqueous phase and the ethanol‐rich organic phase plays a significant role in controlling the diffusion rate of heavy metal ions such as Cr3+, Cd2+ and Fe2+/Fe3+, resulting in a process of salting out. Initially, these metal ions exist in the aqueous layer as fully hydrated complexes, typically in the form [M(H2O)6] n +, where they are tightly surrounded by structured shells of water molecules. This hydration makes them highly polar and energetically favourable in the aqueous phase, effectively preventing them from diffusing into the less polar ethanol‐rich organic layer. As a result, a steep concentration gradient forms metal ions concentrated highly in the aqueous phase and zero in the ethanol phase, but the strong hydration shells function as a kinetic barrier, inhibiting significant mass transfer [17].

When kosmotropic salts, like MgSO4 or CuSO4, are added to the system, they dissociate and release ions (e.g., Mg2+, Cu2+ and SO4 2−) that have a higher affinity for water than the analyte metal ions themselves. These ions compete with the analyte metals for water molecules, partially stripping away their hydration shells, this occurs more for copper compared to magnesium due to its higher charge density [18]. This process reduces the polarity and aqueous solubility of the analyte metal ions, making them more chemically compatible with the ethanol phase [18]. In other cases, the metal ions form less polar species such as ion pairs or neutral complexes, [M(SO4)]+, which can dissolve in ethanol. Once this chemical transformation occurs, the steep concentration gradient between the aqueous and ethanol phases becomes an active thermodynamic driving force, allowing the less‐polar metal complexes to diffuse from the region of high concentration (aqueous phase) to the region of low concentration (ethanol phase) [19]. Equilibrium is then reached when the chemical potential of the metal species is balanced between the phases and net transfer stops. Therefore, maintaining a strong initial concentration difference, combined with salt‐induced chemical modification of the metal ions, is essential for efficient and rapid salting‐out extraction of Cr(II), Cd(III) and Fe(I) from the corn extract.

Figures 2 and 3 show the extraction of Cr(III), Cd(II) and Fe(I) as a function of percentage salt (30–40), extraction solvent volume (5–10 mL) and corn mass (0.25–1 g) when CuSO4 and MgSO4 were used as salting‐out agents, respectively. Both Figures 2 and 3 are colour‐coded, where the red portion of the graph indicated higher extraction, whereas the green section indicated intermediate extraction and the blue low extraction. For CuSO4, the highest Cr(III) extraction occurs at medium salt percentages around 30–34 and moderate extraction volumes of 7–8 mL (Figure 2b), with Cd(II) showing the comparable peaks (Figure 2c) and Fe(I) showing very low extracted concentrations (Figure 2e). At very low salt percentages (34–36), the extraction is weak because there is not enough ionic strength to release metals from the corn matrix (Figure 2e). Generally, for CuSO4 base extraction, at very high salt percentages (38–40), the extraction decreases again, likely due to salting‐out effects or reduced solubility of the metal complexes, which explains the curved surfaces of the response surface methodology (RSM) [20].

For MgSO4 (Figure 3a–d), the overall responses are lower, and the peaks are less pronounced; however, a similar trend is observed to that of CuSO4: Extraction improved from low salt levels up to around 30%–32%. The solvent volume also showed a trend where moderate volumes (6–8 mL) enhanced extraction (Figure 3d). At the same time, too low or too high volumes resulted in reduced metal extraction, possibly because insufficient solvent cannot fully extract the metals. In contrast, excessive solvent diluted the concentration gradient [21]. Mechanisms of Analyte Extraction by CuSO4 and MgSO4

Figure 5 illustrates the reaction mechanism for the salting out of Cd(II) from the aqueous phase into the ethanol phase using CuSO4 (Figure 5a) and the extraction of Cd(II) using MgSO4 (Figure 5b). The main differences between the extraction agents (MgSO4 and CuSO4) were the types of metals used. Copper has smaller atomic radius and higher charge density than magnesium [22]. The higher charge density of copper allows it to attract more water molecules compared to magnesium (Figure 5a) [22]. The reaction mechanism indicated that the hydration sphere around Cd(II) (analyte) is easily removed in the presence of CuSO4 (Figure 5a) as Cu has a lower hydration energy compared to Mg (Figure 5b). Therefore, CuSO4 promotes the transfer of Cd(II) from the aqueous to the ethanol phase (Figure 5a). In Figure 5b, the higher hydration energy of Mg makes it difficult to remove the water surrounding Cd(II) resulting in it, remaining in the aqueous phase. Cadmium also interacts with organic compounds that are derived from corn matrix, that interaction allows it to migrate into the ethanol phase, whereas for Mg, the interaction with water is minimal and hence Cd(II) is surrounded by the hydration sphere, resulting in it being trapped in the aqueous phase. Therefore, the salting‐out of the analytes studied is driven by the hydration behaviour of the CuSO4 or MgSO4. Additionally, the high concentration (concentration gradient) of the dehydrated Cd, in the presence of CuSO4 and its interaction with the organic compounds in the corn matrix drive it into the ethanol phase. Sivamani et al. [23] also reported on a similar trend where Re(VII) partitioned into the surfactant‐rich phase of the surfactant‐salt ATPE system and was driven by the concentration gradient and complexation.

Comparison of the Concentrations of the Heavy Metals Obtained to the World Health Organization (WHO) Permissible Limits

The concentration of heavy metals obtained from corn when CuSO4 was used as a salting‐out agent, as shown in Table 4, indicated that two of the three metals were within the WHO maximum permissible limits. Iron (Fe) was detected at 0.0018 mg kg−1, which was lower than the WHO permissible limit of 0.3 mg kg−1, suggesting minimal risk from iron accumulation in the sampled corn. Similarly, Cr(III) was found at 0.0061 mg kg−1, which was also below the WHO threshold of 0.1 mg kg−1, indicating safe levels with minimal health concern. However, Cd(II) was recorded at 0.328 mg kg−1, which is notably higher than the WHO maximum limit of 0.2 mg kg−1. This excess Cd(II) is concerning, considering its toxic and bioaccumulative property often resulting in kidney damage and other chronic health effects. The elevated level of Cd(II) suggests potential contamination in the growing environment, likely due to factors such as the use of contaminated water, fertilizers or soil, and may pose significant health risks if the sampled corn is consumed regularly.

Conclusion

This work evaluated ATPE for enrichment of Cr(III), Fe(I) and Cd(II) from corn. Two salts were studied (CuSO4 and MgSO4) as extracting agents. CuSO4 was significantly more effective in extracting Cr(III), Cd(II) and Fe(I) than MgSO4. The superior performance of CuSO4 was attributed to its ionic strength and concentration gradient, which facilitated a more efficient extraction process. Generally, for both salts, the order of extraction of the metals from the highest to the least was Cd(II) > Cr(III) > Fe(I). When the extracted metal levels were compared to the WHO permissible limits, both Fe(I) and Cr(III) were found to be within the prescribed limits. However, a major concern was the level of Cd(II), which was found to be 0.328 mg kg−1. This concentration exceeded the maximum permissible limits set by both the WHO (0.2 mg kg−1). This indicated a potential health risk from consuming this corn in the Muledane village, emphasizing the need for regular monitoring and remediation efforts to ensure food safety. It is also recommended that future studies explore the use of alternative eco‐friendly materials such as natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES), biochar and plant‐based adsorbents for heavy metal extraction.
