Training machine learning-based spirometry reference equations: a comparison with GAMLSS and GLI reference equations
Authors: Walid Al-Qerem, Anan Jarab, Judith Eberhardt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionInterpretation of spirometry data depends on the availability of reference equations that reflect the physiological norms of the assessed population. Although GAMLSS models provide clinically acceptable models, they may lack simplicity and ease of application. This study evaluated the efficiency of machine learning (ML)-based spirometry reference equations as an alternative for Jordanian adults.MethodIn this cross-sectional study, ML models were trained using age and height to predict FEV₁, FVC and FEV₁/FVC. Model development was based on the same datasets previously used to construct GAMLSS-based Jordanian equations, which included 1,948 participants (54.2% females). External validation was performed on a newly recruited sample of healthy, non-smoking adults (n = 487, 46.6% females).ResultsML predicted and lower limits of normal (LLNs) values were compared with those from the Jordanian GAMLSS, GLI equations, using z-score distributions, residual plots, and clinical diagnostic agreement. For both sexes, ML models consistently produced comparable mean squared errors (MSE) to the Jordanian GAMLSS equations and lower MSE values and z-scores closer to zero when compared with global reference equations. Agreement analyses revealed that the ML and Jordanian models more reliably classified individuals within ± 0.5 and ± 1.0 z-score thresholds, emphasizing their superior calibration. ML and Jordanian models were the only ones to classify all the healthy study sample as normal spirometry.ConclusionML-derived spirometry equations demonstrated strong alignment with the observed data and outperformed global standards in representing Jordanian adults. These findings support the use of reference equations customized for specific regions in respiratory diagnostics.

Introduction

Spirometry plays a key role in respiratory medicine, offering objective metrics of lung function through parameters such as forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), and FEV₁/FVC ratio [1]. These measurements are essential for diagnosing and managing various pulmonary conditions. Population-specific reference equations are required to accurately interpret spirometric data [2]. These equations enable individual measurements to be classified as normal or abnormal by comparing them with predicted values and lower limits of normal (LLNs) [3].

Traditionally, reference equations have been developed using generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS), which account for the skewed and age-dependent nature of the spirometric parameters [4]. Although GAMLSS models are very powerful, they require advanced statistical expertise, rely significantly on spline-based transformations, and involve distributional assumptions that may not always be valid in real-world populations. Their implementation may be challenging because of the need for complex statistical software and interpretation, particularly in clinical or resource-limited settings [5,6].

Machine Learning (ML) offers an adaptable data-driven alternative for predictive modeling. ML algorithms can identify complex, non-linear relationships between predictors and outcomes without requiring predefined distributional forms [7,8]. The latest developments in ML have demonstrated possible applications in healthcare, including diagnostic support and risk prediction [9,10].

In the context of spirometry, ML models may simplify the development of reference equations, while improving prediction accuracy and generalizability [11]. Therefore, the main aim of this study was to develop sex-specific machine learning models to predict FEV₁, FVC and FEV₁/FVC using age and height as predictors. This study sought to evaluate the performance of these ML-based equations against existing reference equations: Jordanian GAMLSS-based equations [12], and the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) equations, Global Lung Initiative (GLI) 2012 [13], and GLI 2022 international standards [14], which are the current global standard and were developed using GAMLSS approach. This comparison included model fit, agreement between predicted and LLN values, and diagnostic classification outcomes.

This study evaluated the performance of the ML-based equations against established reference sets: the Jordanian GAMLSS-based equations [12], the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) 2012 equations [13], and the GLI 2022 update [14], all developed using a GAMLSS/LMS approach and widely adopted as international standards [15–17]. The comparison encompassed model fit, agreement of predicted LLN values across methods, and diagnostic classification performance.

Methods

Design

A two-phase approach was used in this study. In the first phase, machine learning (ML) models were developed using the same datasets previously employed to derive the Jordanian GAMLSS-based spirometry equations [12]. These datasets consisted of healthy, non-smoking Jordanian males and females from different locations in Jordan, ensuring a steady foundation for comparison across modeling methods. The second phase enrolled healthy, non-smoking Jordanian adults from various locations across Jordan using convenience and snowball sampling between 1 November 2024, and 27 March 2025. Participants were approached in pharmacies, hospitals, universities, and shopping malls. Invitations to participate were also shared via social media platforms, including Facebook and WhatsApp. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were aligned with those of the prior Jordanian GAMLSS derivation study to ensure comparability. Eligible participants were 18–70 years of age, born in Jordan, and resident in Jordan; were lifelong non-smokers, not obese (BMI < 30), had no history of chronic respiratory, cardiovascular, or neuromuscular diseases, and were not taking medications known to affect lung function. Individuals with upper or lower respiratory tract infections in the four weeks prior to participation were excluded.

All participants completed a structured screening questionnaire to confirm eligibility. The questionnaire covered diagnoses of asthma, COPD, or other lung diseases; current and past smoking status; infection-related symptoms within the past four weeks (e.g. coughing, wheezing, shortness of breath); and any hospitalizations for respiratory illness in the past six months. It also collected information on medications used for chronic conditions. Data from the newly enrolled participants were used to validate the ML equations and compare their performance with the Jordanian GAMLSS, GLI 2012, and GLI 2022 models.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB no 5/10/2024–2025) of Al-Zaytoonah University of Jordan. All participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Sample size calculation

Based on the work of Quanjer et al. [18], a minimum of 150 males and 150 females is required to minimize sampling error when validating spirometry reference equations. The Phase 2 external validation sample exceeded this threshold, comprising 260 males and 227 females.

Spirometry test

Spirometry was conducted according to the 2019 American Thoracic Society (ATS) and European Respiratory Society (ERS) guidelines [15]. A trained researcher performed all spirometry tests using a validated computer-based portable spirometer (Spirobank II; MIR Medical International Research, Rome, Italy), ensuring standardization of the measurement technique. Calibration checks were performed daily, and at least three acceptable and two repeatable efforts were recorded for each participant. The highest acceptable values were retained for the analysis.

Anthropometric measurements, including standing height and weight, were obtained using calibrated stadiometers and digital scales. Height was recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm without shoes, and weight to the nearest 0.1 kg.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and modeling procedures were performed using R version 4.3.2.

Between-phase differences in spirometric indices (FEV₁, FVC, and FEV₁/FVC) were evaluated using independent-samples comparisons. Mean differences with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p values (α = 0.05) were reported. When homogeneity of variance was violated, Welch’s correction was applied. To account for multiple testing across the three primary spirometric outcomes, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni method.

Six regression models were trained for each spirometric outcome using training datasets stratified by sex: Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (svm) with Radial Basis Function (svmRadial), Generalized Linear Model (glm), Gradient Boosting Machine (gbm), k-nearest neighbors (knn), and an ensemble model (mean prediction across models). The same models were fitted with both log-transformed and untransformed parameters.

GLM was fitted with a Gaussian family and identity link. Assumptions for GLM were evaluated using residual-versus-fitted and Q – Q plots, variance inflation factors, and influence diagnostics to verify linearity of the conditional mean in age and height, homoscedasticity with approximately normal residuals, absence of problematic multicollinearity, and lack of undue influence. No material violations were detected.

For each sex and outcome, the best-performing model was selected using cross-validation performance metrics (Root Mean Squared Error [RMSE], Mean Absolute Error [MAE], and R2) on the training set. Cross-validation was performed using five-fold cross-validation repeated three times to support a reliable and unbiased estimation of model performance. Residual distributions were further evaluated using z-scores/age plots stratified by age group to examine calibration across the lifespan. These diagnostic plots helped to identify any systematic deviations or age-dependent biases in model predictions, adding an important layer of model validation beyond global performance metrics. LLNs were estimated using the following formula: LLN = predicted value − 1.645 × RMSE. The Z-scores were computed as (observed – predicted)/RMSE.

ML-based predictions and LLNs were compared with those from the Jordanian GAMLSS, GLI 2012, and GLI 2022 equations using the Phase 2 dataset. Agreement was assessed using Pearson’s correlation between ML-based z-scores and those generated by Jordanian, GLI 2012, and GLI 2022 equations. The study further examined how well the z-scores from each model matched the ML-based z-scores in the test dataset, calculating the proportion of values falling within ± 0.5 and ± 1.0 of the ML reference [13,19]. A threshold of ± 0.5 reflects tight clinical alignment, while ± 1.0 is more lenient but still considered acceptable in clinical practice [20–22].

To classify ventilatory patterns, this study followed the ERS/ATS technical standards for interpreting routine lung function tests [16,23]. Spirometric impairments were identified by evaluating FEV₁/FVC and FVC against all the studies equations.

An obstructive ventilatory impairment was identified by an FEV₁/FVC z-score below the lower limit of normal (LLN), defined as z < −1.645. If a reduced FEV₁/FVC was accompanied by a concurrently low FVC, the pattern was classified as a potential mixed ventilatory impairment. A restrictive or non-specific pattern was considered when the FEV₁/FVC ratio was preserved but FVC was reduced. Finally, a normal spirometry profile was characterized by both FEV₁/FVC and FVC z-scores remaining above the LLN. Age-height grids were generated to support the clinical interpretability of ML-based predictions.

To compare the performance of spirometric reference models in males and females, predicted values and LLNs were generated using a fixed height of 173 cm for males and 158 cm for females. In males (Figure S1), the Jordanian and the selected ML model for each parameter showed closely aligned predictions, both consistently lower than those from the GLI equations. In males, FEV₁ predictions from the Jordanian model showed the smallest bias (mean difference = 0.03) and the highest agreement, with 100% of values falling within ±0.5 SD. In contrast, the GLI 2012 Caucasian model showed a substantial negative bias for FVC (mean difference = 0.99), with only 7% of values within ±0.5 SD, indicating systematic over- or underestimation.

In females, ML predictions for FEV₁ showed strong agreement with both the Jordanian (r = 0.98; mean difference = 0.06) and GLI 2022 (r = 0.95; mean difference = 0.20) equations, with more than 97% of values falling within ±0.5 SD. In comparison, the GLI 2012 Caucasian model predicted higher FEV₁ values (mean difference = 0.76), with only 10% of z-scores within the ±0.5 SD threshold.

For FVC, agreement was moderate with the Jordanian equation (r = 0.80) but considerably weaker with the GLI reference models. The GLI 2012 Caucasian equation, in particular, showed a mean difference of 1.09 and only 4% of values within ±0.5 SD. For FEV₁/FVC, the Jordanian model demonstrated near-perfect correlation (r = 1.00), while all GLI models exhibited substantial negative bias: GLI 2022 (−0.65), GLI 2012 Other (−0.69), and GLI 2012 Caucasian (−0.79).

Tables S3 and S4 present the mean squared error (MSE) with 95% confidence intervals, as well as the observed and predicted mean values (with standard deviations) for FEV₁, FVC, and FEV₁/FVC ratio in the Phase 2 dataset. Among males, ML models produced the lowest MSEs for FEV₁ (0.119) and FVC (0.122), with mean z-scores close to zero, indicating good calibration with observed data. The Jordanian equations yielded comparable MSEs (e.g. 0.120 for FEV₁), while the GLI 2012, GLI 2022 Other, and GLI 2012 Caucasian models showed higher MSEs and more negative z-scores, particularly for FEV₁ and FVC, suggesting systematic overestimation.

A similar pattern was observed in females (Table S4), where the best-performing ML and Jordanian models again produced lower MSEs for FEV₁ (0.061 and 0.063, respectively) and FVC (0.072 and 0.054, respectively), with z-scores near zero, reflecting closer alignment with observed spirometric values. In contrast, the GLI 2012 Caucasian model exhibited the highest MSEs and most deviant z-scores across all outcomes (e.g. z = −1.34 for female FVC). Although the GLI 2022 model showed some improvement, it continued to underperform compared with the ML and Jordanian equations.

Spirometric pattern classification was assessed using all studied reference equations. In males, both the ML and Jordanian models classified all individuals as having normal lung function. The GLI 2012 equation identified 713 individuals (71.7%) as normal, while 282 (28.3%) were classified as having restrictive or non-specific patterns. The GLI 2022 equation classified 98% of males as normal and 2% as restrictive or non-specific.

In females, the ML, Jordanian, and GLI 2022 models classified nearly all participants as having normal lung function (99.1%, 100%, and 100%, respectively), with no individuals identified as having obstructive patterns. In contrast, the GLI 2012 equation classified only 70% as normal and 30% as restrictive or non-specific, indicating a tendency to overclassify abnormalities.

Discussion

This study examined the practicality and potential value ML models as alternatives to conventional spirometry reference equations. Using age and height, sex-specific ML models were developed to predict FEV₁, FVC, and FEV₁/FVC. These models performed comparably to the Jordanian GAMLSS-based equations and the internationally recognized GLI standards, and in some cases showed a closer fit to observed data. The combination of strong predictive performance and conceptual simplicity suggests that ML models may offer a viable and attractive option, particularly in settings where population-specific reference equations are lacking or difficult to implement.

ML was used to estimate the conditional mean of FEV₁, FVC, and FEV₁/FVC from age and height, with final learners for each parameter selected by cross-validated prediction error. In contrast, GAMLSS specifies a parametric distribution and models location, scale, and often shape via penalized smoothing, requiring careful family selection and tuning of smoothing structures [24]. Most ML learners used here (e.g. random forests and gradient boosting) do not require an a priori outcome distribution and can accommodate non-linear age – height relations, while GLM recovers an interpretable linear form [25]. This workflow prioritizes out-of-sample predictive accuracy and yields deployable outputs. ML provides a reproducible approach that is simpler to maintain and update than GAMLSS, while yielding z-scores and LLN by centering on the ML-predicted mean and scaling by the empirical residual variance.

When compared with the observed spirometry data, ML predictions generally aligned more closely with measured values across age groups. This was particularly evident in the z-score by age group plots, where ML-based predictions showed more uniform dispersion and less age-related distortion. Notably, a higher proportion of z-scores fell within the commonly accepted thresholds of ±0.5 and ±1.0 for both the ML and Jordanian models, indicating greater consistency. In contrast, the GLI-based equations, particularly for FEV₁/FVC and FVC, exhibited more variable performance across age strata. These findings are consistent with a study conducted in the Iranian population, which reported that equations developed using KNN regression more closely matched observed values for FEV₁, FVC, and FEV₁/FVC compared with traditional GAMLSS equations [26]. Similar results were found in studies comparing ML models with multiple linear regression, where ML-based predictions demonstrated better alignment with the data and improved overall fit [27].

Although the ML equations developed in this study showed a comparable fit to the GAMLSS models, the R2 values for FEV₁ and FVC were lower than those reported in previous studies [27,28]. However, those studies were conducted in different contexts. For example, Pramila and Mahesh enrolled only 198 participants [28], whereas Kristensen et al. developed their models using multi-ethnic datasets [27]. Although applying logarithmic transformations could have slightly improved the R2 values in the present study, we opted to retain the non-log-transformed models to ensure greater practical applicability, particularly in diverse or resource-limited settings such as rural areas.

Evaluating the lower limits of normal (LLNs) and predicted values in relation to observed lung function measurements revealed several recurring patterns. The GLI 2012 equations consistently overestimated FEV₁ and FVC, thereby broadening the range of values considered ‘normal’ and increasing the risk of overlooking early signs of impairment. Although the 2022 update improved on population diversity, it still diverged noticeably from the regionally trained models at the lower end of the distribution. These calibration differences are clinically significant and may affect the accuracy of respiratory health assessment and classification. The findings from this study contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the superiority of locally developed reference equations over international standards such as GLI 2012 and GLI 2022 [29–35].

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study has several strengths. It employed an independent Jordanian sample for external validation, allowing for an assessment of model generalizability across different subsets of the population. The results highlighted the practical implications of applying different reference equations: while both the ML and Jordanian models aligned with clinical expectations, the GLI 2012 model was more likely to misclassify healthy individuals, echoing concerns raised in previous evaluations. The modeling process emphasized both stability and generalizability by using repeated fivefold cross-validation to reduce overfitting and ensure consistent performance. Multiple algorithms were tested, and the most appropriate model was selected for each spirometric outcome, accounting for physiological variation. In addition, the study introduced age – height grids to support clinical use by translating complex model outputs into accessible tools for practice [36].

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although age – height grids enhance usability, the underlying models rely on software implementation, which may limit their application in settings where digital infrastructure is lacking. Furthermore, sampling in Phase 2 relied on nonprobability approaches, which may have skewed the age – height profile and other demographics, limiting generalizability at the distribution tails. However, the derivation cohort (Phase 1) used to fit the equations had median heights of 173 cm in males and 158 cm in females, closely aligning with national STEPS estimates for Jordanian adults [37], indicating that the modeled structure was broadly representative. Phase 2 was used solely for external validation, so any recruitment imbalance affects the precision of validation rather than the fitted equations, though caution remains warranted for under-represented strata. Furthermore, the produced equations covered ages 18–70 years; children, adolescents, and adults older than 70 years were not represented. Generalizability to these age bands is therefore limited.

Future research should explore the applicability of these ML-based models in broader demographic and geographic contexts, including populations with varying socioeconomic backgrounds, and produce equations that extend recruitment to younger and older age groups to enable reference equations with full life-course coverage. Larger, more diverse datasets could help improve model generalizability and robustness. Additionally, efforts to develop user-friendly tools for clinical implementation, particularly in low-resource settings, would further support the integration of ML-based spirometry models into routine practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of ML models as practical and accurate tools for spirometry interpretation. The ML-based reference equations developed here showed strong agreement with observed data and performed comparably, and in some cases better, than both national (Jordanian GAMLSS) and international (GLI 2012 and 2022) standards. Their relatively simple development process, ability to capture non-linear patterns, and strong calibration make them well suited for clinical use, particularly in settings where conventional equations are unavailable or poorly aligned with local populations. The use of an independent Jordanian sample highlights the value of population-specific reference equations and illustrates the shortcomings of applying global standards without local adaptation. With appropriate infrastructure and clinical integration, ML-based spirometry equations could improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce misclassification, and support more equitable respiratory care across diverse settings.
