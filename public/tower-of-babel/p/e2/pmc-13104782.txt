Novel Inositol Acyltransferase (Gwt1) Inhibitor for Fungal Keratitis Treatment: Evaluation of In Vitro and In Vivo Efficacy and Safety
Authors: Lakshminarayanan Gowtham, Yerraguntla Meghana, Tamizhmathy Mannangatty, Singitam Vasavi, Jayabalan Nirmal, Bhupesh Bagga, Prashant Garg, Dilip Kumar Mishra, Sanhita Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeFungal keratitis (FK) is a vision-threatening infection affecting ∼1 million people annually. This study assesses the therapeutic potential of Manogepix (MGX), a first-in-class Gwt1 fungal enzyme inhibitor, using in vitro and in vivo models.MethodsMGX's in vitro efficacy was evaluated against clinical keratitis isolates of Candida albicans (n = 10), Fusarium solani (n = 8), and Aspergillus flavus (n = 6) by determining minimal inhibitory and fungicidal concentrations (MIC and MFC), biofilm inhibition, morphological changes, time-kill kinetics at MIC and interaction with amphotericin B. Safety was assessed in vitro on human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) and in vivo via Draize's test. In vivo efficacy was evaluated in a rabbit F. solani keratitis model. Animals received topical MGX (50 µL, 0.01% w/v, 8×/day) for four days, followed by clinical scoring, colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration, and histopathological analysis.ResultsMGX was fungicidal against C. albicans (MIC₉₀: 0.0313, MFC₉₀: 0.156 µg/mL) and fungistatic against F. solani and A. flavus, with IC₈₀ ranges of 0.019–0.312 and 1.25–20 µg/mL, respectively. At MIC and IC80, MGX inhibited biofilm formation, disrupted fungal cellular morphology and proliferation dynamics. MGX exhibited synergistic/additive effects with amphotericin B. MGX was found safe in vitro at 5 µg/mL on HCECs and showed no signs of corneal toxicity in vivo at 100 µg/mL. In the rabbit model F. solani keratitis, MGX treatment reduced the clinical manifestations of FK and significantly reduced CFU counts compared with untreated controls.ConclusionsMGX demonstrated efficacy against major ocular fungal pathogens and showed a favorable ocular safety profile in both in vitro and in vivo models.

Corneal blindness is the fifth leading cause of blindness worldwide, with infectious keratitis (IK) being a predominant etiological factor.1–3 Fungal keratitis (FK) accounts for 40%–60% of IK cases in countries with tropical regions such as South Asia, Africa, and Latin America, whereas its global proportion ranges between 30%–50%.4–6 FK is a sight-threatening corneal infection and a global public health concern. Over 90% of the FK cases are caused by filamentous fungi such as Fusarium spp. and Aspergillus spp. or yeasts such as Candida spp.7 FK predominantly affects young adults in agricultural settings, following trauma with organic matter, imposing substantial socioeconomic burden.4 Annually, FK affects one million individuals worldwide, with ∼100,000 eyes requiring enucleation because of delayed diagnosis, poor prognosis, and suboptimal therapy.4 Natamycin (5% w/v) is the only ophthalmic antifungal approved by the United states Food and Drug Administration (US-FDA) for FK treatment. Amphotericin B (0.15% w/v) and voriconazole (1% w/v) are used off-label as fortified ophthalmic preparations.8 Despite aggressive treatment, therapeutic failure occurs in ∼50% of cases, often necessitating corneal transplantation or enucleation.9,10 Compared with bacterial keratitis (BK) outcomes reported in Steroids for Corneal Ulcers Trial, Mycotic Ulcer Treatment Trial showed that FK had an approximately fivefold higher perforation rate and markedly prolonged healing.11,12 Therefore there is a critical need to evaluate newer classes/generation of antifungal molecules for FK treatment.

Manogepix (MGX) is a first-in-class inhibitor of fungal enzyme inositol acyltransferase (Gwt1)13 and an active moiety of the N-phosphonooxymethylene prodrug fosmanogepix. MGX inhibits the fungal-specific Gwt1, an enzyme essential for the acylation of inositol during the biosynthesis of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored cell wall proteins. Inhibition of this pathway compromises fungal cell wall integrity, disrupts germ tube and biofilm formation, and ultimately results in fungal growth defects.14,15 Fosmanogepix is currently in phase 3 clinical trials for the treatment of invasive fungal infections caused by Aspergillus spp., Fusarium spp., Lomentospora prolificans, Mucorales fungi, or other multidrug resistant molds.13,15,16 MGX possess broad-spectrum efficacy against diverse fungal species, encompassing both filamentous and yeast forms, including key ocular fungal pathogens (Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Candida spp.).14,17 Its specificity is highly confined to fungus and particularly promising against the treatment-resistant FK strains with current first-line agents (polyenes and azoles). However, its efficacy and safety against ocular fungal infections have not yet been evaluated. MGX represents a promising advancement in FK therapy, addressing the urgent need for new treatments in the face of rising fungal resistance in keratitis treatment.

Methodology

Corneal scrapings were collected and processed for pathogen identification at the Jhaveri Microbiology Centre, LV Prasad Eye Institute, following institutional protocols.18 Clinical isolates of Candida albicans (n = 10), Fusarium solani (n = 8), and Aspergillus flavus (n = 6) were obtained from microbiologically confirmed FK patients. Isolates were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) or Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) plates and incubated at 26°C ± 2°C for one week, followed by at least two sub-cultures before experimentation (Supplementary Material S1).

In Vitro Anti-Fungal Susceptibility of FK Pathogens to MGX

A primary MGX stock (Manogepix, E1210; Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA) was prepared at 8 mg/mL in dimethyl sulfoxide with a working stock of 80 µg/mL in Sabouraud Dextrose Broth (SDB). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) of MGX against C. albicans (n = 10) keratitis isolates were determined following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M27-A4 microbroth dilution guidelines.19 Each well of a sterile 96-well microtiter plate was inoculated with C. albicans spores (2.5 × 10³ colony-forming unit [CFU]/mL) and exposed to twofold serial dilutions of MGX ranging from 0.000122–1 µg/mL, followed by incubation at 35°C ± 2°C for 24 hours.

For F. solani (n = 8) and A. flavus (n = 6) keratitis isolates, with susceptibility testing conditions adhered to CLSI M38 guidelines,20 MGX (0.0039–40 µg/mL) was tested with an inoculated of 2.5 × 10⁴ CFU/mL and incubated at 35°C ± 2°C for 48 hours. Optical density (OD) at 570 nm was measured at 48 hours using a microplate reader.

Fungal inoculum without MGX served as negative controls (NC), whereas wells containing amphotericin B (40 µg/mL) served as positive controls for both yeast and filamentous fungi. Growth inhibition was assessed both visually and spectrophotometrically (OD570).

Based on the differential antifungal activity profile of MGX, inhibitory endpoints were selected accordingly. For C. albicans, which exhibited fungicidal susceptibility, MIC and MFC values were determined. In contrast, for F. solani and A. flavus, where MGX demonstrated fungistatic activity, inhibitory concentrations were defined as IC₈₀. MIC was defined as the lowest MGX concentration resulting in complete (100%) inhibition of visible growth in the micro-wells. MFC was determined as the lowest concentration that resulted in complete (100%) killing, verified by sub-culturing MGX-treated inocula onto SDA plates for viability checks. IC₈₀ was defined as the concentration of MGX producing ≥80% growth inhibition based on OD570 values.

Biofilm inhibitory potential, time-kill kinetics and morphological assessment were performed with one representative isolate from each species (L-2644/20 for C. albicans, L-3725/24 for F. solani, L-2422/23 for A. flavus) under the same conditions as susceptibility testing as described in Supplementary Material 1.

Evaluation of the Synergistic Potential of MGX With Amphotericin B Against C. albicans

The checkerboard microdilution method was used to identify the drug interactions of MGX with amphotericin B against three C. albicans isolates. The effects of the drug interactions were calculated as the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) = (MIC of amphotericin B with MGX in combination/MIC of amphotericin B alone) + (MIC of amphotericin B with MGX in combination/MIC of MGX alone). FICI ≤0.5 was considered synergistic, FICI >0.5 to <1 was considered indifferent, and FICI ≥ 1 was considered antagonistic. The experiments were conducted in triplicate for each of the isolates tested.

Determination of In Vitro Safety Concentrations of MGX on Human Corneal Epithelial Cells (HCECs)

The cytotoxicity of MGX on HCECs was assessed using the MTT assay. HCECs were seeded in 96-well plates and maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) until a confluent monolayer was formed. HCECs were exposed to MGX at concentrations ranging from 0.00012 to 5 µg/mL in DMEM (100 µL/well) and incubated for 1.5 hours (short-term), eight hours (mid-term), or 24 hours (long-term) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Subsequently, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for three to four hours under the same conditions to allow reduction to formazan crystals. The crystals were solubilized in dimethyl sulfoxide, and absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

In Ovo and In Vivo Ocular Safety Evaluation

The ocular irritation potential of MGX (10 µg/mL) was initially assessed using the hen's egg test–chorioallantoic membrane (HET-CAM) method. Fertilized hen eggs (n = 6) were incubated at 37°C for nine days, after which MGX (10 µg/mL, 0.1 mL) was applied onto the exposed CAM surface. The membrane was observed for hemorrhage, lysis, and coagulation within five minutes, and irritation scores were calculated. Tests were performed in comparison with normal saline solution (negative control) and 0.1 N NaOH (positive control). In vivo ocular safety was evaluated using Draize's test on New Zealand white rabbits (n = 4). MGX (0.01% w/v or 100 µg/mL, 50 µL) was instilled into the conjunctival sac (four times a day at four-hour intervals), and ocular reactions such as redness, chemosis, and discharge were graded at 72 hours after instillation using slit lamp microscopy (SLCAM_ICM-7; Appasamy-Associates, Chennai, India).

In Vivo Efficacy and Safety Evaluation of MGX in Rabbit Models of F. solani Keratitis

An in vivo rabbit model of F. solani keratitis was established in the right eyes of twelve New Zealand white rabbits. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee, Birla Institute of Technology and Science, Pilani–Hyderabad Campus, Telangana, India (IAEC No. BITS-HYD-IAEC-2025-131). All experiments complied with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and institutional animal care guidelines. Infection was induced by intrastromal injection of 25 µL of F. solani inoculum (2.5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) using an insulin syringe on Day 0 (baseline). The animals were randomly divided into three groups (n = 4 per group). Group 1 served as the untreated control and did not receive any antifungal treatment after infection. Group 2 received topical MGX (0.01% w/v) eye drops administered eight times daily from Day 3 to Day 7 (96 hours), whereas Group 3 received topical amphotericin B (0.15% w/v) eye drops at the same dosing frequency and duration. The left eyes of all animals received an intrastromal injection of 25 µL sterile saline and served as vehicle controls for the applied treatments. Clinical evaluation and scoring were performed by a clinician ophthalmologist on Day 1, Day 3 (before initiation of treatment), and Day 7 (after completion of treatment).21 On Day 7, all animals were euthanized using a combination of ketamine and xylazine, and both corneas were excised and bisected. One half of each cornea was processed for CFU enumeration to assess fungal burden, while the other half was subjected to histopathological examination to evaluate tissue response and healing. Result

In Vitro Inhibitory Potential of MGX Against Clinical FK Isolates

MIC, MFC, and IC80 Determination

MGX exhibited fungicidal effect against C. albicans. The median (Q1, Q3) MIC and MFC of MGX against C. albicans were 0.0156 (0.0156, 0.0312) and 0.125 (0.0625, 0.125) µg/mL, respectively (Fig. 2a). MGX exhibited fungistatic effect against both F. solani and A. flavus till the maximum tested concentrations of 40 µg/mL. The Median (Q1, Q3) IC80 of MGX against F. solani and A. flavus were found to be 0.029 (0.019, 0.156) and 5 (3.125, 8.75), respectively (Fig. 2b). MIC or IC80 values of each of the tested isolates were represented in Supplementary Material S2.

In vitro antifungal efficacy of MGX against, (a) C. albicans (n = 10), (b) F. solani (n = 8), and A. flavus (n = 6). MIC, minimum inhibitory concentration; MFC, minimum fungicidal concentration; IC80, minimum MGX concentration required to inhibit 80% of fungal growth, as determined by optical density measurements at 570 nm.

Inhibitory Effects of MGX on Fungal Biofilms and Time-Kill Kinetics

MGX exhibited a concentration-dependent reduction in biofilm formation, with the maximum inhibition observed at MIC (0.039 µg/mL) for C. albicans and at IC₈₀ for F. solani (0.125 µg/mL) and A. flavus (5 µg/mL) (Figs. 3a–c). The maximum biofilm inhibition by MGX at MIC or IC₈₀ was comparable to that of the positive control (Amphotericin B, 40 µg/mL). No significant inhibition was observed beyond MIC or IC₈₀ to the highest tested concentrations of 10 µg/mL for C. albicans and 40 µg/mL for F. solani and A. flavus.

Biofilm inhibitory potential (a–c) and time–kill kinetics profiles (d–f) of MGX against C. albicans, F. solani, and A. flavus. “PC” (a–c) indicates the positive control (i.e., treatment with amphotericin B [40 µg/mL]).

At MIC, MGX completely suppressed C. albicans growth over 24 hours compared to exponential growth in untreated controls (Fig. 3d). Similarly, MGX at IC₈₀ for Fusarium and Aspergillus prevented fungal proliferation over 48 and 72 hours, respectively, whereas control cultures showed substantial increases in CFU counts, indicating sustained fungi-static activity (Figs. 3e, 3f).

Synergism of MGX With Amphotericin B Against C. albicans

Synergy testing revealed that MGX in combination with amphotericin B yielded FICI values of 0.74 ± 0.21 and 0.74 ± 0.0002 for two C. albicans isolates, indicating additive effects, whereas one isolate showed a synergistic interaction with a mean FICI of 0.395 ± 0.044.

MGX-Induced Morphological Changes on Fungal Cell Structure at Effective Concentrations

Bright-Field and Fluorescence Microscopy After Propidium Iodide (PI) Exposure

PI staining revealed distinct morphological alterations in fungal cells following MGX exposure. In C. albicans, untreated cells remained intact with no fluorescence (Figs. 4a, 4c), whereas MGX (at MIC)-treated cells showed bright PI uptake, indicating membrane damage, along with swelling and aggregation compared to the rounded morphology of controls (Figs. 4b, 4d). In F. solani, control hyphae appeared dense and filamentous without PI staining (Figs. 4e, 4g), whereas MGX (at IC₈₀) treated hyphae displayed intense fluorescence, fragmentation, irregular branching, and collapse (Figs. 4f, 4h). Similarly, in A. flavus, untreated cultures showed healthy, continuous hyphae with normal conidiation (Figs. 4i, 4k), whereas MGX (at IC₈₀) treated hyphae exhibited strong PI uptake, swelling, distortion, and reduced branching (Figs. 4j, 4l).

Bright-field, propidium iodide fluorescence (a–l), and scanning electron microscopy (m–r) images of C. albicans (a–d, m, p), F. solani (e–h, n, q), and A. flavus (i–l, o, r) under control (i.e., no drug treatment) and MGX-treated conditions. MGX exposure was performed at the MIC for C. albicans (b, d, p) and at the minimal concentration required to inhibit IC₈₀ for F. solani (f, h, q) and A. flavus (j, l, r).

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

SEM analysis revealed distinct ultrastructural alterations in fungal morphology upon MGX treatment. Untreated C. albicans cells (Fig. 4m) appeared smooth, spherical, and densely packed, whereas MIC-treated cells (Fig. 4p) showed collapsed, wrinkled, and irregular surfaces, indicating severe membrane and cell wall damage. In F. solani (Fig. 4n), control hyphae were long, smooth, and intact, while IC₈₀-treated hyphae (Fig. 4q) appeared distorted with surface roughness, shrinkage, and breakage, reflecting loss of filamentous integrity. Similarly, A. flavus control (Fig. 4o) exhibited intact, smooth conidiophores and hyphae, whereas MGX exposure at IC₈₀ (Fig. 4r) resulted in collapsed, shrunken hyphae with disrupted surface texture and loss of normal conidiation.

Ocular Safety of MGX

In Vitro Safer Concentration of MGX

At 1.5 and eight hours, cell viability remained above 80% across all tested concentrations, indicating minimal acute cytotoxicity. After 24 hours exposure, a concentration-dependent reduction in viability was observed, with values declining >50% at 2.5 µg/mL and >45% at 5 µg/mL (Fig. 5a). Microscopic examination corroborated these results. After 24 hours of MGX exposure at 5 and 10 µg/mL on HCE cells, bright-field images showed no marked morphological changes (Fig. 5b). Fluorescence microscopy revealed negligible PI staining in control and 5 µg/mL groups, while intense staining was observed at 10 µg/mL, confirming membrane structure compromise at higher concentrations (Fig. 5b).

Ocular safety evaluation of MGX. (a–b) Cytotoxicity assessment of MGX in human corneal epithelial cells (Scale bar: 20 µm), (c) HET-CAM assay, and (d) Rabbit eye safety: topical MGX (0.01% w/v, 4×/day for 72 hours) showed no signs of corneal damage on slit-lamp or fluorescein staining, and histology confirmed intact epithelial and stromal architecture.

In Ovo and In Vivo Safety of MGX

In the HET-CAM assay (Fig. 5c), application of 300 µL of MGX (10 µg/mL) did not induce any signs of irritation such as hemorrhage, vessel lysis, or coagulation up to five minutes, comparable to the negative control (0.9% NaCl). Positive control (0.1 M NaOH) produced rapid vascular damage and hemorrhage, confirming assay sensitivity.

In vivo ocular safety evaluation in rabbits (Fig. 5d) demonstrated that topical instillation of 0.01% w/v MGX (50 µL, four times daily up to 72 hours) caused no visible ocular abnormalities. Slit-lamp examinations revealed intact corneal surfaces without epithelial defects or edema. Histological examination of corneal sections confirmed preserved epithelial architecture and stromal integrity, with no evidence of inflammation or structural disruption (Fig. 5d).

In Vivo Efficacy and Safety of MGX in the Murine Model of F. solani keratitis

Clinical scoring (Fig. 6a) based on slit lamp examination has showed that untreated infected eyes developed progressive stromal infiltrates, stromal haze, hypopyon, anterior chamber reactions, conjunctival congestion by day 7, accompanied by discharge and eyelid edema. MGX-treated eyes showed marked improvement, with reductions in anterior chamber reaction, conjunctival congestion with absence of hypopyon and discharge and it was comparable with amphotericin B treatment (Fig. 6a and Table). In slit-lamp examination, saline solution–treated eyes exhibited worsening corneal opacity and hypopyon, whereas MGX- and amphotericin-treated eyes displayed clear corneas with reduced inflammation by day 7. Fungal burden analysis further confirmed efficacy, with MGX treatment significantly reduced F. solani CFU counts (P < 0.05) relative to saline solution, achieving reductions comparable to amphotericin B (Fig. 6b).

Discussion

The World Health Organization included C. albicans, A. fumigatus under critical priority group, and Fusarium spp. under the high priority group in their fungal priority pathogens list.22 Gwt1 inhibitors selectively target fungi by blocking fungal-specific GPI-anchor biosynthesis while sparing host cells.23 MGX (APX001A) and its prodrug fosmanogepix (APX001) have shown efficacy against various fungal pathogens, including Cryptococcus and Coccidioides species.24–26

In this study, SDB was employed as the fungal culture medium in place of RPMI 1640 (CLSI recommendation), owing to the enhanced solubility of MGX in SDB (up to 40 µg/mL), whereas RPMI 1640 exhibited turbidity at concentrations exceeding 10 µg/mL. MIC values were comparable in both media for three tested Candida isolates (data not shown). MGX possess broad-spectrum anti-fungal activity, with notably low MIC values against C. albicans, suggesting high potency against yeast compared with filamentous fungi. An eight-fold difference between median MIC and MFC values of MGX against C. albicans (Fig. 2a) suggests that MGX suggests fungistatic activity at lower doses and fungicidal effects at higher concentrations. Higher IC₈₀ observed for A. flavus (around 170-fold) compared to F. solani (Fig. 2b) reflects inherent variability in MGX susceptibility among molds.

Across five global surveillance studies (>8,000 isolates; 2017–2021),15,27–30 MGX demonstrated remarkable in vitro potency against Candida, Fusarium, and Aspergillus spp., with minimum effective concentration (MEC)₅₀/₉₀ and 16- to 64-fold greater potency than azoles, retaining efficacy against azole-resistant isolates.13,14,29–31 Antifungal susceptibility varies geographically,32 with Indian Aspergillus and Candida isolates showing higher azole and amphotericin B MICs than European or American strains, likely due to differences in species distribution and antifungal exposure.32–34

The observed additive to synergistic interactions of MGX with amphotericin B against C. albicans suggest potential for combination therapy, which may allow for dose reduction of both agents, minimizing toxicity while enhancing efficacy. Liston et al. reported synergistic, fungicidal interactions between manogepix and calcineurin inhibitors (including FK506) against diverse human fungal pathogens, supported by chemical-genetic analysis of 124 conditional expression mutants covering 95% of GPI-anchor biosynthesis genes.35 Another study quantified synergy between anidulafungin and manogepix against C. auris isolates from clades I and III, with FICI of 0.28-0.75, indicating synergistic activity.36

MGX demonstrated concentration-dependent biofilm reduction and sustained growth suppression (Fig. 3), suggesting a durable effect driven by membrane disruption and fungal network collapse, as confirmed by microscopy (Fig. 4). These findings are particularly significant given that Ceballos-Garzon et al. (2025) reported MGX as the most potent agent against mature Candida biofilms (including C. auris) using the Calgary device. Although their geometric mean biofilm eradication concentration (5.9 µg/mL)37 was 119-fold higher than planktonic MICs, MGX consistently outperformed ibrexafungerp, rezafungin, caspofungin, and amphotericin B.37

Mechanistically, PI staining and SEM confirmed that MGX disrupts membrane integrity, causing pronounced morphological damage across both yeast and filamentous fungi (Fig. 4). These species-specific responses suggest a common mechanism of membrane permeabilization, mimicking the effects of amphotericin B. Notably, this study provides the first reported evidence of MGX-induced morphological changes and time–kill kinetics in fungal isolates. Because the aim of time–kill assay was to assess intrinsic killing kinetics rather than comparative activity, amphotericin B was not included (Figs. 3d–f).

MGX showed minimal cytotoxicity in HCECs up to 5 µg/mL till 8 hours (Figs. 5a, 5b). The decline in viability observed after 24 hours at higher concentrations suggests time- and dose-dependent cytotoxicity, likely attributable to membrane disruption, as reflected by increased PI staining. Importantly, the MIC for C. albicans and IC₈₀ values for Fusarium and Aspergillus lie within or below the non-toxic concentration range, suggesting a favorable therapeutic window for ocular application. The absence of significant morphological damage at lower concentrations further underscores MGX's safety profile, although prolonged exposure at higher doses may compromise corneal epithelial integrity. MGX, the active moiety of fosmanogepix, has demonstrated an excellent safety and tolerability profile in both oral and intravenous clinical evaluations, with no serious treatment-emergent toxicities.38 Preclinical toxicology studies further indicated a wide safety margin with minimal systemic toxicity at therapeutic exposures.39 These attributes, combined with MGX's unique fungal-specific target (GPI-anchor biosynthesis pathway) absent in mammalian cells, reduce the likelihood of ocular cytotoxicity. The absence of vascular irritation in the HET-CAM assay and the lack of ocular surface damage in rabbit studies indicate that MGX is well tolerated at concentrations exceeding those required for antifungal activity (Fig. 5c). The preserved corneal epithelium and stroma, along with the absence of fluorescein uptake, demonstrate that MGX does not compromise corneal barrier integrity, even after repeated dosing (Fig. 5d).

In vivo efficacy of MGX in the rabbit keratitis model highlights its strong antifungal potential and therapeutic relevance. The clinical improvement in corneal clarity, reduced anterior chamber reaction, and absence of hypopyon demonstrate that MGX effectively suppresses fungal infection and associated inflammation (Fig. 6; Table). The reduction in fungal burden to levels comparable with amphotericin B further validates MGX as a broad-spectrum antifungal agent with in vivo efficacy against filamentous fungi. Importantly, MGX achieved therapeutic effects comparable to amphotericin B (0.15%w/v) at a utilized concentration of 0.01%w/v, suggesting a favorable potency and dosing profile.

In experimental models, Gwt1 inhibitors have shown promising results in reducing fungal burden and improving survival rates, supporting their further clinical evaluation for treating ocular fungal infections.25,26 Preclinical studies demonstrate that fosmanogepix (APX001) exhibits potent in vivo efficacy against a broad spectrum of fungal pathogens, including Candida auris, Fusarium, Scedosporium, and Rhizopus arrhizus. It showed therapeutic success in disseminated and CNS infections, including Candida endophthalmitis, with favorable pharmacokinetics and tissue penetration. Moreover, combination therapy with liposomal amphotericin B enhanced survival outcomes in invasive mold infections. These findings substantiate fosmanogepix's broad therapeutic promise.13,40–43 Although MGX-mediated Gwt1 inhibition and disruption of GPI-anchor biosynthesis are well established, additional biochemical validation, such as membrane integrity assays or ergosterol interaction studies, species-specific variations in Gwt1 expression, and their potential impact on MIC variability, offers the direction for future studies.

Conclusions

MGX shows strong promise as a novel ocular antifungal, either as a standalone agent or in combination with existing therapies. Its broad-spectrum activity, selective action against key ocular pathogens (Fusarium, Aspergillus, and Candida spp.), potent biofilm inhibition, sustained fungicidal effects, and wide therapeutic safety margin support its repurposing for topical or intraocular use. In a rabbit model, MGX significantly reduced Fusarium keratitis progression without compromising ocular safety, highlighting its potential as an addition to the ocular antifungal armamentarium. Future studies should focus on optimizing MGX ocular formulations to enhance corneal penetration and sustained delivery, along with long-term efficacy and safety evaluation in advanced animal models and clinical trials for FK.
