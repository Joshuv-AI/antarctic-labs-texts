Voices: Future directions in targeting the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Tanja de Gruijl, Catherine Sautès-Fridman, Daniela S Thommen, Michael A Curran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhat do you envision as the most promising future directions for therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating the tumor microenvironment?

Main text

Cancer immunotherapy: Going early, going local

Tanja de Gruijl

Amsterdam University Medical Center

Co-evolution of tumors and their micro-environment ensures that immune suppressive conditions become dominant and more diverse over time, increasing the likelihood of resistance to immunotherapy in advanced disease. Early immunotherapeutic interventions are therefore more likely to succeed. For melanoma, this has recently been demonstrated with neo-adjuvant dual immune checkpoint blockade becoming standard-of-care for high-risk stage-III disease and PD-1 blockade shown to be effective in stage-II disease. A most promising next step is to not just go earlier, but to also go local. By locally applying low doses of immune modulatory agents, the tumor microenvironment (TME) can be most effectively targeted while minimizing unwanted systemic side effects, which for neo-adjuvant immune checkpoint blockade can be considerable. Importantly, tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLN), proven vital for immunotherapy efficacy, are also potentiated by such local delivery strategies. As initiators of adaptive antitumor immunity, dendritic cells (DC) are often the first to be targeted by tumors in their struggle for survival, both in the TME and in TDLN. For early interventions, this may call for a shift in emphasis from lifting the brakes on T cells to DC activation. Indeed, in randomized Phase-2 trials, we found local delivery of a TLR9 ligand in clinical stage-I/II melanoma to result in DC activation in the sentinel lymph node and increased systemic rates of antitumor T cells, leading to remarkably prolonged recurrence-free survival. Our ever-expanding knowledge of early cancer immune-editing events in space and time should, in the near-future, translate into more effective and customized local interventions.

Don’t forget to consider induction of mTLS!

Catherine Sautès-Fridman

Universite Paris Cité

In my opinion, there is no doubt, the most promising future directions should aim at modulating the formation of mature tertiary lymphoid structures (mTLS) in tumors!

One advantages of mTLS is that they activate not only the T cell arm of the immune response—already demonstrated to be important for anti-tumor immunity—but also the B cell arm, leading to production of memory B cells and of IgG antibodies that bind to tumor cells, inducing tumor cell death. Mechanistically, we propose that immune complexes formed by tumor and self-antigens with IgG antibodies boost the anti-tumor response by increasing antigen presentation by dendritic cells in mTLS or in myeloid/T cells immune niches.

I think that what we learn from the studies deciphering the induction of mTLS in human tumors upon procedures including single or combination neoadjuvant therapies and intratumorally administrated Fc-optimized agonistic CD40 antibody as recently described, and their correlation with a beneficial therapeutic impact, lead us to propose next-generation strategies aiming to induce the most effective mTLS to optimize patient’s clinical outcome.

However, which is the best way to induce the most potent intratumoral mTLS? Can it work for all cancers the same way? These are challenging points. Further investigations are needed on the molecular events and pathways leading to formation of mTLS from the few cells of the immune niches and on the local conditions blocking their maturation. Such knowledge may help to adapt the current therapies and to develop novel therapies aiming to directly target mTLS.

Think local: Immune niches as the next frontier in cancer immunotherapy

Daniela S. Thommen

Netherlands Cancer Institute & Oncode Institute

The critical role of the immune system in eliminating cancer is highlighted by the success of immunotherapies like immune checkpoint blocking agents or adoptive T cell therapies. After uncovering the crucial role of progenitor-exhausted T cells for durable immunotherapy response, research efforts have largely focused on identifying strategies to boost this stem-like T cell pool. Recent findings, however, suggest that tumor-specific T cells require an additional activation step within the tumor microenvironment to gain full effector potential. Advances in single-cell and spatial technologies have uncovered organized immune hubs within tumors—such as antigen-presenting niches or tertiary lymphoid structures—that can enhance T cell activation and antitumor immunity via coordinated cytokine networks and tightly controlled cell-cell interactions. The future development of advanced model systems that allow to functionally study and perturb such immune niches will be critical to capture their diversity and to dissect their contribution to tumor control. Such insights are essential for designing therapies that can either promote local niche formation or substitute critical activation signals for immune effectors. First experimental studies supported the value of such approaches, for instance by enforcing T cell-APC interactions using bispecific antibodies or by directing stimulatory signals to effector cells using cis-targeted cytokines. Such precision immunotherapies that selectively deliver immunomodulatory cues only to defined immune cell populations offer particular promise to increase therapeutic potency whilst reducing toxicity.

A new era of multi-targeted, tumor-specific cancer therapies

Michael A. Curran

Department of Immunology, The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center

Rapid improvement in antibody engineering has already driven a renaissance in oncology encompassing the diaspora of antibody drug conjugates, the graduation of T cell engaging antibodies to solid tumor indications, and improved activity and tolerability for immune checkpoint blockers. Across all of these advances, however, the greatest impact on cancer outcomes will be the continued evolution of technologies to attach multiple payloads to antibodies at specific sites and with highly stable linkers that ensure cargo release only within tumors. There is an almost endless list of compounds beyond the cytotoxic payloads in use today that compromise tumor metabolism, immune privilege, proliferative capacity, invasive potential, and survival that never progressed clinically due to off-target toxicity to normal tissues. Over the next decade these will become the building blocks of a new generation of antibody drug conjugates that drive tumor rejection through the synergistic compromise of multiple “hallmarks of cancer” specifically delivered to all sites of cancer within the body. These drugs will become the new front line therapies for all cancer patients with systemic chemotherapy finally making its long-awaited departure as a standard of care at any stage of disease.
