Measuring the health benefits and costs of antimicrobial-resistant gonorrhea surveillance in the United States: an agent-based model and cost-effectiveness analysis
Authors: Marcy S. Ekanayake-Weber, Minttu M. Rönn, Xuecheng Yin, Katherine Hsu, Yunfei Li, Thomas L. Gift, Harrell Chesson, Rebekah Frankson, Joshua A. Salomon, Yonatan H. Grad, Reza Yaesoubi

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAntimicrobial resistant (AMR) gonorrhea poses an increasing threat to public health, yet evidence on the cost-effectiveness of surveillance systems that inform treatment policy is lacking. We assessed the long-term, population-level health benefits, costs, and cost-effectiveness of the Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Project (GISP), the US national surveillance system for AMR gonorrhea.MethodsWe developed an agent-based model of gonococcal transmission among men who have sex with men to project gonorrhea-associated costs (healthcare sector perspective) and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) lost under GISP and three hypothetical scenarios in the absence of GISP: Randomized Treatment, Test-of-Cure, and a Combination strategy, representing a combination of clinical strategies for treating gonorrhea.FindingsOver 20 years in a population of 100,000 men who have sex with men, all strategies resulted in similar overall gonorrhea incidence, but GISP achieved the lowest treatment failure incidence (1852 vs. 86,526, 144,063, and 119,258) and lowest AMR gonorrhea incidence (105,564 vs. 530,854, 631,123, and 575,309 infections). GISP resulted in lower costs and QALYs compared with each of the counterfactual strategies considered in our analysis.InterpretationOur findings highlight the health and economic benefits of GISP in controlling AMR gonorrhea and can be an efficient use of public health resources in the US. Additional studies are required to investigate the generalizability of our findings for other countries.FundingNational Institutes of Health.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Neisseria gonorrhoeae has developed resistance to all first-line antibiotics used to treat it. In the United States, the empiric treatment of gonorrhea is determined based on data provided by the Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Project (GISP), an ongoing sentinel surveillance system to monitor trends in antimicrobial resistant (AMR) gonorrhea. Despite the important role GISP plays in ensuring that patients with gonorrhea receive effective treatment, no evidence exists about its population's health impact and cost-effectiveness. We searched PubMed for articles published in the indexed literature up to Nov 18th, 2025 (the date of our search), using the following terms: ((gonorrhea) OR (gonococcal)) AND (surveillance) AND (resistan∗) AND ((model∗ OR cost-effectiveness)). We found 152 studies, of which 19 assessed the impact of AMR gonorrhea surveillance programs. None focused on evaluating the cost-effectiveness of GISP compared to counterfactual strategies in the absence of GISP.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this study is the first to assess the population health impact and cost-effectiveness of GISP. Using an agent-based dynamic transmission model of gonorrhea for men who have sex with men (MSM) in the US, we compared the health and cost outcomes under GISP and several counterfactual scenarios to ensure the effective treatment of gonorrhea in the absence of GISP, including randomized treatment, test-of-cure, and a combination of randomized treatment and test-of-cure. We found that GISP, along with other counterfactual strategies considered here, led to a similar gonorrhea incidence rate. However, GISP resulted in the lowest treatment failure rate and the incidence of AMR cases. Compared to strategies considered here, GISP was demonstrated to be cost-saving under various scenarios.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our study demonstrates the effectiveness of GISP in slowing the spread of AMR gonorrhea, which is achieved at no additional cost, compared to alternatives in the absence of GISP. This highlights the importance and value of establishing and maintaining GISP. Additional studies are needed to investigate the cost-effectiveness of national surveillance systems of AMR gonorrhea in other settings.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistant (AMR) infections are a major threat to global health,1 and robust surveillance systems are essential for confronting this problem by providing high-quality data and informing treatment decisions.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 One such surveillance system is the US national surveillance system for AMR gonorrhea, the Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Project (GISP). Established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 1986,10, 11, 12 GISP is one of the longest-running and largest AMR gonorrhea surveillance systems globally.13 Under GISP, around 30 sentinel sites associated with STD clinics submit up to 25 samples per month from known gonorrhea cases for culture-based drug susceptibility testing. These data are then analyzed at the regional and national levels to monitor trends in AMR gonorrhea. The GISP data are used to ensure that only antibiotics with low (usually <5%) resistance prevalence of resistance are included in national treatment guidelines.14,15 Despite the importance of GISP in monitoring trends in AMR gonorrhea and informing treatment guidelines, quantitative evidence on the value GISP is lacking, in terms of improving the health outcomes of patients with gonorrhea and the overall costs associated with controlling gonorrhea in the US.

In this study, we evaluated the health effects, costs, and cost-effectiveness of GISP. For comparison, we designed three hypothetical counterfactual scenarios for how providers might have attempted to ensure the effective treatment of AMR gonorrhea if GISP had not been established. We focused on men who have sex with men (MSM), since this is a subpopulation with a particularly high burden of gonorrhea in the US currently.16 Around one-third of gonorrhea cases occur in MSM, and MSM also bear a disproportionate burden of AMR gonorrhea.17 We developed an agent-based model of gonorrhea transmission, treatment, and surveillance to project the overall costs and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) lost due to gonorrhea under GISP and under hypothetical scenarios in the absence of GISP. Our results shed light on the costs and benefits of GISP specifically and the usefulness of sentinel surveillance systems for AMR gonorrhea.13

Methods

Model summary

We developed an agent-based model of N. gonorrhoeae transmission among sexually active men who have sex with men (MSM) in the US. Our model represents how N. gonorrhoeae might spread in a population of 100,000 MSM over a 25-year period, including strains resistant to a specific antimicrobial treatment (i.e., AMR strains). Gonococcal infections in the model are meant to represent a mix of urogenital, pharyngeal, and rectal infections, but are not explicitly distinguished by anatomical site. Infectious individuals may infect others with N. gonorrhoeae through sexual contact (Fig. 1). During each time interval that they are infectious, an individual has a probability of finding a new casual sexual partner and infecting that partner with N. gonorrhoeae (see Supplementary Methods, Sections S1.1 and S1.2, Figs. S1 and S2, Tables S1 and S2 contain more details on model design). The population is divided into two sexual activity groups: high and low. The high-activity group is assumed to have a higher rate of sexual contact. Sexual partners are preferentially selected from within the individual’s own activity group, with the degree of preference determined by an assortativity parameter.Fig. 1Diagram of the major processes of the model, showing the generic treatment pathway. “sus A” and “sus B” represent susceptibility to drug A and drug B, respectively, and “resist A” and “resist B” represent resistance to drug A and drug B, respectively. Infection detection in our model varies depending on whether the infection is symptomatic or asymptomatic. Individuals with symptomatic infections are assumed to always seek care, with some delay, while asymptomatic infections can only be detected through screening. Otherwise, asymptomatic infections remain undetected and infectious until the infection clears naturally. After the infection is detected, an individual receives antibiotic treatment, which can be successful or unsuccessful, depending on the infection’s susceptibility or resistance. Even if the treatment ultimately succeeds, the individual can continue to transmit the infection until the infection clears. Clearance times are drawn from an exponential distribution with an expected value of three days. If treatment fails for symptomatic individuals, we assume that symptomatic individuals will continue to seek treatment (with some delay between first treatment and subsequent retreatment; see Supplementary Methods, Section S1.1). However, these individuals may not abstain from sexual activity,18 which can potentially lead to onward transmission. If treatment fails for an asymptomatic infection, it is assumed that the individual does not abstain from sexual activity and remains infectious until the infection clears naturally or is detected through future screenings.

The model includes four gonococcal strains (susceptible to all modeled antibiotics, resistant to only drug A, resistant to only drug B, and resistant to both drug A and drug B). Parameters for fitness costs for resistance were set to 0 in our main analysis model, as fitness costs for AMR gonorrhea appear to be low23; however, we tested scenarios with some fitness cost for resistance (see Supplementary Methods, Section S1.3). We assumed that resistance to drug A or to drug B could develop during treatment with these antibiotics and could be imported into the simulated population, as gonococcal infections resistant to ceftriaxone have already been reported in the US,24 the potential for resistance to zoliflodacin has been demonstrated in vitro,25 and gepotidacin resistance was detected in phase 2 trials.26 Therefore, the model has two routes for a resistant-strain infection to arise, other than through inheritance: resistance seeding, where resistant infections were “imported” from outside the simulated population, or developed during treatment, where a susceptible infection could survive treatment by developing resistance to the antibiotic used. More detail on the source of resistant strains in the model is available in the Supplementary Methods, Section S1.1. Multi-drug resistance could arise in two ways: developing during treatment, or secondary exposure. If an individual is infected with a resistant strain (e.g., resistant to A) and receives treatment (e.g., with B), they have a very small chance of developing resistance to the treatment drug, resulting in resistance to both A and B (i.e., multidrug resistance). Alternatively, a multidrug-resistant strain could arise through secondary exposure due to sexual activity. We assume that infection with one resistant strain does not confer cross-immunity against other resistant strains (e.g., an individual infected with a strain resistant to drug A could become infected with a strain resistant to drug B). When such a secondary exposure occurs, the exposed individual is now considered resistant to both drugs A and B, and they can transmit this new multidrug-resistant strain onward to other individuals. We assume that a last-line antibiotic, modeled after a broad-spectrum antibiotic such as ertapenem, is available and is 100% effective against multidrug-resistant infections. In the model, this drug is used when all other drug options have been exhausted, according to each particular strategy (see below).

Model calibration and parameter estimation

To model gonorrhea disease burden, we used a likelihood-based approach27 to select trajectories that best fit estimates of prevalence for MSM,28 rate of detected gonorrhea cases for MSM,29 and the proportion of detected cases presenting with symptoms for MSM30 in the absence of strains resistant to drugs A (representing ceftriaxone), B (representing a new antibiotic in the pipeline), or X (a future antibiotic). We calibrated to pseudo-equilibrium conditions. To find simulated trajectories that fit these estimates, we drew 900,000 samples from specified prior probability distributions assigned for model parameters (n = 23; for more detail, see Supplementary Tables S1 and S2), and produced trajectories simulated over ten years (5-year burn-in period + 5-year calibration period) for each parameter combination. To measure how well each simulated trajectory aligned with data, we calculated the likelihood of observed estimates on prevalence, detected cases, and the percentage of detected cases with symptoms. We then selected the 300 trajectories with the highest likelihood. We then eliminated trajectories in which resistance to Drug A never reaches 5% throughout the simulation, ensuring we use trajectories that realistically describe the spread of resistance. This resulted in 143 final trajectories. Additional details about our calibration procedure are provided in Supplementary Methods, Section S1.1. See also Supplementary Fig. S3 for the resulting parameter estimates.

GISP and counterfactual strategies

We used our model to project disease burden and the costs and QALYs lost associated with gonorrhea under GISP and three other counterfactual strategies, which illustrate how providers might have attempted to treat patients with gonorrhea in the absence of GISP.

Under the GISP strategy (illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1), which represents the status quo, treatment is empiric and according to the guideline, which recommends a therapy with a low (<5%) resistance prevalence for first-line treatment. The annual prevalence of resistance to different antibiotics is estimated based on the results of drug susceptibility testing on isolates collected from the first 25 detected cases each month (i.e., sentinel surveillance). We assumed that the initial treatment guideline is to use either drug A or drug B, with equal probability. Subsequently, if resistance to one drug (but not the other) is estimated to be 5% or greater, then that drug is removed as a treatment option. If resistance to both drugs individually is 5% but drug X is not yet available, the recommended first-line therapy will be a combination of drugs A and B. If drug X is not yet available when the multi-resistant strain passes the 5% threshold, we assumed that the last-line antibiotic (ertapenem) will be used as the first-line therapy until drug X becomes available. If drug X became available before both A and B individually are removed as first-line recommendations, then drug X is simply added to the treatment recommendation, and patients are randomly assigned treatment of either A, B, or X, with equal probability. The estimation of population resistance and updating of treatment recommendations only occurs once per year using isolates from the previous 12 months; thus, some time lag between AMR gonorrhea prevalence and updated treatment recommendations is inevitable.

Under the Randomized Treatment (RT) strategy, to treat an infected individual, drugs A, B, or X (if available) can be used as first-line therapy with equal probability. If treatment with X is selected initially, this is 100% effective, and so no failure can result. If either drug A or B is selected and this treatment is not successful for a symptomatic patient, the other drug will be used as second-line therapy, and then drug X or ertapenem would be used if necessary. Since surveillance is not available under this counterfactual, neither drug A nor drug B are removed as first- and second-line treatment options, even if resistance to these drugs spreads in the population.

Under the Test-of-Cure (TOC) strategy (illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2), detected cases are first treated with drug A. Then, whether asymptomatic or symptomatic, the patients are re-tested following treatment to verify the success of treatment. If the re-test is positive, the individual receives treatment with drug B and then another re-test. If this second re-test is also positive, the patient then receives either drug X (if available) or ertapenem and is re-tested a final time. We assume that an individual who receives initial treatment would return for the Test-of-Cure assessment with 80% probability if they were symptomatic, and 50% probability if they were asymptomatic. We varied the probability that a patient would return for test-of-cure between 20% and 100% for both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases in our sensitivity analysis (results available in Supplementary Results). For symptomatic cases not returning due to Test-of-Cure (but continuing treatment due to their symptoms), treatment follows the path shown in Fig. 1, with drug A always remaining the first-line treatment, drug B the second-line (if treatment with drug A is not successful and the patient seeks retreatment), and drug X or ertapenem, the last-line (if treatment with neither drug A nor drug B is successful and the patient continues seeking retreatment).

We also designed a combination strategy (Combo), which is a combination of Randomized Treatment and Test-of-Cure. This was meant to represent a more realistic counterfactual scenario, where cases within the same population might be treated using different techniques when surveillance data are not available. For each detected case, the patient is randomly assigned to either Randomized Treatment or Test-of-Cure. The base probabilities of being assigned to each treatment path were 50-50 respectively, but these were varied in sensitivity analysis (sensitivity analysis results available in Supplementary Materials). For Test-of-Cure under Combo, we assumed that a similar proportion of patients would not return for the test-of-cure assessment as under the Test-of-Cure scenario (i.e., 80% for symptomatic and 50% for asymptomatic).

Outcomes and cost-effectiveness analysis

We collected several relevant outcomes to measure disease burden, trends in drug resistance, usage of antibiotics, monetary costs, and disutility. We recorded annual number of cases detected, annual number of incident infections (both detected and undetected) and point prevalence (current infections, both detected and undetected, divided by population size) at the end of each year. We calculated treatment failure rate as the number of treatment attempts that were unsuccessful divided by the total number of treatment attempts; this included repeated treatment attempts on the same patient. To calculate the proportion of infections belonging to a particular strain, we recorded the number of new infections with that strain that year and divided by the total number of new infections that year (i.e., total gonorrhea incidence).

Our model includes medical costs from clinic visits, diagnostic tests, drug susceptibility tests, and treatment with antibiotic drugs (values and references in Supplementary Table S3), and costs associated with sequelae, including epididymitis and disseminated gonococcal infection (values and references in Supplementary Table S3), in 2022 US dollars. For each testing event, a discrete random value between 1 and 3 is generated. If the random value is 1, only one site is tested during this test event, and the cost of only one test is added to the total. If the random value is 2, the cost of two tests is added to the total (to represent the testing of two sites); and so on. The cost of maintaining GISP includes the cost of 25 drug susceptibility tests per month in the population; cost per test reflected all costs associated with performing tests, including overhead costs, and these costs approximated the annual per-site budget of GISP sentinel sites. We summarized health outcomes including quality of life losses associated with different health states related to gonorrhea using quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). We considered quality of life decrements due to symptomatic infection, ertapenem side-effects, and gonorrhea-associated sequelae (values and references in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5.31,32 Both costs and QALYs were discounted at an annual rate of 3%.

We took the healthcare sector perspective in our cost-effectiveness analysis. We used Combo as our reference strategy, since this best approximated what might have happened without GISP. We followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) to conduct and report our cost-effectiveness analysis.33, 34, 35 The checklist is available in the Supplemental Methods (Section S1.4).

We also compared the performance of strategies in terms of their impact on the population’s net monetary benefit (NMB) based on varying values of cost-effectiveness threshold.34,36 These analyses can be found in Supplementary Methods, Section S1.3.

Sensitivity analyses

To investigate the robustness of our conclusions to certain model parameters and assumptions, we performed the following one-way sensitivity analyses. First, we assumed that the drug X becomes available in year 10 or year 15 or never, rather than the base case value of 20. Second, we assumed that the treatment guideline is revised in the GISP strategy when the resistance prevalence ranges from 3% to 7%, rather than the base case value of 5%. Third, for the Test-of-Cure strategy, we varied the probability that patients would return for retesting separately for symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, each between 20% and 100%, rather than the base case value of 80% for symptomatic and 50% for asymptomatic patients. Fourth, for the Combo strategy, we varied the probability that each case would be assigned to Randomized Treatment or Test-of-Cure to be 25–75 and 75-25 to compare to the base case of 50-50. We also investigated an additional scenario, wherein there was widespread access to rapid Drug Susceptibility Testing (rDST), and another wherein there was limited access to rapid Drug Susceptibility Testing in combination with randomized treatment and test-of-cure, which we called the Mixed Testing (MT) scenario. We also investigated whether our conclusions changed if we implemented screening heterogeneity, i.e., the different sexual activity groups were screened differently, with the high activity group undergoing more frequent screening. Finally, we investigated whether our conclusions changed if there were fitness costs for resistance (i.e., reduced transmission for resistant strains). More details on these scenarios are available in the Supplementary Methods, Section S1.3. The results of these analyses can be found in the Supplementary Results.

Total cumulative incidence of gonorrhea (both detected and undetected cases) and usage of ertapenem were similar among the strategies considered here, but treatment failure rate varied more markedly across strategies (Fig. 3, Table 1). In particular, GISP had the lowest treatment failure rate; GISP also had slightly lower mean incidence (see Table 1). Differences among the strategies were also apparent when examining trends over time (Fig. 4). Under GISP, the incidence of gonorrhea infections (both detected and undetected) remained stable, although resistance to drug A increased for all simulated trajectories and resistance to drug B and multidrug resistance increased for some trajectories (Fig. 4, first column). For some trajectories where resistance to drug B peaked before drug X became available, the GISP strategy resulted in high use of ertapenem (Fig. 4U). Overall, GISP was successful at limiting the number of AMR gonorrhea cases relative to the other strategies (Table 1). The Randomized Treatment strategy was not effective at limiting AMR strains, and this is reflected in the exponential growth of drug resistance under this strategy (Fig. 4, second column). The cumulative incidence of AMR gonorrhea (both detected and undetected) was more than 5 times as high as under Randomized Treatment than under GISP (Table 1). The Randomized Treatment strategy resulted in use of ertapenem in the years before drug X became available (Fig. 4V), although it had the overall lowest average use of ertapenem. The Test-of-Cure strategy had the highest gonorrhea incidence, the highest incidence of resistance (almost 6x as high as under GISP), and the second-highest usage of ertapenem (Table 1). Our sensitivity analysis suggests that varying when drug X becomes available between year 10 and year 20 minimally impacts the incidence of gonorrhea and the treatment failure rate, and moderately affects the use of ertapenem and the relative cost-effectiveness of the strategies (see Supplementary Figs. S5–S9 for full results). As we increased the time to drug X becoming available, GISP is slightly more cost-effective on average than the other scenarios, although there remains extensive overlap in outcome, particularly between GISP and Randomized Treatment (Supplementary Fig. S9, third and fourth row). Our other sensitivity analyses suggest that other counterfactual parameters would have only minor effects on the relative cost-effectiveness of the strategies, and would not affect our assessment (see Supplementary Figs. S10–S17 for full results). Our analysis of the rapid Drug Susceptibility Testing and Mixed Testing scenarios (see Supplementary Results, Section S2.2, Figs. S18S20, and Table S12 for scenario details) suggests that wide-spread availability of rapid drug susceptibility testing would result in better disease outcomes than GISP (e.g., less AMR gonorrhea, less usage of ertapenem, fewer QALYs lost; see Supplementary Figs. S21–S24 and Tables S13 and S14 for full results) but would cost substantially more than GISP over 20 years. Limited usage of drug susceptibility testing in the Mixed Testing scenario produced similar outcomes to the Combo scenario. Finally, introducing heterogeneity in screening frequency and fitness costs (see Supplementary Results, Sections S2.3 and S2.4, Figs. S25–S40, and Tables S15 and S16 for full results) only minimally impacted our outcomes.

Discussion

Guidelines for the empiric treatment of gonorrhea are determined based, in part, on data provided by GISP, the US national surveillance system for monitoring trends in AMR gonorrhea.10,15 In this study, we assessed the value of GISP for the MSM population in the US by projecting the health and financial burdens associated with gonorrhea under GISP and under counterfactual scenarios which illustrate clinical management strategies in the absence of GISP. These counterfactuals included 1) randomly selecting treatment from available antibiotics (random treatment, or Randomized Treatment), 2) repeated diagnostic testing following each treatment attempt (Test-of-Cure), and 3) a combination of Randomized Treatment and Test-of-Cure (Combo). We found that gonorrhea incidence cases were similar among scenarios, though treatment failure rate, trends in AMR gonorrhea, and consumption of antimicrobials varied greatly. GISP was effective at maintaining a low treatment failure rate and a low incidence of AMR gonorrhea over 20 years. Compared to the counterfactual scenarios, GISP demonstrated to be cost-saving on average while improving the population QALYs. GISP and Randomized Treatment had similar average costs and QALYs, but Randomized Treatment resulted in more than 4 times as many AMR infections as GISP.

In our analysis, the cost-effectiveness of GISP changed moderately with the availability of novel antibiotics (i.e., “drug X” in our analysis). Under the scenario where drug X is not expected to become available in the next 25 years, GISP performed slightly more favorably, relative to the counterfactual strategies. This is consistent with the results of a recent modeling study that demonstrated the cost and health outcomes under GISP are affected by the antibiotic pipeline.31 While GISP was marginally cost-effective compared to other strategies considered here, it could lead to high use of last-line treatment (here, ertapenem) if a new drug suitable for first-line therapy does not reach the market in time (see Supplementary Fig. S5). Ertapenem has high cost and adverse side effects, and hence, if used as first-line therapy, it could result in substantial cost and loss in population health. Rapid drug-susceptibility tests could keep treatment failure rates low but it is not clear when they become widely available to use as a strategy to control AMR gonorrhea.

In the absence of an AMR gonorrhea surveillance system to inform the national treatment guidelines, the selection of antibiotic could be made at the point-of-care for individual patients. These patient-level strategies might include those three alternative strategies we evaluated here (i.e., Randomized Treatment, Test-of-Cure, and Combo). We found that these strategies are unlikely to be more cost-effective than GISP, since they are projected to cost the same or more and produce similar health outcomes as measured by QALYs. More importantly, the long-term health and economic consequences of AMR gonorrhea may not be fully captured by cost-effectiveness analysis; hence, the finding that, compared to other strategies, GISP is more effective in mitigating antimicrobial resistance further underscores its benefits.

As with any modeling study, there were several simplifications and assumptions made in model development which limit the applicability and interpretation of our results. Firstly, we only modeled gonorrhea in men who have sex with men (“MSM”) in the US. In the US, gonorrhea prevalence and the levels of AMR gonorrhea are both higher in MSM than in men who have sex with women (MSW) and women.17,37 We note that GISP includes isolates only from men with urethral infection who seek treatment in participating sentinel sites each month. In 2017, GISP was updated to the Enhanced Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Program (eGISP), which also include isolates from women and from different anatomical sites of infection. While the prevalence of gonorrhea and AMR gonorrhea is lower among men who have sex with women and women, the long-term impact per gonococcal infection on quality of life is much greater for women than for men.38 These sequelae, which include pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, and neonatal complications, could lead to much greater loss in QALYs due to gonorrhea than we modeled here, since we only included MSM. Future studies can investigate the cost-effectiveness of eGISP.

Secondly, due to high computational burden of our agent-based model, we limited our analysis to an MSM population of size 100,000, consistent with previous models.39 We do not anticipate that increasing the population size impacts the relative performance of strategies considered here, provided that the GISP sampling proportion remains the same. If instead the population increased from 100,000 but still only 25 isolates were being sampled each month, then GISP might be less able to detect increases in AMR gonorrhea, and therefore, not prevent as many treatment failures and AMR infections. However, finding the optimal level of sampling for GISP is outside the purview of this study.

Thirdly, we assumed that when the GISP isolates are reviewed annually, if the prevalence of resistance to current first-line therapy has reached the specified switch threshold, the recommendation for the first-line therapy is immediately updated for the entire population. However, a recent modeling study suggests that revising treatment recommendations locally, based on the local estimate for the resistance prevalence could increase the effectiveness of first-line therapy and extend the useful lifespan of newer antibiotics.40 This suggests that the cost-effectiveness of GISP would also depend on whether the data from GISP surveillance sites are used to inform treatment guidelines at the local level or at the national level. Given that our model represents a population of size 100,000, our projection would be more suitable for the scenario where GISP estimates are used locally. Using GISP estimates to inform national treatment guidelines could lower the cost-effectiveness of GISP due to overlooking the local prevalence and trends in AMR gonorrhea.

Fourthly, we were not able to represent all possible costs of each of the counterfactual scenarios, which might affect their relative cost-effectiveness. For GISP’s surveillance mechanism, we used the cost of 25 drug susceptibility tests each month. While we recognize that this is an inexact way of accounting for GISP’s costs, the total annual cost of surveillance is similar to unpublished CDC estimates of the annual cost of a GISP sentinel surveillance site. Therefore, despite not explicitly modeling overhead costs of surveillance, we conclude that our overall GISP cost estimates are realistic. However, we note that the economic and health costs of using ertapenem at the scale predicted by our model may be difficult to anticipate, and may represent an additional cost of using GISP which we were unable to account for.

Finally, we did not distinguish among anatomical sites of gonococcal infection in this model. In addition to the urogenital region, gonorrhea is known to infect the rectum and the pharynx, especially among MSM. Infection of these sites is usually asymptomatic and may also have different likelihoods of developing antimicrobial resistance. Accounting for multi-site infection, including differing likelihoods of developing symptoms and drug resistance, could be a priority for future gonorrhea modeling studies.

Using an agent-based model of MSM, we demonstrated that GISP is effective at limiting the health and financial burdens associated with gonorrhea among the MSM population in the US. Our analysis suggests that empiric treatment recommendations informed by GISP could keep the treatment failure rate and treatment costs relatively low. In fact, GISP costs about the same as our naïve randomized treatment (RT) strategy, but GISP prevented treatment failure and limited the incidence of AMR gonorrhea. Without a national surveillance system to guide empiric treatments, gonorrhea treatment would likely be informed by individualized care strategies (such as test-of-cure). Our analysis suggests that a test-of-cure strategy, even with very high probability that patients will return for re-testing, is not a viable alternative to GISP, since it is likely to cost more while leading to higher levels of antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, our model suggests that GISP has been an efficient use of public health resources. Relative to treating gonorrhea in its absence, GISP has likely helped to slow the development of antimicrobial resistance, reduced the rate of treatment failure, and improved health outcomes for decades, while costing similar to a naïve strategy.
