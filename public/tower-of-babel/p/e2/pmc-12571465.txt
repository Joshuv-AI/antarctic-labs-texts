Evaluating immunohistochemical and genetic expression of green-mediated nano-enhanced glass ionomer cement: An animal model study
Authors: Soorya Ganesh, Jessy Paulraj, Subhabrata Maiti, Karthik Ganesh Mohanraj, Selvaraj Jayaraman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:This study aimed to compare the immunohistochemical (IHC) and genetic expression in tissues exposed to green-mediated nano-enhanced glass ionomer cement (GIC) and conventional GIC using an animal model.Materials and Methods:This study investigated the IHC and genetic expression of green-mediated nano-enhanced GIC reinforced with chitosan, titanium, zirconia, and hydroxyapatite nanoparticles. These nanoparticles were integrated into the GIC powder, and nine disc-shaped specimens (5 mm diameter and 2 mm thickness) were fabricated. The specimens were implanted subcutaneously into the dorsal region of 18 Wistar albino rats. Group I received conventional GIC (n = 6), Group II received nano-modified GIC (n = 6), and Group III was the sham control (n = 6). Histological slides were prepared at 3 and 30 days for IHC analysis of interleukin (IL)-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and gene expression analysis by real-time polymerase chain reaction. Statistical analysis was performed with significance set at the 0.05 level.Results:Both the GIC groups showed increased IL-6 and TNF-α on day 3 compared to sham, indicating inflammation. On day 3, IL-6 was higher in conventional GIC (1.26 ± 0.020) and nano-modified GIC (1.23 ± 0.011) compared to sham (1.0 ± 0.001), but by day 30, the levels were similar across all the groups. TNF-α was elevated in both the GIC groups on day 3, with conventional GIC (1.15 ± 0.141) and nano-modified GIC (1.05 ± 0.109), and equal across the groups by day 30.Conclusion:Green-mediated nano-enhanced GIC caused less inflammation and upregulated healing genes compared to conventional GIC, suggesting better integration and improved clinical outcomes. Long-term studies are needed to confirm these benefits.

INTRODUCTION

Glass ionomer cements (GICs) have been extensively utilized in restorative dentistry due to their favorable properties, including chemical adhesion to tooth structures, sustained fluoride release, biocompatibility, and ease of handling.[1] Despite these advantages, conventional GICs exhibit inherent limitations such as inferior mechanical strength, low wear resistance, and reduced fracture toughness, which can compromise their long-term clinical performance.[23] These shortcomings have prompted ongoing research aimed at enhancing the physical, mechanical, and biological properties of GICs to meet the growing demands of modern restorative dentistry.

The advent of nanotechnology has opened new avenues for improving the properties of GICs by incorporating nanoparticles into their formulation. Studies have demonstrated that nano-enhanced GICs exhibit superior mechanical strength, enhanced wear resistance, improved fracture toughness, and better esthetic qualities compared to conventional counterparts.[456] In particular, the incorporation of nanoparticles has also been shown to increase fluoride release and bioactivity, making these materials more effective in caries prevention and promoting remineralization.[7] However, despite these promising advancements, concerns regarding the chemical composition and potential toxicity of synthetic nanoparticles remain a topic of debate, hindering their widespread clinical acceptance.[8] In this context, phytomedicine has emerged as a promising and sustainable approach to address these concerns. Phytomedicine, which involves the use of plant-based materials or extracts for therapeutic and biomedical applications, has been recognized for its ability to minimize toxicity while maintaining or enhancing the functional properties of biomaterials.[910] Recent studies have explored the green synthesis of nanoparticles using plant extracts, providing a biocompatible and eco-friendly alternative to conventional chemical synthesis methods. Green-synthesized nanoparticles have demonstrated favorable characteristics, including reduced cytotoxicity, enhanced bioactivity, and improved mechanical properties.

Among various nanoparticles explored for dental applications, zirconia and hydroxyapatite (HA) have gained significant attention due to their excellent mechanical and biological properties. Zirconium and its oxide (zirconia) are widely known for their superior dimensional stability, toughness, and mechanical strength, attributes comparable to stainless steel, making them highly suitable for biomedical use. Jain et al. highlighted that incorporating green-synthesized zirconia nanoparticles into GIC formulations significantly enhances their mechanical performance, indicating the potential of such modifications in improving the clinical efficacy of restorative materials.[11] Similarly, HA nanoparticles, especially when synthesized through green methods, have shown to considerably improve the functional properties of GICs, including mechanical strength and bioactivity, thereby expanding their potential applications in dentistry.[12] Furthermore, the incorporation of various nanoparticles such as chitosan, titanium, zirconia, and HA into GIC formulations has been reported to yield smoother surface finishes and promote a random yet uniform distribution of nanoparticles within the cement matrix.[13] These structural improvements are directly linked to enhanced mechanical performance and better handling characteristics, positioning nano-modified GICs as promising alternatives to conventional materials.

While the mechanical and physical enhancements of nano-modified GICs are well documented, there remains a significant gap in understanding their biological interactions, particularly with regard to the host tissue response upon implantation. In restorative dentistry, it is imperative to evaluate the biological behavior of materials to ensure their safe integration and long-term success in clinical applications. One critical aspect of this evaluation involves understanding the immunohistochemical (IHC) responses and gene expression patterns elicited by the material. IHC techniques allow for the detection and localization of specific proteins or antigens within biological tissues, providing insights into the material’s interaction with surrounding cells, inflammatory responses, and overall biocompatibility. Excessive or prolonged inflammation is a major concern, as it can impede tissue healing and integration of restorative materials. Two key pro-inflammatory markers – interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) – are frequently used as indicators of inflammatory responses to biomaterials. Elevated expression levels of these cytokines are often associated with tissue damage, chronic inflammation, and impaired healing. Gene expression profiling further complements IHC analysis by providing a deeper understanding of the molecular pathways activated in response to the material. IL-6 plays a pivotal role in immune modulation and tissue repair, while its overexpression is often linked to chronic inflammation and disease progression. Similarly, TNF-α regulates immune responses and inflammation, and its elevated levels are indicative of potential adverse tissue reactions. Evaluating the gene expression profiles of IL-6 and TNF-α following implantation of nano-enhanced GICs can offer valuable insights into their biological safety and therapeutic potential.

Despite the promising advancements in green-mediated nano-enhanced GICs, limited scientific evidence exists regarding their biological response, particularly concerning IHC and gene expression profiles in vivo. While previous studies have demonstrated improvements in mechanical properties, antibacterial efficacy, and overall biocompatibility of nano-modified GICs, comprehensive evaluations of their potential to induce inflammation and their molecular interactions with host tissues remain underexplored. Moreover, the role of green synthesis in mitigating the toxicity concerns associated with conventional nanoparticles, while maintaining or enhancing the material’s functional properties, warrants thorough investigation, especially in animal models that simulate clinical conditions. The current study addresses this critical gap by systematically evaluating the IHC markers (IL-6 and TNF-α) and gene expression profiles in tissues exposed to green-mediated nano-enhanced GIC following subcutaneous implantation in rats. By comparing these outcomes with conventional GIC, this study aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of the biological compatibility, inflammatory response, and healing potential of green-synthesized nano-modified GICs. The findings of this research will contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the safe clinical translation of eco-friendly, nano-enhanced restorative materials in dentistry. Hence, this study aims to evaluate and compare the IHC and gene expression patterns in tissues exposed to green-mediated nano-enhanced GIC and conventional GIC in an animal model. It is hypothesized that there is no significant difference in the IHC and gene expression profiles between green-mediated nano-enhanced GIC and conventional GIC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Research design and ethical approval

The in vivo animal study received ethical approval from the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (Reg. No. 856/PO/ReBi/S/04/CCSEA, dated 06.09.2022) and the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (BRULAC/SDCH/SIMATS/IAEC/04-2024/09), and the study was carried out in accordance with the approved guidelines at the Biomedical Research Unit and Lab Animal Centre (BRULAC), Saveetha Dental College and Hospitals, Saveetha University, India, in June 2024.

Animal study design and group allocation

The sample size was calculated based on a previous study by Menezes-Silva R et al.,[14] aiming for 80% power and 95% confidence. Using an alpha of 0.05 and an effect size of 1.64, a minimum of six animals per group was required, totaling 18. Eighteen female Wistar albino rats (200–300 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Medicine, Chennai, and housed under controlled conditions (25°C ± 1°C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to water and rat pellets (Biogen Animal Health, Bangalore, India). The rats were randomly divided into three groups: Group I (conventional GIC), Group II (nano-modified GIC), and Group III (sham control), with six rats in each group.

Preparation of nanoparticles

Nanoparticles were synthesized using a green synthesis approach. Chitosan (Ch) nanoparticles were formed by mixing eucalyptus extract with chitosan, glacial acetic acid, and distilled water. Titanium oxide (Ti) nanoparticles were prepared by combining neem extract with a 50 mM TiO2 solution. Zirconium oxide (Zr) nanoparticles were synthesized by mixing aloe vera extract with a 20 mM ZrO2 solution and heating overnight at 340°C–350°C. HA nanoparticles were obtained by blending Moringa oleifera extract with eggshell-derived CaO and orthophosphoric acid at a Ca/P ratio of 1.67. Following Rahman et al.’s[15] one-pot method, all solutions were stirred at 80°C, refluxed with ethanol, evaporated, and then freeze-dried at −92°C for 48 h to yield the final nanoparticle powder.

Fabrication of samples

Type IX GIC (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was modified by incorporating 5% by weight of chitosan, titanium, zirconia, and HA nanoparticles. The combined powder was mixed in an agate mortar for 30 min to ensure uniform nanoparticle dispersion. The liquid component of the cement remained unaltered. The resulting mixture was prepared into a paste and placed into a stainless steel mold (5 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness), with a microscope slide used to cover and shape the surface. After solidification, the specimens were removed, measured for thickness, and inspected for defects. Defective specimens were discarded, and the remaining ones were polished and sterilized under ultraviolet light, while instruments were autoclaved.

Procedure of surgery

Rats were anesthetized with intraperitoneal ketamine (70 mg/kg) and intramuscular xylazine (10 mg/kg). Once unconscious, they were placed in the prone position on a sterile board. The implantation site, 5–6 cm from the dorsal midline toward the tail, was shaved and disinfected with povidone iodine. A 1 cm incision was made to create a subcutaneous pouch, and the test material was inserted 2 cm deep [Figure 1]. The wound was then sutured and disinfected.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Following each experimental period (on days 3 and 30), three animals from each group (Group I, Group II, and Group III) were evaluated at the designated time points. The materials and surrounding tissue were excised and cut into rectangular pieces for proper embedding and sectioning. After fixation in 10% buffered formalin, the tissue samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 3–5 μm slices, and deparaffinized. Antigen retrieval was performed using heat and buffer treatment to unmask IL-6 and TNF-α antigens. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked, followed by incubation with primary anti-IL-6 and anti-TNF-α antibodies. A secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase was applied, and IL-6 and TNF-α expression was visualized through a chromogenic reaction with diaminobenzidine (DAB). The tissue sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, mounted on slides, and examined under a light microscope at ×10 magnification to evaluate IL-6 and TNF-α expression. The prepared slides were subsequently analyzed.

Analysis for gene expression

RNA was extracted from control and experimental tissue samples using the TRIzol kit (Invitrogen). After homogenizing 100 mg of tissue with Total RNA Isolation Reagent (TRIR) reagent and chloroform, the mixture was centrifuged, and the aqueous phase was collected. Isopropanol was added, and the RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol. The RNA concentration was measured spectrometrically. cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg of RNA using a reverse transcriptase kit (Eurogentec). For real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR), a reaction mixture with primers, 2× buffer (Takara SYBR Green), water, and β-actin was prepared, and PCR was run for 40 cycles. IL-6 and TNF-α expression was quantified from the melt and amplification curves.

Euthanasia

Nine rats were sacrificed at the specified time points (3rd day and 30th day) by being euthanized through inhalation of carbon dioxide gas in a sealed chamber.

Statistical analysis

The biochemical profile data were processed and analyzed using SPSS software, version 21.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance was used to determine significant differences between the groups. Pairwise comparisons were carried out with the Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Immunohistochemical analysis of interleukin-6 expression in rat skin tissue

IHC analysis on day 3 showed IL-6 expression in both the groups, with increased IL-6 (arrows) in Group I due to inflammation triggered by the test material. On day 30, Group I had higher IL-6 expression (arrows) compared to Group II, which showed reduced inflammation and gradual healing without cytotoxicity [Figure 2].

Gene analysis of interleukin-6 expression and tumor necrosis factor-alpha expression in rat skin tissue

The amplification and melt peak analysis graphs for IL-6 and TNF-α are shown [Figure 4]. IL-6 analysis showed higher expression in conventional GIC (1.26 ± 0.020) and nano-modified GIC (1.23 ± 0.011) compared to sham control (1.0 ± 0.001) on day 3 (P = 0.001). By day 30, IL-6 expression decreased in both the groups, with no significant difference between conventional GIC (1.09 ± 0.133) and nano-modified GIC (0.99 ± 0.005). TNF-α expression analysis showed no significant difference between the conventional GIC and nano-modified GIC on day 3, indicating similar TNF-α levels. Both the GIC groups had significantly higher TNF-α expression than the sham control [Table 1]. Tukey’s HSD tests for IL-6 analysis showed significant differences between conventional GIC and sham control and nano-modified GIC and sham control on day 3 (P < 0.05) but no difference between the two GIC groups. By day 30, IL-6 levels were similar across all the groups. In the case of TNF-α analysis, by day 30, no significant differences were observed between the groups with conventional GIC (1.15 ± 0.141) and nano-modified GIC (1.05 ± 0.109), with a mean difference of 0.096 between the GIC groups, indicating similar TNF-α levels [Table 2].

Inference

On day 3, both the conventional and nano-modified GIC groups showed higher IL-6 and TNF-α expression than the sham control, indicating inflammation. By day 30, both the groups had reduced cytokine levels, signaling tissue healing. The nano-modified GIC group had better tissue integration and less inflammation than the conventional GIC group, showing superior biocompatibility and improved healing.

DISCUSSION

Incorporating nanoparticles into conventional GIC has been shown to enhance its mechanical properties, addressing the limitations of traditional GIC. Nanoparticle-modified GIC exhibits improved performance compared to other restorative materials.[1617] However, due to potential biological interactions, evaluating its IHC and genetic effects in an animal model is crucial to ensure clinical safety. The IHC analysis of IL-6 and TNF-α expression revealed that both conventional and nano-modified GIC induced inflammation at day 3, with higher expression of these pro-inflammatory cytokines compared to the sham control. These results are consistent with previous studies that have reported a transient inflammatory response following the implantation of biomaterials. For instance, a study by Menezes-Silva et al. found that GIC induced an inflammatory response in rat tissues, characterized by elevated levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, which decreased over time as the tissue healed.[18] Previous studies by Alarcón Sánchez MA et al., who observed heightened IL-6 levels in response to biomaterial implantation in animal models.[19] Similarly, Meher R et al.[10] demonstrated that the inflammatory response to GIC materials is typically short lived, with cytokine levels returning to baseline as the tissue integrates the material. These findings align with our study, where IL-6 and TNF-α expression were significantly higher at day 3 but decreased by day 30 in both the conventional and nano-modified GIC groups.

The nano-modified GIC group showed reduced inflammation and better tissue integration by day 30, suggesting a more favorable biocompatibility profile. This result supports the findings of a study by Jain et al., who reported that nano-modified GIC enhanced with zirconia nanoparticles exhibited reduced cytotoxicity and a less pronounced inflammatory response compared to conventional GIC.[11] Similarly, a study by Rahman et al. observed that HA-enhanced GIC demonstrated better biocompatibility and lower inflammatory cytokine expression in comparison to traditional GIC materials.[20] This may be due to the enhanced surface properties of nano-modified GIC, which could promote better tissue integration and faster healing. The use of green synthesis for nanoparticle incorporation may also play a role in reducing toxicity, as suggested by Shreyash et al., who highlighted the biocompatibility of green-synthesized nanoparticles in biomedical applications.[21] On the other hand, some studies have raised concerns regarding the inflammatory potential of nanoparticles when used in dental materials. For instance, a study by Schanen et al. reported that certain types of nanoparticles, including titanium dioxide and zirconia, could provoke significant inflammatory responses in vitro and in vivo.[22] This discrepancy may be attributed to the careful selection and green synthesis of the nanoparticles, which could reduce the cytotoxic effects commonly associated with synthetic nanoparticles.

Gene expression analysis further supported the findings of the IHC evaluation. At day 3, both the GIC groups showed higher IL-6 expression than the sham control, with no significant differences between the conventional and nano-modified GIC groups. This suggests that both materials triggered an acute inflammatory response, possibly due to the surgical implantation procedure itself or the presence of foreign materials in the tissue. At day 30, however, IL-6 expression levels decreased in both the GIC groups, indicating a reduction in inflammation as the tissue healed. This reduction in IL-6 suggests that the tissue may have begun to integrate the material, with less need for an inflammatory response. Regarding TNF-α expression, no significant differences were found between the conventional and nano-modified GIC groups at day 30, suggesting that both materials induced a similar immune response by the end of the observation period. This is consistent with the findings of Menezes-Silva et al., who reported that TNF-α expression following GIC implantation was elevated at day 3 but returned to baseline levels by day 30.[18] The fact that the nano-modified GIC group showed less pronounced inflammation at day 30 could be attributed to the unique properties of the nanoparticles used, which may have promoted more efficient tissue healing and integration.Furthermore, another study by Elsaka, who proved reduced inflammation and improved tissue healing with nano-modified GIC,[23] which are consistent with the present study findings.

A study conducted by Alex A et al. reported that the incorporation of Chitosan–Titanium–Zirconium–HA (Ch-Ti-Zr-HA) nanocomposites into the GIC matrix significantly enhanced its mechanical properties, including compressive strength, flexural strength, and microhardness, when compared to conventional GIC formulations.[24] These improvements are attributed to the uniform distribution of fine nanocomposite particles within the GIC matrix, which facilitates even stress distribution and minimizes stress concentration, thereby improving the material’s overall strength. The presence of HA contributes to the formation of polysalt bridges, further enhancing the mechanical performance of the GIC. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) was incorporated due to its chemical stability, excellent biocompatibility, and nontoxic nature.[25] In addition, other studies have emphasized the potential of HA in reinforcing GIC, as its chemical composition and structural similarity to bone and dental tissues make it highly compatible for such applications. Similarly, zirconium dioxide (ZrO2) enhances the strength, fracture toughness, and biocompatibility of GIC, while simultaneously reducing its inherent brittleness.[26] Supporting these findings, another investigation demonstrated that the addition of 5% nano-ZrO2–SiO2–HA to GIC resulted in notable improvements in compressive strength, flexural strength, and hardness compared to conventional GIC, which corroborates the results of the present study.[27] Similarly, research by Moshaverinia et al. indicated that GIC modified with nano-HA and fluorapatite exhibited superior compressive, tensile, and flexural strength over standard GIC.[28] Several other studies have also highlighted enhanced stress resistance and mechanical properties in GIC modified with various nanoparticles and cellulose nanocomposites.[2930] Furthermore, Singer et al. demonstrated that incorporating plant extracts into GIC led to increased flexural strength,[9] which aligns with our results utilizing plant-based nanoparticles. Showkat et al. also reported an improvement in compressive strength in GIC modified with titanium and nano-HA, consistent with our observations. In addition, GIC modified with TiO2 nanopowder showed the highest flexural strength among the tested groups,[31] while Moheet et al. found increased microhardness in GIC containing 10% nano-HA silica.[32] These enhancements are primarily attributed to the ability of the Ch–Ti–Zr–HA nanocomposite particles to occupy the voids between glass particles, resulting in improved packing density and reinforcement of the GIC. The incorporation of nanostructures also contributed to reducing internal air voids and microcracks. Furthermore, these modified GIC formulations demonstrated improved handling characteristics compared to unmodified cements, ultimately leading to significantly superior mechanical properties.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence that green-mediated nano-enhanced GIC exhibits superior biocompatibility compared to conventional GIC, with reduced inflammatory markers and improved tissue healing. The incorporation of nanoparticles such as chitosan, titanium, zirconia, and HA into GIC appears to enhance tissue integration without inducing sustained inflammation. This innovative material not only enhances the material’s functional performance but also could offer significant advantages in restorative dentistry, potentially improving patient outcomes. Unlike conventional GIC, this material offers reduced inflammation, improved tissue integration, and enhanced biocompatibility. Further studies are needed to assess the long-term clinical benefits and safety of these materials in a clinical setting.

CONCLUSION

This study showed that nano-modified GIC outperformed conventional GIC in biocompatibility and tissue response. Nanoparticle incorporation caused less inflammation, as indicated by lower inflammatory markers and gene downregulation, while promoting better tissue healing through gene upregulation and improved tissue integration. These findings suggested that nano-modified GIC could offer superior clinical outcomes compared to conventional GIC in restorative dentistry. However, further long-term studies were needed to confirm its durability and clinical performance.
