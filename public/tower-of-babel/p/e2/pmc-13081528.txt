Splenic rupture during secondary pleural decortication in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis: a case report
Authors: Ran Zhao, Shunan Xie, Hansheng Liang, Xue Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 63-year-old female underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for pleural decortication due to recurrent pneumothorax four months after left lung surgery. The patient experienced hemodynamic instability during repositioning from lateral to supine post-procedure. No injury to the diaphragm or chest wall was identified during the procedure and no source of bleeding was identified intraoperatively. The patient was transferred to the ICU. A bedside ultrasound revealed a large amount of free fluid in the abdomen. Diagnostic paracentesis yielded non-clotting blood, suggestive of intra-abdominal hemorrhage. An emergency exploratory laparotomy was performed, revealing a splenic rupture, necessitating splenectomy. The patient’s hemodynamics stabilized postoperatively, and she was extubated the following day. She was discharged in stable condition 28 days later.

Introduction

The fibrothorax decortication procedure is a primary treatment for pleuritis-related atelectasis caused by diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis. However, this surgery is characterized by a long operative duration and high risk of bleeding. Additionally, due to the specific nature of the underlying disease, rare complications associated with thoracoscopic surgery may arise, requiring special attention. Although there’re a few reports of splenic injury or spontaneous splenic rupture following thoracoscopic procedures [1], we present a case of splenic injury following repeated decortication in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis. This case highlights the potential association between splenic injury and the underlying disease, underscoring the need for heightened vigilance in similar surgical contexts.

Case report

A 63-year-old female presented with recurrent left-sided pneumothorax. A chest CT showed significant thickening of the visceral and parietal pleura with fibrous plaque formation. She had a history of rheumatoid arthritis and had undergone VATS for pleural decortication and left lung wedge resection three months prior due to a left-sided hydro-pneumothorax, with pathological findings consistent with rheumatoid nodules. Postoperatively, she developed left lung atelectasis. Preoperative lab results were unremarkable, and the patient was not on anticoagulants or NSAIDs. Preoperative chest X-ray showed postoperative changes in the left lung, recurrent pneumothorax, and atelectasis (Fig. 1).

Preoperative chest X-ray: left lung post-surgery with recurrent pneumothorax and atelectasis

In the preoperative area, the patient underwent arterial cannulation and paravertebral nerve block. Upon entering the operating room, her vital signs were stable. Anesthesia induction was achieved with midazolam (2 mg), etomidate (12 mg), methylprednisolone (40 mg), glycopyrrolate (0.2 mg), sufentanil (15 µg), cisatracurium (12 mg), and esmolol (50 mg). Left bronchial intubation was successful, and anesthesia was maintained with a continuous infusion of propofol (6 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.05 µg/kg/min). Additional analgesics and muscle relaxants were administered as needed. The patient remained hemodynamically stable during single-lung ventilation.

The patient was positioned in the right lateral decubitus position, and a thoracoscope was introduced through an incision at the left 4th intercostal space along the midaxillary line. Significant pleural adhesions were observed, especially around the pulmonary hilum and the pericardium. The pleural thickening was most notable at the anterior and diaphragmatic surfaces. Through small incisions at the 4th and 7th intercostal spaces, the visceral and parietal pleura were dissected and decorticated. The left lung appeared significantly reduced in volume, encased in a thick, fibrous plaque measuring 2–3 mm. The thickened pleura and diaphragm surface were meticulously decorticated and sent for pathological examination. No injury to the diaphragm occurred during the procedure. After ensuring no active intrathoracic bleeding, two chest tubes (sizes 20 F and 24 F) were placed at the 4th and 7th intercostal spaces. The surgery lasted 5 h, during which the patient remained stable.

After extubation, the patient was repositioned, and her blood pressure dropped to 76/40 mmHg. Vasopressor therapy with ephedrine and phenylephrine was initiated, followed by norepinephrine (0.1 µg/kg/min). Bedside echocardiography revealed a collapsed inferior vena cava, indicating hypovolemia. Central venous access was established, and aggressive fluid resuscitation was initiated. Blood gas analysis revealed a significant drop in hemoglobin (Table 1), suggesting hemorrhagic shock. Despite two units of packed red blood cells being transfused, no overt bleeding was noted from the chest tubes. After intraoperative consultation, the patient was transferred to the ICU for further observation.

In the ICU, the patient remained alert but developed worsening hypotension despite continuous norepinephrine infusion. Her heart rate increased, and scattered wet rales were heard on auscultation. Chest tubes drained small amounts of serosanguinous fluid. Bedside ultrasound revealed a large volume of free abdominal fluid, prompting diagnostic paracentesis, which yielded non-clotting blood. Emergency consultation with the surgical team was obtained, and an exploratory laparotomy was performed. A midline incision below the left costal margin was made, revealing a large volume of dark red blood and clots. Blood was aspirated using an autologous blood salvage system. A 5 cm laceration was identified on the diaphragmatic surface of the spleen (Fig. 2), with active bleeding. No injury was observed to the liver and other intra-abdominal organs. The abdominal surface of the adjacent diaphragm was intact. The patient was diagnosed with splenic rupture and hemorrhagic shock and underwent splenectomy. Pathology later confirmed splenic sinusoidal hemorrhage with areas of necrosis. The patient was extubated the following day and transferred to the general ward on postoperative day 3. Chest tubes were removed two weeks later, and she was discharged on postoperative day 28.

Discussion

Splenic rupture during thoracoscopic surgery is a rare complication. Most reported cases are diagnosed postoperatively using abdominal CT or ultrasound, typically after the patient presents with hemodynamic instability. The etiology of splenic rupture in thoracoscopic procedures is believed to be related to blunt trauma from surgical instruments on the left diaphragm. (Table 2) Several reports in the literature detail cases where the diaphragm was intact, but patients exhibited signs of hypovolemia and hemoglobin drop, with no evidence of thoracic bleeding. Diagnosis is often delayed until after surgery when abdominal imaging confirms the presence of free fluid or splenic injury. In a 2022 case, a patient undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic lobectomy for left lower lung adenocarcinoma developed upper abdominal pain and hemodynamic instability postoperatively. Bedside abdominal ultrasound revealed free fluid in the abdomen, and a subsequent CT scan confirmed splenic injury, necessitating an emergency laparotomy and splenectomy [2]. Another report from 2019 described a patient with squamous cell lung carcinoma who underwent robotic lobectomy and developed persistent hypotension postoperatively. CT imaging revealed a subcapsular splenic hematoma, which was managed with blood transfusions and vasopressors, leading to hemodynamic stabilization [3]. A 2014 case involved a patient who underwent thoracoscopic resection of a pleural mass. The patient exhibited a drop in hemoglobin levels before discharge, and CT imaging revealed a subcapsular splenic hematoma and moderate free fluid in the abdomen. The patient was successfully treated with angiography and splenic embolization [4].

Spontaneous splenic rupture, though less common, has also been reported. The most common cause is the use of anticoagulants, but systemic diseases like amyloidosis, hepatitis, and malignancies can also play a role. In 2017, a case involved a patient who developed hypotension and a significant drop in hemoglobin five days after undergoing a right upper lobectomy for a pulmonary nodule. Despite early splenectomy, the patient died due to other complications that arose in the ICU [5]. Similarly, another report from 2007 described a patient who underwent surgery for metastatic lung cancer and developed hemorrhagic shock three days postoperatively due to splenic rupture, confirmed by abdominal ultrasound [6].

There are also reports of spontaneous splenic rupture occurring in patients with conditions like COPD and rheumatoid arthritis. For example, a patient with COPD underwent bilateral thoracoscopic surgery and developed abdominal pain and hemorrhagic shock 18 days postoperatively due to a large abdominal hematoma and splenic injury, which was treated with coil embolization [7]. This highlights that splenic rupture may not always occur immediately post-surgery, and latent injuries might lead to delayed rupture. The characteristic findings in these cases include hypotension, a drop in hemoglobin, and no evidence of thoracic bleeding.

Non-traumatic (spontaneous) splenic rupture is a rare but recognized entity. Although anticoagulant use is the most common risk factor, systemic conditions such as malignancy, chronic inflammatory disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and rheumatoid arthritis may predispose the spleen to structural fragility and rupture in the absence of direct trauma. Reported cases indicate that spontaneous rupture can occur days to weeks after thoracic surgery without evidence of intraoperative injury, supporting a mechanism related to latent splenic vulnerability rather than procedural trauma [5, 7, 8]. Clinically, patients typically present with unexplained hypotension and a progressive decline in hemoglobin, in the absence of thoracic bleeding. Early recognition and prompt abdominal imaging are therefore critical to enable timely diagnosis and guide appropriate management.

Splenic rupture can also occur during thoracoscopic spinal surgery. In 2015, a patient who underwent thoracoscopic spine surgery for an L1 burst fracture developed hemodynamic instability six hours postoperatively. A CT scan revealed a large splenic hematoma, and the patient underwent splenectomy [9]. The likely mechanism of injury was excessive pressure applied by surgical instruments on the diaphragm, leading to splenic trauma. Other cases have reported splenic injury during thoracoscopic approaches for spinal surgery, with patients developing postoperative hypotension and hematocrit drops, necessitating splenectomy [10, 11].

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) has also been associated with non trauma splenic rupture. Some reports suggest that RA patients may experience splenic rupture even without any trauma. For example, one case involved a woman with RA who presented with diffuse abdominal pain and rectal bleeding. During laparotomy, active splenic bleeding was discovered, supporting the hypothesis that RA may predispose patients to spontaneous splenic rupture [12]. In our case, the patient had a history of RA and, while there were no preoperative signs of splenomegaly or hypersplenism, the spleen was likely more fragile than normal, contributing to its rupture during surgery. The fibrous plaque formation observed in this patient, likely due to RA-related infection, may have further restricted splenic mobility, increasing the risk of trauma during surgery. The diagnosis of complex autoimmune and musculoskeletal diseases remains challenging, as traditional methods—relying on clinical acumen, lab work, and imaging—are inherently limited. Here, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) present a transformative path forward. ML algorithms, capable of analyzing imaging data (e.g., X-rays, MRIs) to detect subtle early signs of inflammation and damage, exemplify this potential. By processing complex, multimodal datasets, AI can enhance diagnostic accuracy and enable personalized care. Applied to the current case, AI-driven analysis of pre-event imaging might have identified early splenic involvement, allowing for preventive measures and possibly avoiding the rupture [13].

Other factors, such as hematologic and immune disorders, can also increase the likelihood of splenic rupture. A review of 845 patients with atraumatic splenic rupture found that splenomegaly, advanced age, and malignancies were associated with increased mortality [14]. A 2017 report documented a case of eosinophilic pneumonia where the patient developed hemodynamic instability and atrial flutter post-surgery. After successful cardioversion, the patient developed acute abdominal hemorrhage due to splenic rupture, which was treated with splenectomy [15].

Focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) has become an essential tool for assessing trauma patients. It offers a rapid, accurate, and non-invasive method for identifying free fluid in the thoracic and abdominal cavities.

Splenic injuries, such as lacerations, hematomas, and infarctions, are commonly encountered in blunt abdominal trauma. On ultrasound, lacerations typically appear as hypoechoic areas with irregular or linear shapes. However, when lacerations are located near the diaphragm, visualization can be challenging, potentially leading to false negatives due to limited acoustic windows in this region. Acute hematomas present with variable echogenicity—hyperechoic, hypoechoic, or anechoic—depending on the stage of hemorrhage, and their visibility on ultrasound is heavily influenced by these echogenic changes over time. Subcapsular hematomas are crescent-shaped and hypoechoic, conforming to the splenic contour, while extracapsular hematomas appear irregularly shaped. Splenic infarctions, a consequence of vascular compromise, lack blood flow in the affected region as confirmed by color Doppler imaging, and their size correlates with the extent of vascular injury [16].

In the early stages of hemorrhage, ultrasound may struggle to provide a definitive diagnosis due to the absence of significant free fluid accumulation or subtle changes in echotexture. Despite these limitations, bedside ultrasound played a pivotal role in this case, enabling the prompt diagnosis of splenic rupture. It remains a valuable tool in the assessment of trauma patients, particularly in scenarios of unexplained hemodynamic instability.

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of considering splenic injury in patients with unexplained hemodynamic instability following thoracic surgery, particularly when blood loss is not evident in the operative field. Additionally, spontaneous splenic rupture has been reported in patients with conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, malignancies, and infections. Though rare, spontaneous splenic rupture is a recognized complication in rheumatoid arthritis, possibly due to splenic fragility in this patient population.
