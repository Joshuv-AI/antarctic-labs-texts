The “Make and Use” All-Suture Suture Anchor in an Open Hip Abductor Repair
Authors: Andrew S. Bi, Ty L. Monty, Chandler A. Sparks, Shane J. Nho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:All-suture anchors have gained widespread adoption in the past decade because of their small diameter profile, which permits smaller drill holes, preservation of bone stock, and the ability to place multiple fixation points. Despite these advantages, commercially available all-suture anchors may be cost-prohibitive in certain health care environments. The make-and-use (MAU) all-suture anchor technique is presented as a low-cost alternative.Indications:This technique is particularly suited for soft-tissue repair in resource-limited health care systems or settings with limited implant availability. Contraindications include short diaphyseal canals, where a bottoming-out effect may compromise fixation, and endoscopic/arthroscopic implantation, as direct visualization is recommended to ensure adequate anchor deployment and purchase.Technique Description:Two high-tensile strength, nonabsorbable sutures are secured using sequential half-hitch knots to create a compact “suture ball” construct. When inserted into a prepared intramedullary bone socket, the construct expands and functions analogously to commercially available all-suture anchors by generating cortical engagement and interference fixation. In this video, the anchor is utilized to achieve double-row fixation of the gluteal tendons to their native footprint on the greater trochanter during an open repair.Results:All-suture anchors have demonstrated comparable or superior biomechanical properties relative to other anchor designs, while their low-profile configuration may better preserve bone stock. The MAU anchor allows the fabrication of an all-suture anchor using readily available high-tensile-strength suture material and a low-cost nonabsorbable suture (eg, Ethibond). This construct can be produced at a fraction of the cost of commercially available all-suture anchors. In this technical demonstration, the anchor provided stable fixation during open gluteal tendon repair.Discussion/Conclusion:The MAU all-suture anchor is a low-cost alternative for soft-tissue fixation, particularly in economically/resource-constrained environments or when standard implants are unavailable. While indications mirror those of commercially available all-suture anchors, potential limitations include increased operative time and technical demand associated with appropriate construct sizing and deployment. Further biomechanical evaluation and long-term clinical outcomes are warranted to determine the durability and reproducibility of this technique.Patient Consent Disclosure Statement:The author(s) attests that consent has been obtained from any patient(s) appearing in this publication. Video Transcript

In this video, we will present our technique for creating and implementing the make-and-use all-suture suture anchor during an open hip abductor repair.

Background

All-suture suture anchors are soft-body anchors that have been developed in the past decade. They have the advantage of requiring smaller diameter holes to implement, limiting the risk for iatrogenic injury and allowing the ability to place more anchors in a given surface area.1,4 However, commercially available anchors can be costly, as well as have limited use in developing and/or low-income countries.2,3 The make-and-use suture anchor has been described recently as a technique for making an all-suture suture anchor with relatively inexpensive materials. 5

To begin creation of the make-and-use suture anchor, two No. 2 high tensile strength sutures are placed side by side of equal length. These are marked about 4 cm apart. The markings can be adjusted with a shorter distance, leading to a smaller suture ball but easier insertion, whereas a longer distance leads to a larger suture ball but more difficult insertion. Next, a No. 2 or No. 5 Ethibond is luggage-attached to itself, creating a bunny loop at the end. This is cinched, and the limbs are separated and wrapped around the high-tensile-strength sutures. A single half hitch is thrown for initial secure fixation, and the loop is held by the assistant. Next, a series of alternating half hitches, all thrown in the same direction, are created along these high-tensile-strength sutures. It is our preference to throw 6 half hitches totaling a distance of about 4 cm in length. The Ethibond suture should be checked to ensure it slides easily over the high-tensile strength sutures, to allow for these sutures to slide after anchor insertion. The following video is footage from our biomechanical testing demonstrating excellent fixation of the make-and-use anchor, with anchor failure occurring through suture breakage rather than anchor pullout.

Indications

We will now present a representative case of a 66-year-old female with a right abductor tendon tear to demonstrate the open insertion of the make-and-use anchor. Coronal and sagittal images of T2 magnetic resonance imaging demonstrate a full-thickness abductor tear. The patient was indicated for a right open gluteus medius/minimus repair with trochanteric bursectomy.

Technique Description and Results

The patient is brought to the operating room and placed in a lateral decubitus position with the operative leg on a padded male to allow for hip abduction. The correct operative side is marked, confirmed, and an intraoperative timeout is performed. The operative hip is exposed in slight flexion for increased exposure of the hip abductor tendons.

Following exposure of the hip abductor tear, the greater trochanter is prepped with a combination of a rongeur and a Cobb to prepare the bony footprint for tendon repair. Next, a 4.75-mm industry-specific suture anchor is placed using its corresponding awl and tap. Following anchor insertion of this polyetherether ketone (PEEK) 4.75-mm anchor, the sutures are clamped and held to the side.

Next, we create our make-and-use suture anchor. Similar to the prior video in the laboratory, on the back table two No. 2 high-tensile-strength sutures are held by an assistant to allow a double-loaded suture anchor. A No. 5 Ethibond is used to create a make-and-use suture anchor with 6 consecutive half hitches with approximately 4 cm in length. The tails are cut, and the make-and-use suture anchor can be loaded on the industry-specific anchor inserter. The tails can be wrapped around the handle, and then the make-and-use anchor is ready for insertion.

The same industry-specific awl is used to create a pilot hole in the greater trochanter. Next, the make-and-use anchor is then placed and malleted into the greater trochanter for anchor insertion. Due to the make-and-use anchor being longer of an all-suture-ball construct than an industry-specific anchor, the anchor inserter may be needed to fully insert the tails of the make-and-use anchor into the greater trochanter. This is particularly important, and why we would not recommend this anchor used in arthroscopic procedures, given the difficulty of insertion occasionally.

The anchor can be set and tested, demonstrating excellent purchase under the cortex. We now have 2 sets of high-tensile-strength sutures that both slide, having completed the insertion of a double-loaded No. 5 make-and-use suture anchor. The remaining repair of the gluteus medius tendon can then be performed according to surgeon preference. Additional make-and-use suture anchors can be placed if desired for additional suture fixation. These sutures are then loaded into a “distal or lateral row” anchor to complete a double repair of the hip abductor tendon.

Technical Pearls

There are several technical pearls when making and using make-and-use anchors. Make-and-use anchors can be single-, double-, or triple-loaded easily. Double- and triple-loaded make-and-use all-suture suture anchors anecdotally provide more secure pullout fixation.

Depending on the anatomic location and bony real estate, a No. 2 Ethibond can be used to create the make-and-use all-suture suture anchor. For No. 2 Ethibond make-and-use anchors, a 2.6- or 2.7-mm drill bit should be used, and for No. 5 Ethibond, a 3.2-mm drill bit or the awl for a 4.75-mm suture anchor should be used for the pilot hole.

We currently recommend avoiding the use of make-and-use anchors arthroscopically or in diaphyseal bone with narrow medullary canals. Given the manual creation of these suture anchors, to achieve adequate “suture ball” fixation multiple knots must be tied, leading to a relatively long insertion length needed. This can lead to problems, including bottoming out on the far cortex in a narrow medullary canal or difficulties fully inserting the anchor when performed arthroscopically.

Finally, utilizing a stiff inserter is critical. A 2.4-mm beath pin with the eyelet cut in half is an excellent option. If using industry inserters in a case already, the stiff rather than flexible ones should be used, but they may also be used to insert future make-and-use anchors.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Standard postoperative rehabilitation can be followed for the specific surgical procedure performed. No differences need to be adjusted when using the make-and-use all-suture suture anchor.

Discussion/Conclusion

All-suture suture anchors have similar or better mechanical properties when compared to other types of suture anchors, and their low-profile design may preserve bone compared to other types of suture anchors. 3 The make-and-use anchor has been recently described by Joshi et al 5 as a means of making an all-suture suture anchor using any suture material. All it requires is any high-tensile-strength suture and a low-cost nonabsorbable suture of choice, which in this case was Ethibond. These anchors are a fraction of the cost of commercially available all-suture suture anchors.
