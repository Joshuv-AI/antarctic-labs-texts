Identification of OCTA Biomarkers and Cutoff Values Associated with Disease Severity in Dry Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Bogdan Dugiełło, Katarzyna Bujała, Bartłomiej Bolek, Adam Wylęgała, Edward Wylęgała
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOptical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) enables noninvasive assessment of retinal and choroidal microvasculature and may provide quantitative biomarkers of disease severity in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD). This study aimed to identify OCTA-derived vascular parameters associated with consecutive stages of dry AMD and determine cutoff values for stage differentiation.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 182 eyes of 123 participants. Eyes were classified according to the Beckman classification into control, early, intermediate, and late dry AMD groups. OCTA imaging was performed using the ZEISS PLEX Elite 9000 swept-source OCTA system. Quantitative analysis of the superficial capillary plexus (SCP), deep capillary plexus (DCP), and choriocapillaris (CC) was conducted using AngioTool software. Vessel density, vessel length, junction-related parameters, endpoints, and lacunarity were analyzed. Group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test with post hoc analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate diagnostic performance and determine optimal cutoff values.ResultsIn the SCP, vessel density decreased from 43.83 ± 2.08% in controls to 41.27 ± 3.96% in intermediate AMD (p < 0.001), and mean lacunarity increased from 0.05 ± 0.01 to 0.07 ± 0.03 (p < 0.001), while other parameters showed no significant differences. In the DCP, vessel density declined from 51.71 ± 2.38% in controls to 48.73 ± 4.66% and 43.80 ± 4.98% across disease stages (p < 0.001), with total vessel length decreasing from 73.32 ± 2.72 to 61.58 ± 6.69 (p < 0.001), indicating progressive vascular simplification. The most pronounced changes were observed in the choriocapillaris, where vessel density decreased from 63.28 ± 0.94 to 60.31 ± 1.83% and 26.62 ± 22.30% (p < 0.001), and total vessel length from 102.80 ± 2.24 to 35.68 ± 38.42 (p < 0.001). ROC analysis identified SCP mean lacunarity (cutoff 0.0575, AUC 0.76) as a marker of early disease. In the DCP, total number of junctions below 254 and total vessel length below 66.10 were associated with advanced disease. The highest diagnostic performance was observed for choriocapillaris parameters, including vessel density below 57.38 and total vessel length below 91.10, with sensitivity exceeding 94% and specificity above 80%.ConclusionOCTA-derived microvascular parameters demonstrate layer-specific alterations in dry AMD. Mean lacunarity in the SCP appeared to be the most sensitive marker of early changes, whereas reductions in junction density and total vessel length in the DCP reflected progressive vascular simplification. The strongest association with disease severity was observed in the choriocapillaris, particularly for vessel density and total vessel length. The identified cutoff values for DCP and CC parameters were associated with different stages of dry AMD and may support stage differentiation and quantitative assessment of disease severity in clinical settings.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a progressive disease causing central vision loss in older adults, particularly those over 60 years of age. At present more than 200 million people are affected and the prevalence is increasing worldwide. The most common form is dry AMD (dAMD), which is characterized by the presence of drusen and may progress to the neovascular form (nAMD) associated with abnormal blood vessel growth [1, 2]. According to the Beckman classification, early dAMD is defined by the presence of medium-sized drusen (> 63 μm and ≤ 125 μm) without associated pigmentary alterations. In contrast, intermediate dAMD is characterized by larger drusen (greater than 125 μm) and possible pigmentary abnormalities, whereas late-stage dAMD is characterized by a geographic atrophy (GA) [3]. AMD remains one of the leading causes of irreversible vision loss in developed countries, and epidemiological studies have emphasized the contribution of modifiable and systemic risk factors, including smoking, diet, and cardiovascular conditions, to disease development and progression [4, 5].

The newest therapeutic advances in dAMD include antioxidant supplementation with AREDS/AREDS2, complement inhibitors such as pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol, which have been approved to slow GA progression [6–8], as well as mitochondria-targeted agents [9], complement-modulating gene therapies [10], regenerative approaches including retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cell transplantation and tissue engineering or nanotechnology-based delivery systems [11, 12], and light-based therapies or artificial intelligence-driven models for disease monitoring and personalized care [13–15]. Despite these advances, dAMD remains a progressive disease with heterogeneous clinical outcomes. Therefore, the continuous emergence of new therapies highlights the need to better identify reliable biomarkers of disease progression and potential therapeutic targets. Recent studies have emphasized the important role of retinal and choroidal microvascular alterations in the pathogenesis and progression of dAMD. Angio-OCT (optical coherence tomography angiography, OCTA) enables noninvasive and repeatable visualization of the retinal and choroidal microvasculature and provides quantitative information on macular vascular density, non-perfusion areas, foveal avascular zone (FAZ) characteristics, and choriocapillaris flow alterations. In non-exudative AMD, OCTA studies have reported choriocapillaris flow voids and flow impairment, particularly in relation to drusen, reticular pseudodrusen, and GA. In addition, alterations in the superficial capillary plexus (SCP) and in the deep capillary plexus (DCP) parameters have been associated with visual function in dAMD [16]. However, although increasing evidence suggests that OCTA-derived parameters reflect microvascular impairment in AMD, validated threshold values for stage differentiation remain insufficiently defined.

This clinical study was undertaken to contribute to this objective by improving the understanding of progression markers in dAMD, which may support the development and optimization of future therapeutic interventions.

Study Aims

The aim of this study was to identify angio-OCT-derived structural and vascular biomarkers associated with disease severity in patients with dAMD. Quantitative parameters of the superficial capillary plexus, deep capillary plexus, and choriocapillaris were analyzed to determine their ability to differentiate between consecutive stages of AMD.

In addition, the study evaluated the diagnostic performance of selected OCTA metrics using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and identified threshold values associated with increasing disease severity. The objective was to assess whether specific parameter ranges may serve as potential imaging markers indicating a higher likelihood of more advanced stages of dAMD.

Methods

This cross-sectional study included a total of 182 eyes from 123 participants diagnosed with dAMD, classified according to the Beckman clinical classification system. The cohort was divided into four groups: control subjects (40 eyes from 27 patients, 22.0%), early AMD (42 eyes from 33 patients, 23.1%), intermediate AMD (54 eyes from 42 patients, 29.7%), and late AMD (46 eyes from 29 patients, 25.3%). This study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Silesia, approval number BNW/NWN/0052/KB1/96/23. The study was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Eligible participants were individuals older than 50 years with a diagnosis of dAMD at any stage, clear ocular media, and high-quality OCTA images suitable for analysis. Late dAMD was defined as non-neovascular AMD with foveal GA corresponding to complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy (cRORA) on structural OCT. Eyes with isolated incomplete RPE and outer retinal atrophy (iRORA) or neovascular AMD were not included in this group. Other exclusion criteria encompassed ocular or systemic disorders that could interfere with image acquisition or influence retinal morphology. These included anterior segment opacities (e.g., corneal pathology, active intraocular inflammation, or advanced cataract), vitreous opacities, neovascular AMD, epiretinal membrane, macular hole, diabetic maculopathy, hypertensive retinopathy, and previous intraocular surgery other than uncomplicated cataract removal. Additionally, OCTA scans affected by significant segmentation errors or shadowing artifacts were excluded from the final analysis.

All participants underwent OCTA imaging using a swept-source device (ZEISS PLEX Elite 9000; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). The system operates at a central wavelength of 1050 nm, with a scanning speed of 100,000 A-scans per second and an axial imaging depth of approximately 3.0 mm (1536 pixels). For each eye, a 3 × 3 mm scan centered on the fovea was acquired, with active eye-tracking enabled to reduce motion artifacts. To ensure reproducibility of the OCTA processing workflow, all scans were reviewed before quantitative analysis. Only images with an OCTA image quality score greater than 7 were included. Scans with poor fixation, motion artifacts, defocus, media opacity-related signal loss, significant segmentation errors, projection artifacts, or shadowing artifacts caused by drusen or RPE changes were excluded if these artifacts affected the analyzed slab. Automated segmentation of the SCP, DCP, and CC was performed using the device’s built-in software and was manually reviewed for accuracy. Quantitative assessment of vascular morphology was subsequently conducted with AngioTool software (version 0.6a; National Cancer Institute, Center for Cancer Research, Bethesda, MD, USA). To maintain consistency, identical analysis settings including threshold values, vessel thickness, and particle removal parameters were applied to all images. The following parameters were calculated for each vascular layer: vessel density, total vessel length, mean vessel length, total number of junctions, junction density, number of endpoints, and mean lacunarity.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 28.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Prior to the selection of statistical tests, the distribution of variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test to evaluate normality. For variables that did not meet the assumption of normal distribution, non-parametric analyses were applied using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test to identify pairwise differences between groups. For variables showing normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the groups. The homogeneity of variances assumption was verified using Levene’s test. When homogeneity of variances was confirmed, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. If the assumption of equal variances was violated, Welch’s ANOVA was used, followed by the Games–Howell post hoc test. ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of vascular parameters in differentiating the analyzed groups. For each parameter, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated to assess overall discriminative ability. The optimal cutoff values were determined using Youden’s J statistic (sensitivity + specificity − 1). On the basis of the selected thresholds, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR−) were calculated. Statistical significance of the AUC was assessed against the null hypothesis of AUC = 0.5. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. ROC analyses were focused on predefined clinically relevant contrasts: control versus early AMD and intermediate versus late dAMD, representing early disease detection and differentiation of advanced atrophic disease, respectively.

Vessel Density

The mean vessel density in the control group was 43.83% (SD 2.08), compared with 41.49% (SD 3.93) in early AMD, 41.27% (SD 3.96) in intermediate AMD, and 42.10% (SD 3.47) in late AMD. Overall group differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001). Post hoc analysis demonstrated significantly lower vessel density in early (p = 0.007), intermediate (p < 0.001), and late AMD (p = 0.029) compared with controls, with no significant differences among AMD stages. These findings indicate early vascular rarefaction within the SCP that does not substantially progress with disease severity.

Total Number of Junctions

The total number of junctions was highest in controls (127.78 ± 21.28), while lower values were observed in early (112.71 ± 29.34) and intermediate AMD (109.93 ± 30.55), with slightly higher values in late AMD (120.89 ± 23.19). Differences between groups were significant (p = 0.007). Post hoc comparisons showed a significant reduction in intermediate AMD compared with controls (p = 0.008), whereas other pairwise comparisons were not significant. This suggests a mild reduction in vascular branching complexity primarily in intermediate disease.

Junction Density

Junction density decreased from 14.32 ± 2.38 in controls to 12.69 ± 3.27 in early AMD and 12.32 ± 3.42 in intermediate AMD, with values of 13.55 ± 2.60 in late AMD. The differences were statistically significant (p = 0.008). Post hoc testing demonstrated significantly lower junction density in intermediate AMD compared with controls (p = 0.008), with no significant differences among other groups. These findings indicate mild impairment of vascular network complexity.

Total Vessel Length

Total vessel length was 51.16 ± 3.16 in controls, compared with 48.15 ± 5.15 in early AMD, 47.39 ± 5.72 in intermediate AMD, and 48.49 ± 4.43 in late AMD. Group differences were significant (p = 0.002). Post hoc comparisons showed shorter vessel length in early (p = 0.018), intermediate (p = 0.002), and late AMD (p = 0.016) compared with controls, with no differences among AMD stages. This suggests early vessel shortening without clear progression.

Average Vessel Length

Average vessel length did not differ significantly between groups (controls, 2.15 ± 0.73; early, 1.90 ± 0.78; intermediate, 1.85 ± 0.74; late, 2.00 ± 0.72; p = 0.158). These findings indicate preservation of individual vessel segment length across disease stages.

Total Number of Endpoints

The total number of endpoints was similar in all groups (controls, 129.68 ± 13.94; early, 131.19 ± 13.85; intermediate, 131.46 ± 12.37; late, 131.41 ± 14.46), with no significant differences observed (p = 0.920). This suggests stable vascular termination patterns in the SCP.

Mean Lacunarity

Mean lacunarity was lowest in controls (0.05 ± 0.01) and higher in early (0.07 ± 0.02), intermediate (0.07 ± 0.03), and late AMD (0.07 ± 0.02). Differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001). Post hoc analysis revealed significantly higher lacunarity in all AMD stages compared with controls. These results indicate increased vascular heterogeneity in dAMD.

Deep Capillary Plexus

Quantitative analysis of the deep capillary plexus revealed clear stage-dependent microvascular alterations in dAMD. Compared with the SCP, the DCP demonstrated more pronounced changes, characterized by progressive reduction in vascular density, branching complexity, and vessel length, accompanied by increased vascular heterogeneity. These alterations were mild in early AMD, became more apparent in the intermediate stage, and were most pronounced in late disease, indicating progressive microvascular impairment within the deep Vessel Density

The mean vessel density in the control group was 51.71% (SD 2.38), compared with 50.26% (SD 3.68) in early AMD, 48.73% (SD 4.66) in intermediate AMD, and 43.80% (SD 4.98) in late AMD. Post hoc analysis showed no significant difference between early AMD and controls, whereas intermediate AMD demonstrated significantly lower values compared with controls (p < 0.001). Late AMD showed significantly reduced vessel density compared with all other groups (p < 0.001). These findings indicate progressive capillary rarefaction that becomes statistically evident from the intermediate stage and markedly worsens in late AMD.

Total Number of Junctions

The total number of junctions decreased with disease severity, from 308.35 ± 25.28 in controls to 293.71 ± 38.43 in early AMD, 277.50 ± 48.41 in intermediate AMD, and 219.70 ± 46.77 in late AMD (p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons revealed significantly fewer junctions in intermediate AMD compared with controls (p = 0.011), while late AMD differed significantly from all other groups (p < 0.001). No difference was observed between early AMD and controls. This suggests progressive loss of vascular branching complexity in the DCP.

Junction Density

Junction density followed a similar pattern, decreasing from 34.52 ± 2.84 in controls to 32.89 ± 4.29 in early AMD, 31.07 ± 5.41 in intermediate AMD, and 24.60 ± 5.23 in late AMD (p < 0.001). Post hoc analysis demonstrated significantly lower values in intermediate AMD compared with controls (p = 0.012), while late AMD differed significantly from all other groups (p < 0.001). These results indicate progressive simplification of the vascular network.

Total Vessel Length

Total vessel length declined from 73.32 ± 2.72 in controls to 71.32 ± 4.86 in early AMD, 69.29 ± 6.26 in intermediate AMD, and 61.58 ± 6.69 in late AMD (p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons showed significantly shorter vessel length in intermediate AMD compared with controls (p = 0.003), and in late AMD compared with all other groups (p < 0.001). Early AMD did not differ from controls. These findings demonstrate progressive vascular rarefaction with advancing disease.

Average Vessel Length

Average vessel length also decreased with disease progression (controls, 6.15 ± 4.27; early, 4.63 ± 1.69; intermediate, 4.48 ± 3.40; late, 2.46 ± 1.58; p < 0.001). Post hoc testing showed significantly shorter vessels in intermediate AMD compared with controls (p = 0.029), while late AMD differed significantly from all other groups (p < 0.001). These results indicate shortening of remaining vascular segments in advanced disease.

Total Number of Endpoints

The total number of endpoints increased with disease severity, from 136.73 ± 20.58 in controls to 142.69 ± 24.14 in early AMD, 153.98 ± 33.13 in intermediate AMD, and 182.39 ± 31.22 in late AMD (p < 0.001). Post hoc analysis revealed significantly higher values in intermediate AMD compared with controls (p = 0.022), while late AMD differed significantly from all other groups (p < 0.001). This increase may reflect compensatory vascular remodeling and fragmentation of the capillary network.

Mean Lacunarity

Mean lacunarity increased progressively from 0.03 ± 0.00 in controls to 0.03 ± 0.01 in early AMD, 0.03 ± 0.02 in intermediate AMD, and 0.05 ± 0.02 in late AMD (p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons showed significantly higher lacunarity in intermediate AMD compared with controls (p = 0.001), while late AMD differed significantly from all other groups (p < 0.001). These findings indicate increasing vascular heterogeneity with disease progression.

For differentiation between intermediate and late AMD, several CC parameters demonstrated excellent performance. Vessel density below 57.38, total number of junctions below 497, junction density below 55.62, total vessel length below 91.10, and average vessel length below 7.24 were strongly associated with late-stage disease. Discussion

Optical coherence tomography angiography has provided strong evidence that vascular impairment plays a key role in the pathophysiology of dAMD, particularly at the level of the choriocapillaris. The progressive involvement of vascular layers supports the hypothesis that increasingly disorganized and less interconnected vascular networks may contribute to localized retinal hypoxia and, ultimately, to functional sensitivity loss [17, 18].

In the present study, a layer-dependent pattern of microvascular impairment was observed, with relatively subtle alterations in the superficial capillary plexus, more pronounced changes in the deep capillary plexus, and the most advanced deterioration within the choriocapillaris. This hierarchical involvement supports the hypothesis that deeper vascular layers better reflect disease severity in dAMD. One of the first studies in this topic by Toto et al. aimed to assess changes in superficial and deep retinal vascular densities as well as choroidal thickness in early and intermediate AMD. Using OCTA, the authors demonstrated a significant reduction in both superficial and deep vessel density in AMD eyes compared to healthy controls, together with a marked thinning of the choroid. Notably, these vascular alterations were already evident at the intermediate stage of the disease, supporting the concept that microvascular impairment is an early event in AMD pathogenesis rather than a late consequence of advanced structural damage [19]. Our findings partially confirm these observations. Although reduced vessel density was detected in the SCP, differences between AMD stages were limited, suggesting that superficial plexus alterations occur early but do not substantially progress. According to Overbey et al. study using SS-OCTA in which 270 patients took part, demonstrated a clear, stage-dependent increase in choriocapillaris flow deficit percentage (CCFD%) across dAMD, from early disease to GA. Importantly, choriocapillaris impairment was already significant at the intermediate stage and further aggravated in GA, supporting the concept of a progressive vascular breakdown rather than an abrupt late-stage phenomenon. The strong associations between increased choriocapillaris flow deficit percentage, subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDDs), incomplete RPE and outer retinal atrophy (iRORA), and RPE atrophy highlight that choriocapillaris dysfunction closely parallels both structural degeneration and disease severity. These findings are consistent with the view that increasing flow deficits reflect a progressively fragmented and inefficient microvascular network, which may contribute to the functional impairment observed in advanced stages of AMD [20]. Consistent with these reports, our results demonstrated progressive reduction in choriocapillaris vessel density, branching complexity, and vessel length, with the most pronounced alterations in late AMD. These parameters also showed the highest diagnostic performance in ROC analysis, supporting their potential role as quantitative imaging biomarkers of disease severity. In a recent longitudinal study using SS-OCTA, Romano et al. demonstrated that progressive impairment of the choriocapillaris, quantified as inner choroid flow deficit percentage (IC-FD%), is strongly associated AMD stage progression. Importantly, distinct spatial patterns of choriocapillaris loss were linked to different late AMD outcomes: a more widespread increase in IC-FD% within the 6-mm area predicted the development of GA, whereas a more central increase within the 3-mm area was associated with conversion to neovascular AMD. These findings support the concept that choriocapillaris dysfunction is not only progressive but also spatially heterogeneous. From a network perspective, increasing flow deficits likely reflect a progressive loss of vascular continuity and connectivity, consistent with reduced vessel density, shorter vessel length, fewer junctions, more endpoints, and higher lacunarity within the choriocapillaris, as observed in quantitative vascular analyses [21]. Our network-based analysis supports this interpretation. We observed progressive reduction in junction density and total vessel length together with increased lacunarity, particularly in the DCP and choriocapillaris, suggesting vascular fragmentation and simplification with advancing disease. In another comprehensive review focusing on OCTA, Taylor et al. highlighted growing evidence that retinal microvascular alterations are present in early and intermediate stages of AMD, in addition to the well-established involvement of the choroid and choriocapillaris. The most consistent finding across OCTA studies was a reduction in vessel density within the superficial vascular complex (SVC) of the retina, which predominantly supplies the ganglion cell layer. Alterations in the deep vascular complex (DVC), comprising the intermediate and deep capillary plexuses, were reported less consistently. Importantly, the authors emphasized that these retinal vascular changes are detectable before the onset of late-stage disease, supporting the concept that microvascular dysfunction may contribute to early AMD pathophysiology rather than representing a purely secondary consequence of advanced retinal atrophy. At the same time, the review underscores the need for cautious interpretation due to methodological heterogeneity and lack of standardized OCTA metrics [22]. Our results extend these observations by demonstrating clear stage-dependent impairment within the DCP, including reduced vessel density, decreased branching complexity, and shortened vessel length. In a prospective cross-sectional study including 105 eyes of 105 participants, Savastano et al. used OCTA to evaluate choriocapillaris vascular density in different stages of AMD. The study demonstrated a clear stage-dependent reduction of choriocapillaris perfusion, with significantly lower macular vascular density in eyes with GA, late exudative disease and disciform scar compared with healthy controls. Importantly, choriocapillaris vascular density measured in the foveal region was already significantly reduced in early AMD, despite the absence of significant changes in whole macular measurements. Furthermore, in eyes with GA, reduced choriocapillaris vascular density correlated with the extent of ellipsoid zone disruption and with the area of chorioretinal atrophy, supporting its role as an imaging biomarker of disease severity [23]. In agreement with these observations, choriocapillaris parameters in our study demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy and provided clinically relevant ROC-derived cutoff values. These thresholds may represent practical imaging markers associated with increased likelihood of more advanced disease. Only limited data exist regarding quantitative OCTA cutoff values in dAMD. Biesemeier et al. proposed that choriocapillaris dropout may precede RPE degeneration and represent an early vascular biomarker [24].

The recent approval of complement inhibitors for GA, including pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol, highlights the need for quantitative imaging biomarkers in dAMD. OCTA-derived parameters may be useful in future longitudinal studies to characterize baseline vascular phenotypes and monitor whether GA therapies influence choriocapillaris impairment or vascular network complexity. However, the present cross-sectional study cannot assess treatment response or prognostic value.

Taken together, our results support a layer-specific model of vascular involvement in dAMD, with early subtle SCP alterations, progressive DCP impairment, and the most pronounced changes in the choriocapillaris. The addition of ROC-derived cutoff values further strengthens the potential clinical utility of OCTA for disease staging and risk stratification.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Firstly, OCTA measurements remain susceptible to segmentation errors, signal attenuation, and projection or shadowing artifacts despite careful image review. Secondly, the analysis was based on a single imaging platform and a single quantitative workflow, which may limit direct generalizability to other devices and software settings. The ROC-derived cutoff values identified in this study should be considered specific to the imaging protocol, ZEISS PLEX Elite 9000 platform, and AngioTool-based post-processing workflow used in this analysis. In addition, both eyes from some participants were included in the study populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated layer-specific alterations in OCTA-derived microvascular parameters in dAMD. In the superficial capillary plexus, mean lacunarity showed the best sensitivity as a marker for early disease, although SCP parameters had limited ability to differentiate disease stages. In the deep capillary plexus, total number of junctions, junction density, and total vessel length can be associated with disease severity, suggesting reduced vascular complexity in more advance stages. The choriocapillaris parameters, particularly the vessel density, junction density, and total vessel length, showed the highest discriminatory power and best performance in ROC analysis.

In this study, several cutoff values were identified. These thresholds, derived from our methodology, were associated with different stages of dAMD and may support stage differentiation and quantitative assessment of disease severity. However, further longitudinal studies are required to determine their prognostic value and potential utility in predicting disease progression.
