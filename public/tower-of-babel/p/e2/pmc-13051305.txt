Determining ED50 of Nalbuphine for Intraoperative Analgesia in Elderly Patients Undergoing Percutaneous Kyphoplasty: A Prospective, Double-Blind, Single-Center, Dose-Finding Study
Authors: Peng Zhao, Xin Song, Yu Liu, Ying Luo, Zhiyong Cui, Yanyang Guo, Xin Wang, Jingjing Yang, Huipeng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNalbuphine is a novel intravenous anesthetic. Studies have shown that it features a rapid onset of action, a fast recovery time, slight inhibition of respiratory and cardiovascular functions, and a low incidence of adverse reactions. This study aims to determine the median effective dose (ED50) of nalbuphine for intraoperative analgesia in elderly patients undergoing percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) and to evaluate its safety profile, thereby providing a reference for its rational clinical use.MethodsWe included 22 patients aged 60–85 years of either sex who underwent PKP with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA)grade I to II. In this study, the dose-finding strategy of nalbuphine followed a modified Dixon’s up-and-down method with an initial dose of 0.1 mg/kg and an increment of 0.01 mg/kg. Dixon up-and-down sequential methodology was utilized to ascertain the ED50 of nalbuphine. NRS pain score≤3 was considered as effective for analgesia, otherwise it was considered as ineffective for analgesia. Circulatory parameters and adverse effects were recorded.ResultsThe analgesia was effective in 13 patients and ineffective in 9 patients according the NRS Pain score during operating. ED50 of single-dose intravenous nalbuphine injection in inhibiting intraoperative analgesia in elderly patients undergoing PKP was (0.062 mg/kg, 95% CI: 0.041–0.081 mg/kg).ConclusionThe ED50 (0.062 mg/kg, 95% CI: 0.041–0.081 mg/kg) for intraoperative analgesia with single-dose intravenous nalbuphine were established in elderly patients undergoing PKP. Nalbuphine exhibited a lower incidence of anesthesia-related adverse events.Trial RegistrationChiCTR2300071476.

Introduction

As population aging progresses in China, the proportion of elderly osteoporotic patients undergoing surgery has increased consecutively.1 Worldwide, an estimated 20% of the population over 70 years of age and 16% of postmenopausal women are affected by vertebral compression fractures. To address this, minimally invasive interventions such as percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and kyphoplasty (PKP) have emerged as standard treatments to alleviate pain and minimize functional impairment.2,3 While these procedures are often performed under local anesthesia, its analgesic effect can be incomplete, failing to fully suppress the perioperative stress response.4 This is particularly concerning in elderly patients, who frequently experience preoperative anxiety and tension. These factors can easily lead to sympathetic excitation, resulting in hemodynamic instability, inadequate analgesia, and an increased risk of cardiovascular complications.5–7 The ideal anesthetic state for vertebroplasty combines effective analgesia, cardiorespiratory stability, and preserved patient communication.8 Achieving this balance requires careful sedation management by anesthesiologists. Local anesthesia for percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) often leads to significant intraoperative pain and stress response, which may induce severe cardiovascular fluctuations, postoperative delirium, and other adverse events in elderly patients. Commonly used anesthetic and analgesic drugs have limitations such as respiratory depression, hypotension, and excessive sedation, which further increase the anesthetic risk in elderly patients. Therefore, optimizing systemic analgesia during PKP is essential, particularly in elderly patients.

Nalbuphine hydrochloride, with its potent analgesia, moderate sedation, mild respiratory depression, and minimal hemodynamic effects, presents itself as an ideal agent for monitored anesthesia care(MAC) in elderly patients.9,10 Accordingly, the primary objective was to quantify the ED50 intravenous nalbuphine hydrochloride required for effective intraoperative analgesia during PKP in elderly patients, using an up-down sequential allocation methodology.

Methods

Ethics and Registration

This study has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the 964th Hospital of Chinese People’s Liberation Army Joint Logistics Support Force (approval number: 2023Y0001), and registered at http://www.chictr.org.cn (registration number: ChiCTR2300071476, 5/16/2023). The detailed study protocol is available online.11 This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A small number of elderly patients had mild cognitive impairment or limited communication ability due to advanced age, so their legally authorized relatives signed the informed consent forms on their behalf after fully understanding the study protocol. This operation was approved by the Ethics Committee and conforms to the relevant ethical regulations. All participants or their legal guardians provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and the study was conducted under the guidance of relevant ethical guidelines with institutional approval.

Study Design

This was a prospective, double-blind, single-center, dose-finding study. To maintain blinding, all study drug preparations (nalbuphine at various doses) were performed by an anesthesiologist not involved in patient care or data collection. They were identical in appearance and volume. The attending anesthesiologist, surgical team, patients, and the investigator responsible for outcome assessment were all blinded to the dose administered. The study was conducted at the 964th Hospital of Chinese People’s Liberation Army Joint Logistics Support Force.

Patient Selection

We enrolled patients scheduled for PKP between May and July 2023. Inclusion criteria were: age 60–85 years, BMI 18–28 kg/m2, ASA physical status I or II, and imaging-confirmed vertebral compression fracture. Patients aged 60–85 years were defined as elderly according to previous relevant studies.12,13 Exclusion criteria included: severe cardiac, cerebral, hepatic, renal, or pulmonary dysfunction; spinal tuberculosis, metastasis, or infection; history of psychiatric disorder; known allergy to local anesthetics, opioids, or nalbuphine; history of chronic alcohol, sedative, or analgesic abuse; long-term use of sedatives/analgesics; or inability to communicate effectively.

Up-and-Down Method

In this study, the dose-finding strategy of nalbuphine followed a modified Dixon’s up-and-down method with an arithmetic sequence.14 The modified Dixon’s up-and-down design is a classic method to explore ED50 of nalbuphine for injection where the dose of a participant is determined based on the response of the previous one.15 The starting dose of nalbuphine (Yichang, Hubei Human well Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd) was set at 0.10 mg/kg.16,17 The dose adjustment increment (step size) was set at 0.01 mg/kg to allow for precise titration. According to the Dixon’s method, the analgesic response of one patient determined the dose for the next. Analgesia was deemed “effective” if the patient reported a Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) score ≤3 during the most stimulating surgical steps (eg, working channel establishment or cement injection). If “effective”, the dose for the next patient was decreased by 0.01 mg/kg; if “ineffective” (NRS >3), it was increased by the same step size. In cases of inadequate pain control (NRS≥4), the clinician administered a rescue intravenous injection of 2 mg nalbuphine, and the response was recorded as “ineffective” for the sequential analysis. The study continued until six crossover points (transitions from effective to ineffective or vice versa) were observed, as this is considered sufficient for reliable ED50 estimation using this method.15 A total of 22 patients were enrolled to meet this requirement. The raw data for the up-and-down method, including patient demographics, intraoperative vital signs, adverse reactions, and individual patient responses, are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Anesthetic Procedure

Patients fasted for 8 hours for solids and 2 hours for clear fluids. No premedication was given. Upon operating room arrival, standard monitoring was established, including continuous electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure (measured at 5-minute intervals), pulse oximetry (SpO2), and respiratory rate. Venous access was secured.

The study drug (nalbuphine at the assigned dose) was administered intravenously over 60 seconds. Following injection, patients received oxygen via a face mask at 5 L/min. They were then carefully turned to the prone position. No additional sedative or analgesic drugs were administered during the interval from nalbuphine injection until the start of surgery, unless rescue analgesia (as per protocol) was required. Ten minutes after nalbuphine injection, local anesthesia was administered with 0.5% lidocaine (15 mL) from the skin to the periosteum. The surgery (PKP) commenced 15 minutes post-nalbuphine administration. All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon.

Intraoperative hemodynamic management followed a strict protocol: a ≥20% decrease in mean arterial pressure (MAP) triggered fluid challenge or IV ephedrine (6 mg); a ≥20% MAP increase received IV urapidil (10 mg); heart rate (HR) <50 bpm was treated with IV atropine (0.5 mg); and HR >100 bpm received IV esmolol (10 mg).

Anesthesia was maintained solely with the initial nalbuphine dose, local anesthesia, and oxygen supplementation (ie, MAC). No inhalational or intravenous anesthetic agents were used for maintenance. Spontaneous ventilation was maintained throughout the procedure.

To ensure objectivity, an independent anesthesiologist, blinded to the study group, recorded all data.

Outcome Measures

Primary Outcome

The ED50 of intravenous nalbuphine hydrochloride for providing effective intraoperative analgesia (defined as NRS ≤3 during key surgical stages) in elderly patients undergoing PKP.

Secondary Outcomes

Patient demographic and baseline characteristics: age, sex, BMI, ASA grade. Hemodynamic and respiratory parameters: MAP, HR, and SpO2 recorded at the following time points:

T0: Baseline (upon entering the operating room)

T1: During the establishment of the working channel

T2: During bone cement injection

T3: At the end of surgery

Incidence and management of adverse events, such as hypotension, bradycardia, hypertension, tachycardia, respiratory depression (SpO2 < 90% or RR < 8 breaths/min), nausea, and vomiting.

Discussion

While minimally invasive, PKP can induce significant perioperative pain and stress, particularly in elderly patients who may not be adequately managed by local anesthesia alone. Nalbuphine, with its favorable analgesic and safety profile, is a promising agent for monitored anesthesia care (MAC) in this context. Given that the up-down sequential method is optimally designed for precise estimation of the median effective dose, this study was designed to quantitatively determine the ED50 of intravenous nalbuphine required for intraoperative analgesia during PKP in elderly patients.

Focus on ED50 and the Rationale for Initial Dose Selection

The up-down sequential method is uniquely suited for precise estimation of the median effective dose (ED50). Our choice of an initial dose of 0.10 mg/kg was based on a synthesis of preliminary pilot observations and published literature suggesting its efficacy and safety in similar procedural contexts.16,17 This starting point was deliberately selected to be within a presumed effective range while minimizing the risk of initial under-dosing or excessive side effects, allowing the sequential design to efficiently bracket the true median.

The Deliberate Omission and Limited Reliability of ED95

While it is mathematically possible to extrapolate an ED95 from the dose-response model fitted to our data, the reliability of such an estimate from a limited sample sized for ED50 determination is inherently low. The up-down method concentrates observations around the median, providing sparse data at the tails of the distribution. Consequently, any calculated ED95 would have an unacceptably wide confidence interval, rendering it statistically unstable and clinically unreliable. Therefore, we deliberately refrain from reporting a specific ED95 value, as doing so could be misleading. Establishing a reliable ED95 would require a study specifically powered for that endpoint, typically employing a different design (eg, a randomized parallel-group dose-response study with a larger sample size across multiple fixed doses).

Interpretation of Effective Doses: Clinical Implications of a Lower ED50

The core finding of this study is the quantification of the ED50 (0.062 mg/kg) for nalbuphine. This is notably lower than doses previously recommended for comparable procedural analgesia. Sesay argued that 0.3 mg/kg of nalbuphine administered intravenously alone was effective for anesthesia during PKP.18 Besides, our ED50 is also lower than the reported recommendation of 0.15 mg/kg to meet all anesthesia requirements of gastroscopy when combined with 2.0 mg/kg of propofol.19 In our study, the ED50 of nalbuphine was only 1/3 of the above mentioned recommended dose, reflecting the advantage in reducing intravenous anesthetic dose, which is in line with expectations.20,21 Differences in surgical stimulus intensity and concomitant sedative use likely contribute to these dose discrepancies.This discrepancy likely reflects a combination of methodological precision and a genuine increase in pharmacodynamic sensitivity within our elderly cohort, a population often characterized by altered pharmacokinetics.22 Establishing a lower ED50 provides a precise, evidence-based anchor for dose titration. It suggests that initiating analgesia at or near this median dose may be effective for half of the population, thereby minimizing the risk of underdosing and unnecessary initial high-dose exposure, which is particularly important for mitigating opioid-related side effects in vulnerable elderly patients.

Hemodynamic and Safety Outcomes

The hemodynamic data provide robust physiological confirmation of nalbuphine’s analgesic efficacy at doses approximating the ED50. The significant attenuation of mean arterial pressure and heart rate in response to surgical stimulus indicates successful blunting of nociceptive-driven sympathetic activation, a primary goal of procedural analgesia. Notably, this control was achieved without precipitating clinically significant hypotension or bradycardia requiring intervention. This stable hemodynamic profile is particularly advantageous for the elderly PKP population, who often have reduced cardiovascular reserve. Compared to pure µ-opioid agonists like fentanyl, which can cause more pronounced and dose-dependent hypotension and bradycardia, nalbuphine’s profile appears favorable.23 Studies in other surgical contexts, such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy, have similarly reported that nalbuphine provides comparable analgesia to fentanyl but with superior hemodynamic stability.24

The safety outcomes further strengthen the clinical value of the determined ED50. The absence of respiratory depression (SpO2 < 90% or RR < 8 bpm), hypoxia, and excessive sedation in our cohort is a critical finding. This aligns with and extends the results of previous research. For instance, a study on nalbuphine for post-cesarean analgesia also reported a very low incidence of respiratory depression, supporting its reputation for respiratory safety.25 Furthermore, the incidence of other opioid-related side effects was low. The rate of nausea in our study was lower than that frequently reported with equipotent doses of morphine or hydromorphone in elderly patients.26 This favorable tolerability profile is a direct consequence of nalbuphine’s unique dual mechanism: κ-receptor agonism provides analgesia and sedation, while its partial antagonism at the µ-receptor is believed to mitigate typical µ-mediated side effects like respiratory depression, nausea, and pruritus.27,28

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has several limitations. First,the sample size, while adequate for precise ED50 estimation using the up-and-down method, limits subgroup analyses and generalizability. Second,as a single-center study involving a specific demographic, external validation is warranted. Third, a key methodological consideration is that this study only included elderly patients with ASA physical status I or II, which may not fully represent the broader elderly population who often present with multiple comorbidities and frailty. This inclusion criterion was deliberately chosen to minimize confounding variables and ensure the accuracy of ED50 estimation in this initial dose-finding study. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to frail or ASA III elderly patients is limited. As a single-center study involving a specific demographic, external validation is warranted.

A key methodological consideration is our focus on the ED50. Future research with larger cohorts using different study designs (eg, randomized dose-response trials) is needed to establish the ED95 and to further refine the complete dose-response relationship. Additionally, exploring the integration of this ED50-based dosing within multimodal analgesic regimens represents a vital direction for optimizing geriatric perioperative care.

Conclusion

In summary, the ED50 of single-dose nalbuphine for intraoperative analgesia in elderly patients with ASA grade I or II undergoing PKP was 0.062 mg/kg, (95% CI: 0.041–0.081 mg/kg). This result is only applicable to this specific population and cannot be generalized to all elderly patients undergoing PKP, especially those with ASA grade III or above. Nalbuphine showed a low incidence of anesthesia-related adverse events.
