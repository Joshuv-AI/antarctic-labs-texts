Optimization of [18F]FBPA production for clinical translation through precursor development and manufacturing route evaluation
Authors: Ting-Yu Chang, Yu-Hou Yu, Zhen-Fan You, Ping-Chuan Cho, Yuh-Feng Wang, Chun-Yi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground4-Borono-2-[18F]fluorophenylalanine ([18F]FBPA) is an important imaging agent for identifying suitable candidates for boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT). This study aimed to develop three new precursors for the no-carrier-added synthesis of [18F]FBPA and to establish corresponding automated radiosynthesis protocols suitable for routine production.ResultsFollowing manual optimization, the optimized conditions were successfully translated to automated synthesis, resulting in non-decay-corrected radiochemical yields of 2.6% ± 1.3% for the BNBA-based protocol, 0.3% ± 0.1% for the P-02-based protocol, and 3.1% ± 0.0% for the P-03-based protocol, respectively. MicroPET/MR imaging and biodistribution analyses demonstrated the highest tumor uptake for [18F]FBPA-sorbitol conjugate ([18F]FBPA-Sor), followed by [18F]FBPA and [18F]FBPA-fructose conjugate ([18F]FBPA-Fr). Tumor standardized uptake values obtained from microPET/MR imaging were 1.72 ± 0.25 for [18F]FBPA, 1.29 ± 0.25 for [18F]FBPA-Fr, and 2.57 ± 0.28 for [18F]FBPA-Sor, with corresponding tumor-to-muscle (T/M) ratios of 3.34 ± 1.07, 4.64 ± 0.60, and 9.38 ± 0.88, respectively. Biodistribution studies further confirmed enhanced tumor accumulation of [18F]FBPA-Sor, with tumor uptake values of 10.46 ± 0.61, 11.51 ± 2.75, and 19.32 ± 1.57%ID/g for [18F]FBPA, [18F]FBPA-Fr, and [18F]FBPA-Sor, respectively. In addition, [18F]FBPA-Sor demonstrated the highest tumor-to-muscle and tumor-to-blood ratios, indicating that alditol conjugation significantly influences tracer pharmacokinetics and biodistribution profiles.ConclusionsThese findings provide practical insights into precursor design, radiosynthesis optimization, and imaging performance, offering a rational framework to guide future development and clinical translation of [18F]FBPA as a companion diagnostic for BNCT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41181-026-00471-x.Keywords: Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT), [18F]FBPA, Nucleophilic substitution reaction, Alditol conjugation

Introduction

Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) is a unique treatment approach characterized by its binary nature. Administration of boron-containing drugs or exposure to thermal (or epithermal) neutron beams alone does not cause cell damage. However, when a boron-10 atom, which constitutes 20% of natural boron, captures a thermal neutron, it transforms into an unstable boron-11 due to its remarkably high cross-section (3837 barns) with thermal neutrons. This reaction generates high linear energy transfer (LET) particles, helium-4 (4He, α), and lithium-7 (7Li), which release their energy completely within a very short range (5–9 μm), leading to the formation of DNA double-strand breaks and subsequent cell death. Furthermore, the limited range of these particles confines the destructive effects to cells that have absorbed boron atoms, thereby sparing neighboring cells. Therefore, boron-containing drugs with excellent pharmacokinetic properties are crucial for the effectiveness of BNCT.

4-Borono-L-phenylalanine (BPA), a phenylalanine analogue developed by Snyder et al. in 1958, is currently the main boron-containing agent for BNCT worldwide (Snyder et al. 1958). Amino acids play a central role in protein synthesis and are essential for cell proliferation. Consequently, BPA is preferentially taken up by tumor cells to support their elevated metabolic and biosynthetic demands, whereas normal cells, characterized by lower proliferative activity, exhibit relatively limited uptake (Watabe et al. 2017). BPA was initially investigated for the treatment of melanoma based on the hypothesis that it would be selectively absorbed by melanin-producing cells (Fukuda 2021).

However, the intrinsic aqueous solubility of BPA at physiological pH is approximately 1.6 g/L, which is insufficient to meet clinical dosing requirements. To address this limitation, BPA has been complexed with carbohydrates containing cis-diol functional groups, such as fructose or sorbitol, to form relatively water-soluble boronate ester complexes (e.g., BPA-fructose or BPA-sorbitol). These complexes markedly enhance solubility to > 30 g/L while preserving the tumor-targeting properties of BPA. Clinical trials conducted in the United States (Chadha et al. 1998, Diaz 2003), Finland (Kankaanranta et al. 2011), Sweden (Skold et al. 2010, Hopewell et al. 2011), Japan (Kageji et al. 2011, Kawabata et al. 2009, Miyatake et al. 2009), and Taiwan (Chen et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2023) consistently demonstrated the superior therapeutic efficacy of 10B-BPA-Fr-based BNCT. Nonetheless, fructose-based formulations are associated with limited long-term stability and potential safety concerns in individuals with hereditary fructose intolerance (Watanabe et al. 2023). As a result, contemporary clinical formulations, such as the commercially approved product Borofalan in Japan, have transitioned to BPA-sorbitol, which offers improved chemical stability and broader clinical applicability (Sato and Hirose 2024, Sato et al. 2024).

Another important reason for the widespread clinical use of BPA-Fr is the availability of a reliable radioactive surrogate. Previous studies have demonstrated that the pharmacokinetics of the 4-borono-2-[18F]fluorophenylalanine fructose conjugate ([18F]FBPA-Fr) closely parallel that of BPA-Fr in vivo (Hsieh et al. 2005, Wang et al. 2004). Consequently, positron emission tomography (PET) imaging with [18F]FBPA-Fr enables noninvasive estimation of BPA-Fr accumulation within the tumors and facilitates patient selection by confirming that the tumor-to-blood ratio exceeds the established treatment threshold (> 2.5). Electrophilic substitution reactions have long been utilized for the synthesis of [18F]FBPA-Fr. Mairinger et al. successfully automated this process on a TRACERlab™ FXFDG platform, achieving the direct synthesis of [18F]FBPA via [18F]F2 gas (Mairinger et al. 2015). Despite the advancement, the approach is limited by relatively low molar activity and restricted availability for cyclotron facilities lacking gas targets. These limitations underscore the need to develop more efficient and clinically applicable methods for the synthesis of [18F]FBPA.

The production of no-carrier-added (NCA) [18F]FBPA has advanced through several nucleophilic substitution methods. In 2019, Yoshimoto et al. reported a copper-mediated approach using arylboronate precursors, which involved sequential radiofluorination, borylation, and hydrolysis steps and yielded [18F]FBPA with decay-corrected (d.c.) radiochemical yields of 20–24% (Yoshimoto et al. 2019). Subsequently, He et al. introduced aryldiboron precursors that eliminated the need for a borylation step, enabling a two-step synthesis with non-decay-corrected (n.d.c.) radiochemical yields of 9–16% (He et al. 2021). However, the synthesis and purification of these boron-containing precursors remain technically demanding. To circumvent these difficulties, we previously employed commercially available starting materials to produce NCA [18F]FBPA via a multistep nucleophilic route, achieving a radiochemical yield of 20 ± 6% (n.d.c.) (Chang et al. 2023).

Despite the availability of multiple synthetic strategies for the production of [18F]FBPA, a comprehensive and systematic comparison of these approaches has been lacking. This gap is largely attributable to substantial differences in precursor design and reaction schemes across studies, which complicate fair and direct evaluation of synthetic performance. In the present work, we address this limitation by designing structurally analogous precursors and performing a parallel comparison of three representative radiofluorination strategies under comparable conditions. By integrating synthetic evaluation with in vivo PET imaging validation, this study aims to provide a rational framework for assessing precursor design, radiochemical yield, and molar activity, thereby offering practical guidance for future clinical translation and routine production of [18F]FBPA.

Materials and methods

Potassium trifluoromethanesulfonate (KOTf), tetrabutylammonium trifluoromethanesulfonate (TBAOTf), tetrabutylammonium bicarbonate (TBAHCO3), tetrakis(pyridine)copper(II) triflate (Cu(OTf)2(py)4), anhydrous acetonitrile, pyridine, N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), N, N-dimethylacetamide (DMA), bis(pinacolato)diboron, and potassium acetate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Potassium carbonate (K2CO3) and hydroiodic acid (HI, 57%) were obtained from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). [1,1’-bis(diphenylphosphino)ferrocene]dichloropalladium(II) (Pd(dppf)Cl2), bis(dibenzylideneacetone)palladium(0) (Pd(dba)2), 2-dicyclohexylphosphino-2’,4’,6’-triisopropylbiphenyl (XPhos), and tricyclohexylphosphine (P(Cy)3) were purchased from AK Scientific (Union City, CA, USA). [18F]Fluoride ([18F]F−) was supplied by the Department of Nuclear Medicine at Taipei Veterans General Hospital (Taipei, Taiwan). Sep-Pak cartridges were purchased from Waters Corporation (Milford, MA, USA). All chemicals and solvents were obtained from commercial suppliers and used as received without further purification. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analyses were performed using silica gel 60 F254 aluminum-backed plates (Merck KGaA). Radioactivity measurements were conducted using a gamma counter (PerkinElmer 1470 Automatic Gamma Scintillation Counter, PerkinElmer Inc., Shelton, CT, USA). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was carried out on a Waters 600 system equipped with a photodiode array detector (Waters 2998) and a radioactivity detector (Flow-Count FC-1000, BioScan). Analytical HPLC analysis was performed on a ReproSil 100 C18 column (Dr. Maisch GmbH) using a mobile phase consisting of acetonitrile and 0.8% acetic acid containing 1 mmol/L sodium 1-octanesulfonate and 1 mmol/L EDTA (15:85, v/v) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Enantiomeric purity was evaluated using a Daicel Crownpak CR(+) analytical column (4 × 150 mm, 5 μm) with a corresponding guard column, employing a mobile phase of perchloric acid solution (pH = 2) and methanol (95:5, v/v) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Cell culture media, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from HyClone (Logan, UT, USA). TrypLE™ Express was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Sterile 0.22-µm membrane filters were obtained from Agela Technologies (Torrance, CA, USA).

Optimization procedures

To optimize the production routes for P-01 and P-02, reaction conditions were systematically evaluated at each stage. For the fluorination step, the effects of reaction solvents (DMSO and DMF), reaction temperatures (110, 120, and 130 °C), reaction times (10, 20, and 30 min), and precursor amounts (5, 10, and 20 mg) on fluorination efficiency were investigated. For the subsequent borylation, the reaction efficiency was screened using selected catalyst, solvent, and temperature combinations. Pd(dppf)Cl2 was evaluated in DMF at 140 °C, whereas Pd(dba)2/XPhos and Pd(dba)2/P(Cy)3 were tested in 1,4-dioxane at 110 °C. For optimization of the P-03 production route, key parameters affecting both the fluorination and hydrolysis steps were systematically examined. For fluorination, the evaluated parameters included the eluent system (KOTf, TBAOTf, or TBAHCO3), reaction solvents (DMA, DMF, or DMF/ACN, 1:1, v/v), and reaction temperatures (110, 120, and 130 °C). The amounts of reaction components were also optimized, including Cu(OTf)2(py)4 catalyst (4.8, 9.5, and 19 mg), pyridine (0.05 and 0.15 mL), and P-03 precursor (5, 10, and 15 mg). For the hydrolysis step, the effects of acid volume (0.25, 0.5, and 1 mL) and the reaction temperature (140 and 180 °C) were evaluated.

Production of [18F]FBPA

Radiosynthesis of [18F]FBPA from Bpin-free precursor

NCA [18F]FBPA was synthesized in accordance with our previously reported method (Chang et al. 2023). Utilizing 4-bromo-2-nitrobenzaldehyde (BNBA) as the starting precursor, the multi-step synthesis comprised sequential radiofluorination, reduction, iodination, alkylation, borylation, and hydrolysis (Fig. 2A). The automated production was implemented on a TRASIS AllinOne synthesizer (TRASIS SA, Belgium).

C Synthetic route utilizing a di-Bpin-containing precursor, benzyl (2 S,5 S)-5-(2,4-bis(4,4,5,5-tetramethyl-1,3,2-dioxaborolan-2-yl)benzyl)-2-(tert-butyl)-3-methyl-4-oxoimidazolidine-1-carboxylate (P-03)

Radiosynthesis of [18F]FBPA from mono-Bpin-containing precursors

The production of 18F-FBPA starting from mono-Bpin-containing precursors (P-01 and P-02) was conducted via three steps: copper-mediated radiofluorination, palladium-catalyzed borylation, and hydrolysis (Fig. 2B). [18F]F− was first trapped on a QMA Sep-Pak cartridge, which had been sequentially conditioned with 10 mL of ethanol, 10 mL of potassium triflate solution (90 mg/mL), and 10 mL of water. The trapped activity was eluted with 0.5 mL of KOTf/K2CO3 solution, which was prepared by dissolving 100 mg of KOTf and 1 mg of K2CO3 in 11 mL of deionized water. The eluate was transferred to a reaction vial and evaporated to dryness at 110 °C, followed by azeotropic drying with anhydrous acetonitrile (2 mL). Subsequently, a solution containing 10 mg of the precursor (P-01 or P-02) in 0.5 mL of a catalyst solution (19 mg of Cu(OTf)2(py)4 dissolved in 0.1 mL of pyridine and 1.25 mL of DMF) was added to the dried residue. After the introduction of oxygen, the reaction mixture was heated at 120 °C for 20 min to complete the copper-mediated radiofluorination. Upon cooling to r.t., the mixture was diluted with acetonitrile (2 mL) and passed through a silica Sep-Pak cartridge preconditioned with 10 mL of acetonitrile. The eluate was evaporated to dryness. A solution of bis(pinacolato)diboron (20 mg), Pd(dppf)Cl2 (7 mg), and potassium acetate (13 mg) in DMF (0.5 mL) was added to the residue. The mixture was heated at 140 °C for 30 min. Hydrolysis was then carried out by adding hydroiodic acid (HI, 57%, 1 mL) and heating at 180 °C for 15 min. The crude product was neutralized with sodium hydroxide solution, passed through a 0.45-µm membrane filter, and purified by HPLC according to previously reported procedures (Chang et al. 2023).

Radiosynthesis of [18F]FBPA from di-Bpin-containing precursor

The synthesis of [18F]FBPA from the di-Bpin-containing precursor was carried out according to the general procedure described for the mono-Bpin-containing precursor, with the borylation step omitted (Fig. 2C). [18F]F− was trapped, eluted, and azeotropically dried as previously detailed. To the dried residue, a solution of P-03 (15 mg) in 0.5 mL of catalyst solution was added. The catalyst solution consisted of (Cu(OTf)2(py)4 (9.5 mg) and pyridine (0.05 mL) in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of DMF and acetonitrile. Following the introduction of oxygen, radiofluorination was performed at 120 °C for 20 min. After cooling and silica Sep-Pak cartridge filtration, the intermediate was subjected to hydrolysis using hydroiodic acid (HI, 57%, 1 mL) at 180 °C for 15 min. The crude product was neutralized, filtered, and purified by HPLC according to previously established conditions (Chang et al. 2023).

Conjugation of [18F]FBPA with fructose or sorbitol

Following HPLC purification, the [18F]FBPA fraction was collected and evaporated to dryness via rotary evaporation at 80 °C. The residue was then reconstituted in a mixture of sodium bicarbonate (0.5 mL, 8.4%) and either fructose or sorbitol solution (1.0 mL, 0.5 mol/L) to facilitate complexation. The mixture was heated at 80 °C for 10 min, cooled to r.t. Finally, the solution was passed through a 0.22-µm sterile membrane filter to provide the injectable formulation.

Cell culture and tumor inoculation

SASV03 human tongue carcinoma cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To establish the tumor model, 6-week-old male Balb/c nude mice were purchased from the National Center for Biomodels (NCB, Taiwan). SASV03 cells (1 × 106 cells in 100 µL of serum-free DMEM) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. When tumor volume reached 100 ± 50 mm3, the mice were subjected to biological characterization studies.

MicroPET/MR

In vivo biodistribution of [18F]FBPA and its conjugates ([18F]FBPA-frucose and [18F]FBPA-sorbitol) was evaluated using microPET/MR imaging. All experiments were performed at Taipei Veterans General Hospital using a 7T small-animal MRI system (Bruker ClinScan 70/30, Billerica, MA, USA) integrated with a microPET/MR Inline scanner (Bruker, Rheinstetten, Germany). Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 2% isoflurane in oxygen (v/v) and placed in a prone position on the scanner bed. T2-weighted MR images serve as anatomical landmarks for the subsequent co-registration of PET data. Static PET scans were performed 1 h after intravenous injection of 5.55 MBq of the respective radiotracer. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually drawn over the tumor and contralateral muscle on the images to calculate the tumor-to-muscle (T/M) ratio for quantitative analysis.

Biodistribution studies

Tumor-bearing mice received an intravenous injection of [18F]FBPA or its conjugates (1.48 MBq per mouse). At 1 h post-injection, the animals were euthanized, and organs and tissues, including blood, heart, lungs, liver, stomach, large intestine, small intestine, pancreas, spleen, kidneys, brain, bone, tumor, and muscle, were harvested. The radioactivity in each sample was measured using a gamma counter. Data were expressed as the percentage of injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g).

Statistical analyses

The values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Prior to hypothesis testing, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. ANOVA was used to evaluate differences among the groups, followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons test to identify specific group differences. The sample size (n) for each independent experimental group is indicated in the corresponding figure legends and tables. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with significance levels denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Optimization of manual synthesis conditions

For the initial radiofluorination step of the three-step synthesis using precursors P-01 and P-02, the effects of solvent and reaction temperature were systematically evaluated to optimize fluorination efficiency. Among the solvents tested, DMF outperformed DMSO, providing fluorination efficiencies of 83.6% ± 5.4% for P-01 and 80.8% ± 3.1% for P-02 (Fig. 3A). Temperature optimization revealed 120 °C as the most favorable condition for both precursors, achieving efficiencies of 84.6% ± 3.9% (P-01) and 83.2% ± 4.3% (P-02), whereas reactions conducted at 110–130 °C resulted in diminished efficiencies (Fig. 3B). A reaction duration of 20 min was sufficient to achieve effective radiofluorination, as extending the reaction to 30 min did not lead to a significant increase in yield (Fig. 3C). Increasing the amount of P-01 from 5 mg to 10 mg resulted in a substantial enhancement in labeling efficiency; however, further escalation to 20 mg produced only a marginal improvement (Fig. 3D).

Optimization of [18F]FBPA production using P-01 and P-02 as precursors. Effects of A Solvent (n = 2), B Reaction temperature (n = 3), C Reaction time (n = 3), D Precursor amount on radiofluorination efficiency (n = 3), and E Catalyst on borylation efficiency (n = 3)

The borylation efficiencies of precursors P-01 and P-02 were evaluated under two published catalytic and solvent conditions to optimize the borylation rate (Fig. 3E). Under identical reaction conditions using Pd(dppf)Cl2 in DMF at 140 °C, P-02 exhibited a higher borylation efficiency of 25.2% ± 4.2%, compared to 20.3% ± 5.4% observed for P-01. The use of Pd(dba)2 with phosphine ligands in dioxane at 110 °C resulted in markedly lower efficiencies for both precursors.

The optimization of the di-Bpin precursor, P-03, revealed solvent requirements distinct from those of the mono-Bpin precursors (Fig. 4A). When pure DMA or DMF was used as the reaction solvent, relatively low n.d.c. radiochemical yields of 1.3% ± 0.2% and 2.8% ± 0.4%, respectively, were obtained. In contrast, the use of a DMF/ACN mixed solvent significantly enhanced labeling efficiency, reaching an n.d.c. radiochemical yield of 7.5% ± 1.0%. Consistent with the mono-Bpin precursors, the optimal temperature for the radiofluorination of P-03 was determined to be 120 °C (Fig. 4B). Further optimization demonstrated that 9.5 mg of the Cu(OTf)2(py)4 catalyst (Fig. 4C) and 0.05 mL of pyridine (Fig. 4D) were optimal for efficient radiofluorination. Increasing the precursor mass to 15 mg resulted in the highest and most reproducible radiochemical yields (Fig. 4E). During hydrolysis, 0.5 mL of hydroiodic acid (Fig. 4F) and a reaction temperature of 180 °C (Fig. 4G) were required to ensure complete deprotection, affording an optimal radiochemical yield.

Comparison of radiolabeling performance among different precursors

Based on the optimized conditions established in manual synthesis, automated syntheses using BNBA, P-02, and P-03 precursors were implemented (Fig. 5 and Table S1). Table 1 summarizes the radiochemical yields obtained for each precursor under its respective optimized reaction conditions. The non-decay-corrected radiochemical yields were 2.6% ± 1.3%, 0.3% ± 0.1%, and 3.1% ± 0.0%, respectively. These values were slightly lower than those achieved by manual synthesis, despite the reduced overall preparation time afforded by the automated process. Additionally, the specific activities for the BNBA, P-02, and P-03 precursors were determined to be 50 ± 22, 43.1 ± 21, and 36.3 ± 17 GBq/µmol, respectively. While the traditional electrophilic production of [18F]FBPA suffered from inherently low molar activity (0.257 ± 0.037 GBq/µmol), our automated nucleophilic synthesis yielded a remarkably high molar activity across all pathways.

Quality control

The chemical identity of [18F]FBPA synthesized from each precursor was confirmed via analytical HPLC (Figure S4A). The radio- and UV-chromatograms obtained from the co-injection of radioactive product with an authentic L-FBPA standard were nearly identical. Chiral HPLC analysis further demonstrated that the synthesized [18F]FBPA was obtained exclusively in the L-configuration, with no detectable R-form product (Figure S4B). A comprehensive summary of quality control parameters for the [18F]FBPA products is provided in Table S2. Notably, irrespective of the synthetic route or precursor employed, all final [18F]FBPA preparations met the quality control release criteria evaluated in this study.

MicroPET/MR imaging studies

To evaluate the influence of alditol complexation on the biological behaviors of the [18F]FBPA, microPET/MR imaging was performed in SASV03 tumor-bearing mice at 60 min post-injection of [18F]FBPA and its alditol formulations. Representative imaging results revealed that all three forms [18F]FBPA predominantly accumulated in the kidneys and tumors (Fig. 6A). The tumor standardized uptake values were 1.72 ± 0.25 for [18F]FBPA, 1.29 ± 0.25 for [18F]FBPA-fructose ([18F]FBPA-Fr), and 2.57 ± 0.28 for [18F]FBPA-sorbitol ([18F]FBPA-Sor). Correspondingly, the average T/M ratios were 3.34 ± 1.07, 4.64 ± 0.60, and 9.38 ± 0.88, respectively.

Biodistribution studies

In SASV03 tumor-bearing mice, tumor uptake of [18F]FBPA, [18F]FBPA-Fr, and [18F]FBPA-Sor was 10.46 ± 0.61, 11.51 ± 2.75, and 19.32 ± 1.57%ID/g, respectively (Fig. 6B). The T/M and tumor-to-blood (T/B) ratios were 6.9 ± 1.1 and 5.1 ± 1.4 for [18F]FBPA, 6.7 ± 1.3 and 5.0 ± 1.2 for [18F]FBPA-Fr, and 11.2 ± 2.0 and 8.1 ± 1.2 for [18F]FBPA-Sor, respectively (Fig. 6B). In healthy tissues, no significant differences in tracer uptake were observed among the three tracers, except in the pancreas, bone, and bone marrow. Specifically, [18F]FBPA-Fr exhibited the highest pancreatic uptake (36.75 ± 6.56%ID/g), followed by [18F]FBPA (26.51 ± 4.56%ID/g) and [18F]FBPA-Sor (23.86 ± 4.77%ID/g).

Discussion

The growing development of BNCT as a targeted radiotherapy modality for refractory malignancies has intensified the demand for precise companion diagnostics. In Taiwan, BPA-Fr is the primary boron delivery agent used for BNCT, and [18F]FBPA-Fr serves as the PET probe for the noninvasive prediction and quantification of tumor boron accumulation to support patient selection. Mairinger et al. demonstrated that a nucleophilic FDG synthesis module could be retrofitted with stainless steel gas lines to accommodate [18F]F2 for electrophilic labeling, achieving a radiochemical yield of 8.5% (Mairinger et al. 2015). However, this approach highlights the substantial degree of “custom engineering” required to perform electrophilic synthesis on non-specialized platforms. In addition, electrophilic methods are generally associated with low molar activity, which, although not necessarily a limiting factor for imaging contrast with [18F]FBPA-Fr (Chang et al. 2023), may raise regulatory concerns regarding classification as a radiotracer versus drug-level administration. These limitations have motivated the development of nucleophilic radiofluorination strategies, which offer improved molar activity and superior compatibility with automated synthesis platforms.

To contextualize the performance of our optimized methodologies, a comprehensive comparison with previously reported [18F]FBPA production methods is summarized in Supplemental Table 3. Early production methods primarily relied on electrophilic substitution using 4-borono-L-phenylalanine as the starting material. Although this approach offers the advantages of a single-step reaction and short synthesis time, it is inherently limited by low molar activity, which has been reported as 0.257 ± 0.037 GBq/µmol (Mairinger et al. 2015). In contrast, recent advances in nucleophilic substitution radiofluorination have significantly improved the achievable molar activity. In the present study, automated production using the non-Bpin-containing precursor achieved a molar activity of 50 ± 22 GBq/µmol (Table 1), which was comparable to that obtained using the corresponding manual synthesis method (Chang et al. 2023). The molar activities obtained from the mono-Bpin and di-Bpin precursors were 43 ± 21 and 36 ± 17 GBq/µmol, respectively. These values represent a marked improvement over the electrophilic substitution approach, with a 200-fold increase in molar activity. Nevertheless, the biological impact of this difference in molar activity should be interpreted cautiously. In our previous study, we found that the difference between NCA and CA [18F]FBPA did not appear to significantly affect imaging, as tumor uptake and tumor-to-muscle ratios were comparable in tumor-bearing mice administered either formulation (Chang et al. 2023). However, whether such differences in molar activity may influence the biological characteristics or clinical imaging performance in patients remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

The Bpin-free precursor, BNBA, was adapted from the [18F]FDOPA synthesis reported by Libert et al., following a similar multi-step sequence with the addition of a borylation step to introduce the boron moiety (Libert et al. 2013). The reaction conditions for BNBA had been optimized in our previous studies and were directly translated to the automated synthesis platform in the present work. In contrast, the newly developed precursors required systematic optimization of reaction conditions prior to their implementation on the autosynthesizer. For P-03, the effects of different eluting agents on radiochemical yield were evaluated. Among the conditions tested, KOTf in water afforded the highest n.d.c radiochemical yield of 7.51% ± 1.00%. In contrast, TBAOTf in water produced a substantially lower yield of 1.85% ± 0.64% (Table S4). No detectable product was observed when 1-butanol or DMA was used as eluent (Table S4), in contrast to the radiochemical conversions of up to 55% reported by Zischler et al. (2017). In their study, the use of 1-butanol significantly enhanced copper-mediated radiofluorination, achieving conversions of up to 94% for various substrates, compared with < 65% in aprotic solvents (Zischler et al. 2017). Moreover, the alcohol-assisted approach simplified the synthetic workflow by eliminating the need for azeotropic drying, a common challenge in conventional radiofluorination protocols.

In general, P-01 exhibited optimal reaction conditions similar to those of P-02 (Fig. 3). It is noteworthy that increasing the reaction temperature accelerated the reaction rate; however, excessively high temperatures led to precursor decomposition or side reactions (Fig. 3B). A similar trend was observed for P-03, for which deviations to either 110–130 °C resulted in reduced reaction efficiency, underscoring the importance of precise thermal control during the copper-mediated radiofluorination step (Fig. 4B). The primary limitation in the synthesis via P-01 and P-02 lies in the borylation step. Although several promising conditions reported in the literature were evaluated (Carroll et al. 2024), the borylation efficiency did not exceed 30% under the conditions tested (Fig. 3E). This limited efficiency may be attributed to the use of bulkier protecting groups, which, although simplifying precursor synthesis by reducing the number of synthetic steps, introduce steric hindrance that negatively impacts the borylation reaction rate.

Although the di-Bpin precursor P-03 overcomes low borylation efficiency by virtue of a pre-installed aryldiboron moiety, the presence of two boronate ester groups allows radiofluorination at both the 2- and 4-positions. This lack of regioselectivity leads to the formation of undesired side products, thereby reducing the overall radiochemical yield. A similar phenomenon has been reported by He et al. (2021). Indeed, the optimal solvent system and catalyst loading required for P-03 differed from those for P-01 and P-02, highlighting distinct reaction requirements for this precursor (Fig. 4).

Following the successful manual optimization of reaction conditions, automated production protocols were established for all precursors (Table 1). Although P-01 and P-02 provided nearly identical radiochemical yields, P-02 exhibited a higher borylation efficiency (Fig. 3E), which is advantageous for the subsequent preparation of P-03. Therefore, P-02 was ultimately selected for automated synthesis, as it shares the same protecting group strategy as P-03, allowing for a more direct comparison between the precursor systems. Among the evaluated pathways, the route utilizing P-03 exhibited the highest n.d.c. radiochemical yield (3.1% ± 0.0%) primarily due to a streamlined two-step synthetic procedure and reduced total reaction time (Table 1). The overall yield for mono-Bpin precursor was notably compromised by suboptimal borylation conversion efficiency during the automated sequence. Comparison of these three precursor strategies highlights a critical trade-off between synthetic complexity and radiolabeling efficiency. The BNBA approach benefits from the use of a commercially available precursor, making it a convenient option for routine clinical implementation. However, this strategy relies on a relatively complex, multistep labeling workflow, which places substantial demands on the robustness and reliability of the automated synthesis system. In contrast, the Bpin-based precursors incorporate predefined structural motifs that are specifically designed for radiofluorination. This design enables more efficient labeling reactions and, consequently, higher radiochemical performance, rendering these precursors a more effective platform for streamlined and efficient radiolabeling. Future precursor development may benefit from incorporating alternative leaving groups or redesigned protecting motifs to enforce site-selective fluorination and further enhance labeling efficiency, such as the use of organostannane derivatives and aryliodonium salts (Guerard et al. 2016).

Regarding RCY, several previously reported nucleophilic substitution methods have higher yields under manual or specific reaction conditions. Many of these studies did not fully address key practical parameters relevant to clinical translation, including automation compatibility, overall synthesis time, and routine operational feasibility. In the present study, all evaluated nucleophilic routes, including the non-Bpin, mono-Bpin, and two-Bpin pathways, were successfully implemented on a commercial automated synthesis module, yielding decay-corrected RCYs of 6.1% ± 2.3%, 0.9% ± 0.2%, and 6.4% ± 0.1%, respectively (Supplemental Table 3). The decay-corrected RCYs obtained from the mono-Bpin precursor in our study were lower than those reported by Yoshimoto et al. (2019), and the RCYs obtained from the di-Bpin precursor were also lower than those reported by He et al. (2021). However, it should be noted that Yoshimoto et al. did not perform the synthesis using an automated platform. The relatively lower absolute RCYs observed in our automated settings may be attributed, at least in part, to fluidic transfer dead volumes and activity retention within the synthesis module. Another contributing factor may be the low efficiency of the preceding borylation and hydrolysis steps. This limitation is likely associated with the hardware configuration of the commercial [18F]FDOPA synthesis module used in this study. Specifically, the reaction vessel used for these steps has a relatively large internal volume and lacks a hermetic sealing mechanism. This open-system configuration may prevent the reaction mixture from maintaining a stable and optimal reaction temperature, thereby reducing reaction efficiency. To address this technical limitation, future optimization will focus on improving the hardware configuration, such as implementing a customized, smaller-volume conical reaction vial equipped with an automated sealing clamp. This modification is expected to provide a sealed and thermally stable reaction environment, thereby improving the efficiency and reproducibility of the borylation and hydrolysis steps. He et al. previously developed an automated method for the production of [18F]FBPA using a di-Bpin precursor (He et al. 2021). The difference in RCY between our study and that of He et al. may be related to differences in precursor structure. In the present study, we intentionally introduced more hydrophobic and bulky protecting groups, rather than Boc protection, to improve precursor stability. Notably, the precursor remained intact after storage at -20 °C for 8 months. However, these structural modifications may also have contributed to the formation of undesired side products during radiofluorination. He et al. proposed that potential side products may include radiofluorinated tyrosine and phenylalanine (He et al. 2021). Unfortunately, He et al. did not discuss side-product formation in detail, which limits direct comparison between the two studies. Nevertheless, the successful implementation of a fully automated production, together with reduced manual handling and consistently high molar activity, supports the robustness and clinical translational potential of the optimized manufacturing routes developed in this study.

Although the automated methodologies optimized in this study were designed with clinical translation in mind, full implementation in a cGMP-compliant environment will require further comprehensive validation. Future steps toward clinical regulatory approval should include formal process validation across multiple consecutive batches, long-term formulation stability studies, detailed regulatory risk assessment, and process qualification. In particular, because optimized automated routes involve copper- or palladium-mediated reactions, residual transition-metal impurities must be carefully controlled to ensure patient safety and product quality. Potential concerns include copper-associated oxidative stress mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and hypersensitivity or sensitization risks related to trace palladium exposure (Gaetke et al. 2014, Kielhorn et al. 2002). To mitigate these risks, the automated manufacturing platform incorporates a dual-stage purification strategy combining solid-phase extraction cartridges and preparative HPLC. For future clinical validation, ICP-MS will be required to confirm that residual copper and palladium levels remain consistently below the permitted daily exposure limits defined by international standards.

MicroPET/MR imaging and biodistribution studies demonstrated that alditol conjugation significantly influences the tracer pharmacokinetics (Fig. 6). Although tumor uptake differed significantly among the three tracers, background radioactivity in blood and muscle remained consistently low, with no significant differences observed across groups. Given the emerging trend toward replacing fructose with sorbitol in BPA formulations (Sato and Hirose 2024, Sato et al. 2024), these findings should be interpreted with caution. The significantly higher tumor uptake observed with the [18F]FBPA-Sor complex suggests that the carbohydrate vehicle plays a critical role in modulating the in vivo pharmacokinetic behavior of [18F]FBPA. A possible mechanistic explanation is related to the structural conformation and superior stability of the sorbitol complex. As reported by Hsieh et al. (2026), molecular modeling using molecular mechanics 2 force field (MM2) energy minimization reveals that the flexible, open-chain backbone of D-sorbitol may facilitate favorable auxiliary hydrogen-bonding interactions with the carboxylate group of FBPA, in addition to tetrahedral borate complex formation through interaction with vicinal diol. Because sorbitol is a reduced sugar alcohol, it does not undergo anomeric equilibrium or form reactive aldehyde or keto intermediates. This structural stability, supported by 1H-NMR and HPLC stability analyses, may reduce premature dissociation of the complex and limit the formation of minor decomposition-related species. As a result, the phenylalanine core of FBPA may remain accessible for LAT1-mediated transport into tumor cells.

In addition to stability, the formulation properties of [18F]FBPA-Sor may also contribute to its biological bioavailability. Scanning electron microscopy of lyophilized [18F]FBPA-Sor showed a porous matrix that rapidly hydrated and completely dissolved within 15 s, suggesting excellent aqueous solubility and a reduced tendency for aggregation (Hsieh et al. 2026). This may help maintain the tracer in a more favorable homogeneous and bioavailable state after administration, thereby reducing nonspecific tissue retention. The stable complex may also promote the formation of a hydration layer, reducing nonspecific hydrophobic interactions with serum proteins, such as albumin (Vogler 1998). Collectively, these factors may contribute to more efficient blood clearance, improved tumor-to-background contrast, and enhanced LAT1-mediated tumor accumulation.

Kondo et al. previously reported that [18F]FBPA-Fr does not reliably reflect the pharmacokinetics of BPA-Fr (Kondo et al. 2026). To further elucidate the relationship between PET imaging signals and absolute boron concentration, we evaluated the correlation between tissue SUV and boron concentration measured by ICP-MS (Figure S5). Significant linear correlations were observed for both fructose- and sorbitol-complexed tracers, [18F]FBPA-Sor (R2 = 0.83) and [18F]FBPA-Fr (R2 = 0.66), suggesting their potential utility for predicting in vivo boron distribution. In contrast, unconjugated [18F]FBPA showed the lowest correlation among the evaluated tracers (R2 = 0.57). This lower correlation may be partly attributed to the absence of a hydrophilic shielding moiety in unconjugated [F]FBPA. Its relatively higher lipophilicity may facilitate non-specific passive diffusion into these lipid-dense compartments, such as the pancreas, which is characterized by high LAT-1 expression and a lipid-rich exocrine architecture, leading to pancreas hyper-accumulation. Conversely, conjugation with sorbitol or fructose may reduce the overall lipophilicity of the molecule. This modification may suppress non-specific uptake in lipophilic tissue and reduce pancreatic hyper-accumulation, thereby improving the correlation between PET-driven SUV and ICP-MS-determined boron concentration. Nevertheless, although these correlation data provide a robust chemical rationale for utilizing [18F]FBPA-Sor to estimate boron biodistribution, several biological limitations should be acknowledged to prevent overinterpretation. First, the in vivo imaging and ICP-MS correlation analyses were performed in a single tumor xenograft model. Therefore, the generalizability of these correlation coefficients across different tumor types with variable LAT1 expression requires further validation. Second, the correlation was established based on a single static PET imaging time point at 1 h post-injection. The absence of dynamic PET imaging limits the ability to derive time-resolved pharmacokinetic parameters, which are important for defining the optimal therapeutic window for BNCT. Accordingly, while our findings demonstrate a clear linear relationship between PET-derived SUV and absolute boron concentration for the carbohydrate/polyol-complexed tracers, further longitudinal studies incorporating multiple tumor models, dynamic PET imaging, multi-time-point biodistribution analysis, and organ-level dosimetry evaluation are warranted before this approach can be fully translated into routine clinical treatment planning.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically compared multiple strategies for the production of NCA [18F]FBPA, with the aim of identifying approaches suitable for reliable and automation-compatible clinical translation. By employing structurally analogous precursors, we enabled a fair evaluation of radiolabeling efficiency, synthetic complexity, and production feasibility. Among the strategies examined, di-Bpin-based precursors demonstrated improved radiochemical performance, albeit with distinct optimization requirements. In vivo microPET/MR imaging and biodistribution studies further highlighted the critical role of carbohydrate conjugation, with sorbitol complexation exhibiting enhanced tumor uptake while maintaining low background activity. Collectively, these findings provide practical insights into precursor design, radiosynthesis optimization, and imaging performance, offering a rational framework to guide future development and clinical implementation of [18F]FBPA as a companion diagnostic for BNCT.
