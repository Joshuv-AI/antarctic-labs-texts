Analysis of medication adherence and influencing factors in postpartum patients with persistent hyperglycemia following gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Jiaojiao Liu, Ting He, Ziye Wang, Lingjun Sun, Cheng Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis retrospective cohort study aimed to investigate medication behavior and associated influencing factors among patients with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) who developed abnormal glucose metabolism during the postpartum period, with the goal of informing the development of a patient-centered postpartum glucose management model.Patients and methodsWe included patients diagnosed with GDM who delivered at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022. Using a combination of semi-structured telephone interviews and structured questionnaires, we retrospectively collected and analyzed data related to postpartum medication behaviors and factors affecting treatment initiation.ResultsAmong the 274 participants, 80 received pharmacological intervention and 194 did not. In the pharmacotherapy group, the average interval from delivery to initiation of medication was 12.3 ± 6.6 months, with monotherapy being the most common regimen (72.5% metformin). Telephone interviews revealed generally low medication adherence and overall dissatisfaction with current postpartum glucose management. Multivariate analysis identified the following factors associated with initiation of pharmacological treatment: impaired fasting glucose combined with impaired glucose tolerance (IFG + IGT), dyslipidemia, use of hypoglycemic agents during pregnancy, and higher perceived diabetes risk (OR > 1, p < 0.05). Conversely, elevated HbA1c levels, breastfeeding beyond one year, and higher self-efficacy were associated with reduced likelihood of medication intervention (OR < 1, p < 0.05).ConclusionsPostpartum glycemic management remains suboptimal in patients with persistent hyperglycemia after GDM. Identified barriers to pharmacotherapy—including delayed initiation and misalignment with postpartum daily life—highlight the need for more adaptable and personalized management models to improve long-term medication adherence and outcomes.Trial registrationThis study has been publicly registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) (registration no. ChiCTR2300074444, registration date:2023/08/07, retrospectively registered).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08836-5.

Background

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) refers to varying degrees of glucose metabolism abnormalities first identified during pregnancy, without meeting the diagnostic criteria for overt diabetes [1]. GDM is associated with an increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and stillbirth, and also poses long-term health risks for patients [2, 3]. Without timely intervention, their glycemic status may progressively deteriorate, potentially progressing to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Evidence suggests that GDM patients with early postpartum glucose metabolism abnormalities have a significantly higher risk of developing diabetes within 1 to 3 years after delivery compared to those with normal postpartum glucose metabolism [4, 5]. Therefore, effective glycemic management in postpartum GDM patients with persistent metabolic dysfunction is critical for improving their long-term health outcomes and quality of life.

Chinese clinical guidelines recommend regular postpartum evaluation of glucose metabolism in women with a history of GDM, along with long-term follow-up management [1, 6, 7]. Among GDM patients with abnormal glucose metabolism after delivery, two categories can be identified: those already diagnosed with T2DM, and those in a prediabetic state. For the former, clinical care should follow established diabetes clinical pathways with individualized glycemic targets. For those in a prediabetic state, guidelines recommend lifestyle interventions and/or the use of metformin 7. Effective pharmacologic and lifestyle interventions can reduce the risk of progression to diabetes, improve insulin sensitivity, and support postpartum weight management. However, there remains a gap between clinical practice and guideline recommendations, largely due to low patient adherence. In one study involving 71 postpartum GDM patients prescribed metformin, only 15 (21.1%) took at least 75% of the intended dosage over a one-year follow-up period, while 11 (15.5%) took less than half of the prescribed amount [8]. Poor adherence significantly undermines the effectiveness of postpartum glycemic management. Therefore, understanding patients’ attitudes toward postpartum pharmacological interventions, their medication behaviors, and the influencing factors is essential for developing individualized glycemic management strategies that better align with patient needs, thereby improving adherence and clinical outcomes.

Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the utilization of antihyperglycemic agents in patients with GDM complicated by postpartum glucose metabolism abnormalities and to analyze the potential influencing factors of pharmacological interventions from the patients’ perspective. This study adheres to STROBE statement. It will focus on identifying the weak links in postpartum glucose management and provide supportive data and reliable references for improving patient medication adherence and optimizing a patient-centered postpartum glycemic management model.

Methods

Research subjects

The study participants were selected from patients who delivered at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022, and were diagnosed with GDM during their pregnancy. Inclusion criteria were:(1) Conducted a 75 g Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) between 24 and 28 weeks of pregnancy, with results showing a Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG) ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, or 1-hour Postprandial Blood Glucose (PBG) ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour PBG ≥ 8.5 mmol/L; (2) Postpartum glucose screening between 4 and 12 weeks, diagnosed with Impaired Glucose Tolerance (IGT) (FBG < 5.6 mmol/L and 2-hour PBG > 7.8–11.0 mmol/L), or Impaired Fasting Glucose (IFG) (FBG between 5.6 and 6.9 mmol/L and 2-hour PBG < 7.8 mmol/L), or elevated HbA1c (≥ 5.7%). Exclusion Criteria were:(1) Presence of severe diseases involving the heart, brain, lungs, kidneys, liver, or immune system; (2) No blood sugar re-check after being diagnosed with postpartum glucose metabolism abnormalities; (3) Refusal to complete the survey questionnaire; (4) Severe lack of data.

Sample size and grouping

The group was divided into a drug intervention group and a non-drug intervention group based on whether glucose-lowering drug treatment lasting more than 3 months after delivery. A binary logistic regression statistical test model will be used to analyze the factors influencing patients’ medication behavior. The minimum sample size required was estimated according to the empirical rule of 10 Events Per Variable [9]. Qualitative study: semi-structured interviews

Development of interview guide

The interview guide for the semi-structured telephone interviews was designed using a combination of literature review and expert consultation. Initially, keywords such as “Gestational Diabetes Mellitus,” “Postpartum,” “Prediabetes,” “Prevention,” “Pharmacological Intervention,” and “Lifestyle” were used to search major medical literature databases including CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure), CBM (China Biomedical Literature Database), PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. Potential influencing factors were analyzed and organized to draft the interview guide. Subsequently, an expert consultation group was formed, comprising five professionals with over five years of experience in endocrinology, including doctors, nurses, and clinical pharmacists. The draft guide was reviewed and discussed, with modifications suggested and agreed upon to finalize the interview guide (Table 1).

Interview process

Following the information efficacy model proposed by Malterud et al., [10] the more distinct the characteristics of the respondents, the richer and more relevant the information provided, and thus, the smaller the required sample size. In previous qualitative studies on postpartum glycemic management, the number of GDM patients interviewed ranged from 6 to 31 [11–15]. Considering that the interview questions in this study are relatively few and the participants have strong characteristics, combined with the experience of previous qualitative studies and the opinions of the expert consultation group, the number of patients to be interviewed was determined to be 20. From the Hospital Information System (HIS), 20 GDM patients receiving postpartum pharmacological intervention were included for semi-structured telephone interviews by volunteer sampling. Before each interview, the researcher would first introduce themselves and explain the study purpose in detail. The researcher assured participants of strict confidentiality of interview data and informed them that they could voluntarily withdraw at any time if they felt uncomfortable during the process. Questions were only formally initiated after obtaining the interviewee’s consent. Each interview was limited to approximately 15 min. The researcher maintained a neutral stance throughout, encouraging participants to fully express their genuine opinions while documenting key points. After each interview, patient responses were systematically organized to extract essential themes.

Quantitative research: questionnaire survey

Design of the survey questionnaire

The survey questionnaire was developed through a literature review and expert consultation. Potential factors influencing patients’ choice of postpartum pharmacological intervention were compiled, and the first draft of the questionnaire was developed, comprising four main sections: basic information questionnaire, disease risk perception scale, self-efficacy scale, and self-management level scale.

Patient basic information questionnaire

Age, obstetric history, delivery mode, presence of adverse pregnancy outcomes, severe comorbidities, family history of diabetes, gestational glucose management methods, medication information, and laboratory data were extracted from the HIS system. Self-reported items included: receipt of glucose management health education (antenatal/postpartum), household monthly income per capita, type of health insurance coverage, availability of family assistance for infant care, postpartum depression, postpartum overweight/obesity, and breastfeeding duration.

Disease risk perception scale

The Risk Perception for Developing Diabetes (RP-DD) scale, developed by American scholar Walker in 2003, [16] was used to assess perceived diabetes risk among high-risk populations or prediabetic patients. The RP-DD scale comprises 32 items across five dimensions: personal control, worry, optimistic bias, other disease risks, and environmental risks (see Appendix Table S1).

Self-efficacy scale

This measures an individual’s confidence in executing specific behaviors to achieve desired outcomes. The study employed the Generalized Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) [17], developed by German scholar Schwarzer based on self-efficacy theory. It consists of 10 items (see Appendix Table S2); higher scores indicate stronger self-efficacy.

Self-management level scale

The study adopted the Self-management Scale for Prediabetes (SMSPD) [18], developed by Chinese scholar Ge Guo et al. within a social cognitive theory framework. The scale includes 29 items across nine dimensions: health beliefs, self-efficacy, dietary management, exercise management, rest/sleep management, stress coping, adherence management, family environment management, and social environment management (see Appendix Table S3).

Pilot study

After drafting the initial questionnaire, a convenience sample of 50 eligible patients was randomly selected for pilot testing. Patients were notified by phone and sent a survey link via text message. Questionnaire completion rates and completion time were collected, and non-respondents were contacted by phone to ascertain reasons. Ultimately, only 29 patients completed the questionnaire, with an average completion time of 16.3 ± 4.23 min. Common feedback from non-completers included: (1) excessive number of questions, (2) the ‘environmental risks’ and ‘other disease risks’ dimensions of the RP-DD scale being irrelevant to their daily lives, and (3) partial overlap between items in the ‘health beliefs’ and ‘self-efficacy’ dimensions of the SMSPD scale with items in the RP-DD and GSES scales. With approval from the expert advisory group, appropriate revisions were made to the RP-DD and SMSPD scales, resulting in the finalized second version of the questionnaire. The specific scale modifications are detailed in Appendix Tables S1-S3. The revised instruments demonstrated good internal consistency in our study population (Cronbach’s α: 0.78–0.87).

Variable definitions

Postpartum (4–12 weeks) glucose metabolism abnormality types

Although clinical guidelines require standard 75 g OGTT, a considerable proportion of patients in our hospital database did not complete the standard OGTT. To increase sample size, patients with only abnormal FBG (5.6–6.9 mmol/L) without 2 h PBG measurement were included and defined as IFG. Similarly, patients with only elevated HbA1c (≥ 5.7%) without FBG or 2 h PBG measurement were included and defined as HbA1c elevation.

Comorbidities

Hypertension and dyslipidemia were included as potential influencing factors through qualitative analysis. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 80 mmHg [19]. Dyslipidemia was defined as: triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, total cholesterol ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L, or LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L [20].

Advanced maternal age

Defined as delivery age ≥ 35 years [21].

Adverse pregnancy outcomes

Included macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, fetal distress, postpartum hemorrhage, and other GDM-related outcomes [2, 3, 22].

Postpartum obesity

Defined by Body Mass Index (BMI) at 1-year postpartum: normal (< 24 kg/m²), overweight (24–28 kg/m²), obese (≥ 28 kg/m²) [6].

Statistical tests

All data collected from the HIS and the questionnaire surveys were entered into Microsoft Excel 2023 for consolidation and processing, then imported into IBM SPSS 26.0 for description and analysis. All statistical tests were conducted with a two-tailed P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. The study compared 21 potential influencing factors between the postpartum pharmacological intervention group and the non-pharmacological intervention group. For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was first performed to check for normal distribution; those fitting a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using independent samples t-tests; those not fitting a normal distribution were presented as medians (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using chi-square tests.

Multivariate sensitivity analysis was conducted using a binary logistic regression model. Postpartum pharmacological intervention was the dependent variable (no = 0, yes = 1), with statistically significant indicators from univariate analysis as independent variables. The regression model provided regression coefficients (β), Wald values and their significance, odds ratios (OR), and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). Multiple collinearity tests and forest plots were also conducted.

Results

Medication use among patients

A total of 274 patients were ultimately included in this study as research subjects, comprising 194 in the postpartum non-pharmacological intervention group and 80 in the postpartum pharmacological intervention group (Fig. 1). All 80 patients in the pharmacological intervention group began using antidiabetic medications only after ceasing breastfeeding, with an average interval of 12.3 ± 6.6 months from delivery to the start of medication. None of them exhibited obvious symptoms such as dry mouth, polydipsia, polyuria, or polyphagia at the time of medication initiation. Among them, 28 patients had blood glucose levels that met the diagnostic criteria for T2DM at the start of medication, while the remaining 52 patients were still in a prediabetic state.

In the pharmacological intervention group, 64 patients used a single antidiabetic medication to control their blood glucose, including metformin (67.2%), insulin (14.1%), Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) (9.4%), and α-glucosidase inhibitors (9.4%). Sixteen patients (20.0%) underwent dual therapy, including metformin combined with Sodium-Glucose Co-Transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) (43.8%), metformin combined with α-glucosidase inhibitors (25.0%), metformin combined with Dipeptidyl Peptidase-4 inhibitors (DDP-4i) (18.8%), metformin combined with SGLT-2i (6.3%), and SGLT-2i combined with GLP-1 RA (6.3%). Regarding the route of administration, 63 patients (78.8%) used only oral antidiabetic medications, while the remaining 17 patients (21.3%) had treatment regimens that included subcutaneous injections (mainly insulin and GLP-1 RA) (Table 2).

Qualitative study

Basic characteristics of respondents

During the interviews, the average age of the 20 respondents was 35.2 ± 3.3 years, with an average interval of 11.1 ± 5.4 months from childbirth to the initiation of medication. In the first glucose screening postpartum at 4–12 weeks, 10 patients (50.0%) were diagnosed with IGT, 3 (15.0%) with IFG, 5 (25.0%) with both IGT and IFG, and 2 (10.0%) showed elevated HbA1c levels. At the time of initiating pharmacological intervention, 9 patients (45.0%) were still in the prediabetes stage, while 11 (55.0%) had progressed to T2DM. Further details on the respondents are provided in Appendix Table S4.

Reasons for medication use

The majority of patients stated that their blood glucose levels had reached the diagnostic criteria for T2DM, and that dietary and exercise adjustments had been ineffective, so they followed the advice of medical staff. Some patients expressed a lack of confidence in their self-management abilities and believed that pharmacological blood glucose reduction was more convenient and faster. A few patients were driven by a strong concern about future diabetes risks. A minority of patients, who were overweight or obese after childbirth, initially hoped to lose weight quickly through medication.

Medication adherence

The vast majority of patients exhibited poor adherence due to complex situations such as forgetting to take medication in the morning rush, difficulty in taking medication on time while taking care of children, irregular meal times leading to irregular medication times, and the need to store insulin in the refrigerator. Only three patients reported good medication adherence.

Influencing factors

Most patients mentioned that factors affecting whether to use pharmacological intervention included disease severity, the presence of other comorbidities, self-management ability, and whether they had received adequate medication education. A few patients believed that family history of diabetes, breastfeeding status, adverse pregnancy outcomes, availability of family members to help care for the baby, and insulin use during pregnancy would also influence the choice of pharmacological intervention.

Glycemic control and adverse reactions

Apart from three patients whose poor adherence led to unsatisfactory outcomes, the vast majority of patients were satisfied with the blood glucose-lowering effects of pharmacological intervention. Two patients using oral hypoglycemic agents reported mild gastrointestinal reactions. Two patients undergoing intensive insulin therapy experienced hypoglycemic events.

Suggestions for improvement

Patients proposed suggestions for postpartum blood glucose management and pharmacological treatment: strengthening follow-up, enhancing medication education, and promoting hospital referrals and collaboration.

Factors influencing the choice of drug intervention in postpartum patients with persistent hyperglycemia following GDM

Regression results indicated that having a family history of diabetes did not influence the decision for postpartum pharmacological intervention in patients with GDM (P = 0.967). However, the type of glucose metabolism abnormality at the 4–12-week postpartum visit, presence of dyslipidemia, glucose management during pregnancy, duration of breastfeeding, awareness of diabetes risk, and self-efficacy significantly affected the choice of intervention (P < 0.05). Patients with both IGT and IFG were more likely to choose pharmacological intervention postpartum (β = 1.444, P = 0.042); those with only abnormal HbA1c levels were more reliant on self-management through diet and exercise (β = -1.218, P = 0.041). Patients with dyslipidemia were 3.810 times more likely to choose pharmacological intervention compared to those without dyslipidemia (β = 1.338, P = 0.004). Patients who had used antidiabetic drugs during pregnancy were 4.659 times more likely to opt for pharmacological intervention postpartum than those who had not used medications during pregnancy (β = 1.539, P = 0.001). Compared to those who breastfed for less than six months, patients who breastfed for over a year were less likely to take antidiabetic drugs (β = -1.269, P = 0.015). Patients with higher awareness of future disease risks were more proactive in using antidiabetic medications (β = 0.378, P < 0.001); meanwhile, those with higher self-efficacy were more inclined toward self-management (β = -0.275, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Pharmacological strategies for postpartum glycemic control in GDM patients

Most GDM patients who receive pharmacological intervention postpartum are treated with a single oral hypoglycemic agent. This may be attributed to their generally younger age, shorter disease duration, and absence of diabetes-related complications. Metformin, the first-line therapy for diabetes, is the most commonly prescribed medication [1]. Its use has been shown to reduce the risk of progression from prediabetes to diabetes and is considered cost-effective over the long term [1]. For patients with elevated postprandial blood glucose levels, α-glucosidase inhibitors such as acarbose are often selected, as they lower postprandial glucose by inhibiting carbohydrate absorption in the upper small intestine. Additionally, in individuals with prediabetes, acarbose has been found to reduce the risk of progression from IGT to diabetes [1]. Nine patients were treated with a short-term intensified insulin therapy plan, which can rapidly eliminate glucotoxicity, promote the recovery of pancreatic beta-cell function, and improve insulin sensitivity in target organs, benefiting the long-term prognosis [23]. Nineteen patients chose newer antidiabetic medications, including GLP-1 RA (n = 8), SGLT-2i (n = 8), and DPP-4 inhibitors (n = 2). The postpartum pharmacological treatment plans for GDM patients in this study adhere to guideline recommendations, and the usage rates of various antidiabetic drugs are similar to those found in a survey by Yang et al. [24] among diabetic patients aged 21–40 years.

Analysis of postpartum medication behavior in GDM patients

In this study, telephone interviews were conducted with GDM patients who used antihyperglycemic medications postpartum, based on a self-designed interview guide. The interviews explored five key dimensions: reasons for medication use, medication adherence, facilitating or hindering factors, treatment effects, and suggestions for improving postpartum glycemic management. Overall, patients reported diverse reasons for initiating medication therapy, including following physicians’ advice, concerns about disease progression, and a lack of confidence in their ability to manage blood glucose through diet or exercise alone. Factors influencing medication use were also multifaceted, such as whether the patient had received postpartum health education on glycemic management, a family history of diabetes, comorbid conditions, or support from family members. However, most interviewees reported low adherence to their prescribed medications, with missed doses being common. Some patients attributed their poor adherence to the absence of reminders or supervision from healthcare providers, which in turn reduced their motivation for pharmacological intervention and contributed to dissatisfaction with current postpartum glycemic management services. Based on a thematic analysis of patient interviews, three core themes were identified that shape medication behaviors: a fundamental misalignment between structured treatment regimens and the unpredictable demands of postpartum life, leading to non-adherence as an adaptive response; a utility-driven decision-making model where treatment initiation is influenced by the necessity of clinical treatment for hyperglycemia that meets the criteria for T2DM, lifestyle convenience, and long-term risk/benefit assessments; and a incomplete support system that fails to bridge the transition from intensive pregnancy care, thereby undermining management continuity. These findings demonstrate the need for flexible treatment plans and integrated care models that address these structural, personal, and systemic determinants.

This study also brings to light an important consideration regarding medication safety during lactation. A pivotal finding is that all patients in our pharmacological intervention cohort chose to initiate treatment only after ceasing breastfeeding. This uniform behavior strongly suggests that concern for infant safety is a paramount, non-negotiable factor in postpartum decision-making, effectively creating a self-imposed contraindication to pharmacotherapy during lactation. This profound apprehension likely contributes to the significant delay in treatment initiation (average 12.3 months) and underpins our quantitative finding that extended breastfeeding (beyond one year) is a strong protective factor against pharmacological intervention (OR < 1). In essence, the preference for lactation often takes absolute precedence over glycemic pharmacotherapy, regardless of clinical recommendation. These findings suggest that healthcare professionals should develop personalized plans for diet, exercise, and medication, enabling shared decision-making with patients.

Factors influencing postpartum medication behavior in GDM patients

Analysis revealed that key factors promoting the use of postpartum pharmacological interventions among GDM patients included the presence of both IGT and impaired IFG, dyslipidemia, pharmaceutical intervention during pregnancy, and higher perceived risk of developing diabetes (OR > 1, P < 0.05). Among these, pharmaceutical intervention during pregnancy had the largest absolute regression coefficient (β = 1.539), indicating it was the most influential factor in patients’ postpartum medication decisions. This may be due to increased trust in the effectiveness of pharmacological intervention based on prior treatment experience. In addition, patients’ blood glucose levels at their first postpartum screening also affected their likelihood of using medication (β = 1.444). Compared with those with isolated IFG, patients with both IFG and IGT tend to exhibit greater insulin resistance and are more likely to require pharmacologic glycemic control. Furthermore, GDM patients with dyslipidemia were more likely to initiate postpartum medication use compared to those with normal lipid levels (β = 1.338), possibly due to the added lipid-lowering benefits of certain antihyperglycemic agents. Meta-analyses have shown that medications such as metformin [25], DPP-4 inhibitors [26], and GLP-1 RA [27] can reduce serum total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol in patients with type 2 diabetes, thereby lowering the cardiovascular risks associated with dyslipidemia. Finally, patients with a higher perceived risk of developing diabetes were more inclined to use glucose-lowering medications (β = 0.378). Driven by concerns over serious future complications, these patients were more proactive in participating in postpartum glycemic management, consistent with findings reported by Kim et al. [28] Elevated HbA1c alone, breastfeeding for more than one year, and higher self-efficacy were identified as factors that discouraged postpartum pharmacological intervention among GDM patients (OR < 1, P < 0.05). Patients with only mildly elevated HbA1c levels may have less severe glucose metabolism impairment and are more likely to maintain normal glucose levels through lifestyle modifications (β = − 1.218). Those who breastfed for longer durations were also less likely to use medication (β = − 1.269). This may be due, in part, to the lack of safety data for most oral antihyperglycemic agents during lactation [29], making many women reluctant to initiate pharmacological treatment while breastfeeding. Additionally, breastfeeding itself has the effect of improving the short-term and long-term metabolic status of the mother [30], and a longer duration of lactation plays a protective role in the postpartum disease progression of patients with GDM [31]. Therefore, these patients are less likely to require hypoglycemic agents to control their blood glucose levels. GDM patients with higher self-efficacy were less likely to initiate pharmacological intervention (β = -0.275). This suggests that patients with greater confidence in their self-management abilities may achieve better glycemic control through adherence to lifestyle interventions such as diet and exercise, thereby reducing their requirement for pharmacological treatment. This observation underscores the potential value of enhancing self-efficacy within postpartum glucose management strategies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. Firstly, the single-center design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. Although the number of patients in the pharmacological intervention group (n = 80) was sufficient for our initial regression analysis, it limited our ability to perform more robust subgroup analyses (for example, based on medication types), potentially leaving other influential factors undetected. Secondly, the use of a volunteer sample means participants were self-selected, which may introduce selection bias and limit the transferability of the findings. For instance, individuals willing to participate might have had stronger opinions or particular experiences compared to the broader population. Thirdly, due to the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 to 2022, many patients underwent only fasting blood glucose testing to minimize time spent in clinics and reduce infection risk. To increase the sample size, patients who underwent only FBG and/or HbA1c testing were included in the analysis, which may have affected the accuracy of identifying the target population. However, the primary objective of this study was to investigate patients’ medication adherence behaviors and their determinants, rather than to establish the prevalence or categorical distribution of Persistent Hyperglycemia Following Gestational Diabetes Mellitus. Hence, we consider this potential bias to have limited implications for the core conclusions of our research. Fourth, the reliance on retrospective data collection and self-reported measures, particularly for assessing medication adherence, introduces the potential for recall bias. Medication adherence was assessed via patient interview without objective measures (e.g., pharmacy refill records or pill counts), which could overestimate or underestimate true adherence. Furthermore, the qualitative component was specifically designed to explore the medication experience among patients who had initiated pharmacotherapy. Accordingly, it did not include perspectives from patients who managed their condition solely through lifestyle interventions. While this approach provided rich data on the medication journey, it may not fully capture barriers specifically related to treatment initiation. Finally, this study assessed glycemic status only during the short-term postpartum period (4–12 weeks) and lacked long-term follow-up on diabetes progression. Consequently, the long-term efficacy and sustainability of the pharmacological interventions observed in our study remain unknown. Future research should address these gaps by establishing large, diverse multicenter cohorts to validate findings, then employing mixed methods—including stratified sampling, objective measures, and comparative qualitative designs—to better understand behavioral mechanisms, and ultimately conducting prospective longitudinal studies to evaluate the long-term impact of interventions.

Conclusions

This study evaluated medication adherence and its influencing factors in postpartum patients with persistent hyperglycemia after GDM. Key findings indicate that pharmacotherapy initiation was frequently delayed until after breastfeeding and typically involved single-agent oral regimens. Treatment decisions were often constrained by a mismatch between structured clinical plans and the variable realities of postpartum life. Quantitative analysis identified several determinants: pharmaceutical intervention during pregnancy and the presence of combined IFG and IGT facilitated treatment initiation, whereas higher patient self-efficacy and longer breastfeeding duration were associated with lower likelihood of pharmacotherapy. These results suggest that effective postpartum glycemic management requires strategies that are not only evidence-based but also adaptable to individual contexts. Incorporating pharmacist-led personalized guidance and follow-up into clinical care may help address current gaps in long-term adherence and outcomes.

In summary, pharmacologic intervention remains an underused component of postpartum glycemic management. This study provides an empirical foundation for understanding medication behaviors and barriers, supporting the development of more tailored and integrated management approaches for this population.
