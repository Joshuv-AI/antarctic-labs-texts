Hybrid versus catheter ablation for Hypertrophic CardioMyopathy with Atrial Fibrillation (HCM-AF): study protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Yajie Tang, Le Li, Sipeng Chen, Yumei Xue, Huiming Guo, Lei Song, Min Tang, Yan Yao, Zhe Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAtrial fibrillation (AF) is an independent predictor of adverse outcomes in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). Although catheter ablation is highly recommended for general AF populations, it is less effective in maintaining sinus rhythm in patients with HCM associated with AF. Hybrid ablation, combining a cosmetic approach with a lower rate of AF relapse, lacks comparative studies to verify its efficacy against CA in HCM. This study aims to assess the rhythm control effectiveness of hybrid versus CA in non-obstructive HCM (non-oHCM) patients with AF.Methods/analysisThis prospective, multicentre, randomised trial involves a blinded assessment of outcomes in non-oHCM patients with non-paroxysmal AF. Sixty-six candidates from three centres will be randomised 1:1 to either hybrid or CA, including isthmus addressed lesion sets. Participants will be stratified by left atrial (LA) size (LA diameter ≤50 mm or >50 mm). Follow-ups at the 3rd, 6th and 12th months will evaluate the primary endpoint of freedom from documented atrial tachycardia lasting over 30 s within 12 months post-procedure without antiarrhythmic drugs, along with secondary endpoints of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular-related mortality, cerebral stroke, peripheral vascular embolism, heart failure-related rehospitalisation, all-cause rehospitalisation and quality of life assessments.Ethics and disseminationapprovalThe central ethics committee at Fuwai Hospital has approved the Hypertrophic CardioMyopathy with Atrial Fibrillation trial (approval number: 2022-1736). Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is an inherited condition characterised by increased left ventricular (LV) wall thickness and impaired diastolic function, affecting approximately 0.2%–0.5% of the population.1 The disease process often leads to elevated left atrial (LA) pressure and fibrosis, which collectively foster a conducive environment for atrial arrhythmias. Consequently, the incidence of atrial fibrillation (AF) in HCM patients is significantly higher—20% to 25% compared with the general population2 3—resulting in a fourfold and eightfold increase in the risks of death and thromboembolism, respectively.3 4

Due to the AF-associated morbidity and mortality in HCM subjects, it is generally agreed that vigorous maintenance of sinus rhythm (SR) should be attempted.5 6 The most commonly used antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs), such as amiodarone and sotalol, have shown only mild-to-modest effectiveness and are often discontinued due to side effects or the increased risk of pro-arrhythmia, leading to higher hospitalisation rates.7 8 Compared with AADs, catheter ablation (CA) has demonstrated greater efficacy in improving symptoms and maintaining SR,9 and is therefore recommended (Class I) for AF patients with or without HCM who exhibit drug-refractory symptoms or desire long-term SR restoration.2 6 10

While CA is effective in the general AF population, its success rates are lower in HCM patients, with a 61% AF recurrence rate at 12 months, 41% requiring AAD use, and 34% needing repeat ablation after the initial procedure.11 12 Consequently, more proactive therapeutic alternatives for rhythm control are recommended for HCM patients with AF.7

The standard Cox-maze IV procedure has demonstrated promising SR maintenance, even in patients with long-standing persistent AF.13 It has also achieved a 1-year freedom from AF recurrence rate (off-AADs) of 89.5% as an adjunctive procedure to myectomy in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) subjects, largely due to the complete and transmural ablation lesions.14 However, the invasive nature of the open-heart ablation technique has limited its widespread adoption among most HCM patients with AF, particularly those with non-obstructive HCM (non-oHCM). Hybrid ablation, which combines effective rhythm control outcomes with less invasive cosmetic wounds, has emerged as a viable alternative to open-heart surgical ablation, especially for patients at increased risk of CA failure.7

Although theoretically, hybrid ablation should be effective in restoring SR compared with CA for the AF-associated HCM population, this hypothesis lacks evidence-based support due to the scarcity of comparative data. Given the unclear optimal treatment strategies for this subset of patients, this study aims to compare the effectiveness of restoring SR between two minimally invasive procedures—hybrid and CA—in non-oHCM patients with non-paroxysmal AF. The study will be conducted as a prospective randomised controlled trial across three tertiary centres.

Methods and analysis

Study objective

The Hypertrophic CardioMyopathy with Atrial Fibrillation (HCM-AF) trial is specifically designed to test the hypothesis that for non-paroxysmal AF patients with non-oHCM, hybrid ablation is superior to CA in achieving 1-year freedom from the recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmias without the use of AADs.

Study design

The HCM-AF trial is a prospective, multi-centre, randomised, two-arm parallel-group trial. Enrolled patients will be receiving either hybrid or CA for active rhythm controlling and will be randomised in a 1:1 fashion to one of the investigation arms and stratified by LA size (LA≤50 or >50 mm) with a randomly permuted block size of 2 or 4. The randomisation list will be generated with software and integrated into an interactive web-based response system, which will be adopted to preserve allocation concealment. Given the difference in the procedures in each arm, operators and patients will be aware of randomisation results but outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded. The allocation will be unblinded after the completion of follow-up and data analysis.

Participant screening and enrolment

Enrolment (figure 1) will happen in three specialised cardiac centres in Chinese mainland, among which have qualified AF multidisciplinary teams and have completed over 50 hybrid ablation procedures.

Inclusion criteria include patients aged 18 years or older with non-paroxysmal AF—defined as episodes lasting longer than 7 days but less than 3 years, documented by ECG or Holter monitoring prior to inclusion—who are refractory to at least one, or have refused or are contraindicated to any, class I or III AADs, will be eligible. The diagnosis of non-oHCM will be in accordance with established guideline,15 and the absence of LV outflow tract obstruction will be confirmed by measuring a peak gradient of less than 30 mm Hg during exercise or the Valsalva manoeuvre, as assessed by transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) (refer to online supplemental file 1).

Exclusion criteria include LA diameter >60 mm (by two-dimensional TTE on parasternal long-axis view), LV ejection fraction ≤40%, contraindicated to anticoagulation, concurrent with LA or LA appendage (LAA) emboli, concomitant with coronary artery disease or valvular disease that indicated to open heart surgery, history of stroke within 2 months, history of ablation, chest operation history or other conditions that considered not suitable for thoracoscopic ablation operation, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism and end-stage kidney disease (box 1).

Interventions

Patients will undergo either hybrid or catheter-only ablation according to their randomisation (figure 2). Discontinuation will be interrogated for AADs (subtype I/III, and approximately five half-lives before the procedure), but uninterrupted anticoagulants will be encouraged. Transoesophageal echocardiography or CT scan will be conducted for precluding existence of any emboli in LA or LAA.

Ablation lesion sets in both arms. (A/B) Ablation lesions of hybrid arm in left and right atrium. (C/D) Ablation lesions of catheter arm in left and right atrium. CS, coronary sinus; IVC, inferior vena cava; LAA, left atrial appendage; LAAR, left atrial appendage resection; LIPV, left inferior pulmonary vein; LSPV, left superior pulmonary vein; MV, mitral valve; RIPV, right inferior pulmonary vein; RSPV, right superior pulmonary vein; SVC, superior vena cava; TV, tricuspid valve.

Radiofrequency energy (RF) will be the main energy source in both arms, but pulse filed ablation (PFA) catheter would be acceptable when it is authorised for clinical usage by the Chinese Food and Drug Administration. Three-dimensional mapping system (CARTO-Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA; EnSite NavX- St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN, USA; or Rhythmia-Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) will be used at the discretion of operators. A decapolar coronary sinus (CS) catheter, a quadropolar pacing catheter and a high-density mapping catheter will be placed under fluoroscopy.

The operators participating in this study are highly experienced and represent the leading specialists at each centre.

Catheter ablation

Both single and double sheath ablation techniques will be allowed in this study. After being heparinised with an activated clotting time between 300 and 350 s, the three-dimensional electro-anatomical mapping system and catheter will be used to create a LA geometry, activation or voltage maps (the normal voltage areas were defined as voltage >0.5 mV, transmural areas as <0.1 mV and low-voltage areas as 0.1–0.5 mV). Ablation will be conducted with an open-irrigated catheter (TactiCath, Abbot, St. Paul, MN or ThermoCool/SmartTouch, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA). The ablation strategy will be as follows: electrically isolating each pulmonary vein (PV) at the antral level, then making a linear ablation line at the LA roof, and the tricuspid isthmus line will be completed followed by the lateral mitral isthmus line. A septal mitral isthmus line, which connects the right inferior vein to mitral annulus, will be made when AF remains. Electrical cardioversion will be followed by an intravenous Ibutilide, or electrical defibrillation cardioversion when the AF sustains after the completion of all lesion sets. Any atrial tachycardia will be mapped and ablated whenever it occurs.

Hybrid ablation

Candidates will be in general anaesthesia and double lumen incubation. Pacing catheter will be placed in right ventricle via right internal jugular vein. The thoracoscopic ablation will be performed in two-sided ways, which has been described in our previously study.16

Thoracoscopic epicardial ablation

LAA will be first amputated by a stapling device (EZ60, Ethicon Endosurgery, USA), then ligament of Marshall will be coagulated and transected. PVs and LA antrum will be exposed by blunt dissection with a Lumitip dissector (AtriCure, USA) and then ablated by bipolar radiofrequency clamp (AtriCure Isolator Synergy EML2/EMR2) with four or six applications in total of 8–12 procedures to create a transmural PV isolation (the current PV ablation protocol is completed with a single EML2/EMR2 clamp used bilaterally). The LA roof and floor lines (the box lesion of LA posterior wall) and the Coumadin ridge line will be made by bipolar pen (AtriCure Isolator Lenear Pen, MLP1). Right atrium ablation will be started with a linear lesion made on the crista terminalis extending to the superior and inferior vena cava using the bipolar radiofrequency pen. An additional linear lesion will be made by connecting the tip of the right atrial (RA) appendage to the superior-inferior vena cava line on the free wall of RA.

Catheter ablation

The CA will ensue right after the completion of the thoracoscopic ablation procedure. The voltage mapping process will be similar to the CA group but needed to create both right and left atrium voltage mapping images. Epicardial lesion sets will be scrutinised for evaluating the completeness of blockage. Gaps will be defined as low amplitude and fragmented or narrowly split double atrial potentials, or the discontinuation of low-voltage lines on voltage map, which will be subsequently ablated. The isthmus ablation and the intraoperative SR restoration protocol are same to the CA arm protocol.

Block test after completion of all lesions

The block test for all available lines will be mandated rechecked, as per guideline,6 by different available mapping or pacing manoeuvres (online supplemental file 1) after the completion of ablation. Lines which fail in blockage testifying should be re-ablated and re-checked for blockage. Lines which do not reach an ablation endpoint of blockage after balanced over-ablation risks should be noted on the case report form.

End points

The primary end point is the probability of freedom from documented any type of atrial tachycardia >30 s within 12 months (exclude the first 90 days after ablation) after a single procedure without needs for antiarrhythmic medications (type I or III AADs) (box 2).

The secondary end points included all-cause mortality, cardiovascular related mortality, cerebral stroke, peripheral vascular embolism, heart failure related rehospitalisation and all-cause rehospitalisation during the 3rd to the 12th month after operation and 1-year freedom from documented AF.

An AF effect on quality-of-life questionnaire will be added in combination with the modified European Heart Rhythm Association symptoms description score as the evaluation of symptom changes during follow-up. Furthermore, an additional exploratory analysis of the primary outcome will be performed to compare the difference of postoperative (3 months after intervention) LA functional parameters (LA ejection fraction and LA reservoir, active and conduit strains) between groups of AF recurrence or non-recurrence. And comparison of LA and RA dimensions will be made to evaluate the chamber reversal remodelling under different rhythm status.

Post-ablation management

Type I/III AADs, recommended as amiodaron or sotalol as per the guideline, will be administered for rhythm control in all participants 3 months after the intervention and discontinued thereafter. The type II AADs, most commonly seen as beta blockers, will be used anytime at the discretion of clinicians for a basic medical therapy of HCM.

Anticoagulants, either warfarin or new oral anticoagulants, will be used in the early postoperative period for 3 months and continued at the discretion of clinicians thereafter.

Data collection and follow-up

The Chinese Cardiac Surgery Registry (CCSR) website (http://ccsr.cvs-china.com), an established web-based data collection platform with high-level secure socket layer, will be used to collecting study data. The dataset for this study includes the following modules: subject screening, informed consent (online supplemental file 2) and randomisation, baseline in-hospital information, operation information, the 3-, 6- and 12-month follow-up data. The participant hospital would be authorised to access the data system during data submission and follow-up processes. Each module will be lock up after the recorded data are double checked, and any correction thereafter will be authorised by the responsible leader of the HCM-AF.

Follow-up

Candidates will be contacted weekly within the first month by the research team to evaluate any symptoms that may be early indications of possible complications. Patients will be encouraged to contact the study team whenever they have any concerns regarding their health and be brought back to the study centre for further assessment if deemed necessary.

The follow-up data collections will be completed by professionals blinded to the group allocation. 72-hour Holter monitoring (Goodfriend portable ECG, 8-lead, DL-194), and questionnaire will be collected at 3-, 6- and 12-month follow-up. The 3-day Holter monitoring devices will be mailed to participants for wearing, and will be sent back to the project team for data analysis afterwards. To minimise the rate of missed diagnoses of recurrent AF, we adhered to the guidelines to assess the primary endpoint: the probability of atrial tachycardia recurrence. This was evaluated using an 8-lead Holter monitor over three consecutive days at 3-, 6- and 12-month postintervention. If participants experienced any episodes of suspicious cardiac symptoms, the 3-day Holter monitoring will be repeated, and the session containing the symptomatic episode was selected for further analysis. The questionnaire includes questions on subject survival status, cardiac function classification, medication use and episodes of stroke, peripheral thromboembolic, hospitalisation for heart failure and bleeding.

All the follow-up information will be uploaded to the web-based CCSR data collection platform. In addition, we encourage participants to take ECG tests any time after intervention whenever they have cardiac symptoms. The 3-day Holter monitoring, 12-lead ECG and echocardiograms will be analysed by a core lab blinded to the group allocation.

Statistical plans

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using PASS V.11 on the basis of data derived from our estimation that percentage of freedom for AF/atrial flutter (AFL) (off AADs) at 12 months in the hybrid group of 75% versus 41% in the catheter group (rationale of the reference data for calculating sample size see in online supplemental file 1). Using these results to calculate the sample size, 31 per group will be required to detect a difference in the primary outcome with 80% power and a 5% significance level (two-sided). Taking account of a 5% dropout rate, the final sample size is 66.

Statistical analysis

The primary and secondary end points are determined on the basis of the intention-to-treat principle. In addition, a per-protocol analysis is also performed, which includes participants who complete their assigned treatments as scheduled. χ2 or Fisher’s exact test (if >25% of the cells have an expected frequency smaller than 5) will be used for comparisons between the trial arms. Continuous data will be analysed using either Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test and presented as mean±SD, mean (95% CI) or median (IQR), depending on distribution of obtained data. P<0.05 will be considered significant.

Clinical Event Committee

The investigator will be responsible for the accuracy and integrity of the dataset. Participants will be encouraged to strictly adhere to the follow-up requirements. The research team will streamline their follow-up process by shortening the time for ultrasound examinations and outpatient appointments, thereby reducing the risk of patient attrition/loss to follow-up. An independent Clinical Event Committee (CEC), which comprises specialised experts blinded to the randomisation schemes, will adjudicate all clinical outcomes in accordance with the CEC chart.

Ethics and dissemination

The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committees of Fuwai Hospital (approval number: 2022-1736) (online supplemental file 3). The results of this study will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals and conference presentations.

Study design considerations

Although appropriate managements have significantly improved long-term prognosis of HCM,17 AF remains one of the independent risk factors of HF, thromboembolism and mortality18 including those with successful myectomy.19 Rhythm controlling therapy has been associated with better symptom control and survival improvement5 and has been recommended for drug-refractory symptomatic AF patients as the first-line therapy.2 7 10 Nevertheless, SR is less effectively restored and maintained in HCM subjects with AF by either AADs administrations or CA, with a lower 1-year single ablation success rate of 39%.12 The potential mechanisms would be elevated LA pressure and progressive atrial fibrosis20 in HCM patients. Due to the lack of data supporting the optimal treatment approach for AF in patients with HCM, current guidelines can only recommend adopting the CA strategies used for the general AF population.2 6 The hybrid ablation technique, which has shown a superior SR maintenance rate in AF patients with high risk of recurrence after CA, is a reasonable alternative for AF population with HCM as per guidelines.6 7 The HCM-AF trial will be the first study to compare SR efficacy between CA and hybrid ablation.

Rationale of candidates’ enrolment

The non-paroxysmal AF population are clinically challenging subtypes with a higher risk of recurrence after CA, and therefore be reasonable to resort to an additional surgical intervention (IIa/B).2 Although the Cox-Maze IV procedure can be performed during myectomy for HOCM patients with AF, non-oHCM patients, lacking the indication for myectomy, have fewer aggressive rhythm control options, leaving them more exposed to the risks of AF. Accordingly, the study population comprises non-oHCM concomitant with non-paroxysmal AF. Furthermore, the LA diameter is a critical predictor of post-ablation AF recurrence and is thus stratified by 50 mm21 during randomisation, so that to enhance the comparability between the two groups and to ensure the robustness of the conclusions.

Ablation lesion sets

Previous research has revealed that in the HCM population, mechanisms of atrial tachycardia were attributable to isthmus related macro-reentry for 68.5% and localised reentry for 22.2%.22 Since AFL related high rates would be less tolerable for HCM patients due to the innate pathophysiology of impaired diastolic function, the HCM-AF trial has pre-specified a CA based isthmus lines in pursuit of maximising ablation success rate and reducing risk of tachycardia related haemodynamic collapse. Therefore, both tricuspid and mitral isthmus lines are mandated in this study, and the mitral isthmus lines are made in a hierarchical approach from lateral to septal isthmus ablation. The reason for these two mitral isthmus lines is that they can be supplemented to reach block test in multiple ways, including multiple paralleled lines plus ablation within CS (lateral)23 and ablating at the right side of atrial septum (septal), so that to reach a high instant isthmus block rate. In addition, this article also showed a promising long-term freedom from AF after multiple ablations under the concept of ‘stepwise ablation’, which indicated that additional lines with good quality might help achieve better SR maintenance.22

The high ablation success rate of the Cox-Maze IV procedure that has extended to HOCM patients has highlighted the fact that the transmurally as well as the completeness of the maze lesions would finally contribute to a long-standing SR maintenance. Thus, the hybrid arm will try to mirror a full Maze lesion, in hope to diminish all potential macro-reentries.24 Since the exponential rise in the incidence of scar-related re-entrants parallel the increasing use of more extensive substrate ablation, different mapping and pacing manoeuvres are mandated to ensure the completeness of additional lines as per guideline.6 In addition, the two procedures of hybrid arm are thus integrated simultaneously instead of staged way, so that to lower the risk of postoperative flutter due to the incomplete epicardial ablation.

Mandated isthmus-addressed linear lesions in both study arms will provide systematic evidence, with sufficient block test details, on the optimal lesion set beyond PVI in non-paroxysmal AF ablation. In addition, merits from the detailed protocol of mapping and block testing, data regarding LA fibrosis burden on intraprocedural voltage mapping will be available and may be helpful to further analyse the intraoperative block rate and reasons of AF recurrence. Although the VENUS trial25 has reported a 74% instant block rate with vein of Marshall ethanol infusion (VoM), it is used by only a minority of electrophysiologists—approximately 5.3%, according to the guideline26—for first-time ablation of persistent AF. To ensure uniformity in energy sources across the treatment arms and to maximise the generalisability of the study’s conclusions, VoM ethanol infusion will not be recommended in this study protocol. Moreover, given the reported 100% instant block rate for the lateral mitral isthmus line,27 PFA will be allowed whenever possible to enhance ablation efficiency, as approved by the CEC and Ethical Committees.

This study has some limitations. In terms of patient enrolment, given that this is the first randomised controlled trial targeting the HCM population with a hybrid ablation group, we set relatively strict inclusion criteria, requiring that only patients with non-oHCM and persistent AF be considered for enrolment. As a result, it is challenging to complete patient recruitment within a short period. Additionally, due to the significant differences between the two treatment approaches, it is not feasible to blind operators and participants to the randomisation schemes.

In conclusion, the HCM-AF trial is first designed to examine the efficacy of hybrid ablation versus CA procedure with isthmus addressed lesion sets in patients with HCM and non-paroxysmal AF. The findings from this trial may help determine an optimal ablation strategy to further improve the decision-making process for non-oHCM with non-paroxysmal AF candidates for rhythm controlling strategy.
