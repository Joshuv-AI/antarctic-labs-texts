In silico rational investigation of C-terminal arginine-containing white Lupin (Lupinus albus) protein-derived peptides as arginase inhibitors
Authors: Stanley Chukwuejim, Toluwase H Fatoki, Rotimi E Aluko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArginase is a binuclear manganese metalloenzyme that modulates L-arginine availability and downstream metabolic pathways. It was hypothesized that C-terminal arginine-containing peptides generated through simulated gastrointestinal digestion of white lupin (Lupinus albus) proteins would exhibit arginase inhibitory activity. To test this hypothesis, integrated computational and experimental approaches were employed to identify and characterize arginase-inhibitory peptides from white lupin proteins. Based on the REGNH peptide scaffold, 29 derivative sequences were designed and used to screen the lupin genome, followed by in silico proteolysis, yielding 24 C-terminal arginine-containing peptides. None of the peptides showed evidence of allergenicity. Target prediction identified DR, CR, ER, and QR as top candidates, while molecular docking and 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations revealed isoform-specific binding preferences and stability patterns. Five peptides (CQR, DR, DQR, QER, REGNH) were synthesized and evaluated against arginase I using an in vitro colorimetric assay. DQR showed the highest inhibitory activity among the tested peptides, achieving approximately 17% inhibition at 100 μg/mL. Kinetic analysis indicated an apparent mixed-type inhibition pattern, with increased Km (31.2 to 35.4 mM) and decreased Vmax (0.72 to 0.70 ΔAbs/min). Despite a top computational ranking, DR showed negligible in vitro activity (<2%), highlighting the limitations of binding-affinity predictions for forecasting functional enzyme inhibition. These findings demonstrate the potential of integrated computational-experimental approaches for identifying arginase-modulatory peptides encrypted within food proteins.Keywords: Bioactive peptides, Virtual screening, Enzyme inhibition kinetics, Structure-activity relationship, Peptide design

Introduction

Food-derived bioactive peptides (BAPs), typically 2–20 amino acids in length, are encrypted within parent food proteins and released upon enzymatic hydrolysis or gastrointestinal digestion (Udenigwe & Aluko, 2012). In silico approaches have become increasingly valuable for accelerating the discovery of food-derived BAPs. For example, virtual proteolysis using the BIOPEP-UWM database combined with molecular docking has been successfully employed to identify ACE-inhibitory peptides from sheep milk proteins (Iram et al., 2022) and DPP-IV inhibitory peptides from discarded eggshell membrane (Zhu et al., 2024). Similarly, integrated computational pipelines involving in silico hydrolysis, ADME screening, and molecular docking have identified antioxidant peptides from lupin hydrolysates (Guo et al., 2018), ACE-inhibitory peptides from Bambara groundnut (Saetang et al., 2025), and renin-inhibitory peptides from Tartary buckwheat albumin hydrolysates (Li et al., 2024). These studies demonstrate that in silico-guided approaches provide an efficient framework for identifying bioactive peptide candidates from food proteins prior to experimental validation.

Arginase is a binuclear manganese metalloenzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of L-arginine to L-ornithine and urea, playing a central role in nitrogen metabolism and the regulation of L-arginine availability for downstream metabolic pathways including nitric oxide synthesis (Cama et al., 2003; Canè et al., 2025). While synthetic arginase inhibitors have been extensively studied (Pudlo et al., 2017), food-derived peptides with arginase-modulatory activity have not yet been reported. To date, only one peptide-based arginase inhibitor (REGNH) has been described in the literature (Gao et al., 2021). Notably, peptides containing C-terminal arginine residues have shown enhanced bioactivity due to their positive charge and capacity for electrostatic interactions with biological targets (Gautam et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2020) and have demonstrated inhibitory activities against acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase (Asen et al., 2023), supporting the functional significance of this structural feature in food-derived peptides.

White lupin (Lupinus albus), a legume of the Fabaceae family containing 35–40% protein by dry weight, is an established source of bioactive peptides (Chukwuejim et al., 2024). Lupin protein hydrolysates exhibit antioxidant and ACE-inhibitory activities (Guo et al., 2018), and peptides from lupin have demonstrated DPP-IV inhibitory properties (Cruz-Chamorro et al., 2023; Santos-Sánchez et al., 2022). The high arginine content of lupin proteins, combined with their established track record as sources of enzyme-inhibitory peptides, makes white lupin a rational candidate for exploring arginase-modulatory peptides. However, the potential of lupin-derived peptides to modulate arginase activity has not been investigated, and the structural determinants governing peptide-arginase interactions in food-derived peptides remain unknown.

The present study addresses this gap by employing an integrated in silico and in vitro approach to identify and characterize arginase-inhibitory peptides from white lupin proteins. It was hypothesized that simulated gastrointestinal digestion of white lupin proteins would release C-terminal arginine-containing peptides with arginase inhibitory activity. To test this, peptide motifs based on the REGNH sequence (Gao et al., 2021) — the only reported peptide-based arginase inhibitor — were used to guide proteome screening, followed by in silico enzymatic hydrolysis, ADME and allergenicity prediction, target prediction, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations. Selected candidate peptides were then synthesized and experimentally evaluated for arginase I inhibitory activity and kinetic characterization. This approach aims to elucidate the structure-activity relationships governing peptide-arginase interactions in food protein-derived peptides.

Materials and methods

Arginase compound assembling

The small organic compounds that bind to arginase I and II were obtained from the DrugBank database (www.drugbank.ca; accessed: 20-11-2024). A synthetic peptide (sequence: REGNH) that binds to arginase I and II was also obtained from the literature (Gao et al., 2021).

Design of synthetic peptide derivatives and screening of white lupin genome proteins

The white lupin genome proteins file was obtained in FASTA format from https://www.whitelupin.fr/genome_files/prot.fasta (accessed: 05-07-2024). A synthetic peptide (sequence: REGNH) was used for the rational design of 29 sequence derivatives (See Supplementary File 1, Table S1), which were then used to screen the white lupin genome protein file. The white lupin proteins that contain any of these 29 derivatives were selected (See Supplementary File 2) and used for further analyses.

In silico protein hydrolysis

The selected white lupin protein sequences obtained in step 2.2 were subjected to simulated enzymatic hydrolysis using a combination of digestive enzymes, namely pepsin (EC 3.4.23.1), chymotrypsin (EC 3.4.21.1), and trypsin (EC 3.4.21.4) concurrently, utilizing the BIOPEP-UWM webserver (https://biochemia.uwm.edu.pl/en/biopep-uwm-2/; accessed: 25-11-2024) (See Supplementary File 3). The hydrolysate peptides thus obtained were subsequently subjected to screening with pancreatic elastase (EC 3.4.21.36) digestion (Minkiewicz et al., 2019). The cleavage sites of the enzymes are provided in supplementary file 1 (Table S2). In silico digestion assumed physiological pH (pH 7.4), the effect of varied pH and time were not considered. Among the resulting hydrolysate peptide sequences, those with C-terminal arginine residues were chosen for further analysis. The filtering criteria was based on the susceptibility of the sequence to the proteases cleavage, and prior understanding from the organic compounds that are arginase inhibitors (method 2.1) should contain arginine-like molecule. These selected sequences were then converted to the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry Specification (SMILES) format using the BIOPEP-UWM webserver, as previously described (Fatoki et al., 2023).

In silico pharmacokinetic and allergenicity prediction

The assessment of in silico pharmacokinetic or ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) screening for the chosen hydrolysate peptides containing C-terminal arginine, as well as for standard compounds, was conducted on the SwissADME webserver, accessible at www.swissadme.ch, accessed: 27-11-2024 (Daina et al., 2017). This analysis was carried out using the SMILES format of the peptides. Additionally, the evaluation of allergenicity was performed using the AllerCatPro 2.0 webserver, accessible at http://allercatpro.bii.a-star.edu.sg, accessed: 27-11-2024 (Nguyen et al., 2022).

In silico arginase target prediction

The SMILES representation of each of the chosen hydrolysate peptides containing C-terminal arginine, obtained through the in silico enzymatic hydrolysis, as well as the SMILES of the standard compounds, were employed for target prediction analysis on the SEA Search webserver, accessible at https://sea.bkslab.org/, accessed: 28-11-2024 (Keiser et al., 2007). During this analysis, targets that included human arginase I and II were identified.

Molecular docking studies

Peptide-protein molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina v1.2.3, as detailed in the work by Trott and Olson (2010) and Eberhardt et al. (2021). Blind docking approach were used and executed in accordance with previous studies (Fatoki et al., 2023; Obeme-Nmom et al., 2023), in order to capture the types of inhibition exhibited by these peptides on binding to the arginase sites (active site or allosteric site). The small organic compounds (ligands) obtained in step 2.1 as well as REGNH peptide, were used to validate and compare our docking. Human arginase I and II were obtained from the protein data bank (PDB ID: 2AEB and 4IXU). Docking grid parameter used for human arginase I (Center: 69.48, 24.449, 34.841. Size: 126, 126, 126. Spacing: 0.375) and human arginase II (Center: 37.769, 87.718, 57.837. Size: 126, 126, 126. Spacing: 0.375) respectively ensure full coverage of the structure. Before initiating docking, the peptides, ligands and human arginases were prepared in pdbqt format utilizing AutoDock Tools (ADT) v1.5.6, developed by Morris et al. (2009). Subsequently, the binding affinity was determined, and the close interactions between the ligands and the target proteins were scrutinized and visualized using ezLigPlot on the ezCADD webserver available at http://dxulab.org/software accessed: 30-11-2024 (Tao et al., 2019).

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of human arginase I and II in complex with four of our peptides (CQR, DR, DQR and QER), one organic compound (DB03144) and a synthetic peptide REGNH, were investigated. Desmond module in Schrödinger molecular modeling suite v2020–3 was used to implement the complex simulation for 100 ns (Bowers et al., 2006; Pirolli et al., 2023). The protein-peptide complexes at the initial stage for these simulations were acquired from the docking studies. The preparation of the protein-peptide complexes involved maestro's protein preparation wizard, which encompassed optimization and minimization steps of the complexes. All systems were prepared utilizing the System Builder tool. For the solvent model, an orthorhombic box was chosen, employing the TIP3P (Transferable Intermolecular Interaction Potential 3 Points) model. During the simulations, the OPLS-2005 force field was employed (Shivakumar et al., 2010). To achieve neutral systems, counter ions (0.15 M NaCl) were introduced to replicate physiological conditions, as described by Fatoki (2023a). The NPT ensemble, which conserves moles (N), pressure (P), and temperature (T), was chosen for the simulations, maintaining a temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 atm. Prior to the commencement of the simulations, the models were allowed to relax. Throughout the simulations, trajectory data were saved every 100 ps, and post-simulation analysis of these trajectories was conducted, involving the determination of various parameters, including root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), protein-ligand interaction profile, radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent accessibility surface area (SASA). Furthermore, prime molecular mechanics/generalized Born surface area (MMGBSA) calculations were performed, enabling the assessment of binding free energy. The binding free energy calculation procedure was as follows:

•MMGBSA ΔGbind = ΔGcomplex – ΔGprotein - ΔGligand

MMGBSA ΔGbind = ΔGcomplex – ΔGprotein - ΔGligand

•MMGBSA ΔGbind = ΔGCoulomb + ΔGCovalent + ΔGHbond + ΔGLipo + ΔGPacking + ΔGSolvGB + ΔGvdW

MMGBSA ΔGbind = ΔGCoulomb + ΔGCovalent + ΔGHbond + ΔGLipo + ΔGPacking + ΔGSolvGB + ΔGvdW

where ΔGbind is the total Prime energy, “Hbond” refers to the energy associated with hydrogen bonding, “Lipo” signifies lipophilic energy, “Packing” accounts for pi-pi packing correction, “SolvGB” represents the generalized Born electrostatic solvation energy, and “vdW” corresponds to the Van der Waals energy. These terms and their meanings are in accordance with previous works (Fatoki, 2023b; Zhang et al., 2017).

Arginase inhibition studies

Selected peptides (CQR, DR, DQR, QER, and REGNH) were synthesized (≥95% purity) by Genscript Corp (Piscataway, NJ, USA) and subjected to an in vitro arginase inhibition assay according to the method described by Gao et al. (2021). The synthetic peptide REGNH, a previously validated arginase inhibitor (Gao et al., 2021), was included as a positive control to verify assay performance and to provide a benchmark for evaluating the inhibitory activities of the lupin-derived peptides. Enzymatic activity was quantified by measuring urea formation. Reactions were set up in 96-well microplates using a standard assay buffer composed of 100 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.4) and 130 mM NaCl. Each well contained a total volume of 250 μL, with arginase I from bovine liver (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) adjusted to a final concentration of 7 units/mL in assay buffer. Wells without enzyme served as negative controls. Peptide inhibitors (50 μL) were added according to the experimental design at concentrations ranging from 10 μg/mL to 100 μg/mL. Reaction mixtures were pre-equilibrated for 30 min at 37 °C with gentle shaking (100 rpm), after which substrate solution (125 μL) consisting of assay buffer with 100 mM L-arginine, 500 mM glycine, and 1.25 mM MnCl2 was added. Incubations were performed for 1 h at the same temperature and shaking conditions and terminated by adding a stop reagent. The stop solution was prepared freshly by combining 2 parts of antipyrine in 50% (v/v) sulfuric acid (5 g/L) with 1 part of an oxime solution (0.8 g of 2,3-butanedione monoxime in 5% acetic acid). Following quenching, plates were kept protected from light at room temperature for at least 18 h to allow full color development, then heated to 45 °C for 4 h to enhance chromophore formation. The resulting brown coloration, proportional to urea concentration, was recorded by measuring absorbance at 466 nm using a Synergy H1 Hybrid Reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Arginase I was selected for in vitro validation because recombinant human arginase II was not commercially available at the time of this study. To characterize enzyme kinetics, reactions were additionally performed across a range of L-arginine concentrations (12.5–800 mM). The initial velocities were fitted to the Michaelis–Menten model, and a double-reciprocal plot was used to determine Km, Vmax, and inhibition constant (Ki). The graphs were generated and fitted using GraphPad Prism version 9 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS statistical software version 28 (Chicago, IL, USA). All measurements were carried out in triplicate and are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Data were subjected to a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test to identify statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) among the mean values within each group.

The result of the ADME and allergenicity as shown in Table 1, indicate that all the 24 selected C-terminal arginine-containing lupin hydrolysate peptides possess the following features: molecular weight (MW) in the range of 271 to 789 g/mol, lipophilicity (Log P) range of −1.28 to −4.21, high solubility in water (Log S), low gastrointestinal absorption (GIA), no permeation across blood-brain barrier (BBB), no inhibition of cytochrome P450s (CYP450s), high skin permeation (log Kp) and no evidence of allergenicity. Moreover, the ADME results also showed that CR, QPR, PER, PPR, QEPR, PQCR, PPPR, and CPPPR peptides are not substrates of p-glycoprotein. The ADME profiles of the standard compound and synthetic peptide are also similar, except that some exhibit high GIA values.

The result of target prediction of all the 24 selected C-terminal arginine-containing hydrolysate peptides for possible activity showed that all the peptides containing proline (PR, QPR, PPR, QEPR, PQCR, PPPR, CPPPR, and PPPPPER) have no arginase I and II inhibitory activities (Table 2). Based on the p-value of the prediction, the order of arginase inhibitory activity was DR > CR > ER > QR > DDR > DER = CDR > QQQR > QQR and others. Moreover, the result showed that DB04197 and the synthetic peptide (REGNH) have no arginase inhibitory activity.

Discussion

Peptide assembly

The computational screening strategy based on REGNH derivatives and C-terminal arginine motifs identified peptides with potential arginase-modulatory activity. Structural inspection of known arginase inhibitors from DrugBank revealed the importance of arginine-like structures with terminal carboxylate or thiol groups (Cama et al., 2003; Mortier et al., 2017). This guided the design of 29 REGNH derivative sequences, which were used to screen the white lupin genome and were subsequently subjected to simulated gastrointestinal digestion with chymotrypsin, trypsin, pepsin, and elastase. The in-silico hydrolysis yielded 24 unique C-terminal arginine peptides characterized by enrichment of acidic residues (aspartate and glutamate), polar uncharged residues (glutamine), and cysteine at N-terminal or internal positions. Notably absent were hydrophobic or aromatic residues, suggesting that the lupin proteome's amino acid distribution and the chosen digestion enzymes favor the generation of charged, hydrophilic peptides. The presence of C-terminal arginine is functionally significant, as previous studies have demonstrated that peptides with this motif exhibit diverse bioactivities including inhibition of cholinesterases (Asen et al., 2023), furin (Fatoki et al., 2022), and ACE (Iroyukifujita et al., 2000; Zhao et al., 2020).

Physicochemical and allergenicity profiling

ADME screening indicated that all 24C-terminal arginine peptides are hydrophilic, highly water-soluble, and exhibit low predicted gastrointestinal absorption (Table 1). No peptides showed evidence of allergenicity or cytochrome P450 inhibition. All proline-containing peptides showed no predicted arginase inhibitory activity despite some exhibiting more favorable P-glycoprotein profiles, suggesting that proline's cyclic structure may prevent productive arginase binding. It should be noted that SwissADME was developed for small organic molecules, and its reliability for short, charged peptides remains uncertain.

Target prediction

Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) target prediction, which identifies protein targets based on chemical similarity to known ligands (Keiser et al., 2007), was applied as an exploratory screening step to prioritize candidate peptides for downstream computational analysis. Of the 24C-terminal arginine peptides, 15 were predicted to have arginase I and II inhibitory activity, with a predicted activity hierarchy of DR > CR > ER > QR > DDR > DER = CDR > QQQR > QQR and others (Table 2). All eight proline-containing peptides showed no predicted arginase inhibitory activity. However, the predictive reliability of SEA for short peptide ligands was limited. Notably, the synthetic peptide REGNH, a validated arginase inhibitor (Gao et al., 2021), was not predicted to have arginase inhibitory activity, indicating that binding modes or allosteric mechanisms not represented in the SEA training dataset are overlooked by this ligand-similarity approach. Furthermore, subsequent experimental validation revealed a poor correlation between SEA rankings and functional inhibition: DR, the top-ranked peptide, showed negligible activity (<2%), while QER, ranked 15th, achieved the highest inhibition (∼18%). These discrepancies confirm that SEA predictions served as one of several complementary filters for candidate prioritization rather than as reliable predictors of functional enzyme inhibition.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking of the 16 predicted-active peptides against arginase I (PDB: 2AEB) and arginase II (PDB: 4IXU) yielded binding affinities of ≤ −5.0 kcal/mol for most peptides (Table 2). The overall binding affinity ranking was DR > QER > DQR > CQR > EER > PER > DER > DDR, among others. This ranking differed from the SEA target prediction hierarchy, with QER (ranked 15th in target prediction) achieving the second-best docking score. Interaction analysis identified the engagement of active-site residues involved in substrate recognition and catalysis, including His101, His126, Asn130, Asp181, Asp232, Asp253, and Glu296 (Mortier et al., 2017). The reference compound DB03144 exhibited binding affinity comparable to that of the hydrolysate peptides, while the boronic acid-containing inhibitor DB03904 could not be evaluated due to limitations in parameterizing boron atoms within the force field. Importantly, AutoDock Vina's scoring function does not explicitly model coordination interactions with the binuclear manganese cluster, which is critical for arginase inhibition (Kanyo et al., 1996). The reported binding affinities therefore reflect general protein-ligand interactions rather than specific metal-coordination geometry, and this limitation likely contributes to the discrepancies observed between predicted binding affinities and experimental inhibitory activities. Indeed, subsequent experimental validation confirmed that docking rankings did not reliably predict functional inhibition: DR, the top-ranked peptide, showed negligible activity (<2%), while lower-ranked peptides (DQR, QER) achieved significant inhibition. These findings underscore that docking-derived binding affinities should be interpreted cautiously and used as a prioritization tool rather than as quantitative predictors of inhibitory potency.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations over 100 ns were performed for top-ranked peptides (CQR, DR, DQR, QER) and controls (DB03144, REGNH) in complex with both arginase isoforms to assess binding stability (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). RMSD analysis revealed isoform-specific stability patterns. CQR showed stable binding to both isoforms (RMSD <3 Å), while DR exhibited contrasting behavior: moderate fluctuations with arginase I but consistent stability with arginase II (ligand RMSD <0.5 Å), suggesting possible preferential binding to arginase II. DQR maintained moderate stability with arginase I (RMSD 1.5–2.5 Å) but showed ligand dissociation from arginase II after ∼60 ns (RMSD ∼10 Å). QER maintained stable interactions with both isoforms throughout the simulation (RMSD <3 Å). DB03144 was stable with arginase I but unstable with arginase II, whereas REGNH maintained interactions with both isoforms, consistent with its reported dual-isoform activity (Gao et al., 2021). These computationally inferred isoform preferences remain to be experimentally verified, as only arginase I was available for in vitro testing.

RMSF analysis (Fig. 7, Fig. 8) indicated that arginase I exhibited greater ligand-dependent flexibility, with different peptides inducing distinct fluctuation patterns in the substrate-binding pocket and active-site loop regions. In contrast, arginase II exhibited more uniform responses across ligands, suggesting a more structurally constrained active-site architecture (Failla et al., 2024). Protein-ligand contact analysis (Fig. 9, Fig. 10) showed that hydrogen bonding was the dominant interaction type across most complexes, consistent with the charged, hydrophilic nature of C-terminal arginine peptides. Ionic interactions were more prominent in arginase II complexes. The active-site residues most frequently engaged were His101, His126, Asn130, Asp181, Asp232, and Glu277, which are known to participate in substrate recognition and catalysis (Ash, 2004). Radius of gyration (Rg ≤ 0.6 Å) and SASA (∼1500 Å2) remained stable across all systems (Figs. S1–S4). It should be noted that the MD simulations were performed as single 100 ns trajectories without replicates, and the OPLS-2005 force field used was not specifically parameterized for short charged peptides or for explicit modeling of binuclear manganese coordination. The RMSD/RMSF observations, therefore, represent computational indicators of relative binding stability rather than evidence of a biological mechanism.

Binding energy of interaction of arginase I and arginase II

MMGBSA binding free energies were calculated at 0 ns and 100 ns to evaluate relative changes in binding stability during simulation (Table 3). For arginase I, DR showed increased binding energy (−17.7 to −35.0 kcal/mol), while DQR (−56.8 to −51.8 kcal/mol) and QER (−46.6 to −45.3 kcal/mol) remained relatively stable. For arginase II, QER showed substantial stabilization (−33.2 to −61.2 kcal/mol), while DQR showed destabilization (−65.3 to −47.9 kcal/mol), correlating with the ligand dissociation observed in RMSD analysis. Electrostatic interactions were the major favorable contributors, partially offset by unfavorable polar solvation energies, consistent with highly charged peptide-protein systems. Notably, DR's favorable arginase I binding energy did not translate to experimental activity (<2% inhibition), illustrating the disconnect between computed binding energies and functional inhibition. MMGBSA values are semi-quantitative estimates that do not account for entropic contributions and are best interpreted as relative comparisons between systems rather than absolute measures of binding affinity (Claveria-Gimeno et al., 2017).

Peptide arginase inhibitory activities

Five synthesized peptides (CQR, DR, DQR, QER, REGNH) were evaluated against arginase I, yielding an experimental activity hierarchy of QER (18% inhibition at 100 μg/mL) ≈ REGNH (17%) ≈ DQR (16%) > > DR (<2%) ≈ CQR (0%), with 3 of 5 tested peptides showing measurable inhibitory activity. However, given that only 5 of 24 candidate peptides were experimentally evaluated, the generalization of this observation to the broader candidate pool remains uncertain. The failure of DR, the top-ranked peptide in both target prediction and docking, and CQR's complete inactivity highlight the limitations of current in silico methods for predicting functional enzyme inhibition. Despite possessing theoretically optimal features (aspartate carboxylate for manganese coordination, C-terminal arginine for electrostatic interactions, minimal steric bulk, highest predicted binding affinity), DR exhibited negligible activity (<2% inhibition even at 100 μg/mL). This disconnect between binding prediction, and functional activity has been observed in other metalloenzyme systems (Saini et al., 2021). DR's compact dipeptide structure may lack sufficient contact points to achieve the residence time required for effective inhibition, and MD simulations suggest a possible preferential stability toward arginase II over arginase I — though this remains experimentally unverified. CQR's similar computational preference for arginase II, coupled with its inactivity toward arginase I, supports this hypothesis.

QER's experimental success despite its lower computational ranking (15th in target prediction, 2nd in docking) is notable and suggests that the extended tripeptide structure provides advantages not captured by scoring functions. The glutamine-glutamate-arginine sequence may enable simultaneous engagement of multiple active-site residues, thereby prolonging residence time relative to the shorter DR dipeptide. The glutamate residue could form critical hydrogen bonds with active site His126 or Asn130, residues known to participate in substrate recognition and orientation (Kuramitsu et al., 2023; Mortier et al., 2017). This multi-point attachment mechanism would explain why QER achieves functional inhibition despite potentially lower intrinsic binding affinity than DR at any single site. The results suggest that it is the synergy of multiple weak-to-moderate interactions, rather than a single strong interaction, that produces effective catalytic disruption by QER (Tommasi et al., 2018). Comparison of DR (inactive), DQR (moderate activity, Ki = 150 μM), and QER (highest activity, Ki = 330 μM) indicates that effective arginase inhibition requires not merely strong binding but strategic positioning of functional groups to disrupt catalysis. The observation that DQR has a lower Ki yet lower percent inhibition than QER further illustrates the complex relationship between binding affinity and functional inhibition.

Kinetic mechanisms reveal complex peptide-arginase interactions

Enzyme kinetic analysis of DQR, QER, and REGNH revealed a consistent pattern of increased apparent Km and decreased Vmax in the presence of peptide inhibitors (Table 4, Fig. 11B–D), consistent with an apparent mixed-type inhibition pattern in which the peptides may interact with both the free enzyme and the enzyme-substrate complex. Kinetic parameters were derived from direct nonlinear regression fitting to the Michaelis-Menten model, with Lineweaver-Burk plots used to visualize the inhibition pattern. DQR exhibited the lowest Ki (150 μM), indicating the highest binding affinity among the three peptides tested. QER displayed a higher Ki (330 μM) despite achieving the highest percent inhibition (∼18%), further illustrating the complex relationship between binding affinity and functional inhibition. Although QER differs from DQR by only a single residue substitution (Asp→Glu at position 2), this difference in the positioning of the acidic side chain appears to influence inhibitory efficacy, consistent with studies demonstrating that minor modifications in inhibitor geometry can substantially affect potency (Di Costanzo et al., 2005).

The apparent mixed-type inhibition observed for all three peptides contrasts with the non-competitive allosteric mechanism previously reported for REGNH (Gao et al., 2021). This discrepancy may result from differences in the enzyme source (recombinant human arginase versus bovine liver arginase). REGNH displayed kinetic parameters (Ki ∼320 μM) and functional inhibition (17%) comparable to those of the lupin-derived peptides, confirming that the computational pipeline successfully identified peptides with activity comparable to the only known peptide-based arginase inhibitor. The modest inhibitory activities observed should be interpreted in the context of the assay conditions. The 100 mM L-arginine substrate concentration far exceeds physiological levels (100–200 μM), which substantially reduces the apparent efficacy of competitive and mixed-type inhibitors. Additionally, the maximum inhibition did not reach 50%, precluding IC₅₀ determination; Ki values therefore provide a more informative measure of inhibitory potency for these peptides.

Methodological limitations and future directions

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. The in silico enzymatic hydrolysis using BIOPEP-UWM assumes ideal cleavage at all predicted sites without accounting for protein tertiary structure, kinetic competition between proteases, or sequential gastric-intestinal pH transitions. Consequently, the predicted peptide profiles may differ from those generated under physiological digestion conditions (Minkiewicz et al., 2019). ADME predictions from SwissADME were developed for small organic molecules, and their reliability for short, charged peptides remains uncertain. The Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) failed to predict activity for the known inhibitor REGNH (Gao et al., 2021), and poor correlation was observed between computational rankings and experimental outcomes, confirming the limited applicability of these tools to peptide-based enzyme inhibitors. Molecular docking employed rigid receptor structures (PDB: 2AEB and 4IXU) that do not capture arginase active site plasticity (Mortier et al., 2017), and AutoDock Vina's scoring function does not explicitly model coordination interactions with the binuclear manganese cluster. Conventional docking validation (crystal ligand redocking, enrichment tests) was not performed due to the absence of curated peptide-specific decoy datasets; validation relied on a comparative strategy using known inhibitors under identical conditions. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed as single 100 ns trajectories without replicates, using the OPLS-2005 force field, which was not specifically parameterized for short charged peptides. The computational observations, therefore, represent preliminary indicators of relative binding stability rather than definitive mechanistic conclusions.

Experimental validation was restricted to bovine liver arginase I, which may differ from human recombinant arginase in kinetic behavior and inhibitor sensitivity. Human recombinant arginase II was not commercially available, leaving the computationally predicted isoform-specific interactions experimentally unverified. Only 5 of the 24 candidate peptides were synthesized and tested due to cost constraints, and the observed inhibitory activity was modest (∼17% maximum inhibition at 100 μg/mL), insufficient for IC₅₀ determination. No experimental assessment of peptide stability, gastrointestinal survival, membrane permeability, or bioavailability was conducted. The translation of in vitro enzyme inhibition at supraphysiological conditions to any physiological relevance remains entirely undemonstrated and warrants investigation using Caco-2 transport assays, simulated gastrointestinal digestion stability studies, and ultimately in vivo models. Finally, the REGNH scaffold-based design strategy inherently constrained the chemical diversity that could be explored. Alternative approaches, such as high-throughput hydrolysate screening or machine learning-guided peptide design, may identify more potent and structurally diverse arginase-modulatory peptides.

Conclusions

This study employed an integrated computational-experimental approach to identify and characterize arginase-modulatory peptides from white lupin (L. albus) proteins. Through rational design based on the REGNH scaffold, in silico proteolysis, target prediction, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations, 24C-terminal arginine-containing candidate peptides were identified. Of the five peptides synthesized and evaluated against arginase I, three (DQR, QER, REGNH) showed modest but reproducible inhibitory activity (∼16–18% at 100 μg/mL), with DQR exhibiting the lowest Ki (150 μM) and an apparent mixed-type inhibition pattern. Notable discrepancies between computational predictions and experimental outcomes, particularly DR's inactivity despite top computational ranking, demonstrate that binding affinity alone does not reliably predict functional enzyme inhibition. The inhibitory activities observed were modest under the assay conditions used, and the translation of these findings to physiologically relevant contexts remain to be established through assessments of peptide stability, bioavailability studies, and testing with human recombinant arginase isoforms. These findings represent a preliminary characterization of the arginase-modulatory potential of white lupin protein-derived peptides and provide structure-activity insights that may inform future efforts to identify more potent enzyme-modulatory peptides from food protein sources.
