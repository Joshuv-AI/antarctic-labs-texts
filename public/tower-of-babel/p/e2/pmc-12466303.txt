Effects of different drying methods on the quality of Lycium barbarum: Focus on the carotenoids and polysaccharides
Authors: Yinglin Jian, Ningli Wang, Zhiming Han, Yunchun Li, Ting Huo, Yuan Gong, Duolong Di, Ling Ma, Dong Pei, Jianfei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrying plays a significant role in determining the quality and bioactivity of wolfberries. This study compared four drying methods—natural drying (ND), hot air drying (HAD), vacuum freeze-drying (FD), and vacuum microwave combined with vacuum pulsation drying (VMD)—focusing on their effects on the appearance and bioactive content of wolfberries, particularly carotenoids and polysaccharides. Wolfberries dried using FD and VMD appeared plumper, smoother, and more brightly colored. Notably, VMD-treated samples (LB-V) retained the highest total carotenoid content (6.42 mg/g), including zeaxanthin (3.41 μg/g), β-carotene (52.05 μg/g), and zeaxanthin dipalmitate (139.72 μg/g). Polysaccharides (LBPs-V) extracted from LB-V displayed a lower molecular weight (3.51 × 104 Da), a higher arabinose content (51.66 %), and reduced aggregation. Moreover, LBPs-V significantly decreased ALT and AST levels (by up to 67.53 % and 60.84 %), alleviated oxidative damage by reducing ROS, MDA, and NO levels (up to 84.28 %), and increased GSH (up to 77.51 %), thereby providing protective effects against ethanol- and APAP-induced liver injury. These findings highlight the significant role of drying techniques in preserving the bioactivity of wolfberries and provide valuable insights for optimizing their processing in functional food applications.Keywords: Wolfberry, Drying technology, Zeaxanthin dipalmitate, Carbohydrates

Introduction

Wolfberry (Lycium barbarum L.) is one of the earliest traditional Chinese medicines (TCM) classified as both a food and a medicine herb. It is known for its therapeutic effects, such as nourishing the kidneys, moisturizing the lungs, clearing the liver, and enhancing vision. Modern studies have shown that wolfberries contain a variety of phytochemicals, such as carotenoids, polyphenols, polysaccharides, flavonoids, spermidine, saponins, and polypeptides, which exhibit diverse bioactivities, including immune modulation, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hypoglycemic, and neuroprotective effects (Zhou et al., 2017). Carotenoids are the primary active compounds in wolfberries, contributing to their eye-protective benefits and distinctive red color. They constitute approximately 0.03 % to 0.5 % of the total weight of dried wolfberries, with zeaxanthin and its palmitate making up a significant portion. Wolfberries are also recognized as the main natural source of zeaxanthin (Zhou et al., 2022a). In addition to carotenoids, Lycium barbarum polysaccharides (LBPs) are key bioactive components of wolfberries, known for their therapeutic properties. They play a crucial role in supporting liver health, enhancing immune function, and providing neuroprotection (Wu et al., 2018).

Most TCM undergo preliminary processing after harvest, with drying being a widely used technique. The choice of an appropriate drying method is critical in determining the quality, efficacy, and economic value of TCM. Different drying techniques have varying impacts on the pharmacological properties and therapeutic efficacy of wolfberries, making the choice of an appropriate method highly important. Polysaccharides and carotenoids often undergo thermal denaturation and decomposition during drying, and the drying method may alter their chemical structure and conformation, affecting their bioactivity (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Zhou et al. (2022b) reported that Cordyceps polysaccharides treated with vacuum freeze-drying exhibited lower molecular weight and more significant antioxidant activity. Similarly, Nzimande et al. (2024) reported that the ultrasonic freeze-drying method preserved higher levels of total carotenoids, lutein, β-carotene, lycopene, and antioxidant activity compared to all other experimental methods.

Drying is also a critical step in wolfberry processing, with hot air drying (HAD) being the most widely used method. Some growers still use traditional natural drying (ND) techniques. In recent years, as consumer demand for the appearance quality of wolfberries has increased, advanced drying methods such as vacuum freeze-drying (FD) and vacuum microwave combined with vacuum pulsation drying (VMD) have been introduced. While these advanced methods satisfy consumer demand for better visual quality, they also significantly increase drying costs (Jiang et al., 2017). With advancements in drying technology, various dried wolfberry products are now available on the market, exhibiting significant differences in appearance, texture, and price. These variations can influence consumer preferences and perceptions of product quality. Although several studies have compared different drying methods of wolfberries (Cui et al., 2022), the comprehensive evaluation of their impacts on both quality attributes and health benefits is still limited.

Herein, fresh wolfberries were dried using four methods: natural drying (ND), hot air drying (HAD), vacuum freeze-drying (FD), and vacuum microwave combined with vacuum pulsation drying (VMD). The overall experimental workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. The quality, including color parameters, three-dimensional structure, and active compounds, was thoroughly analyzed and evaluated. Special emphasis was placed on carotenoids and LBPs, the primary bioactive compounds responsible for the traditional therapeutic effects of wolfberries, especially their roles in “liver protection and vision improvement.” These compounds are highly sensitive to alterations caused by different drying methods. This study provides scientific evidence to guide the selection of optimal drying techniques for fresh wolfberries and offers evidence-based recommendations for consumers to choose the most suitable wolfberry products.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Fresh wolfberries were collected from Zhongning County, Zhongwei City, Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, China, and were identified by Ling Ma of National Wolfberry Product Quality Inspection and Testing Center as fruits of Lycium barbarum L., which belongs to the genus Lycium. Fresh wolfberries were processed using four mainstream drying methods ND, HAD, FD, and VMD. Seven monosaccharide standards were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Zeaxanthin and β-carotene were obtained from Shanghai YuanYe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Zeaxanthin dipalmitate was prepared by Dr. Yuan Gong from Lanzhou University of Arts and Science. Acetaminophen (APAP) was sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The BCA protein concentration assay kit was purchased from Beijing Lanbolide Trading Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The human normal liver cell line L-O2 was obtained from Wuhan PunoSai Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Silymarin (Sil) and N-acetylcysteine (NAC), were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) kits were purchased from Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). NO, ROS, MDA, and GSH assay kits were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Biological Engineering Research Institute (Nanjing, China). Other chemicals were of analytical grade or higher.

Drying methods

Fresh wolfberries of equal weight were processed using four mainstream drying methods, including natural drying (ND), hot air drying (HAD), vacuum freeze-drying (FD), vacuum microwave combined with vacuum pulsation drying (VMD). In the ND method, fresh wolfberries were washed with an aqueous Na₂CO₃ solution to remove wax, rinsed with water, drained, and then naturally dried in a well-ventilated outdoor area under ambient conditions (25–35 °C, 30 %–70 % relative humidity, and 8000–12,000 lx light intensity) for 4–5 days, until the outer skin hardened and wrinkled. The HAD method involved the same initial washing procedure, followed by a three-stage drying process: 40 °C for 10 h, 50 °C for 10 h, and 60 °C for 12 h. For the FD method, the fruit was frozen to a core temperature of ≤ −35 °C without prior de-waxing, then placed in a freeze-drying chamber (cold trap ≤ −40 °C, vacuum 10–20 Pa). The temperature followed a nine-stage increase: 5 °C per stage for stages 1–5, 10 °C per stage for stages 6–8, and 15 °C in the final stage, maintained for 5 h. In the VMD method, wolfberries were pre-refrigerated at 4 °C for 2 h before vacuum microwave drying (vacuum 5–15 kPa, 45 °C). Microwave power was set to 2 W/g for 15 min, increased to 3 W/g (15–40 min), and reduced to 2 W/g (40–60 min). The samples were then subjected to vacuum-pulsed drying (40 °C), with an initial high vacuum (5–15 kPa, 0–30 min) followed by pulsed vacuum-atmospheric cycling (5–50 kPa, 30–90 min, 5–min cycles), and a gradual temperature reduction (90–120 min). Each drying method was applied to 20 kg of fresh wolfberry fruit, and the drying process was independently repeated three times. From each drying batch, 1 kg of dried product was randomly sampled and pooled to form a representative composite sample for analysis. The dried samples were designated as follows: LB-N (from natural drying, ND), LB-H (from hot air drying, HAD), LB-F (from vacuum freeze-drying, FD), and LB-V (vacuum microwave combined with vacuum pulsation drying, VMD). To determine moisture content, 5 g of each sample was dried at 105 °C for 6 h until a constant weight was reached, and the moisture content was calculated as the ratio of weight loss to initial weight. According to Appendix B of the Chinese national standard GB/T 18672-2014, the total sugar content in the four dried wolfberry samples was determined using the direct titration method.

Appearance analysis

Three-dimensional structure analysis

Three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions of LB-N, LB-H, LB-F, and LB-V were performed using an X-ray microscope (Xradia 515 Versa, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The scanning parameters were as follows: X-ray voltage of 60 kV, power of 5 W, exposure time of 1 s, with a 0.4× objective lens. A total of 1024 images were captured, and the resolution of the 3D reconstructed images was 18.9 μm. Micro-CT image reconstruction, visualization, and quantitative analysis were performed using 3D Viewer software.

Color difference measurement

The color of LB-N, LB-H, LB-F, and LB-V was measured using the method described by Yao (Yao et al., 2020). After calibrating the colorimeter with a standard white plate, the samples were placed directly under the sensor of a colorimeter (Model 10QC, 3nh Inc., Shenzhen, China) for measurement. To ensure accuracy, precautions were taken to prevent light leakage. Each sample was measured ten times, and the average value was recorded. Carotenoid analysis

Total carotenoid analysis

Carotenoid extraction using a hydrophobic deep eutectic solvent (H-DES) was carried out using a modified version of our previous protocol (Wei et al., 2022). Briefly, L-menthol (1 mol) and octyl alcohol (2 mol) were stirred at 70 °C until a homogeneous and transparent liquid formed, resulting in the H-DES. LB-N, LB-H, LB-F, and LB-V powders (4.0 g) were each mixed with H-DES at a 1:20 (w/v) ratio and extracted at room temperature for 5 min using a high-speed disperser emulsifier (HSDE) (T 25 digital, IKA, Staufen, Germany) operating at 10,000 rpm. All sample handling and extraction steps were performed protected from light, and care was taken to minimize exposure to air to reduce oxidation. The mixtures were then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. The final volume of the supernatant was adjusted to 100 mL in brown volumetric flasks for subsequent analysis. A 4 mg β-carotene standard was dissolved in H-DES and diluted to 50 mL in a brown volumetric flask to prepare standard solutions of varying concentrations. Using H-DES as a blank, absorbance was measured at 450 nm. A standard calibration curve was constructed by plotting absorbance (Y) versus concentration (X), and the corresponding regression equation was obtained. The total carotenoid content in the four test solutions was then calculated.

Zeaxanthin, β-carotene, and zeaxanthin dipalmitate analysis

The four test solutions from LB-N, LB-H, LB-F, and LB-V were filtered through 0.45 μm organic membranes and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent 1260 Infinity II, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). Conditions were as follows: YMC C30 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm); flow rate 0.5 mL/min; injection volume 20 μL; room temperature; detection at 450 nm. Mobile phases: A – methanol: methyl tert-butyl ether: water (92,4:4, v/v); B – methyl tert-butyl ether: methanol: water (90,6:4, v/v). Gradient elution: 0–10 min, 90 %–25 % A; 10–40 min, 25 % A. Standards of zeaxanthin, β-carotene, and zeaxanthin dipalmitate were dissolved in H-DES via ultrasonic-assisted dissolution to prepare calibration solutions. Standard curves were established by plotting peak area (Y) against concentration (X), and used to quantify target compounds in the samples.

Polysaccharides analysis

Preparation of LBPs

The extraction of LBPs was performed using a temperature-switchable deep eutectic solvent (TS-DES), with modifications based on a previously published protocol by our research group (Cao et al., 2024). Briefly, 100 mL of TS-DES was mixed with 200 mL of water, followed by the addition of 20 g of wolfberry residue remaining after pigment extraction. The mixture was treated with a high-speed shear emulsifier at 10,000 rpm and 70 °C for 20 min. The resulting crude extract was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min and then left to stand at 10 °C for 10 min to allow phase separation, yielding an upper aqueous phase and a lower TS-DES phase. The aqueous phase was collected and deproteinized using Sevag reagent (chloroform: n-butanol = 4:1, v/v). The resulting polysaccharide solution was dialyzed in flowing distilled water using a 3500 Da molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) dialysis membrane for 72 h and subsequently freeze-dried to obtain polysaccharide powder. The polysaccharides extracted from the wolfberries dried by ND, HAD, FD, and VMD were named LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V, respectively. The polysaccharide yield was calculated using the Eq. Determination of chemical composition

The uronic acid content was determined using the sulfuric acid-carbazole method (Blumenkrantz & Asboe Hansen, 1973). The protein content was measured using the BCA assay kit. The polysaccharide content was determined using the phenol‑sulfuric acid method (Tang et al., 2023).

FT-IR analysis

Accurate amounts of LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, LBPs-V and analytical-grade potassium bromide were thoroughly ground and mixed in a mortar and pressed into pellets. The FT-IR spectra were recorded in the range of 4000–500 cm−1 on FT-IR spectrometer (V70 Advance, Bruker, Massachusetts, USA), using potassium bromide as the background reference. Each spectrum was obtained with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans.

Mw analysis

The molecular weight and conformation of LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V were determined by high-performance size exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering and refractive index detectors (HPSEC-MALLS-RI) based on our group method (Cao et al., 2024). Briefly, a 5 mg/mL polysaccharide sample solution was prepared in 0.2 mol/L NaCl and passed through a 0.45 μm filter before sampling. Then, the average molecular weight (Mw) was measured on a HPLC system (Agilent 1290, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, California, USA) equipped with multiangle laser light scattering system (DAWN HELEOS II, Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, California, USA) and differential refractive index detector (RID-G7162 A, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, California, USA). The conditions were as follows: Shodex Ohpak 806HQ and 804HQ columns, 0.2 mol/L NaCl mobile phase at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Data collection and calculation were performed using ASTRA 8 software.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

The monosaccharide composition analysis of LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V was determined using pre-column derivatization with PMP and HPLC (Zhou et al., 2020). Briefly, the samples were hydrolyzed with TFA, the reacted at 70 °C in the dark with PMP, filtered (0.45 μm), and analyzed by HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity II, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). Conditions: SinoChrom ODS-BP column (10 μm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm); mobile phases: (A) 50 mmol/L phosphate buffer (pH 6.7), (B) acetonitrile; flow rate: 1.2 mL/min; gradient: 0–30 min, 83 % A; 30–55 min, 83–87 % A; 55–75 min, 87–83 % A; detection at 245 nm; injection volume: 20 μL.

Morphology analysis

A small amount of dried LBPs was fixed onto a sample holder using conductive adhesive and gold-coated under vacuum. The morphology of the LBPs sample was observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JSM-6701F, JEOL Co., Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV and a magnification of 500×, with a scale bar of 50 μm to capture surface morphology images. The LBPs samples were dissolved in ultrapure water to a concentration of 10 μg/mL and mixed thoroughly using a vortex mixer. A 10 μL aliquot of the solution was placed onto a silicon wafer and allowed to dry at room temperature. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed using an Si3N4 probe with an atomic force microscope (Multimode 8, Bruker, Santa Barbara, California, USA). Tapping mode was employed for measurement, and all images were recorded with a scan size of 1 μm × 1 μm and a scale bar of 200 nm. Data collection and analysis were conducted using Nanoscope Analysis software.

Thermal properties analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TG) and differential thermogravimetric analysis (DTG) of the polysaccharide samples were conducted using a simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA449F3, NETZSCH Co., Germany). A 2.0 mg sample of LBPs was placed in the sample pan and heated from room temperature to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, with N₂ as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 50 mL/min.

Effect on ethanol- and APAP-induced liver cell injury

Effects of LBPs on the viability of L-O2 cells induced by ethanol or APAP

Preliminary Work: cell culture and subculture procedures; assessment of L-O2 cell viability under various LBPs treatments; and development of ethanol- and acetaminophen (APAP) -induced L-O2 liver injury models. The CCK-8 assay was used to assess the effects of LBPs intervention on the viability of L-O2 cells injured by ethanol or APAP. L-O2 cells were seeded evenly into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. L-O2 cells in the normal control (NC) group were treated with fresh medium, while cells in the injury model groups were exposed to 200 μL of medium containing 6 % (v/v) ethanol or 40 mmol/L APAP for 4 h to induce cell injury. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing LBPs at concentrations of 50, 100, 200, and 400 μg/mL. For the model control (MC) group, only the medium was replaced without LBPs intervention. Following 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10 % CCK-8 reagent, and the cells were further incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded, with each group tested in triplicate.

Effects of LBPs on liver function-related indicators in L-O2 cells

L-O2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. After ethanol induction for 4 h, the experiment was divided into the following groups: (1) NC group; (2) MC group (6 % (v/v) ethanol); (3) positive control group (50 μg/mL silymarin, Sil); and (4) high-, medium-, and low-dose sample groups (100, 200, and 400 μg/mL LBPs). Each group had three replicates. The levels of ALT, AST, MDA, NO, and GSH were measured according to the instructions provided with the assay kits. The experiment was repeated using APAP instead of ethanol, with the positive control group treated with 200 μg/mL N-acetylcysteine (NAC), following the same procedures.

Detection of ROS levels in L-O2 cells

Following treatment, L-O2 cells cultured in 96-well plates were washed with a small volume of PBS to remove residual medium. A fluorescent probe, DCFH-DA (10 μg/mL), was added to the cells, which were then incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The cells were subsequently washed twice with 1 mL of ice-cold PBS to remove excess dye and the stained cells were resuspended in 1 mL of PBS for intracellular ROS level detection using flow cytometry.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 18.0 software. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), with experiments conducted in triplicate. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test was employed to compare multiple groups, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Wolfberry processed by different drying methods

The drying process typically consists of two stages: initially, the removal of unbound surface water, followed by the extraction of bound water from the interior until the desired moisture content is reached (Deng et al., 2018). Selecting an appropriate drying method is essential not only for inhibiting microbial growth but also for preserving the quality of medicinal materials and maintaining the stability of active compounds. Different drying techniques significantly impact drying time, cost, and the overall quality of wolfberries. As shown in Table 1, natural drying (ND) took the longest time (4–5 days) but is the most cost-effective, at a cost of 0.2 Chinese Yuan (CNY)/kg. However, the resulting fruit (LB-N) had a soft, sticky texture and a darker color, with a moisture content of 16.8 %, exceeding the national standard of 13.0 %. This high moisture level shortened its shelf life, making it prone to caking and bacterial growth in humid environments. Hot air drying (HAD), the most commonly used method, required 32–36 h at a cost of 2.5 CNY/kg. It produced LB-H with a deep red color but caused surface hardening and browning. Despite its low cost, low moisture content (10.20 %), and long shelf life—making it ideal for storage and transportation—it results in a less visually appealing product. Vacuum freeze-drying (FD) took 42–45 h and was the most expensive method (25 CNY/kg). However, it yielded LB-F with a smooth surface, crisp texture, and vibrant color, making it the best option for preserving visual quality. It also had a low moisture content (10.12 %) and an extended shelf life. Due to its highly porous structure, nitrogen-filled packaging was required to prevent damage during transportation. Vacuum microwave combined with vacuum pulsation drying (VMD) was the fastest method, completing the process in just 5–8 h, but shared the highest cost (25 CNY/kg) with FD. The resulting fruit (LB-V) exhibited minimal shrinkage and retained a color closest to fresh wolfberries, indicating superior preservation of natural pigments and texture. Additionally, it had a slightly higher moisture content (12.63 %) and an improved taste.

Visual quality and color parameters

The 3D structures of LB-N (A, a), LB-H (B, b), LB-F (C, c), and LB-V (D, d) are shown in Fig. 2. By analyzing cross-sectional views along the XY, YZ, and XZ planes, we observed distinct differences in pore distribution, internal structure, and overall morphology among the samples. LB-N exhibited a spindle-shaped structure with uniformly distributed pores, likely reflecting the arrangement of intercellular spaces or cavities. LB-H displayed a structure similar to LB-N but with more compact pores, which might have been closely related to moisture content. A lower moisture level could have led to a denser cellular structure. In contrast, LB-F had a spherical appearance with a more uniform pore distribution and higher symmetry, possibly due to the expansion and cavity formation promoted by FD. LB-V appeared elliptical with a smoother peel and irregular pore distribution, which might have reflected structural changes induced by VMD. The 3D reconstructions obtained via X-ray microscopy provided valuable insights into the effects of different drying methods on the internal structure of wolfberries. Variations in pore distribution, density, and morphology might have influenced the distribution of bioactive compounds and nutritional properties, potentially impacting their applications in functional foods.

Color, as one of the important sensory indicators of dried products, directly affects consumer preferences and market acceptance. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of drying methods on color by measuring the chromatic parameters (L⁎, a⁎, b⁎, and C) of wolfberries. The results are shown in Table 1. The L⁎, a⁎, b⁎, and C values of LB-F and LB-V were significantly higher than those of LB-N and LB-H. Particularly, LB-V exhibited the best color characteristics across all parameters, including higher brightness (L⁎ value), stronger red tone (a⁎ value), more yellowish hue (b⁎ value), and the highest color saturation (C value). These findings suggested that VMD effectively preserved the vivid color of wolfberries. This was because, under low pressure, the vacuum drying chamber contained minimal oxygen, so both FD and VMD could inhibit color degradation caused by oxidation and the loss of heat-sensitive components, which was consistent with the findings of Geng et al. (Geng et al., 2023). In contrast, LB-N displayed a duller color and lower color saturation, which might have been due to prolonged exposure to sunlight during HAD, leading to pigment degradation. Additionally, appearance color was mainly related to carotenoid degradation and non-enzymatic browning (Maillard reaction) (Pham et al., 2021). The Maillard reaction and carotenoid degradation in aerobic environments and at higher drying temperatures led to darker colors, which explained why the colors of sun-dried and hot air-dried products were darker (Li et al., 2022).

Carotenoids analysis

Carotenoids are essential pigments in wolfberries, contributing to their color and supporting visual health by absorbing blue light and reducing retinal damage (Zhao et al., 2023). Since the human body cannot produce carotenoids, wolfberries serve as a key dietary source. Therefore, preserving carotenoid stability during processing was crucial to maintaining their nutritional value. The regression analysis showed that the standard curve for total carotenoids was Y = 1.95X − 0.015 (R2 = 0.997). The standard curves for the three major carotenoid monomers were as follows: zeaxanthin, Y = 562.33X + 55.024 (R2 = 0.999); β-carotene, Y = 190.64X − 18.007 (R2 = 0.999); and zeaxanthin dipalmitate, Y = 2079.2X + 111.90 (R2 = 0.998). All calibration curves exhibited strong linearity, indicating their suitability for the quantitative analysis of carotenoids in subsequent samples. Table 1 and Fig. S1 showed that different drying methods significantly affected carotenoid content and composition. LB-V had the highest total carotenoid content (6.42 mg/g), followed by LB-F (5.27 mg/g) and LB-N (4.86 mg/g), with LB-H having the lowest (3.85 mg/g). This suggested that lower temperatures and reduced oxygen exposure help prevent carotenoid degradation, which was consistent with Zeng et al.'s findings (Zeng et al., 2023). Further analysis revealed that LB-V retained the highest zeaxanthin content (3.41 μg/g), whereas LB-H had the lowest (1.17 μg/g). Similarly, β-carotene and zeaxanthin dipalmitate retention were highest in LB-V (52.05 μg/g and 139.72 μg/g, respectively) and lowest in LB-H (30.13 μg/g and 114.54 μg/g, respectively). FD and VMD proved more effective in preserving carotenoids, whereas ND and HAD led to significant losses. This was due to carotenoids' sensitivity to heat, light, and oxygen, which caused degradation through oxidation or isomerization. VMD reduces oxidative damage by creating a vacuum environment that limits oxygen exposure and simultaneously accelerates moisture removal, thereby shortening the time carotenoids are exposed to oxidative conditions. In contrast, FD preserves carotenoids primarily through sublimation under low temperature and pressure, which minimizes thermal and oxidative degradation but generally requires longer drying times. Similar findings have been reported by Fratianni et al. (2018) and Souza et al. (2022), highlighting the negative impact of HAD on pigment stability. Overall, our study confirms that FD and VMD are the most effective methods for preserving carotenoids in wolfberries, with VMD offering the additional advantage of faster drying while limiting oxidative damage.

Polysaccharide analysis

LBPs are the primary bioactive components in wolfberries. The total carbohydrate contents of LB-N, LB-H, LB-F, and LB-V were 4.05 %, 4.14 %, 4.37 %, and 4.39 %, respectively (Table 1), indicating that the differences among drying methods were relatively minor. Although the drying method did not significantly affect the total carbohydrate content, factors such as temperature, oxygen exposure, and drying duration may influence the chemical composition and structural characteristics of polysaccharides, thereby impacting their bioactivity. Therefore, we further investigated the effects of different drying methods on the structural properties and bioactivity of polysaccharides. To achieve this, H-DES was employed to extract pigments from the four types of wolfberries. Subsequently, tailored TS-DES systems were utilized to efficiently extract LBPs from the wolfberry residues.

FT-IR spectroscopy was utilized to characterize the functional groups of LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V and confirmed that all the four LBPs shared typical polysaccharide structural characteristics (Fig. S2). A broad and strong absorption peak at 3389 cm−1 was attributed to O—H stretching vibrations, while a weak band at 2928 cm−1 corresponded to C—H stretching. Weak absorptions at 901 cm−1 and 867 cm−1 were characteristic of α- and β-glycosidic linkages, respectively (Zhang et al., 2022). Spectral differences among LBPs prepared via different drying methods, evident from shifts in peak intensity and wavenumber, suggested that drying strongly affects their hydrogen bonding and conformational structures. Among the samples, LBPs-V exhibited the highest polysaccharide yield (4.87 %), exceeding LBPs-F (4.63 %), LBPs-N (4.35 %), and LBPs-H (4.13 %). This variation could likely be attributed to the lower pressure conditions utilized during the drying processes of LBPs-V and LBPs-F, which might had facilitated the interconnection and expansion of micropores within the wolfberry samples (Wu et al., 2022). Such alterations in the physical structure likely enhanced the permeability of the tissue or cellular matrices, thereby improving the efficiency of polysaccharide extraction and increasing the yield. In contrast, the reduced yield of LBPs-H might been a consequence of surface hardening of the fruit during the high-temperature drying process (Chen et al., 2024). The polysaccharide content followed the order LBPs-V (80.42 %) > LBPs-F (77.94 %) > LBPs-N (74.23 %) > LBPs-H (73.79 %). Protein content ranged from 5.69 % to 6.95 % across all samples, while uronic acid content varied, with LBPs-N (8.42 %) and LBPs-V (8.04 %) exhibiting higher values compared to LBPs-H (5.78 %) and LBPs-F (5.06 %). These results suggested that the drying method significantly influenced the composition of LBPs.

The Mw and chromatograms of LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V are presented in Table 2 and Fig. S3. Specifically, all LBPs exhibited three distinct molecular weight distribution peaks (Mw I, Mw II, and Mw III), indicating that the four polysaccharides were polydisperse with significant variations in molecular weights and their respective proportions. The Mw of the four LBPs was LBPs-V (3.51 × 104 Da) < LBPs-F (7.06 × 104 Da) < LBPs-N (7.43 × 104 Da) < LBPs-H (1.15 × 105 Da). Polysaccharides from ND and HAD showed higher Mw due to thermal effects during drying, which promoted aggregation by removing hydration layers and disrupting structures (Li et al., 2021). In contrast, VMD caused chain breakage and degradation, resulting in lower Mw, which was consistent with findings in seedless chestnut rose polysaccharides (Chen et al., 2024). HPSEC-MALLS-RI determined the relationship between Mw and root-mean-square (RMS) radius, providing insights into RMS conformation. The α value indicated polysaccharide chain conformation: 0–0.3, 0.4–0.8, and 1.0 correspond to spherical, random coil, and rod-like structures in NaCl solution, respectively (Blumenkrantz & Asboe Hansen, 1973). As shown in Fig. 3, LBPs-F and LBPs-V exhibited α values of 0.46 and 0.48, indicating random coil conformations, while LBPs-N and LBPs-H had α values of 0.27 and 0.28, suggesting spherical conformations. Lower α values reflect more compact spherical structures and greater thermal aggregation. Four LBPs contained varying molar percentages of mannose (Man), rhamnose (Rha), galacturonic acid (GlcA), glucuronic acid (GalA), glucose (Glc), galactose (Gal), and arabinose (Ara). Among these four polysaccharides, the contents of Gal and Ara were relatively abundant (Table 2 and Fig. S4), which indicated that these polysaccharides are primarily composed of Ara and Gal. The results further suggested that different drying methods significantly influenced the monosaccharide composition of LBPs, potentially due to variations in temperature and oxygen exposure associated with each method. When polysaccharides underwent drying in an oxygen-rich environment or at elevated temperatures, hydroxyl oxidation and the cleavage of intermolecular hydrogen bonds may occur, leading to alterations in monosaccharide content (Shang et al., 2018).

SEM images of LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V at 500× magnification revealed distinct morphological differences in shape and size (Fig. 4). LBPs-V and LBPs-F exhibited rough, porous surfaces with a rod-like structure, likely attributed to low-temperature vacuum drying. During this process, water sublimation disrupted the internal structure, resulting in a loose and porous morphology (Ahmadi et al., 2019). In contrast, LBPs-N and LBPs-H presented compact, irregular scaly structures, which might have been due to molecular aggregation induced by thermal effects and water absorption during drying. These findings were consistent with previous studies demonstrating a strong correlation between polysaccharide morphology and drying methods (Ma et al., 2013). AFM provided nanoscale insights into the surface morphology and molecular characteristics of polysaccharides. As shown in Fig. 4, the samples primarily consist of linear chains, branched structures, and aggregates. LBPs-N and LBPs-H exhibit clustered structures, with LBPs-H forming larger aggregates, likely due to extensive hydrogen bonding between side chains (Zhou et al., 2022a). In contrast, LBPs-F presented long, straight chains with minimal branching, whereas LBPs-V displayed highly branched short-chain structures that formed dense, network-like morphologies. This network formation may have resulted from reduced thermal aggregation under milder pressure cycling and shorter drying durations, which better preserved the polysaccharide structure. These findings underscored the heterogeneity and structural complexity of LBPs subjected to different drying methods and further confirmed that thermal aggregation occurred during drying, consistent with RMS analysis.

The thermal stability of polysaccharides is primarily influenced by their structural characteristics and functional group modifications (Chen et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. 5, the TG and DTG curves of LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V exhibited similar trends, with three distinct weight loss stages. In the first stage (35–150 °C), all polysaccharides underwent weight loss due to bound water evaporation, an endothermic process. The second stage (>200 °C) is marked by rapid weight loss, attributed to the degradation and depolymerization of polysaccharide chains, including glycosidic bond cleavage and backbone breakdown (Kou et al., 2022). The mass loss rate (200–500 °C) followed the order: LBPs-H (61.57 %) > LBPs-N (54.92 %) > LBPs-F (53.16 %) > LBPs-V (48.84 %), with LBPs-V exhibiting the highest thermal stability, likely due to its fewer intramolecular hydrogen bonds and a more loosely packed structure. In the final stage (>500 °C), gradual weight loss was associated with the decomposition of residual carbonized compounds (Bai et al., 2023).

Previous studies demonstrated the hepatoprotective potential of LBPs (Wang et al., 2020). As L-O2 cells serve as a well-established in vitro model for studying liver injury and its underlying mechanisms, this study employed ethanol- and APAP-induced liver injury models to evaluate the hepatoprotective effects of LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V. As illustrated in Fig. S5A and S5B, treatment with 6 % ethanol and 40 mmol/L APAP resulted in cell viability levels of 54.24 % ± 1.15 % (p < 0.01) and 50.08 % ± 0.53 % (p < 0.01), respectively. Based on these findings, 6 % ethanol was selected to establish the ethanol-induced L-O2 cell injury model, while 40 mmol/L APAP was used to construct the APAP-induced L-O2 cell injury model for subsequent experimental analysis. Additionally, none of the four polysaccharides exhibited significant proliferative or cytotoxic effects on L-O2 cells within the concentration range of 50–400 μg/mL (Fig. S5C). Fig. 6A and 6B present the protective effects of the four LBPs on L-O2 cells subjected to 6 % ethanol and 40 mmol/L APAP treatment. Compared with the MC group, treatment with varying concentrations of LBPs resulted in a concentration-dependent increase in L-O2 cell viability. Specifically, at a concentration of 400 μg/mL, LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V increased cell viability by 18.54 %, 15.92 %, 21.05 %, and 21.75 %, respectively, in ethanol-treated cells. Similarly, for APAP-treated cells, viability was enhanced by 14.90 %, 13.84 %, 17.17 %, and 18.49 %, respectively. These findings suggested that LBPs exerted significant protective effects on L-O2 cells following ethanol- and APAP-induced injury. Moreover, LBPs-F and LBPs-V demonstrated superior efficacy compared to LBPs-N and LBPs-H in mitigating cell apoptosis. Considering the relatively limited protective effect observed at 50 μg/mL, the concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 μg/mL were selected for subsequent investigations to further evaluate the effects of the different polysaccharide components on ethanol- and APAP-induced L-O2 cell damage and oxidative stress.

AST and ALT are predominantly localized in the cytoplasm and mitochondria of hepatocytes. Upon cellular damage, these enzymes are released in significant quantities into the extracellular environment, serving as key biomarkers of liver cell membrane and mitochondrial injury (Govindan et al., 2021). As demonstrated in Fig. 6A, L-O2 cells treated with ethanol and APAP exhibited a marked increase in AST and ALT activity levels compared to the NC group (p < 0.01), confirming the occurrence of cellular damage and enhanced membrane permeability. Conversely, treatment with LBPs for 24 h mitigated this damage, as evidenced by a significant reduction in AST and ALT activity levels relative to the MC group. The reduction in enzyme activity displayed a clear concentration-dependent effect (p < 0.01), indicating that LBPs effectively alleviated liver injury induced by ethanol and APAP. These results suggested that LBPs could enhance liver function and protect against liver damage. Additionally, within the tested concentration range, the protective effects of the polysaccharides on L-O2 cells followed the order: LBPs-V > LBPs-F > LBPs-N > LBPs-H. The observed differences in protective efficacy highlighted the influence of drying methods on the biological activity of LBPs.

Oxidative stress is a key driver of liver injury, caused by excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS-induced lipid peroxidation produces malondialdehyde (MDA), while glutathione (GSH) serves as a critical antioxidant. Nitric oxide (NO), generated by inducible nitric oxide synthase during liver damage, contributes to cellular toxicity (Wang et al., 2012). The levels of ROS, MDA, GSH, and NO are important indicators of oxidative damage and antioxidant effects. As depicted in Fig. 7, in the ethanol-induced damage model, the MC group exhibited a substantial increase in ROS, MDA, and NO levels, accompanied by a marked reduction in GSH levels compared to the NC group (p < 0.01). This indicated that ethanol-induced injury disrupted cellular redox homeostasis and exacerbated oxidative damage. However, treatment with LBPs significantly alleviated these effects in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.01). Specifically, LBPs reduced ROS, MDA, and NO levels while simultaneously increasing GSH levels in ethanol-damaged cells, highlighting their potent antioxidant properties. Among the oxidative damage indicators, LBPs demonstrated a particularly pronounced effect in reducing MDA levels. Notably, at a concentration of 400 μg/mL, the effects of LBPs on MDA clearance were comparable to those of the positive control group (p > 0.05), suggesting that LBPs mitigated oxidative stress by scavenging lipid peroxides. Furthermore, at 400 μg/mL, LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V reduced intracellular ROS levels by 15.22 %, 13.53 %, 16.33 %, and 26.75 %, respectively, relative to the MC group. Similarly, GSH levels were approximately 40 %, 36 %, 53 %, and 70 % higher, respectively, in the LBPs-treated groups compared to the MC group. Among the tested polysaccharides, LBPs-V exhibited significantly stronger antioxidant activity than the others (p < 0.05), indicating its superior efficacy in inhibiting peroxide formation and mitigating oxidative damage in vitro.

In the APAP-induced liver injury model, treatment with NAC or LBPs significantly reduced ROS, MDA, and NO levels while increasing GSH levels in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.01), consistent with the effects observed in the ethanol-induced liver injury model. At 400 μg/mL, LBPs-N, LBPs-H, LBPs-F, and LBPs-V reduced ROS levels by 37.66 %, 29.95 %, 38.94 %, and 40.09 %, respectively, and decreased MDA content by 53 %, 55 %, 47 %, and 45 % compared to the MC group. Notably, at a high concentration (400 μg/mL), no significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between the four LBP groups and the positive control (NAC).

All four LBPs reduced ALT and AST levels, protected liver cells, and enhanced antioxidant capacity by lowering ROS and MDA while increasing GSH. They also suppressed inflammation by reducing NO levels. To explore the structure-activity relationship of LBPs, we used Pearson correlation and heatmap analysis. The differences in the biological activities of LBPs were mainly attributed to variations in their Mw and monosaccharide composition. As shown in Fig. 8, Mw positively correlated with liver injury markers (ALT, AST, ROS, MDA), with correlation coefficients of 0.916, 0.966, and 0.926, respectively, in an APAP-induced liver injury model. Among the four LBPs, LBPs-V had the smallest Mw, suggesting that lower Mw enhances hepatoprotective and antioxidant effects. This aligned with Wang et al.'s (2023) study, which found that lower Mw exposed more active hydroxyl groups, boosting antioxidant activity. In contrast, high-Mw polysaccharides had a compact structure with fewer active sites, reducing their ability to neutralize free radicals (Wang et al., 2020). Regarding monosaccharide composition, antioxidant capacity negatively correlated with Glc content, consistent with Cao et al.'s (2025) findings that lower Glc improved antioxidant activity. Additionally, Ara content negatively correlated with ALT, AST, ROS, MDA, and NO, while positively correlating with GSH. Xu et al. (2018) identified Ara as a key component in LBPs' hepatoprotective and antioxidant effects. Overall, Mw and Ara content were crucial factors influencing LBPs' hepatoprotective activity. Furthermore, the dispersibility of the samples might affect the interaction surface area between polysaccharide molecules, thereby influencing their biological activity (Long et al., 2022). LBPs-V exhibited the strongest protective effect, likely due to its lower Mw, higher effective monosaccharide content, porous structure, and reduced aggregation, enhancing its bioactivity. Future research should further explore LBPs' molecular mechanisms and clinical potential.

Conclusion

Wolfberries are well known for their health benefits, and ongoing research continues to investigate and confirm their bioactive properties. Drying plays a crucial role in processing, directly impacting the final product's quality and bioactive composition. With advancements in drying technologies, a diverse range of dried wolfberry products has emerged on the market, differing significantly in appearance, texture, and price. These variations can shape consumer preferences and perceptions of product quality. This study systematically examined the impact of four commonly used drying methods on the appearance and bioactive compounds, specifically carotenoids and polysaccharides, in wolfberries. The results indicate that different drying techniques have a significant impact on the physical characteristics of wolfberries. LB-F and LB-V produced plumper, smoother samples with vibrant colors, whereas LB-N and LB-H exhibited greater shrinkage and darker coloration. This difference is likely attributed to the low-pressure conditions in vacuum freeze-drying (FD) and Vacuum microwave combined with vacuum pulsation drying (VMD), which minimize oxygen exposure and effectively inhibit oxidation-induced carotenoid degradation, the Maillard reaction, and the loss of heat-sensitive compounds.

Carotenoids are the key bioactive compounds in wolfberries, playing a crucial role in their vision improvement properties and characteristic red coloration. Due to the conjugated π-electron double-bond structure of carotenoids, they are highly susceptible to isomerization under environmental factors such as temperature, oxygen, and light. In this study, whether in their total form or as specific monomeric compounds such as zeaxanthin, β-carotene, and zeaxanthin dipalmitate, FD and VMD were more effective in preserving both structure and content. In contrast, natural drying (ND) and hot air drying (HAD) resulted in significant losses, particularly with HAD, where prolonged exposure to high temperatures led to the lowest retention of pigment compounds. FD and VMD effectively minimize oxidation by reducing oxygen exposure, thereby enhancing carotenoid stability and preserving their bioactive components. Polysaccharides are also among the primary bioactive components in wolfberries and are considered key contributors to their hepatoprotective effects. In terms of overall polysaccharide content, the four drying methods had minimal impact, with VMD exhibiting a slight advantage. Consequently, LBPs derived from wolfberries dried using all four methods demonstrated protective activity against alcohol- or drug-induced liver damage. However, drying methods significantly influence the structural properties of polysaccharides, including molecular weight, monosaccharide composition, microstructure, and thermal properties, all of which collectively contribute to variations in biological activity. Notably, LBPs-V obtained from VMD-treated wolfberries exhibited lower thermal aggregation, smaller molecular weight, a higher proportion of bioactive monosaccharides, and enhanced hepatoprotective efficacy. In contrast, LBPs-N and LBPs-H likely underwent greater thermal aggregation, leading to a reduction in their hepatoprotective properties.

From an industrial perspective, the present findings provide practical guidance for optimizing wolfberry processing. VMD and FD demonstrate superior performance in both drying efficiency and product quality, facilitating the production of high-value products with improved retention of carotenoids and polysaccharides. By contrast, ND and HAD represent cost-effective options for large-scale production, though they are less effective in preserving bioactive components. The substantial differences in pretreatment methods, drying temperatures, and heat transfer mechanisms among these techniques likely influence interactions between the cell wall and intracellular components. Therefore, careful selection of drying methods should consider not only processing time and economic cost but also the impact on the stability of bioactive compounds, in order to achieve an optimal balance between quality and feasibility. Overall, these results underscore the potential of advanced drying technologies to enhance both quality and cost-effectiveness in future industrial applications of wolfberry products.
