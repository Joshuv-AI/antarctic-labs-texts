The effects of intravenous iron on mobility in older adults following hip fracture surgery: protocol for a multicentre, parallel group, randomised controlled trial – the IronHip trial
Authors: Nicolas Tekin Jones, Morten Tange Kristensen, Robin Christensen, Jens Peter Alva-Jørgensen, Anja Løve Berger, Mark Andreas Eggertsen, Nicolai Bang Foss, Marius Henriksen, Thomas Giver Jensen, Kristian Kidholm, Troels Haxholdt Lunn, Mads Suhr Nielsen, Henrik Palm, Pelle Baggesgaard Petersen, Helene Stampe, Bjarke Viberg, Søren Overgaard, Martin Aasbrenn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionHalf of all patients with a hip fracture (HF) do not recover their pre-fracture mobility after 1 year. Mobility and functional capabilities may be impaired by postoperative low haemoglobin levels and iron deficiency. The primary objective of this trial is to compare the effect of a single postoperative dose of ferric derisomaltose 20 mg/kg body weight relative to placebo on the recovery of functional mobility in patients.Methods and analysisThe IronHip trial is a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with participants allocated in a 1:1 ratio to receive intravenous iron or placebo. The eligible population is participants aged 65 years or older who have undergone HF surgery and have a haemoglobin concentration below 105 g/L measured between postoperative day 1 and 5. In total, 210 participants will be randomised. Main analyses will be conducted in the intention-to-treat population using a mixed model for repeated measures, including assessments at baseline and at 4, 6 and 12 weeks after randomisation. Treatment effects will be estimated from the treatment-by-time interaction and reported as between-group differences in least-squares mean New Mobility Score with 95% CIs. The secondary outcomes are haemoglobin, red blood cell transfusions, fatigue, quality of life, self-rated health, fear of falls, 30 s sit-to-stand test, activities of daily living, pain, days alive and at home up to 30 days, mortality and serious adverse events. Exploratory outcomes are cost-effectiveness, physical activity, hand grip strength, knee extension strength, cognition, ferritin and transferrin saturation. Recruitment was initiated on 9 June 2025.Ethics and disseminationThe IronHip trial has been approved by the Danish Medicines Agency and the Danish Medical Research Ethics Committee. The protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Introduction

Acquiring a hip fracture (HF) can have serious consequences for older adults. In Northern Europe, around 12%–16% of patients are readmitted and 6%–10% die within the first 30 days after their fracture.1 2 According to patients, stable mobility with the capacity to handle day-to-day activities is a major concern after HF.3 However, after 1 year, only about 50% of the patients recover their mobility.4 5 Lack of mobility after a HF is associated with increased infection, readmission and higher mortality rates.6,11 Since mobility also is the top priority for patients, improving treatments that enhance recovery is warranted.3 4

Anaemia is very common after a HF and is associated with increased mortality and impaired mobility.12,14 Moreover, anaemia may increase the risk of postoperative infections and cardiovascular events. Iron deficiency is also associated with decreased mobility, lower functional level and reduced skeletal muscle function.13,16 The intestinal uptake of orally administered iron is limited in the weeks following HF surgery, but iron can be absorbed intravenously in this period.17

Observational and intervention studies investigating preoperative and perioperative intravenous iron have focused on 30-day mortality and the need for postoperative blood transfusion.18,22 The HiFIT trial, a 2×2 factorial trial evaluating tranexamic acid and intravenous iron, demonstrated a significant effect of preoperative intravenous iron on the secondary outcome haemoglobin at 4 weeks.23 A Cochrane review from 2023 states that there is a lack of evidence on the effect of iron treatment in HF patients, due to few and small studies, and lack of adequate inclusion of patient reported outcomes measures.24 This is supported by a recent systematic review highlighting the need for high-quality studies on intravenous iron in HF patients.25 More trials focusing on patient-relevant and functional outcomes are needed.2223 25,29 We hypothesise that intravenous iron administration 1–5 days post-surgery will improve the recovery of functional mobility through an increase in erythropoiesis and enhanced muscle function.

The primary objective is to compare the effect of a single postoperative dose of ferric derisomaltose (FDI) 20 mg/kg body weight relative to placebo on the recovery of functional mobility in patients. Functional mobility is measured as the change from baseline (pre-fracture) in the New Mobility Score (NMS) after a series of repeated measures from 4, 6 and 12 weeks, with the between-group difference at 6 weeks post-surgery specified as the primary endpoint.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

The IronHip trial is designed as a multicentre, parallel-group, superiority, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with 1:1 allocation to FDI or placebo, administered between postoperative days 1 and 5 following HF surgery.

The trial was initiated on the 2 June 2025 at Copenhagen University Hospital, Bispebjerg and Frederiksberg. It was subsequently initiated at Copenhagen University Hospital Herlev and Gentofte on the 20 August 2025 as well as at Odense University Hospital Svendborg on the 2 November 2025. We anticipate the recruitment phase to end in 2027.

Setting

In the IronHip trial, we recruit patients at three Danish public hospitals: Copenhagen University Hospital Bispebjerg and Frederiksberg, Copenhagen University Hospital Herlev and Gentofte, and Odense University Hospital Svendborg. All the sites have a high flow of patients with a HF, with a combined total of 1701 patients in 2024.1 The treatment of patients with HF in Denmark is monitored with quality indicators by the Multidisciplinary Danish Hip Fracture Registry.1 This framework shares a similar focus on improving patient care as the ‘National Institute for Health and Care Excellence’ guidelines and the National Hip Fracture Database in the UK for the management of patients with a HF.2 30 All participating sites aim to operate on patients within 24 hours, regardless of anticoagulants, to administer tranexamic acid during surgery to limit bleeding and to follow a restrictive red blood transfusion regime (stable patients get erythrocyte transfusion at haemoglobin 97 g/L preoperatively and 81 g/L postoperatively). Patients with severe anaemia get blood transfusions according to guidelines but do not get intravenous iron as part of the standard care during the acute admission. There are no Danish national guidelines for oral iron supplement for HF patients. Management of anticoagulation and thromboprophylaxis will follow the Danish guidelines for perioperative regulation of antithrombotic treatment.31 Clinical choices, including blood transfusion and anticoagulation, are ultimately left to the discretion of the treating physician following a pragmatic trial approach.

Study population

Patients that undergo surgery for an acute proximal femur fracture and are 65 years or older are screened for eligibility. If they fulfil inclusion criteria and do not meet any exclusion criteria on postoperative days 1–5, they are deemed eligible (table 1). Eligible patients are informed in writing and orally about the trial. Participant information sheet and consent form can be found in the online supplemental material. If they sign the informed consent to participate, they are enrolled in the trial. A trained physician obtains the informed consent. There is close communication between the central trial coordination and each site. At each site, there is dedicated and trained trial staff to both screen potential candidates and to inform patients about the trial.

Eligibility criteria

To evaluate the external validity of the trial, an adjunct study, N-IronHip (No to IronHip), has been established to run parallel to IronHip. The N-IronHip study compares eligible patients who decline participation in IronHip with those who consent.

Patient and public involvement

Patients with a HF were involved in several phases of the trial. During the design phase, we interviewed patients at the 2-month follow-up in our orthogeriatric outpatient clinic. This time-point was aligning with the primary endpoint in the trial. We asked what was important for their recovery and whether they found our suggested primary outcome measure relevant. The patients prioritised functional mobility—an answer that was both in line with earlier studies and the developed core outcome set.3 32 Patient input informed the selection of primary and secondary outcomes for the trial. During the recruitment planning phase, admitted patients with HF from the orthopaedic ward, corresponding to the anticipated participant enrolment, were consulted regarding the clarity and adequacy of the participant information materials.

We have developed a public website for the trial which includes a questions and answers section formulated in plain language for lay persons. The participants will be informed about the trial findings when the trial is over. A patient with HF will assist in the dissemination of trial findings.

Intervention and placebo

The participant receives intervention or placebo postoperatively on days 1–5, administered by a healthcare professional specifically trained in maintaining blinding, monitoring participants, managing potential side effects and determining when to discontinue the intervention.

Participants in the intervention group receive a single intravenous infusion of 20 mg FDI/kg body weight with a maximum dose of 2000 mg, diluted in 100 mL isotonic saline. FDI is an established therapeutic agent with a well-documented safety profile.33,35 FDI was chosen because it can be administered in a single high dose and has a low risk of severe adverse drug reactions compared with other intravenous iron products, including reduced risk of hypophosphataemia.36 37

Participants in the placebo group receive 100 mL isotonic saline/sodium chloride 0.9% which is such a small amount that it is considered placebo.

Treatment allocation

Sequence generation: after signing informed consent, participants will undergo baseline measurements before being randomly assigned to FDI or placebo. Randomisation is done in permuted block sizes of 2, 4 and 6 using computer-generated random numbers (SAS Proc Plan, 420 potential envelopes, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). We expect 70 participants per site, who will be randomly allocated and stratified by fracture type (intracapsular or extracapsular). Each site will have 140 ‘digital envelopes’ (70 for each of the two strata), in the electronic case report form (eCRF, REDCap, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA), totalling 420 across all three sites (70×2×3). The allocation sequence is constructed and incorporated in REDCap by the database manager.

Allocation concealment: blinded, dedicated project staff will access REDCap to perform randomisation for each participant. REDCap provides a randomisation number corresponding to a number on a package available at the site. An unblinded trained healthcare professional subsequently receives the randomisation number, opens the corresponding package that reveals what is allocated and contains all the required blinding equipment (figure 1). It is on opening the package that the assignment is disclosed to the participants.

The package from the pharmacy that contains the investigational medicine or placebo and the blinding equipment.

Blinding

The intervention is a non-transparent brown solution whereas the placebo is transparent aqueous. Therefore, a procedure for blinding is prepared and performed by a trained healthcare professional. The blinding equipment includes a specially designed infusion bag and sleeves that the staff use to cover the solutions, thus ensuring it remains unidentifiable (figure 2). The covered solution is suspended on an intravenous drip stand and given to the participant. Treatment allocation is not visible in the database and consequently the following individuals and groups in table 2 will remain blinded to allocation until the study database is locked and all analyses specified in the statistical analysis plan have been completed.

Unblinding procedures

Blinding may be broken intentionally if needed for safety reasons. If blinding is broken for one of the above-mentioned individuals, it is maintained for other blinded staff. Blinding can be broken immediately by the unblinded staff given the solution, or by opening the sealed randomisation envelopes. In case of unblinding, intentionally or by accident, it is documented with the reason for unblinding, when it occurred, who requested the unblinding, who had access to the unblinded data, and in cases where unblinding occurred by accident: the consequences of the unblinding and any preventive actions taken. A system for emergency unblinding outside daytime hours is established.

Oral iron supplement during trial

Low absorption of oral iron supplements is common among older people during systemic inflammation reactions that typically occur after HF surgery.17 38 Participants on an oral iron medication at the time of admission will not be excluded; but their oral iron treatment will be paused for 2 weeks after inclusion in the trial, corresponding to the period with a high level of systemic inflammation. Standard care will otherwise continue unchanged. For instance, when a physician deems it necessary to initiate oral iron therapy during the follow-up period, these subjects will not be excluded from the trial.

Demographic, baseline and follow-up data

All demographic and baseline data will be obtained by project staff and reported in the eCRF. The following data will be obtained at inclusion by the project staff trained in obtaining the data: sex, age, height, weight, residential status, current medication (including anti-coagulants, anti-platelets and iron supplements), ASA classification, clinical frailty score, type of anaesthesia, blood tests from admission, current infection, type of infection, comorbidities from admission, time to surgery from admission, blood loss during surgery, fracture type, operative method and duration of surgery. Baseline values for primary and secondary outcomes are also attained after consent, but before randomisation. Data regarding postoperative infections, length of stay, complications (including re-operations and thromboembolic events), number of falls, discharge destination (own home, 24-hour facility and nursing home) are collected from electronic patient records. This includes tracking readmissions as well as acute and outpatient hospital visits. Socioeconomic data (work, sick leave, salary and education) is acquired at the 12-week follow-up.

Organisation, monitoring and data quality assurance

The trial steering committee has been involved in designing the study. According to Good Clinical Practice (GCP) requirements, one primary investigator is appointed per site. Each site has dedicated academic staff as a part of the trial. The study sponsor holds regular meetings with each site, together with the study coordinator, to ensure recruiting and to solve ongoing issues.

The trial sponsor is responsible for the ongoing monitoring of the risk and benefit ratio. The primary investigator oversees the local sites. The trial will be monitored according to Danish law and International Council for Harmonisation (ICH)-GCP guidelines by the GCP unit of the Copenhagen University Hospital. A database manager is appointed and is responsible for building the database. For each participant, study data will be collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted by the Capital Region of Denmark.39 40 To ensure validity and data quality all outcome-assessors are trained specifically in obtaining the outcomes and using REDCap. REDCap’s built-in data quality tools are used to ensure validated responses and minimise missing data, including range checks for certain variables, outlier detection and verification of missing fields. All data entries and modifications are logged in REDCap.

Strategies to improve adherence

To promote participant retention, transportation to the in-person follow-up is organised for the participants. If a participant withdraws from the trial but consents to limited follow-up, permission will be asked to collect further outcome data and monitor SAEs with a call 6 weeks after intervention.

Outcomes and endpoints

Participant follow-up is after 4, 6 and 12 weeks (figure 3). The 4-week assessments will be conducted by telephone. The 6-week and 12-week assessments are planned to be performed in person in the hospital clinic. To improve adherence and minimise bias, transport (taxi or hospital transport system) is organised and paid by the project. To further minimise loss to follow-up, a home visit is planned for participants who cannot manage to get into the clinic. If a home visit is not possible (eg, if they are on holiday) for the participant or if the participant refuses a home visit, a phone call is allowed to obtain all the interview-based outcomes. In follow-up done only by a phone call, haemoglobin, sit-to-stand test and some exploratory outcomes are not obtained.

Primary outcome

The NMS is a modified patient-reported outcome score from 0 to 9, with 0 points indicating no walking ability and 9 points indicating fully independent indoor and outdoor abilities, including being able to shop without assistance (table 3).41,43 A higher score reflects a superior outcome. NMS has a high inter-rater reliability.41

NMS will be assessed at baseline and at 4, 6 and 12 weeks after randomisation. The window for obtaining primary and secondary outcomes at 4, 6 and 12 weeks is −3/+7 days from intervention.

Baseline NMS will be obtained retrospectively and defined as the pre-fracture NMS of participants in the week preceding the fracture. All outcome assessors will receive standardised training in attaining NMS and other study outcomes.

Recovery of mobility from the pre-fracture level will be compared using repeated-measures linear mixed models. The primary endpoint is change in the NMS at 6 weeks, estimated as the between-group difference in least-squares means. Measurements at baseline and at 4 and 12 weeks will be included in the longitudinal repeated-measures analysis.

1. Haemoglobin

Blood tests will be obtained, and haemoglobin will be analysed at baseline and following the 6-week and 12-week follow-up visits. The baseline haemoglobin is the first haemoglobin value that falls below the inclusion criteria ≤105 g/L on any of the postoperative days 1–5. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mean haemoglobin concentration in a longitudinal assessment, with week 6 as the key secondary endpoint.

2. Red blood cell transfusion requirement

RBC transfusion will be attained from electronic patient records and reported as the total number of units transfused per patient on postoperative days 7 and 30. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in the number of units of RBCs.

3. Fatigue

Fatigue will be measured with ‘Verbal rating scale – Fatigue’ from 0 to 4 where 0 indicates no fatigue and 4 represents extreme fatigue. It is measured at baseline, 4, 6 and 12 weeks after intervention. The 4 weeks assessment is done over the phone. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in fatigue in a longitudinal assessment, with week 6 as the key secondary endpoint.44

4. Quality of life with EuroQol 5 Dimensions, 5 Levels

Quality of life will be measured by project staff using the questionnaire EQ-5D-5L, on inclusion as a retrospective baseline (pre-fracture) defined as the weeks before admission, and at 6 and 12 weeks after intervention. The EQ-5D-5L questionnaire measures health-related quality of life across five dimensions, with scores ranging from 1 (no problems) to 5 (extreme problems). Higher scores indicate worse outcomes, reflecting greater health issues. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mean EQ-5D-5L in a longitudinal assessment, with week 6 as the key secondary endpoint.45

5. EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale 0–100

The EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale (EQ VAS) 0–100 (range) is a visual analogue scale where 0 represents the worst imaginable health state and 100 represents the best. Higher scores on the EQ VAS indicate a better health outcome. Measured as a retrospective baseline (pre-fracture) the weeks prior to admission and at 6 and 12 weeks after intervention. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mean EQ VAS 0–100 in a longitudinal assessment, with week 6 as the key secondary endpoint.46

6. Fear of falls

Fear of falls is measured using the Short Falls Efficacy Scale – International (Short-FES-I) for assessment of concern about falling during different activities measured at baseline and at 6 and 12 weeks after intervention. Short-FES-I scores range from 7 (no concern about falling) to 28 (severe concern about falling). Higher scores indicate greater concern about falling. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mean Short-FES-I in a longitudinal assessment, with week 6 as the key secondary endpoint.47

7. 30 s sit-to-stand test

The 30s-STS is measured by instructing the participants to fully stand and sit as many times as possible from a chair within 30 s, with correct form monitored by the tester. Lower scores indicate a worse outcome. It is measured at baseline, 6 and 12 weeks. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mean 30s-STS with week 6 as the key secondary endpoint.48

8. Activity of daily living

ADL is measured with the Barthel Index 20-point scale at baseline (as retrograde pre-fracture) and at 6 and 12 weeks after intervention. The Barthel Index 20 measures a patient’s level of independence in daily activities, with scores ranging from 0 (complete dependence) to 20 (complete independence). Higher scores indicate a better outcome, reflecting greater independence. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mean Barthel Index 20 in a longitudinal assessment, with week 6 as the key secondary endpoint.49

9. Pain

HF-related pain during activity will be evaluated with the verbal rating scale, 0–4 points, at baseline and at 6 and 12 weeks after intervention. A score of 0 indicates no pain, while a score of 4 represents unbearable or the worst imaginable pain. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mean pain in a longitudinal assessment, with week 6 as the key secondary endpoint.50

10. Days alive and at home up to 30 days

The DAH30 is determined using a combination of electronic medical records (obtaining length of stay) and direct participant inquiries at the 6-week follow-up. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mean DAH30.51 52

11. Serious adverse events

SAEs are defined following the ICH-GCP guidelines and will be assessed at the 6-week follow-up (see the ‘Safety and harms’ section). The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in the number of SAEs.53

12. Mortality

Ninety days after enrolment, survival status is obtained from the electronic patient records and the 30-day and 90-day mortality rates are calculated. The secondary endpoint is the between-group difference in mortality.

Explorative outcomes are given in table 5. The exploratory outcomes were selected because they are not included in the core outcome set and were intended to assess other additional potential effects of intravenous iron, including economic impact, physical function, cognitive function and iron availability. Outcomes B–G were selected because they may reflect a separate biological pathway that warrants separate investigation. Physical function will be assessed using outcomes B–D. The shared aims of the exploratory outcomes B–D are to apply more mechanistic measures of physical function, which may provide insight into the potential pathways through which intravenous iron influences functional mobility. A. Cost-effectiveness analysis

Cost-effectiveness analysis will be performed from a societal perspective, including cost of the FDI and infusion, hospital bed days, hospital readmissions, outpatient visits and costs related to lost productivity of the participants (financial loss). The analysis will be performed according to the guidelines for health economic evaluation from the Danish Health Technology Council.54 55 The Danish guideline recommends a societal perspective. The cost analysis will be made independent of the size of the clinical effects found. Costs of informal care will not be included because of the difficulties in identifying a relevant price for informal care.

The analysis will be based on data from the electronic patient records, data on the duration of participants’ sick leave and absence from work based on participant surveys and a government register of employment (Statistics Denmark).56 The main results from the analysis will be a comparison of the mean costs per participant in the intervention and the control group, the mean change in quality adjusted life years gained in the two groups (based on EQ-5D-5L) and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio.

B. Physical activity

The 24-hour physical activity will be measured with SENS motion activity monitor.57 The SENS motion monitor will be affixed at the 6 weeks follow-up. Data retrieved from the device is retrieved after 10 days. The monitor measures the amount of time spent lying and sitting (sedentary periods), standing and walking (upright time), and transitions. The average amount of time they are standing and walking is defined as ‘upright time’. The between-group difference in mean daily ‘upright time’ is calculated.

C. Hand grip strength

The hand grip strength is measured with a dynamometer at baseline and after 6 and 12 weeks.48 It will be assessed in kilograms, with a higher score representing better hand grip strength. The aim is to determine the effect of intravenous iron on hand grip strength by comparing longitudinal changes between groups, with the between-group difference in mean hand grip strength.

D. Knee extension strength

Knee extension strength of the fractured and non-fractured leg will be measured in N m/kg using a hand-held strap fixated dynamometer, with a higher score representing better knee extension strength.58 It is measured at the 6-week and 12-week follow-up.

The aim is to determine the effect of intravenous iron on knee extension strength by comparing longitudinal changes between groups, with the between-group difference in mean knee extension strength.

E. Cognition

Cognition will be measured by using Orientation-Memory-Concentration test (OMC) at baseline, weeks 6 and 12 post-intervention.59 The OMC test ranges from 0 to 28 points, with a score of 25–27 indicating no or minimal cognitive impairment, and a score of 0–7 indicating severe impairment. The aim is to determine the effect of intravenous iron on the OMC test by comparing longitudinal changes between groups, with the between-group difference in mean OMC score.

F. Ferritin levels as a measure of iron status

Ferritin is the preferred biomarker of iron stores in individuals without inflammatory conditions and is measured in ng/mL.60 Ferritin will be measured at baseline and at 6 and 12 weeks post intervention. The aim is to determine how intravenous iron therapy affects ferritin levels in comparison to the placebo group.

G. Transferrin saturation as a measure of iron status

Transferrin saturation is another biomarker of iron status that estimates the amount of bioavailable, circulating iron. It is clinically useful in individuals with inflammatory outcomes.60 Transferrin saturation will be measured at baseline and at the 6-week and 12-week follow-up. This outcome will evaluate how iron availability is affected by intravenous iron therapy compared with placebo.

Safety and harms

In line with current Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) harms guidance, all SAEs will be reported transparently for each treatment group, irrespective of frequency or statistical significance.61 Reporting will include the number of participants at risk, the number experiencing at least one adverse event, withdrawals due to harms, deaths and—where appropriate—the total number of events. For composite outcomes such as SAEs, all individual components will be specified and reported separately to support clinical interpretation and enable downstream evidence synthesis. Zero-event data will be reported for systematically assessed harms to minimise selective reporting and facilitate meta-analyses. SAEs are recorded until 6 weeks after intervention. This is a part of the monitoring safety in the trial but will also be reported as one of the secondary outcomes. Definitions, registration and reporting for SAEs, reactions and suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions are defined and followed from ICH-GCP and EU Clinical Trials Regulation 536/2014. The trial is categorised in the Clinical Trial Information System as ‘low risk’. Suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions are defined following the summary of product characteristics sections.

All sites are university hospitals and have all emergency equipment and staff capable of tackling any severe reactions. Staff are trained in the possible side effects from FDI and how to tackle them. Serious breaches affecting participant safety or trial performance are reported to the Danish Medicine Agency Ethics Committees.

Sample size calculation and power consideration

The trial will enrol and randomise 210 participants (approximately 105 per group). Under the prespecified assumptions used in the power calculation (two-sided α=0.05, mean difference=1 unit, SD=2; corresponding to the heuristic effect size of 0.5 if communicated as a standardised mean difference), a sample size of 100 per group yields approximately 94% power to detect the target between-group difference.62 Increasing to 105 per group provides a small additional margin of power and, importantly, protects against loss of efficiency due to expected missing outcome data in a HF population with repeated follow-up assessments (4, 6 and 12 weeks).

Thus, enrolling 210 participants (randomised 1:1) should ensure robust power for the prespecified primary estimand while allowing for anticipated attrition and incomplete longitudinal data, consistent with the registered enrolment target.

The sample size calculation is based on the assumption that intravenous iron will result in at least 1 NMS point better than that of the placebo administration. We chose the target difference corresponding to the minimal important difference to one unit in NMS and SD based on previous studies.62,64 An increase of transferrin saturation of 10% was associated with an increase of NMS in 0.74 in observational data.14 Administration of one dose of FDI is associated with an increase in transferrin saturation of around 15%.33

Statistical analysis

Primary analyses

The primary analyses will be based on the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, that is, on the full analysis set on all randomised individuals with an available baseline measure.65 The ITT principle asserts the effect of a treatment policy which is the planned treatment regimen, rather than the actual treatment given (ie, it is independent of treatment adherence and withdrawals). Accordingly, participants allocated to a treatment group (FDI and placebo) will be followed up, assessed and analysed as members of that group, irrespective of their adherence to the planned course of treatment (ie, independent of withdrawals and cross-over phenomena). Missing data in the ITT population will be handled indirectly and statistically modelled using repeated-measures linear mixed models (for continuous outcome measures). These models are considered valid if data are ‘missing at random’.66

All 95% CIs and p values will be two-sided. We will not apply explicit adjustments for multiplicity; rather, we will analyse the secondary outcomes in a prioritised order.

Statistical model: in this study, participants will be randomly assigned to two treatment groups (FDI vs placebo), and observations will be collected at 4 time points for the primary outcome measure (ie, at baseline (t=0) and 4, 6 and 12 weeks from intervention (figure 3). Contrasts between groups will be estimated based on repeated-measures analysis of covariance applied in mixed linear models (ie, 6 weeks from baseline being the primary endpoint assessment). The main statistical model consists of fixed effect factors (Strata; intracapsular and extracapsular HF), group, time and the interaction between group and time) and random effects (participant ID). Fixed effects define the expected values of the observations, and random effects define the variance and covariances of the observations. Accordingly, we will analyse continuous outcome measures (including NMS) as change from baseline using repeated measures mixed linear models, incorporating participants as random effects. These models will feature fixed effect factors for randomisation group, week and their corresponding interaction (group×week), while adjusting for baseline values and the stratification factors. Data from all available time points will be used. Results will be reported as least square means with SEs, and differences between least square means will be reported with two-sided 95% CIs. The between-group difference in the means of the groups, the primary endpoint, will be confirmed by a two-sided test with a significance level (α) of 0·05. Dichotomised responder analyses and harms will be estimated and presented as categorical data (as counts (%)) and groups compared using OR with 95% CIs.

To explore the presence of important contextual factors, we will perform subgroup analyses with comparison between subgroups and a p value for interaction.67 We will analyse the outcomes according to the following pre-exposure covariates:

Discussion

Despite a compelling pathophysiological rationale, we do not currently have the evidence to conclude whether intravenous iron should be a part of the standard treatment programme for patients with moderate anaemia after a HF. We expect that the IronHip trial will help fill this knowledge gap. There is a need for treatments that can give patients better recovery of functional mobility after a HF.

Many patients with a HF do not fully recover their functional mobility after the fracture, and the risk of permanent loss of function is particularly high in old and frail patients.4 5 9 The IronHip trial will test whether one high dose of intravenous iron improves functional mobility after HF surgery in older adults. A study on patients with HF found that improvement in functional mobility, measured with NMS, was associated with increased iron stores. At 2 months, a higher transferrin saturation was associated with an increase in NMS, with a 10% increase in transferrin saturation corresponding to a 0.74-point increase in NMS (95% CI 0.17 to 1.31).14 These findings provide a rationale for choosing NMS as the primary outcome.

We have chosen intravenous iron and not oral as iron stores can be replenished relatively easily and rapidly through intravenous iron. FDI makes it possible to administer a single high dose with low risk of side effects. Some orthogeriatric centres have started to give intravenous iron routinely because of the compelling pathophysiological rationale.18 However, the evidence supporting it is fragmented.22,25 It is plausible that a better iron availability in the weeks after the fracture can help recovery for several reasons. Iron binds oxygen both in erythrocytes and in muscle cells and plays a key role in delivering oxygen to the mitochondria for cellular energy metabolism. Therefore, iron deficiency can reduce the physical work capacity due to changes in the morphology and function of mitochondria in skeletal muscle.15 16 Iron is also a necessary element in the production of erythrocytes, and insufficient iron stores will therefore lead to anaemia.60 Both iron deficiency and anaemia limit the ability of a patient to exercise and are related to fatigue.60 All these pathophysiological mechanisms can cause reductions in functional mobility.

In the IronHip trial, we will test the postoperative administration of intravenous iron, in contrast to other studies that administer intravenous iron preoperatively.22 23 The rationale for postoperative infusion is that we may be able to better identify the subgroup of patients who lose erythrocytes and thereby iron during surgery.69 Many patients with HF are dehydrated on admission with concomitant haemoconcentration and masked anaemia.69 If intravenous iron is administered based on the admission haemoglobin, there is a risk of excluding some of the patients who need it most. In the postoperative period, we expect that the measured haemoglobin concentration reflects the available haemoglobin in the body.

We suspect that the patient-relevant effects of intravenous iron occur later than the increase in iron stores. Therefore, the timing of outcome measurement is paramount. After infusion of intravenous iron, the effect on biomarkers of iron stores is most prominent within the first 2 weeks.33 The effect on haemoglobin continues for up to 2 months and the effect on muscle cells does probably have a similar time trend.33 70 We expect that the physiological advantage of the intervention group compared with the placebo group may be highest around 6 weeks after the surgery. Therefore, we choose to measure the outcomes in this period, at timepoints where patients are not routinely seen in most orthogeriatric centres.

A high proportion of all patients will have absolute and functional iron deficiency after HFs due to blood loss from fracture, surgery and the inflammatory load.14 However, it is difficult to diagnose iron deficiency precisely after acute trauma because such trauma leads to inflammation, which has a strong impact on the biomarkers of iron metabolism.60 Therefore, we have chosen to test the intervention of intravenous iron in patients with moderate anaemia independently of biomarkers of iron metabolism.

In addition to the negative consequence for the patients with increased risk of complications and mortality, permanent loss of function is very costly to societies, since persons without independent mobility require more care.71,73 If intravenous iron improves mobility slightly after a HF, it might still be a cost-effective treatment.

Strengths of the study include use of the core outcome set for HFs, and a primary outcome which is relevant to the patient and society as a whole. NMS is easily captured via phone conversations, which ensures data completeness across timepoints and minimises loss to follow-up. Furthermore, the trial is a blinded, multicentre randomised controlled trial conducted across Denmark, enhancing the internal validity and generalisability. The study is adequately powered to assess the primary endpoint, incorporating an anticipated dropout rate of 15% and includes feasible home-based outcome assessments to further reduce attrition. Due to national identification numbers and a hospital system where all acute admissions are to public hospitals, all hospital-linked data and fatalities are recorded and ensure completeness on those outcomes. As the intervention involves a medicinal product, the trial is monitored by the ‘GCP unit’ in Denmark, ensuring compliance with regulatory standards, safeguarding participant safety and enhancing the quality and reliability of the data collected.

A limitation of the trial is that patients who are unable to provide consent on their own behalf are not eligible for this trial, and patients with severe dementia and those living in nursing homes also are excluded. The participants are, therefore, expected to be healthier than the average HF patients. This limits the generalisability of the trial. It is uncertain whether the effect of iron on mobility is lower in patients living in nursing homes as they have difficulty recovering mobility, but we will not be able to conclude on this based on the IronHip trial.

Ethics and dissemination

Strict adherence to the highest ethical standards is important following the Declaration of Helsinki and the GCP guidelines. The trial findings will be reported according to the CONSORT statement. The rigorous execution, including high scientific standards, are important as the result may change the clinical guidelines and patient care practices. The trial has been authorised by the Danish Medicines Agency and the Danish Medical Research Ethics Committee through the Clinical Trial Information System with the reference and approval number: 2024–5 15 116-42-00. The registered protocol can be found in the European Union Drug Regulating Authorities Clinical Trials Database. The statistical analysis plan will be uploaded before unblinding of participants. The protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trial (SPIRIT) guidelines.

Following ‘ICH guideline for GCP’, modifications are communicated to all primary investigators, and through them all project staff are informed.

All information related to this trial will be treated with strict confidentiality, and those responsible for the trial are bound by confidentiality agreements. Patients will be anonymised in all reported results. Data handling will comply with the General Data Protection Regulation, the Danish Data Protection Act and Danish Health Care Act. The trial will be covered by the Danish Act on Right to Complain and Receive Compensation within the Health Service.

The findings will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal. We will also disseminate our findings in layperson terms for a general public audience, which is a mandatory requirement according to the ‘Independent Research Fund Denmark’.

Primary and secondary outcomes will be reported in the primary publication from the trial. Explorative outcomes will be reported in separate publications. The sponsor will submit a summary of the results to the European Medicines Agency, including a non-technical layperson summary, within 1 year of the conclusion of the trial.
