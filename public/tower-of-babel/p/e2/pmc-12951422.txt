Mechanical thrombectomy for tandem internal carotid artery–middle cerebral artery occlusion in a patient with a persistent hypoglossal artery: illustrative case

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for large-vessel occlusion has become the standard treatment for acute ischemic stroke, contributing significantly to improved neurological outcomes through early reperfusion. Occasionally, patients with cerebrovascular anomalies are encountered, requiring individualized consideration in both the endovascular approach and procedural technique. A persistent hypoglossal artery (PHA) is a rare and significant embryonic carotid-basilar anastomosis. During fetal development, several transient anastomoses exist between the primitive internal carotid arteries (ICAs) and the longitudinal neural arteries that later form the vertebrobasilar system. The PHA is one of these embryonic vessels, which normally regresses by the 5th week of gestation. When it persists, it arises from the cervical ICA, passes through the hypoglossal canal, and connects to the basilar artery (BA).1,2 The reported prevalence ranges from 0.03% to 0.29%, making it a rare finding in clinical practice.3–5

In this report, we describe a case of acute ischemic stroke in which a functionally significant PHA was identified during MT, and we discuss its anatomical characteristics and the crucial technical considerations for endovascular treatment.

Illustrative Case

Patient History and Examination

The patient was a woman in her 80s with a history of thoracic aortic aneurysm repair, moderate aortic stenosis, nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, and chronic heart failure. She was on rivaroxaban (30 mg/day) for anticoagulation. She developed sudden-onset impaired consciousness and left hemiparesis at home and was urgently transported to our hospital, arriving 55 minutes after symptom onset. On arrival, her neurological findings included a Japan Coma Scale score of II-10 and a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 20. Specific deficits included impaired consciousness, dense left hemiparesis, dysarthria, and sensory extinction.

Imaging and Diagnosis

An immediate noncontrast head CT scan showed no signs of intracranial hemorrhage but revealed a hyperdense middle cerebral artery (MCA) sign in the distal M1 segment of the right MCA, indicative of a thrombus (Fig. 1A). The Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score was assessed as 10. Suspecting acute ischemic stroke, we performed CT perfusion imaging using Vitrea software (Canon Medical Systems Corporation) to evaluate the ischemic core and penumbra. The imaging demonstrated a large penumbra area of 169 mL and a relatively small ischemic core of 22.7 mL, yielding a mismatch ratio of 8.46, which strongly favored intervention. Importantly, the penumbra area was not confined to the right MCA territory but also involved bilateral posterior cerebral artery (PCA) territories (Fig. 1B and C). CT angiography (CTA) confirmed the absence of visualization of the right ICA. The right M1 segment was faintly opacified via collateral flow from the anterior communicating artery, but there was no vessel visualization beyond the distal M1 segment, confirming a tandem lesion involving both the cervical ICA and the intracranial MCA (Fig. 1D). Critically, the CTA also identified a PHA originating from the right ICA and passing through the right hypoglossal canal (Fig. 1E). Treatment and Procedure

Based on the clinical and imaging findings, intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase was initiated, and the patient was immediately transferred to the angiography suite for MT. The preoperative CT identified a low origin of the brachiocephalic artery, consistent with a type 3 aortic arch,6 which prompted the selection of a right brachial artery approach for more stable catheter access.

After placement of a 4-Fr sheath in the right brachial artery, an 8-Fr OPTIMO EPD guiding catheter (Tokai Medical Products) was inserted using a dedicated TMP dilator (Tokai Medical Products). An 8-Fr OPTIMO EPD guiding catheter, a 6-Fr Simmons catheter (Technocrat Corporation), and a 0.035-inch guidewire (Terumo Corporation) were navigated into the right ICA. Angiography of the right ICA demonstrated occlusion at the cervical segment, with visualization of the posterior circulation via the PHA (Fig. 2A and B). Initially, an 8-Fr OPTIMO catheter was positioned in the right ICA proximal to the PHA origin, and balloon inflation was performed. Immediately, transient bradycardia occurred, attributed to brainstem ischemia secondary to PHA flow interruption. Consequently, the balloon was deflated and repositioned distal to the origin of the PHA. A React 71 aspiration catheter (Medtronic), Phenom 21 microcatheter (Medtronic), and CHIKAI Nexus 0.014-inch guidewire (Asahi Intecc) were used to cross the M1 lesion. The microcatheter was successfully navigated into the superior division of the right M2 segment, with angiography confirming lesion crossing (Fig. 2C and D). An EMBOTRAP III (6.5 mm × 45 mm) stent retriever (Cerenovus) was deployed across the thrombus from the distal MCA, and an aspiration catheter was positioned proximal to the ICA occlusion. Thrombectomy was then performed in a single pass using the aspiration-retriever technique for stroke (ARTS). A large red thrombus was retrieved within the stent. Postthrombectomy angiography confirmed recanalization of the right ICA and the right M1 segment, although the inferior division of the right M2 remained occluded. Using the same system, the microcatheter was navigated into the inferior division of the M2 segment, and another EMBOTRAP III was deployed and retrieved. Final angiography confirmed recanalization of the M2 inferior division. Although one distal branch exhibited slow flow, no further intervention was performed given the noneloquent territory and the potential risks of further manipulation. Recanalization of the occluded segment was achieved in two passes. On right internal carotid angiography, slow flow was observed in part of the M2–3 branches during the venous phase; therefore, the final modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) score was assessed as grade 2b (Fig. 3A and B [arterial phase], Fig. 3C and D [venous phase]). On final right ICA angiography, no apparent reduction in opacification was observed in the major arteries of the posterior circulation visualized via the PHA (Fig. 3E and F). Postoperative Course and Outcome

The patient’s neurological status improved rapidly following the procedure, with marked recovery in her level of consciousness and left-sided motor function. Postoperative MRI revealed acute infarcts in the right MCA territory as expected, but also scattered infarcts in both PCA territories, the cerebellum, the basal ganglia, and the thalamus (Fig. 4A–C). MR angiography revealed hypoplasia of the right vertebral artery (VA). In addition, the left VA was smaller in caliber compared to the PHA, suggesting that the posterior circulation was largely dependent on the PHA (Fig. 4D). Intracranial MR angiography also demonstrated visualization of the BA via the PHA (Fig. 4E). A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a mobile thrombus in the left atrial appendage, leading to a definitive diagnosis of cardiogenic embolic stroke. Anticoagulation therapy was changed to apixaban (5 mg twice daily). At the final follow-up, the patient’s NIHSS score was 2, attributable to mild disorientation and a partial visual field defect, and her modified Rankin Scale score was 3. She was transferred for rehabilitation on the 28th hospital day. Discussion

Observations

This case involved a patient with acute large-vessel occlusion due to a tandem lesion consisting of right ICA occlusion at the carotid canal and right MCA occlusion. MT resulted in successful recanalization with an mTICI grade of 2b. The most noteworthy finding was the presence of a PHA, visualized during right ICA angiography, which is thought to have been the primary supply to the entire vertebrobasilar system. The PHA is an embryonic carotid-basilar anastomosis that arises from the ICA and traverses the hypoglossal canal to connect to the VA. In cases in which it persists, it can serve as a major, and sometimes sole, collateral pathway to the posterior circulation, especially in the setting of VA hypoplasia or occlusion.7–10 Because the PHA originates from a relatively high position of the cervical segment of the ICA, at the level of the first to third cervical vertebrae, its identification using carotid ultrasonography is challenging.11 Therefore, careful evaluation using contrast-enhanced CT or MRI is essential to assess both the structural and functional aspects of such vascular anomalies.

Retrospectively, the preoperative perfusion CT demonstrated an extensive penumbra not only in the right MCA territory but also in both PCA territories. Although this finding initially appeared incongruous—given that such a wide ischemic area is unusual for an anterior circulation occlusion—it might have raised suspicion for a persistent primitive artery, such as a PHA. A more careful review of the CTA and source images at that point could potentially have led to the recognition of the PHA before the procedure.

In our case, the functional significance of the PHA became strikingly apparent during the procedure. When the balloon-guiding catheter (BGC) was positioned proximal to the PHA origin in the ICA, the patient experienced transient bradycardia. After deflating the BGC and repositioning it distal to the origin of the PHA, this instability resolved rapidly. This event strongly suggests that the PHA was providing critical perfusion to the posterior circulation, and that even temporary flow interruption during catheter manipulation had profound effects on both cerebral and systemic circulation. Previous reports have documented cases in which the PHA was the dominant supply route to the posterior circulation, and its occlusion led to brainstem or cerebellar infarction.7,11

A literature review identified 6 reported cases of MT performed in patients with PHA (Table 1).12–17 Etiology was categorized as cardiogenic (n = 4) and atherothrombotic (n = 2). Among all cases, 4 experienced BA occlusions, leading to elevated NIHSS scores associated with altered consciousness. Of these 4 cases, successful recanalization was achieved in 3 via thrombectomy through the ICA-PHA pathway, a strategy used because their VAs were hypoplastic. Conversely, the presence of a PHA increases the likelihood of ischemia extending beyond the typical vascular territory. Lessons

This case highlights the clinical importance of recognizing a PHA in patients undergoing endovascular treatment for acute ischemic stroke. When a PHA serves as the primary or sole arterial supply to the posterior circulation, even transient flow interruption can result in severe hemodynamic instability and neurological injury. Meticulous preoperative imaging assessment, including careful evaluation of perfusion patterns and source images, is essential, particularly when ischemic findings appear atypical for an anterior circulation occlusion. Furthermore, careful intraoperative planning and appropriate catheter positioning aimed at preserving flow through anomalous vessels are critical to ensuring procedural safety. Early recognition and appropriate management of such vascular anomalies may help prevent posterior circulation ischemia and improve clinical outcomes.
