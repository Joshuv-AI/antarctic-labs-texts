Optimization of imaging parameters for Yttrium-90 bremsstrahlung emission computed tomography after selective internal radiotherapy
Authors: Xin Xie, Qi Wang, Yuemin Zhang, Hui Xu, Lulu Yang, Aimin Yang, Xin Zheng, Huasi Hu, Jianjun Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYttrium-90 labeled microspheres based selective internal radiotherapy (90Y-SIRT) is an established local-regional therapy for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) which demonstrating significant survival improvements in multiple clinical trials. Post therapeutic 90Y imaging is essential for in vivo 90Y microspheres biodistribution evaluation and absorbed dose estimation. We aim to study the effects of 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging parameters including energy window center, width and acquisition time on the quality of 90Y SPECT/CT to provide optimal imaging conditions after 90Y-SIRT.ResultsUsing the NEMA IEC NU2 body phantom and Siemens Symbia™ T16 SPECT/CT scanner equipped with a high-energy general-purpose collimator (HEGPC), seven energy window settings and two acquisition times (20s / frame and 40s / frame) were investigated and analyzed. 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging with 94 keV ± 40% energy window limits and 20s / frame acquisition time demonstrated the highest contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) and contrast recovery coefficient (QH). Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. Several therapies are currently used in HCC clinical practice, including surgical resection, external beam radiotherapy, systemic intravenous chemotherapy, thermal liver ablation (radiofrequency or microwave) and hepatic arterial embolization with or without hepatic arterial chemotherapy [2–4]. Surgery is the primary treatment for HCC, but some patients are diagnosed with advanced HCC and are unable to receive curative surgery, indicating that other therapeutic options are needed for tumor control [5, 6]. First introduced in 1965 as a novel treatment approach, termed as Yttrium-90 (90Y) based selective internal therapy (90Y-SIRT), was developed using an interventional method to deliver radioactive 90Y loaded microspheres through the hepatic artery into the tumor foci. This method involves the delivery of a sustained high dose of radiation to the tumor cells while keeping radiation exposure to the adjacent nontumoral liver tissue within acceptable organ dose limits [7]. 90Y-SIRT has been used clinically for more than two decades and is now considered an established therapeutic option for unresectable primary HCC and liver metastases [8–11].

Postoperative imaging of 90Y-SIRT is essential both for determining the distribution of 90Y loaded microsphere and dosimetry calculations, which can be achieved by bremsstrahlung emission computed tomography (BECT) or PET/CT imaging [12, 13]. 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging utilizes the X-rays generated when high-energy β-particles emitted during 90Y decay interact with the atomic nuclei in surrounding tissue, which converts the kinetic energy of 90Y β-particles to a continuous stream of bremsstrahlung X-ray photons [14, 15]. Owing to the continuous and broad energy distribution of bremsstrahlung X-rays without a well-defined photopeak, 90Y planar and SPECT imaging are generally considered to have low spatial resolution. The image quality varies depending on the energy window center and width used, which makes it one of the most challenging problems in nuclear medicine imaging [16]. Compared with SPECT-based 90Y BECT imaging, PET/CT imaging is more precise and quantitative [17]. However, PET/CT imaging is more expensive than 90Y BECT imaging. 90Y BECT / CT offers advantages of wider availability and lower cost for lesion localization and observation of gastrointestinal and pulmonary shunts in patients with 90Y-SIRT, which explains why this study focuses on situations where only SPECT acquisition is available.

SPECT systems used in clinical nuclear imaging are designed primarily for detecting γ-emitting radionuclides. Collimators function as mechanical filters that define the direction of incident photons and select photons based on their energy characteristics through septal penetration properties [18]. Studies indicate that the quality of 90Y bremsstrahlung images depends on the energy window settings and collimator type [19, 20]. High-energy photons are more likely to penetrate the septa of collimators designed for lower energies, resulting in image degradation. For 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging, a high-energy general-purpose collimator (HEGPC) with a thicker septum provides better rejection of high-energy photons that would otherwise penetrate the collimator and contribute to image noise [20, 21]. In our previous study, we compared the sensitivity, percentage of the source counts in the useful field of view, and the target-background ratio of the low-energy high-resolution collimator (LEHRC) and the HEGPC for different energy windows of 90Y bremsstrahlung planar imaging and reported that the 90Y bremsstrahlung image quality of HEGPC was superior to that of LEHRC, with 94 keV ± 40% being the optimal energy window [22]. In the current study, we investigated the effects of the energy window and acquisition time on the quality of 90Y BECT/CT imaging using HEGPC. The purpose of this series of studies is to establish optimal acquisition parameters for 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging and to provide practical guidance for posttherapy imaging of 90Y-SIRT.

Materials and methods

Detection system and phantom description

A Symbia™ T16 SPECT/CT scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a high-energy parallel-hole collimator (HEGPC) was used to acquire images of the NEMA IEC NU2 Body Phantom (Data Spectrum Corporation, USA). The 90YCl3 solution used in this study was purchased from New Radiomedicine Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China), and the radioactivity was measured and calibrated by radioactivity calibrator (CRC-55tR, Capintec Inc., USA). A NEMA IEC NU2 body phantom comprising six fillable spheres containing 90YCl3 solution was used for this study.

90Y bremsstrahlung energy windows

The NEMA IEC NU2 phantom contained six spherical inserts with inner diameters of 37, 28, 22, 17, 13, and 10 mm. These spheres were filled with 90YCl3 solution at a total activity of 1.1248 GBq. The sphere-to-background radioactive concentration ratio was 10.64 : 1 (1124.80 kBq/mL : 105.73 kBq/mL). The lung insert of the phantom used to evaluate lung error () was not filled with 90YCl3 solution.

Based on our previous 90Y planar imaging studies, seven energy windows were selected for SPECT/CT imaging [22]. The energy window center and width settings were 90 keV ± 10% (81–99 keV), 100 keV ± 10% (90–110 keV), 90 keV ± 20% (72–108 keV), 110 keV ± 20% (88–132 keV), 80 keV ± 30% (56–104 keV), 100 keV ± 30% (70–130 keV) and 94 keV ± 40% (56.4-131.6 keV) [22]. These energy windows were chosen to evaluate the effects of varying window widths and centers on bremsstrahlung image quality, considering the continuous bremsstrahlung spectrum characteristics of 90Y [23]. The selection included both narrower windows (± 10%, ± 20%) that may reduce the scatter contribution but limit sensitivity, and wider windows (± 30%, ± 40%) that increase sensitivity while potentially including more scattered photons.

90Y BECT/CT imaging

For each scan, 32 projections were acquired in step and shoot mode, with an angular step of 5.6° in the 360° range and times per projection of 20 and 40 s. A body-contour orbit was used to keep the camera detectors close to the phantom. SPECT data were recorded using an image matrix of 128 × 128 with a pixel size of 4.42 mm. SPECT image reconstruction was performed using an iterative ordered-subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm with 8 iterations and 4 subsets (32 equivalent updates), followed by post-reconstruction Gaussian filtering with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 8.4 mm. These reconstruction parameters were selected based on the basis of the manufacturer’s recommendations and previous phantom studies demonstrating a balance between contrast recovery and noise characteristics for bremsstrahlung imaging [14, 24, 25]. Higher iteration numbers generally improve contrast recovery but increase image noise, whereas the Gaussian filter helps to suppress high-frequency noise, which is particularly prominent in bremsstrahlung imaging because of the low photon yield and continuous energy spectrum [26, 27]. The same machine CT scanning parameters were used: Helical mode was used with a tube voltage of 100 kV and a current of 5 mA, and the matrix size was set to 512 × 512. A CT scan was performed for attenuation correction (AC) and to outline the region of interest (ROI).

Statistical analysis

Owing to the small sample size, normality cannot be assumed. Therefore, a nonparametric Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for paired data to analyze the significant statistical differences in contrast percentage and CNR at different energy window settings and acquisition times. All tests were conducted at a significance level of 0.05.

Discussion

Our study investigated the optimal energy window settings and acquisition times for 90Y BECT/CT imaging using an HEGPC (Table S6). Our results demonstrate that the energy window of 94 keV ± 40% (56.4-131.6 keV) with an acquisition time of 20 s/frame provides the highest CNR among all tested parameters, which is consistent with our previous findings in planar imaging [18]. To assess image quality in posttherapy 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging, we employed COV, QH, ΔClung, and CNR as metrics to evaluate background noise variation, contrast recovery, lung spillover error, and the ability to distinguish target tissue from background noise, respectively. Our results revealed that for each energy window setting using HEGPC, COV, QH, and ΔClung showed relatively small variations, whereas the CNR demonstrated greater variability across different parameters (Figs. 1 and 2 and Tables S1, S2). The CNR was higher for energy windows of 94 keV ± 40% (56.4-131.6 keV) and 80 keV ± 30% (56–104 keV) at an acquisition time of 20 s, and for 94 keV ± 40% (56.4-131.6 keV) and 100 keV ± 30% (70–130 keV) at 40 s, respectively.

Although 90Y undergoes a minor positron-emitting decay (e+) branch occurring in only 31.86 ± 0.47 per million decays, it has traditionally been considered a pure β−-emitter (e−) [28]. Upon decay, 90Y emits high-speed electrons (β− particles), which cannot be imaged directly with conventional γ- scintillators because of the absence of primary photon emissions. As these fast-moving β− particles traverse adjacent tissues and undergo deceleration upon interaction with atomic nuclei or other charged particles, a portion of or all of their kinetic energy is converted into bremsstrahlung X-rays, characterized by a continuous energy spectrum [21]. Although bremsstrahlung X-rays and γ-rays are both forms of photon radiation, they are distinguished by their origin: X-rays are generated extranuclearly through electron interactions, whereas γ-rays originate from intranuclear transitions [29].

Currently, bremsstrahlung-emitting imaging, comprising whole-body planar imaging combined with SPECT/CT, has become one of the most valuable procedures after 90Y-SIRT because of the real-time in vivo imaging of 90Y microspheres biodistribution. Acquiring high quality bremsstrahlung images is essential for accurate post-operative 90Y validation via SPECT imaging. Multiple factors adversely affect 90Y bremsstrahlung image quality, including tissue attenuation, varying count rates of bremsstrahlung photons, limited spatial resolution, the continuous and broad emission spectrum of 90Y bremsstrahlung radiation, collimator selection, and photon energy window settings [30, 31]. Alternatively, PET/CT imaging which uses the trace positron emissions from the 90Y decay represents another viable option for post 90Y-SIRT assessment, offering critical clinical information regarding hepatic 90Y distribution and extrahepatic shunting [7, 32]. Notably, Anand et al. reported excellent concordance between 90Y PET/CT and 90Y BECT/CT imaging, with exact agreement observed in 97.14% of cases (34/35 cases), indicating the postoperative BECT/CT imaging may serve as a more widely accessible and cost-effective option for routine clinical practice [33].

To evaluate the image quality of postoperative 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging, COV, QH, ΔClung, and CNR were used to assess target detectability. These parameters reflect the coefficient of variation of background noise, contrast recovery capability, residual counts in the lung insert (representing extrahepatic spillover), and the ability to distinguish target activity from background noise, respectively. The results of our study revealed that for each energy window center and width of the HEGPC, COV, QH and lung error were less variable, whereas the CNR was more variable (Table 1; Fig. 1). The CNR was higher for energy window limits of 94 keV ± 40%, 80 keV ± 30% and 94 keV ± 40%, 100 keV ± 30% at acquisition times of 20 s and 40 s respectively. Therefore, an energy window limit of 94 keV ± 40% is the optimal acquisition condition for BECT/CT imaging of HEGPC, which is consistent with our previous findings in planar imaging [22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the optimal hole length and diameter of the 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging collimator are 84 mm and 3.5 mm respectively [15], which are longer than those of the HEGPC used in this study, suggesting that the high energy collimator is more suitable for 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging. The bremsstrahlung X-ray spectrum is continuous, leading to septal penetration and scattering, resulting in poor image quality [34]. To maximize sensitivity, SPECT images of bremsstrahlung need to be acquired within wide energy window limits, which is consistent with our study indicating that 90Y bremsstrahlung images with a wider energy window width (such as ± 30% or ± 40%) lead to better image quality [35]. In addition, we also obtained another unexpected result: extending the image acquisition time (40 s / frame) did not necessarily help to improve the image quality of the fast bremsstrahlung X-ray detection (20 s / frame).

Notably, the current study demonstrated no significant differences in COV, QH, and ΔClung between acquisition times of 20 s and 40 s per projection. However, the CNR was higher at the 20 s acquisition time than at 40 s acquisition time for most energy windows, suggesting that prolonging the acquisition time for 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging may not improve image quality and could decrease it (Fig. 1, Table S1). This counterintuitive finding has not been reported previously reported in the literature and warrants further investigation.

We hypothesize that this phenomenon may be related to the accumulation of scattered photons and noise over longer acquisition times. In bremsstrahlung imaging, compared with conventional γ-ray imaging, the continuous X-ray spectrum results in a greater proportion of scattered photons [21, 36]. With wider energy windows (such as ± 40%), longer acquisition times may lead to proportionally greater accumulation of scatter-related noise relative to the useful signal from the target region. Additionally, detector characteristics and count rate limitations may contribute to this effect [37]. However, we emphasize that this observation should be considered preliminary, as we compared only two acquisition times (20 s and 40 s). Further studies with multiple acquisition time points and quantitative scatter analysis are needed to fully characterize the relationship between the acquisition time and image quality in 90Y BECT imaging. In comparison with conventional SPECT imaging of γ-emitting radionuclides, which typically uses a single photopeak with a window width of approximately ± 10% and where Compton scattering represents a relatively small fraction of detected events, 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging presents unique challenges because of its continuous spectrum without a well-defined photopeak. Studies have shown that approximately 23,000 bremsstrahlung photons with energies greater than 50 keV per MBq are produced by the interaction of the β-particle with tissue [38]. Notably, however, the apparent spectral feature observed at approximately 72–75 keV in bremsstrahlung spectra acquired with lead-based collimators corresponds to the lead K-shell X-ray fluorescence peaks (Kα1 at 74.97 keV and Kα2 at 72.80 keV), which arise from the photoelectric interactions of bremsstrahlung photons with the lead septum of the collimator [22, 39]. Consistent with our previous study of 90Y planar scintigraphy, we found the energy centers of 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging were at 75.3 keV and 72.7 keV with LEHRC and HEGPC, respectively. However, the energy center of 90Y was 83.2 keV without any collimators, indicating the spectral signal between 72 and 75 keV was from the lead collimator [22]. To improve the sensitivity of BECT imaging, a wider energy window (± 40%) was used in the current study, resulting in more scattered photons and noise in the acquired data. Similarly, we found a remarkable decrease in image quality and the CNR with increasing 90Y bremsstrahlung acquisition time, which may have been caused by the accumulation of noise information such as scattered photons or lead K-shell X-ray fluorescence signals compared with the useful intratumoral emitting bremsstrahlung signal, indicating that a proper acquisition time needs to be considered during postoperative 90Y BECT/CT.

Anand et al. revealed that the HEGPC gave better resolution than the medium-energy general purpose collimator (MEGPC) or LEHRC did, suggesting that the HEGPC is preferable for bremsstrahlung imaging, especially if the count rate is sufficiently high, which is consistent with our current study [21, 33, 40, 41]. However, several limitations exist in our study. An investigation of 90Y bremsstrahlung imaging with MEGP collimators is lacking; therefore our results of this study are applicable only to LEHRC and HEGPC SPECT/CT. This study was only a preliminary investigation of the acquisition time of 90Y BECT imaging, and the unexpected results obtained by comparing the image quality for the acquisition time of 20 s with 40 s suggesting that additional research is needed to determine the optimal acquisition time for 90Y bremsstrahlung emitting imaging. Finally, the current phantom-based study presents challenges for potential clinical translation, such as real-time respiratory motion, heterogeneous tissue attenuation, and patient body habitus, which can cause 90Y BECT image blurring and may affect quantification accuracy. Future clinical validation studies comparing image quality metrics in patient populations would be valuable for confirming these phantom-based recommendations.

Conclusion

90Y-SIRT is an established internal radiotherapy approach involving the trans-arterial administration of 90Y microspheres for the treatment of unresectable primary HCC or metastatic liver cancer. Postoperative imaging is necessary to assess the distribution of 90Y microspheres and therapeutic response. In this study, we investigated the optimal energy window center, width and acquisition time for 90Y BECT imaging by using the NEMA IEC NU2 phantom. The results show that for HEGPC, 94 keV ± 40% (56.4–131.6 keV) is the optimal energy window center and width for 90Y BECT imaging, followed by 80 keV ± 30% (56–104 keV). In addition, we also found that the image quality of 90Y BECT imaging with an acquisition time of 20 s/frame is better than that with an acquisition of 40 s/frame, which might be caused by invalid signal accumulation of lead collimator X-ray fluorescence included in wide energy width and needs further validation in the future. In general, this study provides optimal BECT imaging conditions for 90Y-SIRT patients and helps to elucidate the real-time distribution of 90Y microspheres in vivo.
