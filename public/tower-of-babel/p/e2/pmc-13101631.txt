From screening to sequencing: Investigating the monoethylene glycol (MEG) biodegradation by Stutzerimonas stutzeri EX72 strain to optimize Oil and Gas industrial produced water treatment
Authors: Mohammad Yousaf Ashfaq, Gaurav Thareja, Farhan A. Khan, Sara Al-Marri, Haneen I. Eldos, Ismail Al-Shaikh, Ali A. Sultan, Karsten Suhre, Maryam Abdulla, Mohsin M. Raja, Suhur Saeed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monoethylene glycol (MEG) is widely used as a hydrate inhibitor in produced water operations; however, its presence complicates treatment processes by increasing chemical oxygen demand (COD) and posing potential environmental risks to receiving water bodies. This study aimed to isolate and identify efficient MEG-degrading bacteria through systematic screening, followed by whole genome sequencing (WGS) of the top-performing strain to elucidate its biodegradation potential and genetic basis. Among 14 bacterial strains representing 9 genera, Stutzerimonas stutzeri EX72 exhibited the highest degradation performance, achieving a 62.28% reduction in COD within 10 days. Detailed kinetic experiments demonstrated near-complete removal of MEG from the medium within 14 days, confirmed by Gas Chromatography-Flame Ionization Detection (GC-FID). Integrated COD, total organic carbon (TOC), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), and biomass measurements revealed substantial mineralization of MEG, with apparent biomass yields of 0.167–0.188 g biomass per g of MEG-C consumed, indicating efficient carbon partitioning into both biomass formation and respiration. WGS revealed that EX72 encodes pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ)-dependent alcohol dehydrogenases (ADH) and aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDH) responsible for the initial oxidation of MEG to glycolate, followed by assimilation via the glycerate pathway. Phylogenetic and synteny analyses showed that these enzymes cluster with environmental Stutzerimonas/Pseudomonas lineages and possess conserved genomic organization, supporting a conserved catalytic role in MEG oxidation that is mechanistically analogous, though evolutionarily distinct, from well-characterized glycol-degrading systems. Notably, two ADHs (SS.57032 and SS.63794) exhibited 80.58% and 84.91% sequence identity to those encoded by the gene pedH from Pseudomonas putida KT2440 and the ExaA gene from Pseudomonas aeruginosa, respectively. Overall, this study advances understanding of microbial MEG biodegradation in produced water systems and establishes S. stutzeri EX72 as a strong candidate for biological MEG removal, providing a foundation for future investigations into pathway regulation and metabolic fate.

Introduction

Oil and Gas industry operations generate large volumes of produced water [1]. Produced water consists of a mixture of injection water used for well stimulation and recovery of hydrocarbons, and formation water, which is the naturally occurring brine entrained in wells and discharged during oil and gas extraction [2,3]. For a water-scarce country like Qatar, the large volumes of untreated produced water present an opportunity for efficient treatment, recycle within the system, or reuse for non-potable purposes like landscaping. However, produced water typically contains complex mixtures of organic compounds, dissolved salts, and trace metals. Among these constituents, process chemicals such as monoethylene glycol (MEG) can substantially increase the chemical oxygen demand (COD) and complicate treatment processes [4,5].

MEG is a colorless, odorless dihydric alcohol characterized by high viscosity, low volatility, and strong hygroscopicity, properties that support its widespread use in plastics manufacturing, antifreeze formulations, and natural gas processing [6]. In the petroleum industry, MEG is continuously injected as a thermodynamic hydrate inhibitor to prevent the formation of gas hydrates that may obstruct pipelines and disrupt production operations [7]. Once introduced, MEG becomes entrained in produced water often at considerable concentrations where it significantly elevates COD and contributes to the overall organic load [8]. Because of its high miscibility and resistance to volatilization, it persists in wastewater during physical or mechanical treatment stages. Hence, its widespread use, persistence, and impact on treatment performance make MEG a key organic contaminant in produced water and highlight the need for effective removal or degradation strategies.

Several technologies have been evaluated for MEG removal from industrial wastewater. Physicochemical methods, including advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), and ozonation combined with catalysts, have shown varying degrees of success [[8], [9], [10]]. Teamkao and Thiravetyan [10] demonstrated that the burhead plant Echinodorus cordifolius can effectively phytoremediate MEG-contaminated synthetic wastewater, removing about 2000 mg/L of MEG within 18 days and reducing COD to below 120 mg/L. Biodegradation of MEG by highly adapted environmental microbiota presents a promising technological approach for its removal from produced water, yet only a limited number of studies have explored microbial MEG degradation. For example, Esmaeili and Loghmani [11] used Aspergillus tubingensis, a common soil‑dwelling fungal strain, to remove MEG from gas‑field wastewater. After 10 days of incubation, the MEG concentration was decreased from 400 to 244 mg/L, indicating that the strain biodegraded 39% of the MEG. In another study, Ghogare and Gupta [7], reported five microorganisms, Alcaligens faecalis, Bacillus niacin, Oliptrichum macrosporum, Aspergillus terreus, and Streptomyces sp., that were capable of using MEG as a carbon and energy source. Among them, Oligotrichum macrosporum showed the highest degradation efficiency at 70.21%. These studies demonstrate the feasibility of microbial MEG degradation but also indicate that the diversity and efficiency of naturally occurring MEG-degrading microbes remain poorly understood.

The Stutzerimonas (formerly Pseudomonas stutzeri) genus is of particular interest due to its metabolic versatility, environmental adaptability, and documented capability to degrade a wide range of hydrocarbons and industrial chemicals [12,13]. Members of this genus typically possess robust oxidative pathways, genetic flexibility, and strong environmental resilience, making them promising candidates for degrading recalcitrant organic contaminants [14]. Despite these favorable traits, the MEG-degrading potential of Stutzerimonas species has not been systematically investigated.

In this study, non-halophilic bacterial strains previously isolated from diverse environmental samples were screened for MEG utilization as a sole carbon and energy source. The screening experiments, together with molecular identification of the strains using 16S rRNA, led to the selection of Stutzerimonas genus for detailed characterization through biodegradation kinetics, biomass yield and genomic analyses. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) enabled prediction of enzymes and metabolic steps potentially responsible for MEG degradation. By integrating phenotypic screening with genomic analysis, this research expands current understanding of MEG biodegradation and highlights the potential of a Stutzerimonas strain as an effective MEG-degrading microorganism.

Materials and methods

Selection of bacterial strains for MEG biodegradation

Various halophilic and non-halophilic bacterial strains had previously been isolated from contaminated wastewater and soil during earlier research [12,15]. Such environments are typically rich in organic contaminants and are therefore considered promising sources of microorganisms with diverse metabolic capabilities [16,17]. For the present study, 14 non-halophilic bacterial strains were selected for exploratory screening of MEG biodegradation. This selection was based on their ability to grow in low-salinity mineral salt medium (MSM), which is essential for reliable COD-based assessment of MEG biodegradation, as COD measurements are sensitive to elevated chloride concentrations [18,19].

At the time of selection, the taxonomic identity of these strains was unknown, and only a limited number had previously been evaluated for hydrocarbon degradation [15]. Consequently, inclusion of all 14 strains enabled a broad and unbiased assessment of MEG-degrading potential among isolates originating from contaminated environments. Preserved isolates were revived from a microbial bank maintained at −80 °C and cultured in freshly autoclaved Luria-Bertani (LB) medium. The LB medium contained (g/L): peptone (10), yeast extract (5), sodium chloride (10), and agar (15) [12].

Molecular identification of the isolates

For bacterial identification, the 16S rRNA gene sequencing was employed. Freshly prepared pure cultures of the isolates were submitted to Weill Cornell Medicine – Qatar for 16S rRNA-based identification. Genomic DNA was extracted out using DNeasy UltraClean Microbial Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was performed using commercially available primers. The PCR products were resolved on an agarose gel, and ∼592 bp amplicons were excised and purified using QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Sanger sequencing was carried using an ABI 3130xl genetic analyzer, and strain identification was performed using the BLASTN tool against the NCBI database [12].

WGS, de novo assembly, and genome annotation

Genomic DNA extracted from the bacterial isolate of interest (EX72) was sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform in a paired-end 150 bp configuration (300 cycles). Briefly, 500 ng of genomic DNA was fragmented to a size range of 200–700 bp using an E220 ultrasonicator (Covaris, Inc.), Library preparation was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, E7645S). The quality and quantity of the final Illumina libraries were assessed using a Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent Technologies) and the KAPA Library Quantification Kit for Illumina (Roche, 07960140001) prior to sequencing.

The sequencing data was assembled using the latest version of NCBI’s Read Assembly and Annotation Pipeline Tool (RAPT) with default parameters [20]. Genome annotation was performed using the PATRIC database available through the BV-BRC server to identify genes of interest and classify them based on functional subsystems [21]. In addition, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database was consulted for pathway analysis, and BLASTN tool was used to identify the closest homologs of genes of interest in EX72.

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses

Phylogenetic analysis of key catabolic enzymes was also conducted using the PATRIC platform. Briefly, amino acid sequences of the target enzymes were queried against the PATRIC database using BLASTP. The top-matching sequences, together with amino acid sequences from reference organisms (Pseudomonas putida KT2440, Escherichia coli K-12 strain) were selected for multiple sequence alignment (MSA) using the MAFFT algorithm. The resulting alignment was imported into the Gene/Protein Tree tool in PATRIC for phylogenetic tree construction using RAxML as the tree building algorithm with the LG substitution model.

Synteny analysis of the target enzymes were performed using the Compare Genome Viewer tool in PATRIC with the following parameters: region size of 10,000 bp, number of genomes set to 5, comparison method based on PATRIC cross-genus families, (PGfams), and inclusion of all available genomes. The resulting phylogenetic trees were further refined and visualized using Archaeopteryx.js (version 2.1.1.a1).

Media preparation for MEG biodegradation study

MSM medium, previously established for studying substrate biodegradation [15], was used to screen bacterial isolates for MEG biodegradation. The MSM contained the following components (g/L): NH4NO3 (4), Na2HPO4 (2), KH2PO4 (0.53), K2SO4 (0.17), and MgSO4·7H2O (0.1) [15,22]. The presence of phosphate salts (Na2HPO4, and KH2PO4) provided sufficient buffering capacity to maintain pH stability during the incubation period, as commonly reported for mineral media used in biodegradation studies [23].

A trace element solution was prepared as (g/L): EDTA (0.10), ZnSO4, (0.042), MnSO4 (0.178), H3BO3 (0.05), and NiCl2 (0.1). The trace element solution was filter-sterilized and added to the autoclaved MSM medium as a ratio of 1 mL per L of medium [15]. MEG was supplied as the sole carbon source at a concentration of 0.05% (500 mg/L). This concentration was selected to reflect environmentally relevant MEG levels reported in produced water under investigation.

Initial screening for MEG biodegradation

Pure cultures of the selected bacterial strains were prepared on solid LB medium as described in Section 2.1. After overnight incubation, bacterial cells were harvested and transferred to freshly autoclaved liquid MSM medium. Based on the optical density (OD600) of each strain, an inoculum volume (0.5 – 1.0 mL) was adjusted to achieve an initial OD600 of approximately 0.05 in 25 mL MEG-containing MSM medium contained in 50 mL centrifuge tubes. MEG-containing MSM medium without inoculation served as an abiotic control. For each strain, experiments were performed in triplicate, and the mean values and standard deviations were calculated and reported.

Cultures were incubated in a shaking incubator (KS 4000i control, IKA, USA) at 25 °C and 150 rpm for 240 h (10 days). Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring OD600 using a UV/Visible spectrophotometer (UV-1900i Shimadzu). Maximum specific growth rates (µmax) were calculated using Eq. 1.1μmax=lnOD2−lnOD1T2−T1(1)Where OD2 and OD1 indicate the OD measurements at times T2 and T1 (h) during the exponential growth phase, and µmax is expressed in h−1 [24].

COD was used as a rapid and cost-effective proxy to track MEG removal in aqueous media, compared with direct quantification by Gas chromatography (GC) [10]. COD was measured at regular intervals using the APHA 5220 D closed reflux colorimetric method [25], with absorbance measured on a DR 3900 HACH spectrophotometer. The COD of the MSM medium without MEG supplementation was below the detection limit.

MEG biodegradation kinetics and bacterial growth dynamics

Initial screening demonstrated that strain EX72 could utilize MEG as a sole source of carbon and energy. Therefore, further experiments were conducted to investigate MEG biodegradation kinetics and bacterial growth dynamics using the same methodology described in Section 2.6. The experiments were performed in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 125 mL of working volume. To achieve an initial OD600 of approximately 0.05, about 5 mL of inoculum was added to 120 mL MEG-containing MSM medium. For each experiment, flasks containing MEG-supplemented medium without bacterial inoculation served as abiotic controls.

To investigate degradation kinetics over time, multiple parameters were measured at regular intervals over a 14 day incubation period (336 h). These included: OD600 (bacterial growth), cell dry weight (biomass formation, and yield, Yx/s), COD, total organic carbon (TOC), dissolved organic carbon (DOC) (organic carbon removal) and residual MEG (parent substrate degradation).

Cell dry weight was determined by collecting 15 mL samples at selected time points and centrifuging them at 4500 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was decanted, and the resulting biomass pellet was washed twice with sterile DI water by centrifugation (4500 rpm, 10 min) between washes. Following supernatant removal, the biomass was transferred to a sterile glass Petri dish and oven-dried at 60 °C for 48 h to constant weight. Biomass carbon content was assumed to be 50% of the dry biomass weight, consistent with commonly reported values in the literature [26].

TOC and DOC were measured using a Total carbon analyzer (Skalar Formacs HT-i) following the APHA 5310 B method. For DOC analysis, samples were filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter to remove bacterial cells and particulate biomass. MEG concentrations were quantified using gas chromatography-flame ionization detection (GC-FID) at an external analytical laboratory. Sample analysis, instrument calibration, and data processing were performed by the laboratory following validated standard operating procedures for glycol determination in aqueous matrices. Calibration was performed using certified MEG standards, and peak identification was based on retention time matching with the corresponding standard analyzed under identical conditions. Chromatographic data were processed using instrument software, which performed automated baseline correction, peak integration, and signal processing prior to quantification. MEG concentrations were calculated using external standard calibration, and values below the laboratory-defined detection limit i.e. <10 µg/L were reported as below detection limit (BDL).

Bacterial growth and COD degradation by the isolated strains

MEG (0.05%)-containing MSM medium was used to evaluate the growth and biodegradation potential of the isolated bacterial strains, with MEG supplied as the sole source of carbon and energy. Bacterial growth served as an indicator of the ability to assimilate MEG, which was accompanied by a reduction in the COD of the medium.

The results showed that only 6 of the 14 strains were capable of MEG biodegradation, as evidenced by measured COD reduction. Among the isolates, Stutzerimonas stutzeri, EX72 exhibited the highest COD removal, achieving a reduction of 62.28% within 240 h (10 days) of incubation. This was followed by Shewanella sp., EX86 and Bacillus sp. EX69, which reduced COD by 22.7 and 10.4%, respectively. Bacillus sp. (EX64) (8.9%), Nitratireductor sp. (8.0%), and Staphylococcus sp. (EX60) (6.3%) were among the other strains which demonstrated COD reductions (Fig. 1). The remaining 8 strains showed negligible COD reduction in the MEG-containing medium.Fig. 1COD reduction and µmax of the isolated strains in a MEG-containing medium.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Consistent with these observations, the strains that exhibited COD reduction also demonstrated higher specific growth rates (µmax), indicating successful adaptation to MEG as a carbon and energy source (Fig. 1). Overall, the trends in COD removal closely correlated with bacterial growth performance, supporting the use of these parameters as a proxy for MEG biodegradation capacity.

Growth dynamics and MEG biodegradation behavior of S. stutzeri EX72

The isolate S. stutzeri EX72 exhibited the strongest ability to reduce COD in a MEG-containing medium during preliminary screening. To characterize the growth behavior of EX72 and its MEG biodegradation kinetics, experiments were conducted at two MEG concentrations: 0.05% (500 mg/L) and 0.1% (1000 mg/L). These experiments enabled the determination of key growth parameters (µmax and doubling time) as well as apparent first-order decay kinetics. In addition to COD analysis, TOC, and DOC measurements and residual MEG analysis at specific timepoints provided more insights into the MEG biodegradation by EX72.

At 0.05% MEG, EX72 exhibited a clear exponential growth phase between 144–168 h, during which the μmax reached 0.0217 h⁻¹, corresponding to a doubling time of 31.9 h (Fig. 2a). At the higher concentrations of 0.1% MEG, growth was slower with μmax = 0.0139 h⁻¹, and a doubling time of 49.9 h (Fig. 2b). Over 336 h (14 days) of incubation time, EX72 achieved 77.2% COD removal at 0.05% MEG and 56.0% COD removal at 0.1% MEG (Fig. 2c, 2d). COD removal followed first-order decay kinetics with respect to total oxidizable organic carbon at both concentrations. Removal was faster at 0.05% MEG, with k = 0.0045 h⁻¹ and a corresponding half-life (t₁/₂) of 153 h (R² = 0.98), whereas biodegradation proceeded more slowly at 0.1% MEG, with k = 0.00231 h⁻¹ and t₁/₂ = 300 h (R² = 0.99) (Fig. 2e). Monod modeling was not pursued due to limited substrate concentration resolution and the use of COD as a lumped/proxy parameter.

TOC and DOC trends closely followed COD removal patterns. At the start of the experiment (t = 0 h), TOC and DOC values were nearly identical, indicating negligible particulate matter and minimal initial biomass (Fig. 3a, b). As the incubation progressed, DOC decreased sharply pointing towards MEG biodegradation, while TOC fell slowly and the gap between TOC and DOC increased. At 0.05% MEG, TOC decreased by 75.9%, closely aligning with 77.2% reduction in COD, while DOC decreased by 92.6%, indicating efficient removal of dissolved organics (including MEG) (Fig. 3a). The close agreement between COD and TOC removal suggests a high degree of mineralization rather than mere transformation and the increasing gap between TOC and DOC reflects the accumulation of POC associated with biomass formation. In contrast, at 0.1% MEG, TOC decreased by 51.6%, closely tracking 56.0% COD removal and DOC decreased by 65.0%, suggesting persistence of dissolved organics (parent substrate and/or intermediates) under potential substrate inhibition and oxygen limitation (Fig. 3b). Importantly, the parallel reduction in both TOC and COD across both concentrations indicates that the measured COD was primarily associated with oxidizable organic carbon (MEG and its intermediates) rather than reduced inorganic species.

By the end of the experiment (336 h), POC, calculated as POC = TOC – DOC, reached 37.75 mg/L at 0.05% MEG, which was approximately half of 68.55 mg/L measured at 0.1% MEG. This difference was also reflected in final OD600 values, which reached 0.200 at 0.05% MEG, and 0.306 at 0.1% MEG. However, the particulate fraction (POC/TOC) was substantially higher at 0.05% MEG (70.6%) than at 0.1% MEG (32.4%), demonstrating that EX72 converted dissolved organics into particulate biomass far more efficiently at the lower MEG concentration. A full carbon mass balance was not attempted due to the absence of direct CO2 measurements; however, combined COD, TOC, DOC, and biomass data provided a robust qualitative assessment of carbon partitioning.

MEG concentrations were measured at three key timepoints: (i) the starting point (time zero), when biotic and abiotic controls contained identical MEG levels; (ii) a mid-point corresponding to approximately 50–60% COD removal; and (iii) the endpoint at 336 h. In flasks inoculated with EX72, the relative MEG concentration (C/C₀) declined progressively at both 0.05% and 0.1% MEG (Fig. 3c, d). By the endpoint, MEG at 0.05% fell below the detection limit, confirming complete removal, whereas residual MEG persisted longer at 0.1% MEG, consistent with observed signs of substrate inhibition and slower overall biodegradation at the higher concentration. Further analysis revealed the presence of multiple enzymes involved in the biodegradation and metabolism of xenobiotics. One of the most prominent pathways identified was the benzoate degradation pathway, which includes catechol 1,2-dioxygenase (EC 1.13.11.1), benzoate 1,2-dioxygenase (EC 1.14.12.10), 1,6-dihydroxycyclohexa-2,4-diene-1-carboxylate dehydrogenase (EC 1.3.1.25), and muconolactone Delta-isomerase (EC 5.3.3.4). S. stutzeri EX72 also encodes 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (EC 1.1.1.35), catechol 1,2-dioxygenase (EC 1.13.11.1), and benzaldehyde dehydrogenase (NAD (+)) (EC 1.2.1.28) which participate in 2,4-dichlorobenzoate degradation.

Similarly, EX72 also contained muconate cycloisomerase (EC 5.5.1.1), and 1,6-dihydroxycyclohexa-2,4-diene-1-carboxylate dehydrogenase (EC 1.3.1.25) enzymes along with additional enzymes involved in fluorobenzoate degradation. For chloroalkane and chloroalkene degradation, the genome encodes alcohol dehydrogenase (EC 1.1.1.1), alcohol dehydrogenase (cytochrome c) (EC 1.1.2.8), aldehyde dehydrogenase (NAD (+)) (EC 1.2.1.3), and (s)-2-haloacid dehalogenase (EC 3.8.1.2) enzymes. The presence of these enzymes across benzoate and xenobiotics degradation pathways highlights the broader metabolic versatility of S. stutzeri EX72. This suggests that EX72 can process chemically related intermediates such as alcohols, and aldehydes, which also arise during the MEG oxidation steps described below.

Phylogenetic analysis showed that the EX72 PQQ-ADH forms a well-supported clade with Stutzerimonas/Pseudomonas strains, including S. stutzeri ATCC 17,588, S. stutzeri A1501, P. chloritidismutans AW-1, and P. balearica DSM 6083. More distant homologs including P. pseudoalcaligenes CECT 5344, P. taeanensis MS-3, and Pseudomonas sp. 20_BN, branched deeper in the tree. In contrast, the two known PQQ-ADHs of P. putida KT2440 (pedE, Ca²⁺-dependent; pedH, lanthanide-dependent) formed a separate branch, consistent with their distinct functional and regulatory properties described in the literature [31].

The comparative synteny analysis was also carried out for PQQ-ADH enzyme to understand functional conservation, evolutionary relationships, and genomic context of enzyme-coding genes across different species. Results in Figure S3 further supported these relationships: the EX72 PQQ-ADH resides in a highly conserved, co-linear gene neighborhood shared with Pseudomonas sp. UBA4034 and P. putida KCJK7916, indicative of a stable operon-like genomic unit. P. putida KT2440 PQQ-ADHs did not appear in this synteny comparison due to its different genomic arrangement [32]. Collectively, the phylogeny and synteny analyses indicate that the EX72 enzyme is orthologous to PQQ-dependent ADHs from environmental Stutzerimonas/Pseudomonas.

In the second step of the pathway, glycolaldehyde is converted to glycolate by an aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH). EX72 carries three ALDH genes with BLAST similarity to E. coli AldB and PuuC (Table 4). Phylogenetically, the EX72 ALDH clusters strongly with Stutzerimonas/Pseudomonas strains (e.g., S. stutzeri A1501, S. stutzeri ATCC 17,588, P. balearica DSM 6083, and S. stutzeri YC-YH1) while remaining distinct from P. putida KT2440 and E. coli K-12 (Fig. 7b). Synteny analysis (Figure S4) showed the EX72 ALDH lies within a conserved multi-gene cassette, most similar to that in Pseudomonas sp. PIA16. More distantly related Actinobacteria retained only the core ALDH block, with peripheral rearrangements, consistent with a conserved catabolic module and potential horizontal gene transfer. The third reaction, oxidation of glycolate to glyoxylate, is catalyzed by glycolate dehydrogenase (GADH; EC 1.1.99.14). The GADH enzyme was previously reported in fungus Trichoderma harzianum AIU 353 [33], and in some bacteria including E. coli [34]. This enzyme is composed of three subunits encoded by glcD, glcE, and glcF [35], all of which were identified in the EX72 genome, supporting the capacity for downstream oxidation of glycolate to glyoxylate.

Downstream of glyoxylate formation, EX72 appears to follow the glycerate pathway, in which glyoxylate is converted to tartronate semialdehyde by glyoxylate carboligase (Gcl; EC 4.1.1.47) and subsequently reduced to glyceric acid by GarR tartronate semialdehyde reductase (EC 1.1.1.60). While this route is consistent with previously reported MEG degradation pathways in bacteria, experimental verification (metabolites and enzymatic assays) is required to confirm pathway activity in EX72. The absence of Gcl in P. putida KT2440 is known to limit its ability to utilize MEG for biomass formation, whereas P. putida JM37 successfully grows on MEG due to the presence of Gcl and activation of the glyoxylate shunt enzymes malate synthase (GlcB) and isocitrate lyase (AceA) [36,37]. In EX72, the subsequent conversion of glyceric acid to glycerate-2-phosphate is facilitated by D-glycerate 2-kinase (GckA; EC 2.7.1.165), which has been documented in E. coli [38].

After the formation of glycerate-2-phosphate, further assimilation proceeds through central carbon metabolism. In S. stutzeri EX72, all enzymes of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle were identified in the genome. Phosphopyruvate hydratase (Eno) converts glycerate-2-phosphate to phosphoenolpyruvate, which is subsequently converted to pyruvate by pyruvate kinase (pyk). Pyruvate is then oxidatively decarboxylated by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (aceE) to yield S-acetyl-dihydrolipoamide-E, and acetyl-CoA is produced through the action of dihydrolipoamide acetyltransferase (aceF).

Interestingly, S. stutzeri EX72 also encodes malate synthase G (glcB; PP_0356), enabling the direct condensation of acetyl-CoA with glyoxylate to form malate. Glyoxylate for this reaction can be supplied either from MEG oxidation or through the native glyoxylate shunt, depending on enzyme expression and environmental conditions. Although the reaction catalyzed by GlcB is oxygen-independent, the upstream formation of glyoxylate may involve oxygen-dependent oxidation steps, as noted for similar pathways [39]. Consequently, the metabolic route utilized by EX72 is likely determined by the relative activities and regulation of MEG-oxidation enzymes versus the endogenous glyoxylate shunt.

Discussion

In this research, we screened various bacterial strains isolated from different environmental samples for their ability to biodegrade MEG. The aim was to isolate bacteria with specialized metabolic traits and high MEG degrading ability that can survive and grow under challenging conditions such as those found in produced water. The 14 strains isolated and identified belonged to 9 genera: Bacillus, Corynebacterium, Cytobacillus, Paenibacillus, Shewanella, Stutzerimonas, Nesisseria, Nitratiredectuor, and Oceanibacillus. Bacillus sp. is ubiquitous in nature and has been isolated from various environments [40,41]. Various species of Bacillus have also demonstrated potential in environmental applications like hydrocarbon bioremediation [12,42], biomineralization [43], and treatment of hydrocarbon contaminated water [44]. 3 strains of Bacillus sp.; EX69, EX68, and EX64, showed variable growth rates in a MEG-containing medium. However, despite the broad metabolic range of Bacillus, their limited MEG assimilation indicates that MEG is not readily biodegradable by these strains and suggests the absence of key enzymes required for MEG oxidation. Other Bacillaceae members in the isolate collection (Cytobacillus, Oceanobacillus, and Paenibacillus) similarly showed no appreciable MEG biodegradation.

Shewanella is a sole genus within the Shewanellaceae family. It represents a unique lineage in microbial taxonomy [45]. It is widespread in seawater [46,47], freshwater [48], invertebrates [49], and marine algae [50]. Literature associates Shewanella with various biodegradation processes [51,52], bioaugmentation-based bioremediation [53], and treatment of hydrocarbon contaminated water [54]. In this study, Shewanella carassii EX86 grew and produced biomass in MEG-containing medium but achieved only a 22.7% reduction in COD. Although this strain was the second among our isolates, EX86 does not qualify as an efficient MEG bio-degrader compared with reports in the literature [7].

Stutzerimonas stutzeri (formerly Pseudomonas stutzeri) inhabits diverse environments and exhibits broad physiological and biochemical versatility [55]. Strains have been isolated from marine samples, salt marshes, and highly alkaline environments such as bauxite residue/red mud [13,55]. They are frequently noted for denitrification [56,57], metal-oxidation [58], and biodegradation of hydrocarbons [59,60] and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) [61]. This ecological versatility suggests that members of this species may be well adapted to fluctuating carbon availability and industrial wastewater environments where glycols are commonly encountered. In this study, S. stutzeri EX72, identified by 16S rRNA and WGS, achieved the greatest COD reductions among the screened isolates (62.27% in 10 days, and 77.20% in 14 days at 0.05%), indicating superior removal of oxidizable organic carbon in a MEG-amended medium. EX72 showed more efficient overall MEG depletion and organic removal at 0.05% MEG, while the reduced growth rate and slower COD decay at 0.1% MEG are consistent with potential substrate inhibition and/or oxygen transfer limitations at higher organic loading [62]. Based on TOC reduction, COD removal and biomass formation, a substantial fraction of MEG-derived carbon was mineralized or respired for maintenance, although direct CO2 measurements were not performed. The apparent biomass yields obtained in this study (0.167–0.188 g biomass-C per g MEG-C consumed) are lower than values commonly reported for readily biodegradable substrates (0.4–0.6 g/g) [63] and aromatic compounds such as benzene (0.75), toluene (0.58), and phenol (0.44) [64]. In contrast to many reduced organics (including hydrocarbons, and several aromatics), MEG is a highly oxidized substrate, and prior carbon-balance discussions of biodegradation emphasize that substrate oxidation state and free-energy content influence the partitioning of carbon between CO₂ evolution and biomass formation; more oxidized/low-energy substrates tend to support lower biomass yields because a larger fraction of substrate carbon is respired for energy rather than incorporated into cell material [65]. Thus, the relatively modest biomass-C yields observed here are consistent with the expectation that MEG supports comparatively lower assimilation efficiency than energy-rich organics (e.g., sugars) and that carbon partitioning can shift further under inhibitory or sub-optimal conditions [66].

In terms of overall MEG degradation performance, Esmaeili and Loghmani, [11] reported 39% MEG degradation by A. tubingensis, while, Ghogare and Gupta reported 70.21% by O. macrosporum [7]. The ability of S. stutzeri EX72 to achieve near-complete MEG removal from the aqueous phase is therefore notable. A limitation of the study is the absence of positive control using well-characterized MEG degraders such as Pseudomonas putida [36] or Rhodococcus jostii [67]; these strains were not available during the experimental phase. In future work, EX72 will serve as an internal positive control alongside established MEG-degrading strains for comparative benchmarking.

Within the genus Stutzerimonas, organic degradation capacity spans Multiple species. For example, S. balearica displays extensive aromatic-hydrocarbon catabolism underpinned by an open pan-genome and frequent occurrence in oil-impacted environments, which together expand its xenobiotic-processing repertoire [68]. Likewise, S. stutzeri lineages can oxidize recalcitrant polymeric substrates, releasing low-molecular-weight products (e.g., fatty acids and succinate) that feed central metabolism [69,70]. Importantly, classical work on S. stutzeri demonstrated robust polyethylene-glycol degradation via a periplasmic PEG dehydrogenase that generates glyoxylate, which enters the dicarboxylic-acid cycle, providing a mechanistic analogue for short-chain glycol oxidation and supporting inherent glycol-mineralizing capacity within the clade [71]. Against this backdrop, S. stutzeri EX72 is notable for MEG mineralization in MSM, whereas most prior Stutzerimonas reports emphasize transformation of aromatic compounds or polymers rather than full mineralization of simple glycols [68]. Furthermore, recent pangenomic/transcriptomic analyses across Stutzerimonas indicate that differences in denitrification genes and trans-membrane transporter systems are major axes of metabolic plasticity; it is hypothesized that these modules, together with EX72’s unique gene complement, underpin its efficient MEG uptake and oxidation. A targeted comparative genomics of EX72 against these functions should clarify the determinants enabling its distinctive MEG-degrading phenotype [72].

WGS showed that the genome of S. stutzeri EX72 is circular with a GC content of 63.85%. Through PATRIC for annotation, 1056 hypothetical proteins, 3198 proteins with functional assignments were identified, with 1058 EC numbers, 890 GO assignments, 775 pathway assignments, 775, and 1442 subsystems assignments. The genome encodes catabolic functions consistent with degradation of xenobiotic compounds, including modules relevant to MEG turnover. Combined with the observed phenotype (observed biomass growth, COD/TOC/DOC removal), these features support a MEG biodegradation route in EX72 in which pyrroloquinoline-quinone (PQQ)-dependent alcohol dehydrogenases (ADHs) oxidize MEG to glycolaldehyde, followed by an aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) step to glycolate, with downstream assimilation via the glycerate pathway for biomass formation.

To strengthen the genotype–phenotype connection using genome data, WGS annotation was complemented with phylogenetic reconstruction and gene-neighborhood (synteny) analyses of the two key entry enzymes (PQQ-ADH and ALDH). In both cases, EX72 enzymes clustered with environmental Stutzerimonas/Pseudomonas lineages and showed conserved local genomic organization relative to closely related taxa, supporting orthology and providing stronger comparative-genomics evidence than gene presence alone. At the same time, it is acknowledged that MEG biodegradation pathway reported here remains inferential, and not direct demonstration of expression or pathway flux, in the absence of qPCR, metabolite quantification, or enzyme assays. Nevertheless, comparison with well-studied MEG degraders indicates that the entry reactions are conserved and involve MEG oxidation to glycolaldehyde (PQQ-ADHs) followed by oxidation to glycolate (ALDH). In P. putida KT2440, this is mediated by pedE/pedH and an ALDH, with strong proteomic induction of pedE (34-fold) and pedH (21-fold) during growth on MEG; sequence analyses also identify ExaA-like alcohol dehydrogenases (PP_2674/PP_2679) [36]. In Ideonella sakaiensis, MEG oxidation involves PedE/PedH/XoxF and PedI and is typically examined in a poly(ethylene terephthalate), PET-linked monomer context i.e., ethylene glycol, EG, generated alongside terephthalic acid during PET depolymerization rather than provided as the sole carbon source [73]. R. jostii uses a mycofactocin-associated dehydrogenase (EgaA) together with AldA1/AldA2 for EG oxidation/assimilation [67]. Research has also shown that the strain GN346 that lacks ADHs and ALDHs was not able to grow on an alcohol i.e. n-butanol [74], demonstrating the importance of expression of these enzymes for bacteria to grow on alcohols. EX72 aligns mechanistically with the entry reactions of these models because WGS-based annotation identified PedE/ExaA-like ADHs and an AldB-like ALDH, consistent with MEG oxidation to glycolate. Phenotypically, however, EX72 differs by achieving near-complete removal of MEG-derived organic carbon in MSM at 0.05%, in contrast to reports that emphasize assimilation, pathway induction, or PET-monomer utilization [36,67,73].

After glycolate formation, routing into central metabolism can proceed via the glycerate pathway, the glyoxylate shunt, or the β-hydroxyaspartate cycle [75]. In EX72, the presence of gcl/garR/gckA and associated central-metabolism genes supports a genome-inferred glycerate route, in which glyoxylate carboligase (Gcl) plays the key role in MEG assimilation and biomass formation. Prior work in P. putida KT2440 identified the absence of Gcl as a bottleneck for efficient MEG/EG metabolism and biomass formation, whereas Gcl presence or overexpression (e.g., in P. putida JM37 or engineered KT2440) restores growth-coupled flux [36,37]. The glyoxylate shunt offers an alternative via malate synthase to S-malate, which then enters the TCA cycle [76]. In Paracoccus denitrificans, the BHA cycle converts glyoxylate to oxaloacetate via BhcA/B/C/D (aspartate-glyoxylate aminotransferase, β-hydroxyaspartate dehydratase, β-hydroxyaspartate aldolase, and iminosuccinate reductase) [77]. Finally, the inability of strain GN346, which lacks ADHs/ALDHs, to grow on the alcohol n-butanol underscores the centrality of these oxidoreductases for alcohol assimilation more broadly [74].

Altogether, the conserved entry reactions (PQQ-ADH → ALDH), together with the phylogenetic placement and conserved gene-neighborhood architecture of these key enzymes, and the observed near-complete MEG removal/mineralization in MSM medium, position S. stutzeri EX72 as a strong candidate for MEG degradation in produced water treatment. Future work should validate pathway activity directly using targeted metabolite profiling (glycolaldehyde, glycolate, glyoxylate), gene-expression measurements during growth on MEG, and targeted inactivation of key genes (such as PQQ-ADH, ALDH) to determine whether these enzymes are required for MEG conversion in S. stutzeri EX72.

Conclusion and future perspective

MEG is a common byproduct in industrial processes which contributes significantly to the high COD of produced water underscoring the need for effective biodegradation strategies. In this study, 14 bacterial strains isolated from diverse environments were screened for their MEG-degrading capabilities. The identification results revealed that these strains belong to 9 different genera, reflecting notable microbial diversity. Among these strains, S. stutzeri EX72, was the only isolate capable of near-complete MEG mineralization at 0.05%, indicating the efficient MEG-degrading bacteria are not widely distributed in natural environments. This finding highlights the potential relevance of targeted microbial selection or bioaugmentation approaches for MEG-containing wastewater.

WGS and annotation of EX72 identified a set of genes consistent with a stepwise MEG oxidation route (PQQ-dependent ADH to glycolaldehyde and ALDH to glycolate), followed by genome-inferred assimilation capacity via glyoxylate/glycerate routing into central metabolism. These entry reactions align with experimentally characterized MEG oxidation systems reported in model organisms such as P. putida KT2440 and the PET-associated ethylene glycol metabolism in I. sakaiensis, although this study did not directly measure gene expression or intermediate metabolites.

Future work will focus on: (i) validating pathway activity in EX72 through targeted measurements of metabolites and intermediates (glycolaldehyde, glycolate, and glyoxylate), gene expression profiling during growth on MEG, and targeted inactivation of candidate genes (e.g., PQQ-ADH and ALDH) to test whether they are required for MEG conversion; and (ii) enhancing MEG removal by optimizing operational parameters such as inoculum volume, temperature, nutrient availability, and incubation time. Collectively, these experiments will help define both the mechanistic basis and optimal operating conditions for MEG biodegradation in EX72 and provide a sound foundation for any subsequent evaluation under more application-representative conditions.
