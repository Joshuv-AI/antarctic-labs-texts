The effect of eating lollipop at early stage of recovery after gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Lu Yin, Anna Jia, Jiayao Zhang, Siyuan Huang, Qinqin Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation frequently experience adverse reactions such as hypoglycemia, dizziness, nausea and vomiting during the early post-sedation recovery period. These adverse reactions not only delay the post-procedural recovery process of patients but also further exacerbate their economic and psychological burdens. In light of the significant impact of these adverse reactions on patients' postoperative recovery and quality of life, the present study aimed to evaluate the effect of eating lollipop during early recovery after gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation.MethodsThis study was designed as a randomized controlled trial and conducted from November 2023 to December 2024 in a tertiary hospital in Sichuan Province, China. A total of 628 patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation were enrolled as study participants. Ultimately, 496 patients completed the study, with 248 in the observation group (eating lollipop during recovery) and 248 in the control group (routine care). The primary outcome was hypoglycemia at 30 min after the procedure, defined as fingertip blood glucose ≤ 3.9 mmol/L. Secondary outcomes included nausea/vomiting, pain, dizziness, hunger, post-sedation recovery time, comfort score, and documented safety-related events. Effect estimates and adjusted odds ratios were calculated.ResultsCompared with the control group, patients in the observation group exhibited a significant decrease in the incidence of hypoglycemia (3.2% in the observation group vs. 8.5% in the control group)[absolute risk reduction, 5.2 percentage points (95% CI, 1.1–9.3); relative risk, 0.38 (95% CI, 0.17–0.84)]. After adjustment for clinically relevant covariates, eating lollipop remained associated with lower odds of hypoglycemia (adjusted OR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.13–0.80; P = 0.020). Additionally, the incidence of nausea and vomiting was also notably reduced in the observation group (4.0% in the observation group vs. 10.5% in the control group, adjusted OR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.17–0.93; P = 0.039). Comfort scores were higher in the observation group [98 (95, 100) vs. 96 (95, 99); P < 0.001].During the process of eating lollipops, none of the patients in the observation group exhibited adverse events such as aspiration, suffocation, dyspnea, coughing, or choking.ConclusionFor patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation, Eating lollipop during early post-sedation recovery was associated with lower rates of hypoglycaemia and nausea and vomiting, with a small increase in comfort score. Introduction

Gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation, as a crucial means for the early detection and treatment of digestive diseases, has been widely applied in clinical practice because of its significant advantages of being rapid, and highly tolerable for patients (Zhu et al. 2023). According to statistics, 65.1% of digestive endoscopy centers in China have introduced painless gastrointestinal endoscopy diagnostic and therapeutic techniques, in tertiary hospitals, this proportion reaches 93.5%, making it a universally preferred diagnostic and therapeutic method for both doctors and patients (Zhang et al. 2012; Thomas 2017).

The popularization of painless gastrointestinal endoscopy has greatly alleviated patients' adverse reactions during diagnosis and treatment, effectively mitigated their fear and anxiety toward endoscopic procedures, enhanced their tolerance and comfort, thereby ensuring the smooth conduct of endoscopic examinations. However, in clinical practice, patients undergoing painless digestive endoscopy may experience a series of adverse reactions during recovery due to the use of anesthetic agents, such as hypoglycemia, dizziness, nausea and vomiting (Shang et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2017). These adverse reactions not only impact the post-procedural recovery process of patients but also increase their financial and psychological burdens. Therefore, optimizing recovery process for patients undergoing painless digestive endoscopy and devising effective strategies to mitigate or avoid the aforementioned adverse reactions are crucial for improving the quality of patients' postoperative recovery.

In recent years, the concept of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) has gained widespread recognition and promotion in the clinical field. Among its various interventions, early postoperative oral feeding has been proven to alleviate surgical stress responses and reduce the incidence of postoperative adverse reactions, thereby significantly improving patients' postoperative recovery quality (Li et al., 2020; Zhang and Zhai 2017). Previous studies have demonstrated that eating lollipops during anesthesia recovery in pediatric patients can improve pain, hunger, and anxiety without increasing residual gastric volume (Feld et al. 1989; Yang et al., 2015a, 2015b). Tang et al. (2022) also reported that early eating lollipops during anesthesia recovery in outpatient hysteroscopy patients with infertility can significantly reduce anxiety and depression, alleviate postoperative hunger and pain, increase patient satisfaction, decrease the occurrence of adverse reactions during anesthesia recovery. However, there is still a lack of sufficient evidence supporting the effects of early eating lollipop in adult patients after digestive endoscopy under procedural sedation.

This study aimed to comprehensively investigate the clinical effects of early eating lollipop during recovery in patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation. The objective was to alleviate postoperative adverse reactions such as hypoglycemia, hunger, pain, nausea and vomiting, thereby increasing patients' comfort and early recovery quality. Ultimately, this research endeavored to improve patients' healthcare experience, provide valuable references and insights for clinical applications. In this context, the following hypotheses were tested in this study;

Methods

Study design

This study employed a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effect of eating lollipop during early recovery after gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation. The design, implementation, and analysis processes of this study were conducted in accordance with the recommendations outlined in the CONSORT (Wang 2011) statement.

Participants

This study enrolled all patients who underwent gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation and met the inclusion criteria at the Endoscopy Center of a tertiary hospital in Sichuan Province between November 2023 and December 2024.

Randomization and blinding

This study was designed as a randomized controlled trial, utilizing a random sampling method to select patients eligible for gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation on the basis of predefined inclusion criteria. Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the observation group or control group. An independent researcher not involved in enrolment, intervention delivery, outcome assessment, or data analysis generated the random allocation sequence using Excel random-number generation. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened only after informed consent and baseline assessment. The anesthesia nurse administering the interventions were aware of the patients' group assignments, while the data analysts remained blinded to the group allocation information. Although blinding the patients themselves was not feasible, the research team avoided mentioning specific group details when explaining the study procedures to minimize psychological suggestion effects and to ensure that all participants, regardless of whether they received the specific intervention, received equal attention and high-quality nursing care. The lack of participant and care-provider blinding was considered when interpreting subjective outcomes.

Sedation protocol

Both groups underwent standard pre-procedural care and sedation protocols. Prior to surgery, patients were instructed to fast for 8 h and refrain from oral intake for 4 h, as per medical advice. After reporting to the gastrointestinal endoscopy registration area, patients entered the examination preparation room 30 min ahead of schedule. In the intravenous puncture room, the nurse established intravenous access for the patients via a 22-gauge intravenous catheter. Ten minutes before entering the examination room, patients received topical anesthesia of the oropharynx with 10 ml of Dacron Jelly. Upon entering the examination room, the patient was assisted in biting down on a bite block and properly secured in a left-lateral, knee-flexed position with protective restraints applied. Electrocardiographic monitoring was subsequently established, and routine monitoring of noninvasive blood pressure, electrocardiogram, heart rate, and pulse oximetry was subsequently conducted. Oxygen was administered via a nasal cannula at a flow rate of 3 L/min. On the basis of the patient's weight, intravenous injections of sufentanil citrate at a dose of 0.1 μg/kg and propofol at a dose of 1–1.5 mg/kg were administered. After the disappearance of the eyelash reflex was achieved, the endoscopist initiated the endoscopic procedure. If the patient exhibited involuntary movements during the diagnostic or therapeutic process, the anesthesiologist administered additional propofol at a dose of 0.2–0.5 mg/kg. Intraoperatively, the patient's vital signs were maintained within stable limits. No advanced airway device or controlled ventilation was routinely used; therefore, the technique was described as procedural sedation rather than general anaesthesia. After the procedure, patients were transferred to the post anaesthesia care unit (PACU) for post-sedation recovery.

Peri-procedural fluid and antiemetic treatment

Intravenous access was mainly maintained for sedative and rescue medication administration. Intravenous fluids were not routinely administered during the peri-procedural period; however, detailed infusion volume and rate were not systematically collected. Antiemetic treatment was not used routinely as prophylaxis for all participants. Rescue antiemetics could be administered at the discretion of the attending clinician if clinically significant nausea or vomiting occurred. Prophylactic and rescue antiemetic use were not systematically captured as prespecified variables.

Intervention and data collection

Intervention

In the control group, upon arrival at the PACU, patients received routine nursing care. This included bedside transfer of patient information from the anesthesiologist and endoscopist to the anesthesia nurse. Patients were then monitored via electrocardiography and received oxygen therapy via a nasal cannula at a flow rate of 3 L/min. Once patients regained consciousness, the head of the bed was elevated to facilitate respiration. Patients' conditions were closely monitored, and any abnormalities were promptly reported to the anesthesiologist for appropriate management. Patients were allowed to leave the hospital accompanied by family members when their Pos-Anesthetic Discharge Scoring System (PADSS) score (Trevisani et al. 2013) reached 9 or above, at which point they were informed of the relevant post procedural precautions along with their family members.

After arriving at the PACU, patients in the observation group received routine nursing care at baseline. Once the patients regained consciousness, exhibited restored autonomous reflexes, demonstrated stable vital signs, and expressed a willingness to eat a lollipop(product standard number: SB/T 10018, sugar and starch syrup hard candy),Before lollipop administration, oral-intake readiness was assessed clinically by a trained PACU nurse using predefined bedside criteria rather than a formal swallowing scale. Lollipop consumption was allowed only when the patient was awake, able to follow simple commands, breathing stably, haemodynamically stable, and able to swallow effectively, with no active coughing, choking, nausea, vomiting, excessive oral secretion, or signs of airway obstruction. The nurses instructed the patients to begin eating the lollipop, which continued until they left the PACU. During this time, researchers stayed at the bedside to closely observe the patients' reactions. If any adverse events, such as coughing, choking, nausea, or vomiting occurred, the patients were immediately instructed to stop eating, and appropriate measures were taken to address the situation.

Data collection and compilation

Data were collected via a self-designed general information questionnaire, which included patient demographics and study-related information.

Prior to the examination

Thirty minutes before the examination, researchers collected general information from the patients, including sex, age, BMI, anesthesia grade, Procedure type, and the presence of hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease.

During the examination

Researchers recorded the start and end times of the examination, as well as the dosages of propofol and sufentanil administered.

After the examination

Thirty minutes after the examination, researchers collected patients' fingertip blood glucose levels, incidence of nausea and vomiting, VAS scores for pain, dizziness, hunger. Before the patients left the recovery room, the post-sedation recovery time and the patients' comfort scores during recovery were recorded.

The research data were uniformly collected, organized, and promptly reviewed for any omissions or deficiencies by the researchers on a daily basis. Additionally, two research members were assigned each week for further sorting and verification of the data. In case of any doubts or uncertainties, they consulted and crosschecked with other research members to ensure accuracy.

Outcome indicators

Primary outcome indicator

The primary outcome was hypoglycemia at 30 min after the procedure, defined as fingertip blood glucose ≤ 3.9 mmol/L (Su and Xu 2014). This outcome reflected glucose status at a single post-procedure time point rather than serial peri-recovery glucose dynamics. During the recovery, patients' facial expressions, consciousness, and other signs were observed. If patients reported symptoms of hypoglycemia, such as dizziness, palpitations, cold sweats, or tremors, blood glucose levels were immediately measured, and appropriate interventions were recorded and administered.

Secondary outcome indicators

Secondary outcomes included nausea and vomiting, pain score, dizziness score, hunger score, post-sedation recovery time and comfort score during post-sedation recovery. Assessment of nausea and vomiting: Thirty minutes after the examination, the severity of nausea and vomiting was assessed via the WHO nausea and vomiting grading scale: Grade 0, no nausea or vomiting; Grade 1, nausea without vomiting; Grade 2, transient vomiting accompanied by nausea; Grade 3, vomiting requiring treatment; and Grade 4, uncontrollable nausea and vomiting. Among these, grades ≥ 1 were considered the occurrence of nausea and vomiting (Tang 2021). When patients reported nausea or vomiting during recovery, immediate assessment and management were conducted, and incidents were recorded. Pain: Thirty minutes after the examination, the visual analog scale (VAS) was used for pain assessment. A horizontal line marked with scores ranging from 0 to 10 was drawn on paper via a ruler, with higher scores indicating greater pain intensity. Dizziness: Thirty minutes after the examination, the VAS for dizziness assessment was employed. Similarly, a horizontal line marked with scores from 0 to 10 was indicated on paper, with higher scores reflecting more severe dizziness. Hunger: Thirty minutes after the examination, the VAS hunger score was assessed, with higher scores indicating more severe hunger. Post-sedation recovery time: This was defined as the duration from the patient's entry into the PACU to their discharge from the PACU. Patient comfort during recovery: Prior to the patient's discharge from the PACU, a VAS was used to assess their comfort level, with scores ranging from 0–100. A score of 0 represented extreme discomfort, whereas a score of 100 denoted extreme comfort.

The incidence of adverse reactions in the observation group includes aspiration, suffocation, dyspnea, coughing, choking. Specifically, aspiration refers to the inadvertent entry of oral or gastric contents into the larynx or lower respiratory tract of the patient (Yu et al. 2025). Dyspnea represents a subjective experience of respiratory discomfort with varying degrees of intensity and nature reported by the patient (Zhang et al. 2026).

Sample size

A review of previous reports revealed that the incidence of hypoglycemia among patients after gastrointestinal endoscopy was 16% (Ye et al. 2020). Following interventions, it is estimated that the incidence of hypoglycemia decreases to 6%. The sample size was calculated via the following formula (Zheng 2023): n = 2pq(Zα + Zβ)2/(p1—p2)2, where n represents the sample size per group. The values for Zα and Zβ were obtained from statistical tables. Typically, α is set to 0.05 for a two-tailed test, and β is set to 0.1 for a one-tailed test, yielding Zα = 1.96 and Zβ = 1.28, respectively. p1 and p2 represent the incidence rates in the observation group and control group, respectively. P represents the mean of p1 and p2, and q represents the mean of 1-p1 and 1-p2. In this case, p = 0.11 and q = 0.89. Substituting these values into the formula, we calculated that approximately 206 samples were required for each group in this study. Considering a 20% sample loss rate, 248 patients should be included in each group.

Statistical analysis

The raw research data were entered into an Excel spreadsheet. Statistical analysis of the data was conducted via SPSS version 20.0 software. For categorical data, frequencies and percentages (%) were used for descriptions, whereas for continuous data, means ± standard deviations () or medians with interquartile ranges Median [interquartile range] were used. Comparisons between groups for categorical data were performed via the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. For continuous data that followed a normal distribution, the t test was used for comparisons between groups. For continuous data that did not follow a normal distribution, the nonparametric rank-sum test was applied. Statistical significance was considered when P < 0.05.

Additional analyses were conducted utilizing R software (version 4.3). For hypoglycemia and nausea/vomiting, absolute risk reduction, relative risk, odds ratio, and 95% confidence intervals were computed. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to estimate adjusted odds ratios and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals, with adjustments made for clinically relevant covariates including age, sex, body mass index, diabetes mellitus, procedure type, examination duration, propofol dose, and sufentanil dose. Exploratory subgroup or sensitivity analyses were performed based on procedure type: gastroscopy only, colonoscopy only, and combined gastroscopy and colonoscopy. Based on the observed hypoglycemia rates of 8.5% in the control group and 3.2% in the observation group, with 248 patients per group and a two-sided α of 0.05, the achieved power was 72.4%. No formal multiplicity adjustment was applied to secondary outcomes; thus, these analyses were considered exploratory.

Adjusted analyses

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the associations between eating lollipop and the outcomes of hypoglycemia as well as nausea/vomiting. After adjusting for potential confounders, including age, sex, body mass index, diabetes mellitus, procedure type, procedure duration, propofol dose, and sufentanil dose, eating lollipop continued to demonstrate a significant association with reduced odds of hypoglycemia (adjusted OR = 0.34; 95% CI:0.13–0.80; P = 0.020). Similarly, a significant association was observed between eating lollipop and decreased odds of nausea/vomiting (adjusted OR = 0.42; 95% CI:0.17–0.93; P = 0.039) (Table 4).

Exploratory analyses

Exploratory analyses stratified according to the type of examination revealed generally consistent directions of the effects for both hypoglycemia and nausea/vomiting. The interaction P-values were 0.165 for hypoglycemia and 0.512 for nausea/vomiting, suggesting that there was no clear evidence to indicate that the intervention effect varied by procedure type. These analyses were considered exploratory due to the small number of events within each stratum (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated eating lollipop during early post-sedation recovery in patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation. The main finding was that eating lollipop was associated with a lower incidence of hypoglycemia at 30 min after the procedure. This association remained after adjustment for clinically relevant covariates. Patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy may be vulnerable to hypoglycemia because of fasting, bowel preparation in selected procedures, and delayed oral intake. Hypoglycemia can cause palpitations, cold sweating, tremor, pallor, weakness, and, in severe cases, impaired attention or consciousness, posing potential risks during recovery (Li et al. 2024). Studies have indicated that postoperative hypoglycemia was associated with increased postoperative mortality and morbidity (Johnston et al. 2017). Additionally, our findings are consistent with those of Xu Meixiang et al. (2023) and Shi Haixia 2020). Xu Meixiang et al. noted that early postoperative candy sucking can effectively reduce the incidence of hypoglycemia in patients undergoing endoscopic intestinal polypectomy. Shi Haixia's research also indicated that the planned administration of sugar lozenges to patients after endoscopic colonic polypectomy and can reduce the incidence of hypoglycemia during anesthesia recovery. However, because blood glucose was measured only once at 30 min after the procedure, our results should be interpreted as a reduction in hypoglycaemia at this specific time point rather than evidence of improved peri-recovery glucose stability.

Nausea and vomiting are common complications after gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation (Wang et al. 2011). During the diagnostic and therapeutic procedures of gastrointestinal endoscopy, a large amount of gas is usually infused into the digestive tract to obtain a clear operative field or meet diagnostic and therapeutic needs, some diagnostic and therapeutic procedures also require the injection of water or other reagents. The accumulation of these gases and liquids can cause mechanical stretching of the digestive tract (Borrat et al. 2019) and subsequently increase the incidence of nausea and vomiting. The results of this study revealed that during the recovery period, the incidence of nausea and vomiting in the observation group was 4.0%, which was significantly lower than that in the control group (10.5%). Notably, a study by E. Ozdemir et al. (2008) also indicated that eating strawberry-flavored lollipops could significantly reduce nausea and vomiting symptoms during peripheral blood stem cell infusion. Research by Biyik, I. et al. (2019) revealed that sucking on lemon-flavored lollipops could decrease nausea and vomiting during pregnancy. We speculate that the positive effect observed in our study may be attributed to the action of eating lollipops, which stimulates the vagus nerve, thereby promoting increased gastrointestinal activity, improving gastrointestinal motility and emptying function, consequently reducing the occurrence of nausea and vomiting to some extent. Possible explanations include salivation, oral sensory stimulation, distraction, and psychological comfort. However, these mechanisms were not directly measured in this study and should be regarded as speculative. Additionally, multiple studies (Echeverria-Villalobos et al. 2022; Yokoyama et al. 2024; Laufenberg-Feldmann et al. 2019) have clearly demonstrated that anxiety was one of the risk factors for postoperative nausea and vomiting. The sweetness and texture of lollipops may provide psychological comfort to patients to some degree, alleviating their anxiety and indirectly reducing the incidence of nausea and vomiting. We also did not assess anxiety, vagal activity, or gastrointestinal motility, and therefore cannot determine the pathway underlying this finding. However, despite our study identifying a potential role of eating lollipops in reducing the incidence of nausea and vomiting, further scientific research is needed to delve deeper into the specific causes and refine our understanding. Future studies can design experiments to verify these hypotheses by utilizing physiological indicators (such as vagus nerve tone) and psychological scales.

During the recovery, patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy under procedural sedation often report discomfort symptoms such as dry mouth, abdominal distension, and pain (Wang et al. 2011), which significantly impact patients' comfort levels. Previous studies have clearly indicated that increasing patient comfort after anesthesia can effectively reduce the use of analgesic medications and shorten the recovery time (Tian et al., 2021a, 2021b). The comfort score was significantly higher in the observation group from a statistical perspective. Nevertheless, the absolute difference was relatively minor, and its clinical significance remains inconclusive. Consumption of lollipops may alleviate dry mouth and offer oral sensory stimulation during the recovery period. However, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of pain score, dizziness score, hunger score, and post-sedation recovery time. Consequently, the findings do not substantiate a widespread improvement across all recovery-related symptoms.

Exploratory analyses stratified by procedure type showed generally consistent directions of effect for hypoglycemia and nausea/vomiting across gastroscopy, colonoscopy, and combined gastroscopy and colonoscopy. There was no clear evidence of effect modification by procedure type. However, because event numbers within strata were small, these analyses were exploratory and should not be overinterpreted.

Conclusion

In this study, we implemented the method of eating lollipop for patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy during early post-sedation recovery. This approach is not only simple to perform but also easily acceptable to patients. Eating lollipop during early post-sedation recovery was associated with a lower incidence of hypoglycemia at 30 min after the procedure and nausea/vomiting. Comfort scores were slightly higher in the observation group, although the absolute difference was small. No major documented safety-related events were observed. These findings suggest that eating lollipop may be considered in selected patients who meet oral-intake readiness criteria, but further studies with serial glucose monitoring and more systematic safety assessment are needed.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as a single-center trial, it may limit the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, blood glucose was measured only once at 30 min after the procedure; baseline glucose and serial post-procedure glucose measurements were not obtained, preventing this study from describing peri-recovery glucose dynamics. In addition, the observed hypoglycemia rate in the control group was lower than originally assumed in the sample size calculation, and the achieved power based on the observed rates was 72.4%. Thirdly, the exact amount of carbohydrate ingested from the lollipop could not be quantified because intake depended on patient willingness, sucking speed, and PACU stay duration. Additionally, although institutional fasting and fluid restriction instructions were standardized, individual fasting duration, bowel preparation details, antiemetic use, and some nausea and vomiting-related factors were not systematically captured. Lastly, since participants and PACU nurses could not be blinded, subjective outcomes such as nausea, pain, dizziness, hunger, and comfort may have been influenced by performance or detection bias. Diabetes has been incorporated into the multivariate model; however, due to the limited number of diabetic patients, a subgroup analysis for diabetes was not conducted. Multiple secondary outcomes were analysed without formal multiplicity adjustment; therefore, secondary and subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution.
