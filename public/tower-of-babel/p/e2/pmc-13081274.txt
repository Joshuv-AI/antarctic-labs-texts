Patients’ caring needs for a patient-friendly magnetic resonance imaging examination: a qualitative study
Authors: Hye Jin Yoo, JaeLan Shim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMagnetic resonance imaging is essential to accurately check patients’ health status and apply optimal nursing care and treatment. Understanding patients’ experiences and caring needs is crucial for completing the examination safely and improving the quality of the images that aid decision-making regarding treatment. This study explored patients’ caring needs for patient-friendly magnetic resonance imaging.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study design was used. Participants were 20 patients (12 men and eight women) aged 19–80 years who received magnetic resonance imaging at a tertiary hospital. Participants were recruited using a combination of flyer-based recruitment in the magnetic resonance imaging units and snowball sampling. Data were collected through in-depth semi-structured interviews from May to June 2024 and analyzed using conventional content analysis. The COREQ guidelines were used to check the rigor of the study.ResultsThree themes were derived: (1) information support and effective communication; (2) attentive care that minimizes physical limitations; and (3) emotional consideration to relieve lingering negative memories of being overwhelmed by machines.ConclusionDuring disease identification and treatment, patients’ perspectives should be fully considered to ensure high-quality examination experiences. To perform a patient-friendly magnetic resonance imaging procedure, healthcare providers should actively participate and work together to create an examination environment that improves patients’ physical and mental well-being.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is an efficient method for diagnosing diseases and determining appropriate treatment [1]. It uses magnetic fields to create detailed images of the human body’s structure and provide accurate medical information [2, 3]. Unlike X-ray or computed tomography, MRI involves no risk of radiation exposure. Additionally, it allows taking various types of images, from static to functional, by controlling the signal [4]. However, MRI examination takes longer than other imaging tests, ranging from 20 min to 1 h [5]. Additionally, MRI image acquisition is affected by changes in the current switching of the gradient coil—when a large current flows in different directions for a short period, it causes vibration and noise [6]. MRI can cause stress, anxiety, and claustrophobia in patients because of the 65–100-dB noise generated within the enclosed space [7, 8]. During the procedure, extreme emotional anxiety can cause patients to move, which may reduce the image quality and diagnostic value. Such emotional reactions can also lead to the premature termination of the examination and patient refusal to receive follow-up procedures, resulting in negative health outcomes [9].

MRI is crucial for accurately assessing patients’ health status and providing optimal treatment and nursing care. Patient factors, such as cooperation, level of understanding, and satisfaction, are important to improve image quality and complete examinations [10]. Thus, it is imperative to have an in-depth understanding of patients’ experiences while receiving MRI examinations. However, MRI is conducted in a healthcare provider-centered environment with limited consideration of patients’ perspectives. Healthcare providers and imaging technicians often overlook patients’ conditions and focus only on their work [11].

Patient-centered care has become a global treatment guideline. Addressing problems arising during MRI procedures and improving patients’ experiences is important [12, 13]. Currently, studies related to MRI focus on examination techniques and methods to improve diagnostics [14] or interventions to reduce anxiety [5, 15]. Very few qualitative studies have comprehensively explored patient-centered experiences during MRI.

With the escalating global demand for MRI, concerns regarding the appropriateness of its utilization have intensified. Notably, in countries such as South Korea, Germany, the United States, and Iran, researchers have highlighted the critical need to evaluate clinical indications to mitigate overprescription and ensure healthcare efficiency [16]. In the context of this rising volume of examinations, enhancing the quality of the patient experience through patient-centered care has become paramount.

Methods

Study design

The study adopted a qualitative descriptive design, a valuable method for exploring patient experiences during MRI and identifying their caring needs [17].

Theoretical framework

This study was based on the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Healthcare (ACSQHC) framework, which states that related systems and processes should be safely provided to protect patients from harm, improve the quality of health service delivery, and meet expected standards of patient safety and quality [18]. This study applied the seven key attributes of the ACSQHC framework, which support person-centered care (Fig. 1).

This framework, which highlights seven core attributes of person-centered care: respect for patients’ values and preferences, emotional support, physical comfort, information and communication, continuity and transition, coordination of care, and involvement of family and friends. These attributes served as analytical lenses for identifying caring needs during MRI.

Participants and setting

Outpatients aged 19–80 who were prescribed an MRI for diagnosis or treatment confirmation were eligible to participate. Exclusion criteria included inpatient treatment, communication inability, or a psychiatric disorder diagnosis.

The study purpose, selection requirements, and data collection method were explained before starting MRI to aid participant selection. This study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in South Korea and included patients who voluntarily agreed to participate. Participants were recruited using a combination of flyer-based recruitment in the MRI units and snowball sampling. Recruitment and participant information were managed exclusively by a member of the research team who was not involved in the participants’ clinical care and was unknown to them prior to the study. The researcher provided a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures, and participation was entirely voluntary.

Data collection

The researchers have over 15 years of clinical experience, with an extensive understanding of the MRI procedure. One researcher collected the data directly from the 20 patients between May and June 2024. Participants’ age, sex, and education levels were recorded before the interviews. Data were collected until saturation using interview guide for individual interviews. Table 1 presents the interview guidelines. Interviews were conducted in Korean. A pilot interview was conducted with one participant to check the clarity of the questions; no major changes were made to the guide. One researcher conducted all interviews in a quiet seminar room near the hospital at a time convenient for participants. All participants were recruited voluntarily, and no participants withdrew from the study. Snowball sampling was conducted after MRI, whereby three enrolled participants recommended additional patients. All three recommended individuals expressed interest in participating in the study, received a full explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures, and voluntarily provided written informed consent. Interviews were completed on the same day immediately following the MRI and lasted 30–45 min. All interviews were audio recorded with participants’ permission. Participants’ facial expressions were documented as field notes and used for data analysis. Throughout the interview process, two researchers engaged in ongoing discussions to ensure consistency and agreement in data collection.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using a conventional content analysis method [19]. First, the researchers transcribed and read the interview responses repeatedly to understand the meaning. Second, two researchers independently coded the transcripts line-by-line. Third, the codes were compared, and related codes were grouped to derive sub-themes. Fourth, themes with more comprehensive meanings were derived based on the interconnectivity between the sub-themes. Fifth, to validate the sub- and main themes, the confirmed and analyzed contents were comprehensively described by comparing them with the original data. Researchers discussed until a consensus was reached in case of disagreement. Microsoft Excel was used for the analysis. This iterative process facilitated the transition from raw data to the final conceptual framework.

Rigor

Sandelowski’s [20] criteria were used to ensure the validity of the results. First, an environment in which people could talk comfortably was created to increase credibility, and semi-structured interviews were conducted to prevent data omission. Second, to establish auditability, data collection and analysis methods were described in detail, enabling other researchers to track them. Third, data were collected to achieve fittingness until no new content emerged, ensuring a variety of content, following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist [21] (Supplementary File 1). Fourth, to ensure confirmability, efforts were made to minimize researcher bias by performing member checks for unclear interview details.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Dongguk University Institutional Review Board (no. DGU 2024-0005). All participants were provided written informed consent prior to data collection, including consent for audio-recording of interviews and the use of de-identified quotations in publications. All participants were informed about data recording and provided written consent. The research complied with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki (2013 Fortaleza revision and subsequent amendments) and the Bioethics and Safety Act of the Republic of Korea.

Theme 1: information support and effective communication

Patients fully understood that MRI was necessary for diagnosing and treating their disease; however, they needed to become more familiar with the specific examination methods or procedures. Additionally, they expressed frustration during the MRI because of incomplete communication with the healthcare providers.

Incomplete understanding of the MRI process

Healthcare providers educated patients before the test and completed their consent forms; however, the information focused more on the importance of MRI in disease diagnosis and treatment decisions. Patients received the examination without knowing the real difficulties expected during the process.The doctor said I needed an MRI, and the MRI process was explained to me. However, because I was preoccupied with worries about the test, I was not mentally prepared for the frustration and discomfort in the MRI room. As a result, I was surprised and shocked. (Participant 1)

Patients experienced a lack of information or education that considered their perspectives. They emphasized the need for specific and vivid information about their situations during MRI.First, they told me to fill out a consent form and wait, so I sat on a sofa in the waiting room and waited quietly. While waiting, I thought it would be good if the healthcare provider could calmly explain the situations and problems that may arise during the test. It is well-known that patients are told not to carry metals or cell phones, but little information is given about what will happen during the test. (Participant 4)

However, I was frustrated because there was no way to know how much time had passed during the test, and there was no one to tell me if it was progressing well. (Participant 2)

Theme 2: delicate care that minimizes physical limitations

Patients complained of discomfort owing to the closed space and repetitive sounds during MRI. They had no choice but to endure a fixed position for a long time in a restrictive environment.

A body trapped in a bridle from which there is no escape

Patients had to hold their breath and remain steady in a narrow space during the test to obtain accurate results. They did their best to control their body movements because any small movement could prolong the testing process.I am usually unable to lie down straight, so it was really hard when they told me not to move. I tried very hard to stay still, fearing that even the slightest movement would ruin the test results. Although difficult, I persevered tenaciously, thinking it was best to endure and get it done with. (Participant 11)

Patients were bothered by the vibrating noise throughout MRI. Theme 3: emotional consideration to relieve lingering negative memories of being overwhelmed by machines

Patients experienced anxiety, fear, and thoughts of death during the test as they were isolated in an unfamiliar environment. They perceived MRI as a test they would never want to repeat. They emphasized the importance of healthcare providers understanding the difficulties patients experience during MRI procedures and actively interacting with them to alleviate their concerns, offering emotional support and encouragement.

Uncomfortable emotions resulting from a machine-centered environment

Patients experienced anxiety and fear during MRI owing to concerns about their physical condition and uncertainty about their disease. Some patients experienced a death-like feeling, recalling a coffin because of the spatial characteristics of the narrow container in which the MRI was performed.I have never had to lie down in such a small space in my life. I was worried about my health and, simultaneously, I felt like I was going to die. It was scary because I kept feeling that I was lying in a coffin. (Participant 19)

I have never had to lie down in such a small space in my life. I was worried about my health and, simultaneously, I felt like I was going to die. It was scary because I kept feeling that I was lying in a coffin. (Participant 19)

Recognized as a treatment process that is extremely difficult to endure alone

Patients who received MRI for health management expressed a negative experience of the process for multiple reasons, such as isolation caused by spatial separation during MRI, insufficient emotional support, and a bleak environment surrounded by machines.I felt like I was dropped on a desert island alone. I did not have a cell phone, I did not have any family, and I was lonely, even if it was just for a moment. I felt like I did not have anyone by my side to encourage me that I would do well. If I am asked to have an MRI again, I will feel worried. (Participant 17)

Discussion

This study identified the care needs experienced during MRI from the patient’s perspective to provide patient-centered care. Specifically, three main themes emerged: information support and effective communication, delicate care that minimizes physical limitations, and emotional consideration to relieve lingering negative memories. The first theme was “information support and effective communication.” Patients reported a lack of sufficient understanding of the examination process. Repetitively providing information using audiovisual materials rather than verbal explanations before an MRI can reduce patient anxiety and uncertainty [7, 22]. Virtual reality can also provide vivid information and improve the understanding of medical examinations conducted in special environments, such as MRI procedures [2, 23]. Virtual reality allows one to experience situations like real life without experiencing them [24]. In this way, patients can experience both the visual and auditory senses encountered during MRI and receive comprehensive information about the process and its purpose. However, while VR is a promising tool, cost and technical accessibility remain barriers to its universal application in all clinical settings. Additionally, patients expressed the desire to receive continuous information through communication during MRI [6]. Interaction between healthcare providers and patients effectively reduces patient anxiety during the process [25], and simple explanations or guidance regarding the procedure can improve patient satisfaction [10]. Therefore, it is necessary to develop and apply a communication protocol that comprehensively delivers important information to patients during MRI, such as elapsed time, remaining test time, and overall progress.

The second theme was “delicate care that minimizes physical limitation.” Long examination times, noise, and space-related limitations can interrupt the MRI or lead to poor-quality imaging [2, 26]. Discussions exist worldwide regarding practical examination methods that provide a comfortable testing environment and alleviate movement restrictions caused by the closed space [27]. The physical discomfort experienced during MRI can be addressed by providing an alternative testing environment to efficiently obtain high-quality images while reducing patient movement and shifting their focus. This can be achieved by using devices such as audiovisual or sensory aids [26]. Efforts are underway to overcome the technical limitations patients experience when acquiring MRI images, such as closed spaces and noisy environments. However, since this technology is difficult to fully apply, various interventions addressing patients’ physical difficulties should be considered. For example, improving the design of the radio frequency coil, which detects image signals during MRI, or allowing changes in patient posture could help reduce discomfort in confined spaces [5, 26]. Another method includes building an audio system and applying music therapy to overcome the physical discomfort caused by noise [15, 28].Music therapy is an economical and safe method that can induce physiological changes and emotional stability by affecting the human brain and autonomic nervous system [29], although individual patient preferences for music genres must be considered for it to be effective. Thus, changes in equipment and the environment can divert patients’ attention during the examination; however, cooperation between healthcare providers is essential for comforting patients.

The third theme was “emotional consideration to relieve lingering negative memories of being overwhelmed by machines.” Patients experienced anxiety, fear, and, in severe cases, a feeling of being in a narrow coffin reminiscent of death owing to the enclosed space and uncomfortable sounds [6]. An alternative approach is being developed to replace these negative emotions by controlling the physical environment that affects patients’ sensory perception ability, for instance, by providing scent pads or birdsongs for comfort [26, 30]. Additionally, negative emotions experienced by patients can be influenced by the attitudes and behaviors of healthcare providers [25]. Bluntness or carelessness by healthcare providers can negatively impact patients’ experiences. In contrast, demonstrating interest in the patient and sufficient patient-centered interactions can provide comfort and stability [25, 26].

As consumers of medical services, patients expect tailored patient information, emotional support based on sufficient understanding and empathy, and therapeutic communication combined with the technical capabilities of healthcare providers [25, 31]. To provide tailored patient information, audiovisual educational materials must be developed to help patients fully understand the physical environment and the examination process during an MRI procedure. Virtual reality environments can provide such information [24]. Additionally, healthcare providers should support patients during the isolating and anxiety-inducing MRI procedure. Accordingly, they should understand the patient’s needs, provide relevant information about the process, encourage the patient to comply, and induce a satisfactory experience through empathetic interactions. Patient-centered interaction will contribute to accurate and systematic examination performance and improved satisfaction of medical service providers and patients [12, 32], leading to more efficient acquisition of diagnostic-quality images, effective treatment processes, and positive outcomes [26, 32]. The findings of this patient-centered study could provide a valuable platform for establishing tailored nursing interventions and an appropriate medical environment for MRI examinations and other examinations targeting patients.

This study has several limitations. First, the data relied on self-reported narratives, which are susceptible to recall and social desirability biases. Although we used iterative coding and analyst discussions to enhance trustworthiness, the limited use of formal member checking and researcher reflexivity journaling may have influenced the interpretation of the data. Future research should incorporate more rigorous qualitative techniques, including systematic member checking, reflexivity, and triangulation, to strengthen credibility. Second, the participants were relatively young (mostly under 65) and recruited from a single tertiary center in South Korea. It is essential to recruit older and more diverse patient groups across multiple sites and various MRI systems. Third, the cross-sectional design was conducted within a specific scanner environment and institutional protocol, which may limit the transferability of the findings to different settings or MRI systems. We suggest future longitudinal or interventional studies to validate and refine the patient-centered MRI strategy derived from this study and confirm its effectiveness in various clinical settings.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a meaningful, incremental contribution to the advancement of patient-centered imaging services. Unlike previous research focused primarily on diagnostic accuracy or technical optimization, this study captures the vivid, lived experiences of patients to identify their specific caring needs. Ultimately, these results facilitate the transition from a machine-centered environment to a truly patient-centered one, improving both the quality of diagnostic images and the overall patient experience.

Conclusions

This study identifies patients’ caring needs during MRI examinations and suggests directions for healthcare providers and hospital managers to minimize the difficulties encountered by these patients. Healthcare providers should understand the physical and emotional challenges that patients experience during an MRI procedure and take an active interest in resolving them. In practice, this means: (1) structured information and communication before and during MRI (2), strategies to minimize physical discomfort (noise, posture, confined space), and (3) deliberate emotional support to counter feelings of isolation and fear. The findings of this patient-centered study are expected to guide nursing interventions and support an appropriate medical environment for MRI examinations.
