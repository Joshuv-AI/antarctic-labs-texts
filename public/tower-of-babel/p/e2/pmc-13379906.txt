Beyond Device Sizing
Authors: Wei-Ting Sung, Ching-Wei Lee, Shih-Hsien Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: 3-dimensional imaging, bubble echocardiography, complication, echocardiography, occluder, stroke

Patent foramen ovale (PFO) is identified in approximately half of patients with cryptogenic stroke.1 Although PFO closure device is an effective treatment in preventing future events among high-risk patients, residual right-to-left shunting (rRLS) is detected in up to 25% of patients during long-term follow-up.2 Currently, no standardized guideline-directed management strategy exists for rRLS. From previous case reports and registry, the decision of second intervention may depend on the severity of rRLS, device design, etiology, and anatomical features.

Sizing of the PFO Device Is Inherently Difficult

In this issue, Kozak et al3 described a rare complication. After implantation of a 25-mm Amplatzer PFO occluder, hypermobility of the septum primum was noted, leading to mechanical abrasion and eventual erosion. Although the initial sizing instruction provided by the manufacturer listed aortic rim <9 mm as a contraindication for device implant,4 Stefanescu et al5 found that in real-world experience, device implantation in such patients did not lead to worse outcome compared with those with sufficient aortic rim, even with more than half of the patients receiving a 35-mm Amplatz occluder. Unlike atrial septal defect closure, PFO occluders have a thinner disc so the erosion risk is lower. Current sizing instructions did not put an emphasis on the deficit to aortic rim. In hindsight, a larger PFO occluder might potentially have provided broader disc apposition and stabilized the septum primum in this case, although this remains speculative.

Accurate sizing for the PFO occluder is challenging because of its irregular shape and the opening of the tunnel is not always visible.6 Even though the sizing guide provided by the manufacturer is straightforward, numerous clinical variables still influence the decision-making process. The author provided an important factor to be considered during PFO closure—not only is the preprocedural image important, the periprocedural echocardiography also matters. Although the PFO tunnel length and height can be measured under standard transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), 3-dimensional en face image is sometimes needed to measure the PFO width. Datta et al7 have proposed an innovative sizing technique called “wire sizing.” It measures the PFO features after crossing a guidewire through PFO tunnel. In complex PFO anatomy and those with large shunt, this maneuver could open the tunnel without repeating the Valsalva maneuver and avoid potential risk of septal rupture caused by sizing balloon.7 However in this case, the hypermobility of the septum primum was only visible after the deployment of the device. The authors demonstrated that during PFO procedures, in addition to confirming the relation between the device and the surrounding structures, tissue stability within the device also should be evaluated before releasing the device.

The Intervention for rRLS

The etiology of rRLS can be classified into intradiscal residual shunt, extradiscal residual shunt, and unusual cause. Although standard recommendation for the management of rRLS is lacking, most experts agree that intervention can be considered in those with large shunt or recurrent stroke.8 Treatment options include vascular plugs, coils, or another closure device depending on the lesion characteristics.

In this case report, the decision was more complex. First, tunnel tortuosity only allowed passage of a 0.014-inch hydrophilic guidewire. Sometimes a torqueable microcatheter such as the Corsair Pro (Asahi Tec) may facilitate crossing via rotational advancement, and then enable guidewire exchange. Nevertheless, guiding catheter and device crossing might remain challenging. Second, erosion-related rRLS is distinct from incomplete healing process or size mismatch given its propensity for progressive worsening. Stabilizing the floppy septum primum was crucial, as coils or vascular plugs were not viable options because they cannot securely anchor onto the eroded tissue between the discs. Therefore, it would be prudent to use another occluder overlapping the previous device to treat the rRLS. Third, the transseptal site is also important to avoid damage to the original device while ensuring sufficient closure effect. In most cases, transseptal puncture performed a few millimeters adjacent to the original disc ridge is safe and feasible.

Follow-Up After Device Implant

This case reinforces that procedural success at the time of implantation does not preclude late complications. This underscores the importance of systematic echocardiographic surveillance after PFO closure. Although early animal studies showed complete endothelialization at 3 months after implantation,9 Tanabe et al10 demonstrated the endothelialization may not be completed even 12 months after implantation. TEE performed at 6 months post procedure may provide insights into device stability. If rRLS is absent or only minimal rRLS was observed, annual transthoracic echocardiography may suffice, and dual antiplatelet therapy can be reduced to single antiplatelet therapy safely. Otherwise, repeat TEE every 6 months with agitated saline test and Valsava maneuver may be considered. As PFO closure continues to expand in clinical practice, optimizing preprocedural anatomical assessment and standard follow-up algorithms (Figure 1) remain important areas for future investigation.
