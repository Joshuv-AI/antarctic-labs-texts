Liver-bone organoids reveal senescence-driven interorgan crosstalk
Authors: Yingting Zhang, Yue Li, Fuxiao Wang, Zhenglin Dong, Jian Wang, Yi Chen, Yinkun Fu, Yuheng Lu, Xinyu Bao, Yuxiao Lai, Yingying Jing, Jianhua Wang, Jianping Peng, Chao-Po Lin, Jiacan Su, Ming He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe liver-bone axis plays a critical role in age-related diseases. However, current models inadequately capture its complex inter-organ communication. Here, we established novel, physiologically relevant senescent liver and bone organoid models using engineered bionic hydrogels combined with doxorubicin (DOX)-induced senescence. These models successfully recapitulated hallmark aging characteristics: bone organoids exhibited reduced mineralization accompanied by elevated senescence markers, and liver organoids demonstrated DNA damage along with structural deterioration. Notably, aged mouse serum effectively induced senescence in both organoid types, confirming the existence of systemic aging regulators. The platform demonstrated robust bidirectional crosstalk, with senescent liver organoid-conditioned medium potently driving degradation in bone organoids and senescent bone organoid-conditioned medium aggravating dysfunction in liver organoids. Mechanistically, we identified 27-hydroxycholesterol (27-OHC) as a novel hepatocyte-derived factor mediating liver-to-bone communication. 27-OHC not only induced bone organoids senescence but also synergized with DOX treatment to exacerbate bone loss, a finding corroborated by in vivo mouse studies that validated the relevance of our platform in the context of pathological damage. This study pioneers the first organoid-based platform that elucidates multi-organ aging mechanisms, uncovering 27-OHC as a pivotal regulator of liver-bone axis dysfunction and proposing novel treatment strategies for age-related systemic disorders.Keywords: Senescence, Bone organoids, Liver organoids, Bidirectional crosstalk, 27-OHC

Introduction

The global demographic shift toward an aging population has led to a marked increase in age-related liver and bone diseases, notably metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD) and osteoporosis, which severely impact the health and quality of life of elderly individuals [1,2]. Although epidemiological and mechanistic studies have established a clinical association between liver and bone disorders [[3], [4], [5]], the inter-organ communication mechanisms underlying this “liver-bone axis,” particularly in the context of aging, remain poorly understood. Current research models primarily include in vitro cell-based systems and in vivo animal models. Although cell models such as co-cultures of hepatocytes with osteoblasts or osteoclast precursors provide a controlled platform for investigating molecular mechanisms, they fail to recapitulate the structural complexity, multicellular architecture, and systemic physiological context characteristic of intact organs. Animal models, on the other hand, allow investigation of systemic interactions but are limited by species differences that can hinder direct extrapolation to human physiology, longer experimental timelines, and ethical considerations [6]. Given these limitations, organoid technology has emerged as a promising approach to more accurately model liver-bone crosstalk [[7], [8], [9]]. Liver and bone organoids can recreate key aspects of tissue architecture and function within a three-dimensional (3D) context [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. When combined in co-culture or integrated into organ-on-a-chip systems, they offer significant potential for uncovering the mechanisms of the liver-bone axis and advancing research on aging-related diseases.

Recent studies from our group highlight the liver's regulatory role in skeletal homeostasis, demonstrating that aged hepatocytes disrupt bone remodeling through secreted factors [4]. These findings underscore a functional liver-bone crosstalk contributing to age-related bone loss [14,15]. Despite these insights, a major limitation remains. While seminal studies have identified key molecular mediators of the liver-bone axis, such as the role of hepatic PP2Acα-LCAT signaling in hepatic osteodystrophy [3] and innovative nanotherapeutic strategies for its modulation [16], they primarily rely on conventional models. These models cannot fully capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of interorgan communication within a human-relevant aged microenvironment, a pathological niche driven by chronic inflammation and extracellular matrix remodeling [[17], [18], [19]]. Organoid technology offers a transformative approach to modeling inter-organ interactions in a physiologically relevant 3D context [20]. Recently, liver organoids have advanced through stem cell engineering and biomimetic matrices, and bone organoids have employed mineralized scaffolds to replicate osteogenic microenvironments [[21], [22], [23]]. However, current liver and bone organoid systems have predominantly focused on individual developmental biology or disease-specific states [9,20,24], with minimal attention to the crosstalk between these two organs. In addition, the lack of aging-adapted organoid models has impeded progress in dissecting the mechanisms underlying age-associated comorbidities involving the liver-bone axis.

To address this gap, we established an innovative organoid platform combining senescent liver and bone models using engineered bionic hydrogels, a biomaterial system designed to mimic the hybrid organic/inorganic extracellular matrix of bone, and doxorubicin (DOX) induction. This system recapitulated key aging phenotypes including impaired bone mineralization, hepatic DNA damage, and responsiveness to aged serum-derived factors. Meanwhile, the platform revealed bidirectional senescence propagation between organs, where senescent liver organoids exacerbated bone matrix degradation while senescent bone organoids worsened hepatic dysfunction. Furthermore, these findings provide the first direct evidence of 27-hydroxycholesterol (27-OHC)-mediated liver-to-bone communication in aging (Fig. 1). This integrated platform offers novel capabilities to investigate physiologically relevant interorgan communication, combining experimental tractability with faithful recapitulation of spatiotemporal liver-bone crosstalk dynamics. By transitioning from reductionist single-organ studies to an integrative systems biology approach, we have created a transformative research framework that simultaneously advances fundamental understanding of multi-tissue aging networks and facilitates translational discovery of targeted therapeutic strategies for age-related pathologies.

Materials and methods

Cells

Mouse bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were derived from commercial cell lines and obtained from OriCell (Shanghai, China). AML12 hepatocytes were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured as per ATCC protocols. BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. AML12 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10 % FBS, 10 μg/ml insulin, 5.5 μg/ml transferrin, 5 ng/ml selenium and 40 ng/ml dexamethasone. All cell lines were confirmed mycoplasma-free. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator, with the culture medium being replaced every two days. The concentration of doxorubicin (DOX) used for senescence induction was optimized prior to organoid formation. Primary bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) were treated with a gradient of DOX concentrations (0.1 and 0.5 μM) for 72 h. Cell viability and senescence induction were assessed using CCK-8 assay and SA-β-galactosidase staining, respectively. The 0.5 μM concentration was selected as the optimal dose because it induced a robust senescent phenotype (>60 % SA-β-gal+ cells) while maintaining cell viability above 80 %, thereby ensuring a high yield of viable senescent cells for subsequent organoid culture. To assess the time-dependent effects of senescence induction, organoids were treated with 0.5 μM DOX for two distinct durations: 3 days (to establish minimal sufficient induction) and 6 days (to achieve a more robust senescent phenotype).

Animals experiments

Eight-week-old C57BL/6 mice were purchased from SLAC Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Hydrodynamic transfection (HDT) liver cancer model was performed as previously reported [25]. HCC inducing oncogenes (PT3-NRAS/PT3-AKT) along with transposase SB100 were introduced into eight-week-old mice by HDT. For 27-OHC treatment, 2 days after HDT, 5.5 mg/kg 27-OHC/day were intraperitoneal injected for 20 days. 27-OHC (25 mg, Sigma) was dissolved in 2.5 mL ethanol and then diluted to 1.1 mg/mL in corn oil. All experimental procedures followed the approved guideline protocols by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine.

Bone organoids construction

Bioink preparation

Bioinks were prepared following a previously established method with modifications [23]. Briefly, 10 g of gelatin and 0.5 g of sodium alginate were each dissolved in 42 mL of deionized water in separate containers. Subsequently, 4 mL of 1 M calcium chloride and 0.6 M sodium dihydrogen phosphate were added dropwise at a rate of 50 μL/min. The pH of each solution was adjusted to a range of 8–10 under vigorous stirring. Afterward, the two solutions were combined, and 4 mL of methacrylic anhydride (MA) was added while maintaining the pH within the same range. The mixture was then reacted at 50 °C for 3 h. Upon completion, the reaction was halted by rinsing with warm deionized water, followed by dialysis at 37 °C for 7 days to eliminate residual small molecules. The resulting materials, referred to as Gel/Alg/HAP, were freeze-dried for future applications. The GelMA/AlgMA/HAP hydrogel exhibited a compressive strength of 170 kPa and a compressive modulus of 0.40 MPa, representing an approximate 400 % increase compared with GelMA alone [26].

Cell-laden 3D bioprinting

BMSCs were isolated following standard protocols (Bioactive Materials 45 (2025) 388–400). Briefly, femurs and tibiae were dissected aseptically, epiphyses removed, and marrow flushed in complete medium (α-MEM containing 1 % P/S and 1 % FBS). The cell suspension was centrifuged (800×g, 3 min), resuspended in complete medium, and cultured at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator.The cells were suspended in the bioink at a final density of 1.0 × 106 cells/mL. To fabricate the bioprinted constructs, BMSCs were homogeneously blended into a 10 wt% bioink matrix. The resulting cell-loaded hydrogels were incubated under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5 % CO2) to support cell viability and growth. Three-dimensional construct models were created using 3D Studio Max (Autodesk, USA), and the bioprinting process was carried out on a digital light processing (DLP) system (EFL-BP-8601 Pro, China). After printing, the structures were immersed in α-MEM culture medium to promote further cell proliferation and maturation.

Micro-CT evaluation

Bone organoids were analyzed using a micro-computed tomography system (Skyscan 1172, Skyscan, Aartselaar, Belgium). Scanning was performed at 65 kV and 80 μA, employing an aluminum filter, with an image resolution set to a pixel size of 13.8 μm. The acquired raw data were reconstructed using NRecon software, while quantitative analysis was conducted through CTAn. For three-dimensional visualization, CTVol software was utilized. The key structural parameters including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N) and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp)were extracted using the analysis tools integrated into the micro-CT system to evaluate the degree of bone regeneration. Error bars represent mean ± SD of biological replicates (n = 3 independent organoids).

Liver organoids construction

Derivation of liver organoids

The derivation of liver organoids was based on the protocol established by Broutier et al. [27]. with slight modification. Following euthanasia, the liver was excised from the mouse and minced into 0.5 mm3fragments using surgical scissors. The fragments were transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube and washed twice with 10 mL of wash medium (DMEM containing 1 % P/S and 1 % FBS), with each wash involving 10 times pipetting and settling periods of 3 min. After aspirating residual medium, the tissue was digested in 10 mL of filter-sterilized digestion solution (wash medium supplemented with 0.125 mg/mL Collagenase and Dispase II) at 37 °C for 1 h with shaking. The suspension was periodically pipetted and monitored microscopically for ductal structure release. Once sufficient structures were observed, the supernatant was collected, centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and washed twice with wash medium. The final pellet was filtered through a 100 μm strainer, and retained ductal structures were resuspended in 200 μL Matrigel, plated as 30 μL droplets in a 48-well plate. After solidification for 5 min at 37 °C, Matrigel droplets were overlaid with isolation medium expansion medium [(advanced DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 1 % P/S, 2 % B27, 1 % N2, 1 mM N-acetylcysteine, 5 % R-spondin 1 conditioned medium, 10 mM nicotinamide, 10 nM Leu15-gastrin I, 50 ng/mL EGF, 100 ng/mL FGF10, and 50 ng/mL HGF)] with 30 % L-WRN conditioned medium and 10 μM Y-27632). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 with medium changes every 3–5 days.

Passaging of liver organoids

For subculture, medium was aspirated, and organoids were dissociated by adding 300 μL TrypLE followed by 10 times pipetting with a 1 mL tip. After 4 min of room-temperature digestion, the suspension was neutralized with double the volume of DMEM/F-12 containing 10 % FBS, transferred to a 1.5 mL tube, and centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in Matrigel at a 1:4 split ratio, replated as 30 μL droplets, and solidified as described. Fresh isolation medium was added onto Matrigel droplets, followed by culturing at 37 °C, 5 % CO2.

Differentiation of liver organoids

To induce differentiation of liver organoids, isolation medium was replaced with the expansion medium for 3 days. Cultures were then switched to the differentiation medium (advanced DMEM/F-12 with 1 % P/S, 2 % B27, 1 mM N-acetylcysteine, 10 nM Leu15-gastrin I, 50 ng/mL EGF, 100 ng/mL FGF10, 50 nM A83-01, and 10 mM DAPT) for 9 days, with daily medium changes. Terminal differentiation was achieved by adding 3 mM dexamethasone to the differentiation medium for a final 3-day incubation.

Mice serum treatment on organoids

Serum was collected from 4-month-old and 14-month-old mice and stored in -80 °C. Following the differentiation of liver and bone organoids, 10 % mice serum were used in the organoid culture medium alongside existing FBS. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 with medium changes every 2 days for a week before detection.

Senescent organoids conditional medium treatment

After the initial successful establishment of the bone organoids, they were subjected to a three-week induction period using an osteogenic differentiation medium to promote maturation and extracellular matrix deposition. Subsequently, senescence was induced by a 6-day treatment with DOX. Subsequent to the 6-day DOX induction period, the organoids were meticulously washed three times with DOX-free culture medium to ensure complete removal of the senescence-inducing agent. Following this washing cycle, the organoids were re-cultured in fresh osteogenic induction medium and maintained for an additional 24 h. The conditioned supernatant was harvested at the conclusion of this final 24-h incubation period. Conditioned medium (CM) from senescent liver organoids was collected using a similar protocol.

Immunofluorescence staining

Bone organoids were frozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) and cut 10 μm thick sections. The sections or liver organoids were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (5 min, RT), permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 (30 min, 4 °C), and blocked with 3 % BSA (1 h). Primary antibodies against p21 (1:200, CST, 64016), γ-H2A.X (1:200, CST, 80312) were applied overnight (4 °C). After PBS washes, Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI (1 μg/mL) were applied (1 h, RT). The β-Gal probe was used as described by Hu et al. [28], XZ1208 (10 μM) was added to BMSCs, liver and bone organoids overnight. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope.

Cell proliferation analysis (CCK-8)

BMSCs (P3 and P23, 1.5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. At designated time points (0, 24 and 48 h), 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. After 3 h incubation (37 °C), absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Histological and immunohistochemical staining

Bone Organoids were fixed in 4 % PFA (24 h), dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (5 μm). H&E staining and Masson's trichrome staining were employed to analyze collagen deposition and tissue's structure in the organoids. Sections were then subjected to standard Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining. Mineralization status was evaluated through ARS staining.

Cells were fixed with β-galactosidase IHC staining fixative at room temperature for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS. The staining was performed by incubating the cells overnight at 37 °C (without CO2) in a working solution containing X-Gal, prepared according to the manufacturer's protocol. After staining, cells were observed under a light microscope. For short-term storage, cells were maintained in PBS at 4 °C.

Mass spectrometry proteomics analysis

Secretomes from liver tissues (young vs. aged mice), serum, or conditioned media from mice liver organoids (48-h collection) were concentrated (3-kDa filters, Millipore), trypsin-digested, and desalted. The MS spectrum was acquired using an Orbitrap Fusion LUMOS mass2 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to an Easy-nLC 1200 via an Easy3 Spray (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Western blot

Cells were lysed using SDS. Lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5 % non-fat milk, and probed overnight (4 °C) with antibodies: p21 (1:1000, Zenbio, R381102), p16 (1:1000, Zenbio, R23897), p53(1:1000, Zenbio, 345567). HRP-conjugated secondaries (1:5000, Proteintech) and ECL reagent (Bio-Rad) were used for detection.

Statistical analysis

Data represent mean ± SD of ≥3 independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups used unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. Multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (GraphPad Prism v9.0). p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results and discussion

Construction of senescent bone organoids using bionic matrix hydrogels via replicative senescence

To establish an in vitro model for studying bone aging mechanisms, we developed multiple strategies to generate senescent bone organoids. Consistent with established aging phenotypes in C57BL/6 mice, 14-month-old male mice exhibited characteristic significant age-related decreased osteoblast activity compared to 4-month-old controls (Fig. S1). Notably, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity was detected in both bone tissues and BMSCs from aged mice (Fig. 2A and B). We first evaluated a novel bionic matrix hydrogel with a double-network structure and multifunctional components for its capacity to support 3D bone organoid formation (Fig. 2C). After 4 weeks of culture, BMSC-laden hydrogels demonstrated significant mineralization capacity (Fig. 2G), as evidenced by BMD, BV/TV and Tb.Th analysis (Fig. 2H–J). However, primary BMSCs isolated from 14-month-old mice failed to form viable organoids due to their limited proliferative capacity in the 3D hydrogel environment, despite its biomimetic bone tissue composition.

To overcome this limitation, we adopted a replicative senescence approach using long-term cultured BMSCs (Fig. 2C). Serial passaging induced progressive cellular senescence, with a markedly reduced proliferation rates becoming apparent by passage 10 (P10) and more pronounced at passage 23 (P23) compared to early-passage (P3) cells (Fig. 2D). This was accompanied by increased SA-β-gal activity in P23 BMSCs (Fig. 2E). Notably, senescent P23 BMSCs generated bone organoids with significantly lower BMD, BV/TV and Tb.Th values after 4 weeks of culture, along with elevated expression of senescence markers (Fig. 2F–J). Collectively, these data establish that replicative senescence in BMSCs enables the successful generation of physiologically relevant senescent bone organoids within bionic matrix hydrogels.

DOX-induced senescence in bone organoids using bionic matrix hydrogels

Cell senescence is a defensive mechanism activated by various endogenous and exogenous triggers, like DNA damage, telomere disruption, and oxidative stress [29,30]. As a potent DNA-damaging agent, DOX induces senescence through double-strand DNA break accumulation [31]. To establish whether DOX could similarly induce senescence in 3D organoid systems, we first validated its effects in primary BMSCs. Treatment with DOX for 48 h significantly increased senescence markers (SA-β-gal), confirming its senescence-inducing capacity in vitro (Fig. 3A).

For bone organoid studies, we administered 0.5 μM DOX after 3 weeks of culture, with treatments lasting 3 or 6 days (Fig. 3B). Micro-CT analysis demonstrated time-dependent impairment of mineralization, with significantly reduced mineral deposition in DOX-treated groups compared to controls (Fig. 3C–F). Notably, the 6-day treatment group exhibited more pronounced mineralization defects than the 3-day group (Fig. 3C). To complement the micro-CT analysis and assess functional osteoblast activity beyond mineralization, we performed alkaline phosphatase (ALPL) staining and RNA-seq analysis. Senescent bone organoids (DOX-induced) exhibited a significant reduction in the number of ALPL-positive osteoblasts compared to controls (Fig. 3D–F and S2C). Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis of bone organoids showed that the expression levels of osteogenic genes Runx2 and Bmp2 decreased after DOX induction, while the levels of adipogenic genes PPARα and PPARγ increased (Fig. S2D). Senescence characterization revealed time-dependent increases in SA-β-gal activity, p21 expression, and γ-H2A.X in DOX-treated organoids, indicating successful induction of both the senescence program and DNA damage response (Fig. 3G and S2).

Comparative analysis of senescence inducers (D-galactosamine and H2O2) demonstrated DOX's superior efficacy in establishing senescent bone organoids, as evidenced by more significant reductions in mineralization and stronger senescence marker expression (Fig. 3H–L). These results collectively establish DOX as an optimal inducer for generating senescent bone organoids in bionic hydrogel systems.

Establishment of senescent mouse liver organoids via DOX induction

To model hepatic aging in vitro, we generated liver organoids from mouse ductal progenitors using an established 3D culture protocol (Fig. 4B and S3). Mouse liver organoids were further subjected to differentiation for gaining hepatocytes. Initial attempts to derive intrinsically senescent organoids from 14-month-old mice were unsuccessful, as evidenced by unchanged levels of SA-β-gal compared to organoids from 4-month-old controls (Fig. 4A and C). We then adapted the DOX-induced senescence approach for hepatic systems based on our success in bone organoids. Preliminary validation in AML12 hepatocytes confirmed DOX's efficacy, showing characteristic senescent phenotypes including reduced proliferation, morphological alterations, and upregulated markers (SA-β-gal, p21, p16) (Fig. 4D–F and S4).Given this, 0.5 μM DOX was added to the culture of mouse liver organoids for 3 and 6 days after the differentiation of mouse liver organoids into hepatocytes (Fig. 4G). We found that DOX induction of mouse liver organoids increased the level of SA-β-gal and p21. Moreover, γ-H2A.X staining showed that DOX treatment for 6 days induced substantial DNA damage, whereas 3 days of exposure did not (Fig. 4H). To functionally validate the structural changes in senescent liver organoids, we assessed metabolic aging hallmarks. DOX treatment significantly increased lipid accumulation (BODIPY staining), confirming steatosis (Fig. 4I). This work establishes, for the first time, that DOX-induced senescence can be reliably induced in murine liver organoids, thereby extending its utility as a cross-tissue aging induction strategy.

Systemic senescence induction via aged serum in liver and bone organoids

To confirm the existence of systemic aging regulators, we administered serum from 14-month-old mice to both liver and bone organoid systems. Strikingly, aged serum triggered robust senescence activation across tissue types: liver organoids displayed markedly elevated SA-β-gal activity (Fig. 5A), whereas bone organoids manifested coordinated upregulation of SA-β-gal, p21, and γ-H2A.X - hallmarks of both senescence program activation and DNA damage response (Fig. 5B). Functional assessments revealed aged serum-mediated impairment of bone matrix mineralization (Fig. 5C), with reduced deposition volume and decreased BMD, BV/TV and Tb.Th values compared to young serum controls (Fig. 5D–F). Together, these results collectively demonstrate the cell-non-autonomous propagation of senescence signals through blood-borne factors, establishing serum as a potent mediator of inter-tissue aging propagation.

Bidirectional senescence propagation between liver and bone organoids

Building on the systemic effects of aged serum, we hypothesized that paracrine signaling mediates inter-organ senescence propagation. To test this, conditioned medium (CM) was harvested from DOX-induced senescent liver/bone organoids after 24-h DOX withdrawal (Fig. 5G). Strikingly, CM from senescent bone organoids significantly enhanced SA-β-gal activity in recipient liver organoids (Fig. 5H), while reciprocally, CM from the senescent mouse liver organoids (MLOs) not only increased senescence markers (SA-β-gal, p21, γ-H2A.X) but also impaired mineralization (reduced BMD, BV/TV and Tb.N) in bone organoids (Fig. 5I–M). Together, these findings reveal a self-reinforcing senescence circuit, where both organ types actively exchange pro-aging signals through secreted factors, mirroring the pathogenic crosstalk observed in physiological aging. The conserved bidirectional propagation suggests circulating mediators sustain a vicious cycle of inter-tissue decline during organismal aging.

Proteomic profiling reveals metabolic reprogramming in aged liver

To systematically identify mediators of inter-tissue senescence communication, LC-MS/MS proteomics of liver tissues from 4-month-old and 14-month-old mice was performed (n = 5 per group). Principal component analysis (PCA) confirmed robust sample stratification by age, with clear separation between groups demonstrating data reliability (Fig. 6A). Comparative quantification revealed 646 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in aged liver tissue (398 upregulated, 248 downregulated), visualized by volcano plot (q-value <0.05 and 0.6 <|log2FC|)(Fig. 6B). Additionally, we performed gene ontology (GO) analysis on the significant DEPs, classifying DEPs into biological processes. Compared to the 4-month-old group, the 14-month-old group exhibited significant DEPs associated with biological processes, most notably metabolic reprogramming (fatty acid metabolic processes and lipid transport) (Fig. 6C). Similarly, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed revealing significant upregulation of lipid metabolic pathways and senescence signaling pathway, such as Retinol metabolism, mitophagy, DNA replication, and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Fig. 6D). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) specifically implicated steroid hormone synthesis as a hallmark of hepatic aging (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that aged liver tissue undergoes substantial remodeling of metabolic and secretory functions, particularly in steroidogenic pathways that may mediate systemic aging.

Proteomic profiling identifies hepatic sterol dysregulation as a mediator of liver-to-bone senescence propagation

To systematically characterize the molecular mediators driving interorgan senescence propagation, comparative LC-MS/MS proteomics of serum from 4-month-old and 14-month-old mice, and organoid-conditioned medium from senescent and normal liver organoids were performed. The aged serum proteome exhibited significant alterations in protein catabolism and oxidative stress (Fig. 6F and G), while senescent liver organoids exhibited significant changes in lipid catabolic process and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Fig. S5). Intersectional analysis revealed 10 conserved differentially expressed proteins shared between aged serum and senescent organoid secretomes (Fig. 6H and I). Notably, three consistently upregulated proteins - Pltp (Phospholipid transfer protein), ApoH (beta-2-glycoprotein 1), and Qsox1 (Sulfhydryl oxidase 1) - were particularly noteworthy due to their collective involvement in cholesterol trafficking and steroidogenic efficiency. These findings establish hepatic sterol metabolism dysregulation as a principal mechanism underlying the endocrine-like propagation of pro-senescent signals from liver to bone.

27-OHC as a novel hepatic-derived mediator of bone senescence

Building on our proteomic identification of sterol pathway dysregulation, we investigated 27-OHC, the most abundant circulatory oxysterol, as a candidate mediator of liver-to-bone senescence propagation. Strikingly, treatment with physiological concentrations (0.25 μM) of 27-OHC for 6 days induced characteristic senescent phenotypes in bone organoids, including elevated SA-β-gal activity and p21 expression without γ-H2A.X activation, establishing its distinct non-genotoxic mechanism (Fig. 7A). Functional assessments revealed that 27-OHC potently impaired mineralization (BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.Th reduction), while the 27-OHC and DOX combination showed synergistic exacerbation of osteogenic suppression (Fig. 7B–E). Histomorphometric analysis demonstrated that 27-OHC recapitulated key features of aged bone matrix degeneration: disrupted ECM architecture (H&E), diminished collagen deposition (Masson's trichrome), and impaired calcium mineralization (ARS staining) - phenotypes markedly aggravated by co-treatment with DOX (Fig. 7F).

To establish clinical relevance, we evaluated 27-OHC effects in an hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) model where elevated serum 27-OHC correlates with skeletal metastasis. Hydrodynamic transfection experiments confirmed that 27-OHC suppressed trabecular bone formation (reduced BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.N; increased Tb.Sp) (Fig. 7 G-K).These findings position 27-OHC as a central metabolic effector linking hepatic dysfunction to osseous aging.

Discussion

This study pioneers the first organoid-based platform to investigate bidirectional senescence propagation between liver and bone, revealing unprecedented insights into interorgan aging communication. By synergizing bionic hydrogels with engineered senescence induction strategies, we have overcome critical limitations of traditional aging models, enabling systematic dissection of liver-bone axis dysfunction. Our senescent organoids not only recapitulate tissue-specific aging phenotypes physiologically but also identify 27-OHC as a key hepatic-derived mediator of bone senescence induced by pathological damage, while uncovering a self-reinforcing vicious cycle between liver and bone that exacerbates systemic aging.

Conventional aging research using aged mouse models demands over 12 months of aging with substantial financial investment [32,33], whereas in vitro cell cultures fail to replicate microenvironmental complexity. Our platform addresses these limitations by efficiently generating aged phenotypes from young mouse-derived cells within 3D architectures, maintaining cellular diversity and tissue-specific features. We further advance this concept by establishing the first liver-bone organoid co-culture platform that models age-related interorgan communication. Through innovative combinations of bionic matrix hydrogels with replicative senescence or chemical induction, we generated functionally senescent bone and liver organoids that faithfully mimic key aspects of tissue aging, including reduced BMD/BV/TV in bone organoids and DNA damage in hepatic organoids. Notably, initial attempts to generate organoids directly from aged primary cells were unsuccessful. Senescent BMSCs from aged mice demonstrated poor viability in 3D culture, likely due to their compromised regenerative capacity. Conversely, liver organoids derived from aged ductal progenitors did not spontaneously exhibit senescence, as the progenitor cells themselves remained functionally competent [10,27]. These limitations underscore the necessity of the engineered senescence induction strategies employed in this study.

Our bionic hydrogel-based senescent organoid platform identifies circulating factors as key mediators of liver-bone communication. By combining matrix engineering with replicative/chemical senescence induction (e.g., DOX), we established bone organoids displaying hallmark aging phenotypes (reduced BMD/BV/TV, upregulated senescence markers) and liver organoids showing DNA damage/morphological degeneration (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Notably, the use of doxorubicin (DOX) to induce senescence warrants discussion. While DOX acts through acute genotoxic stress, unlike the multifactorial nature of physiological aging, it remains a validated model that recapitulates fundamental downstream hallmarks of cellular aging, including sustained DNA damage response, p53/p21-mediated cell cycle arrest, and SASP secretion—core features shared with natural aging [34,35].

The integrated proteomic and functional analyses identify 27-OHC as a pivotal hepatokine mediating liver-to-bone senescence propagation. 27-OHC, primarily known as an oxysterol derived from cholesterol metabolism, may influence bone metabolism through pro-inflammatory pathways and oxidative stress, contributing to imbalances in bone remodeling. For instance, in the lung adenocarcinoma microenvironment, 27-OHC enhances osteoclastogenesis by suppressing miR-139 expression and activating the STAT3/c-Fos/NFATc1 signaling pathway [35]. It suggests potential implications for metabolic bone diseases like osteoporosis, especially in conditions where cholesterol metabolism is dysregulated. Our integrated proteomic screening identified Pltp, ApoH, and Qsox1 as consistently upregulated hepatic proteins in aging contexts, with bioinformatics analysis strongly implicating them in cholesterol transport pathways (Fig. 6). While the precise mechanistic roles of these candidates in regulating 27-OHC biogenesis require future validation, their coordinated upregulation provides a plausible upstream mechanism for the observed 27-OHC overproduction in senescence. This proteomic-to-functional pipeline from identifying candidate regulators to validating their downstream metabolic effector (27-OHC) in vivo offers a comprehensive framework for deciphering interorgan aging communication.

Accumulating evidence indicates that metabolite-induced senescence can occur through DNA damage-independent mechanisms. For instance, age-related disturbances in some metabolites, such as α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), succinate, and fumarate, can lead to stochastic epigenetic changes, altering DNA and histone methylation patterns and driving senescence without initial DNA damage [36]. Based on our bionic hydrogel-based senescent organoid platform, 27-OHC was recognized to be one of these metabolites, inducing osteogenic senescence via p21 activation without DNA damage.This metabolite's dual role as both a standalone senescence inducer and a synergistic amplifier of matrix degradation (with DOX) underscores its central position in the liver-bone vicious cycle. Clinically, the 27-OHC-driven suppression of bone formation (reduced Tb.N) explains the heightened skeletal fragility observed in hepatic disorders like HCC [[37], [38], [39]], where dysregulated cholesterol metabolism fuels a pro-metastatic niche. These findings not only link hepatic sterol dyshomeostasis to osseous aging but also nominate 27-OHC as a therapeutically actionable target for intercepting interorgan aging crosstalk. Future studies will elucidate the precise molecular cascades downstream of 27-OHC and assess therapeutic strategies to disrupt this pathogenic axis in age-related comorbidities.

Our use of an HCC model to validate the role of 27-OHC in bone loss warrants further discussion. Although our initial focus was on aging and MASLD, we selected the HDT AKT/Ras-driven HCC model for several strategic reasons. First, it is well-established that MASLD can progress to HCC, representing a severe end-stage manifestation of metabolic liver dysfunction [40]. Second, this model is particularly relevant as it recapitulates key metabolic features of human MASLD-HCC, including hepatic steatosis and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [41]. Therefore, investigating 27-OHC in this context allows us to explore its pathophysiological role across a spectrum of liver diseases, from early metabolic dysfunction to advanced cancer. Our finding that 27-OHC exacerbates bone loss in this model (Fig. 7G–K) strengthens the proposal that it is a key hepatocyte-derived factor contributing to skeletal complications not only in metabolic diseases like MASLD but also in their oncological sequelae.

While our integrated liver-bone organoid platform offers a novel and tractable model for interrogating interorgan senescence communication, it is important to acknowledge its inherent limitations. A primary constraint, shared by most current organoid systems, is the absence of key physiological components such as vascularization, innervation, and immune cells [[42], [43], [44], [45]]. Specifically regarding the bone organoid model, it does not fully incorporate other critical components of the native bone marrow niche, such as osteoclasts or vascular networks. This design focused specifically on modeling osteoblast-mediated matrix production and its dysregulation by paracrine senescence signals, a central mechanism in age-related bone loss [43]. Consequently, our findings primarily illuminate the parenchymal cell crosstalk within the liver-bone axis. Notwithstanding these limitations, we have taken concrete steps to enhance the physiological relevance of our system: 1) by employing a biomimetic hydrogel for bone organoids that supports 3D matrix mineralization and multicellular differentiation, better mimicking the native osteogenic niche than standard 2D cultures; and 2) by utilizing adult duct-derived liver organoids, which retain mature metabolic functions (e.g., CYP450 activity) and epigenetic signatures critical for modeling interorgan crosstalk. Consequently, our platform successfully identified and validated 27-OHC as a soluble hepatocyte-derived mediator of bone senescence, a finding corroborated in vivo, thereby providing a valuable tool for deciphering core parenchymal communication mechanisms.Moving forward, a key priority will be to incorporate vascular, immune, and osteoclastic components to create more physiologically complex models. Such integrated systems would better capture critical aspects of systemic aging, including inflammatory processes, nutrient exchange, and coupled bone remodeling. Specifically, future iterations of our platform would greatly benefit from including these elements to more comprehensively model the complex cellular crosstalk and metabolic interactions that characterize in vivo physiology. To address the current lack of immune components, future investigations using in vivo models will be necessary to determine whether 27-OHC promotes bone senescence through the induction of chronic low-grade inflammation, as assessed by monitoring key inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Such advancements will further bridge the gap between reductionist models and whole-organism physiology.

The clinical relevance of this platform is underscored by the rising prevalence of age-related liver and bone diseases. The prevalence of MASLD, liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC increases significantly with age, with frailty prevalence exceeding 90 % in cirrhotic patients aged 70 or above [40]. Despite this clear clinical pattern, research has been largely confined to epidemiological associations due to the lack of suitable models that can disentangle the cell-autonomous effects of aging from other comorbidities. Our newly developed senescent liver-bone communication organoid platform addresses this critical gap despite its certain limitations in fully capturing the complexity of physiological aging. This system enables investigation of how senescence and SASP factors (e.g., 27-OHC) exacerbate lipid accumulation, inflammatory responses, and fibrotic progression in MASLD, or to elucidate their role in fostering a pro-metastatic niche in HCC [40,46]. Moreover, while this study primarily examines liver-derived signals affecting bone, the platform uniquely facilitates investigation of the reverse pathway: how bone-derived senescent cells and their SASP may drive hepatic aging and age-related pathology, potentially exacerbating liver inflammation, fibrosis, or HCC progression [33]. This bidirectional capability offers a comprehensive approach to deciphering complex interorgan communication in aging. Furthermore, comparative secretome analysis of organoids derived from diseased versus healthy tissues enables identification of novel disease-relevant mediators. Moving forward, we will actively develop next-generation systems that incorporate patient-derived human organoids from aging or MASLD-osteoporosis comorbidities, which could offer simultaneous hepatoprotection and osteoprotection by selectively interrupting physiologic or pathogenic liver-bone communication. Through this iterative optimization process, we aim to establish an innovative research framework that bridges fundamental discoveries to therapeutic interventions for age-related multi-organ dysfunction.

Conclusion

In summary, through the novel integration of multi-organ aging paradigms, we pioneer a transformative platform that mechanistically deciphers the spatiotemporal dynamics of liver-bone crosstalk, unequivocally establishes 27-OHC as the master regulatory metabolite orchestrating hepatic-to-osseous senescence propagation, and deliver a robust discovery pipeline for targeting interorgan aging networks - fundamentally advancing our capacity to intervene in age-related systemic decline.
