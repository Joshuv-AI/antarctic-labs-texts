Associations between hopelessness, negative thinking styles, and depression in Chinese adolescents: a large-sample cross-sectional study
Authors: Xiaomei Bu, Xiaosong Gai, Peipei Zhang, Xin Yu, Chan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrawing on Self-Determination Theory and the Hopelessness Theory of Depression, this study reconceptualized hopelessness along the dimensions of three basic psychological needs and meaningfulness, broadened negative thinking styles to include rumination and irrational beliefs, and systematically examined their associations with depressive symptoms in Chinese adolescent students.MethodsWe analyzed 17,561 students and assessed hopelessness and negative thinking styles through self-developed questionnaires, the reliability and validity of which have been verified. Depression was measured using the Children’s Depression Inventory and the Depression Self-Rating Scale for Children. Both variable-centered and subgroup analysis strategies were used to examine the relationships between variables.Results(1) Variable-centered analysis showed that, after controlling for demographic variables (including gender, grade, and family socioeconomic status), hopelessness and negative thinking styles explained 50% of depression variance, with explanatory power increasing with educational level; (2) Subgroup analysis revealed that 64.01% of depressed students exhibited at least one risk factor among hopelessness or negative thinking styles, but only 15.06% of non-depressed students. The depression detection rate was 66.77% among students with ≥ 1 risk factor, versus 1.44% among those with none.ConclusionAdolescent depression is strongly associated with hopelessness toward basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness, and meaningfulness) and negative thinking styles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03549-4.Keywords: Depression, Hopelessness, Basic psychological needs, Meaningfulness, Negative thinking styles

Introduction

Depression is a globally recognized mental health issue, with its prevalence exhibiting a rising trend, particularly pronounced among adolescents. From 1990 to 2017, the global incidence of depression increased by 49.86% [1]. In the United States, the detection rate of depression among adolescents (ages 12 to 17) increased at the fastest rate between 2005 and 2015 [2]. Among Chinese adolescents, after controlling for variables such as journal type, gender ratio, region, and academic stage, the year of data collection (1991 to 2018) accounted for 12% of the variance in depression levels. Compared to 1991, the average depression score among Chinese adolescents in 2018 had increased by 5.92 points, equivalent to a rise of 0.62 standard deviations [3]. Additionally, the “Report on National Mental Health Development in China (2021–2022)”, compiled by Chen Zhiyan’s research team, revealed that 14.8% of students in the adolescent group are at varying degrees of risk for depression in China [4].

The impact of hopelessness on depression

The hopelessness theory of depression posits that hopelessness serves as a proximal and sufficient cause of depressive symptoms [5]; empirical evidence has demonstrated the strong explanatory power of hopelessness in predicting depressive symptoms [6–9]. Abramson, Metalsky and Alloy’s proposed that hopelessness encompasses two key dimensions: (1) negative expectations about highly valued outcomes (e.g., the belief that one will fail to attain desired achievements or rewards), and (2) expectations of powerlessness to alter these negative outcomes, reflecting a sense of helplessness (e.g., the conviction that no amount of effort can improve performance) [5]. Network analysis has further identified a third core feature of hopelessness—perceived goal blockage accompanied by a tendency to disengage (e.g., the belief that one will never obtain what they truly desire) [10]. Thus, hopelessness consists of negative future expectations, helplessness, and goal blockage [11].

Under varying social conditions, the domains of high-value goals that individuals pursue may differ. For instance, in economically underdeveloped societies, there is often a greater focus on material needs, whereas in developing and developed societies, psychological needs may become more prominent. The fulfillment of autonomy, competence, and relatedness is crucial for healthy development [12–14]. When students’ basic psychological needs are unmet, their risk of depression increases [15–20]. For example, a study analyzed the data of 27 European countries from the European Social Survey and found that after controlling for demographic factors and socioeconomic status, psychological needs were significantly negatively correlated with depressive symptoms in each country [18].

Given that contemporary Chinese adolescents are particularly susceptible to feelings of hopelessness due to the frustration of these basic psychological needs, it is necessary to incorporate the thwarting of the three basic psychological needs into the conceptualization of hopelessness. First, adolescent students spend most of their times within the school environment, where their daily activities are typically controlled by the school. This can easily lead to feelings of helplessness and engender hopelessness regarding their need for autonomy. Secondly, the rapid development of the information network era exposes students to more opportunities for upward social comparison [21]. Additionally, high expectations and demands from parents and schools regarding educational outcomes—typically manifested by grades and admission rates—have been shown to significantly exacerbate academic stress among adolescents [22]. This atmosphere of competition and “involution” may render students more susceptible to perceiving goal blockage, thereby evoking the hopelessness associated with unmet competence needs. Lastly, the competitive atmosphere of the school environment undermines the spirit of cooperation, increases hostility among classmates, and weakens the social connection between each other. Similarly, in the family environment, high parental expectations and controlling behaviors strain the parent-child relationship, diminishing the bond between students and their parents. In such school and family environments, students are likely to experience hopelessness concerning the thwarting of their need for relatedness.

With the progression of modernity, Self-Determination Theory has incorporated an additional basic psychological need—the need for beneficence [23]. This need involves the desire to contribute to the collective beyond personal interests and is considered a component of the meaningfulness in life [24–26]. The sense of meaning in life refers to individuals’ perceptions of the significance and value of their current existence, as well as their pursuit of future meaning and goals, covering two crucial dimensions: presence of meaning and search for meaning [27, 28]. Previous research has demonstrated that the fulfillment of the sense of meaning in life not only enhances the experience of positive emotions [23] but also reduces the risk of depression among students [29–31]. However, contemporary adolescents are experiencing a sense of hopelessness stemming from the lack of meaning and the failure in their pursuit of meaning. They experience a sense of meaninglessness in life and even harbor no expectations for the future, which is consistent with the negative expectancy characteristic of hopelessness. For them, traditional life goals and sources of meaning have collapsed, while new goals and sources of meaning have yet to be established. Consequently, they are highly susceptible to entering a “vacuum” state, a form of depression known as “hollow disease” [32].

In summary, the present study defines hopelessness as a psychological state of despair arising from the unmet needs of four basic psychological need, namely autonomy, competence, relatedness, and meaningfulness, and examines the relationship between such hopelessness and depressive symptoms.

The impact of negative thinking styles on depression

The hopelessness theory of depression also posits that negative thinking styles increase the likelihood of experiencing hopelessness and hopelessness-related depression [5]. According to the theory, negative thinking styles manifests in three specific ways. First, individuals make negative inferences about the causes of adverse events. That is, when people tend to attribute negative events to internal, stable, and global factors, they are more likely to develop hopelessness and hopelessness-related depression. For instance, if a student attributes their failure in a math exam to an inherent lack of ability, this attribution style may heighten the risk of future feelings of hopelessness and depressive symptoms. Second, individuals make negative inferences about the consequences of adverse events. Specifically, when people believe that these negative consequences are highly significant, irreparable, unlikely to change, and potentially impactful across multiple domains of life, they are more prone to generalized hopelessness. For example, if a student interprets their failure in a college entrance exam as indicative of their overall worthlessness, this belief may trigger hopelessness. Third, individuals make negative self-inferences from adverse events, especially when they perceive the inferred negative self-characteristics as irreversible or unchangeable. This greatly increases the likelihood of hopelessness. For instance, after experiencing a breakup, if an individual concludes that they are inherently worthless, it may lead to feelings of despair and depression. All three manifestations of negative thinking styles fall under the category of a pessimistic attributional style.

Negative thinking styles encompass more than just the pessimistic attributional style proposed by Abramson and colleagues. They also include additional two aspects: The first aspect is Ellis’s concept of irrational beliefs, which includes low tolerance, demanding fate, perfectionism, and negative biases [33, 34]. For instance, individuals with irrational beliefs tend to interpret life stressors as catastrophic or infer negative self-worth from stressful life events. An increase in these irrational beliefs can subsequently elevate the risk of depression [35, 36]. The second aspect is rumination, which describes a persistent and often unconscious dwelling on one’s behavior and thoughts, as well as repetitive and ongoing contemplation of current emotional states, their possible causes and consequences, and the details of events, rather than actively seeking solutions to problems [37, 38]. The Response Styles Theory also posits that rumination can predict the onset of depressive symptoms [38–40]. For example, using a random forest-based artificial neural network model, Zhou et al. predicted adolescent depression risk and identified rumination as one of the top ten influencing factors [41].

The present study

In summary, to advance the application of the hopelessness theory of depression in understanding depressive phenomena among adolescent students, this study adopted the following approaches: First, we incorporated the four basic psychological needs from self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness, and meaningfulness) with hopelessness, thereby enriching and expanding the sources of hopelessness. Second, we expanded the concept of negative thinking styles by including irrational beliefs and rumination as operational definitions. This study attempts to examine how hopelessness regarding the fulfillment of four basic psychological needs and negative thinking styles relate to depressive symptoms in adolescent students.

Methods

Participants and producers

This study recruited 21,104 students from 32 primary and secondary schools in China. Participants completed electronic (65.58%) or paper-and-pencil (34.42%) questionnaires. Data cleaning excluded: (a) 979 duplicate electronic responses (retaining the most recent), and (b) 35 with < 50% completion rates on any one independent variable and dependent variable [42, 43]. After the above data cleaning, 20,125 (95.36% retention) participants were remained.

Drawing upon the validity scales in the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory [44], data were filtered by response reliability and consistency. On the one hand, four lie-detection items (e.g., “I never cry”) were assessed reliability. After reverse scoring where needed, items were converted to 0–1 using the calculation formula , and then averaged. The higher the mean, the more reliable the student answers. Based on the criteria for screening participants according to the lie-detection items in previous studies [45], the participants with an average score of less than 0.25 on the lie-detection items were excluded. On the other hand, eight1 pairs of items semantically similar or opposite were selected to assess the consistency of students’ responses (see Appendix A). For example, “I think I was as good as my classmates” and “I wasn’t as good as other kids” were pairs of items that were used to assess the degree of consistency. One of these two items was inversely scored as two questions with the same meaning, and if the participant didn’t answer randomly, then the participant’s score on the two questions should not be much different. When calculating the degree of consistency of the students’ answers, first, using reverse scoring to convert each pair of questions into semantically similar ones, then completing the normalization process using the above calculation formula to achieve the subtraction possibility among each pair of items, calculating the absolute value of the difference between each pair of two items, and finally calculating the average of these 8 absolute values as the score of each participant’s response randomness. The higher the score, the lower the consistency of the response. If the score is higher than or equal to 0.50, it indicates that the consistency of answers before and after is low, and there is a greater possibility of answering randomly.

Based on these two criteria, 2,529 participants were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 17,561 valid participants, yielding an effective response rate of 87.41%. The family SES comprises three indicators: household income, parental education level, and parental occupation status [46–50]. However, due to the privacy concern of household income, the family SES in this study only includes two indicators: the parent’s education level and the parent’s occupation [51, 52]. Previous studies have used the method of student reports to measure family SES in upper elementary school students [52] and middle school students [45, 52]. Furthermore, if some students really don’t know the educational level and occupational status of their parents, they can directly choose the option of “unclear”.

The educational level of the parents was assessed on a 6-point scale, ranging from 1 (primary school or below) to 6 (master’s degree or above). Occupations were coded: 1 = “jobless, unemployed, and temporary workers”, 2 = “service and manual labor employees”, 3 = “employees engaged in clerical or secretarial work”/“self-employed individuals with no or few employees”, 4 = other occupational options [53, 54]. SES scores were derived by standardizing and summing both parents’ education and occupation scores, then re-standardizing. In cases where students either failed to respond or selected the “unclear” option for any items regarding parental occupation or education level, these responses were treated as missing data. Consequently, SES scores could not be calculated for these students. Higher scores indicated higher SES. The family SES scores in this study ranged from − 1.88 to 2.11, with complete SES data unavailable for 3,937 student participants

Hopelessness questionnaire

The Hopelessness Questionnaire consists of 12 items assessing hopelessness in three basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, and relatedness) and hopelessness in the pursuit of meaning. (see Appendix B, Table B1). For the factor assessing hopelessness in the fulfillment of three basic psychological needs, one item was selected from the Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS) [55], while the others were self-developed based on SDT [12–14] and developmental characteristics of primary/secondary students. For the factor assessing hopelessness in the pursuit of meaning, four items were selected or adapted from the Chinese version of the Life Meaning Questionnaire [56], Schnell’s Meaning Crisis Subscale [57], and the Meaning in Life Development Status Scale [58], while the remaining two items were self-developed based on concepts of the lack of meaning and failed pursuit of meaning [27, 28]. All items were rated on a 5-point scale, ranging from 1 (does not correspond at all) to 5 (corresponds exactly). The average score of all items was calculated, with higher scores indicating a stronger sense of hopelessness. In the present study, items showed good discrimination (0.62–0.82) and internal consistency (α = 0.93), confirming the scale’s reliability. Confirmatory factor analysis assessed item loadings for self-developed Hopelessness Questionnaire. The model demonstrated acceptable fit indices: χ2 = 3849.48, df = 53, χ2/df = 72.63, CFI = 0.968, TLI = 0.961, RMSEA = 0.064, SRMR = 0.024. When the sample size is greater than or equal to 1000, it is not recommended to use the chi-square statistic to judge the fitting of the model [59, 60]. The CFI and TLI of this study were both higher than 0.90, the RMSEA and the SRMR was less than 0.08 [60, 61], indicating that the model fit was acceptable. All standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.57 to 0.88 (ps < 0.001), indicating strong item-factor relationships.

To examine the validity of the self-developed Hopelessness Questionnaire, an additional survey was conducted (n = 202; see Appendix C for details of participants and scales used). The convergent and criterion-related validity was assessed using the well-established Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS) [55, 62] and the Children’s Hope Scale (CHS) [63, 64]. The results demonstrated that the self-developed hopelessness questionnaire exhibited a significant positive correlation with the BHS (r = 0.748, p < 0.001) and a significant negative correlation with the CHS (r = − 0.416, p < 0.001), confirming its satisfactory convergent and criterion-related validity. To evaluate the discriminant validity, the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) [65, 66] was also included in the survey. The comparative method for correlation coefficients proposed by Meng et al. [67] was employed to examine the magnitude difference between two correlation coefficients. The results indicated that the correlation between the self-developed Hopelessness Questionnaire and the BHS (r = 0.748) was significantly higher than that between the self-developed Hopelessness Questionnaire and the SAS (r = 0.452), Z = 5.39, p < 0.001, which supports the discriminant validity and suggests that the self-developed Hopelessness Questionnaire is more capable of capturing the construct of hopelessness rather than anxiety.

Negative thinking styles questionnaire

The Negative Thinking Styles Questionnaire consists of six items assessing rumination and irrational beliefs (see Appendix B, Table B2). Two items were selected from the Ruminative Response Scale [68, 69], while four items were developed based on Ellis’s [34] theory of irrational beliefs, for example, “Fate must reward me in proportion to my efforts.” Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (does not correspond at all) to 5 (corresponds exactly). Higher average scores across the items suggested a greater degree of negative thinking in real-life contexts. In the present study, items showed good discrimination (0.44–0.68) and internal consistency (α = 0.69), confirming the scale’s reliability. Confirmatory factor analysis assessed item loadings for self-developed hopelessness questionnaire. The model demonstrated acceptable fit indices: χ2 = 450.46, df = 8, χ2/df = 56.31, CFI = 0. 972, TLI = 0.948, RMSEA = 0.056, SRMR = 0.022. Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.28 to 0.86 (ps < 0.001). Although the item “I often write down what I’m thinking about and analyze it” showed a relatively low loading (λ = 0.28), it was retained due to its theoretical relevance to the latent construct [62, 63].

To examine the validity of the self-developed Negative Thinking Styles Questionnaire, an additional survey was conducted (n = 202; the same participant sample used for validating the self-developed Hopelessness Scale was employed in this study; see Appendix C for the scales utilized). The convergent validity of the measure was examined using the Dysfunctional Attitudes Scale (DAS) [70, 71]. Results indicated a significant positive correlation between the self-developed Negative Thinking Styles Questionnaire and the DAS (r = 0.434, p < 0.001), confirming its satisfactory convergent validity. To minimize additional survey items and given the theoretical relationship between hopelessness and negative thinking styles, the BHS was employed not only to examine convergent validity of our self-developed hopelessness measure, but also to assess discriminant validity of the self-developed Negative Thinking Styles Questionnaire. The results indicated that the correlation between the Negative Thinking Styles Questionnaire and the DAS (r = 0.434) was significantly higher than that between the Negative Thinking Styles Questionnaire and the BHS (r = 0.224), Z = 4.28, p < 0.001. This supports the discriminant validity of the self-developed Negative Thinking Styles Questionnaire, suggesting that it can more effectively capture the construct of negative thinking styles rather than hopelessness.

Children’s depression inventory

The Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) consists of 27 items, scored 0 to 2, with 5 items requiring reverse scoring [72]. It has shown good reliability and validity among Chinese primary and secondary students [73, 74]. After reverse scoring, all items were summed to get the scale score. Higher scores mean more depression. A score >12 indicated the presence of depressive symptoms [75, 76]. In this study, CDI’s Cronbach’s α was 0.90, showing good internal consistency.

Depression self - rating scale for children

The Depression Self-Rating Scale for Children (DSRSC) consists of 18 items, scored 0 to 2, with 10 items requiring reverse scoring [77, 78]. It has demonstrated good reliability and validity in assessing Chinese children’s depression [79]. After reverse scoring, the total item scores form the scale score. Higher scores mean more depression. A score ≥ 15 indicated the presence of depressive symptoms [77, 79]. In this study, DSRSC’s Cronbach’s α was 0.89, showing good internal consistency.

Data

Statistical analyses used SPSS 27.0 and Mplus 8.0. Common method bias was assessed via Harman’s single-factor test. Descriptive statistics calculated means and SDs. Pearson/Partial correlations and Hierarchical regression analyzed variable relationships. Subgroup analysis identified high-risk factors in depressed/non-depressed students and detection rates across risk groups.

Subgroup analysis

Students were classified as “depressed” only if they scored > 12 on the CDI and ≥ 15 on the DSRSC; otherwise, they were “non-depressed”. Of 17,561 valid data, 3957 (22.53%) were depressed and 13,604 (77.47%) were not. Applying established extreme group analysis methodology complemented by theoretical median, the two independent variables were sorted in ascending order and categorized as follows: students needed to simultaneously meet both criteria of being in the bottom 27% of scores and scoring below 3 to be classified as low-risk, while those in the top 27% of scores and scoring above 3 were classified as high-risk. 2,533 (64.01%) depressed students had at least one high-risk factor, versus 2,049 (15.06%) non-depressed.

Students were classified into 4 groups based on combinations of the two risk factors. Among 1,309 students with at least one high-risk factor, 874 (66.77%) were depressed (Table 6). Students scoring low on both hopelessness and negative thinking styles (Group 1) exhibited the lowest depression rates (1.44%), whereas those scoring high on both factors (Group 2) showed the highest (81.08%). Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare composite depression scores between Group 1 and Group 2. Results indicated a statistically significant difference, with Group 1 (–0.79 ± 0.54) demonstrating substantially lower depression scores than Group 2 (1.67 ± 0.99), t(1126.06) = − 72.51, p < 0.001, 95% CI [–2.52, − 2.39]. The effect size was determined using the formula [81], yielding d = 3.62, which exceeds the threshold of 0.80, thereby indicating a large effect size [82].

Discussion

The present study introduces two key innovations: First, it extends the concept of hopelessness beyond hopelessness theory to include hopelessness regarding the satisfaction of the needs for autonomy, competence, relatedness and meaningfulness. Second, it operationalizes negative thinking styles by incorporating Ellis’s irrational beliefs and Nolen’s rumination.

Previous research has established that the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs [e.g., 15] as well as the fulfillment of meaning in life [e.g., 29] are associated with reduced depressive symptoms. The current study extends these findings by demonstrating that hopelessness regarding the fulfillment of autonomy, relatedness, competence, and meaningfulness needs shows significant associations with depressive symptoms among adolescent students.

What explains the robust associations between hopelessness regarding autonomy, competence, relatedness, and meaningfulness with depressive symptoms among adolescent students? Firstly, hopelessness in meeting autonomy needs might undermine adolescents’ intrinsic motivation [83], leading them to feel powerless in managing academic and social pressures. This perceived loss of control is considered a significant predictor of depressive symptoms [84–86]. Secondly, hopelessness in fulfilling relatedness needs may lead to feelings of isolation and rejection in social interactions, exacerbating loneliness and depressive states. For instance, left-behind children, who experience prolonged parental separation, may develop a pronounced sense of alienation, increasing their vulnerability to depression [87]. Furthermore, this alienation may deprive them of the social support crucial during significant life events, with social support being a well-established protective factor against depression [88, 89]. Third, hopelessness in satisfying competence needs may erode adolescents’ self-efficacy and sense of accomplishment. Self-efficacy has been identified as a negative predictor of depressive symptoms [90]. This hopelessness may also induce frustration and diminish self-worth, potentially leading to low mood, anhedonia, and a lack of motivation—symptoms commonly associated with depressive disorders [91]. Lastly, Erikson’s theory of psychosocial development posits that adolescence is a critical period for identity formation, during which individuals grapple with questions like “Who am I?” and “What kind of person do I want to become?” [92]. Adolescents who experience a lack of meaning in life often struggle with setting life goals, perceiving personal value, and finding purpose, leading to feelings of confusion and helplessness in the face of academic, social, and life pressures [93]. This identity crisis can precipitate depressive symptoms when adolescents are unable to successfully establish a coherent sense of self.

In the line with previous research [e.g. 36], the present study also identified a significant positive association between negative thinking styles and depressive symptoms. The negative thinking styles encompass irrational beliefs and rumination. On the one hand, Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) proposed by Ellis suggests that irrational beliefs, such as catastrophizing, low frustration tolerance, and self-devaluation, can lead individuals to experience excessive emotional reactions, including depression, when confronted with difficulties [33]. For example, when students encounter challenges in academic, interpersonal, or other life domains, low frustration tolerance may result in feelings of frustration and helplessness; in the face of uncontrollable situations, demandingness may trigger anxiety and frustration; unmet personal perfectionistic standards, may lead to disappointment and self-blame; and excessive focus on personal inadequacies may foster feelings of inferiority and despondency. These irrational beliefs heighten the risk of depression among students. On the other hand, individuals who engage in rumination in response to depressive symptoms tend to focus on the symptoms themselves, as well as on the potential causes and consequences of these symptoms. This tendency can hinder the adoption of effective problem-solving strategies, thereby prolonging depressive episodes [40].

Notably, our findings revealed that students with missing family SES data exhibited elevated levels of hopelessness, more pronounced negative thinking styles, and more severe depressive symptoms. Furthermore, the associations between these risk factors (hopelessness and negative thinking) and depressive symptoms were marginally stronger in this subgroup compared to participants with complete SES data. This suggests that the current study may have somewhat underestimated the effects of hopelessness and negative thinking styles on depressive symptoms.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, given the cross-sectional design employed in this study, causal inferences between the independent and dependent variables cannot be established. Future research should utilize longitudinal or experimental designs to further examine the predictive effects of hopelessness and negative thinking styles on depressive symptoms. Second, negative thinking styles assessment was limited to rumination and irrational beliefs; future work should incorporate attributional styles and Beck’s cognitive distortions. Third, the study omitted negative life events—a crucial factor requiring inclusion in subsequent research. Forth, the variables in this study were predominantly assessed using a single method of student self-reports, which may introduce potential common method bias. Future research should incorporate multiple data collection approaches, such as parent reports, teacher ratings, and peer nominations, to enhance data objectivity and result reliability. Finally, it should be noted that given the unique sociocultural context of adolescent development in China (e.g., collectivistic value orientation, familial expectation pressures, and other culture-specific factors), systematic cross-cultural comparative studies would be essential for precisely delineating the cross-cultural similarities and differences in adolescent depression risk factors between Chinese and Western contexts.

Implications

Despite these limitations, the present findings significantly advance theory of hopelessness depression by elucidating how students’ experiences of hopelessness, meaninglessness, and negative thinking styles serve as crucial proximal risk factors for depression. This advancement not only establishes a foundation for the early identification of students at risk for depression but also offers several actionable entry points for developing targeted intervention strategies for those experiencing depression. Specifically, when developing strategies for the prevention and intervention of adolescent depression, the following considerations should be prioritized: First, fostering adolescents’ autonomy, establishing positive social relationships, and enhancing their sense of self-efficacy are crucial. A supportive environment that encourages independent decision-making, strengthens social bonds, and provides opportunities for success can build psychological resilience and reduce depression risk. Second, enhancing adolescents’ sense of life purpose and meaning can help cultivate a positive mindset and mitigate depressive emotions. Third, identifying rumination and irrational beliefs among adolescents allows educators and mental health professionals to detect at-risk students and offer timely interventions. For instance, teaching students about the negative impact of rumination and irrational beliefs on mental health, and guiding them to reconstruct their cognition and develop positive thinking patterns, can effectively reduce depressive symptoms.

Conclusions

This study examined the relationships among hopelessness, negative thinking styles, and depressive symptoms in Chinese students across grades 4–12. After accounting for demographic covariates, both hopelessness and negative thinking styles showed robust associations with elevated depressive symptoms, with particularly strong effects emerging in high school students. Students endorsing both risk factors displayed heightened depressive symptom severity, while those reporting depressive symptoms were more likely to exhibit these risk factors. These findings not only expand the hopelessness theory of depression to adolescent populations but also pinpoint specific risk indicators for early identification and intervention programs.
