Evaluation of a deep learning model applied to chest X-rays of patients with suspected pneumonia presenting to the emergency department designed to predict admission risk: a retrospective training and prospective non-intervention validation
Authors: Eduardo Pimenta Ribeiro Pontes Almeida, Sameer Masood, Felix Baldauf-Lenschen, Thomas Jay Hennessy, Shazia Akbar, Yangqing Deng, Felipe Soares Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop, train and test a deep learning model Image-based PROgnostication applied to Chest X Rays (IPRO-X) tool that predicts the inpatient (IP) admission risk in patients with suspected pneumonia presenting to the emergency department (ED).DesignThe study consists of a retrospective (training) and prospective non-interventional shadow deployment (validation) of a deep learning model.SettingThree-hospital tertiary care system with two EDs.ParticipantsConsecutive adult patients (18 years old or older) who presented to the ED from December 2022 to February 2023 with a chief clinical complaint potentially related to pneumonia were reviewed for eligibility (n=5567), with a final number of 3677 included in the validation study.Primary and secondary outcome measuresIPRO-X was developed using standard two-dimensional convolutional neural network InceptionNet architecture and processes chest radiographs (CXRs) acquired at admission generating a continuous value from 0 (IP-negative) to 1 (IP-positive). We examined IPRO-X’s ability to predict IP admission using accuracy, area under the curve (AUC), receiver operating characteristic, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value. IPRO-X scores were compared against observed outcomes (admission and discharge) to determine clinical utility in an ED setting. Four thresholds were defined from the retrospective phase: Youden optimal, highest specificity and thresholds equivalent to 30-day mortality rates of Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) Risk class IV and Confusion, Respiratory Rate, Blood Pressure and Age 65 or older Score (CRB-65) scores 1 and 2. Performance was also analysed per group of chief complaints.ResultsIn the validation set, 3677 patients (1777 (48%) female, median age 56 years (min 18, max 99)) were included. IPRO-X predicted IP admissions with an AUC of 0.795, and in the pneumonia-specific chief complaints, AUC increased to 0.828, whereas in non-related pneumonia chief complaints, the AUC decreased to 0.755. IPRO-X score was significantly higher in admitted patients compared with discharged patients (p<0.001). The accuracy of IPRO-X when using the PSI-anchored threshold was 0.751 and with the CRB-anchored threshold was 0.729, with a specificity of 0.803 and 0.968, respectively.ConclusionsIPRO-X applied to CXR of patients with signs and symptoms commonly related to pneumonia in the ED can accurately predict IP admission.

Introduction

According to the WHO, lower respiratory infections were the most fatal communicable diseases in 2019 and ranked as the fourth leading cause of death globally.1 In North America, pneumonia is a common cause of emergency department (ED) visits2 and is the third leading cause of hospital admissions,3 resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality.4 Approximately one-third of patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) require hospital admission and have a higher risk of intensive care unit (ICU) admission and mortality.5 The COVID-19 pandemic has further increased the burden of CAP on patients and healthcare systems, exacerbating already long waiting times in EDs.6

Several clinical prognostic frameworks, such as the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) and Confusion, Respiratory Rate, Blood Pressure and Age 65 or older Score (CRB-65),7 8 have been developed to predict hospital admission and mortality in patients with pneumonia.9 However, these methods show questionable ability for guiding individual patient decision-making and for accurately identifying patients who are likely to deteriorate.10 11 In addition, the computation of 20 variables, including blood tests, required by PSI7 makes it complex and challenging to implement in the ER setting.12 CRB-65 is a framework that does not require any invasive investigation such as bloodwork, making this tool the preferred one in some practices.13 14 Chest radiography (CXR), a commonly used diagnostic and prognostic tool in evaluating patients with respiratory complaints, is performed in almost every patient with suspected CAP according to current clinical practice guidelines.15 16 Although CXR findings may be nonspecific, this test may be useful not only to diagnose pneumonia but also to demonstrate the extent of the pulmonary abnormalities and the presence/absence of complications or comorbidities, which may also act as prognostic indicators.17 However, CXR report turnaround times may vary significantly in the ED and have been linked to increased length of stay (LOS).18 Therefore, developing more efficient solutions applied to CXR to improve the risk stratification of CAP patients presenting to the ED can enhance clinical decision-making by identifying high-risk patients requiring admission and low-risk individuals for whom early discharge may be appropriate.19

Deep learning (DL) has been successfully applied to medical imaging, integrating imaging features that are not readily perceivable by the human eye and providing accurate diagnostic and prognostic information in multiple clinical settings.18 20 In patients with pneumonia, DL models applied to CXRs can diagnose and assess the prognosis and severity of pulmonary infections, including CAP and COVID-19 pneumonia.19 21 The application of automated DL methods to CXRs has the potential to provide useful prognostic information that could expedite treatment decisions and positively impact patient outcomes and Emergency Room (ER) flow.22 In this study, our aim was to evaluate the performance of an automated DL model that can be applied to CXRs of patients with suspected pneumonia in the ED to predict the risk of admission to hospital. A secondary aim was to assess potential time savings produced by the DL model.

Methods

Study design and settings

We present the training and testing (retrospective) and subsequent validation (prospective, non-interventional shadow deployment) phases of the development of a DL model (Image-based PROgnostication applied to Chest X Rays, IPRO-X) in a 3-hospital tertiary care system with approximately 905 beds and two EDs. We report this prediction model study in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis plus Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD+AI) reporting guideline, and provide the completed TRIPOD+AI checklist in the online supplemental appendix.23

Study patients

Patients in the retrospective phase of the study were split into training and testing sets (described below), whereas the shadow deployment phase (validation) was designed to validate the algorithm.

Train and test sets

In this retrospective phase, adult patients (18 years or older) who presented to the ED between January 2007 and June 2021 and had a diagnosis of pneumonia were considered eligible for participation (online supplemental table A1). Patients were included if they had a frontal (anteroposterior (AP) or posteroanterior (PA)) CXR performed as part of the clinical evaluation in the ED, and at least one recorded outcome, including discharge, hospital admission to a ward or ICU or death. Individuals with hospital-acquired pneumonia were excluded. Patients presenting to ED from January 2007 to December 2018 were included in the training set. Two test sets were created: one consisting of patients presenting to ED from January 2019 to December 2019 (prepandemic) and a second from January 2020 to June 2021 (including patients with a diagnosis of SARS-CoV 2 (COVID-19) infection) (online supplemental figure A1).

Validation set

The prospective, non-interventional, shadow deployment phase included all consecutive adult patients (18 years old or older) who presented to the ED from December 2022 to February 2023, with a chief clinical complaint potentially related to pneumonia. Patients were included if they had a frontal CXR performed during the ED visit and a recorded outcome, including ED discharge, hospital admission (ward or ICU admission) or death. Patients with hospital-acquired pneumonia (pneumonia diagnosed >72 hours after presentation) were excluded. Follow-up was performed up to 7 days after discharge from ED to assess for readmission (figure 1).

Sample size

Study size was determined by consecutive accrual during the prespecified shadow-deployment period, rather than being set using a formal prestudy power calculation. We screened 5567 ED presentations and included 3677 eligible participants (1890 excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria), of whom 2542 were discharged and 1135 were admitted (ward/ICU), with minimal risk of outcome misclassification. In line with TRIPOD+AI, we report both the total sample size and the number of outcome events.23 Given that the model predictor was IPRO-X score we evaluated performance using area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) and threshold-based metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV)), and used bootstrap resampling (1000 resamples) to compare these metrics across prespecified groups.

Data collection and management

Chest X-rays

The frontal CXR (AP or PA) obtained during the initial ED visit was used for training, testing and validation. All frontal CXRs performed between ED visit and the recorded outcome (admission, discharge or death) were used for training, while only the frontal CXR taken on the first visit (one per patient) was used for testing and validation. Lateral X-ray projections, if performed in conjunction with the frontal X-ray, were not used and were discarded.

All frontal CXR acquired in the ED were automatically de-identified using the Medical Imaging Resource Centre–Clinical Trial Processor24 and transferred to a separate storage environment where the IPRO-X model was applied. For the validation phase, IPRO-X was immediately applied after de-identification and model output was withheld from the ED team. In case of more than one frontal CXR being performed as part of the same CXR examination, only the first CXR was selected, except in cases where the frontal examination was repeated due to poor quality/technical factor (figure 1). Subsequently, a manual chart review was performed for each patient who had a frontal CXR obtained in the ED department during the study period to identify inclusion and exclusion criteria (figure 1).

Deep neural network

To develop an imaging-based prognostication system that predicts inpatient (IP) admission from an ED CXR, we used a standard two-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) InceptionNet25 architecture (online supplemental figure A2). The output from the model consisted of a probabilistic score between two classes: IP-positive and IP-negative, where 0 denotes IP-negative and 1 denotes IP-positive. As a preprocessing step, CXRs were resized to 512×512 pixels; and rows and columns near the image boundary containing black pixels (ie, no data) were also cropped out. During the training, various augmentation transforms (translation, scaling, flipping, rotation and noise addition) and dropout layers were implemented to prevent overfitting. Cross-validation was not performed during the model’s development as a method to prevent overfitting.

InceptionNet was pretrained with ImageNet weights for stable initialisation. Due to class imbalance, a focal loss (γ=2, α=0.25) function was also implemented to optimise for both classes during training. We used a learning rate of 2e−4 with an Adam optimiser, and a batch size of 32. Training was performed on a 12GB K40m Nvidia Tesla GPU for 25 epochs with 5 times augmentation.

IPRO-X predictions thresholds

Different IPRO-X thresholds were selected based on the analysis of the training set and according to the following criteria: (1) the threshold demonstrating the highest Youden’s Index (Youden optimal); the thresholds with equivalent 30-day mortality risk to (2) PSI and (3) CRB-65, and (4) the threshold with the highest specificity (i.e. the highest potential for hospital discharge). These IPRO-X thresholds were then anchored and applied to the validation set to evaluate their performance.

The thresholds for both clinical tools (PSI and CRB-65) were defined by a literature review. The PSI risk class IV (for which IP admission and treatment is recommended) is associated with an 8.2% 30-day mortality rate.7 The CRB-65 scores of 1 and 2 (for which IP admission and treatment is also recommended) are associated with a 30-day mortality rate of 13.42%.26

The IPRO-X scores that matched a similar 30-day mortality rate of both PSI and CRB tools were defined as the PSI-anchored and CRB-anchored thresholds.

Clinical characteristics and outcomes

All clinical characteristics and outcomes were recorded from the electronic patient records. The primary outcome was defined as admission to a ward or ICU (IP admission). Therefore, the ground truth was defined as ED visits resulting in IP admission (IP positive), or discharge from ED directly (IP negative).

Train and test sets

Basic demographics (age, sex) and comorbidities (online supplemental table A2) were extracted from patient charts. CXRs from patients who presented to ED within 30 days of the index ED visit and were subsequently admitted were also labelled as IP positive (online supplemental table A2).

Validation set

A manual chart review of all patients was performed to extract clinical variables. Demographic characteristics, signs and symptoms at presentation, including vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure and respiratory rate) and chief complaint, and the presence of comorbidities were recorded. CRB-65 was calculated by adding 1 point for each component of the score (confusion, respiratory rate ≥30, systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≤60 mm Hg, and age ≥65 years), ranging from 0 to 4.8 Given the fact that blood urea was not available in the majority of patients, CURB-65 was not calculated. The results of tests for the presence of respiratory viruses (COVID, respiratory syncytial virus and influenza) were obtained when positive. The diagnostic hypothesis at initial assessment as well as the recorded diagnosis at discharge were also recorded, in addition to disposition destination (admission to ward, ICU or discharge to home or long-term care facility). Readmission to hospital/ED was assessed 7 days after discharge from ED.

Multiple time stamps were recorded for each patient, including the time of presentation to ED, the time CXR was performed, the time the CXR report was completed by a radiologist, the time of ED outcome (discharge, admission or death), and the time of ED disposition (when the ED team decided to discharge or admit the patient). Patients who had the CXR performed from 5:00 to before 22:00 were classified as daytime patients and patients who had the CXR performed from 22:00 to before 5:00 of the following day were classified as night-time patients. LOS was calculated by adding the total time patients remained in the ED (ED LOS) and in the hospital (Overall LOS).

Data analysis

Training and testing

Statistical analysis was performed at the dataset level where the AUC of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, sensitivity, specificity, NPV and PPVs are reported. Thresholds were determined from the retrospective validation set and applied to the validation sets.

Validation

Patients were separated into two groups, depending on the chief complaint at presentation to the ED (online supplemental table A3). The chief complaint was recorded by the triage physician/nurse and registered in the electronic patient records at the time of the initial clinical assessment. Only one chief complaint was recorded per patient. For classification purposes, chief complaints considered more likely to be related to pneumonia (group 1) were shortness of breath, general weakness, fever, chest pain with non-cardiac features and cough/congestion. Other chief complaints deemed less likely to be related to pneumonia (group 2) included, for example, chest pain with cardiac features, night sweat, palpitations/irregular heartbeat, sore throat.

Statistical analysis was performed to examine IPRO-X’s ability to predict IP admission and discharge in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, ROC and AUC. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were compared using the bootstrap method with 1000 bootstrap samples, resampling under the null hypothesis of equal performance across subgroups. For performance metrics, no normality assumption is required. Four thresholds were chosen and compared: (1) the threshold demonstrating the highest Youden’s Index (in order to demonstrate the overall performance of the model) and (2) threshold that yields the highest specificity (as a rule out tool), and thresholds that lead to equivalent 30-day mortality rates of PSI Risk class IV and CRB-65 scores 1 and 2, respectively (these thresholds may be used to guide IP admission).7 26 Sample sizes and cell counts were sufficiently large for the normal approximation, so no normality assessment was required. The performance of IPRO-X was also analysed per group of chief complaints. Sensitivity analysis was performed using age (65 years old or older/<65), sex (male/female), and presence or absence of comorbidities, cancer diagnosis or transplant diagnosis, as well as CXR technique (PA/AP). The two continuous variables tested across groups in table 1 were age and number of comorbidities. To maintain consistency, we applied the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test to both variables, which does not require a normality assumption. We chose the non-parametric method because the distribution of the number of comorbidities, a count variable, was not normal. The difference between admitted and discharged in IPRO-X score was also assessed using boxplots and Mood’s median test, a non-parametric test because the distributions did not appear normal based on the boxplots (figure 3). For the subgroup analyses, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were again compared using the bootstrap method with 1000 bootstrap samples, resampling under the null hypothesis of equal performance across subgroups. For table 2, the bootstrap method was used to obtain p values for comparisons of performance metrics. For tables 3 and 4, we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare daytime versus night-time. The analysis was conducted in R (V.4.0.5).

Missing data

There were no missing data for the primary outcome, as ED disposition (discharge vs IP admission) was available for all participants included in the analysis. For the IPRO-X score, 18 participants were excluded due to poor-quality chest radiographs, and these exclusions were applied before model evaluation (figure 1).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Patient characteristics and outcomes

Training and testing

A total of 13 142 patients were included in the training set and 4223 patients in the test set. Basic demographics and outcomes are described in online supplemental table A2. In the training set, 73% of patients were admitted to hospital with a diagnosis of pneumonia, 11% were admitted to ICU and the in-hospital mortality was 9%. In the test set, the admission rate was 52% in 2019 and 50% in 2020–2021, and the in-hospital mortality was 4% and 6%, respectively (online supplemental table A4).

Validation

A total of 3677 patients, median age of 57 (range, 18–99) and 1900 (51.6%) males, were included in the prospective, shadow deployment phase of the study. Baseline information is summarised in table 1. Patients in group 1 were older, had more comorbidities and a higher prevalence of cancer diagnosis, prior organ transplant and positive COVID-19 testing (table 1). The most frequent comorbidity was hypertension, and most patients (92.4%) had a low CRB-65 score (0 and 1). Most patients (69%) were discharged from the ED, and the mean LOS in the ED was 6.8 hours (SD=4.7 hours), with an in-hospital mortality of 2%. Patients in group 1 were more frequently admitted to the ward and had a higher in-hospital mortality (table 1). Out of the 2546 discharged patients, 188 (5%) presented to the ED in less than 7 days after discharge.

IPRO-X performance

Training and testing

In the retrospective test set, IPRO-X demonstrated an AUC for predicting IP admission of 0.804, with an AUC 0.82 in 2020–2021 in comparison to an AUC of 0.786 in 2019 (prepandemic). Sensitivity, specificity, NPV, PPV and accuracy for the retrospective phase are demonstrated in online supplemental table A4.

The corresponding IPRO-X thresholds to predict IP admission in the retrospective testing set which showed a similar 30-day mortality rate as PSI risk class IV was 0.655 and as CRB-65 1 and 2 was 0.962. The IPRO-X threshold with the highest Youden’s index in the retrospective set was 0.75 (AUC 0.804) and the one with the highest specificity was 0.10 (specificity 98.3%).

Validation

In the validation set, IPRO-X showed an AUC of 0.795 for predicting IP admission (figure 2). IPRO-X threshold anchored on PSI risk class IV predicted IP admission of approximately 33.2% of patients with a sensitivity of 63.4% and specificity of 80.3%. When IPRO-X threshold was anchored on CRB-65 scores 1 and 2, the specificity was 96.8%, with a 19.5% sensitivity (table 2). Also, the performance of IPRO-X with the Youden optimal threshold showed a specificity of 85% and a sensitivity of 54.3%, and with the highest specificity showed a sensitivity of 28.4% and a specificity of 95.9%. In patients from group 1 (those with symptoms more commonly associated with a respiratory tract infection), the overall performance improved compared with the full validation set. PSI-anchored threshold predicted IP admission of approximately 36.8% patients, with a sensitivity of 67.4% and specificity of 80.7%. Also, CRB-anchored threshold predicted IP admission of approximately 9.7% patients, with a sensitivity of 22.3% and specificity of 97.6%. In addition, an IPRO-X threshold of <0.1 (highest specificity) would allow the discharge of approximately 17.7% of patients with a sensitivity of 26.3%, specificity of 95.9% and a positive predictive value of 95%.

Sensitivity analysis demonstrated improved accuracy of IPRO-X ’Youden optimal, PSI-anchored and CRB-anchored thresholds for predicting IP admission in patients with <65 years of age, in patients without comorbidities and in patients without prior organ transplant or cancer diagnosis (online supplemental tables A5 and A7–A9). In addition, IPRO-X demonstrated improved overall accuracy using the Youden optimal, PSI-anchored and CRB-anchored thresholds in PA CXR technique in comparison to AP technique (Tableonline supplemental file 1 A10). There was no difference in the accuracy of IPRO-X performance according to sex (online supplemental table A6).

For the IPRO-X highest-specificity threshold (rule out tool), specificity was higher in older patients (0.987 vs 0.913), in patients with comorbidities (0.974 vs 0.916) and in patients with a cancer diagnosis (1.000 vs 0.952). Specificity was also higher for the AP CXR technique (0.996×0.928).

There was no meaningful change in the performance of the different IPRO-X thresholds between males and females.

The time from CXR acquisition to ED outcome was similar between patients who had a CXR acquired during daytime vs nighttime (table 3). However, the time between the acquisition of the CXR and the availability of the radiology report to the ED team was significantly higher during nighttime patients (5.3 hours) in comparison to daytime patients (0.9 hours) (table 3). In addition, the time between the availability of the radiology report to the ED team and the ED outcome was negative and significantly lower for nighttime patients (−0.7 hours vs 3.1 hours, respectively), meaning that decisions regarding admission or discharge during nighttime were generally made before the radiology report became available. Also, the ED LOS of night-time patients was significantly longer in comparison to daytime patients (7.6 (SD=6.2 hours) vs 6.5 (SD=4.1 hours); p<0.001) (table 4).

Discussion

Assessment of patients with suspected pneumonia in the ED depends not only on the computation of multiple clinical variables, but on the performance and timely interpretation of a chest radiograph. In this context, we developed a DL CNN (IPRO-X) to automatically assess frontal chest radiographs of patients presenting to the ED with signs and symptoms potentially related to pneumonia to predict IP admission, and we found that IPRO-X was able to predict admission and discharge accurately. In the current study, IPRO-X was prospectively validated in a consecutive sample of patients presented to the ED, who had a chest radiograph performed and who had signs and symptoms commonly associated with pneumonia. IPRO-X demonstrated an overall AUC of 0.795 in predicting IP admission, similar to the results of the retrospective testing phase. Furthermore, when selecting chief complaints more closely associated with pneumonia, IPRO-X had its performance improved (AUC 0.828). In addition, IPRO results were generated automatically after image acquisition, potentially offering support for clinicians at a time when the turnaround time for reporting X-rays was significantly higher and when LOS in the ED was longer (night-time).

Multiple clinical factors, including the presence of comorbidities (ie, lung disease, heart failure, diabetes, immunodeficiencies), concomitant immunosuppressive treatments and the lack of social support, are considered for the decision to admit/discharge a patient with pneumonia.16 Risk stratification tools, such as CRB-65, CURB-65 and PSI, can combine multiple clinical and imaging findings to derive a more complete prognostic picture of each individual patient. Nonetheless, its clinical utility may be limited.12 Chest radiographs have an essential role not only in establishing the diagnosis but also in helping to assess the prognosis of patients with pneumonia, ultimately guiding levels of care.16 The amount of information present in a chest radiograph is not limited to the direct consequences of the pulmonary infection but includes evidence of multiple other clinical features that can add important prognostic information, such as cardiomegaly/heart failure, osteoporosis/bone fractures, thickness of subcutaneous/chest wall tissues, chest tumours and so on. Therefore, the combination of all these and other features present in the chest X-ray may help in risk stratification. IPRO-X demonstrated an excellent performance for predicting which patients will be admitted with an AUC of 0.795. This result is comparable to the performance of other risk stratification tools, such as PSI,27 with important gains in efficiency. IPRO-X runs automatically and immediately after the radiograph is acquired, without the need for data collection or for computation of multiple variables. When stratifying a broad spectrum of chief complaints into a smaller number of pneumonia-related signs and symptoms,16 IPRO-X demonstrated an improvement in performance for predicting patient admission (AUC 0.828). Considering IPRO-X was trained on patients with a diagnosis of pneumonia, this increased performance is understandable since more specific chief complaints are more closely related to pneumonia.28

IPRO-X demonstrated improved overall accuracy for predicting IP admission in patients with <65 years of age, in patients without comorbidities and in patients without prior organ transplant or cancer diagnosis. These results are not surprising, since these patients tend to have a smaller number of confounding clinical factors that could drive disposition decisions. Notably, when selecting the highest specificity IPRO-X threshold, the specificity was very high in older patients (0.987), in patients with comorbidities (0.974) and in patients with a cancer diagnosis (1.000). These results show a promising utility of IPRO-X as a ruling out tool in these populations presenting to the ED.

Several prior studies have evaluated DL models applied to CXR in patients with suspected pneumonia in the ED. Most of them assessed the diagnostic value of these models to detect/diagnose pneumonia22 or to differentiate pneumonia from other pathologies.29 Others investigated the ability of the DL models in predicting disease severity, including ICU admission and mortality, most involving SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19).30 31 In the retrospective testing phase of our study, the AUC for predicting IP admission was 0.821 for the years of 2020–2021, including patients with a diagnosis of COVID-19. These results are comparable to studies assessing outcomes (need for intubation and death) in CXR of patients with COVID-19 pulmonary disease using a Convolutional Siamese Neural Network,19 or studies assessing intensive care need in COVID-19 patients by applying DL to CXR.32 Due to the low number of COVID-19 diagnoses during the prospective validation phase, it was not possible to assess the performance of the model in this specific population. Other study used a combination of AI applied to CXR and clinical data to predict patient outcomes.33 It is conceivable that the addition of clinical variables to a model evaluating the CXR would generate an improved prediction in comparison to a model assessing the CXR only. Clinical prediction rules, such as PSI and the American Thoracic Society (ATS) severity score, include not only CXR findings (ie, the presence of multilobar infiltrates and pleural effusions, respectively) but many clinical variables and are currently recommended to guide the level of care of patients with pneumonia.16 A model that generates an automated prediction directly from the CXR could expedite management decisions, without the need for collection/computation of additional variables.

In our study settings, the automatic results provided by IPRO-X could be particularly beneficial during the nighttime period (22:00 to 5:00), where the turnaround time for reporting X-rays was significantly higher when compared with the day (0.9 hours vs 5.4 hours), often resulting in disposition decisions being made before the CXR report becomes available. Also, the LOS in the ED of the nighttime patients was significantly higher than the daytime patients (7.6 hours vs 6.5 hours). Having radiology coverage for the ED has been reported to improve the workflow and to reduce the ED LOS of patients requiring CXR.34 According to our results, a potential gain of approximately 4 hours in LOS, on average, could be expected for very low-risk patients if IPRO results were evaluated by the ED team immediately after X-ray acquisition. Another hypothesis that requires additional testing is the potential impact on ED occupancy rates which could be significant, as it is an area that AI can improve in emergency medicine.35

There were limitations to this study. First, IPRO-X was trained and tested (retrospective phase) for the prediction of admission of patients with a diagnosis of pneumonia. In the prospective, shadow deployment validation phase, IPRO-X predicted admission in patients with signs and symptoms commonly associated with the diagnosis of pneumonia. This difference was driven by the substitution of the Electronic Medical Records of the hospital system in between the two phases, enabling the identification of specific Chief Complaints in the validation phase, which was not possible during the retrospective phase. Although a decrease in performance could be expected, IPRO-X maintained the performance during the validation phase, particularly for patients with chief complaints more closely associated with the diagnosis of pneumonia. Second, we were not able to compare IPRO-X with more extensive clinical risk stratification tools such as PSI due to the lack of multiple clinical variables required to compose the score. Nonetheless, this reflects the lack of real-life clinical utility of this clinical tool, which seems to be too complex for clinical use. Blood urea nitrogen was also not available in most patients, precluding the calculation of CURB-65. Of note, performance during the peak of COVID-19 pandemic was slightly improved, despite the fact the model was trained in studies dating from before the pandemic. Third, a small subset of studies with low image quality were excluded from the validation set. Therefore, the model is only applicable to CXR with reasonable quality, meaning in which the entire lungs were included within the field of view. Fourth, the present study was performed in a single hospital system, and deployment in a different setting would require additional validation. Nonetheless, our model demonstrated consistent performance during training/testing and prospective validation sets, spanning a 15-year period. Also, in-hospital mortality of the validation set was approximately 2%, which implies a low-risk population. Results may not be fully generalisable to a higher risk population. Lastly, we were not able to evaluate the performance of the model according to race since this variable was not available. The diversity of race and ethnicity of patients seen in our ED department, nonetheless, reflects the broad diversity of the local population.36 In keeping with TRIPOD+AI, we therefore frame transportability as strongest to similar adult ED populations where CXR is routinely obtained for suspected pneumonia, and we note that external validation across additional sites (including non-tertiary EDs) and other seasons is needed to confirm generalisability.

In summary, an automatic DL-based tool, IPRO-X, when applied to chest radiographs of patients presenting to the ED with signs and symptoms commonly associated with pneumonia can predict patient admission and identify patients at low risk for admission. In addition, IPRO-X may provide support for clinicians in circumstances when the radiology report is not available. Further validation is required to assess the ability of this tool in expediting disposition decisions in the ED.
