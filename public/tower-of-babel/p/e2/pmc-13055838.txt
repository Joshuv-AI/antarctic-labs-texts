Modifiable Lifestyle Factors, Left Atrial Indices, and Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Adi Elias, James P Pirruccello, Francesca N Delling, Catherine Lee, Gregory M Marcus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft atrial (LA) size is an important determinant of atrial fibrillation (AF) risk and predicts stroke and death. The relationship between common lifestyle factors, LA characteristics, and relationships with incident AF remain largely unknown.MethodsIn this cohort study, we evaluated 37 701 UK Biobank participants without prevalent AF and with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging data. Body mass index was measured by study personnel, while other lifestyle factors were obtained from baseline questionnaires. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was performed using a uniformly applied protocol and analyzed using automated methods to determine LA volumes and emptying fraction (EF).ResultsThe magnetic resonance imaging was performed a median 8.9 (interquartile range, 7.4–10) years after the baseline lifestyle factor assessment. In multivariable analyses, a larger body mass index, greater physical activity intensity, and more alcohol intake were each significantly associated with larger LA volumes and smaller LA EFs. In contrast, cumulative pack‐years of smoking were linked to smaller LA volumes and LA EFs. Coffee consumption exhibited no significant associations. Larger LA maximal and minimal volumes and a lower LA EF statistically mediated 25%, 28%, and 26% of the association between an increased body mass index and risk of incident AF, respectively, evaluated in separate models. A lower LA EF mediated 27% of the smoking and AF association.ConclusionsCommon lifestyle factors are associated with unfavorable LA size and function, mediating about a quarter of the AF risk associated with obesity and smoking. Lifestyle‐related structural LA changes may contribute to AF risk.Keywords: alcohol, atrial fibrillation, left atrium, lifestyle, obesity, physical activity, smokingSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Efforts to promote healthier lifestyles may lower atrial fibrillation risk in part by affecting structural and functional left atrial parameters.

Efforts to promote healthier lifestyles may lower atrial fibrillation risk in part by affecting structural and functional left atrial parameters.

Left atrial (LA) size and function are important predictors of incident atrial fibrillation (AF), stroke, and death. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Even relatively small changes in LA size and function are associated with these heightened risks, 1 , 6 , 7 , 8 suggesting that these indices may serve as early heralds of poor outcomes and provide insights into pathogenetic mechanisms.

Prior evidence suggests that common lifestyle factors, such as obesity, physical activity, alcohol consumption, and smoking, may influence LA indices. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 This is particularly relevant given that some modifiable lifestyle factors, including obesity, excessive alcohol intake, and smoking, have been linked to an increased risk of AF, 11 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 while others, such as coffee consumption and more physical activity, have been associated with a lower risk. 12 , 21 , 22

The mechanisms underlying the observed associations between lifestyle factors and AF remain largely unknown. Specifically, with the exception of evidence that enlarged atria may mediate relationships between alcohol and AF, 11 similar examinations related to other lifestyle factors have not been conducted. It is plausible that such lifestyle factors contribute to adverse atrial remodeling characterized by atrial dilation, increased ectopy, fibrosis, and reduced emptying fraction (EF), creating a substrate optimal to the initiation and maintenance of AF. 23 , 24

Understanding these associations could provide insights into potential mechanisms by which lifestyle modifications influence AF risk and how different lifestyle factors may cause distinct LA changes that may or may not alter AF risk.

The UK Biobank collected prospective lifestyle data, accurate LA measurements via cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and AF outcomes, providing a unique opportunity to assess these relationships. 25 Therefore, we aimed to systematically evaluate the association between modifiable lifestyle factors and LA indices, and whether observed LA differences mediate the risk of AF.

Methods

Data Availability

Data are available from the UK Biobank under restricted access.

Study Design and Participants

The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort study that enrolled participants between January 1, 2006, and December 31, 2010, with ongoing follow‐up. The study was approved by the National Information Governance Board and the National Health Service North West Multi‐centre Research Ethics Committee, both of which are in the United Kingdom. 25 Written consent was obtained from all participants. We received certification from the University of California San Francisco institutional review board to perform analysis of the resultant deidentified data set.

Population

The UK Biobank recruited over 500 000 participants aged 40 to 69 years. Participants completed in‐depth demographic, lifestyle, and medical history questionnaires at baseline and follow‐up visits. Race was self‐identified. Inpatient medical records from the National Health Service and approximately half of the cohort's outpatient records were integrated into the data set to monitor for clinical outcomes. Cardiac MRI scans were performed in 38 878 participants in 2014 through 2018.

Participants without LA cardiac MRI measurements, those diagnosed with AF before they underwent cardiac MRI, and those who withdrew from the study were excluded from the present analyses.

Lifestyle Factors

Information about lifestyle factors was collected at baseline enrollment. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)2 using measurements obtained as part of study participation. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and categorized into 3 levels of intensity: low, moderate, and high 26 ; the summed metabolic equivalents for all activity minutes per week were also determined. Coffee intake was categorized as the average number of cups of coffee consumed per day (whether caffeinated or not and the number of cups of caffeinated coffee consumed). Alcohol intake was categorized as “never,” “previous,” and “active” and quantified as the average standard drinks consumed per week (1 standard drink was defined as 8 g [10 mL] of alcohol per a UK standard drink). 16 , 27 Smoking status was categorized as “never,” “previous,” and “active” and quantified as pack‐years, ascertained from baseline surveys.

LA MRI Measurements

Cardiac MRIs were conducted at 4 UK sites, using uniform equipment, staff training, and acquisition protocols. These studies were conducted as part of the study protocol and not for clinical indications. A 20‐minute study was performed using a 1.5‐T scanner (MAGNETOM Aera, Syngo Platform VD13A; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Contrast agents were not administered. An automated, quality‐controlled image analysis pipeline previously developed and validated in a large subset of the UK Biobank 28 , 29 , 30 was used to determine LA maximal volume (measured at end‐systole), LA minimal volume (measured at end‐diastole), stroke volumes (SVs; LA maximal–minimal volumes), and emptying fraction (SV/LA maximal volume). LA volumes were indexed to body surface area.

Outcome Ascertainment

The primary outcome was incident AF that occurred after the cardiac MRI was performed. Incident events were ascertained from medical records of ambulatory care visits, hospital admissions, and the death register up to September 30, 2024, using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes (Table S1). Atrial flutter was included as an AF event. Participants were censored at the date of death if applicable.

Baseline Demographics and Covariate Ascertainment

Age, sex, and race and ethnicity were collected from the baseline survey. Race and ethnicity were self‐identified and categorized as White, Black, South Asian, Chinese, or multiracial. 31 Baseline comorbidities, including hypertension, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, cardiomyopathy, valvular heart disease, dyslipidemia, diabetes, history of cancer, and history of sleep apnea were ascertained from self‐reported medical histories, outpatient records, and/or inpatient records identified by ICD‐9 or ICD‐10 codes documented before the date of enrollment.

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as means±SDs and were compared using t tests, while continuous variables with skewed distributions are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher's exact or χ2 test as appropriate.

A multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to investigate the association between lifestyle factors and left atrial indices, adjusting for variables with P<0.1 in the unadjusted analyses.

Restricted cubic spline regression with 4 knots was used to assess potential nonlinear relationship between continuous LA indices and AF risk. For these analyses, the median of each individual LA index was used as the reference. 32

Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to generate hazard ratios and 95% CIs to assess relationships between predictors (either lifestyle factors or LA indices) and the time to incident AF. Multivariable models included baseline characteristics that exhibited P values <0.1 in the unadjusted Cox analyses. 33

To test for LA indices as potential mediators of the relationship between lifestyle factors and incident AF, we first retained only LA measurements as potential mediators that were significantly associated with the individual lifestyle factors (as predictors) and incident AF risk (as the outcome) after multivariable adjustment; then, we retained only lifestyle factors that were associated with incident AF in this cohort.

For each pair of lifestyle factor (predictor) and LA index (potential mediator) that was retained, we fit a Cox proportional hazards model for AF risk that included both the predictor and the potential mediator with adjustment for baseline characteristics and compared the estimated effect with the corresponding multivariable model that included only the predictor. The mediated (or indirect) effect was considered to be the difference in the effect estimates between the models. For ease of interpretability, we calculated and presented the proportions of AF risk explained by the retained lifestyle factors that was mediated by the retained LA indices on the basis of the percentage difference in multivariable adjusted β coefficients for each individual lifestyle factor with and without individual LA index included in the model. 34 , 35 The 95% CIs for the mediation proportions were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 1000 replications. 36

Statistical analyses were performed using R Studio Version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2‐tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A cohort of 37 701 participants met the inclusion and exclusion criteria for this analysis (Figure S1). The median time between baseline lifestyle factor questionnaires and the MRI was 8.9 (interquartile range, 7.4–10) years. Over the 6.8 years of follow‐up after the MRI, 890 (2.4%) participants experienced incident AF.

Discussion

A larger BMI, more alcohol intake, and more physical activity were each associated with larger LA volumes and lower LA EFs. Smoking was associated with smaller LA volumes and lower LA EFs. Coffee intake was not associated with any LA measurement. Consistent with previous reports, 2 , 37 larger LA volumes and lower LA EFs were each independent predictors of incident AF.

Because alcohol intake, physical activity intensity, and coffee intake were not associated with the risk of AF in the current cohort, LA indices were not assessed as potential mediators of any connections relevant to those lifestyle factors and AF. In contrast, larger LA volumes and lower atrial EFs each mediated about one quarter of the association between a larger BMI and incident AF when evaluated in separate mediation models.

A larger BMI has repeatedly arisen as a risk factor for AF, 38 , 39 , 40 with strong evidence that weight loss among the obese can reduce AF burden. 38 However, the mechanism by which obesity may heighten AF risk remains largely unknown. We found that a larger BMI was associated with a stepwise increase in LA volumes and reductions in LA EFs. While the former has been reported in small retrospective cohorts using cardiac echocardiography, 9 , 10 , 41 the association between BMI and LA EF is, to our knowledge, reported here for the first time. Furthermore, our mediation analyses provide the first evidence that the association between BMI and incident AF may, 38 , 39 , 40 in part, be explained by larger LA volumes and lower LA EF.

Smoking exhibited a distinct pattern of LA changes compared with other lifestyle factors, characterized by both a smaller maximal size and a lower EF. Previous studies have shown that smokers tend to have smaller LA sizes when measured by cardiac computed tomography. 14 , 15 Similarly, a MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) registry‐based analysis observed an inverse relationship between pack‐years of smoking and cardiac MRI‐derived LA volumes. 42 The current analysis provides the first evidence linking smoking to lower LA EF, underscoring the need to explore functional parameters of the LA. Notably, only the lower EF (and not smaller LA volumes) mediated the association between cumulative smoking and AF. This finding raises questions about a unique pathophysiological mechanism specific to smoking, potentially involving nicotine‐induced fibrosis, 43 which may contribute to impaired LA function. 44

The relationship between physical activity and AF is complex; generally, a J‐shaped curve is observed, with lower risk for the general population engaged in some physical activity and higher risk only in male extreme endurance athletes. 22 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 Of note, we found that more physical activity was associated with unfavorable LA indices. These findings from a particularly large population using cardiac MRI studies fit well with prior smaller studies relying on echocardiography or computed tomography. 12 , 13 , 50 While some prior studies have observed that LA enlargement may explain some of the AF risk linked to vigorous activity, 12 , 13 , 22 we were not able to assess mediation in the current study given the absence of a statistically significant association between physical activity and AF incidence in the current cohort.

In the current study, more alcohol consumption was associated with stepwise enlargement of LA volumes and a reduction in LA EF, both of which are established risk factors for AF incidence. 2 , 37 While the association between alcohol consumption and larger LA diameters using echocardiography has been previously observed, 11 the current study validates these findings using MRI‐derived volume and functional indices. These changes may contribute to AF risk, in addition to other well‐documented LA electrophysiological changes. 24 , 51 Although a trend toward an association between alcohol consumption and incident AF was observed in the current analysis, it did not reach statistical significance in the adjusted model, preventing estimation of the proportion of AF risk attributable to the observed LA differences. This lack of association is likely due to restricting the analysis to participants who underwent MRI, which reduced the sample size and may have selected healthier individuals with lower AF incidence, 52 potentially biasing the results toward the null. In fact, as shown by Lyall et al, UK Biobank MRI participants exhibited a “healthy volunteer” bias, providing evidence that testing associations in the imaging subsample alone could lead to potential underestimation of exposure–outcome relationships. 52 Therefore, our findings on alcohol consumption and AF should be interpreted with caution and not generalized, particularly given that most studies suggest a higher AF incidence with greater alcohol intake, 16 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 while only a few studies have failed to replicate this association. 61 , 62

A pertinent negative in the current study was in relationship to coffee, a commonly consumed substance that has generally been associated with a neutral or lower risk of AF. 21 We found no evidence that this possible protective effect is related to LA indices in any direction.

Some risk factors were not associated with LA size or EF, yet remained associated with incident AF. This suggests that other factors beyond LA size (or indeed measurable LA properties) mediate the relationship between certain comorbidities and AF, perhaps related to electrophysiologic changes, propensities to atrial or pulmonary vein ectopy, or fibrosis not adequately captured by LA size and function.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations. Cardiac MRI measurements were available only for a minority of the UK Biobank cohort (although still allowing for an assessment of >30 000 individuals), which could introduce selection bias. Due to the lack of repeated LA measurements, we were unable to comment on the relationships between lifestyle factors and within‐person changes in LA indices. Relying on diagnostic coding for AF ascertainment is dependent on the accuracy of the health care provider documentation, although prior validation of such codes has yielded sensitivities and specificities >90%. 63 , 64 While all hospitalizations should be captured, outpatient diagnostic coding was available in about half of the cohort. Participants were not routinely monitored; therefore, subclinical or asymptomatic AF could have been missed and not captured. However, this limitation likely resulted in reduced power to detect relationships and would not be expected to result in spurious false positive results. Future studies incorporating systematic rhythm surveillance would be valuable to confirm and extend these findings.

Data on LA fibrosis or scar were not available, which may represent an important unmeasured factor mediating the association between lifestyle factors and incident AF. The study cohort was predominantly White people, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to more diverse populations.

Conclusions

Common lifestyle factors like obesity, physical activity, alcohol consumption, and smoking are associated with LA structural and functional changes. Differences in LA indices appear to account for a considerable proportion of the relationships between an elevated BMI and AF as well as between smoking and the risk of AF.
