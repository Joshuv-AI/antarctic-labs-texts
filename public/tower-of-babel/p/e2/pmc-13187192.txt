Experimental and machine learning evaluation of crumb rubber concrete strength at elevated temperatures
Authors: Mohammad Alameri, Badr T Alsulami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study addresses the limited understanding of the strength performance of crumb rubber concrete when exposed to elevated temperatures, particularly the lack of integrated experimental evidence and reliable predictive models for structural applications. The difficulty in quantifying strength degradation and identifying the dominant influencing parameters limits the wider use of crumb rubber concrete in fire related environments. To address this issue, an integrated experimental and machine learning framework is proposed to evaluate and predict the compressive and tensile strength of crumb rubber concrete subjected to high temperature exposure. The experimental program involved replacing fine aggregate with fine crumb rubber at different replacement levels, followed by mechanical testing after exposure to elevated temperatures. The experimental results indicate that increasing the crumb rubber replacement leads to a reduction in both compressive and tensile strength, which is attributed to changes in the concrete microstructure and weakened bonding between the rubber particles and cement matrix, with additional degradation observed at higher temperatures. To complement the experimental investigation, advanced machine learning models including XGBoost, Light Gradient Boosting, and Multilayer Perceptron were developed and achieved high prediction accuracy with coefficient of determination values exceeding 0.90, whereas simpler models such as K Nearest Neighbors and Decision Trees showed lower predictive performance. Model interpretability using SHAP analysis revealed that crumb rubber content, exposure temperature, and curing age are the most influential parameters governing strength prediction. The outcomes of this study provide a reliable predictive framework for optimizing crumb rubber concrete performance under elevated temperature conditions, while future research should consider additional durability parameters, extended temperature ranges, and larger datasets to further improve model generalization and practical applicability in sustainable construction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44147-7.Keywords: Crumb rubber concrete, Compressive strength, Tensile strength, Machine learning, SHAP analysis, Sustainable concreteSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The rapid growth in vehicle usage worldwide has led to a significant rise in waste tire generation, creating a pressing environmental concern due to the non-biodegradable and flammable nature of tire rubber. It is estimated that over one billion ends of life tires are discarded annually, with many ending up in landfills or being incinerated without energy recovery1–3. In response, the integration of crumb rubber, which refers to fine particles derived from shredded waste tires, into cementitious materials has been promoted as a sustainable construction practice that simultaneously reduces landfill burden and conserves natural aggregates4–6. This approach aligns with broader efforts in sustainable construction that seek to utilize recycled building materials and construction and demolition waste as substitutes for natural aggregates, as demonstrated in recent studies on mortar and concrete incorporating dismantled building materials and artificial aggregates7–9. Among the various applications, crumb rubber concrete has gained attention due to its improved ductility, energy absorption, and thermal insulation characteristics. However, the addition of rubber results in a notable reduction in mechanical strength, particularly compressive and tensile capacity, which are essential for structural applications. This performance degradation becomes more severe under elevated temperature conditions, where the decomposition of rubber and the expansion of internal voids accelerate material deterioration. Although various studies have investigated ways to enhance the performance of crumb rubber concrete, a reliable and generalizable predictive framework that can accurately estimate its mechanical properties under varying thermal and curing conditions has yet to be established.

Rubberized concrete has attracted wide attention in the last two decades due to its capacity to absorb energy, improve impact resistance, and reduce environmental waste. Crumb rubber particles, when used as a partial substitute for natural aggregates, significantly alter the fresh and hardened behavior of concrete. However, the strength reduction associated with rubber inclusion remains a central challenge. The bond between the hydrophobic rubber particles and the hydrophilic cementitious matrix is weak, which directly affects the compressive and tensile performance of the composite. The inclusion of rubber also increases air content and decreases the density, which further reduces mechanical integrity10–13. Eldin and Senouci were among the first to demonstrate the drop in strength with rubber inclusion14, and subsequent studies have confirmed that the severity of the reduction depends on rubber content, particle size, and surface characteristics15–19. Similar strength related challenges have also been reported when using recycled fine aggregates derived from dismantled building materials, highlighting the need for optimized material design and performance prediction when alternative aggregates are employed20,21.

To address the strength limitations, researchers have investigated pretreatment methods to improve the bond between crumb rubber and the cement matrix. Treatments such as sodium hydroxide immersion, silane coatings, cement paste pre-coating, and cryogenic treatment have been shown to partially restore strength by enhancing the microstructure at the aggregate and matrix interface22–29. For instance, Zhang et al.23 and Ganjian et al.24 reported that sodium hydroxide treatment improved both compressive and flexural strength by up to 20% compared to untreated rubber mixes. However, despite the improvements, treated rubberized concrete still lags behind conventional mixes in strength, especially at higher rubber dosages30. Comparable findings have been observed in geopolymer based systems utilizing artificial and recycled aggregates, where material optimization is required to balance sustainability and mechanical performance31.

In addition to mechanical properties at ambient temperature, the thermal performance of rubberized concrete has become an area of growing interest. Crumb rubber starts decomposing above one hundred eighty degrees Celsius, and at elevated temperatures, the reduction in strength becomes more pronounced due to increased internal porosity and decomposition of rubber particles32–34. Batayneh et al.32 and Roychand et al.33 observed that the use of rubber delayed explosive spalling but led to significant reductions in residual compressive strength at two hundred degrees Celsius and beyond. Najim and Hall3 noted that mixes with higher rubber content suffered more rapid strength decay, attributed to thermal degradation and expansion of rubber voids. It is also found that while early studies focused on compressive strength under elevated temperature, more recent research has included tensile strength and durability characteristics23,35–40. The interplay of thermal exposure, curing regime, and binder composition complicates strength prediction using traditional empirical models. This complexity necessitates advanced data driven approaches capable of capturing multi-dimensional relationships41–44.

Machine learning models have been increasingly applied to predict the mechanical properties of concrete mixtures, including rubberized concrete45 and recycled concrete powder based systems46, where data driven models have demonstrated improved accuracy compared with traditional code based approaches47. Support vector regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and artificial neural networks have shown high prediction accuracy when trained on diverse datasets43,48–51. Recent studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of machine learning in predicting durability and long term performance of geopolymer concrete incorporating construction and demolition waste and artificial aggregates under harsh environmental exposure52. For example, Moussa et al.41 applied ensemble machine learning methods to estimate compressive strength of rubberized concrete exposed to elevated temperatures and reported R squared values exceeding 0.95. Similarly, Ismael et al.42 demonstrated that artificial neural networks could outperform traditional regression models for strength prediction when sufficient data diversity is available. Despite promising outcomes, existing machine learning studies suffer from several limitations. First, most focus exclusively on compressive strength and do not consider tensile strength, which is crucial for evaluating ductility and cracking potential. Second, datasets used for training often lack variation in temperature exposure or curing durations, limiting the model’s generalizability34,48,53. Third, many studies do not provide experimental validation of the machine learning predictions, raising concerns over reliability in practical applications42–44. In addition, there is a lack of integration between experimental studies and machine learning modelling within a unified framework that targets both strength properties under thermal stress54,55. However, these gaps highlight the need for a comprehensive study that combines experimental evaluation of compressive and tensile strength of crumb rubber concrete under elevated temperatures with advanced machine learning prediction models. In contrast to previous studies that focus on isolated experimental results or single strength prediction, this integrated approach enables simultaneous assessment and prediction of multiple strength properties under thermal exposure. Such integration would not only enhance the understanding of crumb rubber concrete behavior but also provide practical and efficient predictive tools capable of supporting rapid engineering decision making once material and exposure parameters are defined. This study presents a unified methodology that integrates experimental evaluation and advanced machine learning techniques to predict both compressive and tensile strength of crumb rubber concrete exposed to elevated temperatures. By designing and validating a structured experimental program alongside state of the art predictive models such as XGBoost and artificial neural networks, this research addresses the absence of generalizable, dual strength models in the literature. The study not only contributes to the optimization of rubberized concrete mixes for fire resilience but also offers a transferable framework for intelligent materials prediction in sustainable construction.

Materials and methods

Material properties

Ordinary Portland cement CEM I 52.5R was employed as the main binder, conforming to ASTM C150/C150M Type I specifications. Silica fume compliant with ASTM C1240-15 was used as a supplementary cementitious material. The fine aggregate comprised natural quartz sand, with a bulk density of 1881.6 kg/m³ and a specific gravity of 2.57 as determined by ASTM C128-15. Coarse aggregate exhibiting a bulk density of 1776.6 kg/m³ and a specific gravity of 2.81 in accordance with ASTM C127-15. Crumb rubber is utilized as a partial replacement for fine aggregate up to 30%. Mix design

The mixture proportions are presented in Table 2 and the experimental program is shown in Fig. 2. The primary variable in this study was the replacement of fine aggregate with crumb rubber at levels of 10%, 20%, and 30% by volume. Cement, silica fume, and aggregates were first dry mixed for 3 min to ensure homogeneity. Subsequently, the superplasticizer and mixing water were added, and mixing continued for an additional 15 min to achieve a uniform wet mixture. The workability of each batch was evaluated immediately after mixing using the slump test. Fresh concrete was then cast into cleaned and oiled steel molds with dimensions of 150 mm in diameter and 300 mm in height. A total of 108 cylinders were prepared for subsequent testing. All specimens were left to cure under ambient laboratory conditions for 24 h before demolding and commencing the designated curing regime.

Methods

Test setup and procedures

The workability of the fresh concrete mixtures was assessed in accordance with ASTM C14356. All measurements were conducted at an ambient temperature. Besides, the compressive strength of concrete was evaluated in accordance with ASTM C39/C39M57. Concrete cylinders with a diameter of 150 mm and a height of 300 mm were tested using a calibrated compression testing machine. Each specimen was centered on the lower platen and loaded continuously and without shock at a controlled rate of 0.25 MPa/s until failure. The maximum load at failure was recorded, and the compressive strength was calculated by dividing the maximum load by the cross-sectional area of the specimen. The splitting tensile strength of concrete was determined following ASTM C496/C496M Standard Test Method for Splitting Tensile Strength of Cylindrical Concrete Specimens58. Concrete cylinders with dimensions of 150 mm in diameter and 300 mm in length were used for testing. Each specimen was positioned horizontally between the platens of a compression testing machine. A continuous and uniform load was applied along the longitudinal axis of the cylinder at a rate of 1.2–2.4 MPa/min until failure occurred. Features were categorized as numerical only and were imputed using Iterative Imputer and standardized via Standard Scaler. A Column Transformer was used to apply these preprocessing steps, yielding a fully numeric and clean dataset suitable for model training.

Machine learning models

Machine learning (ML), a branch of artificial intelligence, is utilized to enable computers to autonomously learn from data by recognizing patterns and making predictions without explicit programming101. Statistical techniques are leveraged by these algorithms to uncover meaningful relationships within large datasets, with predictive accuracy progressively enhanced over time. In this study, nine distinct models are employed, each contributing uniquely to the analysis and prediction process. Through rigorous evaluation, the optimal ML model has been identified, highlighting its effectiveness in the research context.

K-nearest neighbor (KNN)

The KNN algorithm, developed by Fix and Hodges then enhanced by Cover and Hart102, classifies data by identifying nearby points and using a majority vote, possibly weighted by distance. This approach ensures stable and reliable predictions.

Adaptive boosting (ADB)

ADB is a machine learning technique that combines multiple weak learners to create a strong predictive model103. It iteratively adjusts the weights of training samples, focusing more on those that were previously misclassified. This process improves the model’s accuracy by emphasizing difficult cases, resulting in a robust ensemble model.

Multilayer perceptron (MLP)

is a kind of neural network that mimics how the brain processes information104. It has layers of interconnected nodes that help it learn patterns from data. By adjusting these connections, MLPs can make accurate predictions, such as estimating the strength of rubberized concrete. They are flexible and can handle various tasks, from predicting numbers to classifying data.

Decision tree (DT)

DT is a straightforward machine learning model that works like a flowchart to make decisions105. It breaks down data into smaller parts at each step, leading to a final decision at the end of each branch. This method is easy to understand and helps in predicting things like the strength of rubberized concrete by clearly showing how each decision is made.

Random forest (RF)

RF is an ensemble learning method that builds multiple decision trees and merges their outputs to improve prediction accuracy106. Each tree is trained on a random subset of the data, and the final prediction is typically the average or majority vote of all the trees. This approach reduces overfitting and enhances the model’s robustness, making it effective for predicting the strength of concrete.

Gradient boosting regression (GBR)

GBR is a powerful machine learning technique that builds models in a sequential manner, where each new model corrects errors made by the previous ones107. This approach enhances prediction accuracy, making it effective for complex tasks like predicting concrete strength which is affected by many variables.

Light gradient boosting (LGB)

LGB is an efficient version of gradient boosting designed for speed and scalability108. It handles large datasets quickly, making it ideal for real-time predictions in applications such as construction material analysis.

Xtreme gradient boosting (XGB)

XGB is an advanced implementation of gradient boosting that optimizes performance and speed109. It is widely used for its accuracy and efficiency in handling diverse datasets, including those related to rubberized concrete.

Bagging regressor (BAG)

BAG is a technique that improves model stability by training multiple versions of a model on different subsets of data and averaging their predictions110. This method reduces variance and enhances reliability.

Results

Workability

Figure 6 illustrates the impact of rubber content on slump, showing a clear decreasing trend as rubber content increases. This reduction, reaching up to 48% in the 30Ru mix, can be attributed to the lower density and higher elasticity of rubber particles, which disrupt the concrete matrix and decrease its workability99. Figure 6 also presents the unit weights of the ambient cured cylinders of the mixes. A decreasing trend is noticed, confirming the effect of the lower density of rubber, with a 1.5% reduction in unit weight in the 30Ru mix. This reduction is due to the substitution of denser aggregate materials with lighter rubber particles, resulting in a lighter overall mix. The incorporation of rubber affects not only the mechanical properties but also the density and structural characteristics of the concrete, highlighting the importance of balancing these factors for optimal performance3.

Hardened unit weight and slump results.

Compressive strength

Figure 7 depicts the compressive strength results at different ages under ambient curing conditions. The results indicate a consistent increase in compressive strength from 3 days to 28 days, with improvements of approximately 104%, 52%, 129%, and 82% for NC, 10Ru, 20Ru, and 30Ru, respectively. This increase reflects the ongoing hydration process and the development of the concrete matrix over time. However, at 28 days, a decrease in compressive strength is observed with higher rubber content, showing reductions of 8%, 15%, and 37% in 10Ru, 20Ru, and 30Ru, respectively. This decline can be attributed to the lower bonding efficiency and structural integrity caused by the inclusion of rubber particles, which disrupt the continuity of the cementitious matrix, leading to weaker overall strength80.

Compressive strength at different curing ages for all mixes.

Figure 8 illustrates the compressive strength results for all mixes at different temperatures at 28 days. A general decreasing trend is observed for mixes with rubber content as the curing temperature increases. At 100 °C, the 30Ru mix shows a significant decrease of up to 42% compared to the NC mix. This reduction is likely due to the thermal degradation of rubber particles, which weakens the matrix. Conversely, the NC mix exhibits improvements in compressive strength of 25% and 24% at 100 °C and 150 °C, respectively, due to enhanced hydration and matrix densification at moderate temperatures. However, at 200 °C, a slight decrease of 4% is noted, due to microstructural changes at higher temperatures26,111. The 10Ru mix maintains comparable strength to ambient conditions, achieving around 40 MPa at 200 °C, indicating some resilience to temperature effects. For the 30Ru mix, the compressive strength reduction is limited to about 10% under 200 °C, suggesting that while rubber content affects strength, the impact is less severe at higher temperatures compared to ambient conditions.

28-day compressive strength at different curing temperatures.

Tensile strength

Figure 9 presents the tensile strength results for all mixes under varying curing temperatures at 28 days. The NC mix experiences a 20% decline in tensile strength at 200 °C, while maintaining comparable strength at 100 °C and 150 °C. For the 10Ru mix, tensile strength decreases by 27% compared to the NC mix at ambient temperature and by 24% at 200 °C. This indicates that concrete exhibits notable variations in tensile strength with the addition of rubber particles. However, the reduction in tensile strength across different curing temperatures is relatively minor, with the highest strength at 2.2 MPa for 10Ru under ambient curing and the lowest strength of 1.7 MPa for 30Ru under 200 °C. The presence of rubber particles may disrupt the matrix, affecting tensile properties, yet the overall impact of temperature on rubberized concrete remains limited, suggesting some resilience to thermal effects.

Machine learning results

The predictive performance of the applied machine learning models KNN, ADB, MLP, DT, RF, GBR, LGB, XGB, and BAG for estimating the compressive strength of concrete is illustrated in the subplots Fig. 11. Each model output is evaluated using the statistical indicators of the coefficient of determination R², root mean square error RMSE, mean absolute error MAE, and mean absolute percentage error MAPE. Predicted and actual compressive strength plots for ML models.

The results demonstrate that the multilayer perceptron MLP achieved the highest accuracy with an R² value of 0.92, followed closely by extreme gradient boosting XGB with an R² value of 0.91 and light gradient boosting LGB with an R² value of 0.90. Ensemble-based models such as gradient boosting regression GBR with an R² value of 0.90 and bagging BAG with an R² value of 0.88 also provided consistent performance. By contrast, the lowest accuracy was observed in adaptive boosting ADB, which recorded an R² value of 0.70, indicating limited capability in capturing the variability of the experimental data.

In terms of error magnitudes, MLP, XGB, and GBR exhibited the lowest RMSE values not exceeding 5.9 MPa and the lowest MAE values not exceeding 4.3 MPa, which reflects high predictive precision and reduced dispersion between predicted and actual results. On the other hand, ADB and decision tree DT recorded the largest RMSE values greater than 8.0 MPa together with relatively higher MAE, suggesting weaker predictive robustness.

The analysis of relative prediction error revealed that XGB achieved the lowest MAPE of 12.3%, followed by MLP with 14.7% and LGB with 15.1%, confirming the reliability of these models across different compressive strength ranges. In contrast, ADB reached a MAPE of 37.2% and KNN recorded a MAPE of 23.6%, indicating substantial deviations in percentage terms between predicted and experimental results.

The scatter distribution around the line of equality further supports these findings. The models with superior performance, namely MLP, XGB, LGB, and GBR, demonstrated a dense clustering of data points along the 45-degree line, indicating strong agreement between predicted and experimental values. Conversely, models such as ADB, DT, and KNN displayed wider scatter dispersion with visible systematic deviations, reflecting their limited generalization capacity.

Overall, the comparative evaluation confirms that ensemble-based approaches including XGB, LGB, GBR, and BAG together with the neural network MLP achieved the most reliable predictions of compressive strength, combining high R² with reduced RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. Simpler algorithms, including KNN, DT, and particularly ADB, exhibited weaker performance and are therefore less effective for robust prediction tasks in this domain.

The results indicate that the highest accuracy was achieved by light gradient boosting LGB and extreme gradient boosting XGB, both with R² values of 0.89. These models also exhibited superior error control, as LGB recorded an RMSE of 0.46 MPa, an MAE of 0.32 MPa, and a MAPE of 12.0%, while XGB achieved an RMSE of 0.45 MPa, an MAE of 0.31 MPa, and the lowest MAPE among all models at 11.6%. The bagging BAG and random forest RF models followed closely, each with an R² value of 0.87, and demonstrated low error magnitudes with RMSE values of 0.49 MPa and 0.50 MPa, MAE values of 0.35 MPa, and MAPE values around 13%.

The multilayer perceptron MLP and gradient boosting regression GBR presented moderate performance, with R² values of 0.81 and 0.85 respectively. MLP recorded an RMSE of 0.60 MPa, an MAE of 0.40 MPa, and a MAPE of 14.6%, whereas GBR achieved an RMSE of 0.54 MPa, an MAE of 0.39 MPa, and a MAPE of 16.3%. The decision tree DT model also performed reasonably well with an R² value of 0.84, but its prediction errors were relatively higher than those of the ensemble-based models, as indicated by its RMSE of 0.56 MPa, MAE of 0.42 MPa, and MAPE of 15.6%.

By contrast, the weakest performance was observed in adaptive boosting ADB, which produced the lowest R² value of 0.69, the highest RMSE of 0.77 MPa, and the largest MAPE of 27.4%. The KNN model also showed relatively limited predictive capability with an R² value of 0.78, an RMSE of 0.65 MPa, an MAE of 0.46 MPa, and a MAPE of 18.6%, indicating less accurate predictions compared to the ensemble and boosting methods.

The scatter distribution around the line of equality reinforces these findings. LGB, XGB, BAG, and RF exhibited the densest clustering of points along the 45-degree line, signifying strong alignment between predicted and experimental tensile strength values. On the other hand, ADB and KNN displayed wider scatter dispersion with more pronounced deviations, reflecting their weaker generalization capacity.

In summary, the comparative evaluation confirms that ensemble-based approaches such as LGB, XGB, BAG, and RF provide the most reliable predictions of tensile strength, combining high R² with reduced RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. Neural networks such as MLP and gradient boosting regression GBR demonstrated moderate accuracy, while simpler models including KNN, DT, and particularly ADB exhibited weaker predictive capability and are therefore less effective for robust tensile strength prediction.

The multilayer perception and gradient boosting regression displayed moderate performance, while adaptive boosting and KNN were clearly inferior, showing low correlation and significant errors. Similar observations have been reported in earlier studies, which showed that ensemble and tree based models provide more reliable tensile strength predictions compared to distance based or single tree approaches114.

Overall, the Taylor diagrams confirm the superiority of ensemble-based methods and neural networks in predicting both compressive and tensile strength of rubberized concrete, as they combine high correlation with reduced error and closer agreement with the observed variability. Simpler models such as KNN, decision tree, and adaptive boosting consistently underperformed, highlighting their limited suitability for reliable prediction in this context.

Limitations

The findings of this study provide practical guidance for the use of crumb rubber concrete in sustainable construction, particularly in applications where enhanced energy absorption and fire related performance are required. The integration of experimentally validated machine learning models enables rapid prediction of compressive and tensile strength based on mix composition, curing age, and temperature exposure, which can support preliminary design decisions and material optimization without extensive laboratory testing.

Despite these contributions, several limitations remain. The dataset did not include rubber pretreatment methods as input variables, and therefore their influence on strength performance was not captured in the predictive models. In addition, the use of rubber as fiber reinforcement was not considered due to the limited availability of consistent experimental data. Flexural strength prediction was also excluded because of substantial variability in testing procedures reported in the literature, which limits reliable model development under non standardized conditions. Furthermore, existing standards are primarily developed for conventional concrete and do not explicitly address the altered mechanical behavior and temperature sensitivity of crumb rubber concrete.

Future research should focus on expanding datasets to include rubber pretreatment techniques, fiber based rubber reinforcement, and flexural performance using standardized testing protocols. The development of material specific testing guidelines and performance based design criteria for crumb rubber concrete is also recommended. In addition, integrating durability related properties and long term exposure conditions into data driven models would further enhance the reliability and applicability of predictive tools for sustainable construction.

Conclusion

This study presents an integrated experimental and machine learning investigation into the mechanical behavior of crumb rubber concrete under elevated temperature exposure, with emphasis on both compressive and tensile strength. The findings provide scientific insight into material behavior, predictive modeling capability, and practical implications for sustainable construction as follows:

Replacement of fine aggregate with fine crumb rubber significantly alters the concrete microstructure, leading to reduced density and weakened bonding between the rubber particles and cement matrix. This microstructural modification is the primary mechanism responsible for the observed reductions in compressive and tensile strength.
