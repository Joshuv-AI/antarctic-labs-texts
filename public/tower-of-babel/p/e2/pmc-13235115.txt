Minimally invasive treatment of emphysematous pyelonephritis in diabetic patients: a comparative study
Authors: Shilin Li, Jingjing Zheng, Yue Song, Lianhui Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEmphysematous pyelonephritis (EPN) is a rare but high-mortality infectious disease most commonly seen in diabetic patients, and the timing of percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD) is crucial for its prognosis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on data of 18 patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus complicated EPN who were treated in the Urology of the General Hospital of Northern Theater Command between November 2019 and March 2024. The patient cohort was stratified into two groups according to the timing of PCD implementation: the Early PCD group (E-PCD group, n = 11) (≤ 24 h) and the Delayed Salvage PCD group (DS-PCD group, n = 7) (converted to PCD after failure of conservative pharmacotherapy). By comparing of the comprehensive indicators, the clinical value of early PCD was clarified.ResultThe general status recovery time, average hospitalization time, average recovery time, and the recovery time of related infection indicators at multiple sampling time points after treatment in the E-PCD group were significantly shorter than those in the DS-PCD group. The volume change rate of pus cavity of E-PCD was significantly higher than that of DS-PCD group at different unit time periods respectively.ConclusionsCompared with delayed salvage PCD, early ultrasound-guided PCD within 24 h in diabetic patients with EPN can effectively drain pus, quickly control infection symptoms, and accelerate patient recovery.

Introduction

Emphysematous pyelonephritis (EPN) is an infectious disease caused by pathogenic aerogens. The main feature of EPN is the coexistence of pus and gas. EPN is mostly seen in female diabetic patients or immunocompromised patients. Its typical clinical manifestations include high fever and low back pain. Laboratory tests usually show hyperglycemia, elevated white blood cell (WBC), and elevated serum creatinine levels. Due to the non-specific clinical manifestations, rapid onset, and rapid progression of EPN, septic shock can occur in the early stages with a high mortality rate. A meta-analysis of 628 patients found that the average mortality rate of EPN was 18% [1].

Clinically, conservative treatment and surgical treatment are mainly used to treat EPN patients according to the severity of local and systemic infections, which can achieve certain results. However, nephrectomy has caused great trauma. Conservative treatment has the disadvantages of relatively long treatment time, significant side effects of antibiotics, difficulty in completely clearing the pus cavity, and high recurrence rate of infection. Stein et al. reported a 75% mortality rate in EPN patients treated conservatively with drugs alone [2]. With the development of minimally invasive techniques, percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD) has gradually become a key treatment modality for EPN; however, the impact of the timing of PCD implementation on the prognosis of diabetic patients with EPN remains unclear.

There is a lack of targeted research on the comparison between early PCD and delayed salvage PCD. We conducted a single-centre retrospective comparison of Early PCD group (E-PCD group) and Delayed Salvage PCD group (DS-PCD group) in 18 consecutive diabetic EPN patients to clarify the optimal PCD implementation timing, quantify renal salvage effect, and identify prognostic determinants, so as to provide a more targeted clinical reference for the minimally invasive treatment of this disease.

Methods

Study design

Single-centre retrospective study (Nov 2019–Mar 2024) of all EPN cases treated with early PCD (within 24 h after admission) or initial conservative treatment followed by delayed salvage PCD. All research involving human participants, samples, and data in this study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles stated in the WMA Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria were defined as the presence of abnormal densities and gas within the renal collecting system, renal parenchyma and perirenal tissues on computed tomography (CT) imaging, the manifestation of clinical signs and symptoms consistent with upper urinary tract infection, elevated infectious markers identified via laboratory testing, and a confirmed diagnosis of diabetes mellitus with subsequent treatment via PCD.

Exclusion criteria comprised cases of EPN secondary to trauma or surgical procedures, the presence of a fistulous communication between the lesion site and the gastrointestinal system, management via alternative surgical interventions, loss to follow-up, patients with EPN alone without a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, and those who achieved a clinical response with conservative therapy alone without the need for PCD as well as patients who underwent nephrectomy following failed conservative treatment. This latter exclusion was implemented to ensure both study cohorts consisted solely of patients who received PCD treatment.

In all cases, EPN was the primary diagnosis, with the stones merely serving as an accompanying obstructive factor. The presence or absence of ureteral stones did not affect the diagnosis of EPN.

The demographic parameters of all patients were recorded, including gender, age, clinical symptoms and signs, complications, duration of diabetes mellitus, and state of consciousness. The recorded biochemical indicators included WBC count, neutrophils percentage (NEUT%), platelet (PLT) count, glycated hemoglobin, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), interleukin-6 (IL-6), serum creatinine, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, urine analysis, blood β-hydroxybutyric acid, urine glucose, and urine ketone bodies. Radiological parameters were recorded, including ultrasound scanning, imaging examination, radionuclide renal imaging, and CT scan.

E-PCD group

Pre-op CT quantified abscess/gas volume, septation and adjacent involvement; puncture site was CT-selected. Liquid–gas ratio, drainage rate and infection markers were serially recorded. All PCDs were performed within 24 h under uniform ultrasound guidance; EPN severity followed Huang–Tseng classification [3].

According to the position, size of the pus cavity and its relationship with the renal collecting system/surrounding blood vessels, the lateral edge of the renal parenchyma closest to the pus cavity and without important blood vessels and nerves was selected as the puncture site, and the puncture angle and depth were marked. A puncture kit and serial fascial dilators enabled insertion of an 18 F multi-side-hole drain via a 22 F peel-away sheath, ensuring viscous pus evacuation. Continuous drainage via anti-reflux bags and syringe aspiration when output declined was coupled with immediate carbapenem therapy (for presumed Gram-negative pathogens), glycaemic control, and rehydration. 3 cases in the E-PCD group underwent simultaneous ureteral DJ stent implantation due to ureteral obstruction caused by stones/edema, the stents were removed 4–6 weeks after surgery, and no stent-related complications occurred.

After puncture, antibiotics were adjusted based on the results of pus bacterial culture and drug sensitivity, and patients were given continuous anti-infective treatment. CT scan results of patients were compared before and after PCD treatment, showing that the lesions were significantly reduced in size in all 11 patients, and the relevant infection indicators could be gradually restored to the normal control range. At that time, antibiotics were discontinued, and the patient’s back pain was significantly relieved. To ensure optimal treatment outcomes and avoid recurrence, a CT scan was performed when the patient had recovered from all indicators of infection. When there was no residual gas or pus in the lesion, or only a very small amount of pus could not be completely drained (generally considered to be less than 5 mL), the drainage tube could be removed.

DS-PCD group

All 7 patients in the DS-PCD group were initially treated with conservative drugs due to mild initial symptoms or refusal of immediate invasive treatment, and were later switched to PCD treatment due to no improvement after two weeks of conservative treatment.

The initial conservative treatment plan was formulated according to the clinical severity: all patients received immediate empiric carbapenems (imipenem/cilastatin sodium, 1.0 g q8h), and the antibiotics were tailored to the results of urine/blood culture and drug sensitivity after the report: patients with positive Escherichia coli were changed to cefoperazone/sulbactam, patients with positive Klebsiella pneumoniae were changed to meropenem, and patients with negative culture maintained the initial regimen until infection control.

Deterioration emergency plan during conservative treatment: If the patient had any of the following conditions during conservative treatment, it was judged as disease deterioration: persistent high fever > 39℃ for more than 48 h, progressive increase of infection indicators (WBC/CRP/PCT), increase of pus cavity volume > 20%, and occurrence of septic shock/multiorgan dysfunction. Once deterioration was diagnosed, conservative treatment was immediately terminated and converted to PCD under ultrasound guidance, without waiting for the 2-week observation period. This emergency plan was strictly implemented in this study, and there were no adverse outcomes caused by delayed treatment.

The 7 patients in the DS-PCD group were converted to PCD at 10–14 days after admission (median 12 days), all of whom underwent ultrasound-guided PCD with the same operational specifications as the E-PCD group, and 2 cases received simultaneous DJ stent implantation due to ureteral obstruction during PCD.

Unified analgesic and glycemic control protocol for two groups

The mean duration of diabetes among the 18 patients was 13.8 ± 7.4 years, with 14.1 ± 6.5 years in the E-PCD group and 13.3 ± 9.2 years in the DS-PCD group, showing no statistically significant difference between the two groups. All diabetic patients in the E-PCD group and DS-PCD group, including those with ketoacidosis, received concurrent standardized glycaemic control and rehydration therapy to maintain blood glucose at the target level and correct water and electrolyte disorders. For lumbago management: lumbar pain was managed with 50 mg flurbiprofen axetil as required for patients with normal renal function (serum creatinine ≤ 133µmol/L), and for patients with renal insufficiency (serum creatinine > 133µmol/L), flurbiprofen axetil was discontinued and replaced with oral paracetamol sustained-release tablets 650 mg q12h for analgesia, which has no renal toxicity and is suitable for patients with renal insufficiency. The same analgesic adjustment principle was applied to both groups to ensure the comparability of treatment protocols.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables of demographic characteristics are represented by mean ± standard deviation (SD). Classified data is represented by numbers and percentages. Mann-Whitney U test or t-test is used for continuous variables. GraphPad Prism 9 is used to plot the trend of changes in infection indicators WBC, NEUT%, CRP, and PCT over time for two groups. All statistical analyses are conducted using IBM SPSS version 26.0. P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the 18 patients enrolled in the study, one was male and the remaining were female. The rate of left-sided renal involvement (n = 13) was slightly higher than that of the right side (n = 5). The mean age of the patients was 58 years (range, 33–70 years), with a mean age of 60 (± 6.5) years in the DS-PCD group and 57 (± 10) years in the E-PCD group (Table 1). All patients presented with varying degrees of diabetes mellitus. Four patients had poor glycemic control resulting in diabetic ketoacidosis, and all exhibited symptoms of septic shock to varying degrees. Five patients (23.8%) presented with urinary tract calculi, including three cases (14.3%) of ureteral calculi and two cases (9.5%) of left renal calculi.

Among the 7 patients in the DS-PCD group, 5 presented with mild symptoms at admission, while the other 2 initially refused invasive intervention despite having moderate-to-severe presentations. During the initial conservative treatment phase, none met the predefined deterioration criteria (persistent fever > 39 °C for 48 h, progressive increase in infection markers, or > 20% increase in pus cavity volume). Therefore, all 7 patients completed the 2‑week observation period and underwent PCD at a median of 12 days (range 10–14 days) after admission.

Outcome and evaluation

In comparison, the hospitalization time and average treatment time in the PCD group were shorter than those in the conservative group (Table 2) (Fig. 1A-C). There were significant differences between the two groups in the recovery time of WBC, NEUT%, CRP, PCT, and body temperature (P < 0.05) (Table 3). Compared with the E-PCD group, the DS-PCD group had a longer duration of pain after treatment (based on the VAS) (Fig. 1F).

Among the 7 patients who underwent delayed PCD after conservative medical treatment, all cases showed persistent or worsening gas and pus accumulation on CT. One of them had been misdiagnosed and developed septic shock after receiving antibiotic therapy alone for one month. After conversion to ultrasound-guided PCD, the gas and pus were completely cleared within four weeks, the abscess cavities resolved, and renal function improved concomitantly compared with pre-PCD values. All 18 patients had stable vital signs after PCD drainage. Among them, 3 patients underwent ureteral stent placement due to ureteral obstruction. Two patients had residual small amounts of pus that were absorbed on the 5th and 7th day after discharge, respectively. Multiple renal CT scans after PCD showed that the accumulated gas in the kidney gradually disappeared, and the renal morphology eventually returned to normal. Renal function gradually recovered, and the drainage sites in the surgical area healed well.

Changes in renal function

All 18 patients completed data collection throughout the treatment and follow-up. 14 patients underwent renal dynamic imaging examination on 3rd and 7th after treatment (Fig. 3A-C). The results showed that there was no urinary tract obstruction or renal dysfunction on the healthy side of the kidney. Infected kidneys showed varying degrees of impaired renal function. The glomerular filtration rate was higher in the healthy side of the kidney than in the infected side of the kidney (Fig. 3).

Evaluation of imaging changes

EPN must be diagnosed early, quickly, and correctly to avoid delayed treatment due to misdiagnosis. For diabetes patients initially diagnosed with acute pyelonephritis, when the symptoms such as low back pain and abdominal pain worsen, the disease progressed rapidly, or even shocked. In these cases, EPN should be considered, and the urinary system CT examination should be carried out as early as possible. CT examination can not only confirm the diagnosis, but also evaluate the changes of lesions before and after treatment(Fig. 4A-F).

According to CT image measurement, the gas-to-liquid ratio in the abscess cavity of the 18 patients before treatment was approximately 3.2:2 (Fig. 4D, E). Among them, the gas-to-liquid ratios in the abscess cavities of the DS-PCD group and the E-PCD group were approximately 2.6:2 and 3.6:2, respectively. After 7 days of treatment, the gas-to-liquid ratios in the abscess cavities of the DS-PCD group and the E-PCD group decreased to approximately 2.3:1.5 and 1.5:0.1, respectively (P < 0.05). The mean percentage reduction in abscess cavity within one week was 16% in the DS-PCD group (prior to PCD) and 73% in the E-PCD group, and the difference in the percentage change of abscess cavity per unit time within two weeks between the two groups was statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 3). The rate of abscess cavity reduction in the DS-PCD group before undergoing PCD was significantly slower than that in the E-PCD group, indicating that early PCD intervention is significantly more effective in clearing pus and gas than performing PCD after medical treatment.

Follow-up

In the E-PCD group (n = 11) and the DS-PCD group (n = 7), six-month postoperative CT follow-up revealed no evidence of disease relapse or renal atrophy. At follow-up, all patients in this cohort maintained stable glycemic control, normalized infection markers, and demonstrated improved imaging findings.

Discussion

A meta-analysis of 628 patients showed that the average mortality rate of EPN patients was 18%1. Patients with two or more comorbidities exhibited significantly higher mortality rates [4]. Diabetes serves as a crucial prognostic indicator for EPN patients, with approximately 60–70% of patients attributed to uncontrolled diabetes [5]. In our study, 21 patients were diagnosed with diabetes. Elevated blood glucose levels and compromised immune function increase the susceptibility to urinary tract infections [6]. Nonetheless, EPN can also be manifested in non-diabetic patients with urinary tract obstruction and immunodeficiency [7]. Ureteral stones are one of the potential risk factors for EPN, especially among diabetic patients.Although ureteric calculi were present in some cases and may have aggravated EPN, they did not influence diagnostic criteria; their specific impact on outcomes warrants dedicated investigation.

Over time, the management of EPN shows notable improvement, resulting in a decreased mortality rate of 3–30% [8]. Untreated EPN triggers SIRS, rapid abscess expansion, sepsis, shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and multi-organ failure; early diagnosis and prompt drainage are mandatory to avert fatal complications.

Misdiagnosis of EPN delays optimal therapy; options span conservative care, PCD, or open debridement. Through comparison, early and timely ultrasound-guided PCD, with priority given to bedside placement to avoid pressure spikes associated with transportation, is crucial. Multi-slot drains resist viscous pus occlusion, while simultaneous glycaemic control, ketosis correction and acid–base resuscitation stabilise physiology and buy time for definitive debridement if required.

Many scholars have reported favorable therapeutic outcomes in the treatment of EPN by using ultrasound-guided or CT-guided PCD [9]. However, some authors believe that the ultrasound scanning evaluation of EPN patients is hindered by many factors, such as intestinal gas, which may appear as “artifacts” on ultrasound images [10]. Alan R. Pontin and Richard D. Barnes found that performing this surgery under CT guidance is the best procedure [11, 12]. However, in our study, we found that ultrasound-guided treatment of EPN was effective and accurate. Ultrasound scanning is increasingly used as an auxiliary diagnostic method for renal and perirenal abscesses, especially in emergencies. Irfaan et al. emphasized that for patients with poor infection control after conservative treatment and minimally invasive surgical treatment, emergency nephrectomy should be performed [13]. In Lu et al.‘s study, 32 patients received PCD and antibiotic treatment, 8 patients had EPN recurrence within 3 months, and 3 patients died after surgery [14]. However, in our previous treatment, there were no recurrent patients. The increase in cure rate is achieved through accurate disease prediction, standardized and precise treatment methods, and avoiding surgical management of nephrectomy as much as possible.

A systematic review involving 210 patients revealed that the mortality rate was lower in the drug treatment + PCD group (13.5%) compared to the drug treatment + emergency nephrectomy group (25%) [15]. The advancement of management strategies for critically ill patients and the evolution of bedside treatment techniques have led to the increasing adoption of kidney-sparing plans for treating EPN [16]. Conservative drug therapy primarily focuses on controlling the spread of infected lesions while avoiding sepsis. When dealing with shock patients or early-stage shock patients, early fluid resuscitation, analgesia, anti-infection, correction of water and electrolyte disorders, and adjustment of blood glucose control should be performed. The purpose of these interventions is to stabilize the patient’s condition to certain extent and provide the necessary conditions for further treatment. Based on clinical treatment experience, Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae are the main bacteria associated with EPN [1]. Therefore, timely use of antibiotics that are effective against these pathogens is crucial.

Conservative therapy may be considered only in strictly selected patients with: (1) small abscess cavities (< 3 cm), (2) minimal gas formation (Huang-Tseng class I or early II), (3) no sepsis or hemodynamic instability, and (4) reliable access to close follow-up. However, even in these patients, active treatment should be initiated immediately upon any sign of deterioration (persistent fever > 39 °C for 48 h, rising infection markers, or abscess enlargement > 20%) [17, 18]. In all other cases, early PCD should be prioritized to avoid the morbidity associated with delayed intervention.

Early PCD is significantly superior to delayed PCD performed after medical treatment in terms of infection control, renal function preservation, and mortality reduction. The fundamental difference lies in the timing of intervention, which determines whether the treatment actively blocks the disease course or passively responds to complications. Early PCD achieves direct removal of the infectious source through percutaneous decompression, fundamentally cutting off the pathway for toxins to enter the bloodstream, thereby enabling antibiotics to more effectively clear residual pathogens. In contrast, delaying PCD for two weeks often means that patients have endured multiple episodes of infection exacerbation during this period, with cumulative aggravation of systemic inflammatory responses and an increased risk of multi-organ dysfunction. Furthermore, EPN is characterized by rapid destruction of the renal parenchyma; the pressure within the pus cavity can lead to ischemic necrosis of nephrons, and gas diffusion along the perirenal space can further disrupt the renal supporting structures. If PCD is performed in the early stage of the disease, it can rapidly reduce local pressure, interrupt the ischemia-reperfusion injury cascade, and maximize the preservation of residual renal function. However, when drainage is delayed for two weeks, the pus cavity often progresses from unilocular to multiloculated, the pus becomes more viscous, and some areas may develop fibrous encapsulation. This not only increases the difficulty of secondary puncture but also often results in irreversible damage to the renal parenchyma by this time. Moreover, patients undergoing early PCD experience rapid resolution of systemic inflammation due to timely source control, leading to shorter subsequent treatment courses and reduced hospitalization time. Therefore, for patients with EPN, early PCD is not an alternative to medical therapy but rather a critical treatment strategy for controlling the infectious source and preserving renal function.

Antibiotics alone transiently suppress infection but rarely sterilise the abscess; breakthrough fever and rigors remain common. Chronic administration risks microbiome disruption without assuring cure, underscoring the need for pathophysiology-driven interventions. Advanced EPN often obliterates renal anatomy, rendering emergency nephrectomy technically formidable; immediate open debridement and drainage would otherwise decompress the cavity and temper systemic sepsis [19]. In the present cohort this approach was precluded by severe physiologic derangement, precluding safe anaesthesia and laparotomy.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, single-center, observational study with a small sample size, the treatment groups were not randomly assigned, introducing inherent selection bias. Although patients in the early PCD group may have had more severe initial conditions (larger pus and gas cavities, higher CRP levels), residual confounding factors may still have influenced the results. Second, the delineation of the delayed PCD group reflects the complexity of real-world clinical decision-making, where patients initially received conservative treatment due to milder symptoms or contraindications to intervention. Furthermore, this study only included patients with Huang-Tseng type II and IIIA EPN, and did not cover all EPN classifications; the small sample size (n = 18) also limited the feasibility of subgroup analysis and multivariate regression. Nevertheless, as the first study focusing on the comparison between early and delayed PCD in diabetic patients with EPN, our findings still provide valuable reference for clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

Based on a retrospective analysis of diabetic patients with EPN, this study preliminarily suggests that early ultrasound-guided PCD is significantly superior to delayed PCD performed after two weeks of ineffective medical therapy in terms of infection control rate, renal function preservation, and clinical recovery efficiency. Early intervention achieves direct removal of the infectious source through timely decompression and drainage, effectively interrupting disease progression and maximizing the preservation of renal function. Therefore, we propose that for diabetic patients with EPN, once the diagnosis is confirmed and no absolute contraindications exist, early PCD should be prioritized to achieve optimal prognosis.
