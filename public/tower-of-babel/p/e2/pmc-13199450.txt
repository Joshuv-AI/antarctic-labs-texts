Lifecycle and circular economy assessment of bio based retrofitting strategies for heritage buildings using case studies from Iran, Oman and Saudi Arabia
Authors: By Marjan Ilbeigi, Mohamed Alnejem, Mozhgan Karimi, Samaneh Safaripoor, Farbod Khalili, Elyas Jahanshahi, Yaqoob Al Hindasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sustainable retrofitting of heritage buildings presents a unique challenge in aligning environmental performance with cultural preservation. This study proposes an integrated framework combining Lifecycle Assessment (LCA), Circular Economy (CE) evaluation, Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA), and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) modeling to assess Bio-Based retrofitting strategies for historical structures. Three case studies Ganjali Khan Complex (Iran), Bait Al Zubair (Oman), and Al-Balad District (Saudi Arabia) were selected to represent diverse climatic and cultural contexts. Retrofitting scenarios including Traditional, Bio-Based, and Circular-Optimized approaches were compared based on four main criteria: Global Warming Potential (GWP), Circularity Score, Retrofit Cost, and Heritage Compatibility. Bio-Based materials were selected based on low embodied carbon, biodegradability, and local availability. The LCA was performed using MATLAB and international databases (Ecoinvent, OneClick LCA, ICE) to assess embodied emissions of retrofit materials (Modules A1–A3), operational energy use of the building (Module B6), and end-of-life treatment of retrofit materials (Modules C1–C4). The Material Circularity Indicator (MCI) model was used to evaluate circularity performance. Scenario ranking was performed using the TOPSIS method, while ANN modeling predicted the optimal retrofit strategy and conducted sensitivity analysis. Results indicate that the Circular-Optimized retrofit strategy achieves the highest overall sustainability performance across case studies, delivering significant lifecycle CO₂ reductions and long-term cost savings. Over a 50-year assessment period, cumulative CO₂ savings reach up to 720 tons per building, while financial savings range between $80,000 and $95,000. The findings demonstrate that integrating circular principles with bio-based materials can enhance environmental performance without compromising heritage compatibility. The proposed methodology offers a replicable model for sustainable conservation, bridging the gap between architectural heritage and environmental resilience.

Introduction

Climate change is recognized as worldwide problem Which has imposed significant societal costs and impressed the lives1. To address climate change and greenhouse gas emissions, Research has shown that 35% of final energy consumption and approximately 40% of carbon dioxide emissions are caused from buildings and construction sector2. As countries obligate to net-zero goals, devotion has progressively turned to the renovation and retrofitting of current building standard as a critical approach to decrease embodied and operational carbon3. Therefore, heritage buildings which hold high cultural, historical, and aesthetic value, signify an exceptional challenge. These buildings must not only fulfill with energy and climate targets but also reserve legitimacy, corporeality, and ancient significance4. At the connection of these requirements lies the concept of sustainable retrofitting, which pursues to enhance building performance while maintaining cultural heritage. Although, retrofitting heritage buildings attitudes exclusive technical, supervisory, and proper predicaments5.

Adaptive reuse of existing buildings offers a crucial strategy for reducing carbon emissions and conserving resources. This approach is supported by established certification systems like the Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design (LEED) protocol for Existing Buildings, which emphasizes sustainability in the renovation and continued use of built structures6. Nevertheless, despite advancements in building energy efficiency, the renovation of heritage buildings poses unique challenges due to the need to address both technical constraints and the preservation of historical and architectural integrity7. In addition to lowering carbon emissions, the concept of “sustainable adaptation” has become central to the transformation of traditional buildings. This approach integrates two key principles: adaptive reuse, which involves repurposing existing structures while maintaining their historical character, and energy transition, achieved through the incorporation of renewable energy technologies8. Embodied Energy measured as the carbon dioxide avoided through reuse or as the carbon equivalent of the energy and materials invested in constructing an existing building capitalizes on the extended lifespan of buildings, which in urban environments can last for centuries. Even contemporary structures made of concrete and steel can achieve long lifespans with proper maintenance. However, it’s important to note that while environmental benefits are often associated with building reuse, these benefits are not automatically realized. One key limitation is that reusing existing buildings does not necessarily eliminate the demand for new construction. In some cases, spillover effects may lead to an overall increase in building activity rather than a reduction9. Recent systematic reviews by Alexakis et al.10 and Costa-Carrapiço et al.11 demonstrate the expanding role of GAs in building retrofits. These tools help navigate large solution spaces and generate Pareto-optimal solutions under conflicting objectives. However, challenges persist, including model complexity, data limitations, and integration into decision-making frameworks. Despite their growing application, GA-based approaches are still underrepresented in heritage retrofitting projects, partly due to the qualitative and cultural variables inherent in such buildings11.

Consequently, Circular Economy (CE) principles and Lifecycle Assessment (LCA) methodologies are gaining traction in building renovation dissertation12. The CE paradigm promotes a shift away from the linear “take-make-dispose” model toward closed-loop systems, emphasizing reuse, material recovery, adaptability, and long-term resilience. Lifecycle thinking complements this approach by enabling a comprehensive evaluation of environmental impacts across a building’s lifespan from material extraction to demolition13. Recent developments in Bio-Based materials suggest hopeful prospects to align retrofitting strategies with both CE and LCA frameworks. Resulting from renewable sources such as wood, hemp, straw, and mycelium, these kinds of materials are suited for circular applications due to low embodied energy, carbon sequestration capacity, and biodegradability14. Nevertheless, their integration into heritage retrofitting remains under-explored, with limited studies addressing compatibility, regulatory acceptance, or long-term performance in historical contexts15. Despite the growing interest in circular construction and low-impact materials, the intersection between Bio-Based retrofitting, heritage conservation, and lifecycle/circularity assessments remains underdeveloped. Much of the current literature on sustainable retrofit strategies focuses on modern buildings, while studies on heritage structures often prioritize preservation over performance16. Additionally, circularity assessment tools and LCA models are rarely calibrated to account for the unique constraints of historic materials, construction techniques, and conservation regulations17.

Literature review

Panakaduwa et al.18, stated that the UK is estimated to have around 400,000 historic buildings classified as listed properties or located within conservation areas. While the historical significance of these buildings limits the extent of permissible alterations, the national sustainability agenda emphasizes the need to retrofit existing structures to reduce emissions particularly since heritage buildings are responsible for approximately 5% of the UK’s total emissions. The study identified and thematically categorized nine key challenges, with the primary issue being the tension between preserving heritage values and enhancing energy performance often requiring trade-offs between the two. Additional challenges included the complexity of retrofit procedures and the ambiguity of building characteristics7. presented a methodological framework aimed at enhancing the energy performance of historic buildings in such climates, using Singapore’s Tanjong Pagar Railway Station as the central case study. The approach integrated climate-responsive analysis, heritage-sensitive redesign, and the incorporation of photovoltaic systems. These strategies were validated through advanced digital modeling and performance simulations. The results revealed that tailored photovoltaic solutions can achieve up to an 83% reduction in annual energy consumption without compromising the building’s historical integrity.

Longo et al.19 presented a recent and comprehensive review on the optimization of low-energy building design, with a particular emphasis on Net and Nearly Zero-Energy Buildings. The study examined a range of methodologies, optimization algorithms, variables, objectives, and software tools, identifying a significant increase in research activity related to building retrofits evidenced by the collection of 31 studies. Despite the diverse approaches used across the literature, which made it difficult to establish a unified research framework, the review identified Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO) and Genetic Algorithms (GA) especially NSGA-II as the most commonly applied and effective techniques. Additionally, Evins et al.20 conducted an extensive review of computational optimization techniques, covering both mono-objective and MOO, including various methods such as GA. The review categorizes these approaches into three primary areas of building design: the building envelope, building systems, and renewable energy integration. A dedicated section also addresses retrofitting cases. The study concludes by emphasizing the broad range of optimization strategies employed in sustainable building design. Besides, the complexity of retrofitting where historical integrity must be maintained while optimizing energy and material performance demands sophisticated optimization tools. GAs, particularly NSGA-II, have emerged as robust methods for MOO in retrofit scenarios, balancing trade-offs among cost, energy efficiency, and comfort11.

Furthermore, many researches were conducted regarding to understand the ecological impacts from construction sector which emphasized the standing of LCA for the preceding decade21,22. Iyer-Raniga and Wong23 considered six historical building types and two new-constructed in Australia for LCA. The total primary energy consumption of the buildings studied ranged from 39.15 to 183.72 GJ/m², while their life cycle operational carbon dioxide emissions were between 2.90 and 11.50 tCO₂/m². It was reported that the energy associated with embodied processes, construction, material replacement, and landfill contributed between 4% and 18% of the total life cycle primary energy demand over a 100-year period. Furthermore, when the building life span was reduced from 100 years to 50 years for heritage structures, the average life cycle primary energy use and greenhouse gas emissions decreased by 47% and 46%, respectively.

Likewise, Atmaca et al.5 emphasizes the importance of restoration and retrofitting strategies for heritage buildings, showing that while restoration increases Embodied Energy by 56.6%, it still results in a lower life cycle energy and carbon footprint compared to full reconstruction​. This supports the notion that sustainable restoration can effectively balance conservation and energy efficiency goals. The findings indicated that following the restoration project while maintaining the building’s heritage value and structural integrity, the life cycle energy consumption and associated emissions of the heritage building could be reduced by 22.9% and 28.7%, respectively. In another research, a LCA was conducted on a heritage building located in Medina, New York, over a 75-year lifespan. The study evaluated scenarios including heritage conservation, refurbishment, and new construction, along with six distinct energy performance objectives. Findings showed that the heritage building exhibited higher life cycle energy demand compared to the standards set by the 2012 ICC Building Energy Code. Among the building components, the exterior walls were identified as having the highest primary energy demand24. Moreover, Wu et al.25 conducted an analysis of the Life Cycle Energy (LCE) demand and carbon dioxide emissions for a commercial building in China over a 50-year period. The building’s total energy demand was estimated at 68.46 GJ/m², with Operational Energy (OE) accounting for 58.87 GJ/m² (85.99%) and Embodied Energy (EE) contributing 8.43 GJ/m² (12.32%). The total CO₂ emissions over the building’s lifespan were calculated at 15.93 t/m².

While environmental sustainability in construction has been widely researched, academic literature specifically addressing the integration of CE principles into construction remains limited16. Existing reviews tend to be based on a relatively small number of studies and often focus on specific, fragmented topics rather than offering a comprehensive overview. A closer examination of Circular Built Environment and Construction Economy (CBECE) literature reveals that research is primarily concentrated in four key areas: Construction and Demolition Waste (CDW), municipal and industrial waste, the built environment and construction industry, and sustainable construction materials26. Early literature reviews, such as that by Ghisellini et al.27, focused on CDW management strategies in China, highlighting the transition toward cleaner production. In a follow-up study, Ghisellini, Ripa, et al.28 examined core CE principles applicable to CDW. Jin et al.29, expanded the scope by reviewing CDW studies from 2009 to 2018, aiming to bridge existing practices and theoretical frameworks with emerging trends. A comparative review by30 analyzed CDW management policies in China and the United States, identifying key regulatory differences. More recently, López Ruiz et al.31, explored the factors influencing CE adoption within the CDW industry, offering insights into industry-specific barriers and enablers.

Furthermore, regarding the importance of the circular economy, CE has gained recognition as a strategic framework to reduce environmental burdens across sectors, including construction. CE principles such as reuse, recycle, and reduce are increasingly embedded in retrofitting policies and practices, offering pathways to reduce Embodied Energy and waste generation​6. However, the operationalization of circularity in heritage buildings remains challenging. Shifting towards a Circular Economy presents a viable approach to addressing material scarcity and waste management issues by reducing the use of primary resources and promoting the conservation of existing materials10,32. Genta et al.12 highlight the importance of stakeholder engagement and localized metrics, like the Consumption Footprint, in evaluating circular strategies. Moreover, integrating CE with LCA provides a broader picture of environmental performance, yet tools to monitor circularity are still evolving and often lack standardization6. The Circular Economy is a model of production and consumption aimed at minimizing natural resource extraction and environmental impact by extending the lifecycle of materials and reducing both material and energy waste. It promotes practices such as transforming used materials into new products, designing for durability, minimizing waste, encouraging reuse and recovery, and shifting consumption patterns toward shared use and service-based models rather than individual ownership. CE prioritizes the use of renewable, non-toxic, and biodegradable materials with the lowest possible life-cycle environmental impact. As a sustainability approach, CE must be integrated within a social framework that supports human well-being while respecting the ecological boundaries of the planet33. Methods

This study employs a comparative LCA approach combined with Circular Economy principles to evaluate the sustainability of retrofitting three historical buildings using Bio-Based materials. To achieve the goal of the research, this research employed a hybrid methodology combining environmental LCA, Circular Economy evaluation, and MCDA to develop and assess retrofitting strategies for heritage buildings. The LCA component included embodied emissions, Operational Energy savings over 50 years, and end-of-life disposal impacts. Material quantities were modeled at the building scale and analyzed using MATLAB to determine net carbon performance. In parallel, circularity was measured through indicators such as material renewability, reusability, and biodegradability, benchmarked using the MCI model. These metrics were synthesized in a scenario comparison framework using the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS), a robust MCDA method. This approach enabled integrated evaluation of environmental, economic, and cultural dimensions across Bio-Based, traditional, and Circular-Optimized retrofit options. Life cycle assessment

LCA was employed as a systematic methodology to quantify the environmental impacts associated with the retrofitting strategies proposed for heritage buildings. The assessment encompassed the entire life cycle of the Bio-Based materials, including embodied emissions (A1–A3: raw material extraction, processing, manufacturing), Operational Energy savings during the use phase (B6), and end-of-life scenarios (C1–C4: dismantling, disposal, or recycling). Post-retrofit Operational Energy demand was estimated using literature-reported HVAC performance improvement ranges applicable to heritage buildings in hot–arid climates. The retrofit strategies were assumed to reduce annual cooling energy demand by approximately 15–25%, depending on material thermal performance and insulation enhancement. These percentage reductions were applied to baseline electricity consumption values to calculate annual energy savings (kWh/year), which were then converted into avoided emissions using country-specific grid emission factors. Operational savings were aggregated over the 50-year assessment period. Data were sourced from internationally recognized databases such as Ecoinvent, ICE, and OneClick LCA, and calculations were performed using MATLAB to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. The total environmental impact was calculated by summing the embodied emissions, operational savings (treated as negative emissions), and end-of-life impacts, enabling a comprehensive comparison across different retrofit scenarios. This cradle-to-grave approach ensured that both immediate and long-term environmental benefits of circular and Bio-Based retrofits were accurately captured. The LCA was conducted using three key components:

Embodied Emissions (A1–A3): Emissions associated with raw material extraction, processing, and manufacturing of retrofit materials were calculated using average emission factors derived from established LCA databases, including Ecoinvent, OneClick LCA, and the Inventory of Carbon & Energy (ICE). Where applicable, regional adjustments were applied to reflect local production conditions.Operational Energy Savings (B6): Operational Energy savings were estimated as reductions in annual electricity consumption resulting from improved thermal performance of retrofit strategies. These reductions were derived from literature-reported HVAC performance improvements and aggregated over the 50-year assessment period. Avoided emissions were calculated by multiplying annual electricity savings (kWh/year) by country-specific grid emission factors (Oman: ~0.50 kg CO₂e/kWh; Iran: ~0.60 kg CO₂e/kWh; Saudi Arabia: ~0.65 kg CO₂e/kWh). Operational savings represent avoided emissions and are therefore treated as negative values in the lifecycle carbon balance.End-of-Life Impact (C1–C4): Emissions associated with disposal, recycling, reuse, or biodegradation of retrofit materials were estimated based on literature-reported end-of-life scenarios. For fully biodegradable or reusable bio-based materials, low-to-negligible emissions were assumed under controlled disposal conditions.

Goal and scope of the LCA

The LCA was conducted to compare retrofit scenarios for each case study building over a 50-year assessment period. The functional unit was defined as “one retrofitted building (200–300 m²) over 50 years.” The system boundary includes embodied impacts (A1–A3), operational impacts related to energy use (B6), and end-of-life impacts (C1–C4). Operational savings were estimated based on literature-reported HVAC/thermal performance improvements and converted using country-specific electricity grid emission factors. A negative sign denotes net CO₂ savings relative to the baseline scenario.

Data sources and assumptions

The data utilized in this study were derived from a combination of established LCA databases, regional market analyses, and relevant peer-reviewed literature. Embodied emissions for construction materials were sourced primarily from the Ecoinvent database, the ICE, and OneClick LCA, with adjustments made to reflect regional variations in manufacturing processes and transportation distances. Operational Energy savings were estimated based on typical HVAC performance improvements documented in building energy simulation studies, and CO₂ conversion factors were applied according to regional electricity grid emission intensities published by the International Energy Agency (IEA). End-of-life emissions were estimated by considering standard disposal or recycling pathways, assuming near-zero emissions for biodegradable or highly recyclable materials. Material costs were approximated based on regional supplier data and cross-referenced with literature benchmarks. Circularity scores were assessed following the MCI methodology developed by the Ellen MacArthur Foundation. Material quantities were projected based on realistic retrofitting scopes for buildings approximately 200–300 m² in size, assuming standard application thicknesses and construction practices.

ANN and sensitive analysis

An ANN was developed to predict the optimal retrofit scenario based on four input criteria: GWP Impact, Circularity Score, Material Cost, and Heritage Compatibility. A feedforward network with a single hidden layer comprising ten neurons was trained using the Scaled Conjugate Gradient Backpropagation algorithm. The input dataset included normalized feature values for each retrofit scenario (Bio-Based, Traditional, Circular-Optimized), and the output layer classified the scenario type. The dataset was divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. Model performance was evaluated using overall classification accuracy and confusion matrices. To evaluate the robustness of the ANN retrofit scenario prediction model, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by perturbing the input features. Specifically, the Circularity Score of each retrofit scenario was increased by 5% to simulate a modest improvement in material circularity while keeping other parameters constant. The ANN was then used to predict the retrofit scenario classifications based on the perturbed inputs. The predicted classes after the input perturbation were compared with the original predictions to assess model stability. If minor changes in inputs resulted in consistent scenario classifications, the model was considered robust; conversely, changes in output would indicate high sensitivity to the altered feature. The analysis was visualized using grouped bar plots, where the original and post-perturbation predictions were compared side-by-side for each sample. This method allowed for a straightforward assessment of the ANN’s resilience to small variations in input data, a crucial factor for practical application in retrofit decision-making where input uncertainties are inevitable. All sensitivity computations and visualizations were performed in MATLAB R2024a using custom scripts.

Dynamic decision-making

The ANN model demonstrated good predictive capability in classifying retrofit strategies based on the selected sustainability criteria. However, the confusion matrix (Fig. 10) reveals classification imbalance across categories. While Bio-Based retrofit scenarios were correctly identified, some Traditional and Circular-Optimized cases were misclassified as Bio-Based. This indicates that the model exhibits bias toward the dominant feature patterns associated with lower GWP and higher circularity values. The error histogram shows that prediction errors are generally small and clustered around zero, suggesting that the model captures the primary relationships among input variables. However, misclassifications highlight limitations in dataset size and class distribution. The ROC curves demonstrate reasonable discriminative capability, but separation between retrofit categories is not perfect. These findings suggest that while ANN provides useful decision-support insight, additional training data and improved class balancing are required to enhance model robustness. The grouped bar plot comparison before and after sensitivity shows identical classification outcomes. These findings suggest that the ANN model captures multi-criteria influences beyond minor fluctuations in a single factor, ensuring robust retrofit scenario recommendations. The flat loss curves across training, validation, and testing datasets suggest that the network generalized perfectly without signs of overfitting or underfitting. The rapid stabilization of the loss values implies that the ANN effectively captured the patterns within the input variables for retrofit scenario classification.

Discussion

This research highlights the promising potential of integrating Circular Economy principles with Bio-Based retrofitting strategies in heritage building conservation. The lifecycle carbon assessment revealed that significant environmental gains can be realized through the adoption of low-embodied-carbon, recyclable, and locally sourced materials. Among the evaluated case studies, the Ganjali Khan Complex exhibited the highest carbon savings, aligning with the broader literature that emphasizes the effectiveness of traditional adobe and lime-based materials in reducing embodied emissions in hot-arid climates16. The circularity performance scores across the three case studies indicated that Circular-Optimized retrofits consistently outperformed Traditional Retrofits, both in terms of environmental efficiency and heritage compatibility. These results resonate with emerging frameworks advocating for material circularity in the built environment, particularly in the context of UNESCO World Heritage Sites and historic urban landscapes. Notably, the relatively high Circularity Scores achieved demonstrate that enhancing sustainability does not necessarily conflict with preserving cultural integrity a key finding that supports integrative conservation models.

The economic evaluation further supports the feasibility of the proposed retrofits, showing considerable lifetime cost savings. Although Bio-Based and Circular-Optimized retrofits exhibited slightly higher initial material costs compared to traditional methods, their operational savings rapidly offset these costs, achieving payback periods well within a typical restoration cycle. ANN modeling proved effective in predicting optimal retrofit strategies based on multi-dimensional sustainability criteria. Despite slight imbalances observed in confusion matrices during classification (especially between traditional and optimized categories), Although the ANN model captured key relationships among GWP Impact, circularity, cost, and heritage compatibility, the classification imbalance observed in the confusion matrix indicates that the model is sensitive to dataset size and feature dominance. The tendency to favor Bio-Based classifications suggests that environmental variables may dominate decision boundaries under the current dataset configuration.

Limitations

This study relies on secondary LCA databases and literature-based assumptions for Operational Energy savings rather than dynamic building energy simulation, which introduces uncertainty in absolute values. End-of-life pathways for Bio-Based materials (reuse, biodegradation, recycling) were simplified and may not reflect local waste management practices. Heritage compatibility scores involve qualitative judgement and may vary by stakeholder or conservation authority; future work should formalize scoring through structured expert elicitation. The TOPSIS analysis used equal weights; results may change under alternative stakeholder-weighted criteria and should be tested via weight sensitivity analysis. Finally, the ANN model was trained on a limited dataset derived from the modeled scenarios; therefore, predictive performance should be interpreted as preliminary and requires validation with larger datasets and real retrofit projects.

Conclusion

This study proposed and evaluated a circular Bio-Based retrofitting approach for heritage buildings, integrating LCA, circularity performance evaluation, TOPSIS, and ANN modeling. Three historical buildings Ganjali Khan Complex (Iran), Bait Al Zubair (Oman), and Al-Balad District (Saudi Arabia) were selected as representative case studies. The results demonstrated that Circular-Optimized retrofitting performed best within the modeled scenarios traditional methods across all environmental, economic, and cultural criteria. Lifecycle carbon reductions of up to ~ 1000 kg CO₂ per building were achieved, with Circularity Scores reaching 0.83 for the best-performing retrofits. Financially, retrofitting yielded annual electricity cost savings between $1,600 and $1,900, translating into cumulative savings of approximately $80,000 to $95,000 over a 50-year operational period. The ANN-based decision-support model demonstrated promising but preliminary predictive performance. While it effectively captured the influence of environmental and circularity variables, classification imbalance indicates that further dataset expansion and validation are necessary before broader practical implementation. Feature importance analysis revealed that environmental factors, particularly GWP impact, played a dominant role in retrofit scenario selection over cost considerations. Overall, the findings reinforce the viability of integrating Circular Economy principles and Bio-Based materials into heritage conservation practices, achieving substantial environmental, economic, and cultural sustainability benefits. While the current study establishes a strong foundation, several future research directions are recommended:
