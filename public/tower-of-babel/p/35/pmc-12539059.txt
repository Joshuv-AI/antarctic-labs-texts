Genotyping of Coxiella burnetii isolates in Iran using Multiple-locus variable-number tandem repeat analysis (MLVA)
Authors: Fatemeh dolatkhah, Safoura Moradkasani, Ashraf Mohabati Mobarez, Mohammad Khalili, Ehsan Mostafavi, Kayvan Tadayon, Saber Esmaeili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCoxiella burnetii is the etiological agent of Q fever, a zoonotic infection recognized in various regions of Iran. Despite its significance, few studies have focused on determining C. burnetii genotypes in Iran. This study aimed to assess the genetic diversity of C. burnetii in Iran using the Multiple-Locus Variable-Number Tandem Repeat Analysis (MLVA) method.MethodsA total of 26 C. burnetii-positive specimens were selected from a repository of 119 specimens collected from various locations across Iran. These samples were previously identified as C. burnetii using nested PCR and TaqMan real-time PCR techniques. In this study, the MLVA method was utilized for genotyping C. burnetii isolates.ResultsGenotyping results from the 26 C. burnetii-positive samples indicated that nearly all loci were informative for genotyping, except two loci (ms23 and ms26). Cluster analysis of the MLVA profiles identified 13 clusters, along with 40 singleton genotypes. Iranian isolates were distributed across a single cluster (Cluster 13), which consisted exclusively of Iranian strains, and 24 unique singleton genotypes, highlighting the genetic diversity of C. burnetii circulating within the region.Discussion and conclusionThe genotypic characteristics of C. burnetii observed in Iran differed significantly from those found in other global regions, suggesting unique local adaptations of this microorganism. To enhance our understanding of the epidemiology of Q fever in Iran, further large-scale studies are required to evaluate the distribution of C. burnetii genotypes across various geographic areas, host species, and environmental reservoirs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04418-1.

Introduction

Coxiella burnetii is a Gram-negative, obligate intracellular bacterium responsible for causing Q fever in both humans and animals. Cattle, sheep, and goats are the primary reservoirs of C. burnetii and are the main sources of human infection. Although infection in livestock is often asymptomatic, it can occasionally cause reproductive issues, including abortion, stillbirth, endometritis, infertility, and mastitis. The primary route of C. burnetii transmission to humans is through the inhalation of aerosolized particles and dust contaminated with the bacterium. However, rare cases of transmission via the consumption of raw milk or dairy products, sexual contact, and blood transfusion have also been reported [1]. In humans, C. burnetii infection is often asymptomatic but can manifest as an acute illness typically with flu-like symptoms, pneumonia, or hepatitis, or as a chronic condition, most commonly endocarditis, although chronic hepatitis and chronic fatigue syndrome may also occur. Additionally, infections in pregnant women can result in adverse outcomes, including abortion and stillbirth [2]. Q fever was first identified in Iran in 1952, and subsequent studies have indicated that it is prevalent in both animals and humans throughout the country [3, 4]. A study conducted across various regions of Iran found that the prevalence of IgG antibodies against Q fever in human samples was 32.86%. In livestock, the reported prevalence of Q fever antibodies is 13.30% in cattle, 31.97% in goats, and 24.66% in sheep [5]. Recent investigations in Iran have shown the prevalence of C. burnetii in the raw milk of dairy animals with a history of abortion, revealing a significantly elevated rate of C. burnetii shedding in these animals. The increased concentration of this bacterium in the milk of animals that have undergone abortion highlights the potential for milk shedding to act as a critical pathway for environmental contamination and subsequent transmission of the pathogen [6]. Serological surveys in Iran investigated the prevalence of Q fever among high-risk groups, revealing the highest prevalence (38%) among butchers who regularly handle cattle, sheep, and goats. Furthermore, a positive correlation was found between age and Q fever seropositivity, suggesting that exposure to the pathogen increased over time [7].

The epidemiology of Q fever is complex owing to its global distribution, multiple animal reservoirs, diverse vectors, and varied transmission routes. Therefore, typing techniques that identify different C. burnetii strains are crucial for tracking bacteria across multiple sources and understanding their transmission dynamics. Multiple-locus variable-number tandem repeat analysis (MLVA) has recently become a valuable tool, enabling direct application to clinical and environmental samples without requiring bacterial culture. This technique offers a straightforward, rapid, cost-effective, and reliable approach for microbial typing, particularly for bacterial strains. This method leverages the variability in the number of tandem repeats (VNTRs) at specific genomic loci, enabling researchers to gain insights into the genetic diversity, evolutionary relationships, and epidemiology of infectious diseases [8]. C. burnetii exhibits significant genetic diversity and has been classified into multiple genomic groups (GGs) using methods such as MLVA and other molecular typing methods [9]. Genotyping and molecular epidemiological studies are essential for understanding the sources of infection, strain diversity, and pathogenic factors in acute and chronic Q fever. These insights can inform targeted health interventions, ultimately improving health outcomes in humans and animals. Given the endemic nature of Q fever in Iran and limited genotyping research on this bacterium, there is a clear need for further study. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate C. burnetii genotypes in Iran using MLVA [10, 11]. This study represents a significant step toward a comprehensive understanding of the epidemiological status of Q fever in Iran.

Materials and methods

Samples

This study included 119 C. burnetii-positive samples that had been previously confirmed using molecular methods, including nested PCR and TaqMan real-time PCR assays. The samples were obtained from a wide range of sources, both human and animal, and were collected from different regions across Iran. Animal-derived specimens comprised abortion materials and milk samples from cattle, sheep, and goats, whereas the human sample was a clinical specimen obtained from a patient diagnosed with Q fever endocarditis. To evaluate the suitability of these samples for genotyping, all 119 specimens were re-screened for the presence of the C. burnetii IS1111 gene using TaqMan real-time PCR assay [11–14]. Based on the quantification cycle (Ct) values, only samples with Ct ≤ 32, reflecting sufficient bacterial DNA quantity and quality, were selected for MLVA. According to this criterion, 26 samples were deemed appropriate for the typing. The dataset included a combination of bovine, ovine, and caprine abortion samples, milk from cows, sheep, and goats, and one human heart valve specimen. The final set used for MLVA genotyping consisted of 25 animal samples and one human clinical sample, as detailed in Table 1 [11–14].

VNTR PCR amplification

PCR amplification was conducted to target nine loci of C. burnetii (ms01, ms03, ms07, ms12, ms21, ms22, ms26, ms30, ms31, and ms36) for genotyping via gel electrophoresis. Additionally, six loci (ms23, ms24, ms27, ms28, ms33, and ms34) were amplified for genotyping by capillary electrophoresis [10–15]. Primer sequences for all loci were aligned with the MLVA database for C. burnetii (Table 2). Specifically, the reverse primers for loci ms23, ms33, and ms34 were labelled with the fluorescent dye 6-FAM, whereas those for ms24, ms27, and ms28 were labelled with 6-carboxy 4,7,2’,4’,5’,7’ hexachloro fluorescein (HEX) [13]. Each PCR reaction was prepared in a total volume of 25 µL, comprising 5 µL of extracted DNA, 12.5 µL of TEMPase Hot Star 2x Master Mix BLUE (Amplicon, Odense M, Denmark), and 400 nM of each primer. Sterile distilled water was added to bring the total volume up to 25 µL. The PCR cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, locus-specific annealing at optimized temperatures for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 10 min to ensure complete amplification of the target loci. The Nine-Mile RSA 493 strain of C. burnetii was used as the reference strain, following established guidelines. To validate the specificity of amplification, sterile distilled water served as a negative control throughout the PCR process, thereby ensuring the reliability and accuracy of the results.

Gel electrophoresis of PCR products

The PCR products corresponding to each locus, including ms01, ms03, ms07, ms12, ms21, ms22, ms26, ms30, ms31, and ms36, were resolved on agarose gels with varying concentrations tailored to the estimated fragment sizes [10]. Specifically, 1% agarose was used for loci ms07 and ms12, 1.5% agarose for locus ms36, 2% agarose for loci ms01, ms03, ms21, ms22, ms30, and ms31, and 2.5% agarose for locus ms26. Following electrophoresis, bands corresponding to the PCR products were visualized using a gel documentation imaging system under ultraviolet (UV) light. In cases where no PCR product band was detected, the amplification process was repeated for the affected isolate. If repeated amplifications failed to yield a product, the corresponding allele was designated as null. Fragment sizes were subsequently analyzed using the Gel Analyzer software (SequentiX) to ensure accurate measurement and characterization of the PCR products.

Capillary electrophoresis of fluorescently labelled PCR products

Following the amplification of the six loci, capillary electrophoresis was performed using the Applied Biosystems Genetic Analyzer model 3130x (UK), renowned for its high precision in fragment sizing. The PCR products were purified using the Kowsar PCR Product Purification Kit, and each sample was then diluted at ratios of 1:5, 1:10, and 1:100. For the analysis, 2 µL of each diluted sample was mixed with 10.5 µL of formamide and 0.5 µL of a 560 bp standard size marker (ROX, Applied Biosystems). This mixture was thoroughly mixed, denatured at 95 °C for 30 s, and placed on ice for 2 min to halt the reaction. The prepared samples were subsequently analyzed in an AB3130XL capillary sequencer using the POP7 polymer at the Pasteur Institute of Iran. The data obtained from capillary electrophoresis were processed using Gene Marker software (version 1.6), focusing on the sizes of the standard markers and corresponding fluorescent signals associated with each locus. The 6-FAM dye emitted a blue signal, whereas the HEX dye emitted a green signal. This differentiation enabled the precise sizing of the fragments, which were identified and recorded based on the peaks generated during electrophoresis.

VNTR data analysis

The fragment sizes obtained for each sample were analyzed to determine the number of repeats at each VNTR locus. The repeat counts for each VNTR locus were calculated using fragment sizes and repeat numbers from the reference strain Nine Mile RSA493 of C. burnetii [16]. The calculation of repeat numbers was based on the following equation: number of repeats (bp) = (fragment size (bp) − flanking regions (bp))/repeat size (bp) [17]. The MLVA data from the collected samples were then analyzed using the online database at http://microbesgenotyping.i2bc.paris-saclay.fr. Each sample’s profile was compared against database profiles, with unmatched profiles classified as novel C. burnetii genotypes. Data from the online database were further analyzed using Bionumerics 6.6 software (Applied Maths, Sint-Martens-Latem, Belgium), in which both the study data and database entries were examined using bioinformatics tools across 15 loci. To compare C. burnetii strains, the Unweighted Pair Group Method (UPGMA) was applied. Cluster analysis was conducted using MLVA/VNTR gel electrophoresis for genomic grouping. The discriminatory power of the MLVA genotyping method was evaluated using Simpson’s diversity index, as per Hunter and Gaston [18], calculated for each locus, each spacer, and the entire MLVA dataset to assess the ability of the technique to effectively differentiate and classify genotypes.

Results

Identification of VNTR loci in C. burnetii

Amplification was successfully achieved for 14 loci of C. burnetii in nearly all samples, except for loci ms23 and ms26. Initial gel electrophoresis for locus ms23 showed an anomalous fragment size of approximately 1500 bp across all samples. This anomaly persisted even after repeating the PCR, and the PCR product was sequenced using a forward primer. Sequence analysis revealed that the IS1111 insertion element was present within this locus, resulting in increased fragment size. A literature review confirmed that IS1111 insertion at locus ms23 has been previously observed in C. burnetii typing studies, suggesting that this locus often serves as a site for IS1111 insertion [19, 20]. Consequently, locus ms23 was excluded from the genotyping. Similarly, amplification of locus ms26 was unsuccessful in most samples, with a success rate of only 35%. Literature indicates that some C. burnetii strains may lack this gene [19, 21], leading us to interpret the absence of amplification as null for this locus. Simpson’s diversity index was used to evaluate the discriminatory power of MLVA across all 15 loci, resulting in an overall value of 0.955. The diversity index was also calculated individually for each locus, revealing that locus ms27 had the lowest discriminatory power (0.508), whereas locus ms33 exhibited the highest (0.909). Amplification success for locus ms26 was achieved in only 8 samples (Table 2).

MLVA genotyping of C. burnetii

This study identified 26 distinct genotypes of C. burnetii (Fig. 1; Table 3). Using MLVA data from 15 loci archived in established global databases, several genotypes specific to Iran were recognized. The Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) analysis was conducted to determine the genetic relationships between Iranian C. burnetii strains and global genotypes (Figs. 1 and 2, and 3). Clustering analysis revealed the presence of 13 clusters along with 40 singleton genotypes (Fig. 3). Iranian strains were grouped into a single major genomic cluster (Cluster 13) and 24 unique singleton genotypes. Sample AS43, isolated from sheep cotyledon tissue, showed a close genetic relationship with a French strain. Likewise, samples AG1 and AG4 (from goat aborted fetal fluids), as well as MG101 (from goat milk), also demonstrated genetic proximity to French strains (Fig. 3; Table 3). Discussion

Q fever is a global public health concern due to several critical factors, including its wide host range, high transmissibility through airborne particles, low infectious dose, and ability to persist in the environment. Studies have demonstrated that C. burnetii strains collected from different regions and sources vary in their virulence and clinical presentation, which is linked to the genetic diversity of their genotypes. Genotyping is therefore crucial as it provides insights into this diversity, helping to identify infection sources and guide outbreak control measures. By genotyping C. burnetii isolates, we can gain a more accurate understanding of how Q fever spreads and develop tailored strategies to effectively manage and control this disease. This research forms the foundation for designing prevention and management plans for Q fever across various communities, thereby enhancing public health. Continued genomic research on C. burnetii, supported by international collaboration, is essential for advancing both diagnostic and therapeutic options for Q fever and strengthening global public health efforts [9, 22, 23].

The findings of this study demonstrate that the successful amplification of most loci in C. burnetii genotyping is achievable; however, certain challenges must be addressed. Notably, the amplification issues observed at loci ms23 and ms26 highlight the complexities within the C. burnetii genome. The results for locus ms23 revealed an unusually sized fragment, indicating potential anomalies that could compromise the genotypic analysis. Sequencing of the ms23 fragment confirmed the presence of the IS1111 insertion element at this locus, indicating that such insertion elements can significantly affect the accuracy and reliability of genotyping methods. This underscores the necessity of carefully selecting loci for genotypic analysis, particularly those that are free from the influence of insertion elements or other genetic variations, to enhance result accuracy and dependability. Consequently, because of the anomaly associated with locus ms23, it is advisable to exclude this locus from C. burnetii genotyping analyses. Furthermore, this study emphasizes the critical role of thorough sequence analysis in identifying potential anomalies and understanding their implications for genotypic results. In light of these findings, future research should prioritize the identification and validation of new and more stable loci that can strengthen genotyping methodologies. Additionally, developing standardized protocols for screening insertion elements and other genetic variations could help minimize errors and improve the accuracy of genotypic analyses. Such efforts will not only deepen our understanding of the genetic diversity of C. burnetii but also enhance strategies for controlling and preventing Q fever [20, 24, 25]. Non-amplification of a locus in genotyping studies is often interpreted as an indication of its absence. This phenomenon can result from two primary factors: the VNTR locus may genuinely not exist or a mutation in the primer-binding site may obstruct amplification. Regardless of the underlying cause, the result is a failure to amplify the VNTR locus, which is a critical consideration in genotyping analysis. This limitation can significantly impact the interpretation of results and the accuracy of genotype identification. To enhance the reliability of these findings, it may be beneficial to utilize alternative primers or complementary methodologies to confirm the presence or absence of the target loci. In our study, we genotyped 26 confirmed C. burnetii samples collected from various locations in Iran, using a 15-locus MLVA panel. The analysis revealed remarkable diversity, with 26 distinct genotypes identified among the samples. This finding underscores the significant genetic variation among C. burnetii strains in Iran. Our results are consistent with those of previous studies. For example, a study in Australia identified 14 different genotypes using a similar 15-locus MLVA approach on clinical samples [26], research in Croatia uncovered 13 novel C. burnetii genotypes from human and animal sources [27]. In our in silico analysis, we observed 13 clusters, categorizing strains from Iran into three distinct groups. Notably, Clusters 13 exclusively contained Iranian strains, which further emphasizes the unique genetic landscape of C. burnetii in this region. This genetic diversity reflects the adaptability of C. burnetii to various hosts and environments and underscores the need for ongoing genomic surveillance and research to deepen our understanding of this pathogen. Furthermore, the incorporation of capillary electrophoresis in our study streamlined the genotyping process and significantly enhanced the reliability and clarity of our results.

Conclusion

This study highlights the remarkable genetic diversity of C. burnetii strains in Iran, as evidenced by the identification of 26 unique genotypes using a 15-locus MLVA panel. The clustering of Iranian strains into three distinct phylogenetic groups, including one exclusive to Iran, emphasizes the region’s unique genomic landscape. While the use of capillary electrophoresis enhanced genotyping accuracy, amplification failure at locus ms23 due to the IS1111 insertion suggests that this locus should be excluded from future panels. These findings underscore the importance of selecting stable loci and incorporating sequence validation to improve the genotypic reliability. These data also reinforce the role of MLVA as a valuable epidemiological tool for tracing the sources of infection, understanding strain-level variation, and informing region-specific control strategies. This observed diversity may influence the clinical manifestations and transmission patterns of Q fever in different settings. Future studies should explore whole-genome approaches and broaden sampling to better link genotypes with virulence, host adaptation, and epidemiology. Sustained molecular surveillance and international collaboration are essential for improving Q fever prevention, diagnosis, and management.
