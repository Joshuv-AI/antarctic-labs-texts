Clinical evaluation of right-sided overlap with single flap valvuloplasty and double tract reconstruction after proximal gastrectomy
Authors: Wenting Xu, Yuan Tian, Rongrong Xia, Yongyou Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRight-sided overlap with single flap valvuloplasty (ROSF) is an anti-reflux technique first described by our center for direct esophagogastric anastomosis after proximal gastrectomy (PG). This single-center retrospective study aimed to compare the surgical safety, short-term, and medium-term outcomes of ROSF versus double tract reconstruction (DTR) in patients undergoing PG for adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG).MethodsPatients with Siewert type II/III AEG who underwent laparoscopic proximal gastrectomy (LPG) at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, from January 2017 to December 2022 were enrolled. Based on the digestive tract reconstruction method, patients were stratified into the ROSF group (n = 43) and DTR group (n = 51). Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor approach with a caliper value of 20% of the propensity score standard deviation, incorporating age, gender, tumor diameter, Siewert classification, clinical T stage, and preoperative L3 skeletal muscle index (L3-SMI) to balance baseline characteristics. A total of 60 patients (30 per group) after propensity score matching (PSM) were included in the final analysis. Postoperative follow-up data (up to 2 years) were analyzed to compare the two groups’ surgical/perioperative parameters, postoperative complications, and serological nutritional indicators.ResultsBefore PSM, tumor diameter differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.05); this difference was eliminated after matching (n = 30 per Group, P > 0.05). Compared with the DTR group, ROSF was associated with significantly faster postoperative recovery and better preservation of nutritional parameters and body composition at 1–2 years compared to DTR (all P < 0.05): time to first liquid intake (3.34 ± 1.45 days in ROSF vs. 5.10 ± 2.76 days in DTR), duration of gastric tube retention (0.86 ± 2.49 days in ROSF vs. 4.19 ± 5.15 days in DTR)., time to removal of the posterior anastomotic drain (9.34 ± 3.38 days in ROSF vs. 14.74 ± 12.96 days in DTR), total hospital stay (10.53 ± 4.45 days in ROSF vs. 15.87 ± 13.82 days in DTR), postoperative prealbumin levels at 18 and 24 months, weight loss percentage from 9 months to 2 years postoperatively, body mass index (BMI) from 1 to 2 years postoperatively, and L3 skeletal muscle index (SMI) at 1 year postoperatively. The two groups had no significant differences in postoperative complications or other nutritional indicators (all P > 0.05).ConclusionFor Siewert type II/III AEG meeting surgical indications, ROSF and DTR are feasible reconstruction options after radical PG. ROSF shows promising medium-term (1–2 year) benefits in perioperative recovery and nutritional preservation, and a lower trend of intestinal obstruction, suggesting it is a valuable alternative reconstruction technique for eligible patients.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a major global public health burden, with approximately 1.1 million new cases and 769,000 deaths annually (GLOBOCAN 2020) [1]. It ranks 5th among common malignancies and 3rd in cancer-related mortality worldwide, with marked geographical heterogeneity: East Asian populations (China, Japan, South Korea) face an age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) > 30 per 100,000 person-years—nearly triple that of North America and Europe [1, 2]. This disparity is driven by genetic susceptibility, dietary factors (e.g., high salt intake), chronic Helicobacter pylori infection, and environmental exposures, highlighting the need for region-specific research [3].

Within gastric malignancies, adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG)—arising at the esophageal-gastric junction (EGJ)—has emerged as a fast-growing subtype. Epidemiological data from Japan’s National Cancer Center, the U.S. National Cancer Database, and large Chinese cohorts confirm a steady rise in AEG incidence over the past three decades [2, 4, 5]. Among AEG subtypes, Siewert type II AEG (tumor center: 1 cm above to 2 cm below the EGJ, per the 1998 Siewert classification) now accounts for 30–50% of East Asian AEG cases [6, 7]. Its unique biological features—straddling the EGJ, with lymph node metastasis to both mediastinal and abdominal stations, and ambiguous surgical margin assessment—make it a controversial focus in gastrointestinal oncology, as optimal management remains undefined [5, 8, 9].

Surgical resection is the curative standard for localized Siewert type II AEG, with two main approaches: proximal gastrectomy (PG) and total gastrectomy (TG) [10]. PG, a function-preserving procedure, retains the gastric fundus and body to preserve digestive function and reduce postgastrectomy syndromes (e.g., dumping syndrome, malnutrition) [11]. It is feasible only if the distal margin is ≥ 2 cm (cT1) or ≥ 5 cm (cT2+), with > 50% residual gastric volume preserved [12]—guidelines established in the 2023 Chinese Expert Consensus on Laparoscopic Surgery for Siewert Type II AEG [5]. Japanese studies further suggest PG for advanced cases with tumor diameter < 4 cm (no need for No. 4d/5/6 lymphadenectomy) and TG for tumors > 6 cm to ensure margin control [13].

Critical evidence gaps persist: high-level data (e.g., multicenter randomized controlled trials [RCTs]) comparing PG and TG are lacking, with retrospective studies showing conflicting 5-year overall survival (OS) results [14, 15]. Post-PG digestive tract reconstruction is another key challenge: early direct esophagogastric anastomosis was abandoned due to high rates of gastroesophageal reflux (> 60%), anastomotic stenosis (20–30%), and malnutrition [16, 17]. Modified reconstruction techniques have since been developed, but no consensus exists on balancing anti-reflux efficacy, surgical safety, and nutritional preservation [18].

To address these limitations, our team developed right-sided overlap with single flap valvuloplasty (ROSF) for post-laparoscopic PG (LPG) in 2021 [16]. Derived from the double-flap technique (DFT) [17], ROSF uses side-overlap anastomosis between the gastric mucosal window (right wall) and esophageal posterior wall (right side), with anastomotic coverage by a gastric seromuscular flap. Its anti-reflux mechanism was described as in a previous report [16].

Given the rising incidence of Siewert type II AEG and unclear guidance on post-LPG reconstruction, this study retrospectively compares LPG+ROSF and LPG + DTR, with the primary aim of evaluating their surgical safety, perioperative recovery, and medium-term (2-year) nutritional/functional outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population

Patients with Siewert type II/III AEG who underwent LPG at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, were included from January 2017 to December 2022. Patients were divided into the ROSF group (LPG+ROSF) and the DTR group (LPG + DTR) based on the digestive tract reconstruction method. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. All participants have signed the informed consent form, fully understood the content of the study, and participated voluntarily.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Aged ≥ 18 years; (2) Preoperative diagnosis of Siewert type II/III AEG confirmed by enhanced computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), gastroscopy, and gastroscopic biopsy pathology; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification ≤ III (no severe comorbidities significantly affecting long-term prognosis); (4) Tumor invasion length in the esophagus ≤ 4 cm, invasion depth ≤cT3 (per AJCC/UICC 8th edition Esophageal Carcinoma TNM Staging System, clinical T stage), no distant metastasis, and preoperative stage ≤ III; (5) Negative pathological margins during/after surgery, with R0 resection achieved.

Exclusion criteria

(1) History of other incurable/recurrent malignant tumors; (2) Severe systemic diseases affecting prognosis (e.g., cerebral infarction/hemorrhage with modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score ≥ 4, or uncontrolled hypertension/diabetes); (3) Failed LPG+ROSF/DTR or inability to achieve R0 resection (based on preoperative/intraoperative evaluation); (4) Postoperative pathology failing to confirm Siewert type II/III AEG; (5) Death or complete loss to follow-up during hospitalization or within 1 year postoperatively.

Preoperative management

Basic patient information (demographics, vital signs, height, weight) was recorded upon admission. Routine preoperative evaluations included: (1) Laboratory tests; (2) Imaging tests; (3) Cardiopulmonary assessment; (4) Nutritional assessment; (5) Venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) Protocol Implementation was applied: (1) Preoperative: oral protein-rich nutritional support (20–30 g protein/day) for patients with NRS 2002 ≥ 3, preoperative respiratory function training (deep breathing + cough exercises) for 3 consecutive days, no mechanical bowel preparation; (2) Intraoperative: temperature maintenance (core temperature ≥ 36℃), fluid restriction; (3) Postoperative: early ambulation (POD 1), multimodal analgesia, early enteral nutrition.

Surgical methods

All surgeries were performed by the same team of senior gastrointestinal surgeons with > 10 years of laparoscopic experience to ensure consistency in surgical technique.

Postoperative management

High-risk patients (operation time > 6 h, unstable intraoperative vital signs, ASA III): Postoperative monitoring in the Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU) for extubation or continuous observation.Routine ward care: (1) Postoperative day (POD) 1: Bedside ECG monitoring (discontinued on POD 2 if stable); (2) Analgesia: NSAIDs (flurbiprofen axetil, parecoxib sodium) were used for short-term (≤ 3 days) multimodal analgesia and combined with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs, omeprazole 40 mg iv qd) throughout the analgesic period to prevent stress ulcers; or patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump, fentanyl-based (100 µg fentanyl + 100 mL normal saline, bolus dose 2 mL, lockout time 15 min) for 48 h postoperatively; (3) Anti-infection: Routine cephalosporin antibiotics (cefotaxime 2.0 g iv q12h) were administered for 24 h postoperatively as prophylaxis for surgical site infection, in accordance with the Chinese Guidelines for Perioperative Antimicrobial Prophylaxis in Gastrointestinal Surgery (2022). Antibiotic duration was extended to 48–72 h only for patients with intraoperative contamination or high-risk factors (e.g., diabetes, hypoalbuminemia); (4) Proton pump inhibitors (short-term) and nutritional support (gradual transition from parenteral to enteral nutrition); (5) Laboratory rechecks: Routine blood, biochemical electrolytes, and coagulation function every 3 days. No patients had delayed dietary advancement due to non-compliance with ERAS protocols; (3) Clear liquid diet (based on drainage and flatus); (4) Semi-liquid diet (based on drainage and defecation); (5) Abnormal symptoms (abdominal pain, distension, nausea) prompted temporary dietary restriction until resolution. For minimally invasive surgery, we adopted a ‘selective drainage’ strategy: only high-risk patients (e.g., large tumor, long operation time) received drainage, and low-risk patients had no routine drainage placement; (2) Subhepatic drain: Removed after postoperative imaging confirmation of no anastomotic leakage or fluid accumulation via abdominal computed tomography (CT) ; (3) Central venous catheter: CVC was only placed in high-risk patients (ASA III, long operation time, massive intraoperative blood loss). For these high-risk patients, the abdominal drain was removed early (on postoperative day [POD] 3–4) based on individual recovery status (drainage volume < 20 mL/24 h, no anastomotic leakage or fluid accumulation confirmed by abdominal computed tomography). The CVC was then removed 1–2 days after abdominal drain removal, with the total CVC indwelling time strictly controlled at 3–5 days. Non-high-risk patients did not undergo CVC placement intraoperatively.

Statistical analysis

R 4.2.3 software was used for analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) if normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk test) and compared using an independent samples t-test, for non-normally distributed data, corresponding non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test for independent samples, Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired samples) were adopted instead. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the χ² test. PSM was used to balance baseline characteristics between groups (1:1 nearest-neighbor matching, caliper = 20% of propensity score SD). Propensity score matching was performed using the following baseline parameters: age, gender, BMI, ASA classification, tumor diameter, Siewert classification, postoperative tumor stage, and preoperative L3-SMI. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

After propensity score matching (1:1), a total of 60 patients (30 in the ROSF group and 30 in the DTR group) were included in the final analysis, and all baseline characteristics were well balanced between the two groups.

Only tumor diameter differed significantly (ROSF: 21.11 ± 12.40 mm vs. DTR: 28.05 ± 15.03 mm, P = 0.022).

Postoperative complications (After PSM)

Table 4 shows no significant differences between groups in perioperative or follow-up complications (all P > 0.05). Oncological outcomes

Regarding the oncological quality of the surgical procedures, R0 resection was achieved in all patients in both groups, with a rate of 100% (30/30 in the ROSF group vs. 30/30 in the DTR group). The median number of harvested lymph nodes was comparable between the two groups. The ROSF group had a mean lymph node yield of 26.87 ± 8.35 nodes, while the DTR group had 25.93 ± 9.12 nodes. There was no statistically significant difference in lymph node retrieval between the two groups (t = 0.08, P = 0.689).

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study compared two reconstruction techniques (ROSF and DTR) in 94 patients with Siewert type II/III esophagogastric junction adenocarcinoma who underwent laparoscopic proximal gastrectomy, using propensity score matching to balance baselines (30 patients per group post-matching). ROSF showed superiority over DTR in perioperative recovery (shorter time to first liquid intake, gastric tube retention, drain removal, and hospital stay) and long-term nutrition (higher prealbumin, less weight loss, higher BMI, and greater L3 skeletal muscle index at 1–2 years), with DTR having a higher intestinal obstruction trend (13.3% vs. 0%). Both techniques are feasible for eligible patients, but ROSF is preferred due to better perioperative outcomes, nutritional preservation, and lower intestinal obstruction risk.

Double tract reconstruction (DTR) and muscle flap plasty (e.g., double-flap technique [DFT], right-sided overlap with single flap valvuloplasty [ROSF]) are increasingly common post-PG reconstruction methods [17, 18]. Muscle flap plasty is a direct esophagogastric anastomosis with anti-reflux effects, reconstructing the cardia valve to align with physiological anatomy [17]. DTR is an indirect anti-reflux technique: the double tract shunts acidic reflux, and the free jejunum reduces requirements for residual stomach/esophageal length—ensuring sufficient tumor resection margins [19]. Physiologically, jejunal interposition mitigates reflux symptoms primarily by disrupting the direct retrograde flow of gastric acid into the esophagus and leveraging the unique structural and functional characteristics of the jejunum: the jejunal segment acts as a physiological buffer that dilutes refluxed gastric contents, and its inherent peristaltic activity promotes unidirectional transit of luminal contents toward the distal intestine; additionally, the jejunum lacks the acid-secreting parietal cells of the stomach and has a higher mucosal resistance to acid exposure, which reduces the caustic irritation to the neoesophagus and proximal intestinal tract that would otherwise trigger reflux-related heartburn, regurgitation or dysphagia.

Baseline imbalance and PSM adjustment

Before PSM, the DTR group had larger tumors than the ROSF group. Larger tumors with longer esophageal invasion make direct esophagogastric anastomosis challenging (excessive tension, high anastomotic difficulty), prompting surgeons to prefer DTR [20, 21]. This highlights DTR’s irreplaceable role for advanced tumors, while PSM effectively balanced this confounding factor to ensure valid outcome comparisons. ROSF is selectively used for patients with small to medium-sized tumors (diameter < 4 cm) and no excessive esophageal invasion, which is a rational clinical selection based on surgical feasibility. For patients requiring 3–5 cm distal esophageal resection (consistent with the therapeutic requirements for EGJ tumors), ROSF remains applicable on the premise that the esophageal invasion length ≤ 4 cm and no excessive anastomotic tension exists. The ROSF technique retains a sufficient intra-abdominal esophageal segment (≥ 5 cm) after resection, and the side-overlap anastomosis with gastric seromuscular flap plasty effectively compensates for the reduced esophageal length, avoiding anastomotic tension and ensuring reconstruction safety. For patients with esophageal resection > 4 cm or uncorrectable anastomotic tension, DTR is recommended as a more appropriate alternative. PSM was performed strictly for patients eligible for both ROSF and DTR, effectively balancing the baseline characteristics and ensuring the comparability of the two groups, thus the study results are reliable for this eligible population.

Perioperative advantages of ROSF

ROSF outperformed DTR in time to first liquid intake, gastric tube retention, anastomotic drain removal, and total hospital stay—aligning with the “enhanced recovery after surgery” (ERAS) concept [22]. A key reasons for these benefits is the simpler anastomosis procedure of ROSF: unlike DTR, which requires three anastomoses (jejunojejunostomy, esophagojejunostomy, and gastrojejunostomy) and extensive intestinal manipulation, ROSF involves only one muscle flap-covered esophagogastric anastomosis, theoretically reducing anastomotic leakage risk and surgical complexity, as well as lowering economic costs associated with stapler use [16, 19]. Additionally, ROSF’s minimal trauma enables early oral intake, ambulation, and tube removal—improving hospital bed turnover and patient satisfaction. It should be noted that the similarity in first flatus confirms that both groups have comparable small bowel motility recovery. However, oral intake is delayed in DTR due to: (1) Higher anastomotic complexity (three vs. one anastomosis) and leakage risk; (2) Residual stomach dysfunction (stasis, bile reflux) from jejunal interposition; (3) Stringent monitoring of DTR-specific drains (splenic fossa) for fluid accumulation; (4) Conservative surgeon judgment due to DTR’s higher complication profile. ROSF avoids these delays by leveraging a simpler, more physiological reconstruction—allowing earlier oral intake without compromising safety, even when intestinal motility recovery is identical.

Complications: focus on intestinal obstruction

While no significant differences in overall complications were observed, DTR’s intestinal involvement and complex digestive tract pathway increase intestinal obstruction risk—a complication often overlooked in short-term follow-up studies [23]. In our 2-year follow-up, 13.3% of DTR patients developed intestinal obstruction (4/30), with 3 requiring repeated emergency hospitalization. This aligns with long-term data showing DTR’s cumulative obstruction risk [24], emphasizing the need for prolonged follow-up to capture late complications.

ROSF’s anastomotic stenosis (13.3%, 4/30) was mild and manageable with endoscopic interventions—consistent with its mechanism (muscle flap plasty allows safe dilation/incision) [16]. Short-term proton pump inhibitor use and early hard food intake (to dilate the anastomosis) further reduced stenosis risk in our practice. Potential mechanisms of Intestinal obstruction might be due to: internal hernia through mesenteric defects, adhesion formation due to multiple anastomoses or abnormal angulation. ROSF may be associated with a lower risk of intestinal obstruction, but this requires further validation.

Nutritional superiority of ROSF

ROSF’s long-term nutritional advantages (prealbumin, weight, BMI, L3 SMI at 1 year) reflect its physiological alignment: direct esophagogastric anastomosis preserves residual stomach function, unlike DTR’s jejunal shunting (which mimics Roux-en-Y anastomosis after total gastrectomy [25]). DTR’s residual stomach hypofunction (bile reflux, jejunal mucosal edema) may contribute to its poorer nutritional outcomes [19]. The non-significant 2-year L3 SMI difference is attributed to incomplete follow-up (only 50% of patients underwent CT), highlighting the need for longer, more complete follow-up.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, it may introduce selection bias (e.g., incomplete pre-admission nutritional data, non-overlapping period, 2017–2020, when only DTR was performed) and limit generalizability. Sepecifically, postoperative pathology confirmation of Siewert type II/III AEG was an exclusion criterion, which may introduce minor selection bias as it is a postoperative factor. However, all patients had a preoperative definitive diagnosis via gastroscopic biopsy and multi-modality imaging, and only 2 cases were excluded due to inconsistent postoperative pathology, accounting for < 2% of the total enrolled population. Thus, the impact of this bias on the study results is considered negligible. Furthermore, excessive anastomotic tension was a selective factor for gastric tube indwelling in individual cases. Although this strictly followed the fundamental surgical principle of avoiding tensioned anastomosis, it represents a form of intraoperative selection bias. In such cases, the surgical team adjusted the mobilization range of the residual stomach and esophagus to reduce tension before indwelling the gastric tube, and no cases required conversion to other reconstruction techniques due to uncorrectable anastomotic tension. Importantly, this individual adjustment was applied equally to both study groups based on real-time intraoperative findings, and thus did not affect the overall comparability between the ROSF and DTR groups.

Second, surgical and perioperative heterogeneity exist: DTR cases spanned a larger time window, potentially leading to variations in technique and management, though all surgeries were performed by the same team of senior gastrointestinal surgeons with > 10 years of laparoscopic experience to minimize technical variability. Third, follow-up completeness is limited: Only 64.2% of patients had complete 1–2 year follow-up data. Based on medical record reviews and telephone follow-up with patients/families, the reasons for loss to follow-up (n = 39 total) included patient-related factors (relocation, refusal of further visits, difficulty accessing care), clinical factors (postoperative death due to cancer recurrence), and administrative factors (lost contact). Since loss to follow-up was balanced between groups (ROSF: 30.2%; DTR: 31.4%) and not associated with outcomes (e.g., no evidence that malnourished patients were more likely to be lost), the risk of follow-up bias is low. Fourth, L3 SMI measurement was limited to bilateral psoas major muscles (not all abdominal wall/iliopsoas muscles), which may not fully reflect overall skeletal muscle function. Fifth, Potential lead-time bias may influence nutritional outcomes: the ROSF group had earlier dietary advancement and shorter hospital stay, which could artificially accelerate the detection of nutritional improvements, reflecting a temporal advantage rather than a true long-term superiority in nutritional efficacy—this should be recognized as a key confounding factor when interpreting disparities in nutritional outcomes. Finally, this study included both Siewert type II and III AEG with different extents of distal esophageal resection; although PSM balanced the subtype distribution between groups, the potential impact of subtle differences in esophageal mobilization extent on anastomotic outcomes cannot be completely excluded.

Future directions

Multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with longer follow-up (≥ 5 years) are needed to validate ROSF’s long-term oncological safety and functional efficacy, particularly regarding recurrence-free survival and overall survival. Optimizing anastomotic techniques (e.g., reducing stenosis risk) and exploring novel nutritional assessment tools (e.g., bioelectrical impedance analysis) will further improve post-PG care. Additionally, subgroup analyses focusing on Siewert type II vs. III AEG, as well as patients with different tumor stages, may provide more tailored recommendations for reconstruction technique selection. Further research should also evaluate the cost-effectiveness of ROSF, given its potential advantages in reducing hospital stay and complication-related healthcare expenditures.

Conclusion

For patients with Siewert type II/III adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) who meet strict surgical indications (tumor diameter < 4 cm, no excessive anastomotic tension), both right-sided overlap with single flap valvuloplasty (ROSF) and double tract reconstruction (DTR) are feasible and safe digestive tract reconstruction options following radical laparoscopic proximal gastrectomy (LPG). ROSF presents promising medium-term (1–2 year) clinical benefits including more favorable perioperative recovery profiles, better preservation of nutritional status (serological markers, weight maintenance, and skeletal muscle mass), and a lower clinical trend of intestinal obstruction compared with DTR. These findings support ROSF as a valuable alternative reconstruction technique for eligible AEG patients undergoing LPG.
