Risk factors and predictive models for fat necrosis in DIEP flap breast reconstruction: A systematic review
Authors: Michael E Chukwu, Oghenewegba M Erivwo, Tamunomiete J Whyte, Oyinem Odumah, Kofi A Obeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutologous breast reconstruction is an integral component of post-mastectomy care. Despite the advantages of deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flaps, fat necrosis remains a common complication that compromises aesthetic outcomes, patient satisfaction, and reconstructive durability. This systematic review evaluates risk factors associated with fat necrosis following DIEP flap breast reconstruction to inform clinical decision-making and optimize outcomes.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. English-language studies between 2008 and 2025 evaluating risk factors or predictive models for fat necrosis following DIEP flap breast reconstruction were included. Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I.ResultsThirteen studies involving 3609 flaps in 3013 patients were analysed. . Fat necrosis incidence ranged from 7.5% to 59.5%. DIEP flaps were associated with higher rates of vascular compromise requiring reoperation (13% p = 0.003). Use of lateral row perforators significantly reduced fat necrosis compared with medial row perforators (8.2%vs. 24.5%; p < 0.001). Intraoperative indocyanine green angiography was associated with reduced fat necrosis (RR 0.49; 95% CI, 0.25–0.97; p = 0.021). Higher flap weight and body mass index greater than 30 kg/m² were consistent predictors. Total flap loss was rare (<1%).ConclusionsFat necrosis remains a predominant complication following DIEP flap breast reconstruction. Optimization of patient selection, perforator strategy, and intraoperative perfusion assessment may reduce ischemic morbidity and improve reconstructive outcomes.Keywords: Diep flap, Fat necrosis, Breast reconstruction, Deep inferior epigastric perforator

Introduction

Breast cancer is a global health challenge plaguing 2.3 million people worldwide. It is the second most common cancer in women in the United States. An estimated 665,684 women died of breast cancer in 2022. By 2040, its incidence is projected to reach 364,000 annually in the U.S.1,2 Given this significant health burden, surgical treatment plays a pivotal role in improving survival rates. Fortunately, surgical options such as mastectomies and lumpectomies, along with various reconstructive options exist. Among these options are autologous and implant-based breast reconstructions.3 One of these autologous techniques is the deep inferior epigastric artery perforator (DIEP) flap.3,4 Breast reconstruction following mastectomy has made it possible for many to live a relatively normal and satisfactory life. Unfortunately, these surgical procedures do not come without risks. One benign postoperative complication is fat necrosis.3,4

Fat necrosis is a common complication of DIEP flaps, with an incidence of 12–45% on clinical exam3 and reportedly 35% on ultrasound.4 It is an inflammatory process that is usually associated with tissue trauma related to biopsies, surgery, radiation, and infection.5 Fat necrosis typically occurs in areas of poor perfusion—particularly the periphery of the flap.5 Although benign, fat necrosis can cause palpable masses, pain, and cosmetic deformity. These can lead to poor functional and aesthetic outcomes that result in patient dissatisfaction and sometimes necessitating further intervention.5

This systematic review aims to evaluate predictive factors for fat necrosis in DIEP flap breast reconstruction. Identifying these factors may minimize the incidence, enhance surgical planning, patient counseling, and optimize outcomes. Ultimately, this would improve patients’ emotional, financial, and physical health.

Methods

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search across PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library to identify studies published between January 2008 and August 2025. We designed the strategy under PRISMA guidance and combined controlled vocabulary with free-text terms to maximize sensitivity. Our main search words used the combination: ((Deep Inferior Epigastric Perforator) OR (deep inferior epigastric artery perforator flap) OR (DIEP Flap) OR (DIEAP Flap)) AND ((Breast) OR (breast reconstruction)) AND (Fat necrosis). We adapted the syntax for each database and applied the PRESS checklist to peer-review the strategy. To supplement database searches, we performed forward citation searching of included studies and relevant articles to identify additional eligible studies. . We limited inclusion to English-language publications to ensure accurate appraisal and extraction.

Eligibility criteria

We specified our eligibility criteria a priori to preserve consistency and reduce bias. We included studies that evaluated adult patients undergoing unilateral or bilateral DIEP flap breast reconstruction and reported either risk factors for fat necrosis or predictive models estimating its occurrence. We considered randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, and extensive case series with at least five patients. We included studies if they reported fat necrosis as a primary or secondary outcome or developed/validated a predictive model with clearly reported variables and performance metrics. Studies that included comparisons between DIEP flaps and other autologous breast reconstruction techniques (e.g., TRAM or SIEA flaps) were also eligible, provided that DIEP-specific outcomes could be clearly extracted and analyzed separately. We excluded case reports, letters, editorials, and commentaries, though we screened their reference lists to identify additional primary studies. We also excluded pediatric, cadaveric, animal, and simulation-based studies. When a study included multiple flap types, we included only if we could clearly separate DIEP-specific outcomes. We excluded non-English publications.

Study selection

We uploaded all retrieved records into Rayyan and screened them there. Two reviewers independently screened all records, and a third reviewer joined to adjudicate conflicts and reach consensus. After consensus screening, we conducted a full-text review to confirm eligibility. Study selection is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data extraction

After we completed a full-text review, we extracted all eligible articles and stratified the information into our prespecified Excel workbook. Two independent reviewers performed the extraction, and we resolved all discrepancies by consensus. In the sheet, we recorded study-level details (paper title, author, year, study type, sample size, and location), key clinical and methodological features (intervention and patient characteristics), and outcome definitions and reporting (fat necrosis criteria, diagnostic method, incidence, timing, and need for additional imaging or intervention). We also captured operative variables (for example, flap weight, ischemia time, number of perforators, and intraoperative adjuncts), as well as summary findings and performance metrics for any predictive models (such as included predictors, analysis approach, validation method, and area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and calibration). Where available, we documented age range and sex distribution. When we encountered incomplete or unclear information, we contacted study authors to clarify or supply missing data.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

We assessed risk of bias and methodological quality using ROBINS-I (Table 1).7 Two reviewers independently appraised each included study and resolved disagreements by consensus. . Finally, we graded the certainty of the evidence using the GRADE approach, considering study limitations, consistency of results, evidence's directness, and estimates' precision.

Flap type and fat necrosis

Fat necrosis was the most consistently reported complication, with incidence rates varying widely across studies from 7.5% to 59.5%. In a cohort of 46 flaps, 23.9% developed fat necrosis, with no significant difference between DIEP and SIEA flaps.8

Wu et al. (2014) in a retrospective study, comparing 69 pedicled TRAM (Transverse Rectus Abdominis Muscle) flaps and 69 DIEP flaps, found that fat necrosis occurred more frequently with pedicled TRAM flaps compared to DIEP flaps (53.5%vs. 17.4%; p < 0.001), with most being grade II.9 However, DIEP flaps were associated more with vascular compromise requiring reoperation (13%vs. 0%; p = 0.003). No statistically significant difference in partial flap necrosis or mastectomy flap necrosis between the 2 groups was found. Also, comparing African American and Caucasian patients using the pedicled TRAM flap group, there was a higher rate of fat necrosis (77%vs. 45.6%; p < 0.001) and grade IV fat necrosis in African Americans (42.8%vs. 9.5%; p = 0.005).9 This significant difference was not reiterated within the DIEP group. After stratifying by flap type, race, and BMI, they saw no significant difference between mastectomy flap necrosis or partial flap necrosis between the two racial groups.9

Perforator type and number, and fat necrosis

Large cohort analyses confirmed necrosis rates between 12% and 45%. They highlight that lateral row perforators in bilateral reconstruction and medial row perforators in unilateral reconstruction are associated with reduced necrosis incidence. Also, the studies point to the high incidence of fat necrosis in zones III and IV.3

Kamali et al. (2017) performed a retrospective review of 728 DIEP flaps to analyse morbidity associated with different perforator types: 263 with lateral row perforators, 225 with medial row perforators, and 240 with both medial and lateral row perforators.10 Fat necrosis occurrence was significantly higher in flaps based solely on the medial row when compared to lateral row perforators (24.5 % vs 8.2 %; p < 0.001). There was a significant difference in the occurrence of fat necrosis when comparing medial row perforator use to the use of both medial and lateral row perforators (24.5%vs 11.6%; p < 0.001).10 There was no statistically significant difference in fat necrosis between flaps based only on the lateral row when compared to flaps based on both the medial and lateral rows (8.2%vs 11.6%).10 On further analysis, they deduced that within the same row, increasing the number of perforators decreased the incidence of fat necrosis.10

Indocyanine green angiography and fat necrosis

Regarding the use of indocyanine green angiography (ICGA), one prospective study, analysing differences between 2 groups (24 reconstructions with a DIEP flap and 37 reconstructions with a DIEP flap and concurrent ICGA), demonstrated a necrosis incidence reduction from 59.5% in controls to 29% in the ICGA group (RR 0.49 [95% CI, 0.25–0.97]; p = 0.021), with a major difference in grade II necrosis.11 It was also found that, with ICGA, the number of second surgeries for fat necrosis treatment was also reduced.11

Flap weight, perforator number, and fat necrosis

179 flaps, 166 DIEP and 13 msTRAM (muscle-sparing Transverse Rectus Abdominis Muscle), were retrospectively analysed to find a correlation between flap weight or perforator number and fat necrosis.12 7.5% developed fat necrosis. The study found that African American patients had increased odds of fat necrosis (OR 11.58; p < 0.001).12 Also, it highlighted that the odds of developing fat necrosis significantly increases with flap weight (OR 1.5 per 100 g increase; p < 0.001).12 In single perforator flaps weighing more than 1000 g, 42.9% developed fat necrosis, compared to 14.3% of large multiple perforator flaps.12,13

Bozikov et al. (2009) retrospectively reviewed 100 unilateral reconstructions with DIEP flaps. 30 developed fat necrosis confirmed by ultrasonography and mammography.14 Upon analysis, they found that flaps harvested on a single perforator, patients with a BMI > 30 kg/m2, and revision operations were all statistically significant predictors of fat necrosis.14

Adjuvant therapy

The investigation of adjuvant radiotherapy, on post-mastectomy complications, in a cohort study of 156 DIEP flaps, found that there was no significant effect on the prevalence of flap loss, the prevalence of post-operative fat necrosis, the severity of fat necrosis, on post-operative wound healing involving the reconstructed breast, or the number of volume enhancement procedures following free DIEP flap reconstruction.15 However, multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that older age was the only significant independent predictor of a higher complication rate (OR 1.142; p = 0.033).15

On the other hand, a retrospective review of 88 flaps (3 TRAM, 39 msTRAM, 46 DIEP) found 36.4% of flaps with fat necrosis.16 Upon analysis, they recognised that adjuvant chemotherapy was the only independent risk factor affecting fat necrosis prevalence (25/48 (52.1%); p = 0.001).16

Other complications

Beyond fat necrosis, reported complications included donor-site wound dehiscence, delayed abdominal wound healing, delayed breast wound healing, abdominal bulge/hernia, flap loss, and vascular compromise. Increased BMI, smoking, hypertension, prior radiotherapy17, and thickened abdominal walls18 consistently predicted higher complication rates. Across large retrospective cohorts, total flap loss was rare.17,19,20

Discussion

Mechanistic basis of fat necrosis

The findings of this review support the concept that fat necrosis is fundamentally a perfusion-related complication. Fat necrosis most commonly develops in regions of poor vascular supply, particularly at the periphery of the flap where tissue perfusion is most vulnerable.3,5 This mechanism may explain why flap-specific variables that influence blood flow distribution were among the strongest predictors identified across studies. Increased flap weight was consistently associated with a higher risk of fat necrosis, suggesting that larger tissue volumes may exceed the perfusion capacity of the vascular pedicle.12,14 Similarly, single-perforator flaps demonstrated higher rates of fat necrosis than multiple-perforator designs, likely reflecting a less robust vascular supply to the transferred tissue.3,12,14 Conversely, increasing perforator number was associated with lower rates of fat necrosis, supporting the concept that improved perfusion distribution enhances flap viability.3,10,12 Furthermore, lateral-row perforators were associated with significantly lower rates of fat necrosis compared with medial-row perforators, highlighting the importance of perforator selection during flap planning and harvest.10 Taken together, these findings suggest that fat necrosis is not a random postoperative event but rather a predictable consequence of inadequate tissue perfusion within the reconstructed flap.

Indocyanine green angiography (ICGA)

ICGA is a near-infrared fluorescence imaging technique that provides real-time, objective visualization of tissue perfusion during reconstructive surgery. In the context of autologous breast reconstruction, ICGA has been increasingly adopted to overcome the limitations of traditional clinical assessments that rely on subjective evaluation of skin colour, capillary refill, and bleeding at flap edges, which alone has been associated with necrosis rates as high as 41%.21 The prospective comparative study highlighted that ICGA reduced the incidence of flap necrosis from 59.5% in the control group to 29% in the ICGA group and reduced the need for further interventions for fat necrosis.11

Furthermore, Wu et al. (2014) analyses African American and Caucasian patients using the pedicled TRAM flap. It yielded that there was a higher rate of fat necrosis (77%vs. 45.6%; p < 0.001) and grade IV fat necrosis in African Americans (42.8%vs. 9.5%; p = 0.005). They point out that this disparity is likely due to erroneous clinical assessments associated with darker skin colours. In such cases, we can see the possible benefit of ICGA use.9

This demonstrates the technique’s ability not only to predict ischemic risk but also to reduce secondary interventions and address outcomes related to clinical assessment inconsistencies. Such evidence highlights ICGA as a valuable adjunct that enhances intraoperative decision-making and may translate into superior reconstructive results.

Patient characteristics

Patient characteristics play a universal role in surgical recovery and outcomes. The consensus of our studies identified age, elevated BMI, smoking, and medical conditions such as Diabetes and hypertension as risk factors for complications after DIEP flap reconstruction. For example, Boehm et al. (2020) found that patients with BMI ≥35 had significantly higher odds of delayed abdominal wound healing (OR 10.57, p = 0.0005), delayed breast wound healing (OR 4.45, p = 0.0208), fat necrosis (OR 4.42, p = 0.0212), and unplanned return to the operating room (OR 6.59, p = 0.0155).18 These findings suggest that excess adipose tissue may compromise vascularity and increase metabolic stress, although the risk varies across populations. Fertsch et al. (2021) and Voineskos et al. (2015) support these findings, reporting similar associations between elevated BMI and increased likelihood of fat necrosis. They also identified other factors, such as smoking and diabetes, with increased complications and poorer outcomes. These outcomes19, 22 may be explained by the effects of smoking, which can impair perfusion through vasoconstriction and reduced oxygen delivery. Additionally, Diabetes delays angiogenesis and collagen synthesis, thereby prolonging wound healing and increasing the risk of necrosis.23 The interaction of these factors contributes to increased flap vulnerability.

Several of our studies identified age as a risk factor for fat necrosis. Remy et al. (2025), Taghizadeh et al. (2015), and Boehm et al. (2020) collectively demonstrated that increased age—particularly over 65—was significantly associated with fat necrosis, flap loss, and poorer outcomes.15,17,18 These findings suggest that advanced age may compromise vascular integrity and tissue healing capacity, highlighting the need for careful patient selection and postoperative monitoring in older individuals undergoing breast reconstruction.

Future studies should incorporate patient stratification and emphasize pre-surgical modification strategies, including smoking cessation, glycemic control, and weight reduction, to improve DIEP flap outcomes.

Adjuvant therapy

The main goal of adjuvant cancer treatment is to provide therapies following surgical procedures that help eliminate hidden cancer cells and prevent disease recurrence.24 The two essential treatments for breast cancer patients to achieve better long-term survival are systemic chemotherapy and targeted radiotherapy, which are part of adjuvant therapy. The meta-analysis of 909,077 patients across 35 observational studies showed that breast-conserving surgery with adjuvant radiotherapy provided a 0.72 hazard ratio (95% CI 0.68–0.75, p < 0.001) for improved survival rates.25 The established survival benefits of adjuvant therapy remain well documented, but researchers have not fully understood its impact on reconstructive outcomes, particularly regarding fat necrosis development.

The research indicates that radiation therapy does not create a consistent risk for fat necrosis development.8,19 Some found no evidence that postoperative radiation therapy affects fat necrosis occurrence or severity. Li et al. (2014) discovered that adjuvant chemotherapy acts as the single risk factor which leads to fat necrosis development at a rate of 52.1% (p = 0.001).16 The cytotoxic properties of chemotherapy lead to immune system suppression which makes it difficult for wounds to heal properly after breast reconstruction procedures.25

The timing between autologous reconstruction and radiation therapy delivery affects the development of fat necrosis.26,27 Some surgeons avoid immediate reconstruction when radiation is expected because of fibrosis and contracture. Others believe that adding well-vascularized tissue does not worsen outcomes.26,27 Tran et al. (2001) compared immediate flap reconstruction after PMRT. The immediate group had more fat necrosis (43.8%vs. 8.6%).28 These findings suggest that early exposure of flaps to radiation increases necrotic changes. Delaying reconstruction may reduce this risk. More prospective studies are needed to confirm how the timing of adjuvant therapy influences fat necrosis.

Nevertheless, the overall benefits of adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy in reducing recurrence and improving survival far outweigh fat necrosis as a complication. Current evidence shows controversy regarding a definitive relationship between adjuvant therapy and fat necrosis, with findings varying across studies. Caution with the timing of therapy concerning reconstruction remains important, and further prospective investigation is necessary to define this relationship better and guide clinical decision-making.

Clinical implications and surgical decision-making

The findings of this review have several important implications for clinical practice. First, they suggest that the risk of fat necrosis can be anticipated preoperatively and mitigated through thoughtful patient selection and surgical planning. Patients with elevated body mass index, advanced age, smoking history, diabetes, or other factors associated with impaired wound healing should be counseled regarding their increased risk of postoperative complications and considered for targeted optimization strategies before reconstruction.15,17, 18, 19, 20,22,23

Second, flap-specific factors identified in this review highlight opportunities for intraoperative risk reduction. Surgeons should consider flap weight, perforator location, and perforator number when planning reconstruction, as larger flaps, single-perforator designs, and medial-row perforators were consistently associated with higher rates of fat necrosis.3,10,12,14 When anatomically feasible, the use of multiple perforators or lateral-row perforators may improve perfusion and reduce ischemic morbidity.3,10,12

Finally, the growing evidence supporting intraoperative perfusion assessment suggests that technologies such as ICGA may facilitate more objective decision-making regarding flap design and tissue excision.11,21 By identifying poorly perfused tissue before inset, surgeons may reduce the incidence of fat necrosis, decrease the need for secondary procedures, and improve long-term reconstructive outcomes.11 Collectively, these findings support a proactive approach in which prevention of fat necrosis begins before flap harvest and continues throughout intraoperative decision-making.

Limitations

This review should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, all included studies were observational, predominantly retrospective cohort studies, which are inherently susceptible to selection bias, confounding, and incomplete outcome reporting. Although risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I, several studies demonstrated moderate methodological limitations, reducing the overall certainty of the evidence. Second, substantial heterogeneity existed across studies with respect to patient populations, flap characteristics, reconstructive timing, perforator selection strategies, definitions of fat necrosis, diagnostic modalities, and duration of follow-up. Furthermore, there is no universally accepted definition of clinically significant fat necrosis. Fat necrosis was identified using varying methods, including clinical examination, ultrasonography, and radiographic assessment, which likely contributed to the wide reported incidence range (7.5%–59.5%) and limited direct comparison between studies. Third, differences in surgical technique, surgeon experience, intraoperative perfusion assessment, and postoperative management may have influenced outcomes. Additionally, several identified risk factors, including obesity, flap weight, perforator number, and radiotherapy, are interrelated. For example, obesity is often associated with increased flap weight, while flap size may influence perforator selection and operative strategy, making it difficult to determine the independent contribution of individual risk factors. The exclusion of non-English publications may have introduced language bias, and publication bias could not be formally assessed because of the heterogeneity of study designs and outcome reporting. Overall, the certainty of evidence was limited by the observational nature of the included studies, methodological heterogeneity, and potential sources of bias, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation of the findings.

Future directions

Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies using standardized definitions of fat necrosis, uniform imaging and diagnostic criteria, and longer follow-up periods to improve comparability across studies. Development and external validation of predictive models that account for patient factors, flap weight, perforator anatomy, radiotherapy exposure, and objective perfusion measurements may improve risk stratification and facilitate personalized surgical planning. Further investigation into CTA-based planning, perforator characteristics, and perfusion-related measurements may help establish individualized thresholds for flap design and tissue transfer, ultimately reducing ischemic complications and improving reconstructive outcomes. In addition, future studies should evaluate whether implementation of these predictive tools translates into meaningful clinical benefits, including reductions in revision procedures, healthcare utilization, and patient-reported morbidity. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses of emerging technologies such as ICGA may help determine which interventions provide the greatest value in breast reconstruction practice.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights fat necrosis as the most frequently encountered complication following DIEP flap breast reconstruction, with incidence influenced by patient-, flap-, and treatment-related factors. Flap viability is strongly determined by perforator characteristics, where lateral or multiple perforators confer superior perfusion compared to medial or single-perforator designs. Patient risk factors such as elevated BMI, smoking, diabetes, and advanced age consistently predispose to necrosis and delayed healing, underscoring the need for targeted preoperative optimization. Intraoperative adjuncts, particularly indocyanine green angiography, reliably reduce necrosis rates and reoperations, offering an objective means of assessing flap perfusion.

The influence of adjuvant therapies remains less clear, with radiotherapy demonstrating inconsistent associations with fat necrosis, while chemotherapy appears to increase risk. Importantly, total flap loss remains rare across studies, reinforcing the overall safety of DIEP reconstruction when appropriately selected and executed.

Breast reconstruction represents a pivotal stage in the treatment pathway, and morbidity management remains paramount. Integration of patient optimization, careful perforator selection, and intraoperative imaging may collectively minimize ischemic complications and enhance reconstructive outcomes, while future prospective studies are needed to strengthen the evidence base.
