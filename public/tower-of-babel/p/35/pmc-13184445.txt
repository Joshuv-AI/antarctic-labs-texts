Reply
Authors: Elizabeth Mya Akfaly, Niraj Balakrishnan, Jacob Baxter, Anirudh Vemulapalli, Carly Brovold, Zachary Osborne, Jason Beckermann, Tahlia L Weis, Indrani Sen
Published: 2026

We were very pleased that our report “Etiology and management outcomes of inferior mesenteric artery aneurysm”1 (IMAA) drew a response from Mahida and Jagtap; we read their letter with interest and appreciate their questions. The authors critique the proposed intervention threshold, the etiologic classification methodology, and the absence of patient-level risk factors in the management framework.

As they rightly point out, repair decisions in visceral artery aneurysms in older and comorbid populations can be challenging, particularly in the setting of celiomesenteric occlusion where simple ligation may not be feasible and complex reconstruction is required.2

Our proposed repair cutoff of 1.5 cm is a conservative but safe recommendation. Fatal rupture has been reported in IMAA at a size of 2 cm.3 In a separate scoping review (under review for publication elsewhere) analyzing all publications on IMAA between 1861 and 2025, no ruptures were reported below this size threshold. In our cohort, all IMAA ≥1.5 cm associated with celiomesenteric occlusion with jet flow phenomenon demonstrated progressive enlargement or required repair, whereas aneurysms <1.5 cm remained stable on long-term imaging follow-up. This threshold provides a buffer period for operative planning and medical optimization in complex reconstructions.2 We agree that patient factors, including frailty, prior abdominal surgery, and mesenteric perfusion, must influence clinical decision-making.2 We hope that recognizing the etiologic and hemodynamic patterns reported in our article better inform surveillance and timing of intervention, especially in older or comorbid populations.

With regard to etiologic classification, all patients underwent extensive serological and multimodal imaging tests to rule out inflammatory, connective tissue disease, pancreatitis, iatrogenic, atherosclerotic, or infectious etiology. We do not describe the workup as standardized because standardization would imply the repetitive application of a validated testing protocol, which is just not feasible for IMAA owing to its rarity. Upon retrospective review of clinical data and imaging findings, we did not identify any clinical uncertainty in the final etiologic diagnosis. However, we acknowledge that, at the time of initial presentation, given the rarity of IMAA, a degree of etiologic uncertainty was unavoidable.

Our study highlights the importance of the underlying etiology and mesenteric inflow status in the management of IMAA. Advancements in computational hemodynamic modelling are expected to reach clinical utility for the management of all aneurysms in the near future.4,5
