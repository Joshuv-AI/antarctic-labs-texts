Biomechanical effects of three prosthetic materials on implant-supported fixed restoration following fibula reconstruction of maxillary bone defects: a finite element analysis
Authors: Ruxue Jiang, Baoyu Dong, Xiao Zeng, Huarong Ao, Ying Li, Maohua Meng, Huan Wang, Mengyao Peng, Zhongfei Hu, Rongfei Lu, Shuntao Yu, Siyang Luo, Yu Luo, Qiang Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to evaluate, through finite element analysis, the biomechanical changes in three superstructure materials with distinct mechanical and thermal properties during implant-supported reconstruction of maxillary defects using fibular grafts under environmental temperature fluctuations.MethodsA model of maxillary defect reconstruction was established using images of a vascularized fibula derived from the Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) database. The defect spanned from the right central incisor to the left first molar. For the two different implant position designs, independent finite element analysis models were created. Mechanical study models were constructed according to different temperature conditions (0 °C and 60 °C), prosthetic materials (zirconia, titanium, and polyetheretherketone), and loading modes (intercuspal occlusion, canine-guided occlusion, and group function occlusion), respectively. DICOM data were processed using Mimics Research 21.0, Geomagic Wrap 2021, and SolidWorks 2023 to reconstruct, assemble, and refine the 3D models of the maxilla, fibula, and implant-prosthetic components. Mesh generation for all the parts was performed in Ansys Workbench 2024. A sequential thermal‒stress coupling analysis was conducted to evaluate the mechanical response of the prosthesis under thermal cycling. A cyclic thermal load (0 °C and 60 °C) was applied as a boundary condition, and the resulting transient temperature field was imported into the structural module as a body load. Subsequently, 25 chewing cycles within a 5-second period were simulated. The von Mises stress and principal stresses in all prosthetic components and bone tissue were assessed. Based on implant distribution, two groups were designed: Group A (implant sites 11, 22, 24, and 26) and Group B (implant sites 11, 23, 24, and 26).Results(1) At the same implant site, the von Mises stress values of the restorations, abutments, implants, and central screws made from all three materials were greater under low-temperature conditions than under high-temperature conditions. (2) Under identical temperature and material conditions, the von Mises stress values for Group A were consistently greater than those for Group B, indicating a biomechanical advantage of canine site placement. (3) Among the three materials, the abutments, implants, and central screws of the PEEK prosthesis presented the highest von Mises stress values, with stress primarily concentrated on the implant. (4) The von Mises stress values in the canine-guided occlusion group were lower than those in the group functional occlusion group.ConclusionsLow-temperature environments and material selection significantly influence the mechanical behaviour of restorative systems: stress in restorations made of zirconia is concentrated within the restoration itself, potentially reducing the load on the supporting structures; PEEK, on the other hand, transfers more stress to the implant and abutment; and pure titanium has the most uniform stress distribution. Furthermore, stress is concentrated in the cortical bone neck of the first molar region of the fibular flap, and the use of canine implant sites or canine-protected abutments can reduce overall stress levels.

Background

The maxilla is a central anatomical component of the face, is involved in the formation of the oral, nasal, and orbital cavities, and plays an essential role in facial aesthetics and oral and maxillofacial function [1]. However, owing to its intricate three-dimensional architecture—featuring anatomical elements such as the maxillary sinus and tooth-bearing ridges—replacing or repairing the maxilla after removal (maxillectomy) presents notable difficulties in clinical practice. In order for reconstruction to be successful, it must restore both facial contours and provide a stable anatomical foundation for functional rehabilitation [2]. Consequently, striking the right equilibrium between aesthetic appearance and functional performance continues to be a primary objective in maxillofacial reconstructive procedures [3]. Vascularized bone grafts, leveraging their abundant blood supply and strong bone healing capacity, have become the most commonly used and clinically proven method for maxillary defect reconstruction. Among these options, the vascularized fibular flap, owing to its excellent adaptability, has emerged as the core flap for maxillary defect reconstruction [4]. Patients with jaw defects often suffer from severe oral and maxillofacial dysfunction, necessitating comprehensive reconstruction of both soft and hard tissues as well as occlusion. The combination of vascularized fibular flap reconstruction and implant-supported dentures is widely regarded as an optimal approach for restoring both morphology and function. This approach not only restores facial aesthetics but also achieves stable retention through osseointegration between the implants and the fibular graft. Its advantages include superior masticatory performance and the obviation of foreign body sensation, which substantially improve postoperative outcomes related to quality of life [4, 5]. However, a major clinical challenge associated with vascularized bone flap reconstruction combined with implant-supported dentures is progressive peri-implant bone resorption over time [6, 7]. Chiapasco et al. [7] reported that following maxillo-mandibular reconstruction using vascularized fibular grafts, substantial bone loss around the implants occurred during a nine-year follow-up period, with more pronounced resorption observed in the maxillary reconstructions than in the mandibular reconstructions. Therefore, monitoring peri-implant bone stability following vascularized fibular flap reconstruction is essential, as this directly affects the lasting performance and practical application value of artificial tooth replacements.

The stability of peri-implant bone tissue following jaw reconstruction is closely related to biological and biomechanical factors [8, 9]. Among these, ensuring long-term success rates after implant loading requires particular attention to biomechanical influences, including material selection and structural design of the superstructure, magnitude and distribution of occlusal forces, occlusal movement patterns, and abutment connection types [9, 10]. The mechanical properties of restorative materials directly affect the mechanical behaviour of the implant–abutment–crown complex, thereby altering the load transfer pathways between implant components and surrounding bone tissue [11].

Titanium (Ti) and zirconia (Zr) have been widely applied in clinical settings because of their outstanding mechanical performance, good biocompatibility, and relatively high elastic modulus [12–14]. In recent years, polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and its composites have emerged as potential alternative materials for abutments and restorative frameworks. Their combination of flexibility, lightweight properties, and favourable stress-shielding effects offers new avenues for restorative material selection [15, 16]. Notably, the clinical longevity of dental prostheses is affected by multiple factors. Changes in temperature associated with the intake of hot or cold foods can cause thermal expansion and contraction of restorative materials, leading to internal stress and surface damage [17]. Previous studies on the durability of restorations have often overlooked the potential impact of intraoral temperature fluctuations on material performance [18, 19]. Additionally, most finite element studies have applied static loading conditions, which do not accurately represent the dynamic physiological environment of the oral cavity [20]. In fact, during daily physiological activities, natural teeth and artificial restorations within the oral cavity constantly exist in a dynamically changing microenvironment. They must endure occlusal forces that continuously vary in magnitude and direction while frequently experiencing temperature fluctuations caused by eating, drinking, and other activities [21]. This places greater demands on the mechanical stability of restorations.

When maxillary reconstruction is performed in patients using a vascularized fibular flap and implant-supported prostheses, the resorption of marginal bone in the fibula is a major concern. The distribution of occlusal forces is among the key factors influencing this process [22]. In implant-supported fixed prostheses, group functional occlusion and canine-guided occlusion are typically used to manage biomechanical loads during lateral mandibular movements [23]. Compared with canine-guided occlusion, group functional occlusion may result in increased marginal bone resorption around implants. This may be attributed to increased lateral occlusal forces, unfavourable load directions, nonfunctional contacts, and potential contacts on the nonworking side [24]. In contrast, canine-guided occlusion permits only canine contact during lateral movements, significantly reducing lateral loads on other implant restorations and theoretically promoting osseointegration and marginal bone stability [25]. However, there are currently insufficient evidence-based data to determine the optimal occlusal design for implant restoration following vascularized fibular reconstruction of the maxilla.

Finite element analysis (FEA) serves as an efficient numerical computation method, offering significant advantages in investigating the biomechanical response of complex geometries such as the jawbone-implant complex. Through digital modelling, it can simulate stress distribution characteristics under various loading conditions [26, 27].

At present, clinical evidence regarding peri-implant bone changes in vascularized bone grafts remains limited, and no clear consensus or treatment guidelines exist for maxillary defect reconstruction [28, 29]. Biomechanical studies focusing on implant-supported prosthetic restoration combined with vascularized bone grafts are scarce, with most existing research concentrating on mandibular reconstructions or single variables such as implant number or length [30–32]. To date, no biomechanical studies have reported on implant-supported denture restoration combined with vascularized fibular grafts for maxillary defect reconstruction.

Therefore, this study aimed to establish a three-dimensional finite element model of vascularized fibular flap reconstruction of maxillary defects combined with implant-supported prosthetic restoration. By employing finite element stress analysis, the performance of three prosthetic materials—Zr, Ti, and PEEK—in vascularized fibular flap reconstruction for maxillary bone defects followed by implant-supported prosthodontic restoration was evaluated. Under conditions such as nonlinear loading and environmental temperature fluctuations, changes in stress distribution across components such as bone, implants, abutments, screws, and prostheses were examined. The aim is to provide mechanical research references for material selection and structural optimization in implant-supported maxillary defect reconstruction.

Methods

Modelling

Computed tomography data for fibular reconstruction of maxillary defects were obtained experimentally. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Guizhou Medical University. The data were uniformly exported in DICOM format and subsequently transferred to Mimics 21.0 (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium). Within the software, image segmentation was performed to reconstruct the two-dimensional tomographic bone images into three-dimensional models, which were saved in STL format. The resulting maxillary STL model files were imported into the reverse engineering software Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems, USA). The images were segmented within the software, and two-dimensional cross-sectional bone images were reconstructed into three-dimensional models and saved in STL format. The resulting STL model files of the maxilla and fibula were subsequently imported into the reverse engineering software Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems, USA). The models underwent polygonization and surface smoothing. On the basis of measurement data from the patient’s preoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images, the maxillary cortical bone thickness was adjusted to 2 mm, thereby constructing a layered solid model of the maxillary “cortical-cancellous bone”. The fibula model was subsequently internally smoothed and offset by 1.5 mm to form a layered “cortical-cancellous” structure [31]. The final maxillary and fibula models were exported in STEP format.

Based on Straumann implant data (models: BLT 3.3 × 10 mm, BLT 4.1 × 8 mm), three-dimensional solid models of the implants and central screws were constructed using proportional modelling in SolidWorks 2023 software. Using 3Shape software, we completed the 3D design of two sets of restorative crowns and abutments for different implant positions, which were initially saved in STL format. The STL files for both sets of restorative crowns and abutments were imported into Geomagic Wrap 2021 for surface precision optimization and solidification processing and ultimately exported in STEP format.

The “STEP” files of the maxillary bone and fibula models were imported into SolidWorks 2023. The cortical bone and cancellous bone models of the maxillary bone and fibula were assembled using the original alignment method. Four implants were placed at sites 11, 22, 24, and 26. After the implants, abutments, and central screws were assembled via Boolean operations, the assembly of Model Group A was completed. Boolean checks were performed to ensure that no interference existed before the model assembly was finalized. The above protocol was repeated, and four implants were placed at positions 11, 23, 24, and 26 to complete the assembly of Model Group B. All the models were exported in STEP format (Fig. 1).

Meshing procedure

The “.step” files of both model groups were imported into Ansys Workbench (ANSYS 2024; Swanson Analysis Systems Inc., Houston, PA, USA). To ensure the reliability and numerical accuracy of the biomechanical finite element model, a systematic mesh convergence study was conducted. The objective was to determine an optimal mesh density that balances solution accuracy with computational efficiency while maintaining the relative error below 5%. A series of models with progressively refined element sizes were generated and analysed under identical loading and boundary conditions. The von Mises stress within the implant and the maximum principal stress in the surrounding cortical bone were selected as key indicators for convergence assessment. In this experiment, three dental prosthetic materials were selected as the subjects: Zr, Ti, and PEEK. Two groups were established on the basis of different tooth positions, with each material subjected to both low-temperature and high-temperature conditions. Additionally, three loading configurations—posterior vertical loading, canine-guided occlusal loading, and group functional occlusal loading—were selected. Ultimately, a total of thirty-six independent finite element analysis models, with twelve models per loading configuration, were established (Table 6).

Boundary conditions

In this experiment, it was assumed that complete osseointegration occurred between the implant and the jawbone and that there was fixed contact between the implant and the jawbone as well as between the prosthesis and the implant. The maxillae on both sides were fixed in place, with no rotation or movement in any direction. To perform analyses and obtain accurate results in the developed mathematical model, it was necessary to define the surface relationships between the model components within the analysis software. In this study, the “bonded” contact definition was applied to all contact areas of the model, including the cortical–cancellous bone interface, the implant–bone contact zone, the implant–abutment and screw connections, and the abutment–restoration interface. This definition ensured that the components behave as a single rigid body at the interfaces, with no separation or relative motion. This approach is based on the assumption that the components move in complete coordination throughout the entire motion sequence.

Mechanical and thermal properties of the tissue and prosthetic materials

The oral mucosa has a certain tolerance threshold for temperature; when the temperature exceeds 60 °C, the significant pain occurs and becomes intolerable. Therefore, the maximum temperature in the oral simulation calculations was set to 60 °C. Under low-temperature conditions, such as chewing ice, the minimum temperature inside the mouth approaches 0 °C [37]. On the basis of these findings, to simulate stress conditions under extreme temperatures, the initial temperature of the restoration in the oral environment was established as the normal oral temperature in humans(37 °C). In this study, two temperature scenarios were established:Thermal loading: The temperature of the restoration’s outer surface rapidly increases to 60 °C within 1 s and then slowly returns to the initial temperature (37 °C) over 4 s;Cold loading: The temperature of the restoration’s outer surface rapidly decreases to 0 °C within 1 s and then slowly returns to the initial temperature (37 °C) after 4 s [33]. Convective heat transfer was used for the convection boundary conditions. The convective heat transfer coefficients were set as follows: 595 W/(m²·K) for the thermal load and 737 W/(m²·K) for the cold loading scenario [33]. The total duration of the transient thermal analysis was set to 5 s. Upon completion of the calculation, the resulting transient temperature field was imported into the transient structural mechanics module for subsequent sequential thermomechanical coupling analysis, as shown in Fig. 3.

Experimental observation criteria

In the experiments, the equivalent stress (von Mises stress) and principal stress were selected as the core analytical indicators. The selection of both indicators was based on material mechanical properties and clinical failure risk assessment requirements. Equivalent stress is primarily used to evaluate yield failure risks in ductile materials, whereas principal stress is mainly employed to predict fracture failure in brittle materials [42].

Discussion

The use of autogenous bone grafting to reconstruct continuous maxillary defects presents complex biomechanical challenges, particularly when it is combined with implant-supported prostheses. These challenges stem from the biomechanical heterogeneity of the grafted bone and the altered functional loading environment, both of which may affect long-term implant stability and functionality [43]. On the basis of real-world radiographic data of maxillary reconstruction using a vascularized fibular flap, a three-dimensional finite element model was constructed in this study to evaluate the mechanical response under thermomechanical coupling conditions. Considering the extreme temperature fluctuations caused by dietary intake (e.g., ice water at 0 °C and hot beverages at 60 °C) [37], these values were set as the thermal boundaries. Multiple studies have shown that the posterior dentition is the primary load-bearing region during mastication, where stress concentration is most likely to occur [44–46]. Accordingly, this study adopted a comprehensive modelling approach combining realistic anatomy, controlled variables, and simulated masticatory cycles. Two representative occlusal contact states—vertical loading in the posterior region and oblique lateral loading—were established. By superimposing a 5-second transient temperature cycle with 25 mechanical loading cycles, the mechanical behaviour of multicomponent implant-supported fixed dentures was evaluated under conditions simulating peak functional loads.

Within the realm of dental implant procedures, the arrangement of these fixtures is subject to a variety of determining elements, including the style of replacement teeth, the quantity of bone material present, operational constraints, and individual patient characteristics such as how wide the mouth can open [47]. In this study, regardless of low-temperature or high-temperature conditions, the von Mises stress values for all the components in Group B (implant sites 11, 23, 24, and 26) were lower than those in Group A (implant sites 11, 22, 24, and 26), and this pattern remained consistent across the three prosthesis materials. The superior mechanical performance of Group B primarily resulted from placing the implant in the canine (site 23) rather than the lateral incisor (site 22) position, which achieved a greater anteroposterior (A-P) distance. The A-P span is a key factor influencing the resistance of a prosthesis to rotation and stress distribution [48]. Furthermore, the mechanical advantage of Group B was also related to the anatomical characteristics of the canine region. The maxillary canine, situated at the arch curvature, serves as the mechanical core of the “canine pillar” and plays a crucial role in masticatory force transmission [45]. In the fibula reconstruction model in this study, implant placement at site 23 not only aligned with the biomechanical force transmission pathway of the dental arch but also enhanced the overall stability of the prosthesis.

Temperature fluctuations in the oral environment can generate thermal stress within restorations, posing a potential risk factor for restoration failure [49]. The results of this investigation indicate that compared with thermal stimuli, cold stimuli lead to more pronounced variations in stress levels in both implant structures and adjacent tissues. Consistent with the findings of Yazdani et al. [50], the thermal stresses generated on prosthesis surfaces under low-temperature conditions exceed those under high-temperature conditions. The underlying mechanism is attributed to the higher thermal convection coefficient associated with cold loading, which induces more pronounced temperature fluctuations and consequently generates higher thermal stresses [33].

According to Meric et al. [51] Mechanical loading on dental implants and the resulting bone tissue deformation can be affected by the selection of various prosthetic materials. Zr has a yield strength of 820 MPa, Ti has a yield strength of 795 MPa, and PEEK has a yield strength of 96.9 MPa [39, 44]. In this study, none of the three restorative materials exceeded their yield strength. On the basis of the final restorative strength, the experimental results confirmed the feasibility of all three materials for fibular reconstruction in maxillary defect repair. The findings demonstrated that under all the conditions, the von Mises stress values of the restorations followed the trend of Zr > Ti > PEEK. A study evaluating the biomechanical behaviour of implant-supported fixed restorations in the maxillary posterior region under different restorative materials demonstrated that when rigid Zr is used, stress is primarily concentrated within the restoration structure, thereby minimizing stress transfer to the implant and surrounding bone tissue [52]. In all the models in this study, the connection region exhibited high von Mises stress values, which was likely due to increased bending forces at this site. On the other hand, this study incorporates the influence of thermal cycling. The aforementioned stress trends in restorations can also be reasonably explained by the thermal stress formula (σ = E × α × ΔT, where σ denotes thermal stress, E represents the elastic modulus, α is the coefficient of thermal expansion, and ΔT signifies the temperature change) [53]. The synergistic interaction between the high elastic modulus of Zr and its coefficient of thermal expansion results in the most pronounced thermal stress accumulation effect. In this study, the highest stresses on the abutment were observed with PEEK restorations, whereas Ti restorations exhibited the lowest corresponding abutment stresses. This may be attributed to PEEK restorations causing greater stress accumulation on the abutment, as their low elastic modulus transmits more stress to the implant system and bone tissue [54]. Additionally, the pronounced mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between PEEK restorations and titanium alloy abutments may generate significant interfacial stresses. In contrast, Ti restorations demonstrate superior thermal deformation compatibility with titanium alloy abutments because of their closely matched thermal expansion coefficients.

Compressive and tensile stresses critically influence the load-bearing capacity of alveolar bone and peri-implant remodelling [55]. Given that cortical bone thickness is a key determinant of the primary stability and osseointegration of implants [56], stress within the fibular cortical bone was specifically evaluated in this study. The stress levels across all the groups remained within the physiological limits (tensile ≤ 150 MPa; compressive ≤ 230 MPa) [57], with peak values consistently concentrated in the cervical region of the first molar implant. In contrast, cancellous bone exhibited minimal variations in stress distribution regardless of the restorative material, temperature, or implant location.

Notably, all the models demonstrated marked stress concentration at the implant neck within the fibular graft segment, particularly in the posterior region, where the masticatory load is greatest [56]. These findings are consistent with those of Yildirim et al. [43], who reported elevated stress in vascularized fibula reconstructions, especially at the fibula–host bone junction and implant cervical regions. In maxillary reconstruction, adverse biomechanical effects may be further amplified because of reduced supporting bone volume and altered force transmission pathways, particularly in the molar region. Therefore, strategic placement of implants and progressive functional loading following vascularized fibula flap reconstruction of maxillary defects are essential for altering the biomechanical environment and improving long-term outcomes.

Compared with canine-guided occlusion, group function occlusion generated greater stress values in all prosthetic and bone components, with the posterior region being the primary stress concentration zone. In both patterns, peak stress was localized at the implant neck within the cortical bone, indicating that this interface is a high-risk site for marginal bone resorption [41]. This disparity aligns with the findings of Abdou and Tennant [58], who reported that group occlusion generates approximately double the stress of canine guidance during lateral movements. Clinical data further demonstrate the correlation between the use of group function occlusion with increased marginal bone loss, primarily because of the elevated nonaxial loads imposed on posterior implants [52]. This biomechanical disadvantage stems from the force transmission mechanism: unlike canine guidance—where posterior disclusion minimizes load—group function involves multiple teeth simultaneously, increasing bending and shear stresses at the implant neck and surrounding bone [59].

These findings are consistent with established biomechanical principles regarding the role of cortical bone. Previous biomechanical studies have shown that cortical bone thickness is a key factor in determining initial implant stability and the quality of osseointegration. From the perspective of force transmission, the implant relies primarily on the cortical bone for support. Thicker cortical bone not only effectively reduces postimplantation micromovement but also optimizes stress distribution under functional loads, thereby reducing peak stress levels within the cortical bone. Therefore, although this study did not perform a parameter sensitivity analysis on cortical bone thickness, on the basis of the biomechanical mechanisms described above, it can be concluded that variations in cortical bone parameters may affect the precision of absolute stress values but are unlikely to alter the relative trends in biomechanical responses among different restorative materials.

Although this study preliminarily investigated the biomechanical behaviour of implant configurations and restorative materials using finite element analysis, certain limitations remain. The model assumes that all materials are isotropic, linearly elastic, and homogeneous, with complete osseointegration of the implants. It does not account for frictional contact, component slippage, screw pretension, soft tissue effects, or long-term fatigue loads. Despite these limitations, the findings provide valuable biomechanical insights for optimizing maxillary defect repair and hold significant implications for advancing personalized implant restoration strategies. Future research combining in vitro experiments with clinical follow-up data is warranted to further validate these results.

Conclusions

Under thermomechanically coupled and nonlinear transient occlusal loading conditions, a low-temperature environment can increase stress levels in dental implant systems and bone tissue to some extent. The stress distribution in components made of pure titanium is relatively uniform. Zirconia exhibited a relatively favourable mechanical performance in both implant components and bone tissue, with stress primarily concentrated in the restoration. PEEK exhibited a relatively poor mechanical performance, with stress primarily concentrated in the implant.

Under identical temperature conditions, the stress values of the components in Group A were greater than those in Group B; that is, the canine region is preferable to the lateral incisor region for implant site selection.

Compared with group function occlusion, canine-guided occlusion results in superior mechanical performance.
