The mechanisms and spatiotemporal patterns of the digital economy’s effect on China’s pollution reduction and carbon efficiency
Authors: Boda Xin, Shuliang Ren, Lianhong Lv, Mengyu Xiang, Jingjing Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The digital economy has emerged as a pivotal driver of China’s green and low-carbon transformation; however, its specific transmission mechanisms and spatiotemporal heterogeneity regarding environmental effects remain insufficiently explored. Utilizing panel data from 30 Chinese provinces (2011–2020), this study employs mediation analysis and Geographically and Temporally Weighted Regression (GTWR) to estimate the impact of the digital economy on pollution and carbon emissions. The results indicate that: (1) The expansion of the digital economy significantly mitigates environmental pressures, reducing pollutant and carbon emission intensities by 0.247 and 0.258 units per unit of growth, respectively. (2) Transmission mechanism analysis reveals that technological innovation is the primary channel, mediating 40.01% of the total effect, significantly outweighing the contribution of industrial structure optimization (16.85%). (3) The effects are spatially heterogeneous, with stronger pollution reductions in northeastern and eastern provinces and larger carbon-intensity reductions in western provinces. (4) The synergistic effects strengthened from 2016 to 2020. The earlier spatial misalignment between pollution control and carbon mitigation was effectively eliminated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44813-w.

Introduction

Coordinated pollution control and carbon mitigation have become critical imperatives for sustainable development amidst intensifying global climate change1. As the world’s largest developing country and a major carbon emitter, China faces dual challenges in environmental protection and energy transition. In recent years, the Chinese government has actively promoted the “carbon peaking and carbon neutrality” strategy and incorporated the synergistic governance of pollution reduction and carbon mitigation into the national ecological civilization agenda2.

The core of pollution reduction and carbon mitigation lies in transitioning from traditional governance models, which treat these issues in isolation, toward a synergistic approach3. Amid this transition, Information and Communication Technology (ICT) have attracted extensive attention because they reshape production organization, consumption patterns, and governance capacity, and therefore can affect both pollution abatement and carbon mitigation4,5. Closely linked to ICT, the digital economy is defined as a series of economic activities rooted in the ICT sector, including telecommunications, internet-based services, hardware manufacturing, and software6. As a key driver of green transformation7, the digital economy plays a vital role in reshaping industrial structures8, enhancing production efficiency9, and optimizing resource allocation10,thereby significantly influencing regional pollution control and carbon emission reduction.

Extensive literature has explored the potential of the digital economy to enhance pollution reduction and carbon efficiency11. Early studies primarily focused on the direct impact from a macro perspective, confirming that the widespread adoption of digital technologies improves environmental quality. For instance, Ma et al. (2022) utilized data from 30 Chinese provinces between 2006 and 2017 to demonstrate the contribution of digitalization to low-carbon growth in China12. Additionally, Sun et al. (2022) emphasized that the development of the digital economy leads to a substantial reduction in industrial wastewater, with a more pronounced effect observed in larger cities13. Current research has increasingly focused on the spatiotemporal heterogeneity and underlying mechanisms of these impacts. Regarding methodology, while some scholars have employed Difference-in-Differences (DID) models14,15 and IPAT models16 to evaluate the environmental impacts of digital economy, they fail to explicitly identify or quantify the specific internal pathways of the impact. Consequently, mediation effect models have become essential for accurately analyzing how energy, economic, and technological factors function within this process.

Despite these advances, existing studies that used mediation effect models exhibit notable disparities in variable quantification and spatial analysis (Table 1). First, existing measurement frameworks for the digital economy are often limited, typically relying on a narrow set of dimensions17 or lacking a clear quantification system18. Second, concerning environmental metrics, some studies rely solely on proxies like PM2.5 concentration or measure the synergistic outcome without isolating the individual effects on pollution and carbon19. Finally, while standard spatial econometric models have been used to examine spillover effects, they generally assume spatial stationarity20,21. These models estimate global coefficients and thus fail to capture the specific, localized degree of impact across different provinces. Given the pronounced disparities in digital development, resource endowments, and functional roles across Chinese regions, utilizing methods that account for spatial non-stationarity is essential for precise policy formulation18. In contrast, Geographically and Temporally Weighted Regression (GTWR), which is widely used in economic geography, allows coefficients to vary across space and time, enabling a more precise assessment of regional heterogeneity and offering a stronger basis for regionally differentiated policy design22.

Regarding transmission mechanisms, existing literature commonly identifies technological innovation and industrial structure transformation as two key pathways23–25. From the technological innovation perspective, firm-level and regional evidence suggests that the digital economy can foster green technological innovation, which in turn supports reductions in pollutant emissions and carbon intensity26. Related studies also indicate that digitalization may accelerate clean energy research and development, improve innovation efficiency, and facilitate diffusion and deployment of clean energy technologies, thereby contributing to lower carbon emission intensity27,28. Empirical findings generally support the view that the digital economy can promote industrial upgrading and rationalization, and that these structural changes can partially mediate the relationship between digital development and carbon emission outcomes, sometimes with nonlinear or threshold characteristics29,30.

Although research on the environmental implications of the digital economy has expanded rapidly, several gaps remain. First, digital economy indices used in existing studies often provide limited coverage of the multi-dimensional nature of digital transformation, which may weaken measurement validity and reduce the robustness of empirical conclusions. Second, many studies emphasize synergy by introducing interaction terms between pollution and carbon indicators, but they rarely report a joint performance metric together with separate effects on pollution and carbon outcomes, limiting a comprehensive understanding of specific trade-offs. Third, spatial econometric models such as the Spatial Durbin Model can capture spillovers, but they commonly fail to capture the dynamic regional differences inherent in China’s development, which is directly relevant for designing differentiated regional strategies.

To address these gaps, this study first applies the entropy weight method and pollutant equivalent calculations to quantify the scale of the digital economy and environmental performance. Building on this, we employ panel data models to verify the direct abatement effect of the digital economy and utilize mediation models to uncover the internal transmission pathways of industrial structure rationalization and technological progress. Furthermore, recognizing that global parameters may mask local variations, we adopt the GTWR model. This allows us to investigate the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of these effects and explore the synergistic trends between pollution reduction and carbon mitigation across different regions, providing a scientific basis for tailored policy-making.

This research makes three primary contributions. First, it constructs a comprehensive measurement framework for the digital economy, encompassing digital foundations, industrial digitalization, and digital industrialization, operationalized through 13 secondary and 36 tertiary indicators. Second, it estimates the impacts on pollution and carbon outcomes separately while also evaluating their synergistic effect, offering a more transparent assessment than designs relying solely on interaction terms. Third, it applies the GTWR model to capture spatiotemporal heterogeneity in the environmental effects of the digital economy, providing a clearer understanding of dynamic regional differences than global-coefficient approaches.

Theoretical analysis and research hypothesis

Direct effects analysis

The digital economy is widely recognized for its potential to reduce carbon emission intensity and mitigate pollution31. By enabling platform-based operations and paperless processes, it lowers the frequency of physical interactions embedded in traditional economic activities, thereby reducing pollutant and carbon emissions32. Additionally, the digital economy facilitates low-carbon lifestyles through remote work, online education, and digital transactions, which not only enhance efficiency but also foster public environmental awareness33,34.

The digital economy can also reduce emissions by improving the efficiency of resource allocation. Digitalization helps alleviate resource misallocation, lowers transaction and search costs, and enhances production efficiency, which jointly contribute to reductions in pollution and carbon emissions per unit of output35. At the same time, digital infrastructure and data centers entail substantial electricity demand, which may partially offset the environmental gains. Even so, existing evidence suggests that, when assessed comprehensively, the net effect of the digital economy development remains beneficial for pollution reduction and carbon mitigation36.

Based on this analysis, Research Hypothesis 1 posits that the digital economy contributes to a reduction in the intensity of pollutant emissions and carbon emissions.

Mediating effects analysis

The digital economy can facilitate industrial structure transformation and upgrading. The rapid expansion of technology and data-intensive sectors has been accelerated by digital economy development, which in turn promotes modernization of traditional industries and supports the transition toward ecological civilization37. By embedding digital technologies into production and management, traditional sectors are more likely to adopt information systems and digital governance practices that shift operational modes away from high energy consumption and low efficiency and toward higher efficiency and lower energy intensity38. Additionally, digitalization can improve market regulation and coordination, reduce transaction costs, and optimize industrial value chains39. By improving information flows and strengthening demand-supply matching, the digital economy contributes to a more balanced market environment and enhances the coordination and stability of industrial structures40,41. These changes are conducive to more efficient allocation of resources across sectors and create conditions for industrial green transition.

This transformation of industrial structures has profound implications for environmental performance. Structural optimization reconfigures energy consumption patterns, thereby increasing the demand for and usage of cleaner energy sources42. Through industrial reorganization, energy efficiency improves while pollutant and carbon emissions are effectively curtailed43. Additionally, the rationalization of industrial structures directs resources toward green and energy-efficient sectors44. This shift not only improves resource utilization but also accelerates the transition to a low-carbon economy.

Consequently, Research Hypothesis 2 suggests that the digital economy reduces the intensity of pollutant and carbon emissions by promoting the rationalization of industrial structures.

The digital economy may also influence environmental outcomes through technological innovation. First, the digital economy accelerates knowledge creation and diffusion. By dismantling geographical and sectoral barriers, digital technologies enhance communication and collaboration among entities, enabling the rapid integration of technological knowledge45. Second, digital finance reduces financing costs and lowers entry barriers, thereby providing essential support for corporate innovation efforts46. Third, the intensification of market competition catalyzed by the digital economy compels firms to accelerate technological upgrades and enhance research and development (R&D) capabilities to maintain competitive advantages47. Finally, digital platforms improve market transparency, fostering an environment conducive to the development of high-quality, advanced technologies48.

Technological innovation is a key driver of pollutant and carbon mitigation. Primarily, it drives the development of production technologies that replace high-pollution, energy-intensive processes, significantly enhancing resource utilization efficiency49. Moreover, innovation advances environmental protection equipment, leveraging cutting-edge physical, chemical, and biological methods to improve pollution control devices and eliminate harmful substances50. It also fosters the development of novel remediation technologies, enabling the control of pollutants that were previously difficult to manage51,52. Finally, innovation propels the deployment of clean energy sources, such as solar, wind, and hydropower53. Utilizing these sources reduces carbon emissions and enhances energy efficiency, particularly in traditionally high-emission sectors like construction and transportation.

Therefore, Research Hypothesis 3 proposes that the digital economy mitigates the intensity of pollutant and carbon emissions by fostering technological progress.

Spatial heterogeneity effect

In China, digital economy development exhibits pronounced regional disparities that reflect differences in resource endowments, policy priorities, and development stages across provinces54. This spatial heterogeneity necessitates a nuanced examination of the environmental impacts of the digital economy. The eastern regions, recognized for their advanced digital infrastructure, innovation capacity, and expansive markets, serve as leaders in digital economic development. While the central regions face challenges related to digital infrastructure, they are progressively adopting digital technologies and exhibiting strong developmental momentum. Conversely, the western regions, burdened by weaker industrial foundations and lagging digital technology applications, require substantial investments to support their digital transformation.

Environmental Kuznets Curve theory indicates that the relationship between economic development and environmental outcomes may differ across development stages55. This variability similarly characterizes the relationship between the digital economy and the environment. Disparities in digital development inevitably result in differential impacts on carbon and pollutant emissions. For instance, in regions with mature digital economies and established infrastructure, the marginal utility of digital advancements in improving environmental quality may be more pronounced. In contrast, for regions with inadequate infrastructure that are transitioning from traditional to digital models, the efficacy of the digital economy in reducing emissions requires further empirical verification.

Consequently, Research Hypothesis 4 proposes that the influence of the digital economy on the intensity of pollutant and carbon emissions exhibits regional heterogeneity across various provinces.

To address this limitation, Brunsdon et al. By incorporating both spatial and temporal coordinates, the GTWR model allows for the estimation of localized regression coefficients, thereby accounting for spatiotemporal non-stationarity.

Core explanatory variable

Digital economy intensity is measured based on the Statistical Classification of Digital Economy and Its Core Industries (2021) and the White Paper on the Development of China’s Digital Economy (2017). The digital economy is conceptualized as an economic paradigm supported by hardware, human capital, policy, and user foundations, with data as a key production factor, technological innovation as the driving force, and modern information networks as the enabling infrastructure. It is further characterized by the joint evolution of digital industrialization and industrial digitalization. On this basis, the index system is constructed from three dimensions that include digital infrastructure, industrial digitalization, and digital industrialization. Indicator weights are determined by the entropy weight method, and the resulting weighted index is used to quantify digital economy intensity. The indicator weights are reported in Table 2.

Technological progress is proxied by the number of patents per industrial enterprise. This indicator reflects innovation output at the enterprise level and captures the extent to which knowledge diffusion and collaboration translate into technological progress relevant to efficiency improvement and emissions reduction.

Control variables

To isolate the effect of the digital economy, we control for the following variables. Economic development level (PGDP) measured by per capita GDP, as development stages intrinsically link to environmental performance. Population density (UPD) measured by urban population density, accounting for the dual effects of agglomeration efficiency and resource consumption. Energy consumption (PEC) measured by per capita energy consumption, directly reflecting regional energy dependence. Foreign investment Level (FIP) measured by the proportion of Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) to GDP.

Data source

The dataset encompasses information from 30 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities across China, excluding the Tibet Autonomous Region and the regions of Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan, covering the period from 2011 to 2020. This timeframe represents a critical phase in the rapid development of the digital economy and the intensive implementation of environmental policies in China, such as the “13th Five-Year Plan”. Data after 2020 are not included because the COVID-19 pandemic introduced substantial disruptions to economic activity, industrial structure, and policy implementation, which may confound the identification of structural relationships by mixing persistent effects with temporary shocks.

Carbon emission data are sourced from the CEADs database (https://www.ceads.net.cn), while pollutant emission factors are derived from the “Environmental Protection Tax Law”. Socio-economic data are obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook, the EPS database, and the National Bureau of Statistics. All monetary values are adjusted to 2011 constant prices to account for inflation, and missing values are addressed using linear interpolation. Table 3 presents the descriptive statistics.

Results

Spatiotemporal characteristics of key variables

Digital economy

Figure 1a illustrates that the average scale of China’s digital economy has maintained a consistent upward trajectory, with the average increased significantly from 16.54 to 50.24. While the digital economy was relatively concentrated in 2011, this concentration has gradually diminished, indicating robust national growth characterized by rapid expansion alongside persisting inter-provincial disparities. As shown in Fig. 1b, the spatial distribution follows a distinct gradient. The eastern and southeastern coastal regions exhibit the highest scale, followed by the central regions, while the western areas lag behind. This pattern aligns with findings from Luo et al. (2022) and Cui et al. (2023)58,59, suggesting that provinces with higher openness embraced digital transformation earlier. Conversely, inland regions possess significant potential to narrow this gap. Figure 1c demonstrates that most provinces, with the exception of Liaoning, have experienced growth. Notably, Guangdong Province achieved a 193% increase from 2011 to 2020, underscoring its leadership in digital innovation60. In contrast, Liaoning’s slower growth reflects its focus on revitalizing traditional industrial bases with limited digital integration.

Figure 1d and e depict the intensity of the digital economy, revealing a distribution that differs from its scale. The intensity has shown a sustained upward trend, affirming its pivotal role in driving GDP growth. Provincial heterogeneity is pronounced due to variations in policy environments and industrial structures. Notably, the southeastern coastal regions and Qinghai Province exhibit high digital economy intensity. Qinghai, leveraging national-level cloud computing data centers, has effectively integrated digital initiatives into its local economic structure despite a smaller aggregate scale. Figure 1f confirms a general upward trend across all provinces, with Beijing maintaining a leading position while other regions show balanced development.

The intensity and scale of the digital economy across various provinces reveal significant disparities in digital economic development at the inter-provincial level (Fig. 2). These findings highlight that a large scale of the digital economy does not automatically correlate with a high level of digitization, as scale is a quantitative measure, while intensity more effectively reflects the depth of digital integration in a province’s economic framework.

Emission characteristics analysis

Figure 3a reports national trends for pollutant emission intensity and carbon emission intensity. With the exception of a mild rebound in pollutant emission intensity in 2014, pollutant intensity declined in most years, which is consistent with strengthened environmental regulation and technology upgrading during China’s 12th and 13th Five-Year Plans. By contrast, carbon emission intensity exhibits an overall upward trend, implying that carbon mitigation lagged behind progress in conventional pollution control and that further measures are needed to accelerate decarbonization. Figure 3b shows a pronounced spatial pattern in 2020 that is broadly consistent with high values in the north and lower values in the south, reflecting an industrial configuration that is heavier in northern regions and more service oriented in many southern regions. Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Shanxi, and Ningxia exhibit relatively high intensities for both pollutants and carbon emissions, which is plausibly associated with high energy consumption and a coal-dominated energy mix. These provinces therefore represent priority regions for coordinated pollution and carbon governance, where measures such as energy structure adjustment and clean energy deployment may yield larger marginal improvements.

Figure 3c shows widening provincial differentiation in carbon emission intensity from 2011 to 2020. Resource-dependent provinces such as Inner Mongolia, Shanxi, and Ningxia experienced notable increases, which is consistent with economic reliance on energy and a relatively concentrated energy consumption structure. In contrast, Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangdong show declining carbon emission intensity, which is consistent with cleaner energy structures, energy-saving measures, and industrial restructuring. Figure 3d reports pollutant emission intensity dynamics and suggests an initial increase followed by a gradual decline, indicating progress in pollution control and early gains in environmental governance. Heavy-industry provinces such as Hebei, Shandong, Shanxi, and Inner Mongolia display relatively high pollutant intensities, whereas provinces with higher shares of modern services and advanced technology industries, including Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, and Hainan, show comparatively low pollutant intensities.

The analysis of control variables shows that both PGDP and UPD significantly reduce pollutant and carbon emission intensities. This is largely due to the growth in per capita GDP, which centralizes economic activities, leading to more efficient resource allocation and improved public infrastructure. For example, in densely populated areas, centralized systems such as heating and sewage treatment operate more efficiently, reducing resource wastage and pollution compared to dispersed infrastructure. Additionally, increased population density accelerates urban expansion and urbanization, promoting the integration of green and low-carbon development concepts. This shift optimizes urban structures, promotes green buildings, and enhances the use of renewable energy, effectively lowering emission intensities.

In contrast, PEC has a significant positive impact on pollutant and carbon emission intensities, largely due to China’s reliance on coal, which directly contributes to higher emissions. Within a stable economic context, an increase in energy consumption typically signals a rise in high-pollution, energy-intensive production methods, thereby raising emission intensities. As for FIP, while it reduces pollutant emissions by advancing industrial upgrading and environmental technologies, it contributes to higher carbon emission intensities. This highlights the need for stricter regulation to ensure low-carbon development while benefiting from economic and technological progress.

Mediating effects results

Mediating effect of industry structure optimization

Building on the baseline panel estimates that establish the average mitigation effects of the digital economy, we next examine the underlying mechanisms by testing whether industrial structure rationalization and technological progress mediate these effects. Table 6 presents the results of assessing the mediating effect of industrial structure rationalization. In Mediation model (2)a, the digital economy’s coefficient on pollutant emission intensity is −0.247, significant at the 1% level, indicating a direct reduction in pollutant emission intensity. Similarly, in Mediation model (2)b, the digital economy’s direct effect on carbon emission intensity is −0.258, also significant at the 1% level, demonstrating a direct reduction. Mediation model (3) shows a coefficient of −0.226 for the digital economy on the industrial structure rationalization index, significant at the 1% level, suggesting that the digital economy promotes a more rational industrial structure.

In Mediation model (4)a, where both the digital economy and the industrial structure rationalization index are included, the digital economy’s effect on pollutant emission intensity is −0.206, while the industrial structure rationalization index shows a coefficient of 0.185, significant at the 1% level. Likewise, in Mediation model (4)b, the digital economy’s coefficient on carbon emission intensity is −0.242, while the industrial structure rationalization index is 0.074. This underscores the direct reduction of carbon emission intensity by the digital economy, along with an indirect effect through structural adjustments, where industrial rationalization contributes a partial mediation effect of 6.5%. Consequently, Hypothesis 2 is validated.

GTWR results

While the baseline and mediation analyses identify average effects and their transmission channels, they impose constant coefficients across provinces and years. We therefore apply the GTWR model to capture spatiotemporal non-stationarity and to quantify how the marginal effects of the digital economy vary across locations and over time.

To assess the relative efficacy of model estimates, both the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) and the GTWR models were utilized. The models were evaluated based on their goodness-of-fit and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), with a higher goodness-of-fit and a lower AIC denoting superior model performance. According to the results presented in Table 8, for both Model I (evaluating the impact of the digital economy on pollutant emission intensity) and Model II (assessing the impact of the digital economy on carbon emission intensity), the GTWR model outperformed the OLS model. Consequently, the GTWR model was chosen for subsequent analysis.

Synergistic effects of pollution reduction and carbon mitigation

Figure 6 illustrates the spatial configuration of the synergistic effects of the digital economy on decreasing pollutant and carbon emission intensities. In 2016 (Fig. 6a), a strong synergistic abatement effect was observed in Shandong, Liaoning, and Jilin provinces, predominantly located in Eastern and Northeastern China, where the digital economy exerted a substantial reduction influence on both emissions simultaneously. Conversely, provinces such as Jiangxi, Hunan, Guangxi, Guangdong, Fujian, and Hainan, clustered in Southern China, exhibited a synchronized but weaker response, with lower reduction magnitudes for both pollutants and carbon. This indicates that while a synergistic trend had emerged in these regions, the intensity of the co-benefits varied. Notably, asymmetry in abatement effects was evident in certain areas. Shanxi, Shaanxi, and Ningxia experienced high carbon mitigation but low pollution reduction, whereas Jiangsu showed the reverse pattern. This suggests that the synergistic mechanism between pollution control and carbon mitigation in these four provinces was underdeveloped and required reinforcement during this period. By 2020 (Fig. 6b), the synergistic landscape had improved significantly. Xinjiang and Qinghai joined the cluster exhibiting strong synergistic reduction effects. Meanwhile, the group of provinces showing synchronized, albeit lower, dual impacts expanded to include Henan, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Fujian, Guangxi, and Guangdong. Crucially, the impacts of the digital economy on carbon and pollutant emissions achieved a higher degree of coupling and alignment. The extreme misalignment observed in 2016—where reduction effects were maximized in one dimension but minimized in the other—was effectively eliminated. This evolution demonstrates that the synergy between pollution reduction and carbon mitigation across provinces has become more robust. The temporal progression from 2016 to 2020 confirms that the synergistic capacity of the digital economy to drive dual environmental benefits has been significantly strengthened over the study period.

Conclusion and policy implications

Conclusion

The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) From 2011 to 2020, China’s digital economy expanded rapidly, with intensity growing faster than scale. While regional disparities existed, digital economic intensity remained relatively balanced, driving national economic growth. Pollutant emissions declined after 2014, but carbon emissions continued rising, reflecting effective pollution control but slower carbon mitigation. Emissions were higher in northern heavy-industrial regions and lower in the south, where light industries and services dominate. Resource-rich provinces saw increasing carbon emissions, while economically advanced regions with cleaner energy reduced them.

(2) The digital economy significantly lowers pollutant and carbon emission intensities, with each unit increase reducing pollutant emissions by 0.247 and carbon emissions by 0.258. Industrial restructuring contributed 16.85% to pollutant reduction, while technological progress accounted for 40.01%. For carbon emissions, industrial restructuring had a smaller effect (6.5%), while technological progress played a larger role (13.13%).

(3) The digital economy’s impact on emissions varies by region and time. Heavy-industrial provinces and early-stage digital economies experienced the greatest reductions. The Northeast, dominated by heavy industries, benefited most from digital-driven innovation and upgrading, while the Western region saw notable carbon reductions.

(4) The digital economy increasingly drives both pollution and carbon reduction. By 2020, interregional synergy improved, with fewer provinces lagging behind, highlighting the digital economy’s growing role in environmental sustainability.

Policy implications

(1) Transitioning from structural adjustment to digital-green technological innovation. Given the empirical finding that technological progress contributes substantially more to pollution reduction (40.01%) and carbon mitigation (13.13%) than industrial restructuring, policymakers should prioritize the application of digital technologies in production processes. Governments should establish special funds or tax incentives specifically for the R&D of core technologies such as energy management systems and smart sensors. Instead of merely expanding the scale of the digital economy, policy support should be directed toward high-tech sectors that can directly improve energy efficiency, ensuring that digital investments translate into tangible technological gains in emission reduction.

(2) Implementing regionally differentiated strategies tailored to local industrial characteristics. The GTWR results demonstrate that the reduction effects of the digital economy vary across regions, so uniform policies should be replaced by region-specific instruments. For the Northeast and heavy-industrial bases where the marginal reduction effect is highest, the strategy should focus on integrating the industrial internet with manufacturing. Policies should subsidize the upgrading of heavy industries like steel and petrochemicals with IoT devices to monitor energy flows. For the Western region which shows notable potential in carbon mitigation, policies should align with the East Data West Computing strategy to incentivize the construction of green data centers powered by local renewable energy.

(3) Strengthening the coordinated management of pollution and carbon through integrated digital platforms. Reflecting the conclusion that the digital economy increasingly drives the synergy between pollution reduction and carbon mitigation from 2016 to 2020, regulatory frameworks must evolve from fragmented management to integrated digital governance. A nationwide digital platform should be established to break information silos between environmental and energy departments. This platform would serve as the technical foundation for both the national carbon trading market and pollutant discharge trading market, allowing for precise quota allocation and real-time compliance monitoring based on the real-time data of enterprises.
