Developing a Modular Platform for the Detection of microRNAs Using Rolling Circle Amplification and Multi‐Primed Chain Amplification
Authors: Mahboobeh Nasiri, Kyle Nowlin, Reza Zadegan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicroRNAs (miRNAs) play critical regulatory roles in diverse biological processes and are key biomarkers in a wide range of physiological and pathological conditions, including cancer. However, their inherently low concentrations in biological samples pose a major challenge for reliable detection and quantification. To overcome this limitation, we developed a fluorescence‐based biosensing platform that integrates rolling circle amplification (RCA) and multi‐primed chain amplification (MCA) to enhance signal and detection sensitivity. The system is engineered to allow flexible reconfiguration for different miRNA targets by altering probe and primer sequences. In this modular system, miR‐i, a miRNA commonly expressed in healthy and cancerous samples, serves as a universal initiator for RCA. Signal amplification was subsequently driven by hybridization with two randomly selected miRNAs (miR‐A and miR‐D), enabling evaluation of system performance under varied input conditions. Fluorescence emission was measured following the addition of a molecular beacon and subsequent spectrofluorometric analysis. The biosensor exhibited a strong linear correlation between miRNA concentration and fluorescence intensity, achieving a limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ) below 10 pM in both buffer and human serum. These findings demonstrate the platform's high sensitivity and robustness. Importantly, modular architecture allows for easy reconfiguration to detect a wide array of miRNAs or other non‐coding RNAs, positioning this platform as a broadly applicable tool for molecular diagnostics beyond any specific disease context.

Introduction

miRNAs serve as gene regulation mediators by affecting the transcription and translation of the target mRNAs. Through this, they can either promote or suppress the initiation, promotion, apoptosis, and metastasis of cancer. The miRNA expression signature is disease‐ and tissue‐specific, varying at all stages of pathological conditions such as cancer. Another fascinating feature of miRNAs is their presence not only in the cytoplasm but also in serum, urine, and other biological fluids, allowing for easy extraction [1, 2]. For instance, miR‐10a, a dual‐function miRNA, is strongly associated with multiple cancers, including breast, colorectal, pancreatic, hepatocellular, gynecological, and gastrointestinal carcinomas, positioning it as a significant prognostic biomarker [3, 4, 5, 6]. The miR‐10 family has been identified as a critical regulator of oncogenic signaling pathways, a central modulator of the tumor microenvironment, and a potential therapeutic target in various human cancers [7]. Therefore, quantitative detection of miR‐10a may offer advantages for cancer prognosis and differentiation of early‐stage versus metastatic patients. The other candidate miRNA is miR‐18a, with a dual function as an oncogene or tumor suppressor [8]. miR‐18a mediates many critical biological pathways, such as proliferation, cell cycle, and apoptosis regulation [9]. Several studies have validated its dysregulation in prostate cancer [10], breast cancer, malignant melanoma [11], and colorectal cancer [12]. A meta‐analysis has shown the overexpression of miR‐18a in TNBC compared to the non‐TNBC, and suggests that this miRNA may be a promising diagnostic and therapeutic biomarker [13].

miRNAs are found intracellularly and extracellularly in the bloodstream, allowing for their detection in serum using biosensing approaches. However, their low abundance in serum poses a challenge for sensitive and reliable detection. To address this, various signal amplification strategies are widely used to enhance sensitivity, including PCR‐based methods (such as qRT‐PCR and digital PCR), isothermal amplification (e.g., rolling circle amplification, loop‐mediated isothermal amplification), and other approaches like hybridization chain reaction, exonuclease‐assisted amplification, strand displacement amplification, nanoparticle‐based amplification, surface plasmon resonance, and surface‐enhanced Raman scattering. While many of these techniques offer excellent sensitivity and specificity, some are limited in application due to requirements for specialized facilities, skilled operators, contamination‐free conditions, high costs, or complex data analysis. These limitations significantly restrict their particular use in a point‐of‐care (POC) setting [14, 15, 16].

In contrast, combining fluorescence‐based detection with rolling circle amplification (RCA) offers a powerful approach for POC‐compatible miRNA detection. RCA provides robust amplification under mild, isothermal conditions, eliminating the need for thermocycling and enabling use in portable, and low‐resource environments. Its ability to generate long, repetitive DNA products leads to substantial signal amplification, which, when coupled with fluorescence, enables highly sensitive and specific detection, often down to single‐molecule levels. Importantly, RCA systems are easily integrated with low‐cost and compact fluorescence readout devices (e.g., smartphone‐based detectors or portable fluorimeters), supporting real‐time, quantitative monitoring without the complexity of qPCR instrumentation. Additionally, the low background noise and strong multiplexing capability make RCA‐fluorescence platforms particularly suitable for detecting multiple miRNA targets in a single reaction with high accuracy. These features collectively position RCA as a superior alternative for POC diagnostics, especially when compared to qPCR and other lab‐bound amplification methods that are unsuitable for rapid, on‐site testing [17].

Previous studies have shown that detecting multiple miRNAs improves the accuracy and precision of the diagnosis [18, 19]. In this regard, we developed a modular biosensing system, based on sequential rolling circle amplification and multi‐primed chain amplification (MCA) [20], integrated with fluorescence signal readout, for multiplex miRNA detection. To demonstrate the platform's versatility, two miRNA targets were randomly selected from a broad network of deregulated miRNAs implicated in human disease pathways, without prioritizing any specific cancer type or biomarker relevance. This design choice underscores the adaptability of the system, which is not limited to any particular disease context. The proposed platform offers significant advantages, including the simultaneous, one‐pot detection of at least two miRNAs, adaptability for various non‐coding RNA targets such as long non‐coding RNAs and circular RNAs, and applicability across a range of biological fluids beyond human serum.

Methods and Materials

Chemicals and Instrumentations

Phi29 DNA polymerase, pyrophosphatase, and lambda exonuclease (5 U/μL) were purchased from New England Biolabs. T4 DNA ligase (5 U Weiss/μL), bovine serum albumin (BSA), dNTP set, 100 mM solution, exonuclease III (2000 U/μL), exonuclease I (20 U/μL), UltraPure TBE buffer 10×, TAE buffer 10×, SYBR Safe and SYBR gold (10,000× concentration in DMSO) nucleic acid gel stain, and UltraPure DNase/RNase‐Free Distilled Water were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) components, such as 40% acrylamide/bis solution, TEMED, and ammonium persulfate (APS), were purchased from Bio‐Rad. Absolute ethanol and urea were from Merck. 5′‐phosphorylated single‐stranded DNA (padlock probe), miRNAs and miRNA mimics, and molecular beacon were commercially synthesized and PAGE‐purified by IDT (Coralville, Iowa).

Amplification was done at 30°C for a specific time in a thermocycler (Eppendorf). The Invitrogen iBright Imaging Systems FL 1500 was used to image the gels. The fluorescence emission wavelength was measured using Horiba fluorescence spectroscopy (Horiba Fluoro‐Max 4 fluorometer). 6‐fluorescein (FAM) fluorescence dye was excited with the excitation and emission wavelengths of 495 nm and 517 nm. Centrifugation was done using an Eppendorf microcentrifuge at 4°C. JEOL JSM‐IT800 Schottky FESEM was used for SEM imaging of the samples. Malvern ZEN3600 Zetasizer was used for dynamic light scattering analysis (DLS) to measure the size distribution of the self‐assembled DNA in the shape of DNA nanoflowers.

Experiments

Circular DNA Template Design

A 68‐nucleotide 5′‐phosphorylated single‐stranded DNA was designed as a padlock probe. The template contains three domains in an appropriate orientation for miRNA hybridization and enzymatic amplification. The secondary predicted structure of the linear padlock probe and the RCA product of eight rounds of consecutive amplification are shown in Figure S1a,b. For proof of concept, miR‐18a (miR‐A; amplifier) and miR‐10a (miR‐D; amplifier‐detector) were randomly selected as target miRNAs for biosensing, while miR‐16‐5p (miR‐i; initiator) was used as a reference miRNA due to its consistent expression across both healthy and cancerous samples. The sequences of the miRNAs, padlock probes, and molecular beacon are listed in Table S1.

Rolling Circle Amplification and Multi‐Primed Chain Amplification

Rolling circle amplification was performed through annealing, ligation, purification, and amplification. The annealing was done by adding 0.6 μM padlock probe, 1.2 μM miR‐i (a common miRNA between normal and cancer cells that acts as an RCA initiator), and 1× T4 DNA ligase buffer into ultrapure water in a final volume of 100 μL. The mixture was heated at 90°C for 10 min, then gradually cooled to room temperature for 3 h. Afterward, 50 μL of the padlock probe–miR‐i hybrid was mixed with 2 μL PEG4000 and 3 μL T4 DNA ligase and incubated at 16°C (for 16 h) and 22°C (for 1 h) to optimize the ligation reaction and the formation of the circular probe. To purify and remove the excess miR‐i, and non‐circularized probe, the ligated products were precipitated by adding 300 μL ice‐cold absolute ethanol and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min. The DNAs precipitated at the bottom of the microtube. Throw the supernatant ethanol away and reconstitute the circular precipitated DNA in 90 μL T4 DNA ligase buffer. 2 U/μL Exonuclease I, 3 U/μL exonuclease III, and 0.5 U/μL lambda exonuclease were added to the mix and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The enzymes were inactivated at 75°C for 10 min.

These enzymatic reactions produce a pure padlock probe‐miR‐i hybrid that provides a free 3′ OH end for the phi29 DNA polymerase to amplify thousands of copies from the probe in a tandem repeat. This amplification process is called RCA. A mixture including 50 μL of probe‐miR‐i hybrid (0.3 pM), 8 μL dNTP set (2 mM), 10 μL BSA or recombinant albumin (1×), 10 μL phi29 buffer (1×), phi29 DNA polymerase (1 U/μL), 5 μL pyrophosphatase (Ppase), and ultrapure water up to a final volume of 100 μL was prepared and incubated at 30°C in a thermocycler for 4 and 1 h (R4 or R1). Afterward, the two cancer‐specific miRNAs (miR‐A and miR‐D) with a concentration range from 100 fM to 100 nM were added to the reactions. This amplification step lasts for 16 and 4 h (called multi‐primed or M16 or M4). The total reaction time would be 20 and 5 h (R4M16, R1M4). The highly viscous jelly products were kept in a 4°C refrigerator for further experiments.

Gel Electrophoresis for the Investigation of Ligation and RCA Products

The ligation efficiency at different temperatures (16°C and 22°C) was checked by running the ligated products on 12% denatured DNA urea‐PAGE. The size discrimination of the padlock probe, circularized probe, miRNAs, and the RCA product was investigated on a 1% agarose gel. The gel was stained with SYBR Gold and imaged using the Invitrogen iBright FL 1500 Imaging System.

Characterization of the RCA/MCA Products

Besides gel electrophoresis as a gold standard test to check the ligation efficiency and exonuclease treatment assay, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to investigate the surface morphology of the entangled long single‐stranded product of RCA/MCA in the form of DNA nanoflowers. The RCA product was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 150 μL of 1× PBS and centrifuged at 15,000 g for 10 min, followed by removing the supernatant. The resulting pellet was subjected to sequential ethanol washes using 40%, 60%, and 80% ethanol. After each washing step, the sample was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in absolute ethanol and deposited onto a silicon wafer. The wafer was left at room temperature overnight to dry completely and was coated with a 5 nm Au‐Pd layer. Samples were imaged using a field‐emission SEM (JEOL JSM‐IT800 Schottky FESEM) at 10 kV accelerating voltage and 25,000× magnification. Images were acquired under high vacuum at a working distance of ~16.3 mm. Finally, SEM images were analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ, NIH). Images were converted to 8‐bit grayscale, thresholded using the Otsu method, and binarized. Particle size was measured using “Analyze Particles” with a size filter of 50–100,000 nm2 and circularity range of 0.3–1.0.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to determine the hydrated radius of the entangled products. The size distribution of the nanoflowers containing the hydrogel was measured using a capillary sizing cell at a very low volume of about 3 μL using Malvern Zetasizer Ultra. Due to the product's bulky hydrogel nature, directly measuring the size distribution using DLS proved challenging. Consequently, 2 μL of the sample was diluted in 150 μL TE buffer, vortexed gently, and heated at 90°C for 10 min. After heating, the sample was cooled to room temperature before DLS analysis.

Sensitivity Assay in Buffer and Human Serum

The long products of the sequential RCA/MCA in the presence of three miRNAs self‐assembled into DNA hydrogel‐nanoflowers that can be used as a biosensor for detecting miRNAs at various concentrations. Alternative to in buffer (phi29 polymerase reaction buffer) biosensing, the practical application of the biosensor in biological samples was investigated by spiking miRNAs of various concentrations (100 fM to 100 nM) into 1% human serum. Human serum is rich in proteins that denature during long hours of amplification at 30°C. Therefore, before spiking, the human serum was treated with proteinase K and then heated at 90°C for 1 h in a water bath to inactivate the proteins that may interfere with the amplification process. To determine the biosensor's sensitivity for detecting the mentioned miRNAs, the molecular beacon was added to the reactions and incubated at room temperature.

The intensity of the 6FAM fluorescence emission was measured by fluorescence spectroscopy. Phi29 polymerase reaction buffer was used as a standard sample for fluorescence spectroscopy. The detection sensitivity of the biosensor was determined by measuring the fluorescent emission of the molecular beacon (6FAM) for different miRNA concentrations using Horiba Fluorescence Spectroscopy. The molecular beacon was designed to detect single‐stranded DNA 1 (ssDNA1, complementary to the padlock probe) and ssDNA3 (amplification products by miR‐i and miR‐D). The reporter fluorescence dye at the 5′‐end of the molecular beacon was 6FAM, and the quencher at the 3′‐end was Dabcyl (Dab) with the expected excitation at 495 nm and emission at 510–520 nm. In the experimental part, the molecular beacon with a final concentration of 100 nM was added to the reaction and incubated at room temperature overnight, and then the fluorescence emission was read.

Results and Discussion

Principle of RCA/MCA‐Based miRNA Detection Biosensor

The principle of RCA/MCA‐based signal amplification for miRNA detection is shown in Scheme 1. The detection process of miRNAs includes forming a circularized padlock probe through the hybridization of a common miR‐i and ligation assay. The circular probe was then subjected to the RCA reaction by adding phi29 DNA polymerase and other required components. The RCA amplification produces a very long single‐stranded DNA called ssDNA1. Next, cancer‐specific miRNAs were introduced to the reaction for multi‐prime chain amplification. miR‐A anneals the complementary sequence to the ssDNA1 to amplify the ssDNA2. As the nascent ssDNA2 synthesizes, miR‐D anneals and amplifies the ssDNA3 with the same sequence as ssDNA1. In free‐floating molecular beacons, the close proximity of the FAM reporter and quencher suppresses fluorescence emissions. However, upon hybridization, the beacon undergoes a conformational change to a linear structure, allowing the FAM reporter to fluoresce. The fluorescence intensity is then measured and correlated with varying concentrations of miRNAs.

Schematic representation of the sequential rolling circle amplification/multi‐primed chain amplification (RCA/MCA) for sensitively detecting cancer‐specific miR‐A and miR‐D.

Validation of the Ligation, Purification, and RCA Reaction

Hybridization of the miR‐i to the complementary sequence forms a nick between the two terminals of the padlock probe that is ligated through the function of T4 DNA ligase. Ligation assay was done at two different conditions, and the ligated products were run on 12% denatured DNA urea‐PAGE. As shown in Figure S2, both conditions produced pure circularized probes after exonuclease purification. Afterward, the sequential RCA/MCA in the presence of miR‐i (4 h) and a combination of miR‐A and miR‐D (16 h) was done at 30°C in the thermocycler. Figure S3 demonstrates the shift in the mobility of the linear padlock probe, miR‐i, circularized probe, and RCA/MCA products. The reduced mobility of the circularized probe suggests efficient hybridization between miR‐i and the probe. Additionally, the RCA/MCA products, being heavy and highly viscous, exhibit significantly hindered migration in the gel, often remaining near the well.

Characterization of the Amplification Products

Dynamic Light Scattering

Dynamic light scattering was conducted on the R1M4 products to assess the potential correlation between the size of the nanoflowers and the miRNA concentrations. Our data indicated a linear relationship between the size of the nanoflowers and the miRNA concentrations (Figure 1). The PDI was between 0.38 and 0.83 for various concentrations. The sizes and PDIs represent the average values obtained from triplicate measurements of each sample.

Correlation between nanoflower size and miRNA concentration analyzed by dynamic light scattering. Nanoflower size increases proportionally with miRNA concentration. The R 2 value indicates a strong correlation between nanoflower size and miRNA concentration.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The nanoflower morphology appeared predominantly rounded under SEM, with an average perimeter of 186.1 nm and a mean grayscale intensity of 255, suggesting solid, well‐resolved features (Figure S4). The Supporting Information presents a detailed analysis of the SEM image.

Analytical Performance of the RCA/MCA‐Based Biosensor for miRNA Detection

In Buffer

The RCA/MCA (R4M16) was done in the phi29 polymerase buffer containing various concentrations of miRNA mimics (DNA counterparts for miRNAs), and the fluorescent signals emitted at each reaction were measured through a fluorimeter following the addition of a molecular beacon. Figure 2a,b illustrate a concentration‐dependent and linear increase in fluorescent intensity corresponding to the increased concentrations of miRNA mimics, ranging from 1 pM to 100 nM, respectively. The linear regression equation for fluorescence intensity was determined as F = 343,680C–37,597 with an R 2 value of 0.8091, where F represents fluorescence intensity at 517 nm, and C denotes the concentration of miRNA mimics. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated as 5.944 pM, based on 3.3 times the standard deviation of the blank divided by the slope of the calibration curve. Similarly, the limit of quantification (LOQ) was determined to be 18.013 pM, using 10 times the standard deviation of the blank divided by the slope. In the next step, the miRNA mimics were replaced with custom miRNAs to evaluate the system's detection performance. In this new condition, the regression equation was F = 146,851C + 538,423, with an improved R 2 value of 0.982. The LOD and LOQ for this condition were 2.157 and 6.537 pM, respectively. In summary, the proposed system demonstrated the capability to detect both miRNAs at concentrations below 10 pM, closely aligning with the precise quantification of miRNAs in buffer. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

(a) Fluorescence signal of the RCA/MCA biosensor (R4M16) in the presence of different concentrations of miRNA mimics in buffer. (b) The fluorescence signal of the RCA/MCA biosensor in the presence of custom miRNAs in buffer. nM, nanomolar; pM, picomolar.

In Real Sample (Human Serum)

The human serum (HS) was treated with heat and proteinase K before being added to the reaction. 1% HS [21] was incubated at 56°C for 30 min in the water bath, and then 0.5 U/mL proteinase K was added to the tube. The reaction was incubated at 37°C for 60 min, and the enzyme was inactivated at 95°C for 10 min and cooled to room temperature in the lab environment. The treated HS was spiked with varying concentrations of cancer‐specific miRNAs, followed by amplification of R1M4. Subsequently, the fluorescence emission of the molecular beacon was measured. As shown in Figure 3a, the fluorescence intensity increased gradually as the concentration of miRNAs increased from 100 fM to 100 nM. The regression equation of the samples in the standard reaction buffer was F = 141,191C + 815,499 (R 2 = 0.852) (Figure 3b). The LOD and LOQ were 6.561 and 19.883 pM, respectively. The linear regression equation for samples in HS was F = 125,997C + 982,410 (R 2 = 0.9773). The LOD and LOQ were 2.397 and 7.266 pM, respectively. Accordingly, the lowest detectable quantity of miRNAs in human serum would be about 2 pM, while it can measure the miRNAs at a precise concentration of about 7 pM.

(b) The fluorescence signal of the RCA/MCA biosensor in different concentrations of custom miRNAs in human serum. The error bars represent the standard deviation of three repeated experiments. fM, femtomolar; nM, nanomolar; pM, picomolar.

Recent advances in nucleic acid diagnostics have spurred the development of innovative platforms for miRNA detection in diverse sample types, including buffers, biological fluids, and living cells. However, many of these methods either require extensive instrumentation or target‐specific optimization, limiting their scalability and utility for point‐of‐care (POC) applications. Sensitivity across existing platforms varies widely, from nanomolar to femtomolar levels, depending on the strategy employed and the complexity of the assay [22, 23, 24]. In this study, we present a modular, isothermal nucleic acid detection platform for miRNAs, designed with adaptability, simplicity, and scalability in mind. The system integrates rolling circle amplification (RCA) and multi‐primed chain amplification (MCA), combined with a molecular beacon‐based fluorescence readout. This architecture enables sensitive detection of multiple miRNA targets, with detection limits in the low picomolar range. Importantly, the assay maintained performance in both buffer and biologically relevant conditions (e.g., human serum), confirming its robustness.

While the selected miRNAs in this study—miR‐18a and miR‐10a—are known cancer‐related biomarkers, they were not prioritized for their diagnostic relevance but rather used as model targets to evaluate the platform's functionality and resilience. This strategy allowed us to benchmark sensitivity and adaptability without being constrained by disease‐specific biomarker validation. Compared to other isothermal and amplification‐based miRNA detection strategies, our platform offers a compelling balance between sensitivity and operational simplicity [21, 25, 26]. For example, Mo et al. reported a dual‐fluorophore DNA tetrahedron platform capable of detecting miRNAs with low picomolar sensitivity [27], while other studies have demonstrated femtomolar detection using complex, multi‐step protocols involving reverse transcription and fluorescent nanoparticle conjugation [28]. These approaches, although highly sensitive, often involve specialized reagents or instrumentation, making them less suitable for decentralized or POC settings.

Our design prioritizes POC suitability through a minimalistic, all‐DNA reaction network that requires no thermal cycling or enzymatic reverse transcription. Furthermore, its modular design enables straightforward adaptation to other nucleic acid targets, including long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), or even pathogen‐derived RNA sequences. The use of padlock probes for target recognition provides inherent flexibility, as multiple probes can be introduced for parallel detection in a single assay.

To further improve sensitivity while preserving POC compatibility, future directions may involve the integration of brighter or quencher‐free probes, nanoparticle‐assisted signal amplification, or improving the reaction kinetics. Additionally, incorporating simple target pre‐concentration techniques such as magnetic bead capture or paper‐based sample enrichment may increase effective target input without adding technical complexity. These enhancements would help address the detection of ultra‐low abundance.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a modular, fluorescence‐based biosensing platform for the detection of microRNAs by integrating rolling circle amplification (RCA) and multi‐primed chain amplification (MCA) with molecular beacon‐based readout. The system demonstrated high sensitivity, achieving detection limits in the low picomolar range, and retained robust performance in biologically relevant media such as human serum. Designed with point‐of‐care (POC) applications in mind, the platform's isothermal operation, modular probe architecture, and adaptability to various miRNA targets underscore its versatility and usability in clinical and resource‐limited settings. While the platform already shows strong analytical performance, future improvements may focus on enhancing specificity and further minimizing non‐specific hybridization. These refinements will support broader applicability across diverse biological samples and more complex target panels. Overall, this study establishes a strong foundation for the continued development of sensitive, portable, and scalable miRNA detection technologies.
