Indirect Immunofluorescence-Aided Detection of Antimyelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein Antibodies in Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder/Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein-Associated Antibody Disorder: A Case Series Involving Five Patients
Authors: Debamita Datta, Mala Mahto, Upasna Sinha, Sushil Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Autoantibodies are the cause of the chronic inflammatory diseases known as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD). Serum antibodies (Abs) that specifically target the aquaporin-4 (AQP-4) water channel are the cause of recurrent episodes of optic neuritis, myelitis, and/or brain stem disorders. In contrast to AQP-4 Abs, myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) Abs are detected in some patients exhibiting nonmotor cognitive impairment. These days, the term “MOG-encephalomyelitis” (MOG-EM) is frequently used to describe these clinical syndromes. The diagnosis of these cases involves the use of magnetic resonance imaging, optical coherence tomography, antibody detection, and additional laboratory testing. By detecting the patient’s Abs in their serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) aids in the proper diagnosis. We highlight five NMOSD cases where serum anti-MOG antibody positivity was found using IIF, but CSF was not. In none of the cases, anti-AQP Abs were found. Effective patient management strategies include the treatment of acute attacks and long-term immunosuppressive drugs such as rituximab, azathioprine, and immunoglobulins. IIF is a quick and easy tool to detect anti-MOG Abs in patients with NMOSD/myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disorder. CSF testing for MOG or AQP-4 Abs is not usually advised. It does not offer additional benefits to help with MOG-EM or NMOSD diagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

Unlike multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a rare chronic inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS). The term “neuromyelitis optica” was first used in 1894 by Devic.[1] The majority of patients with this disease have autoantibodies directed against the astrocyte aquaporin-4 (AQP4) water channel, and they frequently experience optic neuritis (ON) or myelitis attacks repeatedly.[12] In rare cases, symptoms such as diencephalic syndrome or region postrema syndrome could indicate brain involvement. A few patients have also reported experiencing headaches, exhaustion, depression, pain, and difficulty falling asleep. AQP4-opposing antibodies (Abs) bind to astrocyte water channels. Depending on the assay, AQP-4 auto-Abs are undetectable in 20%–30% of patients.[3] It is still debatable whether AQP-4 autoantibody-positive or -negative disorders represent different diseases or are variants of the same illness. Antimyelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) was later identified.[34] The glycoprotein known as MOG is present on the surface of cell bodies, oligodendrocyte processes, and the myelin sheath. Abs against AQP-4 and MOG have the capacity to compromise the integrity of the blood–brain barrier and induce inflammation of the CNS. More and more people now consider MOG antibody sickness to be a separate medical condition.[4] Numerous terms are used to describe the illness, such as MOG antibody-related disorder, MOG-associated sickness, and MOG-encephalomyelitis (MOG-EM).[5] Among NMOSD and MOG-EM, longitudinal extensive transverse myelitis (LETM) and ON are the most frequently reported symptoms. The brain stem could be involved. Narcolepsy, sudden diencephalic syndrome, muscle affection, hiccups, nausea, and vomiting are some of the unusual clinical signs of NMOSD.[67] On a radiological image, the presence of MOG-autoantibody positive may suggest extraneural involvement, like compressive paraspinal muscular hyperintensity. In 2015, there were noticeable similarities between the disease presentations of patients with and without AQP-4 Abs.[8] Among patients who test positive for AQP-4, 37%–54% have ON, and 30%–47% have LETM. 18%–33% of patients with MOG-antibody had myelitis, whereas 33%–64% of patients had ON as their first clinical manifestation.[8] In contrast to the less frequent NMOSD with myelitis, ON seems to be the most common symptom in MOG-EM despite the disease’s ongoing progression.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

A 22-year-old man was admitted to the neurology outpatient department complaining of weakness in both the upper and lower limbs, an occipital headache, and decreased sensation in half of his face. Laboratory cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis eliminated the possibility of an infectious cause. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reveals plaque-like T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in the left middle cerebellar peduncle and right ventral midbrain, which are most likely indicative of demyelinating disease. Electrophoresis and indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) were applied to CSF and serum samples. Serum Abs against MOG were detected by IIF, whereas Abs against AQP-4 were not detected [Figure 1]. Conversely, the CSF sample’s IIF for the AQP-4 and MOG Abs was found to be negative. CSF electrophoresis failed to show any oligoclonal bands. Rituximab therapy was initiated for the patient, with a single dose of 1 g every 15 days for 6 months.

Case 2

A female child, 8 years old, was brought into the ophthalmology department complaining of an abrupt loss of vision in her left eye. When the patient noticed a sudden loss of vision in her left eye 4 days ago, she appeared to be in good health. One vomiting episode, a headache accompanied by urine retention, and a noticeable recent weight loss occurred before this. Retinal nerve fiber layer edema, disc hyperemia, tortuosity, and blurred disc margins were all present in the left eye, which had a Grade 1 relative afferent pupillary defect. Her diagnosis was acute demyelinating encephalomyelitis, a spectrum of nonmotor neurodevelopmental disorders primarily affecting children and young adults. Multifocal patchy areas of T2/FLAIR hyperintensity involving subcortical and deep white matter of both cerebral hemispheres were seen on an orbit contrast MRI of the brain, with left ON indicating acute demyelination of the brain. The serum’s indirect immune fluorescence test for NMOSD indicated a positive result for the anti-MOG antibody. The patient received prednisolone for 3 months before being told to taper off.

Case 3

A 15-year-old girl was admitted to our hospital’s neurology department complaining of lower limb stiffness, muscle weakness, and a history of abrupt visual loss. She had been complaining of lower limb tightness for the past 15 days. For the previous year, this has been linked to a burning-like sensation in the lower limbs. Following admission, the patient underwent a thorough workup that included brain/spine MRI [Figure 2], CSF investigations, IIF testing for NMOSD, and CSF electrophoresis. In addition, visual evoked potential was used. A serum sample’s IIF was found to be positive for the MOG antibody. The patient received oral azathioprine 40 mg for 90 days, tablets of gabapentin and nortriptyline for 90 days, and a tablet of prednisolone 20 mg once daily for 7 days, followed by a tablet of prednisolone 10 mg once daily as part of their treatment.

Case 4

A 14-year-old boy was admitted with complaints of a 2-day fever, a 1-day bilateral lower limb weakness, a 1-day incapacity to sit and stand, and a 1-day urine retention. When the patient’s fever started 2 days ago, he appeared to be in good health, with the absence of rash, chills, and rigor in the past. There was a corresponding slurring of the tongue. Following catheterization, the patient experienced a decrease in abdominal swelling and urine retention. He experienced two episodes of nonprojectile vomiting that included food particles. There was no history of previous contact with any known case of tuberculosis. Every standard investigation was carried out. MRI findings are depicted in Figure 3. Prednisolone tablets (15 mg) were recommended for the patient, who was then tapered down to 10 mg daily.

T2 weighted sagittal MRI spine shows multiple short segments T2 hyperintense lesions (white arrows) in the cervical and dorsal spinal cord with cord expansion (red arrow)

Case 5

An 11-year-old girl complained of gradually and painfully losing her right eye’s vision for 5 days. It progressed gradually, starting with blurred vision and ending with the inability to count fingers on one hand. Eventually, the right eye experienced total loss of vision. The left eye did not exhibit any comparable complaints. Paraplegia and transverse myelitis were the patient’s diagnoses. No prior history of fever, tuberculosis contact, or unusual body movement was present. The patient was started on a 40 mg tablet of prednisolone for 5 days, then switched to a 30 mg tablet with the intention of adding an azathioprine tablet in the long run.

DISCUSSION

Devic’s disease is an autoimmune disorder also referred to as NMOSD.[9] It is characterized by chronic inflammation and demyelination of the CNS, and it typically affects the spinal cord and optic nerves. The etiology of NMOSD is still unknown. Despite this, the discovery of two auto-Abs, anti-MOG and anti-AQP-4, has improved our understanding of etiopathogenesis.[10] Despite the fact that Lenon et al., in 2004, used mouse brain and kidney substrates to identify an NMOSD-specific biomarker in the form of anti-immunoglobulin (Ig) G Abs directed against AQP-4, NMOSD is commonly misdiagnosed as multiple sclerosis. However, a recent study found that some NMOSD patients had negative anti-AQP-4 Abs but positive anti-MOG Abs. This provided information on a specific illness called “MOG-EM.”[1011] The most effective method for detecting the particular antibody in serum, which is essential for diagnosing NMOSD and MOG-EM, is cell-based assays (CBAs).[12] These assays in NMOSD have a sensitivity of 80%–100% and a specificity of 86%–100% for anti-AQP-4 Abs. It is not advised to use enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) as the only technique because it could produce falsely positive results.[12] Early in the 1990s, MOG-antibody was first observed in MS patients.[12] Later, an AQP-4 antibody-negative patient with clinical signs of NMOSD was discovered to have MOG-antibody. Nowadays, a standard technique for detecting MOG-antibody is CBA, similar to NMOSD. AQP-4 antibody and anti-MOG antibody levels can fluctuate as the illness progresses.[13] Further antibody testing may be necessary during the disease in patients with suspected NMOSD or MOG-EM who do not initially show signs of seropositivity, particularly during acute attacks and periods without treatment. Although the titer may be lower with immunosuppression and increase during acute attacks, AQP-4 auto-Abs are still detectable during remission.[13] About 80% of the MOG-EM patients who had MOG-Abs during the acute attack continued to be seropositive throughout the remission. Only 50% of patients continued to be seropositive years later, which is a lower rate.[14] CSF testing for MOG or AQP-4 Abs is not usually advised. It does not offer any more details to help with MOG-EM or NMOSD diagnosis. Only 70% of patients with seropositivity and none of the patients with seronegative status had AQP-4 Abs found in their CSF. Similar to AQP-4 Abs, MOG Abs are only generated extrathecally, which makes them less common in the CSF.[1415] In patients with NMOSD, comorbidity with other autoimmune disorders is typically observed. As a result, testing for rheumatologic conditions such as antinuclear antibody, antineutrophilic cytoplasmic antibody, and anti-dsDNA should also be carried out. The International Panel for Diagnosis of NMOSD released the current NMOSD diagnostic criteria in 2015.[15] They aid in distinguishing between NMOSD patients who have anti-AQP-4 auto-Abs and those who do not. In patients without AQP4-Ab, two of the aforementioned symptoms are required for the diagnosis of NMOSD. One or more of these crucial clinical characteristics – ON, LETM, or area postrema syndrome – must be present. In addition, necessary are supportive features in MRIs of the brain, spinal cord, or optic nerve. The serostatus of AQP-4 and MOG-Abs in serum and CSF were the basis for all previous laboratory diagnoses of NMOSD and MOG-EM. Because serum samples are sufficient to provide the necessary information and sampling is invasive, it is generally not recommended to diagnose NMO with CSF. Because of their high sensitivity (mean sensitivity of 76.7%) and specificity (100%) in numerous multicenter and single-center studies, CBAs are considered the gold standard in the diagnosis of NMOSD.[15] In a thorough investigation carried out in 2015, the International Panel for NMO Diagnosis (IPND) used an AQP-4 CBA as a diagnostic standard for NMSOD. In the absence of CBAs, the IPND has also approved the use of ELISAs and IIF assays (mean sensitivity of 64%) with a warning about low-titer values. Many diagnostic techniques, such as ELISA, fluorescence immunoprecipitation assay, radioimmunoprecipitation assay, fluorescence-activated cell sorting, and immunofluorescence CBAs, have been standardized since the discovery of AQP-4 in 2004 using IIF staining based on tissues.[15] However, their specificity and sensitivity are unknown. Protein conformations, serum dilution, isoforms, and posttranslational modifications are common obstacles in the development of a diagnostic procedure. The first-generation diagnostic method for the differential diagnosis of NMOSD was the tissue-based IIF method, which used frozen brain tissue sections from rats, mice, and nonhuman primates.[16] It was considered the gold standard at first, particularly for monkey cerebellums. Tissue-based IIF assays combined with ELISA and CBA are only appropriate for screening; confirmatory assays should not be performed using them, per multicenter comparison studies.[1617] Because of the potential for erroneous results, using ELISAs for anti-MOG Abs in clinical settings is not recommended. In ELISAs and other protein-based assays, unfolded or partially folded proteins – which are not intact – are commonly utilized as the antigenic substrate. Because CBAs reliably and more effectively detect AQP-4 Abs, researchers developed them for the detection of anti-MOG Abs. Furthermore, the success of CBAs in neuroimmunology has enabled the important discovery that native protein is necessary for increasing test sensitivity for identifying NMO IgG.[18] Since CBAs are considered the gold standard, they are recommended in the diagnostic interpretation of NMOSD. In a CBA, transfected cells overexpress a specific target protein that serves as the substrate. After patient serum is added to the fixed cells, unbound Abs are washed out, and fluorescently tagged secondary Abs are used to detect AQP-4 and NMO Abs. Even though recombinant assays are more sensitive than native assays, there are a number of disadvantages to CBAs, such as the requirement for a fluorescence microscope, the maintenance of cell lines, the use of freshly transfected cells, variations in the quality of expressed epitopes, and – above all – the potential that diagnostic laboratories lack the equipment needed to perform a recombinant technique. In 2010, Jarius et al. created a highly sensitive recombinant IFA to circumvent some of these limitations.[18] Using this method, a sizable-batch HEK293 cells, which are mainly transfected cells, were grown on cover slips and kept cold in liquid nitrogen. As needed, the biochips were subsequently adhered to tiny slides. They demonstrated that the reproducibility of the results was unaffected by storing the slides, reporting a sensitivity of 78.1% and a specificity of 100%.[1819] Two young males and three females were part of our study. They all presented with neurological symptoms or ophthalmological involvement. An infectious etiology was ruled out in each case. IIF testing for anti-MOG Abs was positive in all patients, but anti-AQP-4 antibody results in serum and CSF were negative. While Cases 1, 2, and 5 were previous cases who resurfaced with acute episodes, Cases 3 and 4 received a new diagnosis of NMOSD. An assay based on IIF produced by Euroimmun (Germany) was used to identify these Abs. The patient’s sample is diluted (1:10) and incubated with cells that express MOG and AQP4. Certain Abs from the classes IgA, IgG, and IgM attach to the antigens if a positive reaction is seen. The linked Abs are next stained with antihuman Abs that have been fluorescein-labeled, making them visible under a fluorescence microscope. When AQP-4 or MOG antibody levels in the serum are either positive or negative, no reaction is seen in the control well. IIF is a useful tool for quickly identifying anti-MOG Abs in NMOSD. Initially, it was believed that NMOSD was less common than MOG-EM/myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disorder (MOGAD) in children with inflammatory demyelinating disease (IDD) and more common than MOG-EM/MOGAD in adult patients with IDD. Recent research, however, indicates that in both Asian and non-Asian adult populations, cases of MOG-EM/MOGAD may outnumber both anti-AQP4-IgG antibody-positive and -negative NMOSD. The recent increase in knowledge about MOG-EM/MOGAD and the growing accessibility of laboratory tests for the detection of anti-MOG IgG may be the causes of this shift.[20]
