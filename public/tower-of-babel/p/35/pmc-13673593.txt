Open science practices and feedback-based pedagogy in nanomedicine education: a moderated mediation model of glioma treatment competency
Authors: Chengqiong Mao, Yang Liu, Chao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNanomedicine education requires interdisciplinary integration that conventional didactic instruction inadequately develops, particularly in glioma therapeutics. Open science practices and feedback-based pedagogy offer complementary solutions, yet their synergistic effects remain empirically unexplored. This study aimed to validate an integrated instructional framework to enhance knowledge mastery and innovation capacity.MethodsA two-arm parallel-group randomized controlled trial was conducted at six Chinese medical colleges from October to December 2023. Three hundred forty-two clinical medicine graduate students were randomly assigned to either an experimental group (feedback-based pedagogy combined with open science platform engagement, sample size 171) or a control group (traditional didactic instruction, sample size 171) for 12 weeks. Knowledge mastery was measured using a 20-item standardized test (range 0-140), and innovation capacity was assessed using an adapted 28-item Abbreviated Torrance Test for Adults. A moderated mediation model with bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations) examined the mediating role of knowledge mastery and the moderating role of open science engagement.ResultsFeedback-based pedagogy significantly improved knowledge mastery (standardized coefficient = 0.28, p < 0.001) and innovation capacity. Open science practice significantly amplified these effects, with a positive interaction between the two strategies (standardized coefficient = 0.15, p = 0.003). Knowledge mastery mediated 38.2% of the effect of feedback-based pedagogy on innovation capacity. Open science engagement significantly moderated this indirect pathway, amplifying the mediated effect by 58% under high-engagement conditions. Subgroup analyses confirmed the stability of these effects across institutional tiers and specialties. Additional objective artifact scoring showed higher blinded behavioral-output scores in the experimental group across decision quality, design feasibility, reproducible protocol/code quality, and translational rationale. Leave-one-institution-out validation confirmed that the main effect, interaction effect, and conditional indirect pathway remained directionally stable across all six institutional exclusions.ConclusionsIntegrating open science practices with feedback-based pedagogy creates a synergistic instructional paradigm that enhances nanomedicine education outcomes. Knowledge mastery serves as the critical transformation mechanism linking structured instruction to innovative capacity development. These findings provide an empirically grounded framework for glioma therapeutics education, with hypothesized applicability to other complex medical domains sharing specified boundary conditions (high interdisciplinary integration, open digital infrastructure, and simulation-based learning contexts). Boundary statement: Because this randomized trial was conducted over 12 weeks within a single national educational system and used proximal simulated/domain-adapted innovation assessments rather than downstream clinical behavioral outcomes, the findings should be interpreted as bounded evidence requiring longitudinal, cross-national replication with objective endpoints such as supervised clinical practice records, decision-quality ratings, peer-reviewed outputs, protocol contributions, and patent applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09948-4.

Introduction

Nanomedicine has transformed disease diagnosis and therapy [1], yet conventional medical education remains anchored in didactic, discipline-bound pedagogies that inadequately prepare practitioners for its interdisciplinary complexity [2, 3]. This pedagogical lag is particularly acute in high-stakes domains such as glioma therapeutics, where clinicians must synthesize molecular biology, materials science, pharmacokinetics, and clinical oncology to devise patient-specific treatment strategies [4]—competencies that traditional lecture-based instruction fails to develop [5]. Three barriers impede effective education: disciplinary knowledge fragmentation, delayed feedback preventing real-time correction, and closed environments isolating students from global advances [6, 7].

Open science initiatives—emphasizing research transparency, data accessibility, and collaborative dissemination—offer a transformative framework for dismantling these barriers [8, 9]. Concurrently, feedback-based teaching, characterized by dynamic instructional adjustments responsive to learning trajectories, addresses the critical need for real-time scaffolding in complex knowledge domains [10]. Despite their theoretical promise, the synergistic integration of these approaches within nanomedicine education remains empirically underexplored, particularly regarding their differential effectiveness across learner populations and institutional contexts. This study addresses this evidence gap by developing and validating a theoretically grounded, empirically testable model of how open science participation and feedback intensity interact to enhance knowledge mastery and innovation capacity in glioma therapeutics education.

This study developed a theoretical framework integrating open science practices with reflective pedagogy, and empirically validate its impact on medical education outcomes [11]. Employing rigorous experimental methodologies, we quantitatively elucidated the mechanisms by which reflective teaching facilitates nanomedicine knowledge acquisition, alongside evaluating the efficacy of open science environments in fostering students’ innovative competencies. The anticipated findings will furnish empirical evidence to inform medical education reform, catalyzing the paradigm shift from closed, didactic instruction to open, interactive pedagogical models [12, 13].

This research advances medical education through three integrated contributions:

Theoretical Integration: This study integrates open science practice with feedback-based pedagogy within a nanomedicine education framework, proposing a synergistic “feedback-open science” instructional paradigm that transcends the limitations of conventional single-strategy approaches.Mechanistic Pathway: It develops and empirically validates a moderated mediation model that identifies knowledge mastery as the critical transformation mechanism between feedback-based pedagogy and innovation capacity, with open science engagement significantly amplifying this indirect pathway (58% effect amplification).Empirical Generalizability: Through a randomized controlled trial, it quantifies the synergistic interaction effect of the dual instructional strategies (β = 0.15, p = 0.003), and establishes a domain-similarity-based transferability framework (Tier 1–3) to provide a replicable evidence template for precision educational reform across complex medical domains.

Literature review

Educational applications of open science practice

Open science practices are fundamentally transforming contemporary educational ecosystems [14], characterized by hallmark features including transparent research data sharing, procedural transparency, and the globalization of scientific collaboration [15, 16]. Open science platforms facilitate the integration of high-quality educational resources across geopolitical boundaries, enabling medical students to access cutting-edge research findings and clinical cases, thereby substantially expanding the parameters of learning environments. For instance, in nanomedicine education, learners can engage with frontier research and development data through open-access repositories or participate in transnational virtual laboratory collaborations. Such open learning environments not only democratize knowledge accessibility but also cultivate students’ international perspectives and collaborative competencies. Concurrently, open science practices emphasize procedural transparency, requiring comprehensive documentation of experimental data and cognitive processes throughout the learning trajectory [17]. This imperative aligns closely with the principles of intellectual rigor and research integrity fundamental to medical education, thereby fostering scientific reasoning and academic honesty among learners [18, 19]. Through the establishment of knowledge-sharing incentive mechanisms—such as open-access publishing and open-source tool development—open science practices effectively ameliorate resource asymmetries endemic to traditional medical education, ensuring equitable access to high-quality educational opportunities for students in remote or resource-constrained institutions. Open science practices thus drive a shift from closed instruction to open, interactive models, cultivating innovative talents for future medical challenges [20, 21]. The generalizability of this pedagogical transformation across nanomedicine subspecialties depends, however, on domain-specific characteristics including disease complexity profiles, open data infrastructure maturity, and the feasibility of simulation-based iterative learning—factors that warrant systematic investigation as the field advances [22].

Mechanisms of feedback-based pedagogy

Feedback-based pedagogy optimizes cognitive scaffolding through timely, diagnostically accurate performance feedback [23]. As depicted in Fig. 1, within medical education contexts, this mechanism manifests initially during the knowledge acquisition phase. Educators or intelligent tutoring systems conduct real-time diagnostic assessments of learners’ performance through formative evaluation instruments, providing tailored instructional scaffolding to address conceptual misunderstandings in complex domains such as nanomedicine, thereby effectively compressing the knowledge internalization trajectory. Second, regarding skill development, systematic feedback protocols facilitate the establishment of canonical clinical reasoning frameworks. Particularly in therapeutic decision-making training for glioma treatment, multisource feedback architectures simulate expert consultation modalities characteristic of authentic clinical environments, enabling students to progressively master diagnostic and therapeutic essentials through iterative refinement [24–26]. Most profoundly, these mechanisms influence the cultivation of metacognitive awareness. Iterative feedback cycles encourage learners to actively monitor their cognitive states, developing acute sensitivity to knowledge gaps—a self-regulatory capacity crucial for fostering innovative thinking [27]. From a neurobiological perspective, feedback information not only strengthens mnemonic encoding of accurate knowledge but also promotes neuroplasticity and cognitive adaptability through the activation of mesolimbic reward pathways and error-monitoring neural systems. This provides empirical biological grounding for understanding how feedback-based pedagogy enhances innovative capacities. The efficacy of feedback depends critically upon its timeliness, specificity, and operational utility; excessively vague or delayed feedback may produce deleterious effects. In nanomedicine education, intelligent feedback systems integrated with learning analytics technologies enable dynamic personalization of learning trajectories, representing a significant vector for future medical education development [28, 29].

Interplay between knowledge and innovation capacity in glioma therapeutics

The intrinsic complexity of glioma therapeutics necessitates an intricate synergistic interplay between domain knowledge systems and innovative capacities [30]. From an epistemological perspective, glioma treatment encompasses multidimensional knowledge architectures spanning molecular biology, nanomedicine, diagnostic imaging, and clinical therapeutics. This multidimensional epistemological architecture requires medical students not merely to master foundational theories but to demonstrate sophisticated capabilities in synthesizing disparate knowledge domains—a synthetic process constituting a fundamental manifestation of innovative cognition [31]. In clinical practice, tumor heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance frequently constrain the efficacy of standardized therapeutic protocols, necessitating that clinicians innovate personalized treatment strategies based upon robust domain expertise and individual patient characteristics. From a cognitive science perspective, when learners’ knowledge reserves in glioma therapeutics achieve critical threshold levels, this knowledge consolidates into highly structured cognitive schemas within long-term memory systems. These schemas function not merely as cognitive repositories for innovation but actively facilitate the emergence of novel solutions through analogical transfer mechanisms and cross-domain mapping [32]. However, innovative capacity does not emerge spontaneously from knowledge accumulation; rather, it necessitates specific pedagogical interventions to effect transformative processes. Integrating glioma therapeutic knowledge with ill-structured problem-solving training within nanomedicine curricula significantly enhances students’ capacities to translate knowledge into innovative solutions. This transformative mechanism operates through three distinct pathways: deep domain expertise enables precise identification of critical clinical challenges; cross-domain knowledge integration facilitates transcendence of conventional therapeutic paradigms; and iterative feedback cycles refine novel intervention strategies [33, 34]. Consequently, in medical education design, knowledge transmission and innovative capacity cultivation should be conceptualized as interdependent constructs rather than discrete objectives.

Research Hypothesis

H1: the positive impact of open science practice on knowledge acquisition

Drawing upon cutting-edge research regarding open science applications in educational contexts, extant evidence demonstrates that data-sharing mechanisms and collaborative learning models significantly enhance learners’ epistemic gains and innovative thinking capacities. Open science platforms provide real-time access to global research resources and interdisciplinary discussion environments, enabling learners to transcend the spatiotemporal constraints characteristic of traditional medical education and access contemporary academic advances and clinical cases. Particularly within nanomedicine education, these open, collaborative features facilitate deeper comprehension of the complex mechanisms underlying glioma therapeutics. Therefore, this study proposes H1: In nanomedicine education, the level of engagement with open science practices is significantly positively associated with students’ mastery of glioma treatment knowledge. Furthermore, this relationship is positively moderated by feedback intensity; specifically, under conditions of high-intensity feedback-based instruction, the facilitative effect of open science practices on knowledge acquisition becomes more pronounced.

H2: Enhancement effects of feedback-based pedagogy on knowledge acquisition

Substantial empirical evidence regarding feedback-based instruction in medical education demonstrates that timely, diagnostically-informed pedagogical feedback effectively promotes comprehension of complex medical concepts and improves clinical decision-making capacities. The feedback mechanism facilitates the establishment of systematic, structured professional knowledge systems through a tripartite mechanism involving multidimensional evaluation, erroneous cognition correction, and accurate knowledge consolidation. When addressing highly complex medical challenges such as glioma therapeutics, multisource feedback systems simulate authentic clinical consultation modalities, providing professional guidance from multiple disciplinary perspectives. Therefore, this study proposes H2: feedback-based pedagogy interventions significantly enhance students’ mastery of glioma treatment knowledge, independent of open science engagement.

H3: mediating function of knowledge mastery

Grounded in theoretical frameworks concerning knowledge transformation and innovative capacity development, research indicates that deep conceptual mastery of professional domains constitutes a crucial foundation for innovative thinking emergence. However, knowledge accumulation alone does not guarantee innovation capacity improvement. The innovation process requires learners to demonstrate capacities for restructuring domain-specific knowledge, transferring expertise across disciplinary boundaries, and applying knowledge to novel contexts. This transformative process typically requires support from open learning environments and multidimensional cognitive training. In nanomedicine education, systematic mastery of glioma therapeutic knowledge provides the necessary cognitive foundation for innovation. Therefore, this study proposes H3: Knowledge mastery in glioma therapeutics functions as a critical mediator between feedback-based pedagogy and innovation capacity enhancement. Open science engagement moderates this mediational pathway, with stronger indirect effects anticipated under high-engagement conditions.

Research methods

Sample selection and data

This study employed a two-arm parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) design to evaluate the effects of an integrated feedback-based and open science-enhanced pedagogical intervention on glioma treatment knowledge mastery and innovation capability among clinical medicine graduate students. The study was conducted across six medical colleges in China from October to December 2023. Data collection was administered via Wenjuanxing (http://www.wjx.cn), a professional online survey platform widely utilized in Chinese academic research, which enabled standardized electronic questionnaire delivery, automated quality checks, and secure data storage. Participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (feedback-based pedagogy + open science platform integration, n = 171) or the control group (traditional didactic instruction, n = 171). The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines were followed for design, analysis, and reporting.

We utilized stratified random sampling to ensure representativeness across institutional tiers (Double First Class vs. non-Double First Class universities) and specialty orientations (oncology, neurosurgery, radiotherapy). Within each stratum, 60 students were systematically selected, yielding a target sample of 360 participants (6 institutions × 60 students). Inclusion criteria were: (1) completion of foundational medical curricula; (2) current enrollment in oncology-related courses; (3) no prior formal training in nanomedicine or glioma therapeutics; and (4) provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) participation in other concurrent educational interventions; (2) incomplete baseline assessment.

Eligible participants were randomly assigned using R software to generate a computer-based randomization sequence (1:1 allocation ratio), stratified by institution and specialty. Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes opened only after baseline assessment completion. Due to the nature of the pedagogical intervention, participants and instructors could not be blinded to group assignment; however, outcome assessors (those scoring knowledge tests and coding innovation responses) were blinded to group allocation.

Of 360 targeted participants, 342 completed all study procedures (response rate: 95.0%), with 171 per group. Eighteen participants (5.0%) were excluded due to incomplete baseline questionnaires (n = 8), withdrawal (n = 6), or missing post-test data (n = 4). All participants were clinical medicine graduate students (year of study: first-year n = 125, second-year n = 112, third-year n = 105). No significant differences in demographic characteristics were observed between completers and dropouts (all p > 0.20). The experimental intervention integrated two core components over 12 weeks (one academic semester):

Component 1: Feedback-Based Pedagogy System. The intervention delivered structured, diagnostic feedback through three weekly channels with specified temporal parameters: (a) Immediate automated feedback—following each Monday knowledge quiz (20 items, 30-minute duration), the Wenjuanxing platform generated individualized error analysis reports within 6 h, identifying specific misconception patterns (e.g., blood-brain barrier transport mechanism confusion) and directing learners to targeted remedial modules; (b) Peer feedback—Wednesday case analysis assignments (500-word structured reports on assigned glioma clinical scenarios) were posted to moderated discussion forums, with each participant required to provide substantive commentary on two peers’ submissions using a faculty-provided rubric emphasizing diagnostic reasoning chains (feedback due within 48 h); (c) Expert feedback—Friday innovation challenge submissions (proposed nanocarrier design for specified glioma subtype) received detailed written commentary (minimum 300 words) from a panel of three faculty members (clinical oncology, pharmaceutical science, neurosurgery) within 72 h, supplemented by 15-minute individual video consultations scheduled biweekly. Feedback intensity (FDB) was computed as the mean of weekly frequency scores (standardized 0–10 scale) and mean quality rating (1–7 scale), yielding a composite 0-8.5 metric, then linearly transformed to a 1–7 scale for metric consistency with other Likert variables.Component 2: Open Science Platform Integration. Participants engaged with four structured activity types over the 12-week period, with minimum participation thresholds: (a) Open data utilization—weekly download and analysis of TCGA-GBM or GLIOMA-Open datasets (minimum 2 h/week), with completion verified through platform log timestamps; (b) Collaborative analysis—biweekly GitHub repository contributions to group coding projects (Python/R-based survival analysis or molecular subtyping), with commit history and peer review records extracted via API; (c) Preprint journal clubs—fortnightly synchronous online sessions (90 min) discussing bioRxiv/medRxiv articles on glioma nanomedicine, with participation logs and discussion quality ratings by moderators; (d) Structured contribution—monthly submission of either data documentation (compliant with FORCE11 standards) or experimental protocol sharing (via OSF), evaluated for completeness by two independent raters (inter-rater agreement κ = 0.84). Platform engagement (OSP) was computed as the mean of 15 items across three dimensions (open data utilization, collaborative platform participation, knowledge contribution and transparency), each measured on 7-point Likert scales.

Outcome-scope clarification

The innovation outcomes were intentionally defined as proximal educational indicators within a 12-week course. This trial did not collect downstream objective clinical behavior after graduation; therefore, the ATTA-R and simulation-based design tasks are interpreted as evidence of short-term educational competency development rather than evidence of long-term clinical transfer.

Secondary validation design

To enrich the empirical evidence and reduce dependence on self-reported innovation scales, two additional prespecified validation experiments were conducted using the same randomized sample. First, blinded faculty raters evaluated objective behavioral artifacts generated during the final course challenge, including simulated glioma treatment plans, nanocarrier design rationales, reproducible analysis protocols, and translational feasibility statements. Each artifact was scored on a 0-100 rubric by two independent raters, and disagreements exceeding 10 points were resolved by a third reviewer. Second, leave-one-institution-out validation was used to test whether the main coefficients and moderated-mediation patterns were dependent on any single participating medical college. These secondary analyses are reported in Sect. 4.5 and 4.6 and are interpreted as short-term educational validation rather than evidence of downstream clinical behavior.

Definition of variable operations

Based on the theoretical framework and research hypotheses, core variables were operationalized to support the primary moderated-mediation analysis and the two newly added validation experiments. Variable summary

Note on measurement and confounding control

The OSP index captures three engagement levels—consumption, collaboration, and contribution—aligned with open science practices in biomedical research. The ATTA-R was domain-adapted for nanomedicine through item modification, expert validation (CVI = 0.91), and pilot testing (α = 0.87, test-retest r = 0.79, CFI = 0.94). The newly added OBS score is a secondary educational validation endpoint and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of downstream clinical practice performance.

Control variable operationalization

Prior research experience (PRE) was coded as 0 = no experience, 1 = project participation only, 2 = with publications or submissions, based on self-reported research history. Baseline clinical skills (BCS) were assessed using the Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (Mini-CEX) on a 9-point scale (1–3 unsatisfactory, 4–6 satisfactory, 7–9 superior), administered by clinical supervisors blinded to group assignment. Digital literacy (DL) was measured using an 8-item scale adapted from the European Commission’s Digital Competence Framework for Educators (DigCompEdu), assessing information retrieval, data management, and collaborative platform navigation (α = 0.84). Learning motivation (LM) was assessed using the 7-item Academic Motivation Scale intrinsic regulation subscale (α = 0.81). All control variables were measured at baseline to ensure temporal precedence.

Econometric model

Grounded in the theoretical confluence of educational economics and cognitive science, this study develops an integrated econometric framework to rigorously explicate the mechanistic pathways through which feedback-based pedagogy and open science practices influence medical education outcomes [36, 37]. Prevailing research within this domain remains predominantly confined to unidimensional efficacy evaluations of isolated instructional methods, exhibiting a marked paucity of quantitative inquiry into multifactorial synergies and underlying mediating processes. To redress this limitation, the present investigation transcends conventional analytical boundaries by implementing a tripartite econometric validation strategy encompassing main-effect estimation, moderation analysis, and moderated-mediation modeling [38]. This methodological architecture permits simultaneous examination of direct pedagogical impacts alongside the nuanced, indirect mechanisms governing knowledge transformation and innovative capacity development. By explicitly accounting for the inherent complexity and context-dependent nature of medical education, this approach furnishes educational policymakers with empirically grounded, strategically actionable insights that bridge theoretical sophistication and practical implementation.

Main effect model (Model 1)

Theoretical formulation

Grounded in the core input–output paradigm of educational production function theory, this study operationalizes instructional intensity of feedback-based pedagogy (FDB) and engagement levels in open science practices (OSP) as principal pedagogical inputs. We specify a multiple linear regression model to quantify the marginal effects of these instructional interventions on the mastery of glioma treatment knowledge (KNL):1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{KNL}_{\mathrm{i}}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mathrm{FDB}_{\mathrm{i}}+\beta_{2}\mathrm{OSP}_{\mathrm{i}}+\gamma_{1}\mathrm{BKL}_{\mathrm{i}}+\gamma_{2}\mathrm{MAJ}_{\mathrm{i}}+\gamma_{3}\mathrm{UNI}_{\mathrm{i}}$$\end{document}

where β0 denotes the intercept; β1 and β2 represent the regression coefficients for feedback-based pedagogy and open science practice, respectively; γ captures the coefficient vector for baseline covariates, including baseline knowledge, specialty/background, institutional tier, study time, prior research experience, baseline clinical skills, digital literacy, and learning motivation; and ε is the stochastic error term. This specification isolates the net treatment effects of the instructional inputs after adjustment for observed confounders and provides the foundation for the subsequent moderation and moderated-mediation analyses.

All regression models were estimated under standard assumptions: linearity between predictors and outcomes verified through scatterplot matrices and Loess curve fitting; residual normality assessed via Shapiro-Wilk tests (p > 0.05) and Q-Q plots; homoscedasticity confirmed by Breusch-Pagan tests (all p > 0.05) or robust standard errors (HC4 estimator) when violated; multicollinearity excluded with all VIF < 3 and tolerance > 0.3; and observation independence supported by Durbin-Watson statistics (range: 1.7–2.3). Effect size reporting follows Cohen’s conventions: f²=0.02 (small), 0.15 (medium), 0.35 (large); partial η²=0.01 (small), 0.06 (medium), 0.14 (large). For mediation effects, we report κ² (indirect effect relative to maximum possible) with 95% BCa bootstrap confidence intervals. Bootstrap specifications: 5,000 resamples with bias-corrected accelerated intervals, using Hayes PROCESS macros (Models 4, 7, 14).

Moderation effect model (Model 2)

Formula description:

Based on the factor synergy principle in innovation-ecosystem theory, this study specifies the interaction term FDB × OSP between feedback-based pedagogy and open science practice and estimates a hierarchical regression model with cross-products. The coefficient β3 reflects the strength of the moderating effect. When β3 is significantly positive, the open science environment strengthens the effect of feedback-based pedagogy on innovation capability (INN). By decomposing simple slopes and conditional effects, this model reveals the complementary mechanism between teaching strategies and provides a dose-response basis for designing integrated educational interventions.

Adjustment and mediation model (Model 3)

Formula:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Bigg\{ \begin{aligned} & \mathrm{KNL}_{\mathrm{i}}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\mathrm{FDB}_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha_{2}\mathrm{OSP}_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha_{3}\left(\mathrm{FDB}_{\mathrm{i}}\times\mathrm{OSP}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)+\gamma\bar{X}_{\mathrm{i}}+\epsilon_{1\mathrm{i}} \\& \mathrm{INN}_{\mathrm{i}}\;= \beta_{0}\;+ \beta_{1}\mathrm{FDB}_{\mathrm{i}}+\beta_{2}\mathrm{KNL}_{\mathrm{i}}+\beta_{3}\mathrm{OSP}_{\mathrm{i}}+\beta_{4}\left(\mathrm{KNL}_{\mathrm{i}}\times\mathrm{OSP}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)+\gamma\bar{X}_{\mathrm{i}}+\epsilon_{2\mathrm{i}}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Formula description:

This study combines knowledge transfer theory and situational learning theory to construct a moderated mediation model with glioma knowledge mastery level as the mediator and open science practice as a moderator of both the a-path (FDB→KNL) and b-path (KNL→INN). Model identification follows Edwards and Lambert’s (2007) framework: Stage 1 tests moderation of the first stage (a-path), Stage 2 tests moderation of the second stage (b-path), and the index of moderated mediation quantifies the overall conditional indirect effect. The innovation of this model lies in using the Bootstrap method (5,000 resamples) to simultaneously estimate the conditional indirect effect and its moderating component, fully revealing the situational dependence of the “teaching intervention→knowledge internalization→innovation output” mechanism.

As shown in Fig. 4, the econometric architecture advanced herein embodies three pivotal methodological innovations. First, the main-effects specification systematically controls for observed confounders to establish robust causal identification between instructional interventions and educational outcomes, thereby transcending correlational limitations endemic to conventional pedagogical research. Second, the moderation model elucidates the synergistic amplification whereby open science environments enhance the efficacy of feedback-based instruction, furnishing empirical substantiation for integrated pedagogical strategies. Third, the moderated-mediation framework overcomes static modeling constraints inherent in traditional mediation analysis, dynamically delineating the heterogeneous transmission pathways linking knowledge mastery to innovative capacity across varying instructional contexts.

The moderation analysis revealed the bridging role of open science practice between feedback-based teaching intervention and innovation capability. As shown in Table 7 (Model 2), the direct effect of feedback-based pedagogy on innovation capability was significant (β = 0.26, p < 0.001), and the interaction effect (FDB x OSP) was also significant (B = 0.18, β = 0.15, p = 0.003). This indicates that open science practice significantly strengthens the innovation-promoting effect of feedback-based instruction. Simple-slope analysis showed that, under high open science engagement (+ 1 SD), the standardized effect of FDB on INN reached β = 0.48 (p < 0.001), whereas the corresponding effect under low engagement (-1 SD) was weaker, supporting the hypothesized synergistic mechanism (Fig. 9).

Moderation interaction plot

The significant FDB×OSP interaction (β = 0.15, p=0.003, f²=0.04) indicates that feedback-based pedagogy effectiveness varies substantially across levels of open science engagement. Simple slopes analysis reveals that at high OSP (+ 1 SD), each unit increase in feedback intensity enhances innovation capability by 0.48 standard deviations (p<0.001)—more than twice the effect at low OSP (β = 0.22, p<0.001). Johnson-Neyman analysis identifies the region of significance: the interaction effect is statistically significant when OSP exceeds 3.42, encompassing 62.4% of our sample. This suggests the synergistic benefit of combined feedback-open science instruction is realized by the majority of learners, but may not manifest for students with minimal open science engagement.

Mediation analysis confirms that knowledge mastery transmits 38.2% of feedback-based pedagogy’s effect on innovation capacity (indirect effect = 0.13, 95% CI [0.06, 0.21]). Open science practice significantly moderated this pathway (index of moderated mediation = 0.07, 95% CI [0.02, 0.14]), with the indirect effect strengthening from κ²=0.06 at low OSP to κ²=0.16 at high OSP—a 58% amplification.

The indirect effect of feedback-based pedagogy on innovation capability through knowledge mastery is significantly positive across all OSP levels, but conditional process analysis reveals substantial heterogeneity. At low OSP, the indirect effect is modest (κ² = 0.06, 95% CI [0.03, 0.15]); at mean OSP, it strengthens (κ² = 0.11, [0.06, 0.21]); and at high OSP, it reaches maximum magnitude (κ² = 0.16, [0.10, 0.29]). The index of moderated mediation (0.07, 95% CI [0.02, 0.14]) confirms that the overall indirect effect is significantly moderated by open science engagement. Effect contrast analysis indicates the difference between high and low OSP indirect effects is 0.11 (95% CI [0.02, 0.23]), demonstrating that open science participation amplifies the knowledge-to-innovation transformation pathway by approximately 58%.

The results indicate that feedback-based teaching and open science practice have multi-level effects on students’ learning outcomes. The intensity of teaching intervention significantly promotes knowledge mastery and further enhances this positive effect through the mediating path of innovative ability. The moderating effect of open science practice is particularly noteworthy, as it plays a crucial role between teaching interventions and learning outcomes, significantly enhancing the effectiveness of intervention measures. There are certain differences in the intensity of student groups with different professional backgrounds and institutional levels, but the main pathways of action are relatively stable. These findings not only confirm the theoretical expectations of the research hypothesis, but also reveal potential optimization space in educational practice.

To evaluate robustness to potential confounding, we conducted nested model comparisons examining coefficient stability upon progressive inclusion of control variables. Model 1a (demographics only): FDB→KNL β = 0.35, OSP→KNL β = 0.41. Model 1b (+ academic controls): FDB→KNL β = 0.31 (Δ11.4%), OSP→KNL β = 0.38 (Δ7.3%). Model 1c (+ full confounder set including PRE, BCS, DL, LM): FDB→KNL β = 0.28 (Δ20.0% from 1a), OSP→KNL β = 0.33 (Δ19.5% from 1a). Direction and significance remained stable across all specifications (all p < 0.001), with attenuation below the 25% threshold indicative of severe confounding. Notably, PRE demonstrated independent effects on innovation capacity (β = 0.17, p < 0.01) but not knowledge mastery (β = 0.06, p = 0.21), suggesting research experience specifically facilitates creative application rather than foundational knowledge acquisition. Randomization successfully balanced confounders across conditions (all p > 0.37). E-value analysis indicates an unmeasured confounder would need RR = 2.4 associations with both exposure and outcome to fully explain away the FDB→KNL effect, substantially exceeding our measured confounders (max RR = 1.8 for DL).

Robustness test

The subsample analysis further validated the robustness of the research conclusions across year-of-study groups (Table 8). The full-sample coefficient for FDB-> KNL was β = 0.28 (p < 0.001), and the direction remained positive for first-year (β = 0.24), second-year (β = 0.29), and third-year (β = 0.32) students. The OSP-> KNL effect was similarly stable, ranging from β = 0.30 to β = 0.36 across year groups. Importantly, the FDB x OSP interaction remained positive in each subgroup (β = 0.12–0.18), indicating that the synergistic effect was not driven by a single training stage. The indirect pathway FDB-> KNL-> INN also increased gradually from first-year to third-year students, suggesting that accumulated research training may strengthen the translation of knowledge mastery into innovation capability.

The subsample results indicate that feedback-based teaching and open science practice had consistent and stable positive effects on learning outcomes across graduate-year groups. Objective behavioral output validation experiment

To address the concern that innovation capability may be incompletely captured by self-report or domain-adapted creativity scales, we added an objective behavioral-output validation experiment. The analysis used the post-course artifacts already generated during the final glioma nanomedicine challenge. Two blinded faculty raters independently scored four practical outputs for each student: simulated glioma treatment decision quality, nanocarrier design feasibility, reproducible protocol/code quality, and translational rationale. Scores were standardized to a 0-100 scale and averaged across raters. This secondary experiment follows the principle that assessment of professional competence should combine psychometric indicators with observable performance evidence [39–41].

The experimental group outperformed the control group across all four objective domains. Mean scores were higher for simulated treatment-decision quality (84.6 vs. 76.2), nanocarrier design feasibility (81.7 vs. 72.8), reproducible protocol/code quality (79.9 vs. 69.5), and translational rationale (83.4 vs. 73.7). The largest standardized difference appeared in reproducible protocol/code quality, which is theoretically consistent with the open science component of the intervention. The overall objective behavioral score was 82.4 in the experimental group and 73.1 in the control group, yielding a mean difference of 9.3 points and supporting the interpretation that the intervention improved not only perceived innovation capability but also observable educational products (Fig. 10).

Objective behavioral output validation across four practical artifact domains

The objective behavioral validation in Fig. 10 indicates that the dual intervention produced the clearest improvement in outputs requiring transparent reasoning, reproducible workflow construction, and translational explanation. This pattern is important because these artifacts are closer to observable professional behavior than a self-report scale. Nevertheless, the artifacts remain course-based and simulation-supported; therefore, the results strengthen short-term educational validity but do not yet establish long-term clinical transfer. Future replications should extend these measures to audited clinical portfolios, supervised tumor-board performance, peer-reviewed outputs, open-source contributions, and patentable prototype development [42–44].

Cross-institutional leave-one-site-out validation experiment

To further test whether the empirical pattern depended on any single institution, we conducted a leave-one-institution-out validation experiment. The full moderated-mediation model was re-estimated six times, each time excluding one participating medical college. This procedure directly evaluates site-level stability and provides a stronger check of external validity than a single pooled regression, especially in a multi-institution educational RCT.

The coefficients remained directionally stable across all six exclusions. The FDB-> KNL coefficient ranged from β = 0.25 to β = 0.31, the OSP-> KNL coefficient ranged from β = 0.29 to β = 0.36, the FDB x OSP interaction ranged from β = 0.12 to β = 0.18, and the conditional indirect effect ranged from 0.10 to 0.16. None of the leave-one-site estimates reversed direction, and the effect ranges were narrow relative to the full-sample estimates. These results suggest that the main findings are not an artifact of one highly resourced institution or one atypical student cohort (Fig. 11).

Fig. 11Leave-one-institution-out validation of main, interaction, and indirect effects

Leave-one-institution-out validation of main, interaction, and indirect effects

The leave-one-institution-out validation in Fig. 11 confirms that excluding any individual institution produced only modest coefficient fluctuation. The OSP-> KNL effect was consistently the largest path, while the FDB x OSP interaction and indirect effect remained positive under every exclusion. This experiment strengthens the robustness of the trial within the sampled national context, but it should not be interpreted as evidence of cross-national generalizability. Instead, it supports the need for the cross-cultural and longitudinal replication agenda described in the Discussion.

Discussions

Main research results

Boundary conditions for generalizability

The mechanisms identified in this 12-week trial—feedback-enhanced knowledge consolidation, open science-facilitated innovation development, and knowledge mastery as a transformation mediator—are hypothesized to generalize only to complex nanomedicine domains sharing three boundary conditions with glioma therapeutics: high interdisciplinary knowledge integration spanning molecular biology, materials science, and clinical oncology; availability of open digital infrastructures enabling data sharing and collaborative platforms; and “high complexity–low immediate risk” learning contexts where instructional simulation precedes clinical application. Domains diverging on these conditions, such as emergency nanomedicine with high immediate risk and compressed feedback cycles, or infectious disease nanovaccines constrained by biosafety containment limiting open collaboration, require substantive pedagogical redesign before our model can be applied. We therefore frame the following domain transfer discussion as theoretically grounded hypotheses requiring empirical validation, not established generalizations.

Nanotechnology-oncology convergence creates therapeutic possibilities, yet exposes gaps in educating clinician-scientists who can translate advances into patient outcomes [1–3]. This study addresses the urgent need for theoretically grounded, empirically validated instructional models that connect pedagogical design with measurable competency development in high-complexity domains. By integrating feedback-based pedagogy with open science participation within a moderated mediation framework, we demonstrate how structured instructional inputs transform into innovation capabilities through identifiable psychological mechanisms—providing a template for evidence-based educational reform potentially applicable to other frontier medical disciplines sharing the specified boundary conditions.

Our effect size for feedback-based pedagogy (β = 0.28) exceeds meta-analytic benchmarks for general education feedback interventions (β ≈ 0.20), likely reflecting the structured, high-frequency design of our intervention. The knowledge mediation proportion (38.2%) similarly exceeds general education benchmarks (25–30%), reflecting the highly structured nature of medical knowledge systems that facilitate transformation into innovative capabilities.

Our findings align with established feedback and simulation-based assessment literatures, which emphasize that high-quality feedback must be timely, specific, actionable, and connected to observable performance tasks [39–43]. In our model, feedback-based pedagogy provides structured diagnostic guidance, while open science platforms supply collaborative resources and transparent artifacts. The significant interaction effect (β = 0.15) therefore reflects a complementary instructional mechanism: feedback clarifies what learners should improve, and open science engagement provides the resources and social context through which improvement can be enacted.

This study found that feedback-based teaching has a significantly stronger promoting effect on knowledge mastery than the reported effect size in previous research. This difference may be due to the real-time structured feedback design used in this study, which better meets the learning needs of complex medical knowledge compared to traditional delayed feedback. The moderating effect of open science practice also shows strong explanatory power, indicating that the collaborative environment provided by modern digital education platforms may have a more profound impact on teaching than traditional resource sharing. It is worth noting that the strength of the knowledge mediation effect is significantly higher than the research benchmark of general subject education, reflecting the highly structured characteristics of the medical knowledge system, which enables knowledge to be more effectively transformed into innovative capabilities.

While this study focused specifically on glioma therapeutics, the mechanisms identified—feedback-enhanced knowledge consolidation, open science-facilitated innovation development, and knowledge mastery as a transformation mediator—are hypothesized to operate across complex nanomedicine domains. This transferability hypothesis requires empirical validation through the tiered replication framework proposed below. We propose a similarity-based generalization framework wherein our teaching paradigm is most directly transferable to domains characterized by: (a) pathophysiological complexity requiring synthesis of molecular biology, materials science, and clinical therapeutics; (b) rapid methodological evolution dependent on open data sharing and computational collaboration; and (c) educational contexts permitting iterative, feedback-rich learning without immediate patient risk. Conversely, domains with divergent characteristics—such as infectious disease nanovaccines requiring biosafety containment (limiting open collaboration) or emergency nanomedicine applications (prohibiting extended feedback cycles)—may require substantial pedagogical redesign. We recommend future replication studies prioritize Alzheimer’s disease nanotherapeutics and immuno-oncology nanocarriers as “most similar” domains, and emergency nanomedicine as a “least similar” critical test case to validate boundary conditions empirically.

The stability of core findings—feedback effects attenuating by only 20% upon full adjustment for prior research experience, baseline clinical skills, digital literacy, and learning motivation, with persistent significance—strengthens causal interpretation. The differential predictive power of these confounders (PRE and DL specifically predicting innovation capacity but not knowledge mastery) reveals distinct developmental pathways: foundational knowledge responds primarily to structured instruction, whereas innovative application additionally requires research socialization and technical capability. This pattern aligns with theoretical models distinguishing expertise acquisition from creative expertise development.

Theoretical contributions with bounded generalizability

Our findings advance three interconnected theoretical agendas, with explicit boundary conditions. First, we extend educational production function theory by demonstrating that digital collaboration environments amplify traditional instructional inputs—but only when learners possess sufficient digital literacy (DL > 4.0 in our sample) and institutions provide adequate infrastructure. This conditional specification limits generalizability to resourced educational contexts. Second, we identify knowledge mastery as a critical transformation mechanism between structured instruction and innovation output within the specific domain of glioma therapeutics; whether this mechanism operates similarly in less structured domains (e.g., immuno-oncology with higher biological variability) requires direct empirical testing. Third, our subgroup analyses reveal demographic heterogeneity that instructional design must accommodate, but these patterns may reflect China-specific graduate training structures and should not be assumed transferable to Western medical education systems without cross-cultural replication [38]. These theoretical advances position our work within emerging movements toward adaptive, evidence-based medical education reform.

Our analytical approach prioritized comprehensive reporting to enable evaluation and replication. Beyond conventional significance testing, we report effect sizes (Cohen’s f², partial η²) for all primary effects, allowing readers to assess practical magnitude against established benchmarks. Model fit indices (R², Adjusted R², RMSE, AIC, BIC) facilitate comparison with alternative specifications. For moderated mediation, we provide conditional indirect effects at meaningful moderator levels, κ² for effect size quantification, and Monte Carlo confidence intervals for robust inference. The Johnson-Neyman technique identifies precise significance regions for interactions, avoiding arbitrary median-split approaches. These reporting standards exceed conventional practice in medical education research and align with emerging transparency guidelines in quantitative social science.

While our RCT design supports stronger causal inference than observational studies, several limitations warrant restrained interpretation. First, the 12-week duration precludes assessment of long-term retention and clinical transfer. Second, outcome assessor blinding, while maintained, did not extend to participants and instructors, potentially introducing performance bias. Third, the single-nation context limits cross-cultural generalizability. We therefore characterize our findings as promising preliminary evidence requiring replication rather than definitive proof of causal efficacy.

Practical-transfer restraint

The present findings should not be read as evidence that students have already changed real clinical behavior or generated durable translational outputs. Rather, the study supports short-term improvement in nanomedicine/glioma educational competency under a 12-week, China-based, simulation-supported instructional condition. Long-term clinical decision quality, independent research productivity, patentable innovation, and sustained open-science contribution remain empirical questions for future longitudinal studies.

Policy suggestions

Building on the theoretical mechanisms and design principles established above, we propose three policy recommendations with specific implementation specifications.

Recommendation 1: integrated infrastructure investment

Derived from the finding that open science engagement amplifies feedback effectiveness (β = 0.15, p = 0.003) and shows compensatory effects in non-Double First Class institutions (β = 0.30 vs. 0.26), we recommend that education authorities consider integrating open science platforms into accreditation frameworks in medical education accreditation standards, with differential resourcing for resource-constrained institutions. Implementation specification: Require platform utilization metrics (data downloads, collaborative contributions, preprint engagement) as accreditation criteria; allocate dedicated construction funds for institutions lacking digital infrastructure; establish inter-institutional Xplatform consortia to ensure equitable access. Expected outcome: Realize the compensatory potential demonstrated in our subgroup analyses (β = 0.30 vs. 0.26) through systematic infrastructure investment.

Recommendation 2: competency-based feedback systematization

Grounded in our main effects showing that feedback-based pedagogy significantly improves knowledge mastery (β = 0.28, p < 0.001) and established dose-response patterns in educational feedback research, Teacher Development Centers must establish evidence-based feedback training programs. Theoretical rationale: Our main effects (β = 0.28) and dose-response patterns align with established feedback research, yet with critical qualification that feedback quality (diagnostic specificity, actionability) moderates quantity effects—mirroring findings that biomimetic project effectiveness depends on structured reflection mechanisms. Implementation specification: Develop standardized feedback quality rubrics emphasizing misconception diagnosis and strategic guidance; mandate minimum twice-weekly structured feedback with quality audit protocols; integrate video-assisted feedback review systems to enhance instructor reflective practice and calibration.

Recommendation 3: adaptive, learner-centered differentiation

Based on the year-of-study subsample patterns in Table 8 and the additional subgroup checks described in the robustness analysis, teaching reform policies should adopt responsive design principles rather than uniform implementation. Implementation specification: calibrate open science activity intensity based on baseline digital literacy assessments; provide more structured scaffolding for first-year students; emphasize research-output quality and translational reasoning for senior students; and conduct institutional equity audits to ensure that differential resourcing matches demonstrated need profiles. Expected outcome: optimize intervention effectiveness across learner-development stages and institutional contexts.

These recommendations extend beyond glioma therapeutics to complex medical domains sharing three characteristics: (1) high interdisciplinary knowledge integration; (2) availability of open digital infrastructures enabling data sharing; and (3) learning contexts permitting iterative feedback without immediate patient risk. For domains lacking these conditions—such as emergency medicine or infectious disease nanovaccines requiring biosafety containment—educators should substitute structured simulation for open collaboration and compressed feedback cycles for extended iteration. Implementation in non-medical STEM fields requires analogous adaptation: replace clinical case libraries with design project repositories, and translate “diagnostic feedback” into “design iteration critique” while retaining the core feedback-open science integration structure.

Research limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Although the revised analysis now includes objective artifact scoring and leave-one-institution-out validation, the study remains bounded by its 12-week duration, single national educational system, and course-based simulation context. These features strengthen internal validity for short-term educational outcomes but limit claims about long-term clinical behavior, cross-cultural transfer, and durable translational innovation.

Regarding external validity and generalizability, although the sample covers different types of universities, all are from domestic medical schools, and cross-national applicability requires verification. More critically, the teaching paradigm’s transferability to other nanomedicine domains depends on specific boundary conditions. We propose that our integrated feedback-open science model is most readily generalizable to domains sharing three characteristics with glioma therapeutics: (1) high knowledge complexity requiring interdisciplinary integration; (2) availability of open digital infrastructures enabling data sharing and collaborative platforms; and (3) “high complexity-low immediate risk” learning contexts where instructional simulation precedes clinical application. Domains lacking these conditions—such as emergency medicine with high immediate risk or rare diseases with limited open data—may require substantive pedagogical adaptations.

Second, the intervention lasted only one semester and therefore cannot establish the long-term sustainability of teaching effectiveness, especially its influence on students’ clinical practice after graduation. Third, although the primary innovation measure was supplemented by objective artifact scoring in Sect. 4.5, the study still lacks downstream clinical innovation behavior indicators such as audited tumor-board decisions, supervised clinical portfolios, peer-reviewed outputs, patent applications, or sustained open-source contributions. Fourth, individual instructor differences were not modeled in detail, and future work should examine whether teacher feedback style, domain expertise, and digital facilitation capacity moderate intervention effectiveness. These limitations affect generalizability, although the domain-similarity framework and leave-one-institution-out validation provide a stronger basis for bounded interpretation.

Objective behavioral metrics for replication studies

Subsequent replication protocols should pre-register objective endpoints at 6, 12, 36, and 60 months to directly test whether short-term educational gains translate into durable clinical and translational behavior. Recommended endpoints include: (1) audited clinical practice records or electronic learning portfolios documenting glioma-related case participation and treatment-planning quality; (2) blinded supervisor ratings of clinical decision-making using Mini-CEX, OSCE, or tumor-board simulation rubrics; (3) traceable scholarly outputs, including peer-reviewed papers, conference abstracts, registered protocols, open-source code/data contributions, and OSF/GitHub activity logs; (4) translational innovation outputs, including patent applications, invention disclosures, validated protocols, or prototype nanocarrier designs; and (5) implementation indicators, including adoption of student-generated protocols in laboratory or clinical training projects. These metrics would address the current study’s reliance on self-report and simulated assessments and would provide a stronger basis for claims about practical innovation output.

Proposed replication agenda for empirical generalizability testing

To move beyond theoretical speculation, we propose a prioritized replication agenda designed to empirically test the boundary conditions of our model. Tier 1 (direct replication) targets Alzheimer’s nanotherapeutics, which shares glioma’s blood-brain barrier complexity and open neuroimaging data availability; successful replication would support generalizability to neurodegenerative nanomedicine. Tier 2 (adapted replication) targets cardiovascular nanomedicine, requiring substitution of neurological case libraries with immunological scenarios; this tests whether the model withstands domain-content modification. Tier 3 (boundary testing) targets emergency nanomedicine, where immediate patient risk prohibits extended feedback cycles; failure to replicate here would confirm the “low immediate risk” boundary condition. We emphasize that these are proposed tests, not validated generalizations, and encourage independent research teams to preregister replication protocols. Priority replication domains include Alzheimer’s nanotherapeutics (high similarity to glioma) and emergency nanomedicine (low similarity, testing boundary conditions). For instructional design optimization, future studies should test adaptive algorithms that personalize feedback intensity and open science task complexity based on learner baseline digital literacy (DL) and prior research experience (PRE), which our analyses identified as key moderators of innovation capacity development. Extend the tracking period to three to five years to evaluate the long-term impact of educational interventions on students’ career development. Develop innovative capability assessment tools, incorporate them into clinical practice records, and incorporate objective indicators such as papers and patents into the measurement system. Strengthen the research and design of teacher factors, and establish a relevant model between teacher characteristics and teaching effectiveness. Finally, explore the application of artificial intelligence technology in personalized feedback and open science platforms to improve the accuracy and scalability of educational interventions. These directions will help establish a more comprehensive theoretical system for innovative medical education.

These recommendations extend beyond glioma therapeutics to complex medical domains sharing three characteristics: (1) high interdisciplinary knowledge integration; (2) availability of open digital infrastructures enabling data sharing; and (3) learning contexts permitting iterative feedback without immediate patient risk. For domains lacking these conditions—such as emergency medicine or infectious disease nanovaccines requiring biosafety containment—educators should substitute structured simulation for open collaboration and compressed feedback cycles for extended iteration. Implementation in non-medical STEM fields requires analogous adaptation: replace clinical case libraries with design project repositories, and translate ‘diagnostic feedback’ into ‘design iteration critique’ while retaining the core feedback-open science integration structure.

Conclusion

This study reveals the important role of feedback-based pedagogy and open science practice in nanomedicine education through systematic empirical analysis, with identified mechanisms hypothesized to generalize across complex therapeutic domains sharing key boundary conditions with glioma treatment. Research has found that high-quality feedback-based pedagogy can significantly improve students’ mastery of glioma treatment knowledge, and participation in open science platforms further strengthens this effect. Knowledge mastery plays an important mediating role in the cultivation of innovation ability, and this transformation mechanism is more significant among non-Double First Class universities and female learners. Results support the hypothesis that an integrated feedback-open science approach shows promise for enhancing medical education outcomes. These findings are consistent with theoretical predictions, though replication across diverse contexts is needed to establish generalizability. These findings provide domain-specific evidence for glioma therapeutics education and a hypothesized theoretical framework—centered on feedback-open science integration and knowledge-innovation mediation—potentially applicable to other complex disease contexts sharing the three boundary conditions specified above. Empirical validation across diverse contexts is required before establishing generalizability. Based on research evidence, it is proposed that medical education reform should focus on innovative combinations of teaching strategies. For nanomedicine educators, we recommend priority implementation in domains matching our study’s boundary conditions (high complexity, open data availability, simulation-based learning), with anticipated adaptations for contexts diverging on these dimensions. Education practitioners need to establish standardized high-quality feedback mechanisms and actively promote the application of open science platforms in professional courses. Based on the different characteristics of different types of colleges and student groups, it is recommended to adopt the principle of classification guidance and design more targeted teaching intervention plans. Cross-domain validation studies are encouraged to test the generalizability framework proposed herein. Future medical education innovation should focus on the coordinated development of knowledge transmission and innovation ability cultivation, and comprehensively improve the quality of medical talent cultivation by optimizing key links in the teaching process. Therefore, the conclusion should be applied within explicit boundary conditions: the evidence derives from a 12-week single-country educational trial and does not yet prove long-term clinical transfer. Future cross-national and longitudinal RCTs should incorporate objective behavioral and translational metrics, including clinical practice records, supervisor-rated decision quality, peer-reviewed outputs, protocol registrations, open-science contribution logs, and patent or invention-disclosure records. The added objective behavioral-output validation and leave-one-institution-out experiment further show that the findings are not limited to self-report measures or driven by a single institutional site, although longitudinal and cross-national replication remains necessary before claiming durable clinical transfer.
