Modulating effect of positive emotion arousal on inhibition of return in schizophrenia
Authors: Miaomiao Yu, Feilong Qian, Jingfang Liu, Bo Dong, Xiaogang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious research has primarily examined the effect of negative emotional arousal intensity on inhibition of return (IOR) in patients with schizophrenia. However, whether positive stimuli of different arousal levels exhibit asymmetry remains unclear.MethodsUsing a dual-cue paradigm with central cue, the present study systematically investigated the impact of positive emotions with varying arousal levels—specifically, positive high arousal, positive low arousal, and neutral low arousal stimuli—presented at either the cue location or the target location, on IOR in patients with schizophrenia during the attentional disengagement and reorientation stages.ResultsThe results revealed that when stimuli were presented at the cue location, both the schizophrenia group and the control group exhibited significantly larger IOR effects under the positive high arousal condition. In contrast, when stimuli were presented at the target location, only the control group showed a larger IOR effect under the positive high arousal condition, whereas no significant differences were observed across conditions in the schizophrenia group.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that the modulation of the IOR effect by positive emotions is influenced by arousal level and exhibits an asymmetry across attentional processing stages. During the attention disengagement stage, healthy individuals may be more easily drawn to positive high arousal stimuli, whereas patients with schizophrenia are not. During the attentional reorientation stage, healthy individuals may show an approach preference toward positive high arousal targets, while patients with schizophrenia may display a weakened approach tendency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07849-w.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Inhibition of return, Positive emotional arousal

Introduction

Abnormal attentional function has long been recognized as a core cognitive deficit in patients with schizophrenia. Traditional cognitive perspectives emphasize broad and persistent impairments in attention in patients with schizophrenia, who exhibit significant difficulties in tasks involving sustained attention, selective attention, and attentional allocation [1–4]. However, recent research indicates that the attentional deficit in patients with schizophrenia may not stem from a general inability to inhibit distracting stimuli, instead, it manifests as abnormally narrow and intense attentional focusing—a “hyperfocusing” phenomenon [5–8]. The latest research has revealed that this “hyperfocusing” on target-relevant feature information is accompanied by “hyperfiltering” of target-irrelevant feature information in patients with schizophrenia [9].

According to traditional views, attentional deficits in patients with schizophrenia manifest as difficulties in focusing on target information and filtering out irrelevant information. However, studies examining Inhibition of Return (IOR) in patients with schizophrenia have found the IOR effect in patients with schizophrenia [10–13]. IOR refers to the slowed response to previously attended objects or locations [14, 15], which facilitates attentional shifts to new locations and optimizes search efficiency. Findings regarding the interaction between IOR and emotion in patients with schizophrenia are inconsistent [11–13]. Some studies report no impairment in emotion-based IOR in patients with schizophrenia [10, 13, 16], others find that emotional information modulates the IOR effect in patients with schizophrenia [11, 16–18]. These inconsistencies may be influenced by factors such as experimental procedures, the location of emotional stimuli, emotional valence and emotional arousal. A meta-analysis by Mushquash AR, et al. [19] indicated that while patients with schizophrenia show delayed IOR in single-cue paradigms (without central cues), their IOR effects are more consistent with healthy controls in dual-cue paradigms (which include a second cue, i.e., central fixation brightening). Furthermore, the location of emotional stimuli (cue location vs. target location) in the cue-target paradigm also affects IOR in patients with schizophrenia. When emotional stimuli are presented as cues, patients with schizophrenia exhibit impaired attentional disengagement, leading to a reduced IOR effect [20]. Conversely, when emotional stimuli serve as targets, they show enhanced attentional maintenance to negative targets, which amplifies the IOR effect [6, 16].

A recent study employed a dual-cue paradigm with central cue, manipulating the valence and arousal of emotional stimuli presented at either the cue location or the target location, to investigate their impact on IOR in patients with schizophrenia during the attentional disengagement and reorientation stages, respectively [16]. In Experiment 1, where emotional stimuli were presented at the cue location, results showed no significant difference in the IOR effect in patients with schizophrenia across the negative high arousal, negative low arousal, and neutral low arousal conditions. In contrast, healthy controls exhibited a significantly larger IOR effect under the negative high arousal condition. This suggests that during the attentional disengagement stage, patients with schizophrenia are more likely to filter out negative high arousal stimuli, whereas healthy participants enhance perceptual processing of such stimuli. In Experiment 2, where emotional stimuli were presented at the target location, results revealed that the IOR effect in patients with schizophrenia was significantly larger under the negative high arousal condition compared to the neutral low arousal condition. However, no significant differences in the IOR effect were observed across the three emotional conditions in the healthy control group. This indicates that during task-relevant reorientation, patients with schizophrenia may exhibit “hyperfocusing” on high arousal, life-threatening stimuli, whereas healthy controls may avoid these threatening stimuli. These findings demonstrate that although a significant IOR effect can be observed in patients with schizophrenia, they still exhibit attentional deficits when processing emotional stimuli with varying arousal levels across different stages of attention.

For emotional stimuli, patients with schizophrenia exhibit an asymmetry in emotional processing, characterized by significantly reduced experience of positive emotions and deficits in both anticipatory pleasure (reflecting the anticipation of future rewards) and consummatory pleasure (reflecting the capacity for immediate pleasure) [21, 22]. Other researches indicate that patients with schizophrenia report positive emotional events less, yet show higher intensity and longer duration of induced negative emotions. These manifest as reduced positive elements (e.g., smiling) and increased negative elements (e.g., angry tone) in facial expressions and speech [23–25]. The asymmetry in processing positive versus negative emotions in patients with schizophrenia is likewise reflected at the level of neural mechanisms. The right inferior frontal gyrus exhibits decreased activation during positive emotion processing due to hypofunction of dopamine D1 receptors, and increased activation during negative emotion processing due to hyperactivity of D2 receptors [26, 27].

In summary, existing researches have established a valence-based asymmetry in emotional processing in patients with schizophrenia [28–30]. While Yu M, et al. [16] primarily investigated the impact of negative emotional stimuli, it remains unknown whether positive and negative emotional stimuli exert asymmetric effects on IOR in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy individuals. Furthermore, emotional arousal intensity is a critical factor modulating the IOR effect in both healthy individuals [31] and patients with schizophrenia [16]. Whether positive stimuli of varying arousal levels influence IOR in patients with schizophrenia asymmetrically compared to negative stimuli is also unclear. Finally, previous studies have shown that the pattern of IOR modulation differs when emotional stimuli are presented at the cue location [32–35] versus the target location [36–38]. Therefore, building upon the work of Yu M, et al. [16], this study employs a dual-cue paradigm with central cue to examine the effects of positive emotional stimuli with different arousal levels on IOR in patients with schizophrenia during the attentional disengagement stage (emotional stimuli as cues) and the attentional reorientation stage (emotional stimuli as targets).

Experiment 1

Method

Participants

Experiment 1a

33 patients with schizophrenia were recruited from the Suzhou Social Welfare General Hospital. One patient with schizophrenia was excluded due to an overall accuracy rate below 80%, which may have resulted from inattentiveness or lack of focus during the task. Consequently, data from 32 patients with schizophrenia were included in subsequent analyses. All patients met the diagnostic criteria for patients with schizophrenia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5). Symptoms of each patient were assessed by at least two experienced psychiatrists through structured clinical interviews. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) substance abuse or dependence within the 6 months prior to the experiment; (2) a history of head injury accompanied by prolonged loss of consciousness or neurological sequelae; (3) cerebral metabolic abnormalities due to neurological or other medical conditions. All participants reported no visual impairments, were able to perform the experiment normally, and were naïve to the experimental purpose. The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale was used to assess symptoms [39]. Medication dosages of patients with schizophrenia were converted into chlorpromazine equivalents [40]. Apparatus and materials

The experiment was programmed and data were recorded using PsychoPy. All stimuli were presented on a 22-inch ViewSonic P225f monitor with a resolution of 1024 * 768 pixels and a refresh rate of 100 Hz. The background of the experiment was set to gray. A central fixation cross (“+”) was presented at the center of the screen (0°, 0°), with both horizontal and vertical visual angles of 0.5°. The cued central fixation cross had horizontal and vertical visual angles of 1°. Two peripheral boxes, with a horizontal visual angle of 5.3° and a vertical visual angle of 4°, were displayed to the left (-4°, 0°) and right (4°, 0°) of the fixation point. The target was a white five-pointed star with both horizontal and vertical visual angles of 3°. During the experiment, the participant’s head was stabilized using a chin rest to maintain a constant viewing distance of approximately 60 cm from the center of the display screen.

Thirty-four pictures were selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) [44], developed by the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH). Among them, 30 pictures (10 positive high arousal, 10 positive low arousal, and 10 neutral low arousal pictures) were used in the formal experiment, and 4 pictures were used for practice trials. The main effect of emotional arousal was significant, F (2, 18) = 281.37, p < .001, η² = 0.97. However, no significant difference in arousal was found between the positive low arousal and neutral low arousal conditions, t (9) = 1.35, p > .05. Therefore, positive high arousal and positive low arousal pictures are distinguished by a significant difference in arousal level; positive low arousal and neutral low arousal pictures are distinguished by a significant difference in valence.

Experimental design and experimental procedure

Both Experiment 1a and 1b employed a 2 (cue validity: valid, invalid) × 3 (emotional arousal intensity: positive high arousal, positive low arousal, neutral low arousal) within-subjects design. The dependent variable was reaction time (RT).

The experimental procedure was as follows (see Fig. 1). Initially, a fixation cross “+” was presented at the center of the screen, with a rectangular box on each side. After 800 ms, an emotional stimulus picture of varying arousal intensity was randomly presented as a peripheral cue in one of the boxes for 200 ms. Following a 300 ms interstimulus interval, the central fixation cross “+” was replaced by a central cue “+” (a boldened fixation cross) for 300 ms, after which it reverted to the standard central fixation cross “+”. After another 300 ms, the target stimulus (a white star) appeared randomly in one of the two rectangular boxes. Participants were required to press the “G” key as quickly and accurately as possible upon target appearance. Following the participant’s response or after 1500 ms had elapsed, an inter-trial interval screen was presented for a random duration between 1000 ms and 2000 ms, after which the next trial commenced.

The procedure of Experiment 1

To familiarize participants with the procedure and task requirements, each completed a practice session comprising 24 trials, which included 8 catch trials (where only the emotional cue was presented without a target). The formal experiment consisted of 264 trials, including 24 catch trials (where no target appeared, and no keypress was required). In the remaining trials, all levels of the two variables—cue validity (valid, invalid) and emotional arousal intensity (positive high arousal, positive low arousal, neutral low arousal)—were presented in a mixed and randomized order. And all emotional stimulus materials were balanced across different positions and conditions. The formal experiment was divided into two blocks, with participants allowed a brief one-minute rest between blocks.

Results

Trials with incorrect responses and reaction times exceeding ± 3 standard deviations from the mean of each experimental condition were excluded from the original data. The mean reaction time for each participant under each condition was then calculated. On average, 4.39% of trials were excluded in the schizophrenia group, while 1.99% of trials were excluded in the control group. An independent samples t-test was conducted on the percentages of excluded trials between the two groups. The results showed that the percentage of excluded trials in the schizophrenia group was significantly higher than that in the control group, t (63) = 2.83, p < .05, d = 0.70. Additionally, a 2 (cue validity: valid, invalid) * 3 (emotional arousal intensity: positive high arousal, positive low arousal, neutral low arousal) repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on the mean reaction times using SPSS 25.0. The one-way repeated-measures ANOVA on the IOR effect size (Fig. 2c) met the assumption of sphericity (p > .05). The results showed that a significant main effect of emotional arousal intensity, F (2, 62) = 3.61, p< .05, = 0.10. Discussion

In Experiment 1, a significant main effect of cue validity was observed in both the schizophrenia group and control group, indicating the presence of a significant IOR effect when emotional stimuli were presented at the cue location in both groups. For both the schizophrenia group and control group, the IOR effect did not differ significantly between the positive low arousal and neutral low arousal conditions. However, the IOR effect size in the positive high arousal condition was significantly smaller than that in the neutral low arousal condition. This suggests that the IOR effect for positive emotional stimuli does differ from that for neutral stimuli, but only when the positive emotion is of high arousal, indicating that the arousal level of the emotion influences IOR. It is noteworthy that although both groups exhibited the smallest IOR effect under the positive high arousal condition, the magnitude of its difference from the neutral low arousal condition was modest. Specifically, the IOR effect size in the schizophrenia group under the positive high arousal condition was 20 ms smaller than that under the neutral low arousal condition, while in the control group, it was 13 ms smaller. Furthermore, after controlling for baseline reaction time between the two groups, there was no significant difference in the proportional IOR scores across different emotional conditions in the schizophrenia group. This suggests that the absolute IOR effect size may have overestimated the IOR in patients with schizophrenia, and it is likely that their IOR effect is not influenced by emotional arousal level. It should be noted that the trend of the proportional IOR scores was consistent with the original absolute IOR effect: the IOR effect for patients with schizophrenia under the positive high arousal condition was lower than that under the neutral low arousal condition. Experiment 1 specifically examined the influence of positive emotional arousal on IOR in patients with schizophrenia during the attentional disengagement stage. Experiment 2 will present emotional stimuli at the target location to further investigate the effect of positive emotional arousal intensity on IOR during the attentional reorientation stage.

Discussion

In Experiment 2, where emotional stimuli were presented at the target location, significant IOR effects were still observed in both the schizophrenia group and control group. However, the pattern of how emotional arousal intensity influenced the IOR effect differed between the two groups. For the schizophrenia group, the IOR effect did not differ significantly across the three emotional conditions. In the control group, while the IOR effect size in the positive high arousal condition did not differ significantly from that in the positive low arousal condition, it was significantly larger than that in the neutral low arousal condition. These findings suggest that patients with schizophrenia exhibit a lack of modulation in the IOR effect by arousal level.

General discussion

This study employed a dual-cue paradigm to examine the effects of positive emotions with varying arousal levels on IOR in patients with schizophrenia during attentional disengagement and reorientation stages. Results revealed significant IOR effects in both schizophrenia group and control group across both attentional stages. However, the impact of emotional arousal on IOR demonstrated distinct patterns across different processing stages. When emotional stimuli appeared as cues, both groups showed significantly smaller IOR effects under the positive high arousal condition compared to neutral condition. When emotional stimuli appeared as targets, only the control group exhibited enhanced IOR effects for positive high arousal stimuli. These findings reveal asymmetries with previous research using negative emotional stimuli parametrized by arousal intensity [16], and further demonstrate divergent patterns of how positive versus negative stimuli of different arousal levels modulate IOR across distinct attentional stages.

In both Experiments 1 and 2 of this study, reaction times under the valid cue condition were significantly slower than those under the invalid cue condition for both schizophrenia group and control group, indicating the presence of a significant IOR effect in both groups. This finding is consistent with previous research [10, 13, 16]. Prior studies suggest that the presentation of a secondary central cue facilitates the disengagement of attention from the cued location, which aligns with the “reorienting” hypothesis of IOR, for both patients with schizophrenia and healthy participants [45–48]. Conversely, in paradigms lacking a central cue, the IOR effect in patients with schizophrenia is often delayed [11, 49]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that significant IOR effects can be reliably elicited using the dual-cue paradigm, regardless of whether the emotional stimuli are positive, as in the current study, or negative, as in previous research [16].

Furthermore, the influence of positive emotional arousal on IOR in both schizophrenia group and control group exhibited an asymmetry dependent on the stage of attentional processing. During the attentional disengagement stage, positive high arousal emotional stimuli attenuated the IOR effect in both groups. This is likely because the salient motivational significance of positive high arousal stimuli automatically captures and holds spatial attention, making it difficult to disengage from the cued location, thereby delaying the detection of novel targets [50–53]. During the attentional reorientation stage, the modulatory effect of arousal demonstrated a dissociation between groups. Positive high arousal emotional stimuli significantly enhanced the IOR effect in the control group, whereas this modulatory effect was absent in patients with schizophrenia. Positive emotions are linked to approach motivation, while negative emotions are associated with avoidance motivation [54–58]. This motivational differentiation has evolved to form an approach preference for positive emotional stimuli. The control group responded faster to positive high arousal stimuli at the target location, demonstrating approach tendency. In contrast, the schizophrenia group showed impaired approach motivation or anticipatory anhedonia [59, 60], exhibiting a lack of modulation in the IOR effect by arousal level. It should be acknowledged that this study did not directly measure neural or motivational mechanisms. The findings may likely be influenced by generalized response slowing and reduced sensitivity to arousal differences in patients with schizophrenia.

Compared to the study by Yu M, et al. [16], the present research reveals a valence asymmetry in the interaction between emotion and selective attention in patients with schizophrenia. This asymmetry is evident across both the attentional disengagement and reorientation stages and is modulated by emotional arousal. During attentional disengagement, healthy individuals typically process emotional stimuli with a characteristic “approach benefit and avoid harm.” For instance, stimuli associated with reward can enhance visual attentional salience, making it more difficult for attention to disengage from them [61–64]. When facing negative stimuli, individuals typically exhibit an avoidance tendency [65, 66], prompting individuals to disengage attention more rapidly from that location and orient it toward new external positions. However, after controlling for baseline reaction time, no significant differences were found in the proportional IOR scores of the schizophrenia group across different emotional conditions. This suggests that the absolute IOR effect may have overestimated the IOR effect in patients with schizophrenia, and it is likely that their IOR effect is not influenced by emotional arousal level.

During the attentional reorientation stage, the healthy control group exhibits “approach-reward, avoid-harm” behavior, allocating more attentional resources to rewarding stimuli and fewer to threatening stimuli. This valence-specific effect aligns with the adaptive principle of emotion regulation: prioritizing the processing of positive stimuli facilitates efficient allocation of cognitive resources, while actively ignoring negative stimuli helps reduce emotional exhaustion, thereby maintaining psychological well-being [67, 68]. In comparison, the excessive focus on negative high arousal stimuli in patients with schizophrenia may prevent effective disengagement from threatening stimuli, potentially endangering survival [16]. Furthermore, during the attention reorientation stage, this study found a significant positive correlation between the IOR effect size under the positive high arousal condition and negative symptom scores in patients with schizophrenia, r = .435, p < .05 (the Pearson correlation tables can be found in the Supplementary materials 1). Anticipatory anhedonia in patients with schizophrenia impairs the formation of positive expectations toward rewarding targets, thereby weakening motivational engagement with positive high arousal stimuli [59, 60, 69]. On the other hand, impaired ability to translate pleasure into action [70] may result in slowed attentional responses when positive stimuli are presented as targets requiring a response.

This study has several limitations. First, there was a difference in baseline reaction time between patients with schizophrenia and healthy individuals, with the schizophrenia group showing significantly slower responses compared to the control group, which is consistent with the majority of previous studies [9, 11, 16]. The participants with schizophrenia in this study were older, had lower levels of education, a longer duration of illness, and were under long-term stable medication. These factors may collectively reflect the typical clinical profile of chronic patients while also being associated with broader cognitive decline, making it difficult to directly generalize the findings to patients experiencing their first episode or those with a shorter illness duration. Moreover, the accuracy rates for both the schizophrenia group and the control group were close to or exceeded 96% − 99%, suggesting a potential ceiling effect. This indicates that error rates may not effectively capture differences in impulsivity or executive control deficits between the two groups. Therefore, the conclusions of this study are primarily based on reaction time data and do not involve the measurement of any neural or motivational mechanisms. Future research should employ tasks with greater discriminative power to investigate the related cognitive deficits. Additionally, The percentage of trials excluded due to incorrect responses and extreme values was significantly higher in the schizophrenia group compared to the control group, which may serve as a confounding variable. The retained valid data after exclusions may be biased toward relatively better-performing trials within the schizophrenia group, potentially leading to an overestimation of their overall cognitive abilities. Future studies should address this potential confounding factor in data analysis, either by including it as a covariate or by employing supplementary analyses with metrics less sensitive to trial exclusions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that the modulatory effect of positive emotion on the IOR is influenced by arousal level and exhibits stage-specific asymmetry across attentional processing stages. During the attention disengagement stage, healthy individuals may be more easily drawn to positive high arousal stimuli, whereas patients with schizophrenia are not. During the attentional reorientation stage, healthy individuals may show an approach preferences toward positive high arousal targets, while patients with schizophrenia may display a weakened approach tendency.
