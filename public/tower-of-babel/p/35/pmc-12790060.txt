Impact of Genetic Testing Family Screening in Hereditary Transthyretin Amyloidosis
Authors: Nerea Mora-Ayestarán, Celia Gil-Llopis, Clea González-Maniega, Maria Victoria Piovano, Daniel Águila-Gordo, Ana Briceño, Emiliano González-Vioque, Fernando Domínguez, Esther González-López, Pablo Garcia-Pavia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical AbstractKey Words: amyloidosis, diagnosis, genetic carriers, genetics, mutation, screening, transthyretin

Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTRv) is an autosomal dominant, multisystem, adult-onset disease caused by variants in the transthyretin (TTR) gene. Sequencing of the TTR gene is recommended for patients with ATTR cardiomyopathy regardless of age to differentiate between wild-type ATTR and ATTRv. Once ATTRv is identified in an index case, genetic testing and counselling are recommended for at-risk adult family members.1,2 Identifying genetic carriers through family screening enables regular monitoring and early treatment for affected relatives.1 However, no studies have examined the adoption and clinical impact of genetic testing among relatives. In this study, we sought to determine the uptake and clinical impact of genetic testing in relatives of probands with ATTRv, as well as the factors associated with genetic screening within affected families.

We studied consecutive ATTRv patients and their relatives evaluated at Hospital Universitario Puerta de Hierro (Madrid, Spain), a nonendemic area, from 2010 to 2023. The study was approved by our ethics committee and complied with the Helsinki Declaration. Probands were defined as the first individuals in the family with a pathogenic TTR variant identified. Cascade genetic testing was recommended for adult at-risk relatives. Carriers of the TTR variant received genetic counseling and were included in follow-up protocols according to clinical recommendations.2 ATTRv diagnosis required a pathogenic TTR variant and neurological and/or cardiac involvement based on established criteria.2, 3, 4 Data were extracted from medical records and through interviews with probands and relatives.

Clinical outcomes included number of: 1) relatives who underwent genetic screening; 2) genetic carriers identified; 3) carriers with ATTRv manifestations; 4) noncarrier relatives discharged from follow-up; 5) relatives undergoing preimplantation testing; and 6) age at diagnosis and treatment initiation.

We also analyzed predictors of genetic testing uptake, including proband characteristics (age and phenotype at diagnosis), relative’s residence (same vs different province/country than the proband), and relative’s demographics (sex, age, relationship to proband).

Age groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test given the heterogeneity found in familial relationships with regards to age. Univariable and multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of genetic screening uptake. Data were analyzed using Stata statistical software version 15.0 (StataCorp), and results were presented as the OR with 95% CI.

Cascade genetic screening was indicated for 749 adult at-risk relatives from 86 families (Figure 1). Genetic testing was conducted in 464 (62%), whereas 239 (31.9%) did not undergo genetic screening, and information was not available for 46 (6.1%). The median age of tested relatives was 46 (Q1-Q3: 34-57 years), and 234 (50.4%) were male. Val50Met (V30M) was the most frequent variant (44.2%), followed by Val142Ile (V122I) (30.2%). The median number of screened relatives per family was 5 (range 0-22). A total of 201 subjects (43.3%) at risk (50.3% male, median age 45 [Q1-Q3: 34-57] years) were identified as genetic carriers (median 2 per family, range 0-13). Clinical evaluation revealed 67 carriers (33%) with ATTRv manifestations (55.2% male, median age 53 [Q1-Q3: 45-61] years). Manifestations included neurologic (79%), cardiac (64%), and ophthalmologic (12%). Of these, 95% (52/55 with available treatment data) initiated disease-modifying therapies.

Affected relatives were diagnosed and started disease-modifying therapies at a significantly earlier age than probands (median age 53 [45-61] years vs 66 [56-73] years; P < 0.001 and 56.5 [49-63] years vs 66 [58-74] years; P < 0.001, respectively). Adjusting by age of diagnosis of probands in each family, relatives were diagnosed 2.1 ± 11.6 years earlier than their probands. Preimplantation genetic testing was performed in 7 variant carriers.

Multivariable analysis identified older age in the proband (OR: 1.06; 95% CI: 1.02-1.10) and relative (OR: 1.04; 95% CI: 1.03-1.06), living in a different province or country than the proband (OR: 2.40; 95% CI: 1.36-4.24 and OR: 22.40; 95% CI: 8.94-56.14, respectively), male sex (OR: 2.17; 95% CI: 1.33-3.54), and a distant relationship with the proband (OR: 5.86; 95% CI: 3.16-10.85) as independent predictors of absence of genetic screening among relatives.

Focusing on the 2 most frequent variants, Val50Met and Val142Ile, 107 of 161 at-risk relatives (66.5%) from 26 families with Val142Ile and 230 of 337 at-risk relatives (68.2%) from 38 families with Val50Met were evaluated. Genetic screening was negative in 68 (63.6%) and 134 (58.3%) individuals, respectively. Among positive individuals, 8 Val142Ile and 33 Val50Met individuals had ATTRv manifestations. Affected relatives were diagnosed and started disease-modifying therapies at a significant earlier median age than probands in Val142Ile: 72.5 [69-77] years vs 60 [49.5-63.5] years and 72 [68-76] years vs 61 [50-62] years; P = 0.005 and P = 0.003, respectively. In Val50Met, although affected relatives were diagnosed at an earlier median age than probands (66 [60-73] years vs 60 [52-70] years; P = 0.039), age at initiation of therapy did not differ (67 [59-74] years vs 63 [59-73] years; P = 0.49). Factors associated with absence of genetic testing in relatives were similar to those identified in the general cohort except for male sex and geographic distance to proband in Val50Met.

Timely identification of patients with ATTR amyloidosis has become increasingly important, as available therapies are more effective when initiated early.5 Identification of genetic carriers at-risk of developing ATTRv is particularly important because it allows periodic surveillance, facilitating early initiation of disease-modifying medications. This study provides important data supporting systematic genetic screening in ATTRv families. It shows the positive impact of systematic genetic screening as affected relatives were diagnosed at a younger age and initiated disease-modifying treatments earlier compared with probands. Genetic screening also offers opportunities for genetic counseling, family/reproductive planning and patient support.

Our study also highlights that one-third of relatives did not undergo genetic testing and identifies factors associated with absence of genetic testing. Based on the factors identified, we believe that future interventions to increase genetic testing uptake would require tools to facilitate education and disease awareness among distant relatives and to find complementary information pathways besides interactions within family members.

This study has some limitations. The study was carried out in a reference center located in a nonendemic area, and its findings cannot be extrapolated to other settings. The potential impact of individual-level factors—such as education, employment, and race—on genetic screening uptake was not evaluated. In addition, unmeasured family-related influences not captured by the included covariates may have affected the results. Detailed data on disease staging in relatives were not available.

In conclusion, systematic cascade genetic screening identifies a median of 2 genetic carriers per proband in ATTRv families. Familial genetic screening enables earlier diagnosis and initiation of disease-modifying medications in affected relatives. Despite its benefits, one-third of relatives did not undergo testing. It is essential to develop strategies to facilitate genetic screening in families with ATTRv.
