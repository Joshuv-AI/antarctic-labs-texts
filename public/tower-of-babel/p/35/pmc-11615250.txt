Modeling carbon intensity of electric vehicles in the well-to-wheels phase under different traffic flow conditions
Authors: Yaping Dong, Xin Guo, Meng Wang, Jinliang Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryWith the proliferation of electric vehicles (EVs), accurately assessing their well-to-wheels carbon intensity is crucial. In this study, a quadratic model was developed, integrating a top-down carbon accounting approach with field test data, to quantify the carbon intensity of electric vehicles under various traffic flow conditions. The model employs the traffic saturation metric, specifically the v/C ratio, to characterize the traffic conditions. The findings revealed that electric vehicles exhibit lower carbon intensity in congested or unsteady traffic compared to free-flow or steady-flow conditions. Specifically, minimum emissions are achieved at a v/C ratio of 0.96. In contrast to internal combustion engine vehicles, the carbon intensity of electric vehicles is less impacted by traffic congestion, particularly at v/C values exceeding 0.75. Maintaining the v/C values between 0.15 and 0.75 can promote low-carbon operation in mixed traffic conditions. These insights are crucial for reducing carbon emissions in mixed traffic comprising diverse powertrains.Subject areas: Applied sciences, Engineering, Transportation engineering, Green engineering

Introduction

The continuous increase in internal combustion engine vehicles (ICEVs) in China has resulted in sustained pressure on oil supplies, leading to significant energy shortages and environmental pollution. The introduction of electric vehicles (EVs) has enhanced the vehicle energy utilization structure. The driving energy for EVs is derived from the electricity supplied by motors, resulting in high energy conversion efficiency and low carbon intensity during vehicle operation. Incorporating EVs into the vehicle fleet is an effective strategy to achieve low-carbon transportation.1,2 The electricity used to drive EVs does not directly result in carbon emissions. Carbon dioxide is released into the atmosphere during electricity generation by the National Grid using various conventional fossil fuels such as coal and oil. The GREET model developed by Argonne National Laboratory in the United States indicates that fuel production, vehicle operation, and all vehicle-associated activities should be considered when determining the carbon intensity from vehicle fuel consumption.3 This phase that comprises the entire life cycle of a vehicle’s energy use is known as the well-to-wheels (WTW) phase and is used to determine the carbon intensity of a vehicle. The WTW phase comprises two stages: the well-to-pump (WTP) and the pump to wheels (PTW). The type and ratio of energy sources during the power generation process and grid transmission efficiency, as well as variations in driving energy due to the technical characteristics of EV vehicles should be considered when quantitatively determining the carbon intensity of EVs in the WTW phase.4,5 Understanding carbon intensity of EVs in the WTW phase is essential for developing low-carbon travel options.

Various methods have been developed to determine the carbon emission levels for EVs at the product and fuel consumption levels to provide guidelines for standardized determination of carbon intensity. The Argonne National Laboratory in the United States established the GREET model for evaluating carbon intensity throughout the vehicle’s life cycle.6 The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) recommended the carbon estimation method,7 which is commonly used to estimate carbon emissions from energy consumption. Assessment of the carbon footprint for EVs using the GREET model demonstrates that the carbon dioxide emissions during the fuel cycle are significant. Using clean energy for electricity production reduces the carbon intensity resulting from power consumption during driving.8 The carbon emissions of EVs throughout the vehicle’s life cycle are approximately half the level resulting from ICEVs.9 This difference is mainly attributed to the high efficiency of electric motors,10 which helps in conserving electricity. Moreover, the high fuel consumption due to the low fuel efficiency of internal combustion locomotive engines, results in high carbon intensity. The carbon intensity of EVs, for the average energy consumption of a unit load vehicle traveling 100 km, is approximately 55.90% lower compared to that of ICEVs during the fuel cycle.11 Notably, the optimization of EV configuration technologies is a continuous process and each country’s unique energy generation structure and grid transmission losses contribute to spatial and temporal variations in EV carbon intensity during the fuel cycle.12,13 Additionally, the traffic flow conditions significantly affect the carbon intensity of individual vehicles.14

Several on-road vehicle carbon emission prediction studies have focused on the theoretical modeling from energy consumption and it impact on carbon emissions, revealing the quantitative relationship between kinematic parameters and carbon emissions. Various detailed models of tailpipe emissions have been developed based on the vehicle dynamics theory and dynamometer test databases. Varying parameters have been used to characterize the vehicle operating conditions in these models. The COPERT-IV model uses average speed,15 the IVE is constructed using fine-grained inputs, including instantaneous speed intervals,16 and MOVES is based on specific power and instantaneous speed data to simulate vehicle dynamics.17 Prediction using COPERT and IVE models indicate lower vehicle carbon intensity than measured values during traffic congestion conditions. These prediction errors are attributed to frequent disruptions and congestion events that are not adequately accounted for in the models.18 Pan et al.19 established a gradient-enhanced regression tree model to estimate the carbon intensity of a new energy bus operating on a fixed track. Speed, time, acceleration, longitudinal gradient, and vehicle load data were used in the model construction. Dreier et al.20 used the advanced vehicle simulator (ADVISOR) to estimate the quantitative impacts of the urban driving cycle, passenger load, and elevation profile on the energy consumption of different buses. Fiori et al.21 conducted a sensitivity analysis using a power consumption model for electric trucks and observed that the driving patterns, driving power, assistance systems, and vehicle load significantly impact on driving energy. These single-vehicle carbon emission models and modeling parameters can serve as references for the quantitative study of carbon emissions from road traffic flow.

The operating conditions of an individual vehicle in traffic flow are significantly correlated with its carbon intensity. Vehicle operating conditions are typically reflected by kinematic parameters such as speed, acceleration rate, and deceleration rate.22 Dong et al.14 observed that drivers typically drive at lower speeds during congested flow, exhibiting frequent acceleration and deceleration patterns. Conversely, under free-flow traffic conditions, drivers can freely drive at different speeds, with occasional acceleration and deceleration patterns observed at higher speeds. Low-speed driving with stop-and-go patterns in congested flow conditions leads to higher carbon intensity than high-speed driving under free flow traffic conditions. Chen et al.23 recorded data on light-duty gasoline vehicle emissions during real-world traffic congestion and observed that a combination of high traffic volumes and low speeds increases transportation-related carbon emissions. Kuppili et al.24 observed that passenger cars driven at speeds between 65 and 95 km/h and accelerations between −0.5 and 0.5 m/s2 exhibited the lowest carbon intensity. Gao et al.25 recommended higher speeds for eco-driving, as they lead to lower diesel consumption and lower tailpipe emissions, with an optimal speed range of 70–110 km/h according to globally harmonized light vehicle test cycles. Chen et al.23 analyzed data retrieved from a real-world traffic monitoring database and the results indicated that frequent acceleration and deceleration pattern caused by traffic congestion significantly increased fuel consumption in light-duty gasoline vehicles. Fiori et al.26 used the CO2MPAS model and demonstrated that EVs consumed less electricity under low congestion than under free-flow driving conditions. Conversely, ICEVs exhibited lowest fuel consumption around 90 km/h, with higher fuel consumption rates observed under congested and free-flow traffic conditions. Mamarikas et al.27 reported that speed restrictions in urban traffic reduce the electrical consumption of EVs while increasing the speed limit and reducing the fuel consumption of ICEVs. Relieving traffic congestion can save 13%, 25%, and 17% of fuel consumption for electric, gasoline, and diesel vehicles, respectively.

Previous findings indicate a strong correlation between vehicle carbon intensity and traffic flow.14,28 ICEVs exhibit higher carbon intensity under free flow conditions and congested flow conditions, but have lower emissions under steady flow conditions. Higher carbon intensity observed under free flow conditions are attributed to higher speeds and sharp acceleration events. Conversely, carbon emission hotspots observed under road congestion flow conditions are attributed to frequent acceleration and/or deceleration characterized by stop-and-go events.14 Theoretical models based on vehicle dynamics indicate that engine efficiency and fuel type also affect carbon intensity.29 EVs and ICEVs exhibit differences in engine efficiency and fuel type. It is imperative to explore the impact of varying traffic flow conditions under mixed traffic on carbon intensity. Understanding the impact of traffic flow conditions on carbon intensity can provide a basis for designing strategies to save energy and reduce carbon emissions during road transportation operations. Consequently, it imperative to quantify the carbon intensity of EVs under different traffic flow conditions and to explore the differences in carbon intensities between EVs and ICEVs.

The objective of this study was to assess the carbon intensity of EVs under different traffic flow conditions, establish a prediction model, and explore the carbon intensity patterns of EVs under different traffic flow conditions. In addition, the study sought to compare the carbon intensity of EVs with vehicles using traditional internal combustion engines under varying traffic flow conditions. These findings will provide a scientific basis for formulating energy-saving and carbon-reducing strategies for mixed transportation. The carbon intensity for a vehicle during the WTW phase, which includes the WTP and PTW phases, was evaluated in this study. WTP emissions are emissions generated from the fuel production and operation activities, including fuel mining, refining, and transmission. PTW emissions resulting from vehicle activity and vehicle operational performance.5 The differences in carbon intensities from transportation using vehicles with different fuel types can be compared by determining carbon intensity of EVs in the WTW phase. Notably, the traffic saturation parameter v/C precisely reflects the traffic carrying capacity and is not affected by different road conditions (such as number of lanes, speed and vehicle composition). Consequently, v/C was chosen as the classification parameter for different traffic flow conditions in this study. The v represents the actual traffic flow and C denotes the maximum capacity of the lane traffic.30 Chinese traffic management authorities use v/C to characterize the actual traffic flow conditions.31 In this study, v/C was used as an explanatory parameter to establish a carbon intensity model for EVs under different traffic flow conditions. The model was used to explore the differences in carbon intensity patterns between EVs and conventional fuel vehicles. The proposed model demonstrates a relationship between traffic flow conditions and the carbon intensity of EVs. The research results provide a foundation for ensuring low-carbon emissions in mixed traffic, which has robust practical significance.

The rest of this article is organized as follows: In the “results” section, the carbon intensity of EVs in the WTW phase is assessed based on the current power generation status and vehicle characteristics in China. The power consumption of EVs under different traffic flow conditions was measured through field tests and the carbon intensity was determined. In “data analysis” section, a prediction model for predicting the carbon intensity of EVs under different traffic flow conditions is presented. In addition, the difference in carbon intensity patterns between EVs and ICEVs is presented in this section. The “discussion” section presents an extensive discussion of the findings, and the "conclusions" section presents a summary of the research results.

Results

This study aims to elucidate the carbon intensity of EVs during the WTW phase, influenced by factors such as the current state of power generation in China’s power grid and the technical characteristics of EVs. A field test was conducted under different traffic flow conditions to determine the carbon intensity of EVs during the WTW phase, which corresponds to the energy consumption by EVs. The carbon intensity considered in this study includes emissions from the fuel cycle and vehicle operation phase, which is consistent with the carbon accounting scope for the WTW phase in the GREET model. The WTW phase comprises the WTP and PTW stages, corresponding to the fuel cycle and vehicle operation phase, respectively.

The IPCC accounting method was chosen to determine the carbon intensity derived from power consumption since there is no precise test specification for EV fuel cycle carbon intensity. The carbon intensity associated with China’s grid electricity consumption was calculated based on China’s current energy ratio and power generation efficiency. The considered carbon intensity was then compared with the carbon intensity of EVs within the WTP phase determined using the GREET model. The energy demand and carbon intensity of the current configuration for EVs were characterized by considering the efficiency of grid transmission to the battery, the efficiency of the EV powertrain, and the energy recovery rate of the EV.

Accounting between power consumption and carbon intensity of EVs

EVs operate without tailpipe emissions, implying that EVs do not release carbon emissions during the PTW phase. Notably, the energy required to power EVs is derived from power plants, and the associated carbon intensity cannot be overlooked. This implies that carbon emissions are generated when producing the energy used by EVs.3 Carbon emissions are equivalent to the product of the electrical energy consumption used to power the vehicle and the carbon intensity. The amount of electricity consumed by a vehicle is determined by the operational status and performance of the EV during the operational phase.

When assessing the carbon intensity of EVs during the WTW phase, special attention should be given to the four phases that affect carbon intensity. First, the carbon intensity associated with energy generation. Second, the energy losses during the transmission of power from the grid to the batteries. Thirdly, the energy efficiency of the EVs during operation. Lastly, the energy recovery during the use of brakes in EVs.

Carbon intensity of electricity generation

The carbon intensity of national grid generation actually depends on the energy mix and generation technologies used for grid generation, as well as the power losses during generation. The current energy mix of China’s power grid generation is shown in Table 1.32 The Guidelines for Accounting for GHG Emissions of Chinese Enterprises33 indicate that the grid power generation efficiency ng-gene is 58.275%. In addition, the standard value of the average carbon intensity for power generation from the national grid is 0.5810 kgCO2/kWh and is set to be widely implemented across the country. This means that the carbon dioxide emissions implied in the consumption of per unit of power generated (1 kWh) is 0.5810 kg. Notably, 0.5810 kgCO2/kWh was adopted as the carbon intensity cg-gene for China’s power grid considering the rapid development of greening grid power generation. The average carbon intensity of grid electricity generation reflects the current carbon intensity from China’s national grid. The average carbon intensity value of the grid power generation is approximately equal to the product of the national grid power generation efficiency and the carbon intensity of coal-only power generation. The carbon intensity of coal-only power generation in the Chinese grid is 0.997 kgCO2/kWh. Enhancing the greening and efficiency of energy sources could reduce carbon emissions during the operation phase of EVs.34

Field test

The carbon intensity patterns of EVs under different traffic flow conditions can be evaluated based on the measured kinematic parameters and electrical consumption data. The field test program should be precisely designed and controlled to exclude the interference of other factors, thus ensuring the credibility of the field test data.

Evaluating electrical energy consumption

The driving energy required for vehicles driven on actual roads is influenced by traffic flow conditions, route alignment, road pavement type, weather conditions, and other factors.14,37,39 The field test was conducted by randomly following cars along the road to obtain the actual power consumption data of EV under different traffic flow conditions, accurately representing the actual road driving conditions. The Tesla Model 3 rear-wheel drive model, which is currently popular in China,42 was used as the test vehicle. The on-board diagnostics (OBD) port was used to record the vehicle speed, mileage, and power consumption data on the trip computer. The carbon intensity model specified in Equation 10 was used to determine the carbon intensity of the EV under power consumption.

Ten test vehicles were used to measure speed fluctuations and power consumption data. The test vehicles were temporarily parked on the emergency lanes after obtaining the approval of the traffic management department. This setup facilitated the test vehicles to join the traffic flow and travel normally alongside the traffic flow. Each test vehicle randomly dispersed within the same segment of traffic flow. For each set of data, the average value was calculated after excluding outliers. On-site tests were conducted intermittently under various traffic flow conditions. The sample size basically covered all traffic flow conditions. Each v/C ratio has at least 38 valid measurement data from vehicles.

The electric energy consumption rate and carbon intensity of EVs under different traffic flow conditions were expressed per 100 km to ensure that the test results were comparable across different test cases despite variations in driving mileage.

Basic highway segments were selected for the tests to ensure that traffic flow conditions were the only variable affecting carbon intensity, while excluding the effect of roadway facility conditions on traffic flow speeds. The basic highway section exhibited a straight design without long vertical slopes or sharp curves. The roadway had no construction zones, entrance/exit ramps, bridges or tunnel structures. The test was conducted under clear weather to avoid the impact of adverse weather. The ambient temperature ranged from 17.95°C to 21.64°C, which is within the effective range of energy efficiency for EVs.43

Measurement of traffic flow conditions

The Highway Capacity Manual44 implemented in the United States and the Technical Standards for Highway Engineering30 implemented in China use different indicators, including density (passenger car/km/lane), v/C, and the average speed of passenger cars, to determine the congestion level of roads. v/C is the ratio of the maximum service traffic volume to the base capacity under base conditions. Base capacity is defined as the maximum hourly traffic volume at the grade 5 service level.

Table 4 presents the classification of service level and traffic flow conditions of Chinese expressways based on the v/C index. Free flow conditions correspond to the grade-1 service level, where there is no interference between vehicles. In addition, at the grade 1 service level, vehicles travel at high speed with frequent and aggressive acceleration and deceleration. Steady flow conditions correspond to the grade 2 and grade 3 service levels, where the speed of a single vehicle can affect the speed of nearby vehicles. Unstable flow conditions correspond to the grade 4 service level, where the speed of the preceding and adjacent traffic determines the travel speed. Congested flow conditions correspond to the grade 5 and grade 6 service levels, where any traffic accident leads to severe blockages and queues, resulting in extreme restrictions on the freedom of movement, with speed changes causing stop-and-go waves.14

v/C and maximum traffic under different traffic flow conditions

Using traffic density and average speed as indicators may not precisely reflect the differences between different road sections due to the different design speeds, number of lanes, and traffic composition of different highways. v/C indicator precisely indicates the actual traffic carrying capacity under different road conditions,31 ensuring the practicality of the study results. In the field test, the v/C value was maintained at a minimum interval not greater than 0.04 to ensure reliable data volume.

A portable laser roadside traffic survey instrument was used to record the actual road traffic flow v/C values of the test section. The instrument uses two laser sensors to provide signals. It captures and records the passing time, driving direction, lane position, instantaneous speed, time headway, vehicle type, body length, and other parameters of vehicles in the traffic flow. After setting up and switching on the equipment and initiating the data acquisition process, the observer withdraws and remains concealed to avoid influencing the driving behavior of vehicles along the road.

To collect a set of traffic volume data from the same segment of traffic flow, three observation points are set up at the start, midpoint, and end of the test road segment. At the first point, the observer selects two distinctive vehicles with a half-hour interval to determine the start and end positions of a specific segment of traffic flow. Observers at the subsequent two points identify the same segment of traffic flow by locating these two characteristic vehicles. (see the STAR Methods section for more details).

Data analysis

The speed variation and power consumption of the EVs under different traffic flow conditions were determined during the field test. Subsequently, the carbon intensity was then evaluated. Carbon intensity was evaluated based on the field test data to explore the effects of multiple factors. A multifactor variable screening modeling was established. Correlation and multicollinearity analyses using the independent variables to evaluate the correlation between multiple factors. A model for predicting the carbon intensity for EVs under different traffic flow conditions was established, with v/C as the independent variable.

Analysis of CO2 affected by multiple factors

The vehicle energy consumption and dynamics theory indicates that vehicle energy consumption is determined by driving resistance, and the engine provides the energy required for driving. Conventional quantitative prediction models reveal the influence of kinematic parameters, such as average speed,15 acceleration and deceleration,17,19 on vehicle carbon intensity. The effect of traffic flow conditions on single-vehicle driving operations should also be considered when determining the actual roadway carbon intensity.14,18,22 These findings indicate that vehicle speed, acceleration, deceleration and traffic flow conditions affect the energy consumption while driving, causing a variation in EV power consumption.

The variation in kinematic parameters and carbon intensity with traffic flow is shown in Figure 1. A higher velocity density in a region of the kernel density plot indicates a denser velocity distribution measured during the field test. The carbon intensity profile shows a decreasing trend until a v/C value of approximately 0.95 is achieved. The left side of the carbon intensity curve corresponds to the high-speed driving scenario characterized by rapid acceleration. The acceleration fluctuates over a wide range, from 0.186 to 0.572 m/s2, and the deceleration shows minima variation, below 0.211 m/s2. The speed increases with a decrease in the v/C value and the acceleration choices are more flexible.14 The energy consumption is partly due to the power load caused by high-speed driving, and partly attributed to the inertial drag caused by the flexible acceleration driving behavior. Moreover, the energy demand of other driving resistance is part of the consumed energy. The speed of the traffic flow is relatively stable when the v/C value is close to 0.95, and the vehicles follow the preceding vehicle at a uniform speed.

The stability of the traffic flow speed is affected when the v/C value exceeds 0.95. At this phase, the vehicles repeatedly exhibit stop-and-go driving scenario. In addition, deceleration occurs frequently and the speed fluctuates from 0.615 to 2.320 m/s2. Acceleration is minimal and the speed is below 0.267 m/s2. At higher v/C values, vehicles interfere with each other and the speeding options are significantly limited, resulting in lower speeds.14 Moreover, frequent vehicle acceleration and deceleration occur, with short periods of acceleration and deceleration. EVs are equipped with motors that work efficiently even at low speeds while accelerating.12 Furthermore, the energy recovery system of EVs recovers the energy lost during braking. Consequently, the carbon intensity exhibits an increasing trend but with slight increases, which is different from the significant increase in the carbon intensity observed in ICEVs.

Carbon intensity rules

Discussion

This study proposes a model for predicting the carbon intensity of EVs during the WTW phase. The prediction is based on the power generation and transmission efficiency of the Chinese power grid as well as the power and energy recovery abilities of EVs. The model for predicting carbon intensity of EVs during the WTW phase was established using field test data under different traffic flow conditions, with the traffic flow parameter v/C as the independent variable.

Significant variations in carbon intensity were observed between EVs and ICEVs under different traffic flow conditions. The carbon intensity profile of EVs exhibited a quadratic curve. The carbon intensity of EVs was highest under free traffic flow conditions. The carbon intensity decreases as the traffic flow became congested until it reached a minimum at a v/C value of 0.96. The difference between the average carbon intensity of EVs under congested and unstable flow conditions was minimal. A previous study demonstrated that the carbon intensity curve of ICEVs exhibits a “U” shape, with the lowest carbon intensity observed at v/C values ranging from 0.4–0.6.14 The curvature of the carbon intensity curve for EVs was lower compared to the curvature of the carbon intensity curves for gasoline and diesel vehicles.

Under steady and free flow traffic conditions, with v/C values between 0.15 and 0.75, the curvature of the carbon intensity curves for both EVs and ICEs was reduced, promoting low-carbon mixed traffic. The carbon intensity of ICEVs increased significantly under unstable and congested traffic conditions. Conversely, the carbon intensity of EVs exhibited a gradual increase under unstable and congested traffic conditions. These findings indicate that the carbon intensity of EVs fluctuates less under congested traffic conditions. As the v/C value exceeds 0.75 and continues to increase, the traffic flow becomes more congested and vehicles are unable to maintain a high, steady speed. Consequently, the vehicle speed gradually decreases, exhibiting a high acceleration and deceleration frequency. Frequent acceleration and deceleration generate inertial resistance, increasing driving energy, ultimately resulting in higher carbon intensity. The fuel efficiency of internal combustion engines decreases significantly with the rapid acceleration of ICEVs,29 leading to a substantial increase in carbon intensity. In contrast, EVs are equipped with stable electric motors that perform efficiently during low-speed acceleration.5 In addition, these vehicles have energy recovery systems that minimize braking energy losses during frequent decelerations.41 These features minimize the impact of rapid acceleration and deceleration on the carbon intensity of EVs, resulting in less fluctuation in their carbon intensity.

Notably, the high number of EVs in the fleet can reduce carbon intensity observed during traffic congestion and aggressive high-speed driving. Further research should aim to enhance the generalizability and accuracy of the model by considering diverse EV models and more comprehensive data sources. Additionally, it is imperative to conduct research on predicting emission characteristics for other types of new energy vehicles.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a carbon intensity prediction model for EVs under different traffic flow conditions on highways, using v/C as the explanatory variable. The model uses a quadratic function to predict the carbon intensity of EVs during the WTW phase. The model shows that the carbon intensity of EVs in China is 0.629 kgCO2/kWh, which is higher than the level of EVs in the United States (0.427 kgCO2/kWh). The model’s v/C ranged between 0.15 and 1.2 due to time and cost limitations. It is worth noting that by combining the carbon intensity prediction model with the carbon intensity accounting method, the corresponding driving energy of EVs can be derived. The results showed consistent pattern changes of EV driving energy and carbon intensity.

The field test results showed that EVs have the highest carbon intensity under the free-flow conditions, followed by the steady-flow conditions, whereas carbon intensity was lowest under the unsteady and congested traffic flow conditions. The carbon emissions of EVs decreased monotonically under steady and free flow conditions, with v/C value ranging from 0.15 to 0.96. The carbon intensity of EVs was minimized when the v/C value was 0.96. Under the same vehicle load and operating conditions (speed, road conditions, wind speed, etc.), traffic flow had the least impact on the carbon intensity of EVs, followed by diesel vehicles, whereas gasoline vehicles were the most affected. The carbon intensity of EVs was less affected by traffic congestion, especially at v/C values exceeding 0.75, compared to ICEVs. This difference is observed mainly because EVs are equipped with stable electric motors that work efficiently at low speeds during acceleration. Moreover, EVs have an energy recovery system that minimizes the braking energy loss during frequent deceleration. The carbon intensity curves exhibited smaller curvature for both EVs and ICEVs when the v/C values were between 0.15 and 0.75.

The prediction model and the carbon intensity patterns reported in this study provide theoretical guidance and a foundation for formulating low-carbon management strategies for mixed traffic flow on highways.

Limitations of the study

The model established in this study should be applied carefully as the study has some limitations. For instance, the data were only collected from a specific Tesla EV model and may not be applicable to other vehicle models. In addition, the parameter values adopted in the top-down carbon accounting method, which correspond to the current power generation status in China, will limit the applicability of the results in other scenarios. Moreover, the modeling of traffic flow and emissions was simplified and other factors that were not included might affect the carbon intensity of EVs. Further research should be conducted to enhance the generalizability and accuracy of the model by considering diverse EV models and robust data sources. This study sought to quantify the impact of traffic flow conditions on carbon intensity in the WTW phase of EVs, focusing on the determination of the carbon intensity in the WTW phase. Consequently, vehicle manufacturing and battery recycling were not considered in the study. Studies should be conducted to integrate the carbon intensity for the entire life cycle of new energy vehicles, revealing the carbon footprint patterns of vehicle production, operation, and recycling.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ten male Asian drivers, each with 8–20 years of driving experience and aged between 28 and 45 years, participated in this study. Each driver was required to drive the test vehicle normally, following the traffic flow and avoiding aggressive or incorrect driving behaviors. There are no specific standards for driving behavior, but we have observed and subsequently eliminated the datasets in which the test vehicle’s driving was disrupted due to interference from aggressive or incorrect driving by other vehicles. Before the field test, the testing procedure was verbally explained and written informed consent was obtained.

Method details

Observation of traffic volume data within the test road section

In field experiments, our primary concern is to ensure the uniformity of the measurement object, which means guaranteeing that the traffic volume data collected is from the same section of traffic flow. The portable laser roadside traffic survey instrument can measure parameters such as the passing time, driving direction, instantaneous speed, headway, time interval, and vehicle type of each vehicle in the traffic flow, fulfilling the requirements for observing traffic volume data.

Three collection points for roadside traffic survey instruments are set up in the test road segment, located at the starting point, the middle, and the ending point respectively. To ensure that the traffic flow collected at the three observation points is from the same segment, we use the same characteristic vehicle as a marker at each measurement point. In other words, the start and end of traffic flow data collection at each measurement point are controlled by the same characteristic vehicle. The characteristic vehicle should be conspicuous and quickly identifiable. At the start of the test, the first group of observers selects the characteristic vehicle and communicates the vehicle’s characteristics and license plate number to the observers in the second and third groups. Each group of observers records the measurement start time upon seeing the first characteristic vehicle, and then records the end time upon seeing another characteristic vehicle. In the traffic flow state parameter v/C, v refers to the hourly traffic volume in the actual road segment. For the convenience of measurement, the first group of observers selects a characteristic vehicle to mark the end of the experiment after half an hour. Through the above experimental process control, it can basically ensure that the observation objects at each observation point are from the same segment of traffic flow. This cycle of data collection continues until the total sample traffic volume basically covers all traffic flow states. During the field test, observers should stand outside the roadside guardrail to avoid any impact on the driving behavior of the drivers.

Equipment used in the experiment

The AxleLightRLU11 vehicle classification statistical instrument was chosen to carry out on-site traffic flow measurements utilizing laser technology. The data analysis in this study is based on IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 and the graphing tool is origin2024.

Verifying normality of carbon intensity data

Based on the central limit theorem of probability theory, the vehicle carbon intensity can be regarded as a random variable, and the carbon intensity data measured through field test should theoretically conform to a normal distribution. By statistically analyzing and testing whether the carbon intensity data follows a normal distribution, the reliability of the overall data can be theoretically verified.

The sample size of valid carbon intensity data for each test group is controlled with a threshold of 38. For sample sizes less than 50, the Shapiro-Wilk test is applied to evaluate the normality of the carbon intensity data distribution. If the p-value obtained from the Shapiro-Wilk test is less than the predetermined significance level, the assumption of normal distribution is rejected; otherwise, the sample is deemed to follow a normal distribution.
