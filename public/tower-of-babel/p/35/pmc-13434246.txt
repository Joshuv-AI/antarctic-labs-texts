Association between thrombocytopaenia and Plasmodium falciparum infection in Ghana
Authors: Richard Vikpebah Duneeh, Shadrack Tetteh Amoada, Elizabeth Yankey, Joshua Appiah Asante, Emmanuel Alote Allotey, Elliot Elikplim Akorsu, Mercy Adzo Klugah, Kenneth Ablordey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum is a major global health challenge, often causing significant haematological disturbances. These changes, such as anaemia and thrombocytopenia, reflect the host’s response to infection and can influence disease severity and diagnosis. This study aimed to evaluate haematological alterations in patients with P. falciparum malaria and to identify factors associated with P. falciparum infection across different localities in Ghana. A comparative cross-sectional study was conducted from June to September 2025 across three sites in Ghana (Ashaiman, Kwesimintsim, and Kyebi). The study involved 388 participants, comprising P. falciparum malaria-positive individuals and malaria-negative using convenience sampling technique. Malaria was diagnosed using standard microscopy, and haematological parameters (haemoglobin, platelet count, white blood cell count, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR]) were measured using automated analysers and the Westergren method. Data on demographic characteristics were obtained from laboratory records. Statistical analysis was performed in R software version 4.5.1, utilizing non-parametric tests, chi-square tests, and binary logistic regression to assess predictors of malaria. Model performance was evaluated using ROC curves. P-value was set at P < 0.050. While overall ESR, WBC counts, and haemoglobin levels showed no significant differences between non-malaria and P. falciparum malaria, site-specific variations were observed. Malaria cases in Kwesimintim had higher ESR, and those in Kyebi had lower haemoglobin compared to their non-malaria counterparts. The most consistent finding was thrombocytopaenia; platelet counts were significantly lower in malaria cases across all subgroups, with an overall median of 152.0 × 10⁹/L versus 234.0 × 10⁹/L in controls (p < 0.001). In regression analysis, thrombocytopenia showed the strongest association with P. falciparum malaria (adjusted Odds Ratio = 11.8; 95% CI: 6.18–24.7; p < 0.001). A model incorporating haematological indices demonstrated superior predictive performance (AUC = 77.6%) compared to a model based on sociodemographic factors alone (AUC = 57.9%). Furthermore, increasing parasite density was associated with higher ESR, lower platelet counts, and reduced haemoglobin, with more pronounced effects in females. This study confirms that thrombocytopaenia is associated with P. falciparum malaria. The integration of routine haematological parameters, particularly platelet count, into diagnostic algorithms can significantly improve P. falciparum malaria risk stratification and clinical diagnosis, offering a cost-effective advantage in resource-limited settings where automated haematology analysers are increasingly available.Keywords: P. falciparum malaria, Thrombocytopaenia, Multicentre, GhanaSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research, Microbiology, Risk factors

Introduction

Plasmodium falciparum malaria remains one of the most significant infectious diseases globally, accounting for the majority of malaria-related morbidity and mortality, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa1. According to the World Health Organization, there were over 282 million malaria cases and more than 610,000 deaths worldwide in recent years, with approximately 95% of cases and deaths occurring in Africa2. West Africa bears a disproportionate share of this burden due to favorable climatic conditions for transmission, high vector density, and socio-economic challenges that limit effective control measures3. Malaria is associated with severe complications such as cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, metabolic acidosis, and multi-organ failure, particularly among children and pregnant women, making it a critical public health concern in the region4.

In Ghana, P. falciparum malaria continues to be a leading cause of outpatient visits, hospital admissions, and mortality despite sustained control efforts5. The disease exhibits considerable regional variation within the country, with prevalence in the Eastern Region (31%) being substantially higher than in the Western Region (24%) and the urban Greater Accra Region (5%)6,7. These disparities highlight differences in transmission intensity, environmental factors, and access to preventive interventions, underscoring the need for context-specific approaches to malaria control.

A hallmark of malaria pathophysiology is the occurrence of characteristic haematological alterations, including anaemia, thrombocytopenia, leukocyte abnormalities, and elevated inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)8,9. These changes reflect the systemic effects of infection, including immune activation, bone marrow suppression, and peripheral destruction of blood cells. Given their relative ease of assessment, these haematological parameters serve as practical and cost-effective biomarkers in malaria-endemic settings.

Among these, thrombocytopaenia has consistently emerged as a common and early indicator of P. falciparum infection5,10. Its strong association with parasitaemia and the routine availability of platelet count testing make it a valuable adjunct in malaria diagnosis, particularly in resource-limited settings where confirmatory parasitological tests may not be readily accessible11.

Despite the clinical importance of these haematological alterations, there remains a paucity of comprehensive data on their variation across different geographical and epidemiological settings in Ghana9,12. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate haematological alterations particularly platelet count, white blood cell count, ESR, and haemoglobin levels associated with P. falciparum malaria across different geographical areas in Ghana.

Methods and materials

Study design

This study was a comparative cross-sectional study designed to assess the haematological profile and inflammatory marker in P. falciparum malaria and non-malaria cases from June to September 2025.

Study site

This study was a multiple site study conducted in facilities in Ashaiman, Kwesimintsim and Kyebi as shown in Fig. 1, which are situated in the Greater Accra Region, Western Region and Eastern Region respectively. These locations were carefully selected for this study as they are well noted for malaria prevalence over the past 5 years13.

Map of Ghana showing Ashaiman, Kwesimintsim and Kyebi. Map was created using ArcMap software, version 10.8; ESRI, USA. (URL: https://www.esri.com)

Study population

The study constituted individuals who visited laboratories in Ashaiman, Kwesimintim and Kyebi for laboratory diagnosis of malaria.

Eligibility criteria

Participants aged 70 years or younger with a confirmed malaria diagnosis based on microscopy, as well as those without malaria confirmed by microscopy, were included in the study. Participants who received anti-malarial treatment in the past two weeks, had co-infections (e.g., typhoid, dengue, HIV, tuberculosis), were pregnant, had haematological disorders (e.g., sickle cell disease, leukaemia), or had a blood transfusion in the past three months were excluded.

Sampling technique

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach from individuals presenting to the selected health facilities for malaria testing during the study period. Eligible participants primarily comprised symptomatic individuals clinically suspected of having malaria and who had laboratory requests for malaria parasite testing by blood film microscopy. The selected study sites were chosen to represent different geographical settings and healthcare facilities with high malaria case attendance, thereby enabling the recruitment of participants from varied demographic backgrounds and transmission settings. However, because recruitment was facility-based and dependent on healthcare-seeking behaviour, the study population may not fully represent the general population, including asymptomatic individuals or persons without access to healthcare services. Consequently, the findings are more reflective of healthcare-seeking individuals suspected of malaria infection rather than the broader Ghanaian population.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using the Raosoft online sample size calculator (http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html). A minimum sample size of 388 participants was estimated based on a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and an assumed response distribution of 50%, which was selected due to the absence of precise prevalence estimates for the target study population. The population size input of 6.6 million was derived from the estimated annual reported malaria cases in Ghana14 and was used as a pragmatic proxy to reflect the substantial malaria burden in the country rather than a true sampling frame. Since the study employed a facility-based convenience sampling strategy without a defined population registry, the calculated sample size served as an approximate estimate to ensure adequate statistical power for detecting associations. Nonetheless, the non-probability sampling approach may limit the representativeness and external validity of the findings.

Data collection

For the purpose of this research, captured data of study participant’s demographics such as age, gender, and residency were obtained from the Laboratory Management system.

Sample processing and laboratory analysis

Blood smear preparation, staining, and examination

Thick and thin blood smears were prepared on the same slide, air-dried, and stained using 10% Giemsa in phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) following standard World Health Organization protocols15. Thin films were fixed with methanol, while thick films remained unfixed to allow red cell lysis. Microscopic examination was performed under oil immersion (100×) for parasite detection and species identification. Parasite density was determined by counting asexual parasites against white blood cells using World Health Organization protocols15. A slide was considered negative after examining sufficient high-power fields with no parasites detected, and positive results were confirmed by two independent microscopists.

ESR determination

About 1.6 ml parts of whole blood were transferred into 0.4 ml of 3.8% sodium citrate tube, and a Westergren pipette was inserted into the sodium citrate tube. The pipette was gently twisted with spiral pressure to bring the blood level to the zero mark. The setup was left on a flat surface undisturbed for one hour at room temperature.

Haematological biomarkers analysis

The CBC analysis was performed to measure biomarkers such as white blood cell WBC, HB, PLT and HCT, using haematological analyser Sysmex XN-350 analyser at Ashaiman Municipal Hospital, Mindray BC-5000 analyser at Kwesimintim District Hospital and Zybio Z3 at Kyebi Government Hospital for each participant’s sample. Internal quality controls (QA) and the scheduled external quality assessment plans were strictly followed as quality measure.

Definition of operational terms

Anaemia is defined as males with haemoglobin of 13 g/dl, non-pregnant females of 12 g/dl and 11 g/dL for pregnant females and children < 5 years16. Leucopenia is defined as a decrease in the total number of WBCs in the blood, typically below 4000 cells per microliter17. Leucocytosis is defined as a increase in the total number of WBCs in the blood, typically above 4000 cells per microliter17. Thrombocytopaenia is referred to as a platelet count of less than 150,000 per microliter of blood18.Low ESR refers to a slower than normal rate of RBC sedimentation, while high ESR refers to a faster than normal rate, indicating variations in RBC aggregation influenced by plasma proteins.

Data handling and analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and checked for completeness in Microsoft Excel 365 and analyzed using R version 4.5.1. No missing data were identified for the variables included in the analyses. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with P. falciparum infection, with adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Variables included in the multivariable models were selected based on biological plausibility, prior literature. Age and sex were included a priori based on established epidemiological evidence of their association with malaria risk20–23, while locality (town) was included to account for potential geographic variation in malaria transmission intensity. Anaemia status was included because of its established clinical relevance, as malaria is a recognized cause of anaemia, whereas elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and leucocytosis were included as inflammatory markers relevant to malaria pathophysiology5,23. Thrombocytopaenia was also included based on its documented association with malaria infection in previous studies5. Model 1 included age, sex, and locality, whereas Model 2 additionally adjusted for anaemia, leucocytosis, elevated ESR, and thrombocytopenia. To minimize overfitting, the number of predictors included in multivariable models was restricted based on sample size considerations, and model parsimony was evaluated using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), and model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Potential clustering at the locality level was addressed by adjusting for locality in all models. Model discrimination was assessed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the ROC curve (AUC). The AUC for each model was estimated with 95% confidence intervals generated using bootstrap resampling with 2,000 replicates. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Differences in predictive performance between Model 1 and Model 2 were compared using the non-parametric DeLong test for correlated ROC curves, which accounts for the paired nature of the data since both models were applied to the same participants. To examine relationships between parasite density and haematological parameters (ESR, white blood cell count, platelet count, and haemoglobin concentration), linear regression models were fitted with log-transformed parasite density included as a continuous predictor to account for skewness. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes (P. falciparum malaria status), while linear regression was applied to continuous haematological outcomes, consistent with standard modelling approaches. Assumptions for the linear regression analyses were formally assessed prior to interpretation. Normality of residuals was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests and Q-Q plots, homoscedasticity using Breusch–Pagan tests and residual plots, linearity using Ramsey RESET tests and scatter plots with loess smoothing, independence using Durbin–Watson tests, and influential outliers using Cook’s distance. All assumptions were satisfactorily met. Adjusted marginal effects were estimated and visualized using margins plots with 95% confidence intervals. Given the multiple subgroup analyses performed, findings were interpreted cautiously, with greater emphasis placed on effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than sole reliance on p-values to minimize the risk of type I error inflation. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics among study participants stratified by localities

A total of 388 participants were included in the study, with nearly equal representation from Ashaiman (n = 128), Kwesimintim (n = 129), and Kyebi (n = 131). The overall median age was 30 years (range: 1–70), with the largest proportion in the 1–10-year group (22.4%), followed by > 60 years (17.3%). Age distribution varied across localities, with Kyebi having an older population (median 51 years, 34.4% aged > 60), while Kwesimintim and Ashaiman were younger (medians 19 and 23 years, respectively). Overall, 59.5% of participants were female, though the proportion of females was higher in Kyebi (73.3%) compared with Ashaiman (50.8%) and Kwesimintim (54.3%). The P. falciparum malaria was 65.7% overall, with similar proportions across the three localities (66.4% in Ashaiman, 65.1% in Kwesimintsim, and 65.6% in Kyebi). (Table 1)

Table 2 shows the variations in haematological parameters across sociodemographic characteristics stratified by malaria status. Overall, ESR, WBC counts, and haemoglobin levels did not differ significantly between P. falciparum malaria and non-malaria participants. However, in Kwesimintsim, P. falciparum malaria cases had a higher median ESR (17.0 [8.8–29.0] mm/hr) compared with non-malaria participants (12.0 [7.0–18.0] mm/hr; p = 0.028). Thrombocytopaenia was strongly associated with P. falciparum malaria, with affected individuals having nearly 12-fold higher odds (aOR = 11.8; 95% CI: 6.18–24.7; p < 0.001).

Model performance of binary logistic regression models

Table 4 shows that the full multivariable logistic regression model (Model 2), which included demographic and haematological variables, demonstrated substantially better discriminatory performance for P. falciparum infection compared with the baseline model containing only age, sex, and locality (Model 1). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) improved from 57.2% (95% CI: 51.2–63.0) in Model 1 to 77.6% (95% CI: 72.7–82.3) in Model 2, indicating good discriminative ability. Model 2 also showed higher sensitivity (70.2% vs. 49.0%), specificity (75.9% vs. 66.9%), positive predictive value (84.8% vs. 74.0%), and negative predictive value (57.1% vs. 40.6%) relative to Model 1. The DeLong test demonstrated that the improvement in predictive performance between the two models was statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study provides a detailed analysis of the haematological alterations associated with P. falciparum malaria across different demographic and geographical contexts in Ghana. The central finding is that thrombocytopaenia is the most robust haematological parameter of infection, demonstrating consistent diagnostic utility. Furthermore, our investigation reveals significant sex-specific differences with increasing parasite burden, adding a critical layer of nuance to the understanding of malaria haematology.

The identification of thrombocytopaenia as a significant factor associated with P. falciparum malaria in this study is consistent with findings from other endemic settings24,25. The marked reduction in platelet counts observed across age groups, sexes, and localities reinforces its role as a common haematological feature of acute infection. This phenomenon is likely multifactorial, involving immune-mediated platelet destruction, splenic sequestration, and platelet adhesion to parasitized erythrocytes10. However, while thrombocytopaenia demonstrated strong association with malaria in this study, its diagnostic specificity remains limited, particularly in tropical settings where other febrile illnesses such as dengue, typhoid fever, and viral infections may similarly present with low platelet counts. Therefore, thrombocytopaenia should not be considered a standalone diagnostic marker but rather an adjunctive indicator that complements parasitological confirmation.

A particularly important finding of this study was the observation of sex-specific differences in haematological responses with increasing parasite density. Females demonstrated a greater decline in platelet count and haemoglobin levels compared to males, while erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) increased more markedly among females as parasitaemia increased. These findings suggest possible differences in the haematological and inflammatory responses to P. falciparum infection between males and females. However, the underlying mechanisms responsible for these differences could not be determined within the scope of the present study and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although hormonal influences and sex-related immunological variations have been proposed in previous studies as potential contributors to differential malaria responses26–29, the current study did not directly assess hormonal or immunological markers to support these explanations. Consequently, these mechanisms remain hypothetical and require further investigation. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies incorporating hormonal profiling, inflammatory cytokine analysis, and immune response characterization are needed to better understand the biological basis and clinical significance of these observed sex-specific haematological patterns.

Furthermore, the observed site-specific variations in ESR and haemoglobin levels may reflect differences in malaria transmission intensity, nutritional status, co-infection burden, socioeconomic conditions, and healthcare access across the study locations. Elevated ESR values may indicate varying levels of systemic inflammation beyond malaria alone, while differences in haemoglobin concentrations could also be influenced by background anaemia prevalence, dietary factors, and endemic infections. These findings highlight the importance of interpreting haematological parameters within their local epidemiological and clinical context.

The modelling work strongly supports the integration of haematological parameters into diagnostic assessments. The model relying solely on demographic factors (age and sex) demonstrated poor discriminatory power. In stark contrast, the model incorporating haematological biomarkers most notably thrombocytopaenia achieved markedly superior predictive accuracy. This finding reinforces the evolving consensus that complete blood count-derived variables can significantly enhance diagnostic workflows in endemic areas30, potentially guiding treatment decisions when parasitological confirmation is delayed or uncertain.

Regarding other haematological markers, the current results present a nuanced picture. The overall lack of significant predictive value for anaemia and leucocytosis in the multivariate model contrasts with some studies5 but aligns with the understanding that these are less specific markers, often confounded by co-morbidities. However, this does not negate their clinical importance. Again, the study found that anaemia was significantly more pronounced in P. falciparum malaria patients within the Kyebi locality, and the stratification analysis further revealed that haemoglobin decline was strongly correlated with parasite density in both males and females. This may indicate that the relationship between P. falciparum malaria and anaemia is highly context-dependent, influenced by local transmission intensity and, as the study has shown, sex31. Similarly, while ESR was not a consistent independent predictor in this presesnt study a finding that supports its status as a non-specific inflammatory marker8, its sharp increase with parasitaemia in females underscores its potential value in monitoring the inflammatory component of the disease within specific patient subgroups.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study emphasizes the importance of haematological profiling, particularly thrombocytopaenia, as a valuable diagnostic marker for P. falciparum malaria. It also highlights the necessity of accounting for demographic variables and sex-specific differences when assessing malaria-related haematological changes. These findings advocate for the incorporation of haematological parameters into malaria diagnosis and management protocols to enhance clinical outcomes across various localities.

Limitations

The assessment of haematological parameters was limited to erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), white blood cell count, haemoglobin concentration, and platelet count, which may not fully capture the broader spectrum of haematological alterations associated with P. falciparum infection. The use of convenience sampling and unequal age distributions across study sites may have introduced selection bias, potentially affecting the representativeness of the sample and limiting both the internal validity and generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the inclusion of participants from only three localities restricts extrapolation to other regions of Ghana with differing malaria transmission dynamics and population characteristics. Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish temporal relationships between malaria infection and the observed haematological changes. As exposure and outcome were measured simultaneously, causality cannot be inferred. Similarly, although parasite density was associated with certain haematological abnormalities, the study design does not permit conclusions regarding directional or causal relationships. The findings should therefore be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of predictive or causal effects. Although some variables were adjusted for during analysis, important confounding factors such as nutritional status, co-infections, underlying inflammatory conditions, and recent medication use were not comprehensively controlled for and may have influenced the observed associations. ESR, being a non-specific inflammatory marker, may also have been elevated due to conditions unrelated to malaria, thereby limiting its specificity in this context. In addition, there is a possibility of misclassification bias, particularly among control participants, where undetected or submicroscopic infections may have occurred, potentially attenuating the observed associations. Finally, the absence of detailed information on socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, and treatment adherence further limits a comprehensive understanding of the determinants of the haematological changes observed.

Recommendations

Future research should incorporate a broader range of CBC parameters beyond WBC, haemoglobin, platelets and ESR to capture more comprehensive haematological alterations associated with P. falciparum malaria. Participants from a wider range of geographic settings should be included in future studies and with more balanced demographic distributions, especially among age groups, to enhance generalizability of findings. Also, a longitudinal study on the effect is recommended to access the effect of antimalarial drugs on haematological parameters during course of P. falciparum malaria treatment.
