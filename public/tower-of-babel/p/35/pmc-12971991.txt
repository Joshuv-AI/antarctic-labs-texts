De novo biosynthesis of putrescine from glucose using engineered Escherichia coli in a two-step process
Authors: Lang Yin, Hongxu Li, Min Du, Zhangyu Liu, Jing Wang, Sheng Xu, Xin Wang, Kequan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPutrescine, is a key monomer for the synthesis of high-performance polymer materials. However, its sustainable microbial production remains challenging in yield and efficiency. In this study, we developed a modular two-step biosynthesis process for the conversion of glucose to putrescine. In the first stage, an l-arginine-overproducing Escherichia coli ARGFM strain was constructed through metabolic engineering. Then, fermentation optimization in a 5 L fermenter achieved a titer of 97.6 g/L with a yield of 0.52 g/g, representing a 49% improvement over the initial process. Notably, by implementing a dynamic dissolved oxygen control strategy (20–30%), the l-arginine productivity was enhanced to 2.03 g/(L·h). In the second stage, the arginine was efficiently converted to putrescine via whole-cell catalysis by another engineered E. coli, reaching a final titer of 48.6 g/L, with a yield of 0.26 g/gGlucose which was the highest level reported for de novo biosynthesis of putrescine. Specifically, this catalytic step employed in situ CO2 generated from the decarboxylation reaction for pH self-regulation, minimizing viability loss caused by exogenous acid/base addition. Furthermore, the protocol can be scaled up to a 50 L reactor with similar yield. Finally, a downstream separation process was established, recovering high-purity putrescine from the fermentation broth with a final purity of 99%. Techno-economic analysis and life cycle assessment predict that putrescine, derived from a two-step process, can reduce carbon dioxide emissions by 30%. Overall, this study established an integrated fermentation-catalysis-separation process for putrescine, demonstrating a viable and efficient strategy for the bio-based production of this valuable diamine.Keywords: Putrescine, Fermentation optimization, Whole-cell catalysis, Isolation and purification

Introduction

Putrescine is an aliphatic diamine containing two primary amino groups [1]. As a crucial chemical intermediate, it exhibits extensive and diverse applications in many industrial fields [2]. In the polyamide industry, it serves as a key monomer for synthesizing high-performance polymers such as nylon-46 [3]. In the medical field, it functions as a drug precursor and biochemical reagent, playing a significant role in the metabolic regulation of animal and plant cells [4]. Traditionally, the industrial putrescine mainly relies on the hydrogenation of succinonitrile [3]. However, this chemical process requires expensive catalysts, generates toxic intermediates, and depends on petroleum-derived feedstock [5]. In recent years, the development of microbial production processes has attracted widespread attention due to their environmental friendliness, mild reaction conditions, and utilization of renewable resources [6].

In E. coli, putrescine could be synthesized via three primary pathways. The ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) pathway converts l-ornithine to putrescine. Based on this pathway, Qian et al. first reported an engineered E. coli to produce putrescine from glucose with a titer of 24.2 g/L by overexpressing ornithine synthesis genes, as well as deleting the argI gene [5]. Noh et al. further engineered E. coli to achieve a titer of 42.3 g/L putrescine from glucose in fed-batch fermentation by downregulating argF and glnA [7]. Alternatively, the arginine decarboxylase (ADC) pathway directly catalyzes the decarboxylation of l-arginine to agmatine by l-arginine decarboxylase, which is then hydrolyzed to putrescine by agmatinase. For example, Deng et al. constructed an engineered E. coli by overexpressing l-arginine decarboxylase and agmatinase, and obtained a titer of 26.2 g/L putrescine from l-arginine through a whole-cell catalysis process [8]. Subsequently, this group further engineered pH adaptation of arginine decarboxylase to efficiently biosynthesize putrescine with a titer of 128.9 g/L from l-arginine in whole-cell catalysis [9]. Our prior study reported a third pathway for putrescine biosynthesis employing arginase and l-ornithine decarboxylase with l-arginine as a precursor, reaching a titer of 76 g/L from l-arginine with whole-cell catalysis [10]. Overall, various biosynthetic routes for putrescine production have been established, primarily via one-step fermentation or whole-cell catalysis. Compared to the whole-cell catalysis approach, one-step fermentation from glucose is less efficient [7]. The development of high-performance strains for one-step fermentation remains challenging, primarily due to our limited understanding of cellular putrescine metabolism and the impact of product toxicity. Meanwhile, the whole-cell catalysis processes depend on expensive precursors like arginine and ornithine, which presents a critical economic bottleneck. Consequently, establishing an efficient de novo synthesis platform for putrescine directly from glucose is highly desired.

Multi-stage biosynthesis based on multi-strain collaboration is gaining significant attention as an effective strategy to overcome the limitations of single-strain systems [11]. This approach leverages the principle of metabolic division of labor, where distinct engineered strains are designed to specialize in specific, complementary steps of a biosynthetic pathway [12]. By distributing the metabolic burden across different strains, this system alleviates the accumulation of toxic intermediates or products that would otherwise inhibit growth and production in a single chassis [13]. For instance, Wang et al. constructed a consortium of two engineered E. coli strains through a specialized division of labor, where one strain was designed for l-lysine production from glucose, and the other one was engineered for cadaverine biosynthesis from l-lysine with glycerol [14]. The modular nature of this multi-stage biosynthesis process offers a powerful alternative to conventional methods. It effectively bypasses the intrinsic inefficiencies of overloading a single strain with complex genetic circuits and the high substrate costs associated with enzymatic catalysis using purified precursors. Consequently, this strategy not only enhances the stability and efficiency of the overall biosynthesis but also opens a novel and promising direction for the industrial-scale production of valuable biochemicals, including diamines.

In this study, we established a two-step process for the de novo biosynthesis of putrescine from glucose using two engineered E. coli. In the first stage, we constructed an arginine-overproducing strain E. coli ARGFM and optimized its fermentation process for l-arginine synthesis from glucose. In the second stage, whole-cell catalysis process was performed using another engineered E. coli capable of converting l-arginine into putrescine. To enhance l-arginine production, we systematically optimized the fermentation medium by examining substrate concentration, type and ratio of organic nitrogen source, compound ratio and amount, C/N ratio, and betaine addition. Key fermentation conditions were also explored, including inoculum size and pH modulation. Furthermore, the dissolved oxygen levels were fine-tuned in a 5 L fermenter, resulting in a final l-arginine titer of 97.6 g/L with a yield of 0.52 g/gGlucose. Subsequently, the l-arginine-containing fermentation broth was employed for whole-cell catalysis, producing 48.6 g/L putrescine at a yield of 0.498 gputrescine/garginine. At the same time, we further improved the activity of SpeC by site prediction. Furthermore, the protocol can be scaled up to a 50 L reactor with similar yield. Finally, based on the characteristics of the catalytic solution, Aspen software was used to simulate the whole separation and purification process, and LCA and tea analysis were done based on the whole simulation process. An integrated downstream process was designed for the separation of high-purity putrescine from the catalytic solution.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids construction, and genome manipulation

The strains and plasmids used in this study were summarized in Supplementary Table S1. E. coli MG1655 was selected as the chassis strain for l-arginine production. E. coli DH5a was used as the cloning host for recombinant plasmids. E. coli ARGFM was constructed based on a previous study for l-arginine synthesis [15]. At the same time, recombinant E. coli TAC that was obtained in our previous study was employed for a whole-cell catalysis process to convert l-arginine to putrescine [10].

The recombinant plasmid pET28a-T7-arg-T7-SpeC was constructed using pET28 as vector, to express the gene arg (arginases) and SpeC (ornithine decarboxylase). Plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. The gene arg was codon-optimized and synthesized by General Corporation (Chuzhou). The gene speC and Pdxk were amplified from the genome of E. coli K-12 MG1655 by primers 28a-SpeC-V-F, 28a-SpeC-V-R. After that, the recombinant plasmids were constructed using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit. Plasmid pCDFDute-pdxk was constructed using the same method mentioned previously.

The chromosomal integration or deletion of target genes based on CRISPR/Cas9 in E. coli MG1655 were performed as previously described [16].

Culture medium

The culture and production of E. coli ARGFM required the use of seed medium, initial fermentation medium, and feed solution. The seed medium was divided into primary and secondary seed media. The primary seed medium contains 1 g/L glucose, 10 g/L peptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L beef extract, 2.5 g/L NaCl. When preparing a solid medium, 25 g/L agar was added. The secondary seed medium comprises 3 g/L K2HPO4, 1.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 5 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L peptone, 0.02 g/L FeSO4·7H2O, 0.02 g/L MnSO4·H2O, 25 g/L glucose, vitamin B1, B3, B5, and D-biotin at 0.002 g/L each. The fermentation medium includes 6 g/L KH2PO4, 1.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 5 g/L yeast extract, 0.02 g/L FeSO4·7H2O, 0.02 g/L MnSO4·H2O, 10 g/L glucose, 10 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 5.45 g/L sodium glutamate, 0.002 g/L vitamin B1, B3, B5, and D-biotin. For the cultivation in the fermentor, the fed-batch strategy was employed, and the feed solution consists of 750 g/L glucose, 400 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 200 g/L sodium glutamate.

The optimized fermentation medium formula was 6 g/L KH2PO4, 1.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 1.75 g/L yeast extract, 5.25 g/L Beef powder, 2 g/L Betaine, 0.02 g/L FeSO4·7H2O, 0.02 g/L MnSO4·H2O, 20 g/L glucose, 11 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 5.45 g/L sodium glutamate, 0.002 g/L Vitamin B1, B3, B5, and D-biotin. The optimized fermentation tank feed was 750 g/L glucose and additional 3 g/L betaine.

The culture and production of recombinant E. coli TAC required the use of seed medium, cell culture medium, and feed solution. For seed culture, we used LB liquid medium. The cell culture medium comprised 6 g/L yeast extract, 12 g/L peptone, 10 g/L glycerol, 2.5 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 0.3 g/L FeSO4·7H2O, 0.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 5 g/L sodium chloride, 3 g/L NaH2PO4, and 25 mg/L kanamycin sulfate, 40 mg/L streptomycin, and 0.25 mM IPTG. The feed solution contained 25 g/L peptone, 75 g/L yeast extract, and 280 g/L glycerol.

Fermentation conditions for l-arginine synthesis

Single colonies cultured on solid plates were inoculated into 5 mL shaking tubes containing seed medium and 1 g/L glucose and cultured for 16 h at 37 °C. For shake flask fermentation, the cultured secondary seed solution was inoculated into the fermentation medium at a 10% inoculum volume and fermented at 37 °C for 20 h.

For fermenter fermentation, the cultured secondary seed liquid was transferred to the 5 L fermenter at a 10% inoculum volume, supplemented with sterilized glucose solution, trace elements, and antibiotics. Fermentation conditions were set as follows: temperature at 37 °C, initial agitation speed at 300 rpm, with adjustments to agitation speed and aeration to maintain dissolved oxygen levels between 20 and 30%. Ammonia was used to adjust the pH to approximately 7.3. Dissolved oxygen rebound was monitored. After substrate consumption, supplements were added to maintain glucose and ammonia nitrogen concentrations in the fermentation broth at 1-3 g/L. The total fermentation cycle lasted 48-60 h.

The bioconversion of l-arginine into putrescine by a whole-cell catalysis process

The bioconversion of l-arginine to putrescine was carried out via a whole-cell catalysis process. A frozen stock (2.5 mL) of recombinant E. coli TAC stored at −80 °C was inoculated into a 2 L shake flask containing 250 mL of LB liquid medium supplemented with 25 mg/L kanamycin sulfate and 40 mg/L streptomycin, followed by incubation at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 10 h. The resulting seed culture was transferred into a 5 L fermenter at a 10% (v/v) inoculation rate, along with sterilized glucose solution, trace elements, and antibiotics. Fermentation was conducted at 37 °C with an agitation speed of 300 rpm. Dissolved oxygen (DO) was maintained at approximately 20% by regulating agitation, aeration, and oxygen supplementation, while pH was controlled at around 7.0 using phosphoric acid and ammonia water. The OD600 of the broth was monitored throughout the process. When OD600 reached 5, IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.25 mM to induce protein expression, and the temperature was lowered to 18 °C.

After fermentation, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 20 min. A catalytic system was established using the l-arginine-rich fermentation broth from E. coli ARGFM as the substrate, supplemented with 10 mM magnesium ions and 0.15 mM pyridoxal phosphate. Whole-cell catalysis was performed using recombinant E. coli TAC at a cell density of OD600 = 40, under controlled conditions of 35 °C and pH 6.0. No additional glucose was introduced into the system, as residual glucose from the E. coli ARGFM fermentation broth was present. At the same time, the strategy of CO2 in situ fixation was adopted to adjust the pH change during the catalytic process.

Molecular docking and site mutagenesis for engineering SpeC

Ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) variants were designed using a structure- and language-model–guided pipeline integrating AlphaFold3 (AF3), ESM3, and FoldX. (1) An AF3-predicted ODC homodimer model was generated, and residues within 5 amino acids of both termini were excluded. Designable positions were further restricted to high-confidence sites with mean and Cα pLDDT >70 and mean and Cα PAE <15 Å. (2) All single substitutions at eligible positions were then scored in a zero-shot manner using the ESM3-open model with a masked-marginal strategy: each target position was masked, log-probabilities were obtained from model logits (log-softmax), and the mutation score was defined as ΔlogP = logP(mutant) − logP(wild-type). (3) Mutations were ranked by zero-shot score, the top 50 were evaluated by FoldX 5.1, and only variants with predicted stability changes ΔΔG ≤ +2.0 kcal/mol were retained for experimental validation. After foldx screening, 15 mutants were selected for experimental measurement according to esm3 score from high to low. The SpeC variants were constructed by site-directed mutagenesis. Detailed construction methods were included in the Supplementary Table S4.

The separation and purification of putrescine

The separation and purification of from the catalytic solution was performed according to a designed process adapted from standard diamine purification techniques [17]. The workflow comprised four key stages. The first stage involved centrifuging the fermentation broth to remove microbial cells and obtain a transparent supernatant. Subsequently, add a certain amount of activated carbon to the ultrafiltrate for decolorization, and investigate the effects of different concentrations of carbon, temperatures, and decolorization times on the decolorization efficiency. In the third stage, the decolorized liquid was concentrated to reduce volume. Finally, the concentrated solution was adjusted to pH 12 with NaOH, and then extracted using an extraction agent under the conditions of a 30 °C temperature, a V (organic phase): V (aqueous phase) ratio of 1:1, an extraction time of 60 min, and a stirring speed of 300 rpm. After the extraction, the mixture was allowed to stand for 1 h for layer separation. The two phases were separated using a separatory funnel, and the organic phase was collected for putrescine detection to calculate the extraction rate. The separated organic phase was subjected to batch distillation under negative pressure, with a reflux ratio set at 1:1. The distillation experiment was conducted by continuously adjusting the temperature of the distillation column's bottom.

Analytical methods

Biomass concentration was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using spectrophotometry. Glucose concentration was determined with an SBA-40c biosensor analyzer (Shandong Academy of Sciences, China) [18]. l-arginine was quantified by a modified Sakaguchi method, while putrescine was derivatized with dansyl chloride and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography [10]. The purity of the final product was assessed by gas chromatography [19].

The pigment removal rate during decolorization was evaluated by spectrophotometry. The yellow pigments in the feed solution exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 430 nm. Absorbance was measured at this wavelength using pure water as a reference. The pigment content in the centrifuged fermentation broth was defined as Ca = 1.0 U/mL. A standard curve relating absorbance to pigment content was established under different dilution factors (Supporting Material), enabling relative quantification of pigment levels in process samples. The decolorization efficiency was calculated based on the decrease in absorbance at 430 nm after treatment.

Results and discussion

Construction of l-arginine-producing strain E. coli ARGFM

In this study, an l-arginine-producing strain was constructed based on a previous study [15]. The core l-arginine biosynthetic pathway from Corynebacterium glutamicum was reinforced through the heterologous expression of the argC, argJ, argB, argD, argF, argG and argH genes. Precursor availability was then improved by heterologously expressing the pyrAA/pyrABE949∗ genes from Bacillus subtilis, which enhanced the synthesis of carbamoyl phosphate [20]. Cofactor regeneration was addressed by overexpressing the native pntA gene to supply NADPH [21]. To eliminate metabolic bottlenecks and competing pathways, we knocked out the repressor gene argR to relieve feedback inhibition. Furthermore, genes involved in the degradation of l-arginine and ornithine-speC, speF (ornithine decarboxylases), speA, adiA (arginine decarboxylases), and astA (arginine N-succinyltransferase), were deleted to minimize carbon loss. Subsequently, the arginine efflux system lysE (arginine exporter) derived from C. glutamicum was introduced to enhance the accumulation of extracellular arginine [22]. All these genetic modifications were performed on the genome to ensure strain stability, resulting in the l-arginine-producing strain E. coli ARGFM (Fig. 1A).

The performance of E. coli ARGFM in producing l-arginine from glucose was evaluated. The strain exhibited rapid growth accompanied by intense oxygen consumption during fermentation. By fermentation of 12 h, the dissolved oxygen (DO) level could no longer be maintained at the setpoint despite increased aeration and agitation, leading to the accumulation of approximately 2.8 g/L acetic acid. To stabilize the process, ammonia water was continuously supplied, which, however, resulted in high ammonia nitrogen levels and an altered carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. Under these conditions, a final l-arginine concentration of 65.47 g/L was achieved, with a yield of 0.37 g/g glucose and a productivity of 1.09 g/(L·h) (Fig. 1B).

Optimization of the fermentation process for enhanced l-arginine production by engineered E. coli ARGFM

Fermentation biotechnology stands as a cornerstone in the industrial production of a vast array of metabolites, including antibiotics, enzymes, organic acids, biofuels, and therapeutic proteins. The optimization of this complex biological process is paramount for achieving high yields and economic viability. To address the challenges of dissolved oxygen depletion, byproduct accumulation, and suboptimal yield identified in the initial fermentation, we systematically optimized the medium composition and cultivation conditions. The objective was to achieve more robust cell growth and higher l-arginine production efficiency.

Optimization of fermentation medium

In this study, glucose was used as the carbon source for l-arginine fermentation. However, high glucose concentrations can exert repressive effects in E. coli, potentially reducing the product formation rate and yield and overall fermentation efficiency [23]. We tested glucose concentrations ranging from 10 to 40 g/L on l-arginine synthesis, and the highest yield of 0.268 g/g was achieved at a glucose concentration of 20 g/L, corresponding to an l-arginine titer of 5.36 g/L after 20 h (see Supplementary materials). Consequently, this concentration was selected for subsequent experiments to maximize substrate conversion.

Nitrogen, as a fundamental constituent of amino acids, nucleic acids, coenzymes, and cell wall components, is a critical determinant of both microbial growth and the metabolic routing toward target product formation [24]. In particular, l-arginine is a compound containing multiple amino groups. Therefore, rational optimization of nitrogen source is a core strategy in developing efficient and scalable fermentation processes. In this study, six common organic nitrogen sources, including peptone, yeast extract, beef extract, soybean peptone, yeast extract, and urea, were selected to investigate their effects on l-arginine fermentation. As shown in Fig. 2A, yeast extract supported the highest l-arginine production of 5.63 g/L, achieving a biomass of 9.21 OD600. Beef extract was the second most effective nitrogen source, yielding 4.1 g/L of l-arginine with an even higher biomass of 10.21 OD600. Notably, a distinct trade-off was observed, where yeast extract appeared to favor product synthesis, whereas beef extract promoted microbial growth. Given their complementary effects, we hypothesized that combining these two nitrogen sources could balance and synergize their effects, leading us to evaluate their performance at different blend ratios.

Accordingly, we systematically evaluated the synergistic effects of yeast extract and beef extract combined at varying ratios of 4:1, 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2B, all combination ratios resulted in superior l-arginine production compared to the single nitrogen source control. Strikingly, the 1:3 (yeast extract: beef extract) group achieved the highest l-arginine titer of 6.64 g/L with a yield of 0.33 g/g, accompanied by a final biomass of 14.45 OD600. However, a further increase in the proportion of beef extract proved detrimental to product accumulation. Therefore, the optimal ratio of yeast powder to beef extract ratio was selected as 1:3. Compared to yeast powder as a single organic nitrogen source, the combined nitrogen source increased the yield by 31.7%, significantly improving l-arginine fermentation performance.

To further optimize the medium, the effect of organic nitrogen concentration on fermentation was investigated by varying from 1 to 9 g/L. As shown in Fig. 2C, when the organic nitrogen concentration reached 7 g/L, the l-arginine concentration exhibited the highest level at 6.81 g/L, with a yield of 0.34 g/g and a biomass of 14.12 OD600. Further increasing the organic nitrogen concentration did not enhance these parameters; instead, it led to a reduced performance. Therefore, an organic nitrogen concentration of 7 g/L was selected for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio is also a pivotal determinant of fermentation efficiency, as an imbalance can lead to either premature growth cessation or the diversion of carbon flux into storage compounds rather than the desired product. Given its pivotal role, we systematically investigated the effect of the C/N ratio (6:1, 5:1, 4:1, 3:1, 2:1, and 1:1) on l-arginine production. As shown in Fig. 2D, when the C/N ratio was 3:1, the l-arginine titer reached the highest level of 7.24 g/L, with a yield of 0.362 g/g and a biomass of 14.69 OD600. This precise balance thereby significantly enhanced overall production performance by optimizing metabolic resource allocation.

Following the determination of the foundation fermentation medium of carbon and nitrogen source, we sought to further enhance fermentation performance by targeting the cellular physiological state. Despite optimal C/N conditions, microbial metabolism often faces intrinsic limitations, such as osmotic stress and redox imbalance, which can constrain the maximum production capacity. Therefore, we turned to metabolic enhancers that could bolster cellular robustness and redirect flux toward the desired pathway. Betaine, a compatible solute with well-documented roles as an osmostress protectant [25] and a methyl donor [26], has been successfully employed to enhance the fermentation of several amino acids, including l-lysine and l-threonine [27,28]. In this study, we investigated its potential to improve l-arginine synthesis. The growth and fermentation performance of the strain were evaluated at betaine concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 2.5 g/L. The results exhibited that the addition of 2 g/L betaine resulted in a marked improvement in l-arginine production, yielding 7.31 g/L l-arginine with a conversion rate of 0.36 g/g (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Optimization of the fermentation conditions

Following the establishment of an optimized medium composition, we subsequently investigated key physical and operational parameters to further improve l-arginine production. The inoculum size, pH, and dissolved oxygen (DO) levels were systematically optimized to enhance process efficiency and product titer.

Inoculum size is a critical process parameter that significantly influences the lag phase, overall fermentation kinetics, and final product yield. An appropriate inoculum size can shorten the fermentation cycle and enhance production efficiency. As shown in Fig. 2E, the growth and fermentation performance of the strain were evaluated at inoculum sizes of 1%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. The results showed that increasing the inoculum size shortened the lag phase and improved l-arginine production during the shake flask fermentation. The highest l-arginine titer of 7.46 g/L was achieved at an inoculum size of 10%, with a yield of 0.37 g/g, which was therefore selected for all subsequent experiments.

PH is a pivotal environmental factor that critically governs microbial growth and product formation during amino acid fermentation [29]. In high-density E. coli cultures, maintaining an optimal pH is essential for maximizing cell density, sustaining key enzyme activities, and directing metabolic flux toward the target product [30]. Deviation from the optimal pH range can disrupt cellular membrane integrity, impair nutrient uptake. Crucially for l-arginine production, it could exacerbate the accumulation of inhibitory by-products such as acetic acid. We therefore evaluated the effect of pH control strategies using various alkaline reagents, including aqueous ammonia, KOH, and NaOH. Among the agents tested, ammonia was identified as the most suitable pH regulator. It required smaller addition volumes, minimized inhibitory effects, and supplied additional nitrogen atoms, supporting l-arginine biosynthesis. As a result, the use of ammonia as a neutralizing agent led to a final l-arginine titer of 7.67 g/L and a yield of 0.38 g/g (Fig. 2F).

While shake flask optimization is valuable, precise DO Dissolved oxygen (DO) is a critical process parameter in aerobic fermentations, particularly for high-yield amino acid production. For example, Xu et al. enhanced the strain's ability to cope with insufficient dissolved oxygen (DO) during the later stage of fermentation by constructing a hypoxia-responsive promoter and introducing heterologous hemoglobin. Subsequently, by combining DO control, pressure optimization, and auxiliary parameter adjustment, they achieved a tyrosine yield of 109.2 g/L under optimal conditions, which is currently the highest reported yield [31]. Adequate dissolved oxygen directly affects respiratory metabolism, productivity, cellular ATP generation, and ultimately, the rate and yield of product synthesis [32]. The engineered E. coli ARGFM strain, with its enhanced metabolic flux toward l-arginine, exhibits intense oxygen demand, which often becomes a limiting factor. To evaluate the important role of dissolved oxygen in l-arginine synthesis, we first performed the fermentation in the flask shake with different shake flask types, shaking speeds, and medium volume. As summarized in the Supplementary Fig. S3, the use of baffled flasks, a shaking speed of 220 rpm, and a working volume of 60 mL were identified as the optimal conditions. Under these optimal conditions, the strain produced 7.97 g/L of l-arginine with a yield of 0.39 g/g, suggesting the significant impact of improved oxygen supply on fermentation performance and providing a robust pre-culture protocol for fermentor experiments.

Control is only feasible in a bioreactor system. Preliminary experiments confirmed that the use of air alone to control DO was insufficient to meet the high oxygen demand of the ARGFM strain in a 5 L fermenter, leading to chronic DO limitations. Such oxygen-deficient conditions are known to trigger anaerobic metabolic pathways, resulting in the wasteful accumulation of by-products like acetic acid, which consumes carbon sources and inhibits both cell growth and product formation [33]. Therefore, we investigated the effects of two different dissolved oxygen controlling strategies on 5 L fermentation, one using air only and another employing an oxygen-enriched air supply. As shown in Fig. 3A, when we used air to control the dissolved oxygen, the engineered strain E. coli ARGFM produced 71.65 g/L of arginine after 48 h of fermentation, with a yield of 0.38 g/g and a productivity of 1.19 g/(L·h). In contrast, with pure oxygen supplementation to maintain DO at 20%–30%, a dramatic improvement was observed. The final l-arginine titer increased to 97.6 g/L after 48 h of fermentation, with a yield of 0.52 g/g and a production efficiency of 2.03 g/(L·h) (Fig. 3B).

Fed batch result of ARGFM in a 5 L bioreactor. (A) Fermentation profile using air only to maintain DO. (B) Fermentation profile with DO maintained at 20-30% via the controlled supplementation of pure oxygen. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3).

The 36% increase in titer and 70% boost in productivity under controlled DO conditions unequivocally highlight the critical role of oxygen sufficiency in scaling up l-arginine production. Maintaining DO at 20–30% likely supported efficient respiratory metabolism, suppressed the acetate pathway, and thereby redirected carbon flux toward l-arginine biosynthesis. This resulted in a more efficient conversion of the carbon source and reduced metabolic burden. While the performance achieved in this study marks a significant improvement over the base process, it remains below the reported benchmark of 132 g/L [15]. This discrepancy suggests that other factors, such as potential substrate inhibition, finer dynamic control of DO throughout different fermentation phases, or further strain engineering, are necessary. Future work will focus on dynamic feeding strategies and DO profiling to further push the boundaries of l-arginine production with the ARGFM strain.

Construction of the whole-cell catalytic synthesis for conversion of l-arginine fermentation broth to putrescine by engineered E. coli TAC

Building upon the high-titer production of l-arginine described above, we further focused on the subsequent biocatalytic conversion of l-arginine into putrescine, a valuable bio-based diamine monomer. Whole-cell catalysis, which utilizes intact microorganisms as biocatalysts, offers a sustainable and efficient alternative to traditional chemical synthesis. This approach leverages the high selectivity and mild reaction conditions inherent to enzymatic processes, making it particularly advantageous for the production of chiral and functionalized chemicals. Here, the engineered E. coli TAC strain, which was previously developed by expressing arginine kinase from cattle and ornithine decarboxylase in E. coli in our group to convert l-arginine to putrescine, was employed for the establishment of this whole-cell catalysis process (Fig. 4A) [10].

Production of putrescine from l-arginine fermentation broth catalyzed by the TAC strain. (A) Schematic diagram of in-situ fixation of CO2; (B) Conventional catalytic process conducted in a fermenter with phosphoric acid; (C) Improved catalytic process utilizing in situ CO2 recycling for pH control; (D) Whole cell catalytic results of SpeC mutants; (E) Catalysis of E325P in a 0.5 L reactor; (F) Scale-up production of putrescine in 50 L.

The E. coli TAC strain has been confirmed capable of producing 76 g/L putrescine from l-arginine with a yield of 0.5 g/g [10]. To verify the performance of the TAC strain in producing putrescine by l-arginine fermentation broth, whole-cell catalysis was carried out with the broth as the substrate [10]. The reaction mixture was supplemented with 10 mM Mg2+, 0.15 mM pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), and 2.5 g/L glucose. The catalysis was performed at 35 °C and pH 6.0 with a cell concentration of OD600 = 40. As a result, the E. coli TAC strain produced 43.4 g/L of putrescine within 10 h, achieving a calculated yield of 0.445 gputrescine/garginine (Fig. 4B). These results conclusively demonstrated the high catalytic efficiency of the E. coli TAC strain in converting fermentation-derived l-arginine into putrescine, which established a foundation for an integrated two-stage bioprocess for de novo putrescine synthesis from glucose.

However, we found that putrescine yield from l-arginine-containing fermentation broth was reduced compared to the system using pure l-arginine reported previously [10]. Meanwhile, the concentration of whole cells decreased with the progress of catalysis, starting from the initial OD600 of 40 to the final OD600 of 30. In the diamine fermentation process, pH was repeatedly adjusted through the addition of an acid neutralizing agent, phosphoric acid. The frequent supplementation of phosphoric acid likely impaired cell viability and ultimately suppressed the catalytic reaction. In a previous study for cadaverine production, Wei et al. developed an environmentally friendly strategy for cadaverine bio-production: in situ utilization of CO2 self-released from l-lysine decarboxylation for pH control [14]. As the conversion process from l-arginine to putrescine, it also involves an ornithine decarboxylation step. Therefore, we applied a similar strategy to utilize the CO2 continuously generated from decarboxylation for pH control. By sealing the reactor at the onset of the reaction, the dissolved CO2 effectively lowered the pH, functioning together with phosphate buffer to maintain the system around the optimal range (Fig. 4A). When internal pressure reached 0.11 MPa, intermittent venting was applied to maintain constant pressure until pH stabilization was achieved. As shown in (Fig. 4C), this approach enabled the TAC strain to produce 48.6 g/L of putrescine from the fermentation broth containing 97.6 g/L l-arginine, achieving a yield of 0.498 gputrescine/garginine. The catalysis time shortened to 8 h from 10 h. During the whole catalytic process, the cell concentration was maintained above OD600 of 39, and no significant decrease was observed. This strategy also presents a promising approach for in situ CO2 capture and utilization in bioprocesses. CO2 emissions from biological systems are often dispersed and difficult to manage, posing challenges for carbon accounting and mitigation. In recent years, several in situ CO2 capture strategies have been developed for polyamine biosynthesis [34], including engineering Rubisco-based carbon fixation pathways [19], coupling polyamine production with CO2-fixing organic acid synthesis [35], and direct reuse of CO2 as a pH regulator or in the form of calcium carbonate precipitates [16]. Our method integrates putrescine biosynthesis with efficient CO2 recycling, offering a scalable model for sustainable chemical production and advanced carbon management in industrial biotechnology.However, high cell OD during the catalytic process may increase additional costs. In the cascade catalysis of l-arginine to putrescine using ARG and SpeC, the low activity of SpeC limited the overall production efficiency [36]. Thus, we obtained 15 mutants with possible catalytic activity based on Alphafold3 ESM3 and Foldx predictions. The whole cell catalytic results are shown in Fig. 4D, indicating that the yield of E325P is 45.6% higher than that of wild-type SpeC, reaching 41.43 g/L under the cell concentration OD600 = 20. Following this, the potential of the recombinant strain to synthesize putrescine was investigated in a 0.5 L fermenter (Fig. 4E). Under the optimal conditions, the recombinant strain E325P converted nearly all L-Arg (97 g/L) to putrescine (50.9 g/L), within 8 h. These results demonstrated that this whole-cell catalytic system could provide an economical method for the bioconversion of L-Arg to putrescine.

To evaluate the effect of scaling up putrescine production using self-released CO2 to control pH, whole-cell conversion was conducted in a 50 L fermenter (Fig. 4F). The changes in pH, concentration of putrescine, and consumption of l-Arginine OD600 were similar with the conversion conducted in the 0.5 L fermenter, converting 97 g/L arginine to 49.5 g/L putrescine. Accordingly, the results indicated that self-released CO2 to control pH could be applied for efficient putrescine production using whole-cell conversion on both small and large scales.

Optimization of separation conditions for putrescine

Following the catalytic production of putrescine from l-arginine fermentation broth, an efficient downstream processing strategy was developed to recover high-purity product from the complex reaction mixture. While extensive research has focused on the separation of bio-based diamines like cadaverine, with methods spanning membrane filtration, adsorption, and electrodialysis, studies specifically on the purification of putrescine from fermentation broth were still rare [17,37]. As the similar chemical properties among bio-based diamines, it allowed us to establish relevant putrescine separation processes accordingly. Notably, liquid-liquid extraction has consistently emerged as a highly effective initial purification step for diamines due to its operational simplicity, scalability, and ability to handle large volumes of complex broth [38]. For instance, the separation of cadaverine often involved extraction into an organic phase followed by distillation, underscoring the technical rationality of this combined approach for diamine recovery [17]. Building on these studies, we established an integrated purification workflow for putrescine through the systematic optimization of decolorization, solvent extraction, and fractional distillation (Fig. 5A). The catalytic solution, obtained after centrifugation to remove cells and solids, exhibited a dark brown, semi-opaque appearance (Supplementary Fig. S4) due to the presence of pigment impurities. To mitigate potential adverse effects on product quality and downstream processing, activated carbon was employed for decolorization. Key parameters, including adsorbent dosage, temperature, and decolorization time, were systematically optimized to maximize pigment removal while minimizing product loss. Furthermore, the minimum effective dosage of activated carbon would be identified to reduce material consumption, minimize waste, and enhance process cost-effectiveness. The effects of activated carbon concentrations (5 g/L, 10 g/L, 15 g/L, 20 g/L, 25 g/L, and 30 g/L) on the decolorization of the catalytic solution and the recovery rate of putrescine were first evaluated. As shown in Fig. 5B, when the concentration of activated carbon reached 15 g/L, the pigment adsorption rate was 93.2%. Beyond this point, further increases in the amount of activated carbon did not significantly enhance the decolorization rate but led to a decrease in the recovery rate of putrescine. Therefore, an activated carbon concentration of 15 g/L was selected as the optimal dosage, balancing decolorization efficiency and cost-effectiveness. Since the adsorption performance of activated carbon is significantly influenced by temperature, identifying an appropriate reaction temperature was also critical for improving the adsorption capacity. The effects of decolorization temperatures at 30 °C, 40 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C, and 80 °C on the decolorization rate and recovery rate of putrescine were investigated. As shown in Fig. 5C. At 50 °C, the decolorization efficiency increased with temperature up to 50 °C, at which the pigment adsorption rate reached 98.05%. However, a further increase beyond 50 °C did not significantly improve the adsorption rate while reducing the recovery rate of putrescine. Consequently, 50 °C was established as the optimal decolorization temperature. The duration of decolorization is a critical parameter governing both process efficiency and cost-effectiveness. An insufficient contact time leads to incomplete pigment adsorption, while prolonged exposure may reduce efficiency and incur higher operational costs. To identify the optimal equilibrium, we evaluated decolorization intervals of 10 to 60 min. As illustrated in Fig. 5D, the pigment adsorption rate peaked at 98.45% within 30 min. Further extension of the adsorption time did not yield significant improvements in decolorization efficiency but resulted in a gradual decrease in putrescine recovery. Consequently, 30 min was established as the optimal decolorization period. Following decolorization, the catalytic solution was concentrated using distillation equipment. This preconcentration step served to significantly reduce the volume of material requiring subsequent processing, thereby decreasing the consumption of alkaline earth metals and extractants in the following alkalinization and liquid-liquid extraction stages. The concentrated solution was then alkalized to pH > 12 using solid NaOH in preparation for the subsequent extraction process.

Optimization of decolorization conditions for the catalytic solution. (A) Process flow of separation and purification of putrescine. (B) Effect of activated carbon dosage on the decolorization rate and putrescine recovery. (C) Effect of temperature on decolorization efficiency and product recovery. (D) Effect of adsorption time on the decolorization rate and product recovery. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3).

Following concentration and alkalinization of the decolorized catalytic solution, liquid-liquid extraction was employed to isolate putrescine. The selection of an appropriate extractant is critical for achieving high selectivity and efficiency. Specifically, organic solvents that form a miscible zone with water, exhibit high polarity, and remain stable under alkaline conditions were considered, particularly polar, dipolar protic organic solvents. Therefore, we focused on straight or branched-chain alkanols (C3–C8). Ten alcohol-based solvents, including ethanol, n-propanol, isopropanol, n-butanol, isobutanol, sec-butanol, n-pentanol, n-hexanol, n-heptanol, and n-octanol were evaluated as potential extraction solvents. As shown in Fig. 6A, n-butanol demonstrated the highest extraction efficiency at 68.98%. Compared to other organic extractants, N-butanol exhibits higher polarity, enhancing the partitioning of putrescine into the organic phase while minimizing co-extraction of impurities. The organic-to-aqueous phase ratio was subsequently optimized to maximize extraction yield while maintaining process economy. As shown in Fig. 6B, a 1:1 (V/V) ratio achieved near-equilibrium extraction. Further increasing the organic phase volume not only failed to improve yield but also diluted the product, complicating downstream purification and increasing solvent consumption. Thus, a ratio of 1:1 was selected for subsequent experiments. Temperature significantly influenced extraction kinetics and equilibrium. Elevated temperatures enhance molecular diffusion and solute transfer, but may also alter phase behavior and solvent stability. We examined the effects of temperatures ranging from 30 °C to 80 °C on the extraction yield. As illustrated in Fig. 6C, extraction efficiency increased with temperature up to 70 °C, beyond which no significant improvement was observed. While moderate temperatures promote the transfer of putrescine to the organic phase, excessively high temperatures increased energy consumption and production costs. Additionally, higher temperatures can increase the solubility of both solute and extractant in the aqueous phase, hindering efficient extraction and separation. Therefore, 70 °C was chosen as the optimal experimental condition. Finally, extraction time was also systematically evaluated, ranging from 30 min to 180 min (Fig. 6D). The results indicated that equilibrium was attained within 150 min, yielding a maximum extraction rate of 83.2%. Shorter durations might result in incomplete mass transfer, while prolonged extraction offered no additional benefit but increased energy and time costs, supporting the adoption of 150 min as the standard extraction period. Overall, the systematic optimization of extractant type, phase ratio, temperature, and time established an integrated extraction protocol using n-butanol at a 1:1 phase ratio, 70 °C, and 150 min. This combination achieved a high extraction yield while balancing efficiency with operational feasibility. The use of n-butanol is particularly advantageous due to its favorable partition coefficient for putrescine, relatively low toxicity, and ease of recovery in subsequent distillation steps. These findings indicated the importance of holistic parameter optimization in developing a scalable and cost-effective recovery process for biobased diamines.

The organic phase obtained from the extraction step was separated using a separatory funnel and subsequently purified via fractional distillation under vacuum (57.8 kPa). A reflux ratio of 1:1 was maintained throughout the process, and fractionation was performed in stages to achieve high-purity putrescine. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S5, the distillation profile revealed distinct phase transitions: residual water was removed at 85 °C, followed by the evaporation of N-butanol at 120 °C. Further heating progressively increased the purity of putrescine in both the column and kettle, with gas chromatography analysis (Supplementary Fig. S6) confirming a final purity of 99.9% at 135 °C. The overall mass yield of the final product was determined to be 24.8%. The developed purification scheme successfully produced polymer-grade putrescine with exceptional purity (99.9%), meeting the stringent requirements for subsequent polymerization processes yield of each step see Supplementary Table S5. However, the mass yield of 24.8% indicates substantial product loss during the multi-step separation process. When compared to the work of Agnieszka Krzyżaniak et al., who reported over 90% recovery of putrescine using 4-nonylphenol as extractant, the current yield appears modest [39]. The disparity in yield can be primarily attributed to the operational mode and extraction mechanism. The reference method utilized reactive extraction with 4-nonylphenol, which typically offers higher distribution coefficients but poses potential environmental toxicity concerns. In contrast, our process employed n-butanol, a widely accessible and lower-toxicity solvent, prioritizing industrial applicability and safety. Furthermore, the substantial loss observed in this study originated largely from the batch distillation process, where prolonged heating and multiple fraction cuts were necessary to ensure the ultra-high purity (99.9%) of the final product. It is anticipated that transitioning from batch to continuous distillation would significantly reduce thermal degradation and solvent entrapment, thereby recovering the yield to a commercially viable level, as further validated in our subsequent process simulation.

Process modeling

Based on the optimal separation and purification results of putrescine, we further evaluated the process of separating putrescine from fermentation broth and the impact of this process on economy and the environment. For this purpose, a process model was developed in Aspen Plus® (Fig. 7).

Aspen simulation of putrescine separation and purification.

The separation and purification process of putrescine was simulated and optimized using Aspen Plus® software to evaluate energy consumption and technical feasibility. The ELEC-NRTL (Electrolyte Non-Random Two-Liquid) property method was selected to strictly represent the non-ideal behavior of the aqueous system containing electrolytes and polar organic solvents. The electrolyte dissociation of putrescine dihydrochloride was defined using the Electrolyte Wizard tool. The simulation component list included putrescine dihydrochloride, putrescine, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium chloride (NaCl), and N-butanol. Glucose was selected as a representative compound for residual biomass, while tetrahydropyridine and aminopyridine were defined as proxies for light and heavy impurities, respectively.

In the evaporation simulation process, the Separators Flash model is selected for the evaporator. Logistics Feed is set with a temperature of 125 °C, a pressure of 3 MPa, and a flow rate of 300 kg/h, the putrescine yield is 99%. In the pH adjustment simulation process, the Blocks RSTOIC model is selected for the reactor. Logistics NaOH is set to a temperature of 25 °C, a pressure of 1 MPa, and a flow rate of 300 kg/h, the putrescine yield is 99.5%. In the extraction simulation process, the Blocks Extraction model is selected for the extraction tower. Logistics BUTAN setting temperature 25 °C, pressure 1 MPa, N-butanol flow rate 300 kg/h, the putrescine yield is 99%. Finally, after continuous distillation, the yield reached 91.28%. This yield is much higher than the 24.8% yield we used for separation and purification. We speculate that continuous distillation can further improve the yield of the product, which is consistent with the previous research results of Wei et al. [40].

The process model was used to inform the mass and energy balances required to determine the relevant metrics associated with the synthesis of putrescine, including the minimum selling price (MSP) and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and to estimate the influence of various process parameters on those metrics. According to the preliminary LCA, 8.9 kg CO2 can be produced by two-step production of putrescine (Fig. S7). Since there are currently no reports on the lifecycle of putrescine, we searched the database for lifecycle analysis of ethylenediamine. Compared to the chemical synthesis of ethylenediamine, the two-step synthesis of putrescine can reduce the emission of 4.2 kg CO2 (Supplementary Fig. S6). The environmental impact is smaller than that of technologies using traditional fossil fuels like coal and oil. According to the preliminary TEA, two-step synthesis of putrescine can generate a profit of approximately 188.4066 USD per ton (Supplementary Note. 1).

Conclusion

Herein, we developed a de novo biosynthetic route for putrescine production from glucose via a two-step process. This route was divided between two engineered E. coli strains, where the first strain ARGFM was constructed to overproduce l-arginine from glucose, and the second strain TAC directly utilized the crude fermentation broth for the catalytic production of putrescine. Meanwhile, modification of ornithine decarboxylase and improved the efficiency of the second decarboxylation reaction. Process simulation using Aspen Plus further validated that replacing batch distillation with continuous rectification could boost the separation yield to 91.28%. Finally, techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) indicated that this two-step process is economically viable (with a projected profit of ∼$188/ton) and offers a reduced carbon footprint compared to traditional chemical synthesis. This integrated strategy avoids toxic product inhibition, and intermediate purification, enhancing overall process efficiency and titer and putrescine with purity of 99.9% was obtained, demonstrating a sustainable and efficient bio-based industrial diamine production route.
