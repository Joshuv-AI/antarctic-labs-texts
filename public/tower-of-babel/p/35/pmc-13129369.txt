Grasping Thin Polypropylene Sutures Even With Less Traumatic Instruments May Decrease Their Tensile Strength
Authors: Damir Vakhitov, Olli Tanhuanpää, Markus Kakkonen, Pasi Kallio, Niku Oksala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAnastomotic false aneurysms can form due to damaged polypropylene sutures (PS). This study aimed to examine whether pinching PS with modern less traumatic vascular instruments affects their tensile strength (TS).MethodsFifteen pairs each of 3–0 Prolene and 4-0 and 5-0 Optilene, as well as 18 pairs each of 6–0 and 7-0 Surgipro II PS were tested. Half of the sutures were compressed with a Jacobson Micro Needle Holder (NH) and half with DeBakey atraumatic tissue forceps (TF) for two seconds using a standardised pressure force of 40.8 ± 5.0 g. The pressure was based on the mean pinch force produced by a total of six experienced vascular surgical consultants. The same unpaired numbers of these PS served as references. Samples were tested for breakage on a traction device with a tensile force sensor. The TS was calculated for each sample and compared with the reference. Two sets of sutures of each size, containing five NH and TF compressions on each set, were examined for possible surface damages with optical tomography.ResultsA statistically significant TS decrease was detected only after NH compression of 6–0 (p = 0.003) and 7-0 (p < 0.001) PS. On optical tomography, the extent of post-compression changes increased with decreasing diameter; the changes were similar throughout the corresponding size and instrument groups. Surfaces compressed with an NH were evenly deformed and rough, with multiple cortex damages. PS pinched with TF were unevenly deformed. The visually detectable surface damage was less extensive compared with what was observed after NH compressions.ConclusionPinching of the tested PS led to structural deformations and superficial cortex damage. Compressions of 6–0 and 7-0 PS with an NH led to significant TS reduction.

INTRODUCTION

Post-operative development of anastomotic false aneurysms is a well recognised complication that remains a problem in modern vascular surgery; the estimated occurrence is 5%.1,2 However, there is scare information on this matter. The data are chiefly presented in case reports, and the occurrence could therefore be under reported. False aneurysms can require urgent or emergency treatment and carry increased morbidity and mortality risks.2, 3, 4

The reasons for anastomotic failure could be multifactorial and include: infection, native tissue degradation, failure of implanted biological or synthetic material, and suture related issues. The last mentioned cause attracted the authors’ attention due to the lack of information on the mechanical properties of the polypropylene monofilament strand, which was first introduced in 1969.5 Despite decades of open vascular surgical experience, surprisingly little is known about the durability of the suture after its exposure to pinch forces with surgical instruments.

Despite the existing instructions for use of polypropylene sutures (PS) and the general suggestion to avoid their compression with metal instruments, the consequent changes that occur in the material are largely unknown; therefore, some surgeons may occasionally grasp sutures with instruments.6 It is believed that the only evidence based study on the matter was conducted with 6–0 monofilaments more than three decades ago.7 Otherwise, most of the authors’ assumptions are subjective and not evidence based.

With the technological development of suture processing and vascular surgical equipment over the past few decades, it remains unclear whether the less traumatic8 or atraumatic instruments that are used today could affect the durability of modern PS. Therefore, this study aimed to test the tensile strength (TS) of the sutures in a modern laboratory based setting and investigate possible reasons for suture breakage.

METHODS

Two fine and precise vascular surgical instruments that are used in everyday practice were chosen to compress sutures: the 25.4 cm Jacobson Micro Needle Holder (NH) (Scanlan International, Inc. Saint Paul, USA)8 and 20 cm DeBakey atraumatic tissue forceps (TF) (KLS Martin SE & Co. KG. Tuttlingen, Germany). The Scanlan needle holder tips are diamond dust coated, the purpose of which is to provide precise control and reduce unnecessary trauma or damage.8,9

Fifteen pairs each of 3–0 polypropylene Prolene (Johnson & Johnson Medical Ltd., Livingston, UK) and 4-0 and 5-0 Optilene polypropylene-polyethylene (B. Braun Surgical S.A., Rubi, Spain), as well as 18 pairs each of 6–0 and 7-0 Surgipro II polypropylene-polyethylene monofilament sutures (Covidien, Dublin, Ireland) were tested. Half of these sutures were compressed with the NH and half with the TF for two seconds without traction. The same unpaired number of these sutures served as references.

Instrumental pinch force

The instrumental pinch force (PF) was based on the mean NH PF produced by a total of six experienced vascular surgical consultants. The force was measured with a pressure force sensor device: RP-C-MK01X (Shenzhen Legact Technology Ltd., Guangdong, China). The surgeons were asked to imagine grasping a PS without looking at the force values that were subsequently recorded ten times per surgeon. The final mean PF was 38.2 ± 8.4, measured in grams-force (g). There was no statistical difference (p = .20; independent samples t test) between the achieved mean PF and the PF produced by the locked position of the NH (40.8 ± 5.0 g); therefore, the latter was used in the experiments for the sake of simplicity. The PF values were re-evaluated before each series of pinching ten times (a total of 50 times for all the diameter groups). To limit the PF of the TF to the level of the needle holder, a rubber piece limiter mounted with glue on the arm of the forceps was used. Similarly, the PF of the TF was then re-evaluated a total of 50 times to make sure that it remained unchanged (40.4 ± 3.1 g). The pinching of suture samples in the experiments was performed by the same person (D.V.).

Tension test

The ends of the sutures were firmly secured between two pairs of rubber plates on each side of the suture. The plates were compressed with screws and fixed on two 2 mm thick acrylic bases. The suture was symmetrically passed around a 1 mm thick dowel placed on each plate. The between the pin distance was the same for all of the studied samples: 40 mm (Fig. 1).Figure 1Images showing: A) A suture sample mounted for testing on an acrylic plate; and B) Testing a suture sample for breakage on a traction device.

Images showing: A) A suture sample mounted for testing on an acrylic plate; and B) Testing a suture sample for breakage on a traction device.

The cross sectional surface area (A) of suture samples was digitally calculated with an Andonstar AD246S-M digital microscope and its software (Shenzhen Andonstar Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). The plates with sutures were then tested on a traction device for breakage using a Standa 8MT167-100-28 motorised translation stage (Standa Ltd., Vilnius, Lithuania). The traction speed was 133 μm/second. The breaking force (BF) required to completely tear a suture apart was measured with a Futek LPM200/FSH03488 tensile force sensor (FUTEK Advanced Sensor Technology, Inc., Irvine, USA), Fig. 1. Both instrument compressed and reference sutures were tested. The TS was calculated for each sample using a TS = BF/A-formula.

Optical tomography

Two sets of sutures of each size, containing five NH and TF compressions on each set, were examined with a Fibroopt optical projection tomography device (Fibrobotics Oy, Tampere, Finland)10 to reveal possible suture damage. The characteristics of the device have previously been reported: the tomograms were obtained with an optical scale of 0.244 μm/px (500 μm × 500 μm field of view).10 A total of 420 images were recorded from a full rotation, with an angle difference (step) of 0.857° between images.10 Tomographic images were processed with Fibroopt software using an open source Python based TomoPy package.10 Three dimensional reconstruction models and tomography slices were examined using ImageJ software (NIH Image, Bethesda, USA). The cross sectional surfaces of the damaged part of the sutures were compared with uncompressed cross sections of the same sutures. Their diameters were calculated digitally with the aforementioned software.

Ethical approval was not required. No patient data or patients were involved.

Statistical analysis

Normality of data was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. The data are presented as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range. Mean values were compared using the independent samples t test and median values with the Mann–Whitney U test. The level of statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.050.

Thick 3-0 and 4-0 sutures demonstrated no significant weakening in TS levels after compressions with the studied instruments. Thinner PS from 5-0 to 7-0 were more prone to TS reduction after instrumental compression. A statistically significant TS decrease was detected only after compression of the 6-0 and 7-0 suture samples with an NH (Table 2).

Gradual extension and deformation of suture segments was detected during the TS tests. The sutures could tear apart at different levels, with a trend towards the centre of the NH compression area.

Optical tomography

On optical tomography, the NH compressions were more prominent than TF compressions, even though the same amount of force was initially applied on the instruments. The observed post-compression changes were similar throughout the diameter ranges and within the corresponding vascular instrument groups.

The surface compressed with an NH was evenly deformed and rough. The cross section of the pinched suture assumed an oval shape. Multiple suture cortex damages were detected (Fig. 2), which altered the passage of light through the material. The suture segments pinched with the TF were unevenly deformed. The suture cross section appeared flattened or demonstrated other altered shapes, depending on where and how the suture was caught between the teeth of the TF. The distorted surface altered the passage of light through the material. However, the visually detectable surface damage was less extensive compared with the damage observed after the NH compression (Fig. 2). Although the level of compression generally increased with the decreasing diameter of the suture (Fig. 3), the trend was not as consistent as after compressions with an NH.

DISCUSSION

This experimental study demonstrated that pinching thin PS with less traumatic instruments may reduce their tensile strength. However, as regarding the suture diameters used in everyday vascular practice, this is only relevant with significant certainty for the 6-0 and 7-0 PS compressed with an NH. A decrease in TS can also occur in sutures of other sizes after compression with either of the studied instruments, but such a reduction appeared to be insignificant in the current tests.

All but one of the sutures, the 3-0 Prolene being the exception, were a combination of polypropylene and polyethylene. According to the instructions for use, Prolene monofilaments only contain polypropylene. In clinical practice, such sutures are considered interchangeable. In a comparative study conducted by Chu et al., two of the above mentioned material alternatives were found to be equally good in terms of mechanical properties.11 No one would question their reliability or hardly give any preference to one manufacturer over another at the current institution.

P. Dobrin studied different mechanical impacts on the durability of a 6-0 PS.7 He found stray knots and suture grasping with TF to be the factors for TS reduction. The methodology of testing was different from the current study, which makes a direct comparison impossible. Nonetheless, the current results support Dobrin's findings, although the impact of TF on the TS in this study did not reach a level of statistical significance. The issue of TF requires attention. Apart from the TF model that was tested in this study, the NH tips are much shorter and, in the authors' opinion, tend to be more rigid. Due to the multi tooth shape and relatively long contact surface on the tips of the DeBakey TF, there is a distribution of applied force over the entire tip surface. Furthermore, sutures of smaller diameters can partially sink in between the tip teeth, which could possibly reduce the effective amount of force they receive. Therefore, it could be hypothesised that the amount of tensile stress applied to the suture with TF would mainly depend on the position of the suture between the tips. This would also explain why the cross sectional shapes of suture segments after compressions with TF varied to such a great extent.

In another study by Dobrin, 5-0 and 6-0 PS were suggested to be more durable compared with the 7-0 PS for longitudinal arteriotomy closure.12 The author pointed out an increase in suture load and consequent breaking stress of thinner strands due to the extension of a pressurised vessel. In the modern laboratory setting, the current authors have demonstrated similar findings within a wider range of sutures and diameters. Optical tomography revealed superficial damage to the suture surface after compression. In addition to compression related deformations, the findings focus on the cortex damage, which can partially explain the mechanism of suture breakage. Dobrin suggested that, due to the cooling and longitudinal traction of sutures during manufacture, the crystals of the outer surface would harden first.13 Furthermore, the surface crystals could be better oriented than the crystals in the core of the suture, with the load being chiefly borne by the former.13 In the current study, flattened segments of smaller diameter sutures with multiple superficial damaged areas would obviously require a smaller BF to tear them apart, which is in line with the previously mentioned assumption. Nonetheless, the same force applied on the thicker sutures (3-0, 4-0, and 5-0) appeared to be insufficient to produce such remarkable changes in TS. In the authors’ opinion, despite superficial cortex damage, these sutures would better withstand stress, chiefly due to their larger diameters. Even damaged sutures demonstrated high breaking strain levels in the experiments. The clinical context of these findings chiefly concerns situations with fine vascular anastomoses, such as distal arterial anastomoses of the leg and foot, vascular access surgery for haemodialysis, anastomoses on supra-aortic branches and potentially other vascular procedures where 6-0 and 7-0 sutures are preferred. Since the PF of ≈40 g, used in these tests, or even higher values, can easily be reached, particular caution should be paid to avoid pinching of such sutures with an NH.

This study has some limitations. A pair of fine, precise, less traumatic instruments that are handled and regularly maintained with care by professionals were used. The impact of unbalanced or non-maintained instruments could be different. A standardised pressure force was used, which was based on the mean PF of the vascular surgeons. Slight, statistically insignificant variations in the force applied could have occurred; however, it is not believed that these would have had a strong influence on the results, as the post-compression changes were similar throughout the sample groups. In real life conditions, PFs and pinching mechanisms can vary and differently affect the consequences. Single, short compression events were studied. Repeated or prolonged compressions could possibly lead to more severe material alterations. Finally, although the imaging technique proved beneficial for the assessment of micro damage, possible deeper fissures extending further into the core could not be detected due to the restrictions of the method. Therefore, the appearance of narrow, fissure like tears cannot be ruled out, which could have an impact on the tensile strength of sutures in the long term. Reproducing in vivo conditions is difficult, but a fatigue test with friction could help support or reject this hypothesis. The authors acknowledge small production and compound differences between the sutures of three manufacturers, but do not view this as a limitation. This study did not aim to compare the sutures of different manufacturers but rather to test the mechanical properties of sutures in each of the diameter categories.

CONCLUSION

Pinching PS led to structural deformations in all the tested sutures and to superficial damage to their cortex, being particularly prominent after compressions with an NH. The detected changes varied depending on the suture size and the instruments used. However, the TS reduction was only significant after compression of 6-0 or 7-0 PS with an NH. A fatigue test with friction could be beneficial to evaluate the consequences of compressing larger diameter sutures with an NH and sutures of all sizes pinched with TF.
