More time spent in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and sleep is associated with a lower incidence of hip or knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Mathilde Mura, Berta Portugal, Caroline Mouton, Romain Seil, Laurent Malisoux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOsteoarthritis is the second most prevalent musculoskeletal disorder and mostly affects hip and knee joints. Movement behaviours have been associated with the incidence of osteoarthritis. However, previous studies have mainly relied on self-reported questionnaires. Moreover, the inherently compositional nature of 24-hour movement behaviours has not yet been considered.ObjectiveThis prospective study aims to investigate the association between objective device-measured movement behaviours and incident hip and knee osteoarthritis, accounting for the compositional nature of movement behaviours.MethodsData from the UK Biobank cohort were used, which included individuals aged 40–69 years old. Seven days movement behaviours were measured with a wrist-worn triaxial accelerometer. Incident hip and knee osteoarthritis cases were prospectively ascertained on hospital inpatient data. Compositional Cox proportional hazard models were used.Results85 706 individuals were included in the study between 2013 and 2015. During a mean follow-up of 7.6 years, 5308 participants were diagnosed with hip and knee osteoarthritis. Results showed that reallocating 30 min/day to moderate-to-vigorous physical activity from sedentary time (HR=0.98, 95% CI 0.97 to 0.99) or from light physical activity (HR=0.94, 95% CI 0.92 to 0.96) was associated with lower incidence of hip and knee osteoarthritis. Similarly, reallocating 30 min/day to sleep from sedentary time (HR=0.97, 95% CI 0.96 to 0.99) or from light physical activity (HR=0.93, 95% CI 0.92 to 0.95) was associated with lower incidence hip and knee osteoarthritis.ConclusionUsing objective measures of movement behaviours, this study provides evidence that moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and sleep may play important roles in hip and knee osteoarthritis prevention, while accounting for the other movement behaviours. Further research is needed to understand whether the associations are causal.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Accelerometer, Hip, Knee, Behaviour

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the second most prevalent musculoskeletal disorder and affects mostly hip and knee joints.1 Previously thought as a ‘tear and wear’ disease, OA is now recognised as a multifactorial degenerative joint condition, involving immune and metabolic factors.2 Globally, more than 403 million prevalent cases of hip and knee OA were registered in 2021. Given OA’s major role in global disability, its rising prevalence and the lack of curative conservative treatments, urgent preventive public health strategies are needed to limit its growing burden. Identifying protective factors is a key component of these strategies.

Previous studies examined individually the relationship between movement behaviours (physical activity (PA), sedentary time (ST) or sleep) and OA, with inconsistent results. A meta-analysis including six cohorts (5065 participants) reported no association between self-reported PA and knee OA incidence.3 Another meta-analysis including 13 cohorts and one case-control study (507 696 participants) reported that high PA was associated with a higher risk of knee OA compared with moderate PA.4 Nonetheless, a potential protective association between PA and OA was found specifically in individuals with obesity.5 However, different definitions of PA and OA make comparisons between previous results difficult. Yet few studies used accelerometry to objectively measure PA6 and yielded inconsistent results.

Regarding ST, multiple studies reported no significant association with incident hip or knee OA.6 7 Insufficient and excessive sleep were associated with a higher risk of OA development, suggesting an optimal range of sleep duration for OA risk mitigation (7–8 hour). Nevertheless, both ST and sleep were exclusively assessed through self-reported data. Given the inherent susceptibility of self-report questionnaires to recall bias and social desirability bias, the findings should be interpreted with caution.8

Despite the literature on individual movement behaviours and OA, no study explored the relationship between the codependency of the 24-hour movement behaviours and OA incidence. The gold standard for assessing human movement behaviours in free-living conditions is device-based measurement (ie, accelerometers), since they provide more valid and accurate data and are less prone to biases. Additionally, due to the 24-hour movement behaviours interdependence and multicollinearity, a change in time spent in one movement behaviour is offset by an equal change in time spent in at least one other behaviour.9 The compositional nature of the data requires novel methods, such as compositional data analysis, to properly understand the relationship between 24-hour movement behaviours and OA incidence.

Therefore, the aim of this prospective study was to investigate the association between objective device-measured movement behaviours and incident hip and knee OA, while accounting for the compositional nature of movement behaviours. We hypothesised that reallocating time from any behaviour to light PA and moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) would reduce the incidence of hip and knee OA.

Methods

Study participants

This study uses data from the UK Biobank cohort, a population-based prospective cohort study of about 500 000 participants recruited between 2006 and 2010.10 After obtaining written informed consent, participants’ data collection included questionnaires, interviews and physical measures. Passive follow-up was facilitated through NHS healthcare data linkage, including hospital inpatient admissions and death registries.11 In this study, participants with non-valid accelerometer data (see below) were excluded. We also excluded individuals with hip and knee OA diagnosis prior to accelerometer wear, night shift workers and those with rheumatoid arthritis (ICD-10 diagnostic codes M05, M06, M07 and M13).

Accelerometer data processing

Accelerometer data were acquired between 2013 and 2015 in approximately 100 000 participants using an Axivity AX3 accelerometer worn on the dominant wrist for 24 hours/day during 7 days.12 Data were recorded at 100 Hz with a dynamic range of ±8 g.

Processing of the raw accelerometer data and classification of 24-hour movement behaviours were performed using the GGIR R-package V.3.1.0.13 Briefly, after calibration and non-wear detection, a validated heuristic algorithm14 assessed the total sleep duration. Euclidean Norm Minus one (ENMO) was calculated over 5 s epochs and the average daily ST, light PA (LiPA) and MVPA was categorised using default ENMO thresholds: ≤40 mg for ST, >40 mg and ≤100 mg for LiPA and >100 mg for MVPA.15

Participants were excluded when presenting non-valid accelerometer data, including improperly calibrated devices, reading errors, postcalibration errors >10 mg and sustained unusual high (raw) acceleration values incompatible with human movement. Non-wear time was imputed as the mean behaviour observed in corresponding time of all other recorded days. To ensure a representative sample of daily movement behaviours, participants had to wear accelerometers for a minimum of 3 valid days, including at least 1 weekend day, two nights and a daily wear duration ≥16 hours.12

OA ascertainment and censoring

Hip and knee OA were, respectively, defined using the ICD-10 diagnostic codes M16.0, M16.1, M16.9 and M17.0, M17.1, M17.9 from the earliest date documented in hospital inpatient data (until 2022, from England hospital episode statistics, Scottish Morbidity Record and Wales Patient Episode Database).16

Participants’ censoring was done at first OA diagnosis date, or at the end of their follow-up period (last date in inpatient register), whichever came first.

Covariates

A directed acyclic graph enabled identification of minimally sufficient adjustments for the association between 24-hour movement behaviours and hip and knee OA (online supplemental eFigure 1). The collection of participants’ demographics was performed through questionnaires and included: birth date, sex (male, female), employment status (employed, retired, unemployed, other), smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption (never, <3 times/week, ≥3 times/week), medication use for cholesterol, diabetes and/or hypertension. Among employees, night shift information (yes, no) was collected. Difference between date of birth and accelerometer recording date enabled calculation of age at accelerometer assessment. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight by height squared and categorised (≤24.9, 25.0–29.9, ≥30.0 kg/m2). Waist circumference was classified ≤88, >88 cm for women and ≤102, >102 cm for men. We used measures closest to accelerometer time point.17 Hypertension, diabetes and hip and knee injuries were assessed using ICD-10.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with R-packages zCompositions,18 epicoda and VIM, in RStudio Pro (V.2023.03.0).

Compositional means were calculated by normalising the daily geometric mean of observed time spent in all movement behaviours (sleep, ST, LiPA and MVPA) to a sum of 1440 min, thereby using relative time spent in different movement behaviours rather than absolute time. A variation matrix was calculated to understand the variability of each behaviour relative to the others.19

Then, isometric log-ratio (ilr) transformation (ie, pivot coordinates) enabled the mapping of compositional parts to Euclidean coordinates, reducing the dimensionality of the data and allowing usage of standard statistical methods. Pivot coordinates enable the comparison of one part of the composition with a balance of the remaining parts, allowing all-to-one and one-to-one comparisons. The first coordinate is the most straightforward to interpret, as it accounts for all behaviours. Consequently, one model per behaviour was computed to create different first coordinates.20

Compositional multivariable cox proportional hazard models were used20 21 with age as the timescale to investigate the association between movement behaviours ilr pivot coordinates and hip and knee OA incidence. This model enables the estimation of HRs, 95% CIs and two-tailed p values (α=0.05). The proportional hazards assumption was tested with Schoenfeld residuals. Main analyses were adjusted for age (timescale), sex, employment status, smoking status, drinking status, waist circumference and history of hip and knee injuries.

Less than 1% of the data were missing, they were assumed to be missing at random, and imputed using the k-nearest neighbour imputation method (k=5).22

We calculated the HR associated with reallocating 30 min to one behaviour from all others proportionally (all-to-one), and the HR associated with specific pairwise time redistributions between movement behaviours (one-to-one). Results were displayed using pairwise time-reallocation plots, which enable visualisation of the four-part composition, where time in two behaviours remained at its mean value and an equivalent amount of time was subtracted from one behaviour and added to the remaining behaviour duration.20 21

Sensitivity analyses were performed to determine the robustness of our findings: a 4-year lag was introduced to address possible reverse causation; sex-stratified analyses were conducted; the analyses were repeated separately for the hip and knee OA; BMI stratification analyses were conducted; movement behaviours were processed using GGIR and the accelerometer python-package; analyses of each movement behaviour tertile; and further adjustment was made for BMI, hypertension, diabetes and medication use for cholesterol, diabetes and/or hypertension.

Results

From the 103 612 participants with accelerometer data, we excluded 8612 participants with non-valid accelerometer data, 4968 prevalent hip and knee OA cases, 4 participants who finished their follow-up prior to accelerometer wear, 3567 participants working night shifts and 755 participants diagnosed with inflammatory arthritis. Thus, our analysis included 85 706 participants (figure 1). Average follow-up duration was 7.6±1.5 years. During follow-up, 5308 participants developed hip and knee OA (2166 hip and 3142 knee cases).

Theoretical reallocations of time across movement behaviours and OA incidence

However, reallocating 30 min/day to LiPA from all other behaviours was associated with 5% higher hip and knee OA incidence (HR=1.05, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.07).

Sensitivity analysis results were consistent with the main analysis. Nevertheless, no statistically significant results were found for reallocating 30 min/day from ST to MVPA with a 4-year exposure lag (online supplemental eFigure 2), among men (online supplemental eFigure 3), for hip OA risk (online supplemental eFigure 4) or among people with a BMI<25 kg/m2 (online supplemental eFigure 5). Among participants with a BMI <25 kg/m2, sleep was more beneficial than MVPA (online supplemental eFigure 5). The analysis further adjusted for potential mediators yielded unchanged results (online supplemental eTables 4 and 5). In analysis using the python-package, reallocating 30 min/day from sleep to MVPA was associated with lower incidence of hip and knee OA (online supplemental eFigure 6).

Stratified analyses of movement behaviours tertiles (online supplemental eFigure 7) also supported the main results. Specifically, increasing time spent in LiPA yielded consistently HRs >1. In the lowest tertile of sleep and MVPA, more time in these behaviours was associated with lower hazards of hip and knee OA. Furthermore, in the highest tertile of ST, replace ST with sleep or MVPA showed lower incident hip and knee OA.

Discussion

The aim of this prospective study was to investigate the association between objective device-measured movement behaviours and incident hip and knee OA, while accounting for the compositional nature of movement behaviours. Our analysis suggests that reallocating time from ST or LiPA to MVPA or sleep was associated with lower hip and knee OA incidence, while conversely, reallocating time to ST or LiPA was associated with higher hip and knee OA incidence. Reallocations to MVPA validate our hypothesis; yet the effect of time reallocations to sleep, and reallocation from ST to LiPA were unexpected.

Protective associations of time reallocations from ST and LiPA to MVPA align with the WHO guidelines,23 but not with previous studies on PA and hip and knee OA, which did not account for the compositional nature of movement behaviours.3 7 These discrepancies may be explained by methodological differences. While we used accelerometer-derived data, earlier studies relied mainly on self-report questionnaires. Still, our findings align with studies including patients with obesity,5 where MVPA may better regulate inflammation24 and weight reduction,25 both closely linked to hip and knee OA.2 26 As OA is also the result of an imbalanced loading of the joints,27 MVPA may provide sufficient mechanical loading on the joints to protect against OA progression.

The favourable associations of reallocating time to sleep align with recent studies using self-reported data.6 28 This indicates lower hip and knee OA risk among individuals with longer sleep, which might promote better immune system homeostasis,29 further preventing hip and knee OA. Indeed, time reallocation towards sleep appears to confer greater benefit in the shortest sleep duration tertile.

Reallocations of time from sleep and MVPA to ST and LiPA is unfavourably associated with hip and knee OA. Previous self-reported data yielded inconclusive findings regarding ST effect on hip and knee OA development.6 7 Besides, no study investigated specific effects of LiPA on hip and knee OA incidence. Surprisingly, time substitution analysis indicates that prioritising ST over LiPA may confer protective benefits against hip and knee OA incidence. This finding remained consistent across all sensitivity analyses, including when using deep learning methods, but does not fully exclude the risk of misclassification between these two behaviours.30 Nevertheless, some biological mechanisms could explain this result, such as LiPA being insufficient to trigger inflammatory regulations and/or mechanical stimulation that MVPA is activated. Additionally, LiPA includes activities such as prolonged standing, which was associated with lower extremities pain31 and varicose veins,32 compromising vascular function. Moreover, human evolution favoured bipedal stance that may also promote poor lower extremities vascularisation, contributing to distal OA development.33 Moreover, prolonged standing generates elevated pressure on the femur34 potentially increasing hip OA risk.

Given individual variability of movement behaviour, we also investigated whether differences in time spent in each time-use composition changed hip and knee OA risk. Reallocating time to sleep was only beneficial in the lowest sleep tertile. In the highest ST tertile, larger effects were observed for reallocating time to MVPA or sleep. Reallocating time to LiPA is consistently detrimental, even in the lowest tertile of LiPA. The largest effect size was observed for an increase of MVPA in the lowest MVPA tertile, with 10% lower incident hip and knee OA. The contrary was observed in the highest MVPA tertile. Altogether, these findings are in favour of individualised recommendations and interventions.

Personalised recommendations should also depend on the presence of comorbidities. Time reallocation from ST or LiPA towards MVPA yielded higher reductions of hip and knee OA incidence in overweight and obese populations than in lean ones. Conversely, time reallocations to sleep exhibited higher reductions in hip and knee OA incidence in lean populations. The high co-occurrence of obstructive sleep apnoea or different sleep patterns in participants with obesity35 may explain the diminished efficacy of increased sleep duration in obese populations. When not treated, obstructive sleep apnoea disrupts inflammatory regulation during sleep, compromising restorative processes typically associated with sleep.36

A major difference between this study and previous work is that a large number of hip and knee OA cases were identified during follow-up. This allowed us to include a 4-year lag while retaining enough incident OA cases. This analysis did not change the main findings, suggesting that reverse causation is unlikely to explain our results. Unlike other studies,37 results in men and women separately were similar to those with men and women combined. Our population was older and most women were in menopause, therefore no longer benefited from oestrogen protective effects on joints.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents several strengths. First, the UK Biobank owns the largest accelerometry dataset, with around 100 000 recordings. In this study, we included 85 706 participants with 1 week of accelerometry data and longitudinal ascertainment of hip and knee OA over a maximum period of 9.5 years. Moreover, accelerometers enable an objective measure of movement behaviours, which is more accurate and exhaustive than questionnaires.38 Furthermore, high compliance to the protocol led to <2% of missing data. In addition, the present study considers the total range of the 24-hour movement behaviours by using compositional data analyses and pivot coordinates, which accounts for the inherent interdependence and multicollinearity of movement behaviours.19 21 Finally, all hip and knee OA cases were ascertained on hospital inpatient data, which are more accurate than self-reported data.

This study also presents some limitations. Processing accelerometer data with the GGIR R-package can lead to misclassification of movement behaviours, such as ST and LiPA, particularly when no posture algorithm correction is applied. Deep learning methods enabled to more accurately identify sitting from standing.30 Although absolute time spent in each behaviour is different between these two different methods to process the accelerometer data, ranking and relative distribution are consistent and they yielded similar results, suggesting that associations were unlikely to be explained by misclassification of activities. Furthermore, moderate and vigorous PA could not be analysed separately due to low participation in vigorous PA. Furthermore, only 1 weekly accelerometer measurement was performed. While 7 days of recording is representative of human movement behaviours, this does not allow to capture the behavioural changes over time. Therefore, our results assume that there was no change of movement behaviours over time. Further studies with multiple measurements throughout the follow-up are needed to confirm these results. Moreover, the UK Biobank database is not representative of the UK population (eg, individuals with low socioeconomic status are underrepresented).11 However, previous studies have shown that associations identified within the UK Biobank were consistent with those observed in more representative populations.39 Finally, 9.5 years of follow-up were available, studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm our results and completely exclude potential reverse causation.

In conclusion, the presented findings suggest that reallocation time to MVPA and sleep can be beneficial, whereas reallocating time towards ST and LiPA may be detrimental. Compared with previous studies, our analyses emphasise the need for a more personalised, data-driven and 24-hour guidelines for hip and knee OA prevention instead of individual behaviour recommendations. Further studies are warranted to confirm whether these associations are truly causal and to understand the dose–response relationships and optimal timing for target populations.
