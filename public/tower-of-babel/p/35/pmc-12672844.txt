Improved Mindfulness Following Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy: Results from a Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial with Trauma-Exposed Black Adults
Authors: Abigail Powers, Rebecca Lipschutz, Emma C Lathan, Yara Mekawi, H Drew Dixon, Bekh Bradley, Nadine J Kaslow, Nicole R Nugent
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTrauma burden and resulting psychopathology disproportionately affect Black adults with limited socioeconomic resources. Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) is a group-based intervention with promise for addressing trauma-related psychopathology that may be of value for Black adults with limited socioeconomic resources because it may be more approachable for individuals with mental health stigma or hesitancy to engage with trauma memories. Trait mindfulness and emotion dysregulation (ED) are proposed mechanisms of action in MBCT, yet how these psychological factors shift as a result of MBCT in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptom treatment for Black adults remains untested.MethodThe goal of this secondary analysis study was to examine changes in trait mindfulness and ED from a pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) of an 8-week MBCT intervention adapted for Black trauma-exposed adults who screened positive for PTSD and depression compared to waitlist control (WLC). Eighty participants (86.3% women) were randomized. Trait mindfulness was measured across facets with the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire: observing, describing, acting with awareness, nonjudgment, and nonreacting. ED was measured using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale. Data were collected at baseline (T1), post-treatment (or 8 weeks following baseline for WLC) (T2), and 1-month follow-up (T3). Latent growth curve (LGC) models were run to examine change in trait mindfulness and ED by group status from T1 to T3.ResultsMindful observing and nonreactivity increased over time (slope) in the MBCT group versus WLC (βslope = 0.41, SE = 0.15, p = 0.005 and βslope = 0.28, SE = 0.10, p = 0.007, respectively). Group status did not predict change in ED (p = 0.93).ConclusionsFindings show that individuals in the MBCT group showed greater improvements in mindful observing and nonreactivity compared to WLC. Future efficacy trials in trauma-exposed Black adults are warranted to disentangle mechanisms of action and determine potential roles of trait mindfulness or ED in MBCT treatment outcomes.PreregistrationThe randomized controlled trial (RCT) that the data for this study came from was preregistered on Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03922581).Keywords: Trauma, Posttraumatic stress disorder, Depression, Trait mindfulness, Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy

Trauma exposure is widespread and can have long-lasting impacts on survivors’ health and well-being (Benjet et al., 2016). Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a common psychological response to trauma exposure and occurs in approximately 8.3% of trauma-exposed individuals within the USA (Kilpatrick et al., 2013). Depression is another frequent consequence of trauma, and PTSD and depression co-occur at high rates (Angelakis & Nixon, 2015; Schein et al., 2021). Importantly, Black adults with limited socioeconomic resources are at substantially heightened risk for trauma exposure, PTSD, and depression and show lifetime rates of PTSD and depression as high as 50% and 40% respectively (Alim et al., 2006; Gillespie et al., 2009; Gluck et al., 2021), making this a critical population for access to treatments that address both disorders.

Although exposure-based treatments (e.g., prolonged exposure therapy, cognitive processing therapy, written exposure therapy) are the established first-line treatments for PTSD (McLean et al., 2022), there is growing evidence that non-trauma focused treatments that address transdiagnostic processes are also effective for addressing PTSD symptoms (Hoppen et al., 2023). This is important because both mental health stigma and lack of readiness to approach trauma memories can impact treatment engagement and retention in trauma-focused treatments, particularly in Black adults (Misra et al., 2021; Ward et al., 2013); non-trauma focused treatments may be more palatable and lead to less drop-out, while still being effective for individuals with PTSD symptoms (Lenz et al., 2017; Wampold et al., 2010). Among non-trauma focused treatments, evidence supports transdiagnostic mindfulness-based interventions as a valuable second-line treatment for PTSD symptoms (Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024).

MBCT, originally designed as a relapse prevention intervention for depression (Kuyken et al., 2016), is now recognized as a transdiagnostic treatment (Geurts et al., 2021). MBCT may be particularly beneficial for individuals with co-occurring PTSD and depression symptoms given its effectiveness in treating both depression (Goldberg et al., 2019) and PTSD (Jasbi et al., 2018; King et al., 2016) and its direct focus on negative cognitions and mood symptoms that cut across both disorders (Wagner & Cáceres-Melillo, 2023). Broadly, mindfulness-based interventions strive to address transdiagnostic processes that build sustained attention and approach unpleasant emotions or memories with a non-judgmental stance (Greeson et al., 2014), which is opposite to the processes of avoidance of internal and external trauma-related emotions and memories that help to maintain PTSD symptoms (Follette et al., 2006). Studies of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) with veterans (Stephenson et al., 2017) and trauma-exposed Black women (Waldron & Burnett-Zeigler, 2022) have shown that changes in trait mindfulness scores are related to change in PTSD symptoms. Thus, trait mindfulness may be an important underlying factor in promoting symptom change (Hopwood & Schutte, 2017; Waldron & Burnett-Zeigler, 2022). Yet, exploration of MBCT effects on trait mindfulness in trauma survivors with PTSD and depression symptoms remains untested.

Mindfulness is the purposeful, nonjudgmental, and accepting awareness of present feelings, thoughts, and sensations (Kabat-Zinn, 2015). Trait mindfulness is the dispositional tendency to be aware of and attend to present moment experiences in an accepting manner across situations and contexts (Tang & Tang, 2017). Unlike state mindfulness, which measures one’s mindfulness level at a given point in time, trait mindfulness is a stable characteristic seen as either a resilience (when present) or risk (when absent) factor in psychological health (Carpenter et al., 2019; Tomlinson et al., 2018) that can be enhanced through mindfulness-based interventions and lead to positive psychological health outcomes (Alsubaie et al., 2017; van der Velden et al., 2015).

One measure that is widely used to evaluate trait mindfulness is the Five Facet of Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) (Baer et al., 2008). The FFMQ captures the multifaceted nature of trait mindfulness and assesses five unique aspects of mindfulness: observing, describing, acting with awareness, non-judging, and non-reactivity to inner experience (Baer et al., 2006, 2008). Briefly, observing is the ability to notice or attend to both internal and external experiences (e.g., thoughts, emotions); describing reflects the capacity to label one’s internal experiences with words; acting with awareness is being present in the moment and engaging in activities with full attention; non-judging reflects a refrain from evaluating or judging one’s internal experiences and approaching oneself with acceptance; and non-reactivity is an allowing of internal experiences to arise without acting on them or trying to change them (Baer et al., 2006). Results from prior research on the psychometric properties of the FFMQ in a veteran PTSD sample (Duffy et al., 2022) and a clinical sample of African Americans (Watson-Singleton et al., 2018) demonstrate the importance of looking individually at the five facets of trait mindfulness rather than an overall trait mindfulness factor. Despite this, most treatment research thus far has only reported on overall trait mindfulness.

Across numerous studies and reviews, trait mindfulness has been shown to be an important mechanism of action in MBCT (Alsubaie et al., 2017; Maloney et al., 2023; van der Velden et al., 2015). There may be many benefits to improving trait mindfulness in trauma survivors (Boden et al., 2012). As a present-oriented strategy, trait mindfulness may facilitate awareness and acceptance of emotions and thoughts, including trauma-related ones, without directly targeting trauma memories through the treatment itself (Schure et al., 2018). Two prior studies using MBSR in veteran samples have directly examined change in trait mindfulness at the facet level. First, in a pilot study of telehealth-delivered MBSR versus PTSD psychoeducation among 24 male veterans, results showed that MBSR resulted in increased observing and describing facets in particular (Niles et al., 2013). Additionally, a secondary analysis across a combined sample of four MBSR studies with veterans (n = 113) showed that increases in acting with awareness and nonreacting facets of trait mindfulness resulted in reduced PTSD symptoms (Stephenson et al., 2017). Results from a study of veterans following cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) treatment showed improved mindful awareness led to reduced PTSD symptoms (Boden et al., 2012). Importantly, prior cross-sectional analyses with trauma-exposed adults have shown that nonjudgment (Vujanovic et al., 2009) and nonreactivity (Kalill et al., 2014) are particularly relevant to PTSD symptomology. Given varied findings and differences across treatment approach and sample, expanding our knowledge of how specific facets of trait mindfulness change as a result of MBCT in particular in a trauma-exposed civilian sample with PTSD and depression symptoms is warranted.

Another relevant psychological factor to consider in MBCT treatment response for trauma survivors is emotion dysregulation (ED) (Seligowski et al., 2015). ED is a transdiagnostic risk factor for the development and maintenance of both PTSD and depression in the context of trauma (Aldao et al., 2010; Pencea et al., 2020; Powers et al., 2015). ED is a multidimensional construct that reflects deficits in the ability to identify, understand, or accept emotions, effectively modulate the intensity or duration of emotions, or implement effective regulating strategies (Gratz & Roemer, 2004; Gross, 2014). Prior research has shown that ED is reduced through mindfulness interventions (Gratz & Tull, 2010) and that emotion regulation may be a mechanism of action in MBCT for depression and anxiety (Zemestani & Fazeli Nikoo, 2020). Specifically, through mindfulness-based interventions, individuals may develop a different relationship with thoughts, emotions, or body sensations by learning to notice and name internal experiences and respond differently (and more purposely) to them (Schure et al., 2018).

To measure ED, many use the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS), a well-validated measure of overall ED and the facets of nonacceptance of emotional responses, difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior, impulse control difficulties in the presence of strong emotions, lack of emotional awareness, limited access to emotion regulation strategies, and lack of emotional clarity (Gratz & Roemer, 2004). Validation studies with the DERS across many samples (Benfer et al., 2019; Osborne et al., 2017) including Black women with limited socioeconomic resources (Mekawi et al., 2020) have shown support for a larger construct of ED in addition to facets. Most treatment research has focused on measuring change in ED using an overall ED factor.

Importantly, trait mindfulness may be a helpful protective factor against ED (Hill & Updegraff, 2012), such that as engagement in mindfulness skills grows, emotion regulation capacity increases, thus reducing ED and its adverse consequences (Freudenthaler et al., 2017; Teper et al., 2013; Vujanovic et al., 2010). Research has shown clear associations between trait mindfulness dimensions and various emotion regulation strategies, with describing and non-reactivity emerging as particularly relevant for adaptive emotion regulation (Iani et al., 2019). Given the relation between trait mindfulness and ED and their relevance in MBCT, looking directly at how trait mindfulness dimensions and ED change following MBCT treatment will be helpful in articulating the potential value of MBCT in trauma survivors.

Despite high rates of trauma exposure, PTSD, and depression in Black populations living in urban, under-resourced areas (Gillespie et al., 2009; Gluck et al., 2021), Black adults are underrepresented in mindfulness treatment research and need greater attention (Watson-Singleton et al., 2019). Prior research from our team has shown that MBCT is feasible and acceptable in trauma-exposed Black adults with PTSD and depression symptoms (Powers et al., 2023; Powers et al., 2024) and is helpful at reducing PTSD avoidance symptoms (Au et al., 2024). While the effect of MBCT on symptom change has been evaluated in veteran (Jasbi et al., 2018; King et al., 2016) and Black civilian (Au et al., 2024) samples, to our knowledge, no one has evaluated change in trait mindfulness dimensions and ED as a result of MBCT in trauma-exposed Black adults with PTSD and depression. In order to better understand the ways in which MBCT helps trauma survivors, examination of treatment effects on these important transdiagnostic psychological factors is necessary.

Thus, in the present secondary analysis study, data collected from a pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) were analyzed to determine if participation in an adapted MBCT 8-week group intervention (compared to waitlist control; WLC) resulted in improved trait mindfulness and reduced ED within a sample of trauma-exposed Black adults who screened positive for PTSD and depression symptoms and utilize healthcare services at an urban safety net hospital. We hypothesized that participation in the MBCT group vs. WLC would improve both trait mindfulness (increases) and ED (decreases) over time. Due to varied evidence regarding individual-level trait mindfulness facets in prior PTSD treatment research, nuanced hypotheses were not made. Instead, we took an exploratory approach and expected improvement across all five measured facets of trait mindfulness.

Method

Participants

Eighty Black adults (86.3% women; Mage = 44.81, SD = 12.98) who were randomized into the study were included in analyses. Most had an income less than US$2000 per month (75.4%) and were unemployed (68.4%), and almost half (41.8%) were on disability. The majority of participants had a diagnosis of current PTSD (n = 53, 67.1%) or major depressive disorder (n = 49, 62.0%). Procedure

The RCT was developed as an in-person group intervention trial to examine feasibility, acceptability, and potential mechanisms of action for MBCT versus waitlist control (WLC) that transitioned to a virtual model in 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Prior feasibility and acceptability data across the in-person (Powers et al., 2023) and virtual (Powers et al., 2024) samples and preliminary PTSD and depression outcomes data across the full sample (Au et al., 2024) were previously published. This study analyzes data gathered from the RCT on psychological factors that may serve as mechanisms of action: trait mindfulness and ED.

Participants were recruited from medical clinics within the hospital (i.e., primary care clinic, gynecology and obstetrics clinic) or via provider referrals or electronic medical record identification based on upcoming medical appointments within a large public hospital in the southeastern United States (see Fig. 1 for CONSORT flow diagram). Screening assessments were conducted in person in medical clinics or the laboratory, by phone, or over HIPAA-compliant Zoom by trained research assistants based on participant preference and/or restrictions related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Interested and eligible participants were enrolled in the RCT. Inclusion criteria were positive PTSD screen (Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5 (PC-PTSD-5) ≥ 3); positive depression screen (Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) ≥ 10); chronic trauma exposure (Traumatic Events Inventory (TEI) ≥ 3 traumas); self-identification as Black or African American; and age 18–65. All participants had to meet all inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria included active mania, psychosis, cognitive impairment, or past-month alcohol or substance use disorder.

CONSORT flowchart for full study sample

Participants enrolled in the RCT completed a baseline diagnostic assessment (T1) conducted by graduate-level practicum students or postdoctoral residents trained and supervised by a licensed psychologist. PTSD and depression diagnoses were obtained using the Clinician Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS) (Weathers et al., 2013) and the MINI International Neuropsychiatric Interview for DSM-5 (MINI-5) (Sheehan et al., 1998), although diagnosis was not required for inclusion in the study; instead screening instruments that could be more easily implemented into busy medical clinic settings to identify eligible patients were used (see details below). Both CAPS and MINI-5 are well validated and seen as gold standard diagnostic measures (Sheehan et al., 1998; Weathers et al., 2001); full details on diagnostic measures are included in Au et al. (2024). Randomization occurred at the end of the baseline assessment by interviewers if inclusion criteria were met and no exclusion criteria emerged (e.g., psychotic symptoms). If the interviewer conducting the baseline assessment had concerns regarding eligibility, the PI was contacted and the situation was discussed between interviewer and PI to determine eligibility. Randomization allocation was predetermined in blocks of 20 through an online random number generator.

Participants were randomized to MBCT group (n = 44) or WLC (n = 36) with the option to participate in the MBCT group after study completion. WLC was chosen as the comparator because the main focus of the study was feasibility and acceptability of MBCT in this trauma-exposed sample. After MBCT or WLC completion, a post-assessment (T2; after group completion or 8 weeks post-baseline) and 1-month follow-up assessment (T3) that mirrored the pre-assessment was completed; pre- and post-assessments were completed by separate interviewers who did not know study condition. In the final MBCT group, information about PTSD and depression treatments and ways to access additional mental health resources was provided to group members A formal debrief process was not included for WLC; however, during the post-assessment, clinicians provided resources and information based on participant needs (e.g., therapy resources, social needs support).

Participants randomized to MBCT attended live in-person (n = 34) or Zoom-based group sessions (n = 46) led by two trained clinicians. The MBCT intervention was patterned after the MBCT for depression intervention (Segal et al., 2002) but adapted to be trauma-informed and culturally responsive. Specifically, trauma-informed adaptations were developed based on trauma-sensitive mindfulness practice recommendations (Treleaven, 2018), review of available literature on MBCT and brief mindfulness-based interventions in trauma populations at the time (e.g., King et al., 2016; Possemato et al., 2016), and clinical experience of the first author (principal investigator [PI] of the RCT) and collaborating clinicians in treating this patient population for the past decade. Relevant psychoeducation related to trauma and PTSD was included to ensure that understanding of both PTSD and depression symptoms in the context of participant’s traumatic experiences was achieved. All clinicians were fully trained in trauma informed care principles and adjustments were made to mindfulness exercise scripts to emphasize participant autonomy at every stage of the intervention. Each group started with the same mindfulness introduction and grounding exercise to promote regularity and structure, as well as to provide a quick, tangible mindfulness skill in case of dissociation or extreme emotion dysregulation for participants in the use of mindfulness practice. Additionally, sitting meditations did not exceed 10 min because of challenges with engagement in meditation that might occur in this group (e.g., due to dissociation, to accommodate rolling admission). Before every mindfulness exercise, an orientation to the exercise was given that included an explanation of what was ahead and how to adapt as needed. Importantly, supervisors overseeing all clinical activities had extensive experience working in trauma populations and using trauma sensitive mindfulness approaches.

Cultural adaptations were developed based on available recommendations for cultural adaptations in mindfulness-based interventions (Fuchs et al., 2013; Watson et al., 2016; Woods-Giscombé & Gaylord, 2014) and consultation with expert Black clinicians familiar with using mindfulness interventions in Black communities across the protocol formulation, training, and intervention administration. Additionally, the choice of the delivery of MBCT in the primary care clinic location where patients already received services was made to enhance access and engagement from Black patients at the clinic. In supervision, clinicians focused on cultural humility principles, how session content could be considered in patient’s cultural context, and how mindfulness practice can be aligned with cultural values (e.g., prayer) with patients in the context of session content. Furthermore, there was less emphasis on written homework to avoid challenges that may arise for participants with difficulty reading or writing; instead, specific recommended practices were given out for formal and informal practices during the week and read aloud in the session. Finally, most groups were conducted with at least one group leader who self-identified as Black, although not all groups included a Black clinician due to availability of group leaders over the course of the study.

The MBCT group consisted of eight, weekly 90-min sessions that were conducted with rolling admission. Sessions involved (1) skills training and in-session practice in mindfulness techniques, (2) psychoeducation, and (3) feedback and supportive group discussion of exercises. The manualized intervention was delivered by trained and closely supervised therapists who were overseen by a board-certified and licensed psychologist with training and expertise in using MBCT to treat trauma-exposed Black adult populations.

To aid retention, participants were called three times to attempt scheduling, additional phone numbers were collected for secondary contact as needed, and all participants were compensated for assessments. Participants were compensated for their time using gift cards. Compensation for the screening assessment was US$40 and baseline diagnostic assessment (T1) was US$60. Participants in the in-person cohort received US$20 for the post-assessment and 1-month follow-up, and participants in the virtual cohort received US$60 for post-assessment and 1-month follow-up (payment increased at this stage to better reflect time demands for assessment engagement and mirror baseline assessment). Participants in the MBCT treatment group received weekly reminders for sessions. They were paid US$20 per attended in-person session or US$10 per attended virtual session (amount reduced due to reduced time/transportation demand for virtual attendance).

Measures

Initial Screening Measures for Study Inclusion

Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5 (PC-PTSD-5) (Prins et al., 2016)

The PC-PTSD-5 is a self-report screening instrument that was used for the identification of PTSD symptoms within the last 30 days. It has been validated across varied samples (Hawn et al., 2020; Van Dam et al., 2010) including in a sample of predominantly Black adult primary care patients (Lathan et al., 2023). Internal consistency in the current sample was adequate: α = 0.80; ω = 0.80. The PC-PTSD-5 includes 5 items reflecting DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for PTSD, with possible total scores in the range between 0 and 5. A total score of ≥3 was used as a cutoff for inclusion (Prins et al., 2016). The PC-PTSD-5 screen was used to assess inclusion criteria (as opposed to formal diagnosis) with future real-world implementation in a primary care clinic setting in mind, given that it is a screening tool used in primary care contexts to identify patients in need of behavioral health support.

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) (Arroll et al., 2010)

The PHQ-9 is a 9-item self-report measure of depression symptoms. The PHQ-9 has been validated in a wide range of ethnicities and in primary care (Arroll et al., 2010). Internal consistency in the current sample was adequate: α = 0.86; ω = 0.86. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), resulting in possible total scores of up to 27. A total score of ≥10, indicating at least moderate depression, was used as a cutoff for study inclusion (Arroll et al., 2010). Similarly to the PC-PTSD-5, the PHQ-9 was used to assess inclusion criteria with future real-world implementation in mind.

Traumatic Events Inventory (TEI) (Gillespie et al., 2009)

The TEI is a self-report scale used to establish the number of traumatic events throughout a person’s lifetime, such as physical assault or serious injury. The TEI has been validated in a sample of trauma-exposed Black adults (Mekawi et al., 2021). The frequency in which they occurred is measured with a scale ranging from 0 (one time) to 8 (greater than 20 times), with separate ratings for events experienced and events witnessed. Types and frequency can be added to create overall scores for each. A cutoff of at least three lifetime incidents was used for study inclusion.

Outcome Measures

Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) (Baer et al., 2006)

Trait mindfulness facets were measured with the FFMQ, a 39-item well-validated self-report measure that assesses five facets of trait mindfulness: (1) observing, (2) describing, (3) acting with awareness, (4) nonjudgment, and (5) nonreacting. The FFMQ has been validated in Black adult samples (Okafor et al., 2023; Watson-Singleton et al., 2018). Participants responded to statements based on a Likert-type scale from 1 (never or very rarely true) to 5 (very often or always true). Given prior psychometric results showing that a higher-order mindfulness factor did not exist, and a five-factor model was best fitting (Duffy et al., 2022; Watson-Singleton et al., 2018), we used only facet scores in analyses, rather than an overall trait mindfulness score. Specifically, scores for each of the five facets were calculated based on summation of scores, with higher scores suggesting higher levels of trait mindfulness. Internal consistencies across the subscales at baseline were all within the acceptable range: (1) observing α = 0.84; ω = 0.84); (2) describing (α = 0.94; ω = 0.94); (3) acting with awareness (α = 0.97; ω = 0.97); (4) nonjudgment (α = 0.96; ω = 0.97); and (5) nonreacting (α = 0.78; ω = 0.78). FFMQ was measured in all participants at baseline (T1), post-assessment (T2), and 1-month follow-up (T3).

Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) (Gratz & Roemer, 2004)

ED was measured using the DERS. The DERS was originally validated as a 36-item self-report measure that assesses difficulties in emotion regulation across six dimensions: (1) nonacceptance of emotional responses; (2) difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior; (3) impulse control difficulties in the presence of strong emotions; (4) lack of emotional awareness; (5) limited access to emotion regulation strategies; and (6) lack of emotional clarity (Gratz & Roemer, 2004). Prior research has shown validity of the DERS in a sample of Black adults with limited socioeconomic resources (Mekawi et al., 2020). However, numerous psychometric studies have shown that validity is highest when excluding awareness (); Mekawi et al., 2020; Osborne et al., 2017thus, we excluded items from the DERS awareness facet. DERS (without awareness) showed excellent internal consistency at baseline in this sample (α = 0.96; ω = 0.95). Participants responded to statements based on a Likert-type scale from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). We used a sum score of ED based on all dimensions except awareness, resulting in a 30-item scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of ED. ED was measured in all participants at baseline (T1), post-assessment (T2), and 1-month follow-up (T3). ED was also measured at weeks 3, 5, and 7 for MBCT group and week 5 for WLC. Due to more missingness in these data points and inconsistency with collection of FFMQ, the additional data points were not modeled in the presented analyses. However, secondary analyses with all data points were run and did not substantially change results.

Data Analyses

A priori power analysis using G*Power.3.1.2 was used to establish the sample size of n = 80 for the original RCT (NCT03922581) to ensure adequate power to detect clinically meaningful effects. Because it is difficult to provide a priori estimates of power for latent growth curve (LGC) models given that power depends on multiple parameters that are difficult to specify prior to the analyses, for conservative minimization of assumptions regarding power gains, we estimated power using a multiple regression framework. Defined by Cohen (1988) conventions for describing effect sizes for multiple regression (f2) are 0.02 for a small effect, 0.15 for a moderate effect, and 0.35 for a large effect. The planned sample of n = 80 was determined adequate (power = 0.80) to detect a moderate small effect of f2 = 0.25. Additionally, the study sample size was above the minimum recommendation of 50 for LGC analyses (Hamilton et al., 2003).

Compared to only analyzing mean changes in trait mindfulness and ED scales and treating individual differences as error variance, the LGC approach allows us to model observed individual-level data in the growth trajectory of the proposed mechanisms of action (Duncan & Duncan, 2009). Another strength of this strategy is the ability to model time parameters that are not evenly spaced, thereby providing greater flexibility matching design to modeled processes. Particularly relevant for our research questions, this approach offers the ability to examine the degree to which our key predictor (group status) exerts impact on both the intercept and slope (Curran et al., 2004). We used the maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR) which is recommended for this type of analysis (Salari et al., 2018). Analyses were conducted with SPSS 29.0 and Mplus Version 8 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) software packages.

First, we examined distributions of key predictor variables. The level of skewness and kurtosis in this sample fell within acceptable parameters for the sample size (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2001). Descriptive statistics of the variables of interest were computed (Table 1).

Then, we ran separate LGC models to assess change based on group status from baseline through 1-month follow-up to determine if level of change over time (slope) in variables of interest varied by group (WLC vs. MBCT). The intercept was modeled at baseline (T1), and time was parameterized based on weeks since baseline that the visit was planned; thus, T1 was modeled at 0, T2 modeled at 9, and T3 modeled at 13 to represent differences in time × weeks from baseline assessment. Group status was coded as WLC (0) and MBCT (1). The influence of group membership was modeled separately for each of the five facets of mindfulness (i.e., mindful observing, mindful describing, acting with awareness, nonjudgment, and nonreacting) and ED, equaling six total models. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple testing of the six models based on p < 0.05, resulting in a corrected significant p-value of 0.008. Previous outcome results did not show a significant difference in symptom change by intervention delivery mode (in-person versus virtual) (Au et al., 2024); and thus, sensitivity analyses considering intervention delivery mode were not conducted.

Discussion

This study presented results on change in trait mindfulness and ED from an RCT of an adapted MBCT intervention versus WLC for trauma-exposed Black adults who screened positive for PTSD and depression symptoms. Results showed initial evidence of improved mindful observing and nonreactivity following engagement in a group MBCT intervention designed for trauma survivors with PTSD and depression symptoms who are utilizing medical care in an urban safety net hospital setting. As efforts continue to diversify trauma treatment, address transdiagnostic processes, and enhance effectiveness in varied populations and settings, current findings support the benefits of MBCT for improving mindfulness skills in a socioeconomically disadvantaged Black adult population.

Our findings on increases in mindful observing and nonreactivity are consistent with prior studies of MBSR in veterans with PTSD that also found treatment resulted in increases in mindful observing (Niles et al., 2013) and nonreactivity (Stephenson et al., 2017) specifically. A major focus of MBCT is on increasing the skill of observing thoughts, feelings, and body sensations and limiting reactivity to internal and external stimuli (Sipe & Eisendrath, 2012), and so change in these facets makes sense. Grounding exercises were included at the beginning of every MBCT group session in this adapted version, and the repetition with this skill may have enhanced observing and nonreactivity skills in particular. Regular mindfulness practice may increase awareness of internal processes and external stimuli, supporting observing and nonreactivity (Mason & Hargreaves, 2001). Results also align with qualitative results showing improvements in self-reported awareness across in-person (Powers et al., 2023) and virtual (Powers et al., 2024) samples from this RCT. Mindful observing, while enhancing awareness and insight, also can bring about increased awareness of distressing thoughts and emotions, which in fact can result in increased distress and psychological symptoms (Ornstein, 2018). However, mindful observing paired with nonreactivity may help to address these potential consequences with observing alone (Desrosiers et al., 2014; Stein et al., 2022). In the context of trauma-related symptoms, nonreactivity may be particularly helpful because of the nature of trauma-related aversive internal experiences (e.g. trauma memories) and the goal in MBCT of being able to experience them without avoidance (Kalill et al., 2014).

There is more to learn about why MBCT may lead to improvements in some but not all facets of trait mindfulness with trauma survivors or what that ultimately means for symptom change. Prior outcomes data with mindfulness or CBT-based interventions have shown varied results in different samples; for example nonjudgment may be relevant for anxiety (Mizera et al., 2016) and depression symptoms (Boden et al., 2012), whereas acting with awareness (Boden et al., 2012; Stephenson et al., 2017), observing and describing (Niles et al., 2013), and nonreactivity (Stephenson et al., 2017) are relevant in the context of PTSD symptoms. This may not be surprising given that trait mindfulness facets have been found to show differential patterns with different psychological symptoms (Desrosiers et al., 2013). The specificity of the current study sample (low socioeconomically resourced Black adults with both PTSD and depression symptoms) and adaptations to MBCT (content, rolling admission) may have influenced results and should be taken into consideration. Continued research in this area is needed, including large-scale efficacy trials that help to disentangle how mindfulness facets relate to transdiagnostic symptom change in similar high-risk trauma-exposed civilian samples.

Contrary to our hypothesis, a reduction in ED in the MBCT group compared to WLC was not found. Prior outcomes research on MBSR has shown that improvements in mindfulness mediated the intervention’s effect on ED (Keng et al., 2012), so it is possible that improved mindfulness is a necessary first step to altering ED during treatment. It is also possible that longer treatment duration (more than 8 weeks) is helpful to see change in ED in chronic trauma-exposed adults with comorbid PTSD and depression. Additionally, the measure that was used for ED in this study may have been less sensitive to short-term change. Given that this study was not a fully powered efficacy trial, conclusions regarding the lack of effect on ED should be taken with caution until a larger scale trial can be run. Future research should also expand the measurement of emotion regulation and dysregulation processes to other modalities, like ecological momentary assessment, to better capture time-sensitive changes associated with treatment.

Limitations and Future Directions

Major strengths of this study included the focus on a high-symptom sample with comorbid PTSD and depression symptoms and limited number of exclusions to promote real-world generalizability, as well as the inclusion of a vulnerable population often excluded from research. There are also several limitations to consider. Although also a strength, generalizability of this sample is limited given that we had predominantly Black women in the study; additional research is necessary to evaluate mechanisms of action for MBCT interventions in samples with larger representation of Black men or gender non-binary individuals. Additionally, given the low number of Black men in the sample, sex differences with regard to outcomes could not be examined and it is possible that results may differ by sex. Finally, while the study had adequate power for the analyses that were run, a larger sample would be beneficial in future research in order to increase statistical power, enhance the generalizability of our findings, and enable complex outcome analyses that can account for individual variability and support meaningful subgroup analyses (e.g., sex differences). Future large-scale outcome studies should examine how changes in psychological factors, such as trait mindfulness, contribute to changes in symptoms following treatment.

Overall, this study provides evidence for the benefit of this MBCT intervention in improving mindful observing and nonreactivity. Rates of trauma exposure and resulting psychological symptoms, like PTSD and depression, are extremely high in low resourced urban Black communities (Gillespie et al., 2009; Gluck et al., 2021), and so it is critical to offer transdiagnostic interventions that can aid trauma-exposed individuals and there may be value in incorporating mindfulness-based group resources into medical clinic settings, like primary care, where many trauma survivors receive care. MBCT and other mindfulness-based interventions have clear potential to serve as one tool in the recovery process, and large-scale efficacy trials are a necessary next step to better understand the benefits and long-term effects of MBCT in trauma-exposed individuals.
