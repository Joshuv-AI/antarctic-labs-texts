Comprehensive analysis of metabolic reprogramming-related gene signatures for predicting ovarian cancer prognosis, the immune landscape, and potential treatment options
Authors: Rendong Han, Enhui Guo, Zhen Li, Huansheng Zhou, Bei Liu, Yan Wang, Xiaomei Hou, Fumin Zheng, Yanan Xu, Jianhong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer (OV) is the most lethal gynaecologic malignancy. Metabolic reprogramming is a distinct feature of cancer and is associated with tumourigenesis and progression. It could be a potential therapeutic target for cancer treatment and a biomarker for assessing cancer prognosis. In this study, we identified metabolic reprogramming-related differentially expressed genes (MRRDEGs) in OV through differential gene expression analysis and conducted a comprehensive characterization of these MRRDEGs. On the basis of the MRRDEGs, we constructed a prognostic risk model that included five model genes for OV. The risk score was an independent prognostic factor that could predict the survival of OV patients. It could classify OV patients into distinct risk groups with significant differences in survival. We observed significant differences between risk groups in terms of biological pathway activity, immune cell infiltration patterns, and immunotherapy responses. Specifically, compared with the high-risk group, the low-risk group had a potentially superior immunotherapy response. These findings significantly advance our understanding of the relationships between metabolic reprogramming and OV pathogenesis, progression, prognosis, and immunotherapy response, laying a foundation for the development of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets in the future and providing an important reference for the formulation of precision medicine strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05295-4.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OV) ranks as the third most prevalent gynaecological malignancy worldwide, yet it has the highest mortality rate among all female reproductive system cancers [1]. Global cancer statistics reveal an annual incidence of 313,959 OV cases and 207,252 related deaths [2], with delayed diagnosis being a major contributor to its poor prognosis. Approximately 66% of OV patients are diagnosed with International Federation of Gynaecological Oncology (FIGO) stage III or IV disease, and the 5-year survival rates are only 41% and 20%, respectively [3]. The standard first-line treatment includes primary cytoreductive surgery (PCS) with platinum-based chemotherapy supplemented by angiogenesis inhibitors (e.g., bevacizumab) and/or poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis) as maintenance therapy [1, 4]. Despite these interventions, most patients experience disease recurrence [2]. The development of resistance not only to chemotherapeutic agents but also to currently approved targeted therapies (bevacizumab and PARPi) highlights the challenges in achieving complete remission in OV [3, 5]. Currently, the 5-year survival rate for patients with OV has not improved significantly, and there are still no suitable models or biomarkers for predicting the prognosis of patients with OV.

Metabolic reprogramming, a well-recognized hallmark of cancer (including OV), is an adaptive response to nutrient deprivation and hypoxia during tumour progression [6, 7]. This metabolic reprogramming provides essential biomolecules and energy to support malignant proliferation [8]. Furthermore, it drives tumour aggressiveness through microenvironmental metabolic competition, contributing to treatment resistance and metastatic dissemination. Emerging evidence has linked similar metabolic alterations to fibrotic disorders via regulation of the extracellular matrix and oxidative stress [9] and to chemotherapy resistance through the activation of the mevalonate pathway [10]. In OV specifically, the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis) has been demonstrated to be critical for tumour initiation and progression [11]. Additional metabolic alterations involving amino acid, lipid, and purine metabolism have also been reported [12–14]. Recent studies have further characterized oxidative phosphorylation-related subtypes in OV [15] and revealed how metabolic signalling pathways mediate immune crosstalk between Tregs and M2 macrophages during OV progression [16]. Despite these insights, the precise mechanisms governing metabolic reprogramming in OV remain incompletely characterized. Our study aims to systematically characterize metabolic alterations in OV and assess their potential role in ovarian carcinogenesis, as well as their utility for prognostic prediction and therapeutic targeting.

Advances in immunotherapy are further revolutionizing the therapeutic strategies for OV [1]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which represent a breakthrough in cancer treatment, act by reactivating antitumour immune responses suppressed by tumour cells [17]. While ICIs have shown promising antitumour effects in gynaecological cancers, only a small subset of OV patients benefit from immunotherapy. Thus, identifying predictive biomarkers for immunotherapy response is critical for optimizing its future application in OV. The limited efficacy is likely attributed to the highly immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME) and its associated alterations [17]. Metabolic reprogramming, a key feature of the TME, has been shown to promote immune evasion and hinder immunosurveillance [18, 19]. Beyond metabolism, gene signatures themselves have emerged as valuable tools for predicting prognosis and the immune landscape, as demonstrated in pancancer analyses [20] and across specific tumour types [21]. Therefore, it is necessary to study aberrant metabolic reprogramming in OV and the TME to develop biomarkers for predicting immunotherapy response. Furthermore, targeting these metabolic alterations might increase therapeutic efficacy and overcome resistance to immunotherapy.

Current research should focus on addressing critical gaps in OV clinical management, particularly in elucidating disease pathogenesis, developing novel targeted therapies, and establishing reliable predictive biomarkers. Our study aims to systematically analyse metabolic reprogramming in OV, evaluate its role in tumourigenesis and immune microenvironment regulation, and explore its clinical implications for prognosis prediction and therapeutic targeting. We integrated OV-related data to identify metabolic reprogramming-related differentially expressed genes (MRRDEGs) in OV and analysed their genomic alteration profiles. Functional enrichment analyses were employed to investigate the association between these MRRDEGs and OV. Furthermore, we constructed a prognostic risk model based on MRRDEGs and validated its predictive performance. Comparative analyses of biological pathway activity and immune microenvironment features were conducted across different risk groups. Finally, we performed differential expression verification and correlation analysis of the model genes across multiple independent datasets. The workflow for this study is shown in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

We obtained ovarian cancer (OV) samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and control samples from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/) using the R package TCGAbiolinks [22]. These datasets were merged to create the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset, which served as our primary test set. After excluding samples lacking clinical information, we retained 374 OV cases and 88 controls whose complete clinical data were available in count format. Corresponding clinical metadata were obtained from the UCSC Xena database [23], and the detailed information is shown in Table 1. Owing to the confounding data source and biological group in the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset (tumour samples from TCGA, normal samples from the GTEx), cross-database batch correction was not applied to avoid removing genuine biological differences. While this approach prioritizes biological signals, it does not fully account for potential source-specific biases. Therefore, all the model genes were subsequently validated in independent, batch-corrected GEO datasets.

OV Ovarian Cancer

Three additional OV datasets (GSE14407 [24], GSE29450 [25], and GSE26712 [26]) were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using the GEOquery package [27] (v2.70.0). All samples were derived from human ovarian tissue, with GSE14407 and GSE29450 using the GPL570 platform and GSE26712 using GPL96. The specific information is shown in Table 2. The GSE14407 dataset contained 12 OV and 12 control samples. The GSE29450 dataset contained 10 OV and 10 control samples. The GSE26712 dataset contained 185 OV and 10 control samples. All the OV and control samples were included in this study.

We searched the GeneCards database [28] with “metabolic reprogramming” as the search keyword, retained only the protein-coding results, and ultimately obtained 1423 metabolic reprogramming-related genes (MRRGs). Five additional MRRGs were obtained with “metabolic reprogramming” as a keyword to search on the PubMed website in the published literature [29]. After merging (1,428 total) and removing genes that were absent from our OV datasets, we retained 1,179 MRRGs for analysis, as presented in Table S1.

Batch effects were removed from the GEO datasets using the sva package [30] (v3.50.0), creating a combined dataset of 207 OV and 32 control samples. Data normalization was performed with limma [31] (v3.58.1). Batch effect removal was validated through principal component analysis (PCA) [32].

Differential expression analysis

Using limma [31], we compared gene expression between the OV and control groups in TCGA_GTEx-OV. A stringent |logFC| > 4 threshold (with adj. p < 0.05) was selected to prioritize high-confidence differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with large effect sizes, thereby reducing noise and enhancing the reliability of subsequent analyses. However, this approach may exclude genes with moderate expression changes (|logFC| 1–4) that could still harbour biological relevance, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the scope of the identified MRRDEGs. Accordingly, DEGs were defined as those whose |logFC| > 4 and adj. p < 0.05. The differential analysis results were visualized as volcano plots using the R package ggplot2 (v3.4.4). MRRGs overlapping with DEGs were designated MRRDEGs and were visualized via Venn diagrams. The top 20 MRRDEGs were displayed in heatmaps using pheatmap (v1.0.12).

Genomic alteration analysis

To analyse the somatic mutation (SM) in the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset, the “Masked Somatic Mutation” data were selected and preprocessed using VarScan software. The R package maftools [33] was used to visualize the situation of SM in OV. To analyse the copy number variation (CNV) in the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset, the “Masked Copy Number Segment” data were selected and analysed. We visualized the CNV type and frequency using the R package ggplot2 (v3.4.4).

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis [34] is a commonly used method for conducting large-scale functional enrichment studies, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [35] is a widely used database for storing information related to genomes, biological pathways, diseases, drugs, etc. We performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of MRRDEGs using clusterProfiler (v4.10.0) [36]. Adj. p < 0.05 and an FDR value (q value) < 0.25 were considered statistically significant thresholds. The p values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA [37] was performed to identify significantly enriched gene sets associated with the phenotype using predefined molecular signatures. In this study, the genes from the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset were ranked by logFC values and analysed using clusterProfiler with the following parameters: random seed = 2020, minimum gene set size = 10, and maximum gene set size = 500. The c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt gene set from MSigDB [38] was utilized, with significance determined at p < 0.05.

Construction of the prognostic risk model

We developed a prognostic risk model for OV using the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset through a multistep analytical approach. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was performed using the glmnet package [39] (v4.1-8), with family = “cox” specification, incorporating MRRDEGs identified from univariate Cox analysis. LASSO coefficient path plots and cross-validation curves were used to visualize the results of the LASSO regression analysis. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was subsequently performed to integrate the clinical variables to identify the final prognostic model genes, and the results are presented in a forest plot. The risk score was calculated on the basis of the risk coefficient of multivariate Cox regression analysis, and the following formula was used to calculate the risk score:

Prognostic risk model validation

We evaluated the prognostic performance of our risk model using multiple approaches. Kaplan‒Meier (KM) survival analysis was performed using the survival package (v3.5-7) to compare overall survival (OS) between high- and low-risk groups stratified by the median risk score [40, 41]. A time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve [42] was constructed on the basis of the risk score and OS using the survivalROC package (v1.0.3.1) to assess the model’s predictive accuracy at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals. The AUC values ranged from 0.5 to 0.7 (low accuracy), 0.7–0.9 (moderate accuracy), and > 0.9 (high accuracy).

In addition, we performed comprehensive validation analyses to evaluate the clinical relevance and prognostic power of the risk score. First, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed on the basis of risk score, patient age, and clinical stage. Forest plots were used to visualize the results. A prognostic nomogram [43] was constructed using the rms package (v6.7-1) to integrate the multivariate Cox regression results, enabling the prediction of 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities.

Calibration curve analysis was used to compare the consistency between the predicted and observed outcomes and assess the accuracy of the model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) implemented with the ggDCA package (v1.1) was used to evaluate the clinical utility of the nomogram for survival prediction.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) of ovarian cancer risk groups

We performed GSVA [44] to investigate differences in pathway activity between the high- and low-risk OV groups. This nonparametric, unsupervised method transforms gene-level expression matrices into pathway-level enrichment scores. Using the h.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt gene set from the MSigDB database, we calculated pathway enrichment scores for all the OV samples in the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset. The samples were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups on the basis of the median risk score. Differences in pathway enrichment were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Validation and correlation analysis of model genes

To validate the differential expression of model genes between the high- and low-risk groups of OV samples, we performed Mann‒Whitney U tests on both the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset and the combined datasets. The results were visualized using comparative boxplots showing expression distributions across risk groups.

To explore the coexpression relationships among the model genes, we performed correlation analysis using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. The results were visualized through chord diagrams generated with the igraph (v1.6.0) and ggraph (v2.1.0) packages. An absolute value of the correlation coefficient (r) below 0.3 indicated weak or no correlation, 0.3–0.5 indicated a weak correlation, 0.5–0.8 indicated a moderate correlation, and above 0.8 indicated a strong correlation.

Identification of molecular subtypes and correlation analysis of model genes

We performed a consensus clustering analysis [45] based on model genes to identify molecular subtypes of OV samples from the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset. The ConsensusClusterPlus package [46] (v1.62.0) was used. This robust algorithm employs iterative subsampling (50 iterations with 80% sample proportion) to evaluate cluster stability and determine the optimal number of molecular subtypes. Specifically, the number of clusters was set within the range of 2 to 9, with clusterAlg = “km” and the distance metric = “Euclidean”. The optimal k was determined by the consensus matrix, consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve, and delta area plot.

The differential expression of model genes across subtypes was visualized using a heatmap generated by the R package pheatmap (v1.0.12), and subtype separation was validated via a 3D t-SNE plot. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between model genes, with significant correlations (p < 0.05) visualized in a heatmap. The top positive and negative correlations were further plotted as scatter diagrams.

Immune microenvironment profiling

The tumour immune landscape was characterized using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [47] to quantify the relative abundance of each immune cell population, including activated CD8 + T cells, activated dendritic cells, gamma-delta T cells, natural killer cells, and various human immune cell subtypes, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs). Enrichment scores calculated from ssGSEA were used to generate an immune infiltration matrix for all the samples. Differential immune cell abundance between the high- and low-risk groups was visualized using a comparative boxplot created with the ggplot2 package, followed by the identification of significantly different immune populations (p < 0.05). Subsequent correlation analyses included examination of immune cell‒immune cell interactions through heatmap visualization (pheatmap package) and evaluation of model gene‒immune cell relationships using bubble plots (ggplot2 package).

Immunotherapy biomarker assessment

The immunotherapy response potential was evaluated through analysis of three key biomarkers. Tumour immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) scores were obtained from the TIDE web portal (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) [48, 49] to predict potential tumour treatment response. The microsatellite instability (MSI) status and tumour mutation burden (TMB) data were acquired from the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/) [50]. Differences in TIDE scores, MSI status, and TMB between the high- and low-risk groups were statistically assessed using Mann‒Whitney U tests to identify significant differences in immunotherapy response potential between risk groups.

Statistical methods

All the statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.3.0). Continuous variables following normal distributions were compared using Student’s t test, while nonnormally distributed variables were analysed with the Mann‒Whitney U test for two-group comparisons or the Kruskal‒Wallis test for multiple-group comparisons. Associations between variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. All the statistical tests were two-tailed, with p values < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Identification of MRRDEGs

Differential expression analysis of the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset identified 3,107 significant DEGs (|logFC| > 4, adj. p < 0.05), comprising 2,534 upregulated and 573 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). Intersection with MRRGs yielded 64 high-confidence MRRDEGs (Fig. 3B). The detailed information is shown in Table S2. Heatmap visualization revealed distinct expression patterns of the top 20 MRRDEGs between OV and control samples (Fig. 3C).

Genomic alterations in MRRDEGs

SM analysis of all genes revealed nine predominant mutation types in OV, with missense mutations being most frequent (Fig. 4A). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) constitute the majority of genetic alterations, with C > T transitions representing the most frequent single nucleotide variant (SNV) in OV. Among the MRRDEGs, MUC16 had the highest mutation frequency (8%) (Fig. 4B). CNV analysis revealed 52 MRRDEGs with significant CNV alterations. The top 20 genes with CNVs were visualized through lollipop plots (mutation types; Fig. 4C) and frequency histograms (Fig. 4D).

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed to elucidate the biological significance of the 64 MRRDEGs in OV (Table 3). The MRRDEGs were significantly enriched in the following terms: mitotic nuclear division, regulation of mitotic cell cycle phase transition, regulation of mitotic cell cycle phase transition, regulation of cell cycle phase transition, mitotic cell cycle phase transition, nuclear division, and other BPs, chromosomal region, spindle, mitochondrial matrix, kinetochore, condensed chromosome, centromeric region, and other CCs. It was also enriched in the cell cycle, cellular senescence, human T-cell leukaemia virus 1 infection, p53 signalling, gastric cancer, and other biological pathways. The results of the GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were visualized through bar graphs (Fig. 5A) and network diagrams (Figs. 5B–D) that illustrate the relationships between molecular entities and functional annotations.

Validation of ovarian cancer prognostic risk model

Time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrated that the OV prognostic risk model exhibited low predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival (AUC: 0.5–0.7; Fig. 8A). KM analysis revealed highly statistically significant differences in OS between the high- and low-risk groups (p < 0.001; Fig. 8B).

The univariate analysis initially incorporated risk score, age, and clinical stage variables, with all the factors for which p was < 0.10 subsequently included in the multivariate analysis (Fig. 8C–D; Table 5). Both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses revealed that the risk score, age, and clinical stage were significant prognostic factors. The nomogram (Fig. 8E) indicated that the risk score contributed substantially more to prognostic prediction than the other variables did, whereas clinical stage had relatively weaker predictive value.

Calibration curves (Fig. 8F-H) revealed optimal predictive performance at the 1-year follow-up. Finally, DCA (Fig. 8I-K) revealed the greatest clinical utility at the 3-year follow-up (3 years > 1 year > 5 years).

Validation and correlation analysis of the model genes

To validate the risk stratification potential of our prognostic model gene signature, we compared the expression patterns of five candidate genes (PTTG1, LPAR3, PAX2, PDK4, and DIO3) between the high- and low-risk OV groups across two independent datasets. All five genes were highly significantly differentially expressed between the high- and low-risk groups in the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset (p < 0.001; Fig. 10A). The expression of four genes (PTTG1, LPAR3, PDK4, and DIO3) remained highly significantly different between the high- and low-risk groups in the combined dataset (p < 0.001; Fig. 10B). PAX2 expression did not significantly differ between the two groups (p > 0.01).

The cross-dataset correlation patterns spearman correlation analysis of the five model genes revealed consistent coexpression patterns between the TCGA_GTEx-OV and combined datasets (Fig. 10C–D). In both the TCGA_GTEx-OV and combined datasets, PTTG1 was positively correlated with LPAR3 and negatively correlated with PDK4 and DIO3. PTTG1 emerged as a central hub in three significant pairs.

Identification of molecular subtypes and correlation analysis of model genes

Consensus clustering analysis based on five model genes (PTTG1, LPAR3, PAX2, PDK4, and DIO3) revealed two molecularly distinct subtypes: Subtype 1 (Cluster 1, n = 302) and Subtype 2 (Cluster 2, n = 72) (Fig. 11A–C). The 3D t-SNE visualization (Fig. 11D) revealed almost complete spatial separation between the two subtypes, validating the robustness of the clustering results. A heatmap of model gene expression (Fig. 11E) revealed that PAX2 was predominantly downregulated in Subtype 1 and upregulated in Subtype 2, suggesting its role as a subtype-defining marker.

A correlation heatmap of the model genes (Fig. 11F) revealed three significantly associated gene pairs (marked with *, p < 0.05): PTTG1-LPAR3, PTTG1-PDK4, and PTTG1-DIO3, which is consistent with the chord diagram results. The top positive (PTTG1-LPAR3, r = 0.108, p = 0.037) and negative (PTTG1-PDK4, Spearman’s r = -0.208, p < 0.001) correlations were visualized using scatter plots (Fig. 11G-H), providing quantitative evidence for coregulatory mechanisms.

Immune infiltration analysis between the high- and low-risk groups

Using the expression matrix from the TCGA_GTEx-OV database, we quantified the infiltration abundance of 28 immune cell types through the ssGSEA algorithm. The comparative analysis revealed significant differences in immune cell infiltration patterns between risk groups. The comparative boxplot (Fig. 12A) revealed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) in seven immune cell types between high- and low-risk OV samples: activated CD4 + T cells, CD56dim natural killer cells, eosinophils, immature dendritic cells, memory B cells, neutrophils, and type 2 T helper cells.

Subsequent correlation analysis (Fig. 12B–C) revealed distinct immune cell interaction patterns between risk groups. In low-risk OV samples, immune cells were strongly positively correlated, with the most significant correlation observed between type 2 T helper cells and activated CD4 + T cells (r = 0.62, p < 0.05; Fig. 12B). Similarly, high-risk samples exhibited predominantly positive correlations, particularly between activated CD4 + T cells and neutrophils (r = 0.60, p < 0.05; Fig. 12C).

Furthermore, bubble plots illustrating the correlation between model genes and immune cell infiltration (Fig. 12D–E) revealed specific gene–immune cell associations. The results revealed that the expression of the DIO3 gene and neutrophils was most strongly positively correlated in the low-risk samples (r = 0.413, p < 0.05; Fig. 12D), whereas the expression of the PTTG1 gene and the number of type 2 T helper cells were most strongly significantly positively correlated in the high-risk group (r = 0.324, p < 0.05; Fig. 12E).

Discussion

OV remains the most lethal gynaecologic malignancy [1]. Owing to the lack of reliable early detection tools, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages [3]. Despite the clinical achievements of progressive treatment modalities, high mortality rates persist. The predictive and prognostic value of classic OV biomarkers, such as carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) and human epididymis protein 4 (HE4), remains controversial [4], and no reliable tools currently exist for prognostic risk assessment. Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer and plays a critical role in tumourigenesis and progression [8]. This process not only represents a potential therapeutic target but also serves as a promising biomarker for cancer prognosis. It has demonstrated strong prognostic performance in various cancers, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma [51], pancreatic adenocarcinoma [52], head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [53], lung adenocarcinoma [54], and hepatocellular carcinoma [54]. Therefore, leveraging metabolic reprogramming to develop valuable prognostic signatures holds significant clinical importance for OV prognostic risk stratification and personalized treatment strategies. In this study, we conducted bioinformatics analyses to identify MRRDEGs in OV and constructed a prognostic risk model based on these MRRDEGs.

The understanding of genomic alterations in OV-associated MRRDEGs remains limited. Our study involved a systematic analysis of these alterations. Consistent with the findings of previous reports, the most common SM of all genes in OV was TP53 (94%) [55]. The mutation type of SM was mainly SNPs, with missense mutations constituting the majority. Among the 64 MRRDEGs, MUC16 had the highest mutation rate (8%). MUC16, also known as CA125, is expressed in OV epithelial cells and is a well-established biomarker for OV screening [56]. Studies have demonstrated that MUC16 plays crucial roles in cellular adhesion, signalling pathways, and immune regulation, thereby promoting tumour cell proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion [57]. CNVs might arise from errors in genome recombination or replication and could influence gene expression through multiple mechanisms [58]. CNVs were detected in 52 of the 64 MRRDEGs, which might result in the aberrant expression of metabolic genes, consequently leading to abnormal cell growth and promoting tumourigenesis. The high frequency of CNVs in OV suggests that metabolic genes might significantly contribute to disease pathogenesis and could serve as potential therapeutic targets.

Metabolic reprogramming serves as a critical mechanism that enables cancer cells to acquire proliferative advantages, resist apoptosis, and adapt to the harsh TME [18, 59]. Our GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed that the 64 MRRDEGs were predominantly associated with cell cycle regulation and mitotic biological processes. These findings were consistent with previous reports that tumour cells evade cell cycle regulation and proliferate rapidly through metabolic reprogramming [60]. Such dysregulated cell cycle progression might reduce the effective time window for chemotherapeutic agents, potentially contributing to tumour drug resistance [61]. Previous studies have reported that cancer cells can bypass cellular senescence and continue to proliferate by altering the expression or function of cell cycle regulatory proteins such as p53 [62]. The observed enrichment of MRRDEGs in senescence-related pathways suggests that tumour cells might modulate the expression of these genes during their evasion of senescence and subsequent influence on neighbouring normal cells. In addition, the MRRDEGs were enriched in the p53 signalling pathway. In OV, the TP53 gene is among the most frequently mutated genes [63]. TP53 mutations not only impair the normal regulatory functions of the cell cycle but also disrupt the regulation of cellular metabolism [64–66]. This dysfunction ultimately results in the uncontrolled proliferation of tumour cells. Metabolic reprogramming of OV cells might also involve feedback regulation of the p53 signalling pathway, further affecting the activity and stability of p53 [64].

On the basis of the MRRDEGs, we developed a prognostic risk model that included five key genes for OV. These genes not only stratify OV patients into distinct risk groups but also reveal critical biological drivers of disease progression. PDK4 emerged as the predominant risk factor in our model, which is consistent with its well-characterized role in promoting glycolytic dependency (Warburg effect) in tumour cells through inhibition of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDH) [67, 68]. Accumulating clinical evidence confirms that PDK4 is a critical metabolic biomarker and oncogene in multiple cancer types [69]. In OV specifically, PDK4 overexpression is correlated with metastasis, chemotherapy resistance, and poor prognosis [69, 70]. The pro-invasive activity of PDK4 appears to be mediated through activation of the STAT3/AKT/NF-κB/IL-8 signalling cascade [70]. Additionally, emerging research has revealed complex regulatory networks involving multiple upstream molecules that modulate PDK4 expression and consequently influence OV progression [71–73]. These findings highlight the role of PDK4 as a potential therapeutic target and prognostic biomarker in OV.

PTTG1 has emerged as a critical oncogene in multiple malignancies, with recent studies highlighting its multifaceted role in OV progression [74, 75]. Elevated PTTG1 expression in OV significantly contributes to tumour angiogenesis, M2 macrophage polarization, and epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) [76, 77]. Moreover, DIO3, a thyroid hormone-inactivating enzyme, plays a pivotal role in the regulation of cellular metabolism [78]. A study by Hou et al. [79] revealed that reduced DIO3 expression is correlated with unfavourable OV patient outcomes, including shorter OS and progression-free survival (PFS). Notably, DIO3 expression was significantly associated with the expression of tumour-associated macrophage (TAM) markers and various T-cell subsets in OV patients. Moskovich et al. [80] further elucidated the context-dependent nature of DIO3, suggesting its functional diversity across different OV subtypes, stages, and histological grades. The tumour suppressive potential of the DLK1-DIO3 locus was further elucidated by Cui et al. [81], who identified miR-134-3p as a key regulator that inhibits OV stem cell proliferation and invasion through RAB27A targeting. LPAR3 contributes to OV progression through multiple mechanisms. Existing evidence confirms its regulation of tumour proliferation via the miR-198/MET axis [82], whereas emerging data suggest its potential role in cisplatin resistance through miR-634/PDK1 signalling [83].

In our study, PAX2 effectively classified OV cases into two molecular subtypes, underscoring its significance in tumour subtyping. Research has shown that the expression profile of PAX2 is unique to OV pathogenesis. While absent in normal ovarian surface epithelium, it becomes aberrantly activated in 61% of OV cell lines [84, 85]. Experimental evidence supports the multifaceted role of PAX2 in tumour progression through [1] promoting angiogenesis [2], enhancing cancer cell proliferation and invasion, and [3] reprogramming fatty acid metabolism [86, 87]. Clinically, elevated PAX2 expression is significantly associated with adverse outcomes in serous OV patients and is markedly upregulated in platinum-resistant patients [87, 88]. However, its occasional tumour-suppressive effects in certain OV subtypes underscore the context-dependent nature of its biological functions [86]. The prognostic model genes examined in this study were significantly associated with OV outcomes. While the clinical relevance of certain genes aligns with our findings, the complete prognostic potential of others warrants further investigation to fully elucidate their roles in OV pathogenesis and treatment response.

We observed that immune cells critical for tumour suppression, such as activated CD4 + T cells and CD56dim natural killer cells, were significantly more abundant in the low-risk group. Additionally, the infiltration levels of type 2 T helper cells were also elevated in these patients. Traditionally, the type 2 immune response driven by type 2 T helper cells is considered immunosuppressive and linked to tumour immune evasion [89]. However, emerging evidence suggests that the type 2 immune response might instead enhance antitumour immunity, challenging the conventional understanding [90]. For instance, Feng et al. [91] demonstrated that the type 2 cytokine Fc–IL-4 could directly act on terminally exhausted CD8 + T cells in tumours, increasing their glycolysis, survival, and effector function, thereby synergizing with type 1 immunity to exert antitumour effects. In contrast, immune infiltration in the high-risk group indicated poor antitumour function and possibly even immune escape. Collectively, these findings indicate that patients in the low-risk group exhibit a more robust antitumour immune response, contributing to their favourable prognosis. Furthermore, correlation analyses between model genes and immune cell infiltration revealed complex interactions, with DIO3 showing particularly strong positive correlations with most immune cell types. These findings suggest that these genes might play dual roles in metabolic and immune regulatory processes.

To further assess immunotherapy responsiveness, we applied the TIDE algorithm to evaluate the OV cohort. A lower TIDE score indicates a reduced potential for immune evasion, suggesting a greater likelihood of clinical benefit from immunotherapy. Our analysis revealed significantly lower TIDE scores in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group, suggesting potentially better immunotherapy responsiveness in low-risk patients. Consequently, low-risk patients might be optimal candidates for immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 blockade), potentially achieving better therapeutic outcomes. These findings could facilitate patient stratification for immunotherapy and enhance treatment efficacy.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the analysis is retrospective and based on publicly available databases. Although we validated our findings in independent GEO datasets, prospective validation in larger, multicentre clinical cohorts is still needed to confirm the generalizability of our risk model. Second, owing to the confounding between the data source and biological group in the TCGA_GTEx-OV dataset (tumour samples from TCGA and normal samples from the GTEx), cross-database batch correction was not applied to avoid removing genuine biological differences. While we used a stringent |logFC| threshold to enhance signal robustness and subsequently validated our model genes in external batch-corrected GEO datasets, potential source-specific biases cannot be completely excluded.

Third, the prognostic risk model showed limited predictive accuracy according to time-dependent ROC analysis (AUC: 0.5–0.7 at the 1-, 3-, and 5-year time points), indicating that its clinical utility as a standalone predictor remains modest. Nevertheless, the risk score successfully stratified patients into groups with significantly different survival outcomes (p < 0.001), supporting its value for risk stratification. Fourth, our assessment of immunotherapy response relied solely on computational surrogates (TIDE, MSI, and TMB) rather than clinical outcome data from OV patients treated with ICIs. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating, and future studies with real-world immunotherapy cohorts are necessary to validate the predictive value of our risk model. Finally, all conclusions are derived from bioinformatics analyses. Functional experiments (e.g., qPCR, Western blot, CRISPR, or in vitro/in vivo assays) are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of the five model genes in OV pathogenesis and progression.

Conclusions

In this study, we systematically characterized metabolic reprogramming-related gene expression alterations in OV. By integrating multidataset bioinformatics analyses, we identified 64 MRRDEGs and constructed a five-gene prognostic risk model (PTTG1, LPAR3, PAX2, PDK4, and DIO3). This risk score independently stratified OV patients into groups with significantly different OS. Notably, the low-risk group exhibited enhanced immune cell infiltration and lower TIDE scores, suggesting a potentially favourable response to immunotherapy.

Our findings establish a connection between metabolic reprogramming and the immune landscape in OV, providing a foundation for future biomarker development and therapeutic targeting. Despite its modest predictive accuracy, the proposed risk model offers a new perspective for patient stratification. Further experimental and prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
