Propofol Inhibits Neutrophil Extracellular Traps Formation and Alleviates Sepsis-Induced Acute Lung Injury
Authors: Wei Wang, Ran Xia, Chengmi Zhang, Muqiao Cheng, Yi Zheng, Haiying Ji, Fengying Xu, Shunan Lv, Nuo Xu, Yanfei Mao, Siyuan Li, Weiwei Li, Xueyin Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Excessive neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation contributes to lung tissue injury in sepsis-associated acute lung injury (ALI). Propofol, a commonly used sedative with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, has been suggested to confer protection against ALI; however, its effects on NET sformation and the underlying mechanisms remain poorly defined. Methods and Results: At the cellular level, we established an in vitro model of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–induced NET formation using human peripheral blood neutrophils to evaluate the effects of propofol. Pharmacological inhibition experiments in vitro suggested that propofol may suppress NET release by modulating Akt/mTOR signaling, with a concomitant reduction in neutrophil autophagic activity. In parallel, we established an LPS-induced sepsis model in mice and administered propofol by intraperitoneal pretreatment. Lung histopathology and NET quantification showed that propofol treatment was associated with attenuated sepsis‑induced lung injury and suppressed NET formation in vivo. These protective effects were associated with increased Akt/mTOR pathway activity in lung tissue; however, because in vivo pathway blockade was not performed, the functional contribution of Akt/mTOR signaling to organ protection in this setting remains to be directly validated. Conclusions: Propofol attenuates sepsis-induced lung injury in association with suppressed NET formation. In vitro pharmacological inhibition experiments suggest that the Akt/mTOR axis may represent one pathway through which propofol suppresses NET release. However, direct in vivo validation of this mechanism (e.g., using pathway-specific inhibitors, conditional knockout models, or other loss-of-function approaches) remains necessary and should be addressed in future studies. Collectively, these findings identify a previously underappreciated mechanism of propofol action and support its potential adjunctive role in sepsis management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10753-026-02552-0.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome characterized by organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Despite advances in critical care and therapeutic strategies that have reduced sepsis-related mortality, case-fatality rates remain unacceptably high, with reported mortality ranging from 20% to 40% [2]. Sepsis frequently progresses to multiple organ dysfunction, and acute lung injury (ALI) is among the most common and lethal complications in septic patients.

Polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs), play a central role in the pathogenesis of sepsis [3]. In addition to classical effector functions such as degranulation and phagocytosis, neutrophils can release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) to entrap and kill invading microorganisms. NETs are web-like extracellular structures composed of decondensed chromatin decorated with histones and neutrophil granule proteins. Although NETs formation contributes to host defense, excessive or dysregulated NETs release can be detrimental, promoting immunothrombosis, amplifying inflammation [4], and causing collateral tissue injury [5]. Consequently, NETs have been proposed as potential therapeutic targets for mitigating inflammatory and thrombotic tissue injury in diseases such as sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), COVID-19, and cardiovascular disorders [4, 6, 7]. In addition, NETs can directly induce epithelial and endothelial cell death through the effects of circulating histones and the cytotoxic activity of myeloperoxidase (MPO) [8]. Accumulating evidence indicates that NETs formation is associated with aggravated lung injury and plays an important role in the pathogenesis of ALI and ARDS [9].

In the intensive care unit (ICU), many patients with sepsis require sedation. Propofol (PPF) is a widely used intravenous anesthetic for the induction and maintenance of anesthesia and is commonly administered for ICU sedation because of its favorable pharmacokinetic profile, including rapid onset and short duration of action [10, 11]. Although propofol is used primarily for anesthesia and sedation, accumulating evidence suggests that it may also exert organ-protective effects in diverse pathophysiological settings through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic mechanisms [12, 13]. Specifically, propofol has been reported to attenuate ALI triggered by diverse insults, including ischemia–reperfusion injury and ventilator-induced lung injury [14, 15]. Propofol also modulates innate immune responses in sepsis [16]; however, the precise mechanisms by which it mitigates sepsis-associated ALI remain incompletely understood. Previous studies have shown that propofol suppresses neutrophil pro-inflammatory cytokine release, chemotaxis, and phagocytic activity [17, 18]. More recently, propofol was also reported to inhibit NETs formation [19].

NETs formation occurs through both NADPH oxidase–dependent and –independent pathways. ROS play a key role, but the process is not exclusively ROS dependent. Autophagy, particularly mTOR-mediated autophagy, has been implicated in NETs release [20, 21]. Autophagy helps maintain redox homeostasis and modulates immunity and cell death. An early in vitro study showed that pharmacologic inhibition of autophagy could prevent chromatin decondensation, thereby inhibiting apoptosis [22]. Furthermore, increased autophagy has been associated with excessive NETs release [20]. In contrast, autophagy inhibition with 3-methyladenine (3-MA) has been shown to block NETs formation [23]. Evidence also suggests that autophagy is a key driver of organophosphorus-induced NETs formation, and that limiting excessive autophagy may prevent this process [24]. Additionally, mTOR-driven autophagy can promote NETs formation through post-transcriptional regulation of HIF-1α [25]. Our prior work found that sepsis-related exosomes facilitate NETs formation via autophagy [26].

Given propofol’s immunomodulatory and NETs-inhibitory effects, together with the established role of autophagy in NETs formation, the effects of propofol on neutrophil autophagy and NETs generation remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether propofol mitigates sepsis-induced ALI by modulating autophagy to influence NETs formation.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Wild-type male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in the animal facility of Shanghai Xinhua Hospital. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Xinhua Hospital and conducted in strict accordance with institutional guidelines.

PMNs Islation and NETs Induction

The isolation of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) and induction of NETs were performed as previously described [7]. PMNs were isolated from venous blood collected from healthy volunteers using a discontinuous density-gradient centrifugation method with Histopaque-1077(Sigma–Aldrich,10771) and Histopaque-1119(Sigma–Aldrich,11191). Isolated human neutrophils were resuspended in RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher) and seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1.8 × 10^6 cells per well. Cells were then stimulated with 500 nM lipopolysaccharide (LPS;Escherichia coli serotype O111:B4; Sigma–Aldrich, USA) for 4 h. After stimulation, the medium was removed and wells were washed with RPMI 1640. At this concentration, LPS did not induce apoptosis or necrosis but elicited the characteristic features of NETs formation.

NETs Quantification Assay

NETs in cell culture supernatants, plasma, and mouse bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were quantified using two methods. Extracellular dsDNA was measured using the PicoGreen dsDNA Quantification Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). MPO–DNA complexes were quantified using a capture ELISA as previously described [26]. Briefly, 1 µg/mL anti-MPO monoclonal antibody (mAb) (Sigma–Aldrich, SAB1409321) was used as the capture antibody in the Cell Death Detection ELISA (Roche,11544675001), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Mouse Model of LPS-Induced Sepsis-Related lung Injury and in vivo Propofol Administration

Sepsis, characterized by a severe systemic inflammatory response, is often triggered by bacterial endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS). In this study, a well-established LPS-induced sepsis model was used. Wild-type male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 20–23 g) were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Propofol was dissolved in DMSO and diluted 10-fold with normal saline. Mice received three intraperitoneal injections of DMSO (vehicle) or propofol at 30-min intervals. After the second injection, mice were challenged with a single intraperitoneal dose of 200 µL LPS (10 mg/kg) or 0.9% saline (sham). Immediately after intraperitoneal LPS administration, all mice were maintained on a constant-temperature heating blanket (FT-3400; KEW BASIS, China). In addition, rectal temperature was intermittently measured every 2 h with a digital thermometer (DT-1320; Hua Qi Zheng Bang, China) to ensure that body temperature remained at 36.8–37.3 °C(Table S1). Mice were closely monitored for physiological changes, with particular attention to sepsis-related signs (e.g., fever, chills, lethargy, and anorexia). Animals were assessed qualitatively for general appearance, behavior, and visible respiratory effort; however, heart rate, arterial blood pressure, respiratory rate, and blood gas parameters were not measured quantitatively or recorded continuously.

Mouse Survival Curve Analysis

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 6 per group). Sepsis was induced by intraperitoneal injection of LPS (10 mg/kg), followed by administration of propofol at different doses. Mice were monitored daily beginning 12 h after injection, and body weight and survival were recorded for 7 days. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and body weight change plots were generated from the collected data.

RNA Isolation and Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mouse lung tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using Hifair III Reverse Transcriptase (YEASEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and analyzed using the comparative 2^−ΔΔCt method. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.

Immunohistochemistry and Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on paraffin-embedded mouse lung sections to assess the colocalization of myeloperoxidase (MPO)(Sigma–Aldrich, SAB1409321) and citrullinated histone H3 (citH3) (Abcam, ab5103). The procedure included tissue fixation, antigen retrieval, blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity, incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, nuclear counterstaining, and mounting.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed according to standard histological protocols, including tissue fixation, dehydration, paraffin embedding, sectioning, dewaxing, rehydration, hematoxylin and eosin staining, dehydration, and mounting. These methods enabled clear visualization of tissue morphology under light microscopy.

Flow Cytometry Assay

PMNs were seeded in 12-well plates. After the indicated treatments, 10 µM DCFH-DA was added to each well. Cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, washed three times, and then analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD FACSCanto™.

Confocal Microscopy

The effect of propofol on NETs formation was evaluated by confocal laser scanning microscopy using SYTOX Green(Thermo Fisher, S7020), a dye specific for double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). PMNs were cultured on lysine-coated coverslips, and NETs were induced with LPS followed by propofol treatment. Cells were then stained with SYTOX Green and fixed with paraformaldehyde, followed by nuclear counterstaining with Hoechst. Confocal microscopy was used to assess the effect of propofol on dsDNA signals associated with NETs. In addition, autophagy in PMNs was evaluated by LC3B(CST, #2775) immunofluorescence staining using Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibodies.

Tandem mass Tag-Based Quantitative Proteomics

This portion of the experiment was performed by Shanghai Zhongke New Life Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Proteins were extracted using SDT lysis buffer and digested with trypsin following the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol. Protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Peptides were desalted using C18 solid-phase extraction cartridges before liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis. Peptides were labeled with isobaric tags (iTRAQ and TMT) and fractionated by high-pH reversed-phase chromatography.

Mass spectrometric data were acquired on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer. Proteins were identified using the MASCOT search engine and quantified based on unique peptides. Bioinformatic analyses included hierarchical clustering, subcellular localization prediction, protein domain annotation, Gene Ontology (GO) annotation, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis using IntAct and STRING, with network visualization performed in Cytoscape. For enrichment analyses, p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Western Blotting Analysis

Polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) were lysed on ice in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein (40 µg per lane) were resolved by 8%–10% SDS–PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 1× Protein Free Rapid Blocking Buffer (EpiZyme) for 20 min at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-mTOR (1:1,000, CST, #2983), anti–phospho-mTOR (1:1,000, CST, #5536), anti-p62 (1:1,000, CST, #5114), anti-LC3B (1:1,000, CST, #2775) anti-PIK3R1(1:1000，LSBio, LS-B8947),anti–PTEN(1:1000, CST, #9188), anti–Akt(1:1,000, CST, #4691), anti–phospho-Akt (1:1,000, CST, #4060) ,anti-GAPDH (1:1,000; CST, #5174), and anti–β-actin (1:1,000, CST, #4970).

Statistics

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data with a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test in GraphPad Prism 9. Survival between groups was compared using the log-rank test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Propofol Inhibits LPS-Induced NETs Formation in Human Peripheral Blood Neutrophils

This section examined the effect of propofol on NETs formation in human peripheral blood neutrophils using an LPS-induced NETosis model (500 ng/mL). Neutrophils were isolated by discontinuous density-gradient centrifugation (Histopaque) and allowed to equilibrate in culture before experimentation. Cells were pretreated with propofol at the indicated concentrations prior to LPS stimulation. Cell-free double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and myeloperoxidase–DNA (MPO–DNA) complexes in culture supernatants were quantified as surrogate markers of NETs formation.

LPS markedly induced NETs formation, as evidenced by increased dsDNA concentrations and elevated MPO–DNA complex levels in the LPS group compared with the control group (Fig. 1A and B). The DMSO vehicle control did not significantly alter dsDNA or MPO–DNA levels relative to the LPS group, indicating that the tested DMSO concentration did not affect NETs formation. In contrast, propofol pretreatment significantly reduced both dsDNA and MPO–DNA levels compared with LPS alone in a concentration-dependent manner. Among the tested doses, 50 µM propofol produced the strongest inhibitory effect on NETs formation. Consistent with these quantitative findings, confocal microscopy with SYTOX Green staining revealed fewer extracellular dsDNA (SYTOX Green–positive) filamentous structures in the propofol-treated groups (Fig. 1D). And flow cytometry was performed to evaluate the purity of neutrophils isolated from human peripheral blood. After surface-marker staining, cells were gated to exclude debris and doublets. Neutrophils were defined as CD66b⁺CD16⁺ cells, and purity was calculated as the proportion of CD66b⁺CD16⁺ cells in the gated population (Fig. 1C).

Propofol Inhibits NETs Formation in the Lungs of Sepsis mice

Building on our in vitro findings that propofol inhibits NETs formation, we next investigated whether propofol suppresses pulmonary NETs formation in a murine model of LPS-induced sepsis. At 24 h after intraperitoneal LPS challenge, serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected to quantify cell-free double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and myeloperoxidase–DNA (MPO–DNA) complexes. Lung tissues were also analyzed by immunofluorescence staining.

Under this exploratory dosing regimen, we observed that systemic propofol treatment significantly reduced serum dsDNA and MPO–DNA complex levels (Fig. 2B C) and decreased dsDNA concentrations in BALF (Fig. 2D). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining of lung sections showed reduced myeloperoxidase (MPO) and citrullinated histone H3 (citH3) signals following propofol treatment, and immunofluorescence staining of lung sections showed reduced NET-associated signals after propofol treatment, with neutrophils identified by Ly6G staining and NETs assessed by MPO and citH3 staining. (Fig.2A). Collectively, these data indicate that propofol attenuates LPS-induced NETs formation in the lungs of sepsis mice.

Propofol Alleviates Sepsis-Related ALI and Reduces 7-Day Mortality in Sepsis mice

This section evaluated the effects of propofol on lung injury severity and survival in mice with LPS-induced sepsis. Mice received intraperitoneal propofol (20 or 40 mg/kg) followed by intraperitoneal LPS (10 mg/kg), whereas control mice received an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Mice received intraperitoneal injections of propofol across a dose range selected based on prior rodent literature, with the aim of encompassing sub-anesthetic behavioral effects rather than achieving direct pharmacokinetic equivalence to human clinical scenarios. At 24 h after LPS administration, lung tissues were harvested for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis to assess lung injury and pulmonary inflammatory responses.

H&E staining of lung sections from LPS-challenged mice showed prominent alveolar septal thickening, increased neutrophil infiltration, and marked disruption of alveolar architecture. These pathological changes were attenuated by propofol pretreatment, as indicated by reduced septal thickening, diminished inflammatory cell infiltration, and decreased hyaline membrane formation (Fig. 3A). Consistently, qPCR analysis demonstrated that propofol significantly downregulated pulmonary mRNA expression of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6) (Fig. 3B), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) (Fig. 3C), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (Fig. 3D). In addition, propofol reduced the lung wet-to-dry weight ratio (Fig. 3E) and decreased total protein concentrations in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) (Fig. 3F), suggesting improved alveolar–capillary barrier integrity and reduced pulmonary edema.

Survival analysis further showed that both propofol doses significantly improved 7-day survival compared with the LPS group (Fig. 3G). Moreover, propofol pretreatment (20 or 40 mg/kg, i.p.) mitigated LPS-induced body weight loss (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these findings indicate that propofol alleviates LPS-induced acute lung injury and reduces 7-day mortality in sepsis mice. Quantitative analysis of core body temperature was performed at 2-hour intervals throughout the experimental period. Based on these discrete measurements, no sustained hypothermia (defined as two or more consecutive readings below 35.0 °C) was observed in any animal; all recorded temperature values remained within the normothermic range (see Supplementary Table S1 for the complete dataset). However, because temperature was measured intermittently, transient fluctuations between sampling time points cannot be excluded. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as evidence for the absence of sustained hypothermia at the sampled time points, rather than as definitive exclusion of brief, transient temperature abnormalities.

During the early optimization phase of this study, we did not consider that hypothermia could affect NET formation; therefore, hypothermia was neither monitored nor corrected. When comparing the effects of different propofol doses on 7‑day mortality in LPS‑induced septic mice, the higher dose (40 mg/kg) appeared to provide less protection than the 20 mg/kg dose. However, following the reviewer’s suggestion to control for potential hypothermia-related confounding induced by the sepsis model, and after maintaining mouse core body temperature within a relatively constant range (36.8–37.3 °C; see Methods 2.4 for details), we observed that both 20 and 40 mg/kg propofol produced clear and reproducible lung-protective effects and survival benefits. Accordingly, the data presented in this manuscript reflect the pharmacological effects of propofol under conditions in which the potential contribution of unmeasured hypothermia was minimized. The variability observed in the pilot phase underscores the critical importance of implementing temperature control in preclinical sepsis studies involving anesthetic agents.

Propofol Decreases ROS Levels and Downregulates Autophagy in Neutrophils

Given the pivotal roles of ROS and autophagy in NETs formation, we examined whether propofol inhibits NETs formation by regulating autophagy in neutrophils. Confocal microscopy (Fig. 4E) showed that propofol treatment reduced LC3 fluorescence intensity in polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs). Consistently, Western blot analysis demonstrated that propofol significantly decreased LC3B protein expression and increased p62/SQSTM1 levels (Fig.4B C–D), suggesting inhibition of autophagic activity.

In parallel, intracellular ROS production was quantified by flow cytometry in the control, LPS, and LPS plus propofol (LPS + PPF) groups. LPS markedly increased ROS levels in PMNs, whereas propofol significantly attenuated this increase (Fig. 4F). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that ROS levels in the LPS + PPF group were significantly lower than those in the LPS group (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these findings indicate that propofol suppresses ROS generation and attenuates LPS-induced autophagy hyperactivation in neutrophils, which may contribute to its inhibitory effect on NETs formation.

Effect of Propofol on Neutrophil Function after LPS Treatment Detected by TMT

To further delineate the signaling pathways involved, we performed TMT-based quantitative proteomics to identify differentially expressed proteins in neutrophils following propofol treatment. In total, 720 differentially expressed proteins were identified (fold change > 1.2; p < 0.05), including 386 upregulated and 334 downregulated proteins (Fig. 5A). These changes are summarized in a heatmap, and the top 30 upregulated and downregulated proteins are shown in Fig. 5B. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fisher’s exact test) revealed that autophagy-related pathways were among the top 20 significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 5C).

Notably, these results indicated that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway plays a critical role in propofol-mediated regulation of neutrophils, accompanied by a significant downregulation of PIK3R1 (the p85α regulatory subunit) (Fig. 5B). Western blotting further validated that propofol treatment significantly downregulated PIK3R1, concomitant with decreased PTEN expression and increased Akt phosphorylation, indicating activation of PI3K/Akt signaling (Fig. 5D and E). Collectively, these findings suggest that propofol may activate the PI3K/Akt pathway by suppressing PIK3R1 expression and/or activity, thereby relieving its inhibitory effect on the PI3K catalytic subunit.

Propofol Regulates Neutrophil Autophagy via the mTOR Pathway to Inhibit NETs Formation

This section investigated whether propofol modulates mTOR-dependent autophagy signaling in neutrophils to suppress NETs formation. Rapamycin((Rapa), a pharmacologic autophagy inducer, promotes autophagosome formation by inhibiting mTOR. To manipulate autophagy activity, neutrophils were treated with rapamycin, and the inhibitory effect of propofol on NETs formation was attenuated in the presence of rapamycin.

Neutrophils were identified as CD66b⁺CD16⁺ cells, and purity was calculated as the percentage of CD66b⁺CD16⁺ cells within the gated population (Fig. 6E). Propofol significantly reduced autophagy-related markers and suppressed NETs formation; however, these effects were markedly attenuated by co-treatment with rapamycin. Autophagic activity was evaluated by assessing LC3 using confocal microscopy and Western blotting. LC3 fluorescence intensity was significantly lower in the LPS+Rapa + PPF group than in the LPS+Rapa group (Fig. 6F), and LC3-II protein levels were likewise reduced (Figs. 6 A, 6D, and 6F). Notably, under rapamycin treatment, propofol partially restored mTOR phosphorylation, counteracting rapamycin-induced mTOR inhibition (Figs. 6 A–C) and thereby mitigating excessive autophagy. Consistently, extracellular dsDNA quantification showed that dsDNA levels were significantly lower in the LPS + PPF and LPS+Rapa + PPF groups than in the LPS and LPS+Rapa groups, respectively (Fig.6G). Collectively, these data support the involvement of mTOR-associated autophagy signaling in the inhibitory effects of propofol on neutrophil autophagy and NET release; however, they do not demonstrate that mTOR activation is the only or an indispensable mechanism underlying these effects.

Discussion

NETs have increasingly been recognized as pivotal mediators in the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI). Accumulating evidence indicates that NETs exert direct cytotoxic effects on pulmonary epithelial and endothelial cells through circulating histones and the cytotoxic activity of myeloperoxidase (MPO). Consequently, NETs play an indispensable role in the development and progression of ALI and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [7, 27]. Clinically and experimentally, elevated NETs levels are closely correlated with greater disease severity in patients with sepsis and in cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) mouse models of acute lung injury (ALI). These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting NETs as a novel strategy for sepsis-induced ALI. Propofol, a widely used intravenous anesthetic and sedative in intensive care units (ICUs), exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and organ-protective effects beyond its anesthetic properties [28, 29]. However, its role in regulating NETs formation and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In the present study, we systematically investigated the effects of propofol on NETosis in vitro and in vivo, and explored the mechanisms underlying its protective effects against sepsis-induced ALI. Our findings provide new insights into the potential therapeutic application of propofol in sepsis management.

To initially clarify the direct effect of propofol on NETs formation, we isolated and purified neutrophils from venous blood samples obtained from healthy volunteers using Histopaque discontinuous density-gradient centrifugation. We then established an LPS-induced in vitro NETs formation model and treated cells with graded concentrations of propofol. The results showed that propofol inhibited LPS-induced NETs formation in a concentration-dependent manner. This finding is consistent with previous reports. Angela Meier and colleagues reported that both propofol and its lipid emulsion vehicle inhibit neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation in human neutrophils [30]. Chen et al. [19]showed that Lipofundin, a key component of Propofol Lipuro®, inhibits phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)- and Escherichia coli-induced NETs formation via a ROS-independent pathway. Nyssen et al. [31] also reported that the oxidative propofol derivatives PPFQ and PPFDQ preserve the immunomodulatory effects of propofol by regulating oxidative reactions in polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) at physiological concentrations. Notably, our in vitro experiments further confirmed the inhibitory effect of intravenous propofol on NETs formation at the cellular level. Moreover, our data (Fig. 1A and B) showed no significant difference in the suppression of dsDNA and MPO, which are core components of NETs, between 50 µM and 100 µM propofol. Therefore, based on the pharmacological principle of achieving high efficacy at the lowest effective dose, 50 µM propofol was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent in vitro experiments.

Building on our in vitro findings that propofol inhibits NETs formation, we further investigated its protective effects and underlying mechanisms in vivo using a septic mouse model. Under this exploratory dosing regimen, we found that propofol treatment was associated with markedly suppressed pulmonary NET formation in septic mice, as indicated by significant reductions in canonical NET markers, including MPO–DNA complexes and dsDNA(Fig. 2B,2 C,2D). Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis of lung tissue sections showed decreased co-localization of MPO and CitH3 (Fig. 2A). Correspondingly, under this exploratory dosing regimen, propofol treatment was associated with alleviated sepsis-associated acute lung injury (ALI) in mice, as evidenced by improved lung histopathology, a reduced lung wet-to-dry weight ratio, and decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) (Fig. 3A and F). Importantly, propofol treatment was also associated with a significantly improved the 7-day survival rate of septic mice(Fig. 3G), further supporting its potential as a protective agent in sepsis management. This study demonstrates that both 20 mg/kg and 40 mg/kg propofol confer protective effects in the LPS‑induced septic lung injury model. Importantly, refinement of the experimental conditions—most notably the rigorous implementation of feedback‑controlled normothermia—enabled a more consistent interpretation of the dose‑dependent effects of propofol. In our initial experiments, we did not anticipate that hypothermia might influence NET formation; therefore, core temperature was neither monitored nor actively maintained. When we compared 7-day mortality across propofol doses in LPS-induced septic mice, the higher dose (40 mg/kg) appeared to confer less protection than 20 mg/kg. We hypothesize that this unexpected pattern may be attributable, at least in part, to hypothermia arising from the sepsis model and/or propofol administration, which can disrupt multiple physiological processes. Consistent with this hypothesis, after mitigating this potential confounder by maintaining normothermia, both 20 mg/kg and 40 mg/kg propofol reproducibly attenuated septic lung injury, with the higher dose producing more pronounced effects. However, because temperature was not recorded in the initial experiments, the causal contribution of hypothermia to the discrepant dose–response relationship cannot be directly established and remains an inference based on indirect evidence.These findings underscore the critical importance of thermoregulation in pharmacological studies of anesthetics using mouse sepsis models. We propose that future preclinical studies evaluating sedatives or anesthetics in systemic inflammatory models incorporate routine core body temperature monitoring and maintenance as a standard quality‑control measure to avoid similar confounding effects.

Although the propofol doses used in this study did not abolish the righting reflex or paw withdrawal reflex, we did not perform continuous monitoring of arterial blood pressure, heart rate, or arterial blood gases. This is an important limitation, because propofol can cause dose-dependent hypotension, bradycardia, and respiratory depression even at subanesthetic doses. Accordingly, we cannot exclude the possibility that propofol induced mild, transient cardiovascular depression and/or respiratory acidosis, which may have secondarily influenced the inflammatory responses, NET formation, and survival outcomes observed in this study. Our conclusions should therefore be interpreted with the caveat that the protective effects reported here reflect the net physiological impact of propofol infusion under the present experimental conditions, rather than definitive evidence for an exclusively direct cellular mechanism. In addition, in the absence of pharmacokinetic measurements (e.g., plasma propofol concentrations), the intraperitoneal bolus dosing regimen used here cannot be directly extrapolated to the steady-state plasma concentrations achieved with continuous intravenous infusion in clinical practice.

Our in vivo findings are further supported by recent studies. W. Ouyang et al. [32]. demonstrated that inhibiting CitH3-driven inflammatory responses reduces LPS-induced acute lung injury (ALI) in mice. Wu et al. [33] reported that propofol alleviates cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis-associated organ injury by suppressing excessive inflammatory responses.Because LPS can induce CitH3 expression in multiple lung cell types, rather than exclusively in neutrophils, we defined NETs more stringently as the co-localization of CitH3, MPO (a neutrophil-associated marker), and Ly6G (a neutrophil-specific marker in mice) to ensure accurate NETs quantification. Propofol treatment was associated with significantly reduced NETs deposition in lung tissue, as assessed by immunofluorescence co-localization of CitH3, MPO, and Ly6G, and decreased NETs levels in mouse serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Collectively, these results suggest that propofol treatment was associated with reduced NET formation and attenuated lung injury in vivo; however, a contribution from unmeasured systemic physiological alterations (e.g., improved hemodynamic stability and/or oxygen delivery) cannot be excluded.

Although our in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that propofol is associated with inhibition of NET formation and protection against sepsis-induced lung injury (SILI), the relative contributions of direct cellular mechanisms versus indirect systemic physiological effects remain to be determined. Furthermore, the precise molecular pathways through which propofol modulates NET formation require further elucidation. ROS are classic and essential mediators of NETs formation, as they can trigger nuclear chromatin decondensation and promote neutrophil elastase (NE) translocation to the nucleus, which are key steps in NETosis [34]. Our results showed that propofol significantly reduced lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ROS generation in neutrophils (Fig. 4F and G), consistent with previous reports demonstrating the antioxidant effects of propofol in sepsis-associated organ injury [29, 31, 35]. Although ROS are considered a key driver of NETs formation, accumulating evidence suggests that NETosis is regulated by additional ROS-independent mechanisms, including autophagy-related pathways. In particular, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)-regulated autophagy has been proposed as a potential contributor to NETs formation [21].Propofol has been reported to exert pleiotropic biological effects beyond anesthesia, in part through modulation of autophagy, including cardioprotective [36], neuroprotective [37], pulmonary protective [38], and antitumor activities [39], and it provides organ protection across diverse pathophysiological settings [40, 41]. Mechanistic studies indicate that propofol protects against LPS- and hypoxia-induced pulmonary epithelial injury by suppressing apoptosis and autophagy. This protection is associated with decreased HIF-1α and Bnip3 expression and increased mTOR phosphorylation (p-mTOR) [42].In the present study, we found that propofol markedly inhibited autophagy in neutrophils, as indicated by a decreased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and increased p62 accumulation(Fig. 4B-D).These results suggest functional crosstalk between autophagy and ROS in propofol-mediated inhibition of NETs formation. Propofol may attenuate autophagy, thereby limiting ROS generation and subsequent NETs formation, or alternatively, reduced ROS may secondarily dampen autophagic activity, ultimately restraining NETosis. Importantly, the concurrent regulation of ROS production and autophagy by propofol represents a key novel aspect of our study and helps address a limitation of prior work, which largely focused on ROS modulation alone in the context of propofol-mediated regulation of NETs formation.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which propofol inhibits NETs formation, we performed tandem mass tag (TMT)-based quantitative proteomic profiling of neutrophils following propofol treatment. In total, 720 differentially expressed proteins were identified (fold change > 1.2, p < 0.05), including 386 upregulated and 334 downregulated proteins. TMT-based quantitative proteomic analysis indicated that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is a key pathway involved in propofol-mediated regulation of neutrophil function (Fig. 5C). A pivotal finding of our TMT-based proteomic analysis is the significant downregulation of PIK3R1 (the p85α regulatory subunit of PI3K) in neutrophils following propofol treatment. Although this may appear counterintuitive, it is consistent with the “p85–p110 balance” model described previously [43, 44] .Under physiological conditions, excess monomeric p85α can act as a competitive inhibitor by sequestering signaling adaptors and exerting allosteric inhibition on the p110 catalytic subunit. Beyond its role in PI3K regulation, PIK3R1 has also been reported to serve as a scaffold that helps maintain PTEN stability by shielding it from E3 ubiquitin ligase–mediated degradation [45]. Accordingly, downregulation of PIK3R1 may impair PTEN stability, accelerate PTEN ubiquitination and degradation, and reduce its lipid phosphatase activity, thereby relieving PTEN-mediated restraint of PI3K signaling [46].Consequently, propofol-induced depletion of PIK3R1 may diminish PTEN activity and impair PIP3 dephosphorylation, facilitating intracellular PIP3 accumulation and activation of the downstream Akt/mTOR signaling axis. Consistent with this proposed mechanism, our Western blot analysis showed increased phosphorylation of Akt (Fig. 5D ). and mTOR(Fig. 6A) in propofol-treated neutrophils, accompanied by a concomitant reduction in the LC3-II/I ratio (Fig.6A ). Collectively, these results support a plausible pharmacological mechanism whereby propofol mitigates neutrophil hyperactivation by modulating the PIK3R1/PTEN/Akt/mTOR aixs to suppress excessive autophagy.

Meanwhile, LC3B, a core autophagy-associated protein, was aslo among the top 30 most downregulated proteins. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis also identified autophagy as one of the top 20 pathways significantly affected by propofol, further supporting an association between propofol treatment and proteomic alterations related to neutrophil autophagy. mTOR is a central regulator of autophagy and immune cell function, and recent studies have underscored a pivotal role for mTOR signaling in the regulation of NETs formation. Liu et al. reported that activation of the mTOR pathway suppresses neutrophil autophagy and NETosis in cigarette smoke-exposure asthma [47], whereas Yang et al. showed that propofol activates mTOR signaling and alleviates sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction [48]. Integrating these findings, we observed that propofol treatment increased the p‑mTOR/mTOR ratio (Fig. 6A, B), consistent with activation of the mTOR pathway, and that this change coincided with reduced autophagy, decreased ROS production, and suppressed NET formation. To assess whether mTOR signaling contributes to the anti‑NET effects of propofol, we performed rescue experiments (Fig. 6) using rapamycin, a selective mTOR inhibitor. Rapamycin pretreatment partially reversed the inhibitory effects of propofol on neutrophil autophagy and NET formation. These findings support the involvement of mTOR-related autophagy signaling in the anti-NET effects of propofol in vitro, but do not establish that mTOR activation is the sole or indispensable mechanism.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

Despite the novelty and potential clinical implications of the present study, several limitations should be acknowledged.First, we primarily employed LPS-induced monomicrobial sepsis models, which do not fully capture the complexity of human sepsis, including polymicrobial infection, inter-individual heterogeneity in host immune responses, and temporal changes over the disease course. Second, in this study, rapamycin was used to probe mTORC1-dependent autophagy. Future work using dual mTORC1/2 inhibitors (e.g., Torin1) could further clarify the contribution of mTORC2 signaling and potential compensatory responses within the broader mTOR network. In addition, because we did not directly validate Akt/mTOR signaling in vivo, we did not pharmacologically inhibit or genetically manipulate this pathway to establish causality. Thus, the mechanistic observations reported here are hypothesis-generating, and further studies are needed to determine whether modulation of the Akt/mTOR axis underlies the in vivo benefits observed in sepsis. Third, although we improved the specificity of NETs detection by co-localizing citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) with the neutrophil marker myeloperoxidase (MPO), NETs quantification still lacks standardized and widely accepted methodologies, which may limit comparability across studies. Fourth, all in vivo experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to both sexes. Future studies should incorporate mixed-sex cohorts to assess potential sex-specific differences in both the protective effects and the underlying mechanisms of propofol in sepsis-induced ALI. Fifth, an important limitation of this study is that intraperitoneal bolus administration in mice may generate metabolic byproducts and peak-to-trough exposure profiles that differ substantially from those achieved with continuous intravenous infusion in critically ill patients. In the absence of bridging pharmacokinetic data, our dosing regimen should therefore be considered exploratory. Specifically, we did not perform pharmacokinetic measurements to relate the administered intraperitoneal doses to propofol concentrations in plasma or lung tissue. The doses used (20–40 mg/kg, i.p.) are considerably higher on a mg/kg basis than those typically used in humans (1–2 mg/kg i.v. bolus or 4–12 mg/kg/h continuous infusion). This discrepancy likely reflects multiple factors, including (i) slower absorption and substantial first-pass hepatic metabolism with intraperitoneal dosing compared with intravenous administration, and (ii) higher metabolic clearance and differences in distribution parameters in mice relative to humans. Consequently, while the observed effects are biologically meaningful within the murine model, they should not be interpreted as direct equivalents of clinical sedation exposure in critically ill patients. Future studies incorporating target-controlled infusion paradigms and/or serial blood sampling will be needed to better align murine dosing with clinically relevant human exposure.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study demonstrates that propofol treatment is associated with marked inhibition of NET formation in vitro and in vivo, accompanied by attenuated sepsis-induced acute lung injury and improved survival. In vitro pharmacological evidence suggests that these effects may involve activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway and suppression of neutrophil autophagy; however, the functional contribution of this pathway in vivo remains to be directly validated. Moreover, the protective effects observed in vivo likely reflect a combination of direct cellular actions and-because hemodynamic and respiratory parameters were not continuously monitored-potential indirect systemic physiological influences (e.g., transient changes in blood pressure, heart rate, ventilation, or oxygen delivery).

Collectively, our findings provide new insight into the molecular basis of the lung-protective effects of propofol in sepsis-associated ALI and support the potential therapeutic relevance of targeting NETs formation in sepsis. Given that propofol is already widely used as a first-line anesthetic and sedative in intensive care units, these data suggest that propofol-based sedation may offer additional benefits in sepsis by limiting NETs formation and mitigating ALI. Future studies should further delineate pathway crosstalk involved in propofol-mediated NETs inhibition and validate these findings in rigorously designed clinical trials to better define the role of propofol in sepsis management.
