ODR1/AtSDR4L alleviates osmotic stress-induced germination arrest via ABA-dependent negative feedback on ABA biosynthesis
Authors: Ling Ding, Xu Ran, Yuan Cao, Xiangfeng Liu, Yong Xiang, Xi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo optimize offspring seed survival in fluctuating environments, seed plants have evolved two key adaptive strategies, seed dormancy under favorable conditions and germination arrest under stress, to control germination timing. The phytohormone abscisic acid acts as a master regulator in both processes, exerting precise control over its endogenous levels and signaling to fine-tune this seed-seedling transition. Previously, we identified ODR1 as a key seed dormancy regulator: it negatively modulates seed dormancy by suppressing ABA accumulation during seed development via inhibiting bHLH57-dependent transcriptional activation of the ABA biosynthesis genes NCED6/9. However, whether ODR1 also modulates seed germination under stress conditions remains unknown.ResultsHere, we showed that loss of ODR1 function arrested seed germination under osmotic stress, while specific overexpression of ODR1 in odr1 mutant seeds rescued this osmotic germination arrest. Osmotic stress induced and maintained high ODR1 expression, but this induction was reduced in the ABA biosynthesis-deficient mutant nced6/9 and ABA signaling mutants abi3-1, abi4-t, and abi5-8, indicating ABA dependence of ODR1 induction. In odr1 mutants, 8 out of 12 key ABA biosynthesis genes were significantly upregulated. ChIP-seq data analysis revealed an ODR1 binding peak in the promoter of the ABA biosynthesis gene ABA1. Specific overexpression of ODR1 in odr1 mutant seeds notably rescued the upregulated ABA1 expression, confirming the repression of ABA1 by ODR1. Furthermore, ABA content was significantly increased in germinating seeds of odr1 mutant, and exogenous application of fluridone, an ABA biosynthesis inhibitor, effectively rescued their germination arrest, linking the phenotype to excessive ABA accumulation.ConclusionsThese results reveal a novel role of ODR1 in osmotic stress-induced germination arrest: ODR1 is induced by osmotic stress via an ABA-dependent pathway and in turn limits ABA accumulation via repressing the expression of ABA biosynthesis genes, thereby alleviating germination arrest under stress. Together, we demonstrate that ODR1 coordinates germination timing via ABA-dependent negative feedback regulation of ABA biosynthesis during both dormancy establishment and osmotic stress responses. This mechanism addresses the critical question of how plants fine-tune ABA signaling to balance survival and germination in variable environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07570-y.Keywords: ODR1/AtSDR4L, Seed dormancy and germination, Osmotic stress, ABA biosynthesis

Background

Seed plants have evolved two pivotal adaptive strategies to maximize seed offspring survival in dynamic environments. The first is seed dormancy, a biological trait where seeds cannot germinate even under favorable conditions [1–3]. This strategy prevents “all-in” synchronous germination during transiently suitable windows, thereby reducing the risk of mass mortality from sudden environmental perturbations. Instead, the sequential germination during dormancy release process enhances resilience against environmental variability. The second is a germination “brake”. Upon perceiving stress signals such as osmotic stress, germinating seeds rapidly arrest germination to limit potential damage, thereby enhancing the overall resilience of offspring populations to adverse environments [4]– [5]. Despite operating at distinct developmental stages, both strategies optimize seed survival in variable environments through precise timing of germination. However, the molecular mechanism that integrates both strategies to coordinate germination timing across different stages remain unclear.

The phytohormone abscisic acid (ABA) acts as a central regulator of germination in both stages, with dynamic fluctuations in its levels precisely modulating seed germination potential [5–10]. During seed development, ABA accumulates gradually to induce and maintain dormancy, preventing precocious germination; however, its levels decline sharply upon imbibition, the early stage of germination [11–14]. Upon sensing stresses like osmotic stress during this stage, seeds rapidly induce de novo ABA biosynthesis and re-accumulation, acting as a “brake” to delay or halt germination [15–17]. This dynamic accumulation pattern establishes ABA as a central molecular hub integrating these two adaptive strategies, yet the molecular regulatory network governing such fluctuations remains elusive.

Transcriptional regulation of ABA metabolism genes constitutes a key mechanism underlying the dynamic ABA accumulation [9, 18, 19]. The nine-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED) family function as critical enzymes in ABA biosynthesis, catalyzing the conversion of violaxanthin to xanthoxin [18–20]. During seed development, the NCED6 and NCED9 genes are highly expressed to sustain ABA accumulation for dormancy maintenance [21, 22]. In contrast, during osmotic stress inhibited germination, ABA biosynthesis is regulated primarily by other biosynthesis genes such as ABA1, whose expression is induced by osmotic stress [23], rather than being dominated by NCED6/9, whose expression is silenced during seed imbibition [24]. Identifying transcription factors (TFs) that directly regulate these genes will reveal critical nodes in the network that integrates these two strategies.

TFs downstream of ABA signaling play crucial roles in ABA-inhibited seed germination by regulating the expression of their target genes. For instance, the B3-family TF ABI3, AP2/ERF-family TF ABI4, and the bZIP-family TF ABI5 are key regulators during these stages [25–33]. On one hand, ABA and osmotic stress can upregulate the expression of these TFs, enhance their protein stability, or promote their transcriptional activity; on the other hand, these TFs can subsequently activate the expression of downstream ABA-responsive genes, thereby amplifying ABA signaling [31, 33–43]. Moreover, the synergistic interaction among these TFs further enhances this signal amplification [44–47], ensuring the execution of both survival strategies via such signal amplification. However, sustained ABA signal amplification can cause excessive dormancy or germination arrest, thereby impairing the flexibility of environmental responses [48–50]. Thus, precise modulation of the signal amplification mediated by these TFs is necessary. Identifying regulatory factors that respond to ABA signaling and exert feedback fine-tuning over its amplification is critical for unraveling the molecular basis of such precision.

We previously identified a dormancy-regulating gene that may help address this issue. We named it ODR1 (REVERSAL OF RDO5 1), as its loss-of-function reverses the dormancy-deficient phenotype of knockout mutant of RDO5, a seed dormancy master regulator [51–53]. ODR1 is a homolog of the rice dormancy gene OsSdr4, and is alternatively designated as AtSdr4L (Arabidopsis thaliana SDR4-like) or SFL1 (Sdr four like 1) in other studies [54–59]. Notably, their dormancy-regulating functions diverge sharply: OsSdr4 regulates dormancy positively in rice [60, 61], whereas ODR1 acts negatively in Arabidopsis [53, 55]. Beyond dormancy regulation, ODR1 influences post-germination seedling establishment, as odr1 mutants exhibit severe embryonic phenotypes in germinated seeds [56, 57].

The expression pattern of ODR1 aligns closely with its functional role in dormancy and germination regulation. It begins to accumulate during seed maturation, peaks in late maturation, remains highly expressed in freshly harvested dry seeds, and upon seed imbibition, declines sharply to become barely detectable by the seedling stage [53, 55], a temporal profile that coincides with the critical periods of dormancy establishment and break. ODR1 expression is transcriptionally regulated by ABI3. ChIP-seq and Y1H assays demonstrated that ABI3 directly binds to the RY2 motif in the ODR1 promoter [53, 62]. Transient expression assays in tobacco leaves further showed that ABI3 represses ODR1 promoter activity, supported by increased ODR1 expression in imbibed seeds of the abi3-1 mutant and decreased expression in ABI3 ectopic expression lines [53]. However, in seeds of abi3-1, abi3-6, and abi3-16 mutants during early development (~ 10 and ~ 14 DAP, days after pollination), a distinct expression pattern was noted: ODR1 transcript levels were significantly decreased rather than increased, suggesting that ABI3 may positively regulate ODR1 expression. Given that loss of ABI3 function impairs the normal activity of TFs such as ABI5 and likely ABI4 [45, 47], the transcriptional regulation of ODR1 during seed development likely does not rely solely on ABI3 but rather involves the participation of other positive regulatory TFs.

ODR1 is a member of ABA-induced transcriptional repressor family [63]. Thus, it has been proposed that the divergent functions of ODR1 and its homologs across species may stem from differences in their downstream target genes [56, 58, 59]. Integrated analysis of RNA-seq and qRT-PCR data from odr1 mutants and their corresponding wild-type identified its candidate targets, including the ABA biosynthesis genes NCED6/9 and the ABA signaling TF ABI4 [53, 56, 57]. Consistent with its role as a transcriptional repressor, NCED6/9 and ABI4 expression is upregulated in odr1 mutant seeds and seedlings [53, 56, 57]. ChIP-seq revealed an ODR1 binding peak 4 kb upstream of ABI4 locus, and this distal binding may contribute to the repression of ABI4 [56]. For NCED6/9, our previous Y1H assays showed no direct interaction between ODR1 and the NCED6/9 promoters [53]. Instead, ODR1 inhibits NCED6/9 expression through an indirect mechanism by dampening the transcriptional activity of its interacting TF bHLH57 on the NCED6/9 promoters, thereby repressing their expression [53]. Together, these findings indicate that during dormancy establishment, ODR1 acts as either a direct transcriptional repressor or an indirect transcriptional co-repressor to inhibit ABA biosynthesis or signaling, thereby preventing over-amplification of ABA action. However, whether ODR1 functions during the stage of seed germination under stress conditions remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we further demonstrate that ODR1 exerts a similar negative feedback regulatory role during seed germination under osmotic stress. Osmotic stress induces and maintains high ODR1 expression during germination, with this induction dependent on ABA biosynthesis and the cooperative function of ABA-responsive TFs including ABI4 and ABI5. Induced ODR1 in turn reduces ABA production by repressing the expression of most ABA biosynthesis genes, ultimately alleviating osmotic stress-induced germination arrest. Additionally, ChIP-seq analysis identified an ODR1 binding peak at the ABA1 locus, implying direct repression of ABA1 by ODR1. Collectively, our findings reveal ODR1 as a core regulator of seed germination timing, functioning in both the dormancy establishment and the germination response to osmotic stress. By responding to ABA induction and feedback-inhibiting ABA biosynthesis, ODR1 forms a negative feedback module that precisely fine-tunes germination timing and optimizes seed adaptation to environmental cues.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The Arabidopsis thaliana genotypes used in this study included the wild-types Columbia-0 (Col-0) and Landsberg erecta (Ler), along with three ODR1 loss-of-function mutants and four ABA-related loss-of-function mutants. odr1-2 (SALK_022729) and odr1-3 (SALK_203161) are widely used T-DNA knockout lines in Col-0 background [53, 55–57, 63, 64]. The odr1-5 mutant was isolated from the abi3-1 odr1-5 double mutant, with both in Ler background as used in our previous work [53, 64]. Well-characterized ABA mutants in Col-0 include abi4-t (SALK_080095) and abi5-8 (SALK_013163), and the nced6/9 double mutant [30, 53, 65].

The ODR1 complementation lines (ODR1_Com) were generated by introducing the full-length coding sequence (CDS) of ODR1, driven by the 12 S promoter, into the odr1-2 mutant via Agrobacterium-mediated floral dip transformation [66]. Three T3-generation single-copy homozygous lines, which showed significantly increased ODR1 expression levels in developing seeds, were selected for subsequent analyses.

Plant growth conditions

Arabidopsis seeds were stratified at 4℃ in darkness for 2–3 days on moist filter paper prior to sowing. For plant propagation, stratified seeds were sown in a soil mixture (potting soil: vermiculite = 3:1, v/v) and grown in either a greenhouse or a growth chamber (Hipoint, FH-650) under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod, with a constant temperature of 22℃–24℃ and relative humidity of 50%–60%. Freshly harvested seeds were stored in a cabinet (Huayuxiandai, HYXD-180KWS) under constant conditions (darkness, ~ 50% relative humidity, 22℃) for after-ripening.

Seed germination and dormancy assays

For seed germination assays, seeds with the same storage periods were surface-sterilized with 10% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 5–10 min, followed by 5–6 rinses with sterile distilled water to remove residual disinfectant. Sterilized seeds were sown on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) solid medium supplemented with the indicated concentrations of mannitol or fluridone, and stratified at 4℃ in darkness for 2–3 days. Plates were then transferred to a growth chamber for germination under controlled conditions. Seed germination and seedling establishment were documented at specified time points using either a digital camera or a stereomicroscope equipped with a CCD (Charge-Coupled Device) imager. For images captured by CCD, the software-generated scale bars embedded in the original images were inaccurate; thus, calibrated scale bars have been re-added to ensure dimensional accuracy. Germination was defined as radicle emergence through the seed coat, and post-germinative seedling establishment was scored as the development of green, fully expanded cotyledons in germinated seeds, as previously described [67]. For osmotic stress treatments, specified concentrations of mannitol or the corresponding solvent control (mock treatment) were added to 1/2 MS medium prior to autoclaving. For fluridone treatments, specified concentrations of fluridone were added to 1/2 MS medium after autoclaving, and seeds were pretreated with 10 μm fluridone for 30 min before sowing.

For seed dormancy assays, freshly harvested seeds (without after-ripening) were used following a standard protocol [68]. Briefly, seeds were sown on water-saturated filter paper in transparent light-permeable containers and incubated in the growth chamber under controlled conditions, without stratification. Germination was recorded 7 days after sowing using a digital camera.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) assays

Total RNA was extracted from seeds using the FastPure Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, RC401) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA sample was treated with DNase I to remove residual genomic DNA and served as the template for reverse transcription using the HiScript III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, R312-01).

qRT-PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711). Relative gene expression was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method with ACTIN8 (AT1G49240) or UBC21 (AT5G25760) as an internal reference. Primer sequences for target genes and the reference gene are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses

Total RNA was extracted as described above. Library preparation and 150-bp paired-end sequencing were performed by Novogene Biotech (Beijing, China) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw data were analyzed following our previous protocol [69]: briefly, low-quality sequences were removed using fastp (version 0.23.1); clean reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1); HTSeq (version 2.0.5) was used for read counting; raw counts were normalized using DESeq2 (version 1.46.0) to compare gene expression across samples. Transcripts per million (TPM) was used to quantify RNA abundance.

Determination of ABA content

Seed samples (~ 200 mg) were harvested after treatment, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground into powder at 30 Hz for 1 min. The powder was then shipped on dry ice to MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (http://www.metware.cn/). ABA contents were determined by MetWare using the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform.

Publicly available data analysis

For analysis of ODR1 expression during seed germination, raw RNA-seq data were retrieved from previous studies [70, 71] and visualized using Excel. For analysis of ODR1 binding to target DNA, raw ChIP-seq data were obtained from published studies [56, 58] and visualized with IGV (Integrative Genomics Viewer).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired Student’s t-test (one- or two-tailed, as indicated) with a two-sample unequal variance assumption. Significance levels are indicated as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01.

Results

Loss of ODR1 function arrests seed germination under osmotic stress

To explore the role of ODR1 in seed germination under osmotic stress, two widely used ODR1 loss-of-function mutants, odr1-2 (SALK_022729) and odr1-3 (SALK_203161) were utilized [53, 55–57, 63, 64], and mannitol treatment was adopted to simulate osmotic stress in this study. Both mutants and their wild-type control Col-0 were sown on 1/2 MS plates supplemented with different concentrations of mannitol to analyze their germination phenotypes after stratification. When no mannitol was added, there was no obvious difference in seed germination between the odr1-2, odr1-3 mutants and Col-0 (Fig. 1A); this observation is consistent with previous reports that stratification can restore the dormant phenotype of odr1 mutants [55]. However, upon mannitol addition, distinct phenotypic differences emerged between the odr1 mutants and Col-0 (Fig. 1A). Specifically, under 200 mM mannitol treatment, most Col-0 seeds were able to develop to the cotyledon greening stage (post-germination growth stage), whereas approximately half of the odr1 mutant seeds remained at the radicle emergence stage (germination completion stage); when the mannitol concentration was increased to 300 mM, some Col-0 seeds could still complete germination, while odr1 mutant seeds were barely able to germinate (Fig. 1A). To quantitatively analyze these phenotypic differences, we counted and compared the radicle emergence rates and cotyledon greening rates of different genotypes under gradient mannitol stress (Figs. 1B and S1). The results showed that at 200 mM and 300 mM mannitol, both the radicle emergence rates and cotyledon greening rates of the two odr1 mutant seeds were significantly lower than those of Col-0 (Figs. 1B and S1). Further tracking the daily germination dynamics uncovered that as early as day 2, the germination rates of odr1 mutants on plates containing 100 or 200 mM mannitol began to diverge significantly from Col-0, with the mutants lagging distinctly in the germination process (Fig. 1C). To further validate the role of ODR1, we also isolated the ODR1 loss-of-function mutant odr1-5 in the Ler background from the previously reported abi3-1 odr1-5 double mutant [53, 64]. Under 200 mM mannitol treatment, a similar attenuation of germination was observed in odr1-5 (Fig. S2). Taken together, these results indicate that ODR1 may be involved in facilitating seed germination under osmotic stress conditions.

ODR1 complementation lines rescues the seed germination arrest of odr1-2 mutants under osmotic stress

Previously, to investigate the role of ODR1 in regulating seed dormancy, we generated multiple homozygous T3 complementation lines with single-copy T-DNA insertions in the odr1-2 background, by expressing the ODR1 CDS driven by the seed-specific 12 S promoter, a highly effective promoter for studying gene functions during seed dormancy and germination stages [72]. To verify the regulatory role of ODR1 in seed germination under osmotic stress, we selected three independent complementation lines (ODR1_Com #8, #11, and #23) for rescue experiments. qRT-PCR analysis showed that ODR1 expression was significantly restored in developing seeds of these complementation lines (Fig. S3A). Correspondingly, the enhanced seed dormancy phenotype of odr1-2 was fully rescued in these complementation lines (Fig. S3B), thus confirming the validity of these complementation constructs.

Subsequently, we sowed Col-0, odr1-2, and these complementation lines on 1/2 MS plates containing different concentrations of mannitol, quantified their germination performance after stratification, and compared the results across genotypes. The results showed that under all tested mannitol concentrations, the germination rates of ODR1_Com #8 and #23 were comparable to those of Col-0 and significantly higher than odr1-2 (Fig. 2A), indicating that both lines could fully rescue the germination arrest of odr1-2 under osmotic stress. For ODR1_Com #11, the line fully rescued the germination arrest of odr1-2 under low osmotic stress (100 mM and 200 mM mannitol), with germination rates indistinguishable from Col-0 (Fig. 2A). Under 300 mM mannitol, the germination rate of ODR1_Com #11 was slightly lower than that of Col-0 but notably higher than that of odr1-2 (Fig. 2A), indicating that this line still partially rescued the phenotype of odr1-2 under high osmotic stress. We also observed post-germination seedling establishment, and found that even under 300 mM high osmotic stress, these complementation lines were able to progress to the cotyledon greening stage like Col-0 (Fig. 2B). Statistical analysis showed their seedling establishment phenotypes were fully (ODR1_Com #8) or partially (ODR1_Com #11 and #23) rescued (Fig. S3C). These results indicate that seed-specific expression of ODR1 can rescue the germination arrest of odr1-2 seeds under osmotic stress, confirming that ODR1 plays a positive regulatory role in seed germination under osmotic stress conditions.

Osmotic stress induces and maintains ODR1 expression during seed germination

The ODR1 expression pattern in seed development is consistent with its function in regulating seed dormancy [53, 55], suggesting a tight link between its function and expression dynamics. Thus, dissecting ODR1 expression dynamics during seed germination, particularly under osmotic stress, is helpful for clarifying its function in this process. To this end, we first characterized ODR1 expression dynamics during seed germination under normal conditions by re-analyzing publicly available transcriptome data [70, 71]. The results showed that ODR1 expression decreases sharply after stratification, and this downward trend intensifies during subsequent germination, reaching nearly undetectable levels by 48 h (Fig. 3A and B).

Next, we investigated the effect of osmotic stress on ODR1 expression by comparing its transcript levels in seeds treated with 300 mM mannitol for 48 h versus those in the mock control. Osmotic stress significantly upregulated ODR1 expression by nearly 30-fold (Fig. 3C).

However, by 48 h, mock control seeds typically progressed to the radicle emergence stage, whereas under mannitol treatment, some seeds either remained ungerminated with intact seed coats or had only advanced to the testa rupture stage. To exclude the possibility that the observed expression differences were due to distinct developmental stages (e.g., germinated vs. ungerminated seeds) rather than osmotic stress itself, we further examined ODR1 expression dynamics under short-term treatments. In the absence of mannitol, ODR1 expression decreased significantly 3 h after imbibition and reached extremely low levels by 48 h (Fig. 3D); this expression pattern is highly similar to those in Fig. 3A and B. In contrast, under mannitol treatment, ODR1 expression remained at a relatively high level throughout the time course (Fig. 3D), with slight fluctuations that may be attributed to inherent circadian rhythms [73, 74]. These results confirmed that osmotic stress induces and maintains high ODR1 expression during seed germination, thereby enabling it to effectively facilitate seed germination under osmotic stress.

Osmotic stress induces ODR1 expression through an ABA-dependent pathway

Given that osmotic stress induces ABA accumulation and activates ABA signaling pathways [15–18], and that ODR1 expression is dramatically induced by ABA in 12-day-old seedlings [63], we aimed to determine whether the osmotic stress-induced ODR1 expression during seed germination is mediated by ABA. To address this, we first analyzed ODR1 transcript levels under osmotic stress in the ABA biosynthesis-deficient mutant nced6/9. After 48 h of 300 mM mannitol treatment, ODR1 expression in Col-0 was significantly upregulated by ~ 20-fold (Fig. 4A), similar to the previous independent result (Fig. 3C). However, this upregulation was significantly attenuated in the nced6/9 mutant (Fig. 4A). Additionally, even without mannitol treatment, the basal expression level of ODR1 in nced6/9 was significantly lower than that in Col-0 (Fig. 4A). These results indicate that osmotic stress-induced ABA accumulation is required for ODR1 induction and that basal ODR1 expression under normal conditions is sustained by endogenous ABA.

ABA-mediated gene expression regulation acts through downstream TFs like ABI3, ABI4, and ABI5, which are critical for seed germination under osmotic stress [25–33]. To investigate whether these TFs contribute to ODR1 regulation, we analyzed the impact of their functional loss on ODR1 expression using abi3-1 in the Ler background and abi4-t and abi5-8 in the Col-0 background. As expected, under non-stress conditions, these mutant seeds germinated faster than their respective wild-type controls while showing a similar germination extent under osmotic stress within 48 h (Fig. S4), which rules out the confounding effects of developmental stage on ODR1 expression. Surprisingly, in abi3-1, ODR1 expression changes under both conditions were not statistically significant (Fig. 4B). This suggests ABI3 may play a relatively minor role in regulating ODR1 expression during seed germination, in contrast to its major regulatory role during seed development [53, 56]. However, in abi4-t and abi5-8 mutants, ODR1 expression was reduced by at least one-third compared with Col-0 regardless of mannitol treatment, indicating that ABI4 and ABI5 likely contribute to the regulation of ODR1 expression during seed germination. Notably, the magnitude and statistical significance of ODR1 downregulation in the single mutants of abi3, abi4, and abi5 were typically less pronounced than in the nced6/9 mutant. These results suggest that ABA-mediated regulation of ODR1 may require the coordinated action of multiple ABA downstream TFs, with individual TFs contributing only moderately. Collectively, these results demonstrate that osmotic stress-induced ODR1 expression is ABA-dependent: ABA accumulation provides a foundational inductive signal, while ABA-responsive TFs such as ABI4 and ABI5 synergistically mediate the robust induction of ODR1 under osmotic stress.

ODR1 represses the expression of ABA metabolic genes during seed germination under osmotic stress

Given that ODR1 expression under osmotic stress is ABA dependent (Fig. 4) and endogenous ABA accumulation under osmotic stress is a key factor inhibiting seed germination [4–10], we sought to determine whether ODR1 in turn exerts feedback regulation on ABA metabolism during stress-inhibited germination, as it does during dormancy establishment [53]. Since ODR1 typically acts as a transcriptional repressor [56, 63], we performed transcriptome analysis of Col-0 and odr1-2 seeds under both osmotic stress and non-stress conditions, focusing on core ABA metabolic genes (both anabolic and catabolic) to determine whether ODR1 loss-of-function affects their expression. Through transcriptome sequencing, we detected TPM values for a total of 23,273 genes. Under non-mannitol conditions, 1,187 genes showed significant differential expression between odr1-2 and Col-0 (|Log₂FC| ≥ 1, P < 0.05), accounting for ~ 5.10% of the total detected genes; under mannitol treatment, 1,142 differential expressed genes were identified, with a similar proportion of ~ 4.91% (Supplementary Table S2). Focusing on ABA metabolism, 7 out of 18 reported key genes involved in this pathway showed significant differential expression between the two genotypes under at least one condition (Fig. 5A), corresponding to a much higher proportion of ~ 38.89%—over 7-fold higher than the background differential gene rate (~ 5%). Additionally, two other ABA biosynthesis genes (NCED1 and NCED4) showed statistically significant expression differences (P < 0.05) but with fold changes below 2 (|Log₂FC| < 1) (Fig. 5A). Collectively, these results indicate that differential expressed genes are significantly enriched in the ABA metabolic pathway, with 9 ABA metabolic genes showing statistical significance in expression between odr1-2 and Col-0 under at least one condition. Notably, all 9 genes were upregulated in odr1-2 (Fig. 5A), consistent with the role of ODR1 as a transcriptional repressor [56, 63]. Among these, 8 (88.89%) are involved in ABA biosynthesis, accounting for 66.7% of the 12 analyzed ABA biosynthesis genes (Fig. 5A), suggesting that ODR1 preferentially represses ABA biosynthesis genes.

To identify key ABA gene targets of ODR1 involved in osmotic stress-inhibited seed germination, we first narrowed down the 9 ABA metabolic genes through their germination-stage expression. ABA1, NCED1, and CYP707A2 emerged as primary candidates due to their markedly higher expression under osmotic stress (TPM > 50 vs. TPM < 10 for others) (Fig. 5B), implying relatively greater functional relevance in this process.

To verify direct binding of ODR1 to the regulatory regions of these candidate genes, we re-analyzed published ODR1 ChIP-seq data derived from two different transgenic constructs, OLexApro::3×HA-AtSDR4L-3×Flag and AtSDR4Lpro::3×HA-AtSDR4L-3×Flag, each represented by two independent lines [56, 58]. No ODR1 binding peaks were detected at the NCED6 and NCED9 loci (Fig. S5), which is consistent with our previous Y1H assays showing no interaction between ODR1 and the NCED6/9 promoters [53], thus validating the reliability of the ChIP-seq data. Distinct binding peaks were detected in the ABA1 promoter across 3 of the 4 lines (encompassing both constructs) (Figs. 5C and S4). In contrast, no binding signals were observed at the NCED1 locus across all four lines, and CYP707A2 exhibited peaks exclusively in the two lines harboring only one construct (Fig. S5), consistent with the lack of interaction between ODR1 and CYP707A2 promoter detected in Y1H assays [53]. Additionally, no reliable binding peaks of ODR1 could be detected in other analyzed loci (Fig. S5), which cross-validates our previous Y1H results [53]. These results indicate that, relative to the other genes examined, the ABA1 promoter is most likely to be directly targeted by ODR1.

To confirm the repression of ABA1 by ODR1, we analyzed their transcript levels in ODR1 complementation lines via qRT-PCR. Consistent with our expectation, the aberrant upregulation of ABA1 in odr1-2 was significantly alleviated or eliminated in the complementation lines (Fig. 5D). This rescue confirms that ODR1 is responsible for repressing ABA1. Collectively, these results establish a link between the transcriptional repressive activity of ODR1 and its function in restraining ABA anabolic gene expression during seed germination under osmotic stress.

ODR1 alleviates osmotic stress-induced germination arrest via repressing ABA accumulation

In the odr1-2 mutant, 8 out of 12 ABA biosynthesis genes showed significantly increased expression, with 5 of these genes exhibiting upregulation under both non-stress and osmotic stress conditions (Fig. 5A and B). This prompted us to hypothesize that ABA levels might be correspondingly elevated in the odr1-2 mutant. However, we also observed that the expression of the ABA catabolic gene CYP707A2 was significantly upregulated in odr1-2 under both conditions (Fig. 5A and B). To clarify the actual trend of ABA levels in odr1-2, we measured ABA content in seeds of odr1-2 and Col-0 at 24 h after imbibition under both conditions. The results showed that ABA levels in odr1-2 were significantly higher than in Col-0 under both conditions, consistent with the upregulation of ABA biosynthesis genes. We speculate that the increased expression of CYP707A2 in odr1-2 is likely a feedback regulatory response to elevated ABA levels, as CYP707A2 expression is ABA-responsive (Fig. S6). Collectively, these results indicate that ODR1 represses ABA accumulation by inhibiting the expression of ABA biosynthesis genes, thereby alleviating the inhibitory effect of ABA on seed germination.

To test whether the arrested seed germination of odr1 under osmotic stress is caused by excessive ABA accumulation, we assessed the germination of odr1 mutants and Col-0 seeds on 1/2 MS medium containing 300 mM mannitol supplemented with increasing concentrations of fluridone, a classic ABA biosynthesis inhibitor [75]. Fluridone alleviated the mannitol-inhibited germination in Col-0 (Fig. 6B), consistent with ABA acting as a key mediator of osmotic stress-induced germination arrest. Importantly, fluridone exerted a more striking rescue effect on odr1 mutants (Fig. 6B). Notably, prolonged fluridone treatment arrested normal seedling greening (Fig. 6C; [76]), precluding further assessment of whether ABA reduction could rescue the cotyledon greening defects of odr1 mutants. Collectively, these results confirm that the arrested germination of odr1 mutants under osmotic stress is attributable to excessive ABA accumulation, suggesting that ODR1 alleviates osmotic stress-induced germination arrest by repressing ABA accumulation.

Discussion

ODR1 acts as a key environmental adaptor for seeds by coordinating dormancy establishment and stress germination processes

Precise regulation of germination timing is a key adaptive strategy of seed plants, directly influencing individual survival and population persistence. In natural environments, early germination exposes the resulting vulnerable seedlings to unfavorable conditions caused by seasonal changes; conversely, late germination may result in missing the head-start for resource competition. To address this, plants have evolved seed dormancy as a trait to balance the survival risks and reproductive benefits of offspring, representing a population-level strategy for coping with environmental fluctuations. Meanwhile, plants have evolved an individual-level strategy to actively cope with such fluctuations: germinating seeds initiate emergency mechanisms to delay or even suspend germination upon encountering adverse stresses, thereby increasing their chances of surviving harsh environments. These two strategies complement each other, collectively enhancing plant environmental adaptability.

Previously, we reported that ODR1 positively regulates seed germination during dormancy establishment [53], a finding validated by multiple independent studies [55–57]. This study further reveals that ODR1 also functions critically in stress-inhibited germination: loss of ODR1 function significantly delays seed germination under osmotic stress (Fig. 1), while restoring ODR1 expression in odr1 mutant seeds effectively rescues the arrested germination phenotype (Fig. 2). These results confirmed that ODR1 facilitates seed germination under stress conditions. Combining both studies, ODR1 emerges as a crucial germination promoter across both processes: it prominently represses dormancy during seed development, and delicately fine-tunes germination under osmotic stress. Therefore, we propose that ODR1 acts as a key environmental adaptor for seeds by coordinating dormancy establishment and stress germination processes.

Intriguingly, ODR1 expression dynamically adapts to seed germination status. When seeds are in a germination-inhibited state (e.g., dormant or under stress), its expression is typically sustained at high levels ([53, 55]; Fig. 3C and D), enabling its germination-promoting activity to antagonize such inhibitory state. Conversely, when seeds gain robust germination capacity (e.g., following cold stratification and transfer to favorable conditions), ODR1 expression is markedly downregulated ([53, 55]; Fig. 3A and B). This downregulation aligns with the biological need to avoid excessive repression of ABA biosynthesis, as moderate ABA levels may be required to coordinate proper seed-to-seedling transition, which is supported by the observation that prolonged fluridone treatment leads to abnormal cotyledon greening (Fig. 6C). Such a regulatory pattern reflects ODR1’s role of “stepping back once its task is done,” further validating its context-dependent function as a key adaptor for seeds. Thus, unraveling the regulatory mechanisms governing ODR1 expression, particularly identifying its upstream regulators, carries substantial scientific significance.

ODR1 expression is primarily induced by ABA, with other specific regulators remaining to be identified

Our earlier work revealed that ODR1 expression rises progressively during seed development [53], a pattern that closely matches the gradual establishment of seed dormancy [2, 12]. This study further shows that ODR1 is highly expressed during seed germination under osmotic stress (Fig. 3C and D). Both findings indicate that ODR1 is specifically induced to high levels during its functional stages, indicating a precise regulatory mechanism governing its expression in seeds.

Here, we propose that ABA functions as an important regulator of ODR1 expression. First, the osmotic stress-induced upregulation of ODR1 is significantly alleviated in ABA biosynthesis-deficient mutant (Fig. 4A), indicating that this induction is ABA-dependent. Second, exogenous ABA application to 12-day-old seedlings dramatically induces ODR1 expression [63], where ODR1 is otherwise not expressed in these seedlings [55]. Third, the accumulation pattern of ODR1 expression during seed development and germination under stress conditions closely matches that of ABA [9, 13, 15, 18, 19, 53, 55]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that ABA acts as a key inducer of ODR1 expression.

ABA regulates the expression of its responsive genes through downstream TFs such as ABI3, ABI4, and ABI5 [25–33]. Previous work showed that ABI3 could regulate ODR1 expression in developing and imbibed seeds [53, 56]. However, in this study, we found that osmotic stress-induced ODR1 expression was not significantly affected in the abi3-1 mutant (Fig. 4B), suggesting that ABI3 plays a negligible role in ODR1 regulation at this stage. This discrepancy is likely due to the dynamic expression of ABI3, whose transcripts are highly abundant during seed dormancy establishment but rapidly decline during seed germination [24, 25, 77]. In contrast, ODR1 expression was significantly lower in abi4-t and abi5-8 mutants compared to Col-0 under both stressed and non-stressed conditions, implying that ABI4 and ABI5 are likely direct regulators of ODR1 at seed germination stage. Future studies using Y1H, ChIP-qPCR, and EMSA assays are needed to verify their binding to the ODR1 promoter, while tobacco transient transformation systems could further validate their role in activating ODR1 promoter activity.

Notably, although osmotic stress-induced ODR1 expression in the nced6/9 mutant was reduced by 64.49% compared to Col-0, ODR1 expression levels still remained higher than those in non-stressed Col-0 (Fig. 4A). This suggests that additional regulators beyond ABA are involved in ODR1 expression. Previous ChIP-seq experiments identified LEC1 binding peaks in the ODR1 promoter, and ODR1 expression was significantly reduced in the lec1-c1 mutant [56], implying that LEC1 may act as a direct positive regulator of ODR1. Whether LEC1 participates in ODR1 regulation during osmotic stress-induced germination remains to be further investigated.

Furthermore, we observed that the reduction in ODR1 expression in ABA-related mutants was more pronounced under non-stressed conditions than under osmotic stress (Fig. 4; e.g., in nced6/9: −77.55% vs. −64.49%; in abi4-t: −81.58% vs. −53.62%; in abi5-8: −60.06% vs. −36.55%). Under non-stressed conditions, seeds of these mutants typically germinate faster than Col-0 (Fig. S4), progressing to later germination stages where ODR1 expression decreases significantly ([53, 55]; Fig. 3A and B). This leads us to hypothesize that a regulator responsive to developmental signals may repress ODR1 expression. To identify such regulators, future screening could be performed using the ODR1 promoter as bait in Y1H TFs libraries.

ODR1 negatively feedback-regulates ABA biosynthesis to antagonize excessive ABA signal amplification and potentially integrates into other seed germination regulatory pathways

ABA-responsive TFs such as ABI3, ABI4, and ABI5 are well-characterized as ABA signal amplifiers that inhibit seed germination both during dormancy establishment and under osmotic stress [25–33]. However, the mechanisms preventing excessive ABA signal amplification to achieve precise regulation of ABA actions remain poorly understood. Our study reveals that ODR1 functions downstream of ABA to antagonize such signal amplification. In the odr1 mutants, most ABA biosynthesis genes are significantly upregulated (Fig. 5A and B), accompanied by a marked increase in ABA content (Fig. 6A). These results indicate that upon induction by ABA, ODR1 conversely represses the expression of ABA biosynthesis genes to prevent excessive ABA accumulation, thereby antagonizing ABA signal amplification.

Analysis of ODR1 ChIP-seq data identified a prominent binding peak of ODR1 in the promoter region of ABA1 (Figs. 5C and S4), and the upregulation of ABA1 expression was significantly suppressed in ODR1 complementation lines (Fig. 5D), suggesting that ODR1 may directly repress ABA1 expression through promoter binding, though this interaction requires further validation using Y1H or EMSA assays. In addition to this, our previous Y1H results showed no direct binding of ODR1 to the promoters of NCED6/9 [53], which were supported by the findings that no ODR1 binding peaks were detected in these loci in the ChIP-seq analysis (Fig. S5). However, ODR1 can regulate NCED6/9 indirectly through interaction with bHLH57 [53]. Thus, ODR1 likely exerts similar indirect regulation via intermediate TFs for other ABA biosynthesis genes. Future screening of Y2H libraries to identify TFs interacting with ODR1 will greatly facilitate the elucidation of how ODR1 regulates ABA biosynthesis genes.

Notably, the ABA catabolic gene CYP707A2 was significantly upregulated in germinating odr1-2 seeds (Fig. 5A and B), a phenomenon also observed in freshly harvested dry odr1-2 seeds [53]. Since no direct interaction between ODR1 and the CYP707A2 promoter was detected in Y1H assays [53] and no reliable ODR1 binding peaks were identified at the CYP707A2 locus in the ChIP-seq analysis (Fig. S5), we hypothesize that the upregulation of CYP707A2 in odr1-2 seeds likely results from feedback regulation triggered by elevated ABA levels. This hypothesis is supported by the observation that exogenous ABA strongly induces CYP707A2 expression in seeds and seedlings (Fig. S6). Such responsiveness of CYP707A2 to ABA provides another important example of the precise feedback regulation of ABA homeostasis in seeds.

Exogenous application of 5 µM fluridone effectively rescued the arrested germination phenotype of odr1 mutants under osmotic stress (Fig. 6B), confirming that ODR1-mediated regulation of germination under osmotic stress is dependent on the ABA pathway. However, even 20 µM fluridone failed to fully restore odr1 germination to wild-type levels, implying additional contributions from other regulatory pathways. Furthermore, although odr1-2 seeds exhibit significantly higher ABA levels than Col-0 under non-stress conditions (Fig. 6A), no corresponding germination differences were observed (Figs. 1 and 2), further suggesting the involvement of other pathways in ODR1-mediated regulation of seed germination. Seed germination is typically coordinated by the antagonistic actions of ABA and gibberellin (GA) [1–7]; thus, we speculate that ODR1 may also modulate the GA pathway, and this speculation is supported by clues from multiple studies.

A previous study on ODR1’s role in seed dormancy showed that freshly harvested odr1-2 seeds have reduced expression of GA biosynthesis genes GA20OX1 and GA20OX2, along with decreased GA4 and GA7 contents, indicating ODR1’s influence on GA biosynthesis [55]. However, exogenous GA application failed to rescue the enhanced dormancy phenotype of odr1-2 seeds [55], suggesting potential involvement of ODR1 in GA signal transduction. Consistent with this, another study reported significantly upregulated expression of RGL2 (a key negative regulator of GA signaling) in odr1-2 seedlings, with ChIP-seq data revealing an ODR1 binding peak at the RGL2 locus [56], implying ODR1 may regulate GA signaling through RGL2. Intriguingly, RGL2 expression remained unchanged in freshly harvested dry and imbibed odr1-2 seeds [55], indicating stage-specific effects of ODR1 on GA signaling between seed dormancy and germination. Thus, clarifying whether and how ODR1 regulates GA biosynthesis and signaling specifically during stress-inhibited germination is essential for improving our understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which ODR1 promotes germination under stress.

ODR1 is a homolog of the rice dormancy gene Sdr4, but the two genes exert opposite regulatory effects on seed dormancy, reflecting conserved yet divergent functions in plant dormancy regulation. This conservation and divergence in their functions is further evident in their roles in ABA-related pathways. Previous studies show that rice Sdr4 loss-of-function mutants have reduced sensitivity to ABA-mediated germination inhibition [78], while Arabidopsis ODR1 loss-of-function mutants show increased ABA sensitivity [63]. Therefore, combined with the finding of our current study that ODR1 regulates ABA biosynthesis, this prompts us to consider whether Sdr4 in rice also regulates ABA biosynthesis and seed germination under stress. Rice production faces key agricultural challenges, including pre-harvest sprouting (PHS) under high humidity and arrested germination under osmotic stress. Clarifying whether Sdr4 regulates osmotic stress responses in rice seeds thus holds important practical significance. Elucidating its role in balancing PHS resistance and stress germination can deepen understanding of homologous gene evolution across species and provide molecular targets for rice breeding.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies ODR1 as a pivotal regulator of seed germination under osmotic stress. ODR1 responds to ABA signaling while repressing ABA biosynthesis, thereby forming a negative feedback loop that prevents excessive ABA accumulation. By balancing ABA signaling and metabolism, ODR1 enables seeds to achieve adaptive germination under adverse conditions. This regulatory mechanism not only advances our understanding of how plants integrate stress signals with hormone homeostasis but also provides a molecular target for enhancing crop resilience against PHS and osmotic stress.
