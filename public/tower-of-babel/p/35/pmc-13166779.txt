Management of a large appendiceal abscess presenting as an abdominal wall collection: a case report
Authors: Masire Mtatiro Mwita, Edouard Karume Rugendabanga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:An appendiceal abscess represents a localized complication of acute appendicitis, resulting from a walled-off perforation. This is an appendiceal abscess presenting as an abdominal wall collection managed with bedside drainage. This case demonstrates that bedside drainage can be safe and effective in the absence of imaging in specific patients, providing a practical management option for resource-limited settings.Case presentation:We report a case of a 36-year-old male who presented with 1 month of colicky periumbilical pain, fever, and a 1-week history of a painful swelling in the right lower abdomen. Physical examination revealed a tender, erythematous, and edematous mass measuring approximately 8 cm in diameter in the right iliac fossa. Under local anesthesia, bedside incision and drainage were performed, yielding 500 mL of thick pus, followed by irrigation with 1500 mL of normal saline. The wound was left open and dressed daily. Culture of the pus showed no bacterial growth, likely due to prior antibiotic exposure. The patient received empirical intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole, followed by oral amoxicillin-clavulanate and metronidazole upon discharge. Clinical recovery was uneventful, and he remained well on follow-up, declining the elective interval appendectomy.Discussion:This case demonstrates that conservative management with abscess drainage and antibiotics can achieve complete resolution of infection in an appendiceal abscess. Careful clinical monitoring and patient education are crucial to detect recurrence or residual disease.Conclusion:Early recognition and prompt nonoperative management of appendiceal abscess can result in excellent outcomes and avoid unnecessary surgical morbidity. Individualized follow-up and selective interval appendectomy remain key components of long-term care.

Introduction

An appendiceal mass encompasses a pathological continuum ranging from phlegmonous inflammation to organized abscess formation[1,2]. An appendicular abscess is a relatively frequent complication of acute appendicitis, occurring in approximately 2–7% of patients at the time of initial presentation, particularly among those with delayed hospital admission. The formation of an appendiceal mass reflects the host’s attempt to localize infection through omentum and bowel wall adhesion, effectively creating a walled-off inflammatory complex[3,4]. Given the rapid progression of appendiceal inflammation, virtually any patient may develop this complication; gangrenous transformation with impending perforation can occur within hours of symptom onset[5].

In the early 20th century, the standard of care consisted of conservative inpatient management with bed rest until spontaneous abscess drainage occurred. By the 1960s, surgical drainage was advocated when technically straightforward[1,4]. Subsequently, concerns regarding iatrogenic dissemination of infection following early operative intervention led to renewed support for a nonoperative strategy[3,6]. With advancements in anesthesia, antimicrobial therapy, and perioperative support, the pendulum later swung toward immediate appendectomy for all stages of appendicitis[3,4]. Contemporary evidence, however, increasingly supports an initial conservative, nonoperative approach, employing broad-spectrum antibiotics, intravenous fluid resuscitation, and bowel rest[1,2,4].

Classical acute appendicitis typically manifests with periumbilical pain migrating to the right iliac fossa, associated with anorexia, nausea, and leukocytosis. When perforation is localized, and an inflammatory mass or abscess develops, patients often present with a mildly distended abdomen, localized guarding, and a tender, poorly defined mass in the right iliac fossa, sometimes with overlying erythema. Vigorous palpation is contraindicated as it may precipitate rupture and intra-abdominal contamination[7].

The likelihood of appendiceal mass formation increases in individuals who delay medical consultation, receive incomplete antibiotic therapy, or present with atypical clinical manifestations leading to diagnostic delays[3,4]. Although emergency appendectomy provides definitive treatment, it is technically challenging in this context due to distorted anatomy and severe inflammation, resulting in higher postoperative morbidity and longer hospitalization[1,3,4].

Percutaneous or surgical abscess drainage in combination with broad-spectrum antibiotics represents a viable alternative to immediate appendectomy. However, this approach may be associated with prolonged drainage duration, larger incision requirements, and an increased risk of postoperative abscess formation.

Appendiceal abscess with parietal wall extension, managed without imaging, bedside drainage under local anesthesia can be considered as an effective, safe and viable option in low-income countries[2].

Case report

We report the case of a 36-year-old male who presented with colicky periumbilical pain for 1 month, fever for 1 month, anorexia, nausea and vomiting for 2 weeks, and a painful swelling in the right lower abdomen for 1 week.

The abdominal pain had a gradual onset, was non-radiating and colicky in nature. It was aggravated by food intake without any relieving factors. The pain was temporarily relieved by paracetamol. Two weeks after the onset of symptoms, the patient experienced episodic, non-projectile, non-bilious vomiting, preceded by nausea and loss of appetite. Subsequently, he noted the gradual appearance of a right lower abdominal swelling associated with localized tenderness and overlying skin erythema.

He denied any change in bowel habits, urinary symptoms, or abdominal trauma. He had previously received undocumented pharmacological treatment at a nearby health facility prior to presentation. Patient did not report any comorbidities or surgical history, no drug allergies, and no history of alcohol or smoking.

On examination, the patient appeared mildly ill but was hemodynamically stable.

Vital signs: Respiratory rate 21 cycles/min, temperature 37.9°C, pulse rate 78 beats/min, and blood pressure 126/67 mmHg.

Abdominal examination: The abdomen moved normally with respiration. A localized swelling measuring approximately 8 cm in diameter was noted in the right iliac fossa, with tenderness, warmth, erythema, and edema of the overlying skin (Fig. 1). The Alvarado score comforted the surgical team with the diagnosis of prior appendicitis complicated by an appendicular abscess. It is important to remind that the Alvarado score was not used to diagnose the abscess or to guide the treatment.

Main differentials considered were appendiceal abscess with parietal extension, primary abdominal wall abscess, psoas abscess, ileocecal tuberculosis, strangulated Spigelian hernia and perforated cecal neoplasm. The working diagnosis after the differentials were ruled out, explains absence of imaging, and clarifies that the Alvarado score was used only to support prior appendicitis; the checklist shows Diagnostic Challenges and Diagnostic Reasoning items ticked. Appendiceal abscess was favored with a history of progressive periumbilical to right iliac fossa pain, Alvarado score 9/10, pointing to a fluctuant mass with overlying erythema, marked leukocytosis and neutrophilia. Alternatives were excluded with no hip flexion/psoas sign (excludes psoas abscess), no chronic wasting/night sweats (less likely TB), acute onset with fever (less likely chronic neoplasm), and no groin hernia defect (excludes hernia). The impact of missing imaging was acknowledged as a diagnostic limitation, but clinical features were sufficiently distinctive to guide safe intervention, and therefore, the surgical team decided to do bedside drainage in this precise case as the best option.

The patient was prepped and draped in a sterile condition. A bedside incision and drainage procedure was performed under local anesthesia with 10 mL of 2% lignocaine infiltrated via an 18-gauge cannula lateral to the mass, approximately 10 cm above the anterior superior iliac spine along the anterior axillary line, between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles to achieve an iliohypogastric and lumbar nerve block.

A 4-cm transverse skin incision was made directly over the swelling, and approximately 500 mL of thick purulent material was drained, leaving a cavity of around 10 cm in depth (Fig. 2) (Supplemental Digital Content Video 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A32). The appendix was not identified on the examination of the medial and anterior wall of the caecum, and it was probably involved in the inflammatory process and digested by phagocytosis. We avoided multiple manipulations due to the risk of bowel injury with the option to explore more thoroughly on a probable upcoming appendectomy. The cavity was irrigated with 1500 mL of normal saline, and the wound was left open with a loose dressing to allow for continued drainage. Figure 2.Incision site and healing scar.

Incision site and healing scar.

A pus swab was sent for culture and sensitivity testing, which showed no bacterial growth after 48 h.

The patient was commenced on empirical intravenous antibiotics consisting of ceftriaxone 1 g once daily and metronidazole 500 mg three times daily, along with analgesia (oral paracetamol 1 g four times daily and oral tramadol 100 mg three times daily).

After 2 days of inpatient management, his condition improved, and he was discharged on oral amoxicillin-clavulanate 625 mg twice daily for 10 days, oral metronidazole 400 mg three times daily for 10 days, paracetamol 1 g four times daily for 3 days, and tramadol 100 mg every 8 h for 3 days.

Post-drainage management included daily wound irrigation with normal saline and regular dressing changes. The first follow-up visit was scheduled for 10 days after discharge, followed by a 1-month review to evaluate for interval appendectomy (Fig. 2). However, the patient declined surgical intervention. Consequently, a 3-month follow-up schedule was instituted for continued clinical monitoring (Fig. 2).

Discussion

An appendiceal mass represents a late complication of acute appendicitis, particularly among patients with delayed presentation or atypical clinical features[3,4]. It forms as a result of localized perforation of the appendix, leading to a contained inflammatory process involving the omentum, bowel loops, and adjacent peritoneal structures[1,2].

The patient in this case presented with a prolonged history of abdominal pain, fever, and localized swelling, consistent with the natural history of contained appendiceal perforation. His gradual onset of colicky periumbilical pain progressing to a tender right iliac fossa mass with erythematous overlying skin is characteristic of a mature appendiceal abscess. Such chronic or subacute presentations are commonly seen in patients who receive partial or undocumented antibiotic therapy prior to diagnosis, which may transiently suppress but not eradicate the infection[3,4].

The diagnostic approach to appendiceal mass relies primarily on clinical evaluation supported by imaging modalities, such as ultrasonography or computed tomography (CT), which help delineate the extent of inflammation and guide management decisions[2,5]. However, in resource-limited settings, clinical assessment remains the cornerstone for diagnosis and management planning.

The modern consensus supports initial nonoperative management, incorporating broad-spectrum antibiotics with or without image-guided or open abscess drainage. This approach is associated with high success rates, reduced risk of intraoperative complications, and shorter hospital stay when compared with emergency appendectomy in the acute inflammatory phase[2].

In this case, the patient underwent bedside incision and drainage under nerve block anesthesia, resulting in the evacuation of a significant volume of purulent material and rapid clinical improvement (Fig. 3). While current guidelines recommend image-guided percutaneous drainage or laparoscopic intervention, the clinical presentation of a pointing, fluctuant abscess accessible through the abdominal wall, combined with absence of ultrasound and limited surgical resources, made bedside drainage under local anesthesia the most pragmatic and safest option for this patient. This approach avoided the risks and resource demands of general anesthesia and laparotomy while achieving effective source control (Fig. 2). The subsequent use of broad-spectrum intravenous and oral antibiotics facilitated complete clinical resolution. Figure 3.Pus drained after incision.

The absence of bacterial growth on culture may reflect prior antibiotic exposure, a common finding in patients referred after empirical treatment elsewhere. Despite this, empirical coverage targeting common enteric organisms – Escherichia coli, Bacteroides fragilis, and other anaerobes – remains appropriate[1,5].

Traditionally, interval appendectomy was advocated to prevent recurrence, with reported rates of 3–21%[2]. In this patient, an interval appendectomy was planned but ultimately deferred after the patient declined surgical consent. Ongoing clinical follow-up was therefore instituted to monitor for recurrence or residual disease. At the time of manuscript submission, the patient had been followed for 4 years (48 months) with no recurrence of abscess, no fistula formation, and no further abdominal symptoms. Standard management of appendiceal abscess in high-resource settings typically involves ultrasound or CT-guided percutaneous drainage with pigtail catheter placement, or laparoscopic drainage and appendectomy. These approaches offer precision and minimize bowel manipulation but require imaging, interventional radiology, or operative theater access. In contrast, bedside open drainage, as performed in this case, is feasible when the abscess has extended to involve the parietal peritoneum and abdominal wall, creating a palpable, fluctuant, pointing collection. While this approach carries theoretical risks of bowel injury and fistula formation, careful patient selection (localized, well-demarcated abscess with clear fluctuation and overlying skin changes) and meticulous technique minimize these complications. The main limitations of conservative management include recurrence risk of 3–21% (monitored through prolonged follow-up in this case), inability to exclude malignancy without histology (particularly relevant in older patients; this patient’s age and clinical course make neoplasm less likely but cannot be definitively excluded), and potential for occult complications (fistula, residual abscess). This case demonstrates that when modern imaging and interventional resources are unavailable, experienced clinical assessment combined with bedside drainage can achieve excellent outcomes, provided appropriate antibiotic coverage, close monitoring, and structured long-term follow-up are maintained.

Strengths and limitations

This case report has several strengths: detailed documentation of a pragmatic surgical approach in a resource-limited setting, with a clear description of technique and decision-making rationale;unusually large abscess volume (~500 mL) demonstrating the feasibility of bedside drainage even for substantial collections;prolonged follow-up (4 years), providing robust evidence of treatment success and absence of recurrence; andinclusion of clinical photographs and video documentation enhancing reproducibility.

The patient was interviewed at his most recent follow-up visit (48 months post-drainage). He reported: “I am very grateful that the doctors were able to treat my abscess without major surgery. I recovered quickly and was able to return to work within 2 weeks. I have had no problems since then and feel completely healthy. I was initially worried about the abscess coming back, but after 4 years with no issues, I am confident the treatment worked well.”

Conclusion

This case highlights the clinical complexity and therapeutic challenges associated with an appendiceal abscess, a late but not uncommon complication of acute appendicitis. Successful management can be achieved through prompt recognition, appropriate drainage, and targeted antimicrobial therapy, even in the absence of advanced imaging or operative intervention. The case reinforces the effectiveness of initial conservative management in achieving infection control while minimizing operative morbidity. However, it also underscores the importance of long-term clinical follow-up and individualized decision-making regarding interval appendectomy. Early diagnosis and adherence to evidence-based management protocols remain pivotal in improving outcomes for patients presenting with complicated appendicitis.
