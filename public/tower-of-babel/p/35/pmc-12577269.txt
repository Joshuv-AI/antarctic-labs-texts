Engineering of Nocardioides acrostichi lipase: utilizing natural variation and mutational free energy to alter expression and functional properties
Authors: Nongluck Jaito, Nattha Kaewsawat, Titiporn Saeoung, Kamollak Sangawthong, Tanaporn Uengwetwanit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapidly growing biocatalysis market demands cost-effective, sustainable technologies. Lipases are vital, versatile enzymes used in food, pharmaceuticals, and biofuels. Nocardioides acrostichi lipase shows promising potential for biotechnological applications, but its low extracellular production limits its industrial utility. Here, we employed a rational design integrating natural variation identified from sequence analysis and predicted mutational Gibbs free energy (ΔΔG) changes. We designed and experimentally tested three multi-site mutants. Engineered variants showed significantly improved extracellular expression and diverse enzymatic profiles. A mutant NaL-2 showed tolerance to both high temperature (> 70% activity at 70 °C) and extreme alkaline condition (stable at pH 11–13), which is a rare trait for lipase. The results provide novel lipase variants and strategies for enhancing enzyme stability and functionality, with implications for biotechnological applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00567-z.

Introduction

Enzymes play an important role as biocatalysts across various industrial sectors, contributing to the efficiency and sustainability of numerous biochemical processes. The global biocatalysis market is experiencing significant expansion, with its value projected to reach USD 2.23 billion by 2032 [1]. This growth is driven by the increasing demand for enzymes capable of replacing conventional catalysts, which often exhibit limited functional stability under specific operational conditions, such as elevated temperatures or extreme pH levels. Additionally, enzymes offer a sustainable alternative to inorganic catalysts, reducing the environmental footprint of chemical manufacturing processes and promoting more environmentally friendly industrial practices [2, 3].

Lipases (glycerol ester hydrolases, EC 3.1.1.3) are a class of enzymes that have been extensively utilized in biocatalysis due to their catalytic versatility and broad substrate specificity. Their ability to catalyze a wide range of reactions, including hydrolysis, esterification, transesterification, and interesterification, has made them indispensable in various industrial applications, such as food processing, pharmaceuticals, detergents, and biofuel production [4–7]. Numerous lipases with diverse catalytic activities and structural features have been identified and characterized [8, 9]. Among these, the lipase from Nocardioides acrostichi (NaL) is a promising candidate due to its high enzymatic activity at elevated temperatures and structural similarity to the mostly used Candida antarctica lipase B (CalB) [10]. Despite its advantageous properties, the industrial application of NaL is hindered by its low extracellular production levels (Jaito et al., 2023).

Protein stability influences expression levels in heterologous systems [11]. Instability in proteins can result in misfolding, which disrupts their native three-dimensional structure and frequently leads to the formation of insoluble aggregates. Protein engineering strategies aimed at improving stability have been successfully employed to enhance protein expression of various natural proteins [11–13]. Protein stability not only influences expression levels but also directly affects overall enzyme activity and function, making it a key determinant of its industrial applicability. A major challenge in utilizing enzymes for biotechnological processes lies in the intricate balance between enzyme activity and stability, where improvements in one often come at the expense of the other [14, 15]. To address this, utilizing evolutionary information is valuable for engineering proteins [16–18]. Natural selection preserves beneficial mutations that enhance structural integrity, functionality, and environmental stress resistance, which can optimize enzyme performance.

In this study, we employed natural variant profiles obtained through sequence analysis, combined with several computational protein stability prediction methods to rationally select a small, focused set of variants. These engineered variants were subsequently designed and experimentally characterized, assessing their substrate specificity, optimum temperature and pH, and their overall temperature and pH stability ranges.

Materials and methods

Determining natural variants and mutagenesis design

To identify and prioritize potential hot spot residues for mutagenesis, combining computational alanine scanning with an analysis of natural sequence variation were employed. First, alanine scanning mutagenesis was performed using CUPSAT [19] to predict residues critical for protein function or stability. Second, a natural variation profile was generated to assess evolutionary conservation and divergence. Homologous protein sequences of Nocardioides acrostichi lipase (UniProt accession: A0A930YCA5) were searched via online NCBI BLAST [20] against non-redundant protein database and MGnify [21]. Alignment results were exported and filtered, retaining sequences with at least 70% identity and 50% coverage for subsequent analysis. A variation profile was then calculated, representing the frequencies of different amino acids at each position within the aligned sequences. From this profile, positions were selected based on two criteria: either they exhibited diverse amino acid types (≥ 5 different types) with at least one type present at >10% frequency, or they were low-variant positions where a specific amino acid type was present at >50% frequency. Moreover, binding site positions were excluded from this study.

Mutant design involved computationally predicting Gibbs free energy changes (ΔΔG) between the wild-type and proposed mutations at selected residues. Predictions were performed using MutationExplorer [22], SDM [23], DUET [24] and INPS3D [25]. While MutationExplorer calculates ΔΔG as the change in free energy from the wild-type to the mutant protein (ΔG(Mu) - ΔG(WT)), the SDM, DUET, and INPS3D tools use the inverse calculation (ΔG(WT) - ΔG(Mu)).

To align the ΔΔG values from MutationExplorer with the other tools (SDM, DUET, and INPS3D), which define a positive value as a stabilizing mutation, we have multiplied the MutationExplorer values by -1. This ensures that all positive ΔΔG values consistently indicate a more stable mutant relative to the wild-type protein, making the data directly comparable across all four tools. To ensure robust prediction, only mutations consistently predicted as stabilizing by at least three of these methods were chosen. These selected mutations were manually combined into three distinct multi-site mutants based on their positions, natural variant frequency and predicted ΔΔG values. Molecular dynamic simulation was conducted using AMBER 2022 [26] to evaluate the structural stability of the proteins (Supplementary Fig. S1). The 3D structures of the variants were predicted using AlphaFold2 [27] and visualized with BIOVIA discovery studio visualizer [28].

Construction of recombinant E. coli BL21 (DE3) for protein expression

The gene encoding the lipase from N. acrostichi [10] (NaL-WT) and designed variants were synthesized by GenScript. The synthesized genes were cloned into the pET28a vector between the NdeI and HindIII restriction sites. A His₆ tag was located at the N-terminus of the gene, while a stop codon was placed at the C-terminus. These recombinant vectors were subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) as a host cell for protein expression. Transformants were selected on agar plates (medium containing 1% w/v tryptone, 1%w/v yeast extract, 0.5% w/v NaCl, and 2.5%w/v agar) containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin, and the successful integration of the target gene was confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) screening.

Protein expression and purification

A single colony confirmed to contain the target gene was cultured in 5 mL of LB medium in a 50 mL centrifuge tube at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 18 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into 1 L of LB medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin. The culture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm until the cell density reached an OD600 of 0.4–0.6. Lipase expression was induced by adding isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.1 mM. The culture was then incubated at 16 °C for 20 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 100 mL of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 8.0. The cells were lysed by sonication, and the lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 17,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris.

The resulting supernatant, containing the crude enzyme, was mixed 1:1 (v/v) with 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 8.0, containing 100 mM sodium chloride and 20 mM imidazole. The mixture was filtered through a 0.2-micron membrane and loaded onto a Nickel Sepharose affinity column (HisPrep™ FF 16/10 column, 20 mL volume, Cytiva) at a flow rate of 3 mL/min. The column was washed with 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 8.0, containing 100 mM sodium chloride and 20 mM imidazole to remove unbound proteins. Bound proteins were eluted using stepwise of 60, 100, 150, 200, and 250-mM imidazole in the same buffer. The samples were collected in 5 ml/tube and analyzed for protein purity using SDS-PAGE with a 12% acrylamide gel. The target bands of SDS-PAGE were quantified using ImageJ version 1.53a [29]. Relative expression levels were calculated as the background-corrected band intensity values. Fractions containing the target protein were pooled and subjected to dialysis against 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 8.0 at 4 °C overnight to remove imidazole and salts. The protein was then concentrated by ultrafiltration, mixed with 50% (v/v) glycerol, and stored at -20 °C for further experiments.

Characterization of optimum temperature and thermal stability of protein

The reaction mixture (1 mL total volume) included 50 mM Tris-HCI buffer pH 8.0, 0.4% Triton X-100, 1 mM substrate (p-nitrophenyl hexanoate; pNP-C6, sigma-aldrich), and enzyme sample (0.70 µg of NaL-WT, 1.07 µg of NaL-1, 4.95 µg of NaL-2, 0.22 µg of NaL-3). To evaluate the optimum temperature of the protein, the reaction mixture was incubated at various temperatures (25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, and 80 °C) for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 300 µl of 100 mM sodium carbonate. The sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 1 min and the supernatant was analyzed by measuring the absorbance at 405 nm to quantify the production of p-nitrophenol. A standard curve was generated using concentrations 0.000, 0.025, 0.050, 0.075, 0.100, 0.125, 0.150, 0.175 and 0.200 µmol/ml of p-nitrophenol. One unit of lipase activity is defined as the amount of enzyme required to hydrolyze the substrate to produce 1 micromole of product per minute. The enzyme activity was expressed as relative activity, with the highest activity observed at each temperature set as 100%.

To evaluate the thermal stability of the protein, (1.34 µg of NaL-WT, 0.52 µg of NaL-1, 7.80 µg of NaL-2, 0.12 µg of NaL-3) incubated at various temperatures (25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, and 80 °C) for 60 min. Following this incubation, the sample was aliquot 50 µL and mixed with the reaction mixture as described above for enzyme activity assay. The reaction was carried out at 50 °C for 10 min. The reaction was terminated, and enzyme activity was measured by absorbance at 405 nm. The residual enzyme activity was calculated and reported as a percentage relative to the activity of the non-incubated enzyme, which was set as 100%.

Characterization of optimal pH and pH stability

To determine the optimal pH for enzyme activity, 50 mM buffers covering a broad pH range were used as follows; sodium citrate (pH 4.0), potassium phosphate (pH 5.0–6.0), Tris-HCl (pH 7.0–9.0), and glycine-NaOH (pH 10). Each buffer was mixed with 0.4% Triton X-100, 1 mM substrate (pNP-C6), and an appropriate concentration of the enzyme sample (0.70 µg of NaL-WT, 1.07 µg of NaL-1, 4.95 µg of NaL-2, 0.22 µg of NaL-3). The total reaction volume was 1 mL. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 50 °C for 10 min. The reaction was then terminated, and the product was measured as previously described. Enzyme activity was calculated, with the highest activity at each pH set as 100% and reported as relative activity.

To assess the pH stability of the enzyme, enzyme sample (2.68 µg of NaL-WT, 8.24 µg of NaL-1, 3.12 µg of NaL-2, 2.42 µg of NaL-3) was mixed with various pH buffer range were used as follows; sodium citrate (pH 4.0–5.0), potassium phosphate (pH 5.0–7.0), Tris-HCl (pH 7.0–9.0), and glycine-NaOH (pH 9.0–13.0). as described above at final concentration 100 mM. The sample mixtures were incubated at 4 °C for 24 h and aliquot 20 µL mixed with 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 8.0, 0.4% Triton X-100, and 1 mM substrate (pNP-C6) for enzyme activity investigation. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 50 °C for 10 min, then stopped reaction and measured releasing product at 405 nm. The enzyme activity was calculated and presented as a percentage of the residual activity relative to the maximum activity of the enzyme, which was set as 100%.

Investigation of substrate specificity

To investigate the substrate specificity of protein, reaction mixtures with a total volume of 1 mL were prepared, containing 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), 0.4% Triton X-100, enzyme sample (0.25 µg of NaL-WT, 1.05 µg of NaL-1, 4.95 µg of NaL-2, 0.22 µg of NaL-3), and 1 mM of various p-nitrophenyl esters as substrates. The substrates used included p-nitrophenyl acetate (C2), p-nitrophenyl butyrate (C4), p-nitrophenyl hexanoate (C6), p-nitrophenyl octanoate (C8), p-nitrophenyl decanoate (C10), p-nitrophenyl dodecanoate (C12), and p-nitrophenyl palmitate (C16). The reaction mixtures were incubated at 50 °C for 10 min. After stopping the reaction, the product was measured at 405 nm, and enzyme activity was calculated. Relative activity was expressed as a percentage of the maximum enzyme activity, which was set as 100%.

Results and discussions

In silico engineering lipases

In this study, we aimed to engineer a lipase from N. acrostichi (NaL) with enhanced stability to improve protein expression and modulate properties. Given the large sequence space, we strategically narrowed down potential targets by combining alanine scanning and evolutionary profiling. Computational alanine scanning predicted 91 residues as critical for NaL stability (Supplementary Table S1). In parallel, we leveraged the natural variation observed in homologous lipase sequences to pinpoint variant positions. Targeting these sites may accelerate the evolutionary process and explore functional diversity while minimizing the likelihood of disrupting the protein’s overall fold or stability. The analysis led to the identification of 60 potential positions with a total of 242 specific amino acid substitutions that were proposed based on the variation profile criteria for further investigation (Supplementary Table S2). The predicted ΔΔG for these proposed mutations was calculated using a consensus approach across four computational tools: MutationExplorer, SDM, DUET, and INPS3D. From this set, 16 mutations consistently predicted as stabilizing (i.e., positive ΔΔG values) by at least three of the four methods were selected (Table 1).

In natural evolution, advantageous traits often arise from the accumulation of multiple mutations over time. Combining predicted single point mutations can mimic this process, leading to additive or synergistic effects and accelerating the discovery of highly optimized variants. To efficiently explore the impact of single point mutations, we strategically grouped them into three distinct multi-site mutants: NaL-1, NaL-2 and NaL-3 (Table 1; Fig. 1). Manual grouping ensured a relatively even distribution of mutation points across the protein sequence. Each of these three multi-site mutants also includes at least one residue position with a natural variant frequency > 50%, suggesting potential important for protein adaptation. NaL-1 incorporated five specific predicted single mutations: G79A, P141A, K249D, G276A, and K290P. These mutations represent dominant variants and strong individual effects. NaL-2 expanded upon NaL-1 by including an additional four predicted single mutations: T40G, T89M, P109T, and A238V. This broader set allowed us to target a more extensive region of the protein. NaL-3 comprised all predicted stabilizing mutations which were further extended by six more predicted single mutations from NaL-2: A44E, V54A, Q92R, D138L, S215D, and S230Y. The structural stability of the designed mutants was confirmed using molecular dynamics simulations (Supplementary Fig. S1). The designed variants were subsequently subjected to experimental tests.

Effect of mutations on protein expression level

Codon-optimized lipases were cloned into the pET28a expression vector and transformed the recombinant plasmid into E. coli BL21 (DE3) for expression. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that the expression of NaL-WT was predominantly in the insoluble pellet fraction (Fig. 2) which was difficult to extract and purify. In contrast, the lipase variants showed improved protein expression levels in the water-soluble fraction (supernatant) (Fig. 2). Using ImageJ analysis revealed that the expression of these NaL variants was increased relative to NaL-WT, with NaL-1, NaL-2, and NaL-3 showing a 1.48-fold, 1.37-fold, and 1.50-fold increase in expression, respectively. It could be implied that mutations promoted proper folding or enhanced the stability of the protein in the soluble fraction [31, 32].

For further biochemical characterization, the recombinant lipases were purified using a nickel Sepharose affinity column. The NaL-3 variant was fully purified, while other variants and WT were partially purified. Nevertheless, the final enzyme preparations achieved at least 90% purity, as determined by SDS-PAGE (Supplementary Fig. S3-S6).

Substrate specificity of recombinant N. acrostichi lipases

To assess whether mutations affected the catalytic properties of the variants, their specific activities were measured on different p-nitrophenyl esters. The variants showed changes in substrate preference profiles compared to NaL-WT (Fig. 3A). NaL-WT exhibited the highest hydrolytic activity on p-nitrophenyl (C2) (Fig. 3A). NaL-1 variant showed high hydrolytic activity on both p-nitrophenyl acetate (C2) and p-nitrophenyl butyrate (C4). With an increasing number of mutations from NaL-1 to NaL-2 and NaL-3, the substrate preference shifted toward p-nitrophenyl hexanoate (C6). Among the variants, NaL-2 showed the broadest substrate specificity. Since the mutations occurred outside substrate-binding region, the changes in substrate preference suggest that these mutations may have induced conformational changes in the protein structure, indirectly affecting the active site and altering substrate interactions.

Temperature and pH effects on lipase activity and stability

The effect of temperature on lipase activity was evaluated within the range of 25–80 °C. The optimum temperature for NaL-WT was 50 °C, while NaL-1 and NaL-2 showed increased optimum temperatures of 65 °C and 60 °C, respectively (Fig. 3B; Table 2). NaL-2 exhibited a thermostability profile similar to the wild-type, maintaining over 70% of its activity between 4 °C and 70 °C (Table 2 and Fig. S7). In contrast, NaL-1 showed slightly lower thermostability (4–60 °C) compared to NaL-WT and NaL-2 (Table 2 and Fig. S7). Given their stability at elevated temperatures, NaL-WT, NaL-1, and NaL-2 can be considered thermostable enzymes [33]. Thermal stability is a highly desirable property in industrial applications such as in detergent industry [34] and biodiesel production [35]. NaL-3, however, was less stable than the wild-type and other variants, with an optimum temperature of 45 °C and retaining at least 70% activity only within the range of 4 °C and 40 °C after 1 h of incubation (Table 2 and Fig. S7).

To determine the optimum pH, the activity of purified lipases was measured within a pH range of 5–9 (Fig. 3C), while pH stability was assessed across a broader range of 4–13 (Fig. S8). NaL-WT exhibited stability between pH 4 and 10, with maximum activity at pH 9 (Fig. 3C; Table 2). The NaL-1 variant had a similar optimum pH of 8 but demonstrated an extended stability range from pH 5 to pH 11. NaL-2 was distinct, showing an optimum pH of 5 and remarkable stability under highly alkaline conditions (pH 11–13), making it might suitable for industrial applications requiring extreme alkalinity, such as cleaning products [36, 37]. For a lipase to be effective in detergent formulations, it must be both alkali-resistant (active at pH 10.0–11.0) and thermostable (maintaining high activity between 30 and 60 °C) [37, 38]. However, a key limitation of many current lipases is their instability in strong alkaline environments and their lack of tolerance to surfactants, which restricts their use as detergent additives. Examples of lipases used in the detergent industry include those from Geobacillus stearothermophilus FMR12 [39], Bacillus smithii BTMS 11 [40]. Given its robust alkaline stability, NaL-2 shows potential for application in detergent formulations. NaL-3 exhibited an optimum pH of 7 and remained stable between pH 7 and pH 11. Its stability in neutral to moderately alkaline conditions suggests potential applications in the food industry and other processes that operate within this pH range [41].

Inferring the effects of mutations

Despite designing mutations outside the substrate binding site, we observed significant changes in substrate preference, which prompted us to investigate how these mutations might influence substrate binding and overall protein stability. Since NaL-WT shares high structural similarity with the well-characterized CalB [10], we identified its binding site by superimposing its structure with that of CalB, revealed key residues: Ile142, Phe153, Glu187, Val188, Leu279, Ala282, and Ala286.

Based on the known mechanism of CalB, we hypothesize that NaL undergoes a similar conformational change upon substrate binding. In CalB, the substrate triggers a conformational change where an alpha-helix (residues 140–147) that forms a narrow channel to the active site unfolds into a loop to enclose the substrate [42, 43]. Given the structural similarity, we hypothesize NaL undergoes a similar process, with its residues 139–146 corresponding to this region.

The P141A mutation, which replaces a rigid proline with a smaller, more flexible alanine, is present in all NaL variants. Despite NaL-1’s continued preference for the pNP-C2 substrate, its notably increased preference for the larger pNP-C4 (Fig. 3A) suggests that P141A was not the sole cause of the shift in substrate preference. The observed change is likely due to epistasis, where the effect of the P141A mutation was modified and amplified by other mutations. While P141A may increase local flexibility to help substrates enter, the combined effect of the other mutations in each variant is what likely creates a larger, more accommodating binding pocket. This ultimately shifts the enzyme’s preference from the smaller pNP-C2 to the larger pNP-C4 and pNP-C6 substrates in the NaL-2 and NaL-3 variants.

In addition to changes in substrate preference, the variants show a shift in their thermal stability. NaL-1, with five mutations (G79A, P141A, K249D, G276A, K290P), showed enhanced thermal stability. Its optimal temperature increased to 65 °C. This suggested that these mutations likely contribute to a more rigid structure, which improves the protein’s resistance to thermal denaturation.

Conversely, NaL-2, with its four additional mutations (T40G, T89M, P109T, A238V) has a mildly destabilizing effect, which indicated by a drop of optimal temperature to 60 °C. This decrease in stability is likely caused by the disruption of key stabilizing interactions, such as hydrogen bonds. For example, the T89M mutation, which replaces a polar amino acid with a nonpolar one, could lead to loss of a hydrogen bond with A85 (Fig. S9). This might weaken the local helix and increase its local flexibility.

The six additional mutations in NaL-3 showed a strong destabilizing effect, lowering its optimal temperature to 45 °C. We hypothesize that the unique mutations, Q92R and D138L, might be the primary contributors. These mutations significantly alter the chemical properties of the amino acids, and their proximity to other mutations could create a synergistic effect. The Q92R mutation replaces polar glutamine with a positively charged arginine. Q92 residue is in a turn region at the end of a helix (Fig. 1 and Fig. S9), a location that is vital for the protein’s overall dynamic structure and proper folding [44]. Furthermore, the proximity of the Q92R mutation to the T89M mutation may have a synergistic negative effect. Similarly, the D138L mutation replaces a small, polar, negatively charged amino acid (D) with a larger, non-polar, hydrophobic one (L). This change in chemical properties could cause steric hindrance with neighboring atoms. Given that D138 is located near the P141A mutation and a region that is hypothesized to control substrate binding via conformational rearrangement, the D138L mutation may have a negative synergistic effect that further contributes to the substantial decrease in NaL-3’s thermal stability.

To fully understand the effects of the mutations, future studies should employ both computational and experimental methods. Molecular dynamics simulations could provide a dynamic view of how the mutations influence protein stability and unfolding pathways. Protein stability calculations could quantify changes in the Gibbs free energy (ΔG). Experimentally, differential scanning calorimetry is ideal for directly measuring the thermal stability of each variant by determining the melting temperature and enthalpy of unfolding (ΔH). To establish the kinetic basis for the observed changes in substrate preference, it would be beneficial to determine the catalytic rate (kcat) and Michaelis constant (KM) for each enzyme variant. Integrating these structural, thermodynamic, and kinetic data would allow for a definitive link between the specific mutations and their effects on both substrate specificity and thermal stability.

Rational design in this study relies on homology, thus its applicability to other enzymes depends on having a sufficient number of related sequences available to perform a meaningful multiple sequence alignment. This allows us to identify and target key residues based on evolutionary context. With the growing availability of sequence data, especially from metagenomics, we can apply this method to a wider variety of enzymes. For enzymes without enough sequence information, alternative design strategies based on the enzyme’s single structure is necessary.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully engineered lipase variants for enhanced stability and assessed their functional properties. Utilizing evolutionary information and predicted protein stability changes from single-point mutations, we designed multi-site variants with increasing numbers of mutations, each built upon a shared core set.

The results showed improved protein expression in all variants, which suggests an increase in protein stability. Notably, even with mutations occurring outside the catalytic binding site, the protein’s substrate preference changed. Furthermore, the designed variants exhibited altered temperature and pH profiles. Given that natural proteins evolve functional characteristics while maintaining their structural stability, it was expected that combining multiple beneficial mutations, especially those predicted solely based on stability, would impact protein properties. The results indeed suggest that evolutionary information serves as a powerful tool in protein engineering, particularly for avoiding the design of unstable proteins. However, further analysis on a detailed understanding of the specific effect of individual mutations is necessary for truly targeted design to overcome distinct bottlenecks. For instance, while one mutation might primarily improve solubility (and thus expression), another could specifically enhance thermostability, and a third might optimize active site geometry for catalysis. Combining mutations with precisely characterized individual effects would enable more precise targeting of multiple desired properties.

The variants obtained from this study will be valuable for biotechnology applications. With remarkable tolerance to high-temperature and alkaline conditions, which are uncommon trait among lipases [45], they might be useful for biorefining, synthesis requiring harsh conditions, high-temperature biotransformation, and similar processes. This study successfully demonstrated the ability to tune enzyme properties through stability-focused engineering. This approach provides valuable insights for future protein design strategies, ultimately expanding the utility of lipases across various biotechnological processes.
