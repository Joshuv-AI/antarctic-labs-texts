Theoretical analysis of low power synergistic sono-optogenetic control of calcium-dependent synaptic plasticity
Authors: Nripesh Dixit, Sukhdev Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracellular calcium () signaling at synapses is fundamental to understanding how the brain processes information, learns and stores memories. However, achieving precise control over calcium dynamics at the level of individual synapses remains a major challenge in neuroscience. Recent advances in calcium-permeable channelrhodopsins (CapChRs) provide a promising optogenetic strategy for directly modulating postsynaptic calcium influx with high spatial and temporal precision. Here, we present a new theoretical model of synergistic sono-optogenetic control of postsynaptic dynamics using CapChR1, CapChR2, C2-LC and PsCatCh2.0 expressed at the postsynaptic spine. We systematically explored multiple stimulation paradigms, including coordinated electrical activation of presynaptic and postsynaptic terminals, optogenetic excitation of CapChR-expressing spines, ultrasound (US) stimulation of pre- and postsynaptic terminals using MscL-I92L and combined synergistic sono-opto stimulation. These approaches reveal multiple tunable pathways for shaping postsynaptic calcium responses, with optical irradiance and US providing an additional degree of control over influx. Our analysis identifies the minimum optical irradiance, optimal inter-stimulus timing and stimulation frequency ranges required to effectively modulate synaptic efficacy. Robust synaptic modulation is achieved at an irradiance of 7 µW/mm² when electrical stimulation of the presynaptic terminal and postsynaptic spine is combined with US and optogenetic activation of CapChR2 at the postsynaptic spine, a significant 61.11% reduction from the previously reported irradiance of 18 µW/mm² in our earlier study Dixit et al. (2025). Similarly, the required irradiance is 8 µW/mm² for CapChR1, 10 µW/mm² for C2-LC, and 34 µW/mm² for PsCatCh2.0. Collectively, these results demonstrate that integrating sonogenetics with synaptic plasticity provides a flexible and energy-efficient strategy for directly controlling -dependent synaptic plasticity, substantially reducing the optical power required for effective synaptic modulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50423-3.Keywords: Optogenetics, Synergistic sono-optogenetics, Synaptic plasticity, Ultrasound, CapChR1, CapChR2, C2-LC, PsCatCh2.0, MscL-I92LSubject terms: Biological techniques, Neuroscience

Introduction

Activity-dependent changes in synaptic strength are widely recognized as the cellular foundation of information processing in the nervous system. Precise spatiotemporal regulation of intracellular signalling molecules is therefore essential for maintaining normal brain function and adaptability1,2. Calcium () ions play a major role in synaptic plasticity, a fundamental process underlying learning and memory in the brain3. At chemical synapses, communication between neurons is initiated by the influx of into the presynaptic terminal following membrane depolarization, which triggers the release of neurotransmitters from synaptic vesicles into the synaptic cleft4. This presynaptic entry tightly couples electrical activity to chemical signaling5. Binding of these neurotransmitters to specific receptors at the postsynaptic spine increases intracellular postsynaptic levels via ligand-gated and voltage-dependent calcium channels2. The magnitude, duration and spatial profile of postsynaptic signals critically determine the direction and extent of synaptic modification6–9. Rise in intracellular acts as a crucial second messenger that initiates a cascade of molecular events, including activation of calcium-dependent enzymes, gene transcription, protein synthesis and remodeling of the actin cytoskeleton3,10. These processes drive synaptic plasticity and lead to long-term changes in synaptic strength, thereby shaping learning, memory formation and adaptive behavior9,10.

Efficient synaptic communication is fundamental to normal brain physiology, as it enables accurate information transfer and coordinated neural network activity11. Even slight synaptic dysfunction can substantially disrupt neuronal signaling and is associated with multiple brain disorders9. Recent studies have highlighted synaptic dysfunction as a central pathological feature underlying many neurological and neuropsychiatric diseases12. In this context, precise spatiotemporal regulation of intracellular calcium () influx is critical for maintaining optimal synaptic transmission and for deciphering the molecular mechanisms that govern activity-dependent modifications of neuronal connections. Such control provides key insights into the cellular basis of learning and memory within the complex architecture of neural circuits13.

Optogenetics has transformed neuroscience and cell biology by enabling non-invasive control and monitoring of genetically modified cells using light, with unparalleled spatial and temporal precision14. Beyond its widespread experimental applications, optogenetics has also demonstrated translational potential, as evidenced by the first successful human clinical trial for vision restoration reported in 202115. However, many early optogenetic studies relied on uniform expression of light-sensitive proteins throughout the entire cell, limiting the ability to probe the functional consequences of highly localized intracellular signalling events16,17. To address this limitation, recent advances have focused on subcellular targeting strategies that enable compartment-specific expression of opsins¹8. When combined with advanced light-delivery techniques, these approaches make it possible to selectively manipulate dendrites and synapses, thereby offering powerful tools to dissect the mechanisms underlying neural computation, learning, memory formation and information processing at the synaptic level.

Recently, -permeable channelrhodopsins (CapChRs) have been developed through targeted mutagenesis16. Among these, CapChR1, CapChR2, C2-LC and PsCatCh2.0 exhibit markedly enhanced permeability, particularly at negative membrane potentials and under physiological extracellular concentrations. Notably, optogenetic activation of CapChRs reliably induces intracellular levels in cultured neurons and enables robust modulation of -dependent signaling pathways. Computational modeling plays a vital role in neuroscience by providing mechanistic insights into ionic transport across neuronal membranes. Previous computational studies of optogenetic control at the cellular, circuit and network levels have substantially advanced our understanding of how light-evoked ionic currents through opsins give rise to neuronal excitation19,20. In parallel, computational models of synaptic plasticity have been developed to quantitatively describe intracellular dynamics underlying long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) under diverse stimulation conditions20,21.

Recently, sonogenetics has emerged as a promising alternative to optogenetics, enabling non-invasive neural stimulation in deep brain regions using ultrasound (US) without the limitations imposed by optical attenuation or scattering. US has a long history of safe use in biomedical applications and unlike light, can penetrate bone and soft tissue with minimal loss, making it particularly suitable for deep-brain neuromodulation22–25. In sonogenetics, genetically expressed mechanosensitive ion channels provide cellular and spatial specificity, allowing selective activation of targeted neural circuits in deeper brain regions24,25. The large-conductance mechanosensitive ion channel family (MscL) consists of pore-forming membrane proteins that transduce mechanical forces applied to the cell membrane into electrophysiological responses. MscL channels exhibit exceptionally high conductance (~ 3 nS), permitting the passage of ions, water and small molecules. Experimental studies have demonstrated that MscL-I92L enables precise excitation of hippocampal neurons at low US pressures (~ 0.25 MPa), well below the ~ 1 MPa levels known to safely penetrate skull and brain tissue with minimal impedance or damage26–32. Although MscL-I92L-mediated sonogenetic stimulation alone enables neuronal excitation at relatively low US pressures relying exclusively on a single modality, it may limit the flexibility and precision required for controlled neuromodulation. The integration of optogenetic and sonogenetic approaches offers complementary advantages that enhance overall control of neuronal activity. Specifically, CapChRs provide high temporal precision and direct regulation of calcium influx, which is a critical determinant of synaptic plasticity, whereas MscL-I92L enables deeper tissue penetration and non-invasive activation via US26–29. The combined use of these modalities facilitates synergistic effects such as achieving effective activation at reduced optical irradiance, thereby minimizing phototoxicity and thermal load. In addition, multimodal stimulation allows finer tuning of neuronal responses and improves robustness across varying physiological conditions, expanding the dynamic range of modulation compared to either approach alone. This hybrid strategy therefore provides a more versatile and potentially safer framework for precise regulation of calcium-dependent synaptic processes. MscL has been previously expressed in mammalian neurons and shown to respond to sub-MPa US, demonstrating its utility as a genetically encoded mechanosensitive actuator27–29. Although spine-restricted localization of MscL has not yet been demonstrated, molecular strategies for subcellular targeting of membrane proteins including PDZ-binding motifs, Homer/PSD scaffolding tags, and synaptic compartment-specific trafficking sequences suggest a plausible route for directing MscL to postsynaptic domains30. The present analysis therefore represents a conservative scenario in which mechanosensitive influx is confined to individual spines31.

A hybrid approach known as sono-optogenetics has recently been introduced, in which nanomaterials are used to convert US into light26. This technique employs mechanoluminescent nanoparticles delivered through the bloodstream, which emit blue light (~ 470 nm) upon focused transcranial ultrasound (FUS) stimulation, enabling millisecond-scale optogenetic activation of ChR232–35. However, this approach is associated with several limitations, including potential immunological responses with prolonged nanoparticle exposure, inefficiencies in US-to-light conversion, limited spatial precision and safety concerns such as tissue heating or unintended off-target effects.

An alternative and potentially more robust strategy lies in the co-expression of multiple functional proteins within the same neuron. In optogenetics, co-expression of excitatory and inhibitory opsins has enabled bidirectional control of neuronal activity using different wavelengths of light36–38, while co-expression of excitatory opsins and fluorescent reporters has facilitated simultaneous all-optical readout and manipulation of neural activity. Extending this concept, co-expression of light-sensitive opsins and mechanosensitive ion channels within the same neuron could enable synergistic excitation using both light and US. Recently, Roy et al. (2025) introduced a theoretical framework for deep, low-power neural excitation using co-expression of a light-sensitive opsin with the mechanosensitive channel MscL-I92L, in which simulations demonstrated that subthreshold US markedly reduces the two-photon irradiance needed to evoke action potentials39. This synergistic sono-optogenetic (synsonoptogenetic) excitation enables millimeter-to-centimeter-scale penetration while preserving spike fidelity, offering a framework for multimodal neuroprosthetic and deep neuromodulation strategies. The approach presented here extends this idea by combining direct optogenetic control of dynamics with US-evoked MscL-I92L, opening opportunities to probe the effects of more naturalistic temporal structure in neuronal signaling40. Accordingly, we have introduced MscL-I92L mechanosensitive ion channel at post synaptic neuron along with NMDA, VDCC and CapChR receptors. The term synsonoptogenetic synaptic plasticity describe the synergistic combination of optogenetics (synaptic plasticity with CapChRs) and sonogenetics within the same neuron, distinguishing it from existing nanoparticle-based sono-optogenetic approaches39,41. By leveraging both modalities simultaneously, synsonoptogenetic synaptic plasticity offers a multimodal, low-power and cell-specific strategy for deep-brain stimulation, providing enhanced precision and flexibility in neural modulation.

The objective of this study is to investigate optogenetic control of -based synaptic plasticity through subcellular expression of channelrhodopsins (CapChR1, CapChR2, C2-LC and PsCatCh2.0) at the postsynaptic spine with US-evoked MscL-I92L mechanosensitive ion channel by developing an integrated computational model that incorporates stochastic dynamics16,17,22. We performed a detailed analysis of how the amplitude and timing of interacting stimulation modalities, including presynaptic activation, postsynaptic activation, optogenetic excitation of CapChRs at the postsynaptic spine, presynaptic activation combined with US-evoked MscL-I92L stimulation, postsynaptic activation combined with US-evoked MscL-I92L stimulation and optogenetic excitation of CapChRs at the postsynaptic spine combined with US-evoked MscL-I92L stimulation, jointly influence synaptic efficacy.

Results

We theoretically investigated optogenetic control of synaptic plasticity using US-evoked MscL-I92L, in which CapChRs (CapChR1, CapChR2, C2-LC and PsCatCh2.0) were externally expressed at the postsynaptic spine to enable light-driven calcium influx across the neuronal membrane (Fig. 1a, b). The dynamics of CapChRs channel opening, calcium influx and the resulting modulation of synaptic efficacy were systematically examined both in isolation and in combination with presynaptic and postsynaptic electrical events, as well as with US-modulated presynaptic and postsynaptic stimulation. The computational model developed for the CapChRs-mediated photocurrent was validated by comparing simulation results with recently reported experimental data by Lahore et al. (2022) and Chen et al. (2022), showing strong agreement (Supplementary Figs. S1, S2 and S3). The variation of expression density with CapChRs-mediated photocurrent is presented in Supplementary Fig. S4. Expression density values for CapChRs were selected based on reported photocurrents in ND7/23 cells, an immortalized dorsal root ganglion-derived neuronal cell line commonly used for electrophysiological characterization of ChRs, with conductance scaled to match steady-state photocurrents at physiological extracellular levels. These densities remain within experimentally achievable ranges and avoid the cytotoxic expression regimes observed for high-conductance opsins, providing a realistic basis for threshold estimation.

To simulate synaptic plasticity, CapChRs photocurrent models were integrated with a theoretical model describing the evolution of synaptic plasticity state variables under US-evoked MscL-I92L modulation (Fig. 1b). These variables include postsynaptic intracellular calcium concentration , leaky calcium integrator () and synaptic efficacy (). The calcium concentration dynamics induced by presynaptic and postsynaptic events occurring at different time lags were further validated by comparison with previously reported results by Graupner et al. (2007), demonstrating good agreement with experimental findings17,23. Although reductive in nature, the proposed model effectively captures the essential dynamics of calcium signalling and synaptic efficacy at a minimal synapse and is well suited to reproduce key experimental observations, highlighting its validity and utility for probing fundamental mechanisms of synaptic plasticity with US-evoked MscL-I92L.

The temporal evolution of these state variables were analyzed in detail during electrical stimulation of the presynaptic terminal ( nA, mV), electrical stimulation of the postsynaptic spine, optogenetic activation of CapChRs-expressing postsynaptic spine, US-evoked MscL-I92L stimulation of the presynaptic terminal, US-evoked MscL-I92L stimulation of the postsynaptic spine, as well as combined optogenetic activation of the CapChR-expressed with postsynaptic spine with US-evoked MscL-I92L stimulation.

Irradiance-response curves (Fig. 2j) revealed that CapChR2 peaked at 2.57 ± 0.06 nA at 0.005 µW/mm² and saturated at 2.54 ± 0.06 nA at 0.01 µW/mm², while CapChR1 peaked at 3.17 ± 0.06 nA at 0.002 µW/mm² and saturated at 3.11 ± 0.05 nA at 0.01 µW/mm2. C2-LC achieved maximal steady current of 3.78 ± 0.06 nA at 0.01 µW/mm², whereas PsCatCh2.0 peaked at 3.44 ± 0.08 nA at 0.01 µW/mm². To comprehensively compare the light sensitivity of CapChRs, a sigmoidal dose-response analysis was performed by fitting normalized photocurrent as a function of irradiance using a Hill function (Fig. 2k). The extracted EC₅₀ values revealed that CapChR2 exhibited the highest light sensitivity (EC₅₀ ≈ 0.0053 µW/mm²), followed by C2-LC (≈ 0.0151 µW/mm²) and CapChR1 (≈ 0.0170 µW/mm²), whereas PsCatCh2.0 required substantially higher irradiance for activation (EC₅₀ ≈ 0.2421 µW/mm²). These results provide a quantitative comparison of opsin efficiency and highlight distinct activation thresholds under optical stimulation. Time-to-peak exhibited a monotonic irradiance-dependent acceleration after an initial plateau phase (Fig. 2l). CapChR2 maintained constant latency up to 5 × 10− 6 µW/mm² (500.03 ± 0.03 ms), then decreased to 5.25 ± 0.06 ms at 5 × 10− 3 µW/mm². CapChR1, C2-LC and PsCatCh2.0 showed similar trends, saturating at 6.65 ± 0.06 ms (2 × 10− 3 µW/mm²), 7.7 ± 0.06 ms (2 × 10− 3 µW/mm²), and 10.95 ± 0.03 ms (5 × 10− 3 µW/mm²), respectively.

The adaptation ratio describes the degree to which a neuron’s response decreases over time during a sustained stimulus. It is defined as the ratio of the steady-state response to the initial response following stimulus onset Fig. 2(m). It decreased with increasing irradiance, consistent with faster desensitization at higher photon flux. The maximum ratios achieved were 0.81 ± 0.06 for CapChR2 and 0.70 ± 0.06 for CapChR1 at 2 × 10− 3 µW/mm², 0.69 ± 0.04 for C2-LC at 10− 4 µW/mm² and 0.53 ± 0.03 for PsCatCh2.0 at 5 × 10− 3 µW/mm². Finally, multi-pulse stimulation at different frequencies (Fig. 2n, o) showed incomplete return to baseline due to insufficient time for photocycle recovery between pulses, thereby generating photocurrent plateaus that increased with stimulation frequency. Opsins with slower turn-off kinetics exhibited larger plateaus. The percentage return to baseline (RTB%) decreased with frequency across irradiances, consistent with limited temporal resolution during high-frequency optogenetic drive44.

Discussion

The present study introduces a novel strategy for direct optogenetic control of intracellular concentration at the postsynaptic spine by combining US-evoked activation of MscL-I92L with expression of CapChR1, CapChR2, C2-LC and PsCatCh2.0. Theoretical analysis shows that simultaneous optogenetic activation of the postsynaptic spine together with US-evoked MscL-I92L produces a substantially greater increase in postsynaptic intracellular than isolated or coincident presynaptic and postsynaptic events or their pairing with US stimulation. Consequently, this multimodal approach enables robust modulation of synaptic efficacy at very low light irradiances. The minimum irradiance thresholds necessary to induce changes in synaptic efficacy, with and without presynaptic, postsynaptic and US-evoked MscL stimulation were determined and optimal time lags between stimulation modalities were identified to maximize efficacy.

Previous studies have reported that light-evoked action potentials generated using ChR2 increase intracellular concentration and thereby enhance neurotransmitter release probability21. This increase arises indirectly from enhanced activation of voltage-gated channels in the stimulated neuron. More recently, it has been shown that type-1 viral ChRs localize exclusively to intracellular compartments and when illuminated, trigger release from IP₃-dependent stores in proportion to irradiance42,43. The direct optogenetic regulation of concentration proposed here using CapChR1, CapChR2, C2-LC and PsCatCh2.0 provides an alternative strategy for modulating synaptic plasticity, complementing previously developed approaches. Synaptic efficacy itself is shaped by numerous interacting mechanisms, including presynaptic release machinery, postsynaptic signalling cascades, gene expression and protein synthesis44−47. Prior works have explored optogenetic stimulation48, sonogenetic stimulation49,50 and nanoparticle-based sono-optogenetics42 independently; however, these techniques influence signaling primarily through indirect mechanisms such as membrane depolarization or US-induced luminescence. In comparison, the present approach directly modulates postsynaptic with subcellular specificity by co-expressing CapChRs and mechanosensitive channels within the same spine. This strategy eliminates nanoparticle carriers, reduces dependence on high-power optical sources and establishes a tunable framework for -dependent plasticity at significantly reduced irradiance thresholds. To our knowledge, no prior study has demonstrated direct, low-power, bidirectionally tunable control of synaptic efficacy through synergistic optical and acoustic stimulation of postsynaptic pathways. Previous experimental work has shown that MscL-I92L enables reliable excitation of neurons at US pressures as low as ~ 0.25 MPa, significantly below pressures (~ 1 MPa) that can safely propagate through skull and brain tissue with minimal risk of thermal or mechanical injury. Consistent with these findings, we employ a US pressure amplitude of 0.44 MPa in our synergistic sono-optogenetic model, which is well within the safe operating window and sufficient to activate the mechanosensitive MscL channel in silico. Experimental and computational studies have demonstrated that the precise timing and temporal sequence of pre- and postsynaptic activity governs the direction of synaptic plasticity, a phenomenon known as spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP)40,51,52. In the present work, coincident stimulation of presynaptic and postsynaptic events together with US further lowers the irradiance required for optogenetic modulation of synaptic efficacy. The irradiance threshold decreases from 35 µW/mm² to 32 µW/mm² when optogenetic activation of the postsynaptic spine is paired with presynaptic electrical stimulation ( = 3 nA, = −70 mV, 470 nm, 1 ms) (Fig. 3g). Incorporating US-evoked MscL-I92L activation in addition to pre-, post- and optogenetic stimulation further lowers the threshold to 10 µW/mm2 (Fig. 3g).

Beyond its theoretical relevance, synsonoptogenetic plasticity offers potential utility for probing the mechanistic basis of memory formation, learning rules in cortical circuits and -dependent metaplasticity. The framework may further extend to neuromodulation applications including sensory restoration, cortical rehabilitation and adaptive neuroprosthetics where deep-tissue stimulation and low-power operation are critical. As US penetrates deeply and transients govern synaptic efficacy, the approach provides a foundation for minimally invasive manipulation of circuit connectivity in vivo.

The emergence of potentiation within the intermediate irradiance windows, 7 and 10 µW/mm² for CapChR2, 8 µW/mm² for CapChR1, 10–15 µW/mm² for C2-LC and 34–45 µW/mm² for PsCatCh2.0 suggests that the system operates near a stochastic threshold where intrinsic noise intermittently enables crossing of the potentiation boundary (). Similar non-monotonic input–output relationships have been reported in synaptic plasticity models in which stochastic calcium dynamics or channel kinetics enable “noise-assisted” potentiation. Beyond this probabilistic window, higher irradiance levels likely shift the system toward regimes dominated by calcium buffering, extrusion or decay pathways, preventing sufficient accumulation of to induce potentiation. These findings indicate that stochasticity can broaden the operational range of optogenetic plasticity and produce probabilistic rather than strictly deterministic outcomes.

Under repetitive stimulation, pulse frequency determines the integrated intracellular concentration, which in turn influences the strength of synaptic plasticity40,53. The simulations demonstrate that repetitive presynaptic firing alone does not induce changes in synaptic efficacy (Fig. 8a). By comparison, optogenetic activation induces a prolonged elevation due to the slow off-kinetics of CapChR2, resulting in no apparent upper limit on the effective stimulation frequency (Fig. 8). The irradiance-dependent variation in photocurrent amplitude observed for PsCatCh2.0 in our simulations is guided by experimental trends reported in the literature where light-evoked currents increase with photon flux. In the present model, the reversal potential was assumed to be 0 mV, consistent with typical values reported for non-selective cation-conducting ChRs under physiological conditions. Near this reversal potential, the net photocurrent is particularly sensitive to the balance between ionic driving forces and light-dependent conductance which may give rise to variations in current direction under different stimulation regimes. Therefore, the observed behavior should be interpreted within the context of the adopted model assumptions and electrophysiological conditions, rather than as a universal characteristic of PsCatCh2.0. (Please see Supplementary Fig. 5).

In optogenetics, the total photocurrent generated by CapChR channels scales with expression density, thereby setting thresholds for irradiance required to evoke functional responses. The recently characterized CapChR2 produces large photocurrents, enabling reliable excitation even at standard expression levels. However, increasing CapChR expression to strengthen responses is constrained by elevated risks of toxicity and immune activation54. To incorporate variability in opsin expression at the presynaptic terminal, a scaling factor () was introduced. In our simulations, substantial increases in intracellular were observed even for small values of (~ 10−6), which is roughly three orders of magnitude lower than the scaling factors required for NMDA receptors and voltage-dependent calcium channels.

Spine neck resistance critically shapes electrical coupling between the spine head and parent dendrite and thus modulates the interaction between pre- and postsynaptic elements55. Pairing of presynaptic and postsynaptic events has been shown experimentally to depend strongly on spine neck resistance56. High resistance isolates the spine head, increasing localized depolarization while limiting its spread to the dendrite whereas low resistance facilitates propagation and enhances overall excitability. Average spine neck resistances of approximately 500 MΩ have been reported to influence both depolarization and spine-dendrite coupling, impacting synaptic strength and signaling57.

Cortical neurons receive thousands of synaptic inputs across highly branched dendritic arbors58. Localized opsin expression would therefore allow fine spatial control over synaptic efficacy in response to different input patterns, although compartment-specific expression remains technically challenging59–62. Multi-photon excitation techniques enable volumetric (3D) opsin activation with longer wavelengths that penetrate deeper into tissue55–57. Such approaches would further improve spatial precision for targeting presynaptic terminals. However, the two-photon response properties of CapChRs remain unexplored. Moreover, CapChRs requires blue light, which experiences strong scattering and absorption in tissue, posing challenges for in vivo application. Current research efforts to overcome these limitations include development of deep light-delivery methods such as two-photon excitation, upconversion nanoparticles to locally convert NIR light to visible wavelengths and red-shifted opsin variants63–65. Integrating these advances may enable optogenetic control of synaptic plasticity using the strategy proposed here.

US stimulation introduces an additional dimension of flexibility for deep-brain modulation, as acoustic waves exhibit minimal scattering through soft tissue and bone. The pressure amplitudes used in this study (0.24–0.54 MPa) remain well below safety thresholds reported for transcranial focused US and are consistent with previously demonstrated activation of MscL-I92L in hippocampal neurons. The corresponding mechanical index (MI) remains under established diagnostic limits, minimizing the risk of thermal or cavitation-induced damage. The pressure amplitudes used in this study fall within the range previously shown to activate mechanosensitive channels in neuronal cultures and brain slices without inducing cavitation-related damage. The corresponding mechanical index MI remains below diagnostic US safety thresholds and the attenuation characteristics of acoustic waves enable penetration through millimeter-scale tissue volumes with minimal scattering. Unlike optical stimulation, which suffers from exponential attenuation and heat dissipation, US can modulate subcortical structures without invasive fiber implantation, suggesting a viable route for deep-tissue neuromodulation. Although the present framework simplifies acoustic coupling to mechanosensitive channels, it is consistent with experimentally reported sonogenetic activation regimes. Importantly, US enables centimeter-scale penetration without invasive optical delivery hardware, overcoming one of the primary limitations of conventional optogenetics. Collectively, these features underscore the translational potential of synsonoptogenetic stimulation for future neuromodulation strategies.

Changes in intracellular dynamics directly influence the direction and magnitude of synaptic plasticity, as acts as the primary second messenger for both long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD). In the present work, synsonoptogenetic control selectively shifts the postsynaptic transients into potentiation-compatible regimes at substantially lower optical power compared to isolated optogenetic stimulation. These dynamics map onto classical plasticity models wherein brief and high-amplitude elevations favor LTP while moderate or prolonged elevations favor LTD in accordance with CaMKII-calcineurin signaling pathways. By enabling the transition of postsynaptic above the potentiation threshold using low irradiance optical drive in combination with US, the proposed framework demonstrates a biologically meaningful shift in the operational plasticity window. This approach therefore not only provides energy-efficient stimulation but also reveals a tunable modality to bias synaptic modification rules.

Overall, this study presents the first approach for direct optogenetic control of concentration with fast opsin kinetics, enabling temporally precise modulation of -dependent plasticity. The irradiance-dependent tuning of amplitude and synaptic efficacy provide an additional degree of control. This framework offers new opportunities for studying synaptic plasticity, memory formation, and -dependent signaling in neurons. Synsonoptogenetic control provides a novel means to directly modulate postsynaptic with high temporal precision and substantially reduced optical power requirements. By integrating mechanosensitive and light-gated pathways, the framework expands the accessible plasticity regime, reveals optimal timing relationships between stimulation modalities, and demonstrates frequency-dependent modulation consistent with biological learning rules. These results establish a theoretical foundation for minimally invasive, energy-efficient neuromodulation strategies and motivate experimental validation in neuronal circuits.

Limitations

The present work is theoretical and therefore abstracts several biological and biophysical features of synaptic physiology. The model does not incorporate dendritic cable filtering, spine neck resistance or non-linear dendritic electrogenesis. These processes influence the spatiotemporal spread of and membrane potential along dendritic branches and may alter the quantitative relationship between local influx and downstream plasticity induction in vivo. The presynaptic vesicle release dynamics, release probability fluctuations and short-term synaptic plasticity mechanisms are not included, and synaptic efficacy is instead represented through a postsynaptic -dependent modification rule. This decouples the model from biophysically realistic presynaptic stochasticity and dynamic resource utilization. US stimulation is represented solely through the activation of MscL-I92L and does not explicitly simulate acoustic propagation, skull attenuation, potential micro-cavitation, thermal diffusion or mechano-electrical coupling in tissue.

Although the present study provides a comprehensive computational framework for investigating synergistic sono-optogenetic modulation of postsynaptic calcium dynamics, it is important to acknowledge that the findings are based on theoretical modeling and simulations. The predicted enhancement in synaptic modulation at reduced light irradiance, achieved through the co-activation of mechanosensitive MscL-I92L and CapChRs has not yet been validated experimentally. Therefore, the quantitative outcomes and specific thresholds reported here should be interpreted as model-based predictions. Future experimental studies, including in vitro and in vivo validations will be essential to confirm the feasibility, efficiency and physiological relevance of the proposed approach. Also, factors such as variability in CapChR expression levels, cellular heterogeneity and biophysical constraints in real neural tissue may further influence the observed responses and should be explored in subsequent investigations.

The localization of MscL-I92L to postsynaptic spines is assumed for functional exploration, although subcellular targeting strategies for mechanosensitive channels remain an open experimental direction. The long-term plasticity is inferred from the amplitude and kinetics of postsynaptic transients rather than modeled through molecular signaling cascades involving CaMKII, calcineurin, Rho-GTPase signaling or actin remodeling that govern structural LTP and LTD. Finally, the simulations do not account for network-scale effects, neuromodulatory state, homeostatic plasticity or metabolic constraints, all of which influence plasticity in vivo. These abstractions enable mechanistic transparency and computational tractability while preserving the primary objective of the work: to characterize how multimodal stimulation strategies shape synaptic signals and lower the irradiance threshold for inducing plasticity. Future work may extend this framework toward anatomically detailed morphologies and experimental validation.

In the present study, the four-state model was adopted as a well-established and widely used approximation to capture the essential light-activated dynamics of ChRs in the absence of detailed experimentally validated photocycle models for the specific CapChR variants considered84. This model was originally developed for Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and may not fully capture the diverse photocycles of other ChR variants particularly engineered CapChRs and those derived from different wild-type proteins. Additionally, mutations introduced to enhance permeability may alter channel kinetics and state transitions in ways that are not explicitly represented in the current framework. Given the structural and functional diversity within the ChR family the direct application of a uniform kinetic scheme may require modification. Future incorporation of variant-specific experimentally validated photocycle models may lead to quantitative differences in predicted photocurrents and the resulting calcium dynamics thereby improving the accuracy of the model.

In the present model the assumption of a fixed reversal potential (= 0 mV) for PsCatCh2.0 which represents a simplification in the absence of experimentally measured, variant-specific electrophysiological data. The actual reversal potential may vary depending on ionic gradients and channel-specific permeability properties which can influence both the magnitude and direction of photocurrents. Furthermore, the predicted photocurrent behaviour particularly near the reversal potential, is sensitive to model parameters such as conductance, ion selectivity and light-dependent transition rates. As ChRs exhibit substantial diversity in their biophysical characteristics these predictions should be interpreted with caution. Future incorporation of experimentally validated parameters may refine the accuracy of the modeled photocurrent and associated calcium dynamics.

Methods

To model the postsynaptic calcium dynamics underlying synaptic plasticity, we incorporated optical calcium influx through CapChRs in addition to endogenous calcium pathways. At the postsynaptic spine, NMDA and VDCC receptors play major roles in maintaining the intracellular concentration23,62. Here, we consider CapChRs (CapChR1, CapChR2, C2-LC and PsCatCh2.0) to be genetically targeted at the postsynaptic spine. On illumination with light, these channels permit influx, thereby shaping the intracellular dynamics at the spine.

The proposed framework suggests several feasible experimental paradigms for validation. Co-expression of CapChRs and MscL-I92L may be achieved in dissociated hippocampal neurons or organotypic slice cultures using viral vectors or transfection, enabling simultaneous optical and US stimulation while imaging with GCaMP66–69. LTP-like potentiation could be detected through peak amplitudes, NMDA kinetics, spine volume changes or electrophysiological readouts46,70,71,73. US delivery through focused transducers at sub-MPa pressures would enable mechanosensitive activation without invasive optical fibers, while optogenetic stimulation can be delivered through epifluorescence or patterned illumination32,48,72. These paradigms provide a realistic route to confirm mode specificity, timing-dependent synergy and irradiance threshold reduction.

Multiple stimulation configurations were examined to capture distinct physiological scenarios governing synaptic plasticity. Presynaptic activation alone represents neurotransmitter-driven entry through NMDA and VDCC channels, whereas postsynaptic depolarization probes the contribution of voltage-dependent pathways31,73. Optical activation of CapChRs models direct postsynaptic influx, while US-evoked MscL-I92L represents a non-invasive mechanosensitive source49,73. Pairing these modalities at controlled time lags enables exploration of Hebbian-like coincidence rules and spike-timing dependent plasticity, while repetitive stimulation reveals rate-dependent effects53,71,74. This systematic combination allows a unified examination of how transients are shaped by both spatial and temporal structure of neural input. Parameter values were obtained directly from published measurements or fitted to match experimentally reported photocurrent and dynamics16,22,50.

CapChRs-mediated current dynamics

In optogenetics, the neuronal membrane potential can be optically modulated with the use of light-sensitive microbial proteins that act as light-gated ion channels75–79. These proteins use embedded retinal chromophores in their structure to sense light79. When exposed to light, the retinal chromophore experiences ultrafast (~ fs) photoisomerization, which starts a photocycle that travels through several intermediate states80–83. A subset of these intermediates contributes to the formation of a membrane-spanning pore through which ions can flow, thereby generating an ionic current.
