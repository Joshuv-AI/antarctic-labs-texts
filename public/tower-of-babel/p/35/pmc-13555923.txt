Prognostic value of the systemic immune-inflammation index in gastrointestinal cancers treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jiamin Lu, Yuqian Feng, Kaibo Guo, Hong Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastrointestinal (GI) cancers, including pancreatic, gastric, esophageal, colorectal, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), have high mortality rates. Despite advances in immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), treatment outcomes remain variable. Identifying reliable biomarkers, such as the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), which combines platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts, could help optimize treatment strategies. Biologically, an elevated SII is thought to indicate a dominance of pro-tumor inflammation and a suppressed adaptive immune response, thereby potentially dampening the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs.MethodsA systematic review was conducted of studies from Cochrane Library, Embase, and PubMed, published until August 15, 2025. This review was conducted in strict adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Eligible studies involved GI cancer patients treated with ICIs, reported baseline or treatment SII data, and provided hazard ratios (HRs) for progression-free survival (PFS) or overall survival (OS). Exclusions included studies on non-GI cancers or incomplete data.Results28 studies with 4752 patients were included. High baseline SII levels were associated with worse OS and PFS in gastric cancer (OS: HR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.84–2.72, I2 = 19.4%; PFS: HR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.35–1.83, I2 = 0%), HCC (OS: HR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.47–2.21, I2 = 2.6%; PFS: HR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.24–2.06, I2 = 53.7%), and esophageal cancer (OS: HR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.53–2.09, I2 = 39.8%; PFS: HR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.12–2.01, I2 = 32.6%). Baseline SII was a stronger predictor of survival than post-treatment or dynamic measurements.ConclusionSII is a promising, cost-effective prognostic biomarker for GI cancer patients receiving ICIs. Elevated baseline SII predicts poorer survival, particularly in gastric cancer, HCC, and esophageal cancer. These findings highlight the potential utility of SII in patient risk stratification and clinical decision-making, thereby supporting its integration into precision oncology strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05014-x.Keywords: Systemic immune-inflammation index, Gastrointestinal cancer, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Prognosis, Meta-analysis, Overall survival.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers, including pancreatic cancer, gastric cancer, esophageal cancer, colorectal cancer, and liver cancer, which is predominantly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounting for 75%-85% of cases, pose a significant global health burden [1, 2]. These cancers represent a significant portion of global cancer morbidity and mortality, with over 5 million new diagnoses and nearly 3.5 million deaths each year [3]. Despite advances in detection [4, 5] and treatment strategies [6, 7], the outlook for patients with advanced gastrointestinal cancers, particularly those with metastatic disease, remains bleak [8, 9]. Conventional treatments like surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation often do not provide lasting remission in these patients, underscoring the critical need for novel therapeutic strategies, highlighting the urgent need for innovative treatment approaches.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, including for GI cancers [10, 11], by targeting immune regulators like programmed cell death‑1 (PD-1), programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4). This enhances the immune system’s capacity to recognize and attack tumor cells, leading to improved survival and outcomes in patients with advanced or metastatic disease [12, 13]. However, not all patients respond equally to ICIs, and there remains considerable variability in treatment outcomes [14]. This variability underscores the need for reliable biomarkers to predict treatment efficacy and to stratify patients for optimal therapeutic approaches.

The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) is an integrated blood-based biomarker that utilizes platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts to capture the complex biological interplay within the tumor microenvironment, reflecting the critical balance between protumorigenic inflammation and antitumor immunity [15]. Elevated neutrophils drive immunosuppression by secreting T-cell inhibitors such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) [16] and arginase-1 [17], while forming neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) that assist metastasis [18]. Simultaneously, circulating platelets support tumor survival by forming physical shields against natural killer (NK) cell lysis and releasing growth factors like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) to promote angiogenesis [19]. Conversely, lymphocytes act as the primary effectors of antitumor immunity, with high circulating counts generally reflecting a robust host immune status capable of effective tumor recognition and elimination [20]. Therefore, a high SII indicates an exhausted immune landscape that undermines the therapeutic mechanism of ICIs and is associated with inferior clinical outcomes. Several studies have investigated its role as a prognostic marker in GI cancers treated with ICIs, but the findings remain inconsistent [21–24]. Some research suggests a high SII correlates with poor survival outcomes in this patient population [21, 22], whereas other studies report no significant association [23, 24]. The variability in findings, combined with the small sample sizes and differing methodologies across studies, introduces uncertainty regarding the true effectiveness of SII in this context.

Beyond inflammation alone, nutritional and immune status have been explored as factors that may be associated with prognosis in patients treated with ICIs [25]. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), calculated from serum albumin concentration and peripheral lymphocyte count, reflects both nutritional reserve and host immune competence [26]. On this basis, some studies have proposed a composite SII-PNI score. Although only a limited number of studies have reported outcomes for the SII-PNI score in gastrointestinal cancer patients receiving ICIs, early evidence suggests that this composite index may be associated with survival outcomes and provide complementary prognostic information to SII alone [27–29].

This review aimed to evaluate whether elevated baseline SII is associated with poorer survival outcomes in GI cancer patients receiving ICI therapy. Secondarily, we evaluated the prognostic value of the SII-PNI composite score in the subset of studies reporting this indicator.

Methods

Study protocol

A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following PRISMA guidelines to explore the association between SII levels at different time points (e.g., baseline and during treatment) and survival outcomes (progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS)) in GI cancer patients receiving ICIs. This study is registered with PROSPERO (registration number CRD420251137062).

Review of the literature

The electronic databases, including the Cochrane Library, Embase, and PubMed, were searched for relevant literature published up to August 15, 2025. For example, the PubMed search strategy employed terms related to GI cancers and the SII, such as: (“pancreatic cancer” OR “hepatocellular carcinoma”) AND (“immunotherapy” OR “pembrolizumab”) AND (“systemic immune-inflammation index”). The full search methodology for all databases is provided in File S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were considered for inclusion if they met the following PICOS criteria: Population: Patients with gastrointestinal cancers (such as liver, pancreatic, stomach, esophageal, or colorectal cancers) treated with ICIs, irrespective of tumor stage or concurrent treatments. Intervention/Exposure: High levels of the SII, measured at baseline, during treatment, or based on changes in SII levels, with thresholds consistent with those used in the original studies. Comparison: Low levels of SII. Outcomes: Studies should report or provide sufficient data to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PFS and/or OS. PFS is defined as the time from treatment initiation to cancer recurrence, progression, death, or last follow-up, while OS is the time from treatment initiation to death or last follow-up. Study Design: Cohort studies, either prospective or retrospective.

The following conditions or factors were used to disqualify studies from inclusion: non-English language articles or conference abstracts; studies with incomplete data or missing outcome measures; preclinical studies; case reports; review articles; studies involving non-gastrointestinal cancers or non-immune checkpoint inhibitor treatments; studies that did not report SII measurements or survival outcomes; and studies with overlapping patient populations, with preference given to the study with the largest sample size.

Study selection process

All records were imported into EndNote software to remove duplicates. Two independent reviewers (KBG and JML) first evaluated the titles and abstracts for relevance, then reviewed the full texts of studies that appeared potentially eligible. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (YQF) when necessary.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The information collected included details about the study characteristics (first author, publication year, country, and study design), patient demographics and baseline features (age, sex, cancer type, and tumor stage), intervention and comparator details (SII cut-off thresholds and their corresponding determination methods, measurement time points, and type of immune checkpoint inhibitor), and survival outcomes (median PFS and OS, HRs with 95% CIs, and associations between SII levels and survival). Studies reporting relapse-free survival (RFS) or disease-free survival (DFS) were also included to provide a more comprehensive estimation of PFS. Follow-up duration was extracted to determine the length of survival monitoring.

For each study, baseline SII was defined as the systemic immune-inflammation index measured closest to and prior to the initiation of the ICI regimen evaluated in that study. When multiple pre-treatment SII measurements were reported, we preferentially extracted the value that the original investigators described as “baseline” or “pre-treatment” for that ICI course.

The certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system. Evidence quality was assessed across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Based on these factors, the overall certainty of evidence was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low. Two reviewers (KBG and JML) independently performed the grading process. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were first addressed through discussion to reach a consensus. If a consensus could not be reached, the disagreement was resolved by a third reviewer (HP), who acted as an adjudicator to ensure methodological rigor.

Each study’s methodological quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates selection, comparability, and outcome assessment, with scores ranging from 1 to 9, where higher scores reflect better quality [30].

Risk of bias assessment

To assess publication bias, both funnel plot analysis and Egger’s regression test were used. Sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out tests, were conducted to assess the stability of the findings by excluding individual studies and verifying the consistency of the results.

Statistical analysis

Between-study variability was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values > 50% or P < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity. HRs for PFS and OS (high vs. low SII) were pooled using fixed-effects or random-effects models based on heterogeneity. The fixed-effects model (inverse variance) was used for I2 ≤ 50%, and the random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird) for I2 > 50%. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.2) with the survival, survminer, and metafor packages.

The meta-analysis aimed to assess the link between SII levels at baseline and during treatment and survival outcomes (PFS and OS) in patients with five types of GI cancers receiving ICI therapy. Secondary analyses assessed the combined prognostic effect of the baseline SII-PNI on survival outcomes. To test robustness and explore heterogeneity, we additionally performed subgroup analyses stratified by the SII cut‑off method [Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) vs. X‑tile vs. median values].

Results

Study selection

A comprehensive literature search initially identified 87 articles relevant to the research question. After applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 28 studies were deemed eligible based on methodological rigor and relevance to the topic. Overall, 28 studies [21–24, 27–29, 31–51] involving 4772 patients diagnosed with gastrointestinal cancers were included, all of whom received ICI therapy. The majority of these studies were retrospective cohort designs (n = 22) [21–24, 28, 29, 31–36, 38–43, 45, 48–50], while a smaller proportion were prospective cohort studies (n = 6) [27, 37, 44, 46, 47, 51]. A predominance of male patients was observed, particularly in HCC studies [21–24, 28, 29, 31–33]. Many studies focused on late-stage cancers, particularly stages III-IV [29, 35–51], and utilized different tumor staging systems, such as the BCLC (Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer Staging System) [21–24, 28, 31–33] for HCC.

The cutoff values used to differentiate high and low SII levels were determined through either the application of X-Tile software or alternative statistical approaches; detailed information is available in Table S2. Data on follow-up duration and treatment timelines across the five GI cancer cohorts are summarized in Table S3. The NOS scores for each study are reported in Table S4. Hepatocellular carcinoma

This meta-analysis, encompassing seven studies [21–24, 31–33] with a total of 1342 patients. Pooled analysis indicated that high SII levels were significantly associated with worse survival outcomes for both OS (HR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.47–2.21, I2 = 2.6%) and PFS (HR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.24–2.06, I2 = 53.7%). In subgroup analyses stratified by sampling time, baseline high SII remained a robust predictor of poor prognosis for OS (HR = 1.75) and PFS (HR = 1.45). Conversely, SII levels measured early in treatment showed no significant correlation with either OS (HR = 2.75, 95% CI: 0.81–9.30, I2 = 69.3%) or PFS (HR = 3.00, 95% CI: 0.65–13.85, I2 = 84.6%) (Fig. 2).

Forest plot of the impact of SII expression on overall survival and progression-free survival at different timepoints in hepatocellular carcinoma patients. A Overall Survival; B Progression Free Survival

The pooled analysis of 2 studies [28, 29] including 402 patients showed a significant association between a high SII-PNI score and poorer OS (HR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.73–2.53; I2 = 47.3%) (Figure S2A). For PFS, the pooled analysis showed no significant association between SII-PNI score and outcome (HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 0.54–2.81; I2 = 51.8%) (Figure S2B).

Pancreatic cancer

Data regarding pancreatic cancer were limited to a single study [34] including 122 patients; therefore, the following results should be interpreted as exploratory. Univariate analysis showed that high baseline SII levels were significantly associated with reduced OS (HR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.41–3.69) and PFS (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.02–2.37) (Table 2). After adjustment for confounding factors, high baseline SII remained independently associated with poor OS (HR = 3.28, 95% CI: 1.28–5.27), whereas the association with PFS was no longer statistically significant.

Gastric cancer

A total of 9 studies [35–43] comprising 1201 patients were included in the pooled analysis for OS. High SII levels were significantly associated with poorer OS (HR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.84–2.72, I2 = 19.4%). Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent prognostic value for baseline (HR = 2.30) and early on-treatment (HR = 2.78, single study) SII; however, an increase in SII after two treatment cycles showed no significant association with OS (HR = 1.14, 95% CI: 0.56–2.33, single study) (Fig. 3A).

Forest plot of the impact of SII expression on overall survival and progression-free survival at different timepoints in gastric cancer patients. A Overall Survival; B Progression Free Survival

Eight studies [27, 35, 37–40, 42, 43] including 892 patients were analyzed for PFS. Elevated SII levels were significantly correlated with worse PFS (HR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.35–1.83, I2 = 0%). This negative association persisted in subgroups based on baseline (HR = 1.54) and early on-treatment (HR = 2.05, single study) SII. In contrast, an increase in SII after two cycles was not significantly related to PFS (HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 0.71–2.41; single study) (Fig. 3B).

Esophageal cancer

The pooled analysis of 5 studies [44–46, 48, 50] involving 1477 patients showed that high SII levels at baseline were significantly associated with worse OS (HR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.53–2.09, I2 = 39.8%) (Fig. 4A). The meta-analysis of 4 studies [44, 46, 47, 49] including 439 patients showed that high SII levels were significantly associated with inferior PFS (HR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.12–2.01, I2 = 32.6%). In the subgroup analysis of PFS, baseline high SII levels were not significantly associated with prognosis (HR = 1.43, 95% CI: 0.83–2.48; I2 = 50.3%). High SII levels measured 3 weeks post-treatment were not significantly associated with PFS (HR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.50–2.09; single study, no heterogeneity). High SII levels measured 6 weeks post-treatment were also not significantly associated with PFS (HR = 1.76, 95% CI: 0.85–3.64; single study, no heterogeneity). (Fig. 4B)

Forest plot of the impact of SII expression on overall survival and progression-free survival at different timepoints in Esophageal Cancer patients. A Overall Survival; B Progression Free Survival

Colorectal cancer

In a limited exploratory analysis of 28 patients with colorectal cancer (one study) [51], baseline high SII levels were associated with inferior PFS (HR = 3.11, 95% CI: 1.1–8.81). After controlling for confounding factors, the association was no longer statistically significant (HR = 2.54, 95% CI: 0.3-20.29) (Table 2). These data are preliminary and hypothesis-generating.

Impact of cut-off determination methods on heterogeneity

Subgroup analyses stratified by cut-off determination methods (ROC curve, X-tile, and median) revealed variations in both prognostic significance and heterogeneity across cancer types (Figures S3-5). In HCC, the subgroup utilizing X-tile analysis demonstrated a significant association with OS (I2 = 0%); conversely, the ROC-based subgroup failed to reach statistical significance and exhibited substantial heterogeneity (HR = 2.24, 95% CI: 0.89–5.69, I2 = 70%). In gastric cancer, both X-tile and ROC methodologies yielded significant pooled HRs for OS and PFS. For esophageal cancer PFS, neither the ROC-based nor the median-based subgroup demonstrated a significant prognostic value, although the latter achieved complete statistical homogeneity (I2 = 0%).

Quality estimation

Methodological quality assessed by the NOS was high (scores 7–9). According to GRADE, evidence for HCC, gastric, and esophageal cancer was rated as high due to large effect sizes and plausible confounding. Conversely, evidence for pancreatic and colorectal cancer was downgraded to very low certainty, as each outcome was limited to a single study, resulting in serious imprecision and potential publication bias.

Identification of potential outliers and assessment of publication bias

The leave-one-out analysis was conducted to identify potential outliers within the studies. The results indicated the presence of potential outliers specifically in the HCC cohort studies with PFS as the endpoint, as depicted in Figure S7A. While, no outliers were detected in the other meta-analyses (Figures S6A and S8A-S11A).

We used funnel plots and the Egger test, with results summarized in Figures S6B-S11B. These figures indicate no evidence of publication bias in most meta-analyses. An exception was noted in the HCC cohort with PFS as the endpoint, where funnel plot asymmetry and a significant Egger test (P = 0.03) indicated potential publication bias. (Figure S7B).

Due to the identified outliers and significant publication bias, a sensitivity analysis was deemed necessary to ensure robust findings. As part of this process, the study by Huang et al. [22] was excluded in an exclusion sensitivity analysis. The results showed that the heterogeneity, as measured by the I2 statistic, substantially decreased from 51.3% to 25.6%. Additionally, the publication bias suggested by Egger’s test was no longer significant (p = 0.26, vs. original p = 0.03). This intervention improved the reliability and validity of the pooled estimate for the HCC cohort with PFS as the endpoint. Further details of the analysis can be found in File S2.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, to our knowledge the first to assess the prognostic significance of SII in GI cancers treated with ICIs, indicates that a higher SII is typically linked to poorer survival outcomes. Among cancer types with sufficient data for quantitative synthesis, such as HCC, gastric cancer, and esophageal cancer, high SII was consistently associated with worse OS and PFS. The pooled HRs indicated potentially clinically relevant effects: for instance, in gastric cancer, high SII was associated with more than a twofold increase in mortality risk (HR = 2.24), while in HCC and esophageal cancer the associations were also notable (HR = 1.80 and 1.79, respectively). Subgroup analyses suggested that baseline SII may carry greater prognostic utility than on-treatment or post-treatment measurements, and the prognostic value of SII was more robust for OS than for PFS, supported by the lower heterogeneity and greater model stability observed in the OS models (Figs. 2 and 4 A). Although heterogeneity was evident in certain analyses, particularly HCC with PFS as the endpoint, the overall pattern of findings was relatively consistent across tumor subtypes where multiple studies were available. Taken together, these results support the potential role of SII as an accessible biomarker for prognostic stratification in GI cancer patients undergoing ICI therapy.

The biological plausibility of SII as a prognostic marker in immuno-oncology is supported by established immunologic mechanisms [52]. SII, calculated from peripheral neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts, reflects the balance between protumorigenic inflammation and antitumor immunity [53]. Neutrophilia has been implicated in tumor progression through secretion of pro-angiogenic factors [54], recruitment of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and cytokine release such as IL-8 that may impair T-cell function [55]. Thrombocytosis may contribute to metastasis by shielding circulating tumor cells and releasing growth factors that promote tumor growth [56]. Conversely, lymphopenia reflects inadequate cytotoxic T-cell activity, a critical determinant of effective ICI response [57]. Thus, a high SII appears to capture a systemic milieu characterized by immunosuppression and tumor-promoting inflammation, broadly consistent with the “cold” tumor immune phenotype that may resist ICI-mediated reinvigoration [58].

Our findings align with prior meta-analyses of SII in non-immunotherapy contexts, where elevated SII has been associated with poor prognosis in resectable and advanced GI cancers treated with chemotherapy or targeted agents [59], as well as in other malignancies such as lung [60] and breast [61] cancers. The current work differs by focusing specifically on immunotherapy, a setting in which systemic inflammation may be particularly detrimental given its potential to counteract the mechanism of ICIs. Other systemic inflammatory biomarkers, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [62] and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio [63], are also well-established prognostic factors in this setting. Similarly, our findings highlight the prognostic utility of SII. By integrating neutrophils, platelets, and lymphocytes, SII likewise captures the complex host immune-inflammatory status, serving as a prognostic metric in HCC, gastric cancer, and esophageal cancer.

Subgroup analyses provided further insight into the prognostic relevance of SII. First, baseline measurements generally outperformed early on-treatment or dynamic SII changes in predicting outcomes. This finding suggests that pre-treatment systemic inflammatory status may exert a durable influence on ICI efficacy, although once therapy has commenced, tumor-immune interactions are likely shaped by multiple factors beyond systemic inflammation alone [64, 65].

Second, the prognostic value of SII appeared more pronounced for OS than for PFS. The OS associations were stronger, less heterogeneous, and more stable across sensitivity analyses. A plausible explanation is that PFS in immunotherapy contexts is affected by atypical response patterns, including pseudoprogression and delayed responses, which may weaken correlations with systemic inflammatory indices [66, 67]. By contrast, OS, as a more definitive endpoint, may better capture the enduring impact of baseline immune status [68].

Third, among the cancer types included in the meta-analysis (HCC, Gastric cancer, Esophageal cancer), gastric cancer demonstrated the strongest association, with high SII linked to more than a twofold increased risk of death (HR = 2.24). Several biological considerations could account for this. Gastric cancer is characterized by pronounced tumor–immune interactions, and systemic inflammation is a well-recognized driver of aggressive disease [69]. Moreover, while many gastric tumors exhibit high PD-L1 expression and microsatellite instability (features generally associated with ICI sensitivity), such benefits may be attenuated in the presence of systemic immunosuppression captured by high SII. In contrast, HCC and esophageal cancer, although also considered immunogenic, may exhibit more heterogeneity in etiologic drivers (viral hepatitis in HCC [70], smoking and alcohol in esophageal cancer [71]) that could influence systemic inflammation independently of tumor-immune interactions.

Fourth, our subgroup analyses indicate that cut-off determination strategies significantly influence statistical outcomes, though this impact varies by tumor type. In gastric cancer, high SII consistently predicted poorer survival regardless of whether X-tile or ROC analysis was used, suggesting the prognostic signal of systemic inflammation in this setting is robust enough to withstand methodological variations. Conversely, in HCC, while X-tile-derived cut-offs demonstrated a significant association with OS and PFS alongside minimal heterogeneity, ROC-based cut-offs failed to reach significance and exhibited substantial between-study heterogeneity. This divergence highlights that “optimal” ROC thresholds derived from small or heterogeneous cohorts may be unstable and prone to overfitting, limiting their external validity. Results for esophageal cancer further illustrate these complexities. Regarding PFS, neither ROC- nor median-based cut-offs yielded significant associations, though the median-based subgroup showed statistical homogeneity. This reflects the limitation of distribution-based methods: while the median is objective and reproducible, it lacks biological rationale and may dilute true clinical signals. Notably, the median-based PFS analysis relied on a single study, limiting its inferential weight. However, for esophageal OS, ROC-derived cut-offs successfully identified high SII as a prognostic marker, confirming that results remain highly sensitive to both threshold definitions and the specific endpoints employed.

Beyond the standard SII, a small body of evidence has examined a composite SII-PNI score that integrates inflammatory burden and nutritional status. In two studies (402 patients with HCC) [28, 29], a high score was associated with worse OS (HR = 2.09), though no significant association was observed for PFS. Given the limited data and imprecise estimates, these findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and highlight the need for validation in larger cohorts.

Overall, the SII is a cost-effective and accessible biomarker with diverse clinical utility. Primarily, SII can serve as a pre-treatment prognostic marker to guide patient counseling before ICI initiation. Additionally, it could function as a stratification factor in clinical trials to balance systemic inflammatory burden between study arms. Furthermore, patients with high SII may represent a specific subgroup suitable for therapeutic intensification, such as combining ICIs with agents targeting neutrophil recruitment (such as CXCR1/2 inhibitors), platelet function, or IL-8 signaling. Finally, although dynamic monitoring of SII showed limited predictive value in our current analysis, it remains a conceptually sound approach for tracking evolving tumor-immune dynamics and warrants further prospective validation.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Most of the included studies were retrospective cohort studies conducted in China, which introduces the possibility of selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors and may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations and healthcare settings. Heterogeneity across studies warrants cautious interpretation. Variation in SII cut-off values was a major contributor, which were derived from different determination methods (ROC curve, X-tile, and median), with thresholds ranging from 303.66 to 1154.67 (Table S2). This lack of standardization complicates direct comparisons and highlights the need for consensus definitions. While publication bias appeared limited, an exception was noted in the HCC-PFS subgroup, where Egger’s test suggested small-study effects. Furthermore, the variation in treatment lines presents a limitation, particularly in cohorts involving second-line settings or ICI rechallenge. In these cases, prior systemic therapies, specifically previous exposure to ICIs, may have already modulated the baseline SII. Due to the lack of individual patient-level data, we could not isolate ICI-naïve patients, potentially introducing residual confounding. Similarly, significant heterogeneity existed regarding therapeutic regimens, which ranged from ICI monotherapy to combinations with chemotherapy or anti-angiogenic agents. Since most primary studies reported aggregated data for these mixed cohorts, we were unable to conduct a subgroup analysis to discern whether the prognostic value of SII varies by treatment modality.

A further important limitation is the scarcity of data for pancreatic and colorectal cancers, restricted to single studies each, which precluded meta-analysis. Although high SII suggested poor prognosis in pancreatic cancer but not in colorectal cancer, these observations rely on minimal data. Consequently, these findings must be interpreted with caution and considered strictly hypothesis-generating, underscoring the critical need for more primary research focused on these malignancies.

The strengths of this review include its comprehensive scope and methodological rigor. We conducted the analysis in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, synthesizing data from several thousand patients across multiple GI cancer subtypes. Most included studies were of high methodological quality by Newcastle-Ottawa criteria, and the GRADE framework supported high certainty for the major cancer cohorts (HCC, Gastric cancer, Esophageal cancer). Importantly, the associations between high SII and worse outcomes were generally consistent across cancer types with sufficient evidence, which strengthens confidence in the overall conclusions.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis highlights the potential of SII as an affordable, accessible, and promising prognostic biomarker for GI cancer patients undergoing ICI therapy. Our findings demonstrate that a high baseline SII is associated with poorer OS and PFS in patients with gastric cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and esophageal cancer. However, for pancreatic cancer and colorectal cancer, the available evidence remains limited, with only one study assessing SII in these cancer types. Consequently, further research is needed to establish the prognostic role of SII in these cancers. These results underscore the importance of systemic inflammation in predicting immunotherapy outcomes and suggest that SII could complement existing biomarkers in clinical decision-making. Future research should aim to prospectively validate SII in large, multicenter ICI trials using standardized cut-off values. Mechanistic studies are also needed to clarify the biological pathways linking systemic inflammation, SII, and ICI resistance, including the contributions of neutrophil- and platelet-derived mediators. The potential role of SII as a dynamic biomarker during treatment, though less evident in our current analysis, remains worth exploring prospectively. Finally, integration of SII with established biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression, Tumor mutational burden, and immune gene signatures could enable the development of composite predictive models, ultimately supporting more personalized immunotherapy strategies for GI malignancies.
