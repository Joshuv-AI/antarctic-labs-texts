Toward Narrow‐Band, Stable Blue MR‐TADF Emission via Combined Heterocyclic Analogues Marching B.T.2020
Authors: Meghana Tirupati, Jung Ho Ham, Subramanian Muruganantham, Se Chann Cha, Young Hun Jung, Jang Hyuk Kwon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of efficient and stable ultra‐narrowband pure‐blue multi‐resonance thermally activated delayed fluorescence (MR‐TADF) emitters is critical for next‐generation wide‐gamut OLED displays. Herein, we present a molecular design strategy that enhances emitter stability and efficiency by reinforcing the weak C─N bonds through selective incorporation of heterocyclic carbazole (Cz) units into the MR framework. Two proof‐of‐concept emitters, m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, were synthesized via high‐yield, lithium‐free borylation. These emitters exhibit pure‐blue emissions at 453 and 463 nm with narrow full‐width at half‐maximums (FWHMs) of 24 and 21 nm in solution and high photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs) of 88% and 95%, respectively. OLED devices based on m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA show emissions at 456 nm (FWHM 24 nm, EQEmax 23%, CIEy 0.06) and 467 nm (FWHM 24 nm, EQEmax 26%, CIEy 0.10), respectively, holding among the highest efficiencies for blue OLEDs without sensitizers. Notably, the tBu‐Cz‐DABNA‐based device shows an LT90 of ∼81 h at 1000 cd m− 2 (EQE 8.95%), representing one of the longest operational lifetimes for blue fluorescent OLEDs with CIEy ≤ 0.10. These results demonstrate a versatile and scalable molecular design strategy for the realization of high‐efficiency, long‐lifetime blue OLEDs approaching the BT.2020 color standard.

Introduction

Organic light‐emitting diodes (OLEDs)[ 1 ] have emerged as a next‐generation display technology owing to their advantages of low power consumption, excellent color purity, broad viewing angles, rapid retort times, and affordability. In OLEDs, emitters deeply impact the operating lifetime, efficiency, and color purity of the devices.[ 2 , 3 , 4 ] Despite significant progress, the lack of highly efficient and stable emitters continues to limit the performance of OLED devices. To overcome these limitations, the development of advanced organic emitters is crucial. Fluorescent green and red emitters have been successfully replaced by the phosphorescent and thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) emitters in full‐color commercial OLED displays. In contrast, since blue emitters exhibit more emission energy than red and green emitters, they endure greater electrical stress during operation, resulting in deterioration to the emitters and significantly reduced lifetime.[ 5 , 6 ] Which makes the development of stable and efficient blue emitters remain a significant challenge. To address these issues, various strategies have been explored, focusing on molecular design and device architecture to enhance the efficiency and lifetime of blue OLEDs for commercial applications.[ 2 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] Despite significant efforts to date, conventional fluorescent blue emitters based on the triplet–triplet annihilation (TTA) approach are still being used for commercial purposes.[ 10 , 11 , 12 ] By harnessing the TTA mechanism, low internal quantum efficiency (IQE) fluorescent emitters can generate additional singlet excitons, enhancing theoretical IQE from 25% up to 62.5%, combined with anthracene‐based host materials. As a result, TTA‐based OLEDs have demonstrated excellent operational stability and good electroluminescent properties.[ 7 ]

However, conventional fluorescent emitters typically suffer from broad full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHMs), large Stokes shifts, and poor color purity. Commercial displays often adopt microcavities or color filters to enhance color purity, sacrificing efficiency. Recently, Hatakeyama et al. developed a promising approach of multi‐resonance thermally activated delayed fluorescence (MR‐TADF) emitters by embedding electron deficient‐boron (B) and electron‐rich nitrogen (N) atoms into a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) framework, which has emerged as a great solution for the aforementioned issue rather than the conventional TADF emitters.[ 13 ] The MR effect significantly suppresses structural relaxation and vibronic coupling between the excited and ground states[ 14 ] As a result, MR emitters exhibit narrowband emission (FWHM ≤30 nm), minimal Stokes shifts, high photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs), large oscillator strengths (f), and excellent color purity. These features make MR‐TADF materials particularly attractive for high‐efficiency, wide‐gamut OLED displays. The blue MR‐TADF emitter DABNA‐1, pioneered in the renowned DABNA series, based on an N–B–N framework, exhibits an emission peak at 459 nm, a narrow FWHM of 28 nm, and Commission Internationale del’Éclairage CIE (x, y) coordinates of (0.13, 0.09). However, OLEDs using DABNA‐1 show a limited external quantum efficiency (EQE) of just 13.5%.

Further, efficiency was enhanced by substituting diphenylamine in DABNA‐1 with 3,6‐di‐tert‐butylcarbazole in the extended π‐conjugated framework tCzBN (DtBuCzB or BBCz‐SB) by 20%.[ 15 , 16 ] Substantial redshifted emissions were observed with CIEy values above 0.40. Subsequent efforts have focused on altering molecular structures for optimized performances. Among these, the most notable emitter is υ‐DABNA, which achieved an exceptionally narrow FWHM of 14 nm, albeit with a less desirable CIEy of 0.11.[ 17 ] Notably, most blue MR‐TADF emitters with FWHM <20 nm are structural analogues of υ‐DABNA.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ] The pursuit to further narrow the emission spectra has been slowed down due to the limited diversity in molecular skeletons, difficulty in synthesis, high molecular weight, and the increased delocalization generally causing redshifted emission, making it difficult to achieve high color purity CIEy <0.1. Despite significant efforts to improve device efficiency, the long‐term operational stability and color purity of MR‐blue OLEDs, suitable for commercial display applications with B.T.2020, remain unmet. In this context, the stability of the OLED could be mainly related to (i) the dissociation and degradation of the fragile molecular structure, (ii) TTA and TPA caused by the non‐utilized triplets.[ 22 , 23 , 24 ] These degradation pathways not only compromise device longevity but also hinder the preservation of spectral integrity during operation. Nonetheless, the rigid polycyclic aromatic architecture of MR emitters would provide sufficient thermal and chemical stability. MR emitters possess a bit wider ΔE ST than conventional TADF emitters, and the RISC process is less robust. Consequently, persisting triplet excitons may aggregate, leading to TTA and TPA within the device, subsequently lowering OLED lifetime and efficiency.[ 25 ]

To overcome these challenges, several strategies have been explored. These include stabilizing the triplet state (T₁) by incorporating PAH units,[ 26 , 27 ] reducing ΔE ST through π‐extension of the MR core,[ 21 , 28 ] and enhancing RISC through heavy atom introduction.[ 29 , 30 ] Kim et al. reported TBE02 by decorating the standard MR unit with carbazole moieties and addressed the stability issue by fabricated OLED using PtON‐TBBI, a platinum‐based phosphorescent sensitizer (PS). By employing PSF, TBE02 achieved LT95 of 72.9 h at 1000 cd m−2 with EQE of 25.8% due to suppressed TTA and TPA.[ 31 ] Subsequently, Wang et al., strategically inserting a mesitylboron locking unit into a carbazole‐embedded MR emitter, reported DBCz‐Mes. Mitigating hydrogen repulsions and enhancing the para‐positioned weak carbon–nitrogen (C─N) bond in anionic states extended the operating lifespan of DBCz‐Mes to 178 h at 660 cd m−2.[ 32 ] However, due to the introduction of the mesitylboron locking unit, the emission of DBCz‐Mes falls under deep‐blue region, and the synthesis complexity makes it difficult for scalability toward the practical applications. Later, Yang et al. reported 3QCzBN for high efficiency through improving horizontal dipole orientation by the introduction of planar triazine moieties. Consequently, OLED devices based on 3QCzBN exhibit an EQEmax of 25.2% with CIE (x, y) (0.12, 0.23) deviating from B.T.2020. The potential for further development of device lifetimes has been significantly restricted by the shortage of a deep understanding of the structure–stability relationship, and this needs to be addressed.[ 33 ] The pursuit of a highly efficient, stable blue emitter consisting of narrow FWHM approaching B.T.2020 standards with efficient synthesis and scalability for commercial use is still in search.

To meet the growing demands for high‐performance displays, we present a simple yet effective molecular design strategy for realizing a stable, high‐efficiency blue MR‐TADF emitter. A mono‐boron‐based MR emitter was systematically engineered by reinforcing the fragile carbon–nitrogen (C─N) bond through the strategic incorporation of a heteroaromatic analogue into the MR core. Electron‐rich carbazole (Cz) units were introduced to simultaneously enhance TADF character via resonance effects, while the rigid, fused aromatic structure of carbazole increases the C─N bond dissociation energy relative to conventional sp3‐hybridized donors. This structural reinforcement markedly improves the thermal stability of the emitter. To precisely control the emission properties, the carbazole unit was selectively attached at the 4‐position, enabling fine‐tuning of the emission wavelength through conjugation extension, while simultaneously suppressing the formation of undesirable by‐products and isomeric impurities during synthesis (Figure 1). This approach led to the development of m‐Cz‐DABNA, a DABNA‐1 derivative, which showed deep‐blue emission at 453 nm with a narrow FWHM of 24 nm and a high PLQY of 88%. Further, based on the previous studies states that the highly rigid and planarity of the molecule can effectively improve the stability of the triaryl boranes by stabilizing the vacant p‐orbital of boron atom through delocalization of π‐electrons.[ 34 , 35 , 36 ] To further enhance stability and electronic properties, t‐butyl phenyl unit was introduced into the para‐position of the boron atom, and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA was developed. As a result, the extended π‐conjugation enhances the internal charge transfer (ICT) character of tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, evidenced by a significant bathochromic shift in the emission wavelength of λ em 463 nm, with an exceptionally narrow FWHM of 21 nm and a PLQY of 95%. This behavior is attributed to the increased molecular rigidity and reduced structural relaxation, which was confirmed by vibrational mode analysis and low reorganization energies. The TADF OLED employing m‐Cz‐DABNA emitters exhibited blue emission with λ EL 456 nm with a narrow FWHM of 25 nm with CIE (x, y) (0.14, 0.06) approaching B.T.2020, simultaneously, the tBu‐Cz‐DABNA OLED shows emission of 467 nm with an FWHM of 24 nm with CIE (x, y) (0.12, 0.10). These devices achieved a high EQEmax of 23% and 26% for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively. Notably, the fluorescence OLED employing tBu‐Cz‐DABNA demonstrated a long operational lifetime of LT90 ∼81 h at 1000 cd m−2 with an EQE of 8.95% and excellent roll‐off (EQE₁₀₀₀ 8.83%). These findings highlight the importance of this molecular design strategy of tBu‐Cz‐DABNA as a highly efficient and stable blue MR emitter toward the next‐gen OLEDs through stabilizing the weak C─N bond dissociation.

Results and Discussion

The target emitter materials were synthesized in a three‐step process; the arylamine precursor compound 1 was synthesized via Buchwald‐Hartwig coupling (yield ∼80%),[ 37 ] and then compound 2 was obtained by Suzuki–Miyaura coupling.[ 38 ] Further, aminations reactions are carried out to afford arylamine precursors 3 and 4 (yield 70%–75%). Finally, lithium‐free one‐shot borylation step was performed under inert atmosphere using stoichiometric boron tribromide (BBr3) and N,N‐diisopropylethylamine, affording the target emitters in high isolated yields of 50% for m‐Cz‐DABNA and 45% for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA. The overall synthetic route to the final emitters, m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, is illustrated in Scheme 1. Further, the synthesized desired materials, purified, were achieved via sequential column chromatography, recrystallization, and gradient vacuum sublimation. The structures of all intermediate and final compounds were confirmed by 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). Detailed information for the synthesis procedure and characterization was given in Schemes S1–S4. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements revealed the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels of m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA as 5.53 and 5.58 eV, respectively. Based on the HOMO levels and bandgap, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels were calculated to be 2.81 and 2.93 eV, respectively (Figure S5). Further, the thermal stability of the desired materials was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Both compounds exhibited excellent thermal stability, with decomposition temperatures (T d, 5 % weight loss) of 439 °C for m‐Cz‐DABNA and 454 °C for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA. The glass transition temperatures (T g), determined from DSC, were 220 and 227 °C for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively (Figure S3). These high thermal thresholds are advantageous for vacuum deposition in OLED device fabrication.

The structure and optoelectronic characteristics of the new emitters were investigated by density functional theory (DFT) calculations for optimizing the ground state and time‐dependent DFT (TD‐DFT) calculations for the excited states at B3LYP‐D3/6‐311G**(d,p) level, utilizing the Schrodinger 2023–2 program. The optimal ground‐state configuration of m‐Cz‐DABNA exhibits a dihedral angle of ∼ 9.8° within the [4]helicene MR core accompanied by nearly orthogonal torsional angles between the peripheral phenyl substituents and the central core, reflecting a twisted molecular configuration. The MR framework of tBu‐Cz‐DABNA adopts a similar backbone structure; however, the incorporation of a tert‐butyl phenyl group at the para‐position of the boron center leads to a pronounced torsional angle of ∼121.7° between the MR core and the appended phenyl ring. Additionally, tBu‐Cz‐DABNA exhibits an increased dihedral angle of ∼21.7° within the [4]helicene core, promoting a more tilted and sterically rigid conformation that contributes to enhanced molecular stability. These emitters have typical MR characteristics, mostly with FMOs predominantly localized on the BN‐core. The HOMOs of both emitters mostly reside on electron‐rich nitrogen atoms, extending to ortho‐ and para‐adjacent carbon atoms. In contrast, the LUMOs are confined to the electron‐deficient boron atom and its neighboring carbons. Notably, in tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, the LUMO extends to the peripheral phenyl unit, hence enhancing its ICT characteristics by the extended π‐conjugation. Consequently, the enhancement of the acceptor strength resulted in a slightly deeper LUMO energy level (Figure 3a). Subsequent calculations reveal that the more extensive conjugated structure of tBu‐Cz‐DABNA results in the reduction of the HOMO–LUMO gap (3.11 eV) compared to m‐Cz‐DABNA (3.20 eV), indicating redshifted emission. Natural transition orbital (NTO) analysis revealed that the S1 states of both emitters exhibit partially intramolecular short‐range charge transfer (SRCT) excitations (Figure S1). The highest occupied natural transition orbital (HONTO) localized on the ortho‐ and para‐positions of nitrogen atoms. While the lowest unoccupied natural transition orbital (LUNTO) is spread on the meta‐position of N atoms and the ortho‐ and para‐positions of B atoms with electron‐withdrawing properties, consistent with the previously reported MR frameworks.[ 39 , 40 ] This leads to the relatively small SOC matrix between S1 and T1. The spin‐orbit coupling matrix element (SOCME) of these materials revealed that the computed value of 〈S1 |ĤSOC |T1〉 for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA were manifested 0.41 and 0.18 cm−1, respectively, indicating that the hole and particle possess almost the same electronic structures at S1 and low triplet excited state (T1).[ 41 ] Notably, the 〈S1 |ĤSOC |T2〉 values are 1.30 and 1.12 cm−1 for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively, higher than 〈S1|ĤSOC |T1〉 values for both materials. This enhancement could be ascribed to the highly twisted B, N, helicene segment in, which induces significant out‐of‐plane transitions and enhances SOC through alterations in angular momentum.[ 42 ] These findings indicate that both the S1 and T1 states exhibit SRCT character, leading to suppressed SOC between S1 and T1, while facilitating efficient RISC via a T2 → S1 pathway. The detailed SOCME data are summarized in Table S1. This behavior is conducive to enhancing oscillator strength (f) and efficient radiative transition for these molecules. In addition, both emitters show similar singlet energy and high f of 0.544 and 0.514 for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively, indicating high radiative decay and similar emission.

Further, molecular vibrational normal modes and Huang‐Rhys factors (HRF) (S) were calculated by “Band shape” tool at the B3LYP/LACVP** at the accuracy level. As shown in Figure 2b,c , tBu‐Cz‐DABNA showed the overall smaller HR factor of 44.25 compared to 58.77 for m‐Cz‐DABNA, attributed to the highly rigid structure and extended π‐conjugation through the carbazole units. The tBu‐Cz‐DABNA exhibited enhanced vibrational frequency in the high‐frequency region of >800 cm−1. This enhancement arises from the steric distortion introduced by the bulky tert‐butyl group, which perturbs molecular symmetry and promotes localized vibrational displacement, leading to the typical C─C stretching and C─H bending vibrations. In contrast, the low‐frequency modes around ∼100 cm−1, prominent in m‐Cz‐DABNA, are significantly suppressed in tBu‐Cz‐DABNA due to increased rigidity that limits large‐scale torsional motions. In particular, a notable suppression of vibrational modes is observed in low‐frequency region (<500 cm−1, dominated by the in‐plane/out‐of‐plane rocking of peripheral tert‐butyl groups and out‐of‐plane rocking of π‐skeleton). Despite the presence of stronger high‐frequency coupling, the overall HR factor of tBu‐Cz‐DABNA remains lower, reflecting reduced vibronic coupling and a more rigid excited‐state geometry beneficial for narrowband emission. The structural distortion was compared by superimposing the S0 and S1 state geometrical structures and given in Figure 2d. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) and reorganization energy (Λ) values of m‐Cz‐DABNA were found to be 0.2661 Å and 6746 cm−1, respectively, whereas the values for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA were 0.1348 Å and 2706 cm−1. The reduced RMSD and Λ values for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA indicate the enhanced molecular rigidity of the B/N‐MR core that can facilitate reduction of the stretching vibrations. That the structural relaxation is further inhibited in the tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, potentially narrowing FWHM and improving its optoelectronic properties. To further assess the impact of molecular design on the intrinsic stability of the emitters, we theoretically calculated the bond dissociation energies (BDEs) of m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA. Both emitters exhibited relatively high BDE values, underscoring their robust molecular frameworks (Figure S4). Notably, tBu‐Cz‐DABNA displayed superior higher cationic BDEs (5.12 eV versus 4.87 eV), indicating better oxidative stability compared to m‐Cz‐DABNA, attributed to the incorporation of the bulky aromatic tert‐butylphenyl moiety.[ 43 ] This substituent contributes to enhanced molecular rigidity and stability through strengthened C─C linkages, thereby further improving thermal durability.

The photophysical properties of m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA were investigated in a dilute toluene solution (1 x 10−5 M). As shown in Figure 3a,b the absorption bands under 350 nm were attributed to a combination of π–π* and n–π* transitions of the molecules. The characteristic absorption peaks of intramolecular charge‐transfer (ICT) states for these compounds are located at 438 and 446 nm for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively. By applying the tangent method to the onset of the long‐wavelength absorption, the optical bandgaps (E g) of m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA were determined to be 2.72 and 2.65 eV, respectively. The room‐temperature photoluminescence (RTPL) spectra were recorded in dilute toluene solution at 300 K. As shown in Figure 3a,b, both emitters exhibit pure blue emission at 454 and 463 nm, with narrow FWHM values of 24 and 21 nm for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively. The narrowing FWHM and bathochromic shift of the emission spectra were in good alignment with predicted theoretical calculations. Furthermore, both emitters exhibit a small Stokes shift of 15 nm (m‐Cz‐DABNA) and 18 nm (tBu‐Cz‐DABNA) due to the fully fused rigid skeleton. The values suggest only a slight geometrical change between the S1 and S0 states, as well as a negligible geometrical reorganization in the excited states. The singlet (S1) and triplet (T1) energy values were derived from the onset of RTPL measured at 300 K and low‐temperature photoluminescence (LTPL) spectra recorded at 77 K following a 30 ms delay from excitation. The S1/T1 energies are calculated to be 2.85/2.67 eV for m‐Cz‐DABNA and 2.79/2.60 eV for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA. The calculated ΔE ST values are 0.18 eV for m‐Cz‐DABNA and 0.19 eV for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA. Furthermore, both materials exhibited favorable CT characteristics, as evidenced by a positive solvatochromic shift of 17 and 10 nm for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA from non‐polar solvent n‐hexane to polar solvent methylene chloride (Figure S6). Further to investigate the solid‐state properties, the doped films of the emitters were prepared by incorporating them into an mCBP‐CN host matrix. The emission characteristics and thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) capabilities are investigated and summarized in Table S3. In 5 wt% of m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA were doped into mCBP‐CN host, and the recorded PLQY's are 88% and 95%, respectively, indicating their potential to achieve high EQE in blue OLEDs. The introduction of the tert‐butylphenyl group effectively reduced vibrational coupling and structural relaxation in tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, resulting in the high PLQY. The corresponding prompt/delayed lifetime (τp/τd) was calculated to be 7.9 ns/10 µs for m‐Cz‐DABNA and 7.5 ns/42 µs for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively, in 5 wt% doped films. The relatively low delayed fluorescence lifetimes arise from the molecular design strategy of integrating carbazole donors with a rigid MR core, consequently minimizing structural relaxation and non‐radiative decay, which aids in mitigating efficiency roll‐off in high current regions. Based on the TRPL spectra and PLQYs in 5 wt% doped films, key rate constants were calculated and summarized in Table S3 and Figure 3c,d. The m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA showed large radiative decay rates (kr ) of 1.1 × 108 s−1 and 1.25 × 108 s−1, respectively, due to short prompt decay time. The RISC rate constant (k RISC) of these emitters was calculated according to the reported method as 0.6 × 104 s−1 and 0.1 × 104 s−1 for m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively, based on the PLQY and decay time, demonstrating efficient MR‐TADF characteristics.

Device Performance

To assess the inherent characteristics of the developed emitters, we constructed a fluorescent device incorporating emitters m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA utilizing the following architecture: ITO/HATCN (7 nm)/PCBBiF (68 nm)/DPFAn: 5 wt% m‐t DABNA or m‐Cz‐DABNA or tBu‐Cz‐DABNA (25 nm)/BPPB (25 nm)/Liq (3 nm)/Al (100 nm). Indium tin oxide (ITO) and aluminum (Al) were used as an anode and cathode, respectively. Dipyrazino[2,3‐f:2′,3′‐h] quinoxaline 2,3,6,7,10,11‐hexacarbonitrile (HATCN) and 8‐hydroxyquinolinolato‐lithium (Liq) were used as a hole and electron injection layer, respectively. BPPB as electron transporting layers. In the EML, DPFAn and 5 wt% of m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA are the final emitters. Stable anthracene derivative, DPFAn, functioning as the host, and the results are correlated with the most widely used stable m‐t‐DABNA final emitter. The device structure was given in Figure S7, and the device results of the m‐t‐DABNA were given in Figure S8 and summarized in Table S4. As shown in Figure 4a, the 5 wt% doped devices demonstrate blue emission with a narrow bandwidth of 456 nm/26 nm FWHM for m‐Cz‐DABNA and 465 nm/24 nm FWHM for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively. The devices show a low turn‐on voltage of 2.6 V (Von at 1 cd m−2), indicating efficient carrier injection and transport. The tBu‐Cz‐DABNA device showcases a remarkable high efficiency EQEmax/EQE1000 of 8.95%/8.83% with a low roll‐off and CIE (x, y) of (0.12, 0.10), whereas the m‐Cz‐DABNA devices display an efficiency of EQEmax/EQE1000 of 6.94%/6.72% with a CIE (x, y) of (0.13, 0.07) well aligned with B.T.2020 color coordinates (Figure 4b). The tBu‐Cz‐DABNA‐based device demonstrated an outstanding LT90 at 1000 cd m−2 of 81 h, almost two times longer than lifetime of the m‐t‐DABNA‐doped device (44 h). These results are well supported by the reduced molecular packing density of the tert‐butyl phenyl along with C─C bond strengths and well‐balanced charge transport, which collectively contributes to its significant enhancement of operational stability.[ 24 ] By adopting a degradation acceleration factor that relates luminance to lifetime (LT90 (100 cd m−2) = LT90 (1000 cd m−2) × (1000 cd m−2/100 cd m−2) n with n = 1.75), the extrapolated blue OLED lifetime of over ∼ 4554 h at an initial luminance of 100 cd m−2 is obtained for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA OLED. Simultaneously, due to the stabilized triplet energy (2.60 eV) and high PLQY through the structural changes, tBu‐Cz‐DABNA records a higher efficiency than m‐t‐DABNA. The rigidity and planar arrangement of tBu‐Cz‐DABNA likely enhances these results by stabilizing the unoccupied p‐orbital of the boron atom via the highly delocalized π‐electrons, hence reducing structural deformation during excitation, which is consistent with our molecular design objective.[ 34 , 35 , 36 ] tBu‐Cz‐DABNA OLED remains as one of the longest lifetimes for pure blue OLED's with CIEy ≤ 0.10 results of the TTA devices are summarized in Table 2.

Benefiting from the superior photophysical properties and high stability of the deep‐blue emitter in conventional fluorescence device, the electroluminescent performance of m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA OLED was evaluated by fabricating TADF OLED utilizing the following architecture of ITO (50 nm)/HATCN (7 nm)/TAPC (50 nm)/DCDPA (10 nm)/mCBP‐CN: 5 wt% m‐Cz‐DABNA or tBu‐Cz‐DABNA (25 nm)/DBFPO (10 nm)/TPBi (20 nm)/Liq (1.5 nm)/Al (100 nm). 4,4′Cyclo‐hexylidenebis [N,N‐bis(4‐methylphenyl) benzamine] (TAPC) and 1,3,5‐tris (1‐phenyl‐1H benzo[d] imidazole‐2‐yl) benzene (TPBi) are used as hole and electron transporting layers, respectively. 3,5‐Di (9H‐carbazol‐9‐yl)‐N,N‐diphenylamine (DCDPA) was utilized as electron blocking layer. For the EML, mCBP‐CN is used as a host matrix with an optimized 5 wt% due to its electron transport type (ET) character with deep LUMO well‐aligned HOMO/LUMO energy levels with adjacent functional layers. 2,8‐bis (diphenyl phosphine oxide) dibenzofuran (DBFPO) was used as a hole blocking layer due to its high triplet energy level. As illustrated in Figure 5, The OLED based on the m‐Cz‐DABNA exhibits deep blue EL emissions peaking at 456 nm with narrow FWHM of 25 nm, which was consistent with the PL emission. Similarly, the device utilizing tBu‐Cz‐DABNA results in a pure‐blue emission of 467 nm with narrow FWHM of 24 nm. Furthermore, as shown in Table 3, these devices displayed impressive EL performances, including low turn‐on voltage (Von) of 2.8 V/2.7 V, high EQEmax of 23.0 with CIE coordinates reaching (0.14, 0.06) approaching B.T.2020 for m‐Cz‐DABNA, and EQEmax 26.0% and CIE (x, y) (0.12, 0.10) for tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, respectively. The high efficiency of the tBu‐Cz‐DABNA is attributed to the high PLQY of 95% and enhanced planarity due to the tert‐butylphenyl unit compared to the m‐Cz‐DABNA. Motivated by the superior EL performance of the tBu‐Cz‐DABNA, we fabricated phosphorescence‐sensitized fluorescence (PSF) OLED by optimized EML[ 5 ] layer. PtON‐TBBI [ 31 ] was chosen as a blue phosphorescence sensitizer with a doping concentration of 12 wt%, and 1 wt% of tBu‐Cz‐DABNA as the final emitter. The device exhibits pure‐blue emissions of 463 nm with narrow FWHM of 27 nm. Despite its planar molecular structure, tBu‐Cz‐DABNA does not show severe redshift or broadening of the emission spectrum since tert‐butylphenyl groups function efficiently as blocking agents, inhibiting aggregation. The tBu‐Cz‐DABNA device shows an enhanced device efficiency with a maximum efficiency of 28.5% and improved device roll‐off of 23.5% at 1000 cd m−2 with CIE (x, y) (0.13, 0.13). The tBu‐Cz‐DABNA‐incorporated PSF system showed an estimated lifetime of 337 h at initial luminance of 100 cd m−2 (Figure S11, Table S5). The PtON‐TBBI‐controlled device and PSF device architecture, along with results, are given in Figures S10 and S11. The improved EQE and efficiency roll‐off of the PSF devices could be attributed to the effective triplet energy transfer from the PtON‐TBBI sensitizer to the MR‐TADF emitter (Figure S9). Thus, it is clearly validated that the developed emitters exhibited superior device performance in both TADF and PSF OLEDs. We believed that by further improving PSF device architectures, the electroluminescent performances of those emitters will be further enhanced.

Conclusion

In summary, a robust strategy for developing highly efficient and stable blue MR‐TADF emitters has been demonstrated by stabilizing the fragile C─N bond through incorporation of a heterocyclic analogue into the multi‐resonance (MR) core. The proof‐of‐concept blue emitters m‐Cz‐DABNA and tBu‐Cz‐DABNA were designed by integrating a carbazole unit into the MR framework, m‐Cz‐DABNA demonstrated a deep blue emission of 454 nm with a narrow FWHM of 24 nm, while tBu‐Cz‐DABNA showed a pure blue emission at 463 nm with much narrower FWHM of 21 nm. Leveraging the advantageous TADF characteristics, including improved PLQY and enhanced planarity, the tBu‐Cz‐DABNA OLED achieves a maximum EQE of 26% and a CIEy of 0.10, while the m‐Cz‐DABNA OLED attains EQEmax of 23% and a CIEy of 0.06, approaching B.T.2020 standards. Notably, utilizing a phosphorescent sensitizer significantly improved the efficiency roll‐off, enabling the tBu‐Cz‐DABNA PSF‐OLED achieve an EQEmax of 28.5% and maintain 23.5% at a high brightness of 1000 cd m− 2. Furthermore, fluorescence OLED employed tBu‐Cz‐DABNA‐demonstrated an impressive efficiency of 8.9% and an extended lifetime of LT90 (time to 90% initial luminance) of 81 h at 1000 cd m− 2 as one of the longest reported lifetimes for blue OLEDs with CIEy ≤ 0.10. These results highlight the effectiveness of this molecular design strategy of enhancing the stability through incorporation of heteroanalogues unit in advancing high‐efficiency, long‐lifetime blue OLEDs with color coordinates aligned with BT.2020 requirements, offering a promising path forward for next‐generation display technologies.
