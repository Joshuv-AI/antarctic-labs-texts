NSUN2 regulates PARP9 progression through m5C modification to mediate breast cancer progression
Authors: Xin Li, Changjiao Yan, Jun Yi, Jun Yun, Fengqiang Cui, Xiaolong Xu, Yike Li, Xin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundm5C modification plays a vital role in the progression of human cancers, including breast cancer (BC), but the function of NOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase 2 (NSUN2), an RNA m5C modification enzyme, remains largely unclear.MethodsqRT-PCR, western blot and IHC assays were carried out to determine the expression of NSUN2, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP9) and Y box binding protein 1 (YBX1). EdU, flow cytometry, transwell, tube formation and sphere formation assays were conducted to evaluate cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, angiogenesis and stemness, respectively. ELISA was performed to examine the concentrations of inflammatory factors. RIP and pull-down assays were used to verify the interaction between NSUN2/YBX1 and PARP9. Murine xenograft model was constructed to explore the functions of NSUN2 and PARP9 in tumor growth in vivo.ResultsPARP9 silencing suppressed the proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, stemness and immune escape, and promoted apoptosis in BC cells in vitro. Mechanically, NSUN2 functioned as an m5C writer that catalyzed m5C modification of PARP9 mRNA, while YBX1 acted as an m5C reader that recognized the modified transcript and enhanced its stability, thereby elevating PARP9 expression in BC cells. YBX1 could elevate PARP9 expression in BC cells. Moreover, NSUN2 knockdown restrained the malignant behaviors of BC cells, with PARP9 overexpression restored the effects. In addition, NSUN2 knockdown blocked tumorigenesis in vivo by regulating PARP9 expression.ConclusionNSUN2-mediated m5C modification stabilizes PARP9 mRNA and is associated with enhanced BC progression and immune escape. The results broadened our understanding of the pathogenic mechanism of BC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13008-026-00192-2.Keywords: Breast cancer, NSUN2, YBX1, PARP9

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common type of cancer among women worldwide and is also the leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1, 2]. Currently, treatment for BC typically involves a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and/or immunotherapy [3–5]. BC patients (especially those in advanced stages) usually have a poor prognosis due to its high rates of recurrence and metastasis, and the lack of effective targeted treatment options [6]. Therefore, it is crucial to further explore the mechanisms underlying BC progression and identify new effective therapeutic targets [7].

m5C is a ubiquitous RNA modification that participates in RNA metabolic regulation and the occurrence of various tumors [8, 9]. m5C methyltransferases regulate substrate levels by catalyzing m5C modification of target RNAs, mediating the cross-linking of a series of oncogenic or anti-tumor factors, thereby influencing tumor occurrence and cancer progression [10, 11]. As a well-studied RNA m5C modification enzyme, NOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase 2 (NSUN2) affects RNA metabolism, including the maturation, translation and degradation of mRNA, and thereby regulates various physiological and pathological processes [12, 13]. Zou et al. claimed that NSUN2 aggravated colorectal cancer malignancy by enhancing SKIL mRNA stabilization via m5C- Y box binding protein 1 (YBX1)-dependent way [14]. Yang et al. declared that NSUN2 stabilized FABP5 mRNA via m5C modification and then promoted osteosarcoma development [15]. The oncogenic role of NSUN2 in breast cancer via m5C modification was reported [16]. Moreover, Zhang et al. demonstrated that NSUN2, together with the m5C reader protein YBX1, enhanced the stability of HGH1 mRNA through m5C modification, thereby promoting BC progression [17]. This study established a clear paradigm of NSUN2/YBX1-mediated m5C regulation in BC. Even so, the precise impact and underlying mechanism of NSUN2-mediated m5C modification on BC are largely unclear. However, the precise impact and underlying mechanism of NSUN2-medated m5C modification on BC are largely unclear.

Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP9) is a member of PARP family, which play significant roles in the course of cellular life, including DNA repair, cell adhesion, lipid metabolism, cell proliferation or death [18]. Multiple studies have revealed that PARP9 is upregulated in tumors and plays an important role in tumor development, such as in gastrin cancer [19], glioma [20] and breast cancer [21], etc. However, the combined role of PARP9 and NSUN2 in BC has not been reported yet.

In the present study, we focused on the effects of NSUN2 and PARP9 on the progression and immune escape in BC. Moreover, the relation between NSUN2 and PARP9 in BC was investigated.

Results

YBX1 overexpression promoted PARP9 expression in BC cells

To identify potential downstream targets of YBX1 in BC, we analyzed the GEO dataset GSE95299 comparing YBX1-overexpressing MCF-7 cells with vector controls. This analysis revealed that overexpression of YBX1 elevated PARP9 expression in MCF-7 cells (Fig. 1A). Given that PARP9 has been implicated in cancer progression, we next examined PARP9 expression in BC using TCGA and GEPIA databases. Both analyses showed that PARP9 was significantly upregulated in BC tissues compared with normal breast tissues (Fig. 1B and C). We then validated these findings in our collected BC cohort (N = 35), confirming that PARP9 mRNA and protein levels were elevated in BC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1D and E). It was also verified that PARP9 protein level was elevated in BT-549, MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells in comparison with MCF-10 A cells (Fig. 1F). Moreover, OE-YBX1 was transfected into MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells to elevate YBX1 expression, which was verified by western blot (Fig. 1G). YBX1 overexpression led to the elevation of PARP9 mRNA and protein levels in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 1H and I). These findings indicated that YBX1 overexpression promoted PARP9 expression in BC.

PARP9 knockdown inhibited the proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, stemness and immune escape in BC cells

To explore whether PARP9 participated in BC progression, we constructed PARP9-knockdown BC cells by transfecting si-PARP9#1 or si-PARP9#2. As exhibited in Fig. 2A, si-PARP9#1 or si-PARP9#2 transfection reduced PARP9 protein level in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells compared to si-NC group. Moreover, si-PARP9#1 or si-PARP9#2 transfection decreased PARP9 mRNA expression in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells compared to si-NC group (Figure S1A). EdU assay showed that PARP9 knockdown repressed the proliferation of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells relative to control group (Fig. 2B). The results of flow cytometry analysis indicated that PARP9 silencing promoted the apoptosis of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 2C and D). As indicated by transwell assay, PARP9 deficiency markedly restrained the invasion of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 2E). Tube formation assay suggested that PARP9 silencing repressed the angiogenesis of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 2F). The sphere-forming ability of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells was suppressed by PARP9 knockdown (Fig. 2G and H). Subsequently, the effect of PARP9 knockdown in tumor cell immune escape was investigated. We found that the levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ in the co-culture medium of PBMCs and BC cells were increased and CD8 + T cell apoptosis was restrained after PARP9 knockdown, indicating tumor cell immune escape was reduced by PARP9 knockdown (Fig. 2I-K). The effects of PARP9 knockdown on BC cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion and immune escape were restored after OE-PARP9 transfection (Figure S1B-F). All these findings suggested that PARP9 knockdown inhibited the malignant behaviors and immune escape of BC cells.

NSUN2 knockdown reduced m5C enrichment on PARP9 mRNA and decreased PARP9 mRNA stability

As analyzed by RMDisease V2.0 database (http://www.rnamd.org/rmdisease2/table.html? species=human&modification=m5c), PARP9 contained m5C modification sites (Fig. 3A). TCGA database revealed an increase in NSUN2 expression in breast cancer (Fig. 3B). NSUN2 mRNA level was higher in BC tissues (N = 35) than normal tissues (N = 35) (Fig. 3C). NSUN2 mRNA level was positively correlated with PARP9 mRNA level in BC tissues (Fig. 3D). Subsequently, the relation between NSUN2 and PARP9 in BC was further investigated. si-NSUN2 was successfully transfected into MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells to reduce NSUN2 expression, which was verified by western blot (Fig. 3E). After NSUN2 knockdown, we found m5C enrichment was decreased in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3F). PARP9 mRNA and protein levels in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells were repressed by NSUN2 silencing (Fig. 3G and H). RIP assay showed that NSUN2 knockdown could inhibit the m5C modification of PARP9 in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3I, J). As presented in Fig. 3K, RNA pull-down assay further verified the interaction between NSUN2 and PARP9 in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3K). After Act D treatment, we observed that NSUN2 knockdown weakened PARP9 mRNA stability in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3L and M). Collectively, NSUN2 interacted with PARP9 and stabilized its mRNA.

YBX1 recognized m5C modification of PARP9

RBPmap database predicted that YBX1 could bind to PARP9 (Fig. 4A). Then the relation between YBX1 and PARP9 was investigated. As shown in Fig. 4B, si-YBX1 was transfected into MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells to silence YBX1 expression and the results were examined by western blot. Silencing of YBX1 decreased the mRNA and protein expression of PARP9 in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 4C, D). RIP assay showed that NSUN2 knockdown reduced the enrichment of PARP9 RNA bound to YBX1, indicating that NSUN2-mediated m5C modification on PARP9 mRNA was required for efficient recognition and binding by the reader protein YBX1 (Fig. 4E, F). Furthermore, Act D assay showed that YBX1 knockdown reduced the stability of PARP9 mRNA (Fig. 4G, H). These findings indicated that NSUN2 mediated m5C modification of PARP9, while YBX1 functioned as a reader protein that recognized the modified transcript.

PARP9 overexpression reversed the effect of NSUN2 knockdown on BC cell progression

Subsequently, the relation between PARP9 and NSUN2 in BC progression was explored. As exhibited in Fig. 5A, OE-PARP9 transfection led to a significant elevation of PARP9 expression in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells compared to OE-NC control group. Knockdown of NSUN2 suppressed the proliferation of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells, with PARP9 overexpression restored the effect (Fig. 5B). Flow cytometry analysis presented that NSUN2 knockdown contributed to the apoptosis of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells, while the influence was reversed by upregulating PARP9 expression (Fig. 5C, D). The inhibitory effect of NSUN2 knockdown on BC cell invasion was abated by PARP9 elevation, as indicated by transwell assay (Fig. 5E). Moreover, NSUN2 deficiency restrained the angiogenesis and stemness in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells, whereas PARP9 enhancement abrogated the effect (Fig. 5F, G). After NSUN2 deficiency, TNF-α and IFN-γ levels were increased and CD8 + T cell apoptosis was suppressed, but the effects were weakened following PAPR9 overexpression (Fig. 5H–J). Taken together, NSUN2 silencing could regulate PARP9 expression to influence BC progression.

NSUN2 mediated ARP9 expression to alter tumor growth in vivo

Sh-NSUN2 was transfected into MCF-7 cells to reduce NSUN2 expression, as shown in Fig. 6A. Next, sh-NC, sh-NSUN2 or sh-NSUN2 + OE-PARP9-transfected MCF-7 cells were introduced the mice. Our results showed the xenograft tumors (including tumor volume and tumor weight) were reduced in the mice of sh-NSUN2 group, but the effects were ameliorated in sh-NSUN2 + OE-PARP9 group (Fig. 6B, C). Western blot assay demonstrated that NSUN2 was downregulated in the tumors of sh-NSUN2 group and sh-NSUN2 + OE-PARP9 group (Fig. 6D). Moreover, western blot and IHC assays showed that PARP9 and Ki-67 levels were reduced in the tumors of sh-NSUN2 group, but recovered in the tumors of sh-NSUN2 + OE-PARP9 group (Fig. 6E, F). Collectively, NSUN2 mediated PARP9 expression to alter tumorigenesis in vivo.

Discussion

The occurrence and development of BC are closely related to epigenetic abnormal changes [22]. m5C modification is extensively involved in the epigenetic regulatory processes within organisms, playing crucial roles in biological processes such as genome stability, gene expression regulation, and cell differentiation [8]. m5C modification is mainly induced by the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2, and has been proven to play an important role in the development of many cancers [23]. In this study, we aimed to explore the function of NSUN2-mediated m5C modification in BC progression. Our results demonstrated the tumorigenic role of NSUN2 in BC that might be mediated by m5C- and YBX1-dependent stabilization of the PARP9 transcript.

The final effect of post-transcriptional regulation mainly depends on the m5C reading proteins, which recognize the m5C modification and have a crucial impact on the output, stability and initiation of translation of mRNAs. YBX1 is a well-known m5C reading protein, which regulates the stability of target transcripts by recognizing m5C modifications [24]. In recent years, more and more evidence has shown that YBX1 plays the role of an m5C reading protein in various human cancers, such as esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [24], bladder cancer [25], ovarian cancer [26] as well as BC [17]. In our study, we found that overexpression of YBX1 elevated PARP9 expression in BC cells through mediating m5C modification of PARP9. Our finding was similar to the results of many studies on YBX1 as an m5C “reader”. Thus, we further explored the effect of PARP9 in BC.

PARP9 was responsible for the development of various types of cancer, including breast cancer [19]. In breast cancer, PARP9 was upregulated and promoted tumor cell growth, invasion and migration [21, 27]. In our research, we demonstrated that PARP knockdown repressed BC cell growth, invasion, angiogenesis and stemness. PARP enzyme has been reported to be involved in the immune evasion of tumors [28]. The dysfunction of CD8 + T cells can lead to immune escape of tumors, and the reduction of TNF-α and IFN-γ contents facilitates the evasion of immune responses by tumor cells [29]. Hong et al. confirmed that PAPR9 deficiency repressed CD8 + cell apoptosis, and elevated IFN-γ and TNF-α expression via the PI3K/AKT/PD-L1 axis [21]. Likewise, our study also exhibited that TNF-α and IFN-γ levels were increased and CD8 + T cell apoptosis was restrained after PAPR9 silencing, indicating the role of PARP9 knockdown in reducing the immune escape of BC cells. Our research results were consistent with the previous reports. More importantly, by revealing that NSUN2 mediated m5C modification, which could stabilize PARP9 mRNA in BC, we have expanded upon these findings and clarified its upstream regulatory mechanism. To our knowledge, this is the first study to link the expression of PARP9 with RNA m5C modification, providing a new regulatory level for the pathogenesis of BC.

Emerging evidence has revealed that NSUN2 functions as an important regulator in disease progression by mediating m5C modification of target genes [30, 31]. Qi et al. uncovered that NSUN2 was correlated with poor prognosis of HCC patients and could aggravate the development and glycolysis of GCC by stabilizing PKM2 mRNA via a m5C modification manner [32]. Zhang et al. revealed that NSUN2 promote the degradation of KDM6B mRNA via the m5C modification of PPIA, thereby facilitating osteoclast differentiation and osteolytic bone metastasis in breast cancer [16]. Moreover, the oncogenic role of NSUN2 in BC was also proved via NSUN2/YBX1/HGH1 axis [17]. Yi et al. verified that the overall expression of NSUN2 in BC was significantly associated with clinical stage, tumor classification, and pathological differentiation degree [33]. Corresponding to the previous literature, we demonstrated that NSUN2 was raised in BC and could stabilize PARP9 mRNA to elevate RPAR expression via mediating m5C modification. Moreover, we demonstrated that silencing of NSUN2 could mediate m5C modification of PARP9 to repress BC cell malignant behaviors in vitro and tumor growth in vivo. In addition to targeting the NSUN2/PARP9 axis, natural products are also potential therapeutic sources for BC. For instance, baicalin has been reported to inhibit the progression of various gynecological cancers, including breast cancer, by regulating multiple signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT [34]. Future research can explore the potential of combined targeted therapy of the NSUN2-PARP9 axis and natural products (such as baicalin). Beyond the RNA m5C modification explored in this study, other layers of RNA-mediated regulation, such as those involving long non-coding RNAs like MALAT1, also played critical roles in breast cancer progression. The multifaceted functions of MALAT1 in modulating gene expression, signaling pathways, and therapy resistance have been well documented [35]. Future studies may investigate the potential crosstalk between m5C modification machinery and lncRNA regulatory networks in BC.

However, our study still has some shortcomings. For example, we did not conduct experiment to verify the direct binding relationship between YBX1 and the predicted PARP9 m5C sites. In future research, we will employ methods such as ultraviolet cross-linking and immunoprecipitation (CLIP) or electrophoretic mobility shift analysis (EMSA) to confirm the direct binding relationship between YBX1 and PARP9 m5C sites. Moreover, although PARP9 overexpression partially rescued the tumor-suppressive effects of NSUN2 knockdown, the in vivo rescue experiment remained largely phenotypic, without deeper mechanistic validation or functional endpoints beyond tumor volume and weight.

In conclusion, our study elucidated NSUN2-mediated m5C modification in BC by mediating PARP9 expression. We discovered a novel regulatory pathway of NSUN2/YBX1/PARP9 in BC, which provided a novel perspective for BC therapy.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The gene expression dataset GSE95299 was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Raw data were processed using the affy package in R (version 4.0.5). The normalization and differential gene expression tests were conducted using the Bioconductor software package DESeq2. Multiple test corrections were performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the limma package with a cutoff of |log₂ fold change| > 4.0 and adjusted P < 0.0001 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction). PARP9 was identified as significantly upregulated (log₂FC = 4.03, adj. P < 0.0001).

The TCGA breast cancer dataset (BRCA) was accessed via the TCGA portal (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html). PARP9 expression was further validated using GEPIA (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/detail.php), an interactive web server for analyzing TCGA and GTEx data. These database predictions indicate that, compared with the normal control group, the significant differences in gene expression in breast cancer tissues are less than 0.05. There are no specific parameters, or filtering or subgroup analysis, etc.

Acquisition of tissues

A total of 35 patients with pathologically confirmed BC who underwent surgical resection at Xijing Hospital, between January 2019 and December 2021 were consecutively enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) no prior neoadjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) availability of both tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of other malignancies within the past 5 years; (2) pregnancy or lactation at the time of diagnosis; (3) severe infectious or autoimmune diseases; (4) metastatic disease at initial diagnosis. Tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues (at least 2 cm away from the tumor margin) were harvested from the patients and preserved at -80 °C until usage. All tissue specimens were histopathologically reviewed by two independent pathologists to confirm the diagnosis and the absence of tumor cells in adjacent non-tumor tissues. Written informed consents were provided by the participants. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital (KY20192014-C-1). All procedures involving human tissues were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture

Normal human breast epithelial cell line (MCF-10 A) and breast cancer cell lines (BT-549, MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7) were acquired from Procell (Wuhan, China). MCF-10 A cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 added with 20 ng/mL EGF, 5% HS, 0.5 µg/mL Hydrocortisone, 10 µg/mL Insulin, 1% NEAA and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. BT-549 cells were clutured in RPMI-1640 added with 10% FBS, 10 µg/mL Insulin and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in DMEM added with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. MCF-7 cells were maintained in MEM (containing NEAA) supplemented with 10 µg/mL Insulin, 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. The mediums were offered by Procell and the cells were kept at 37 °C in a humid incubator cotaining 5% CO2.

Cell transfection

The overexpression vector of YBX1 (OE-YBX1) and empty vector (OE-NC); small interfering RNA (siRNA) against PARP9 (si-PARP9#1, 5’-CAGCGAGCTGGAGCTGGAT-3’; si-PARP9#2, 5’-GCAGCAGGTGGTGGAGGAT-3’), siRNA against NSUN2 (si-NSUN2, 5’-GGAAGAAATGGACTACCTT-3’), siRNA against YBX1 (si-YBX1, 5’-GACAGCTCCGAAGACACTA-3’) and related control (si-NC); PARP9 overexpression vector (OE-PARP9); short hairpin RNA against NSUN2 (sh-NSUN2) and related control (sh-NC) were all designed and synthesized by GeneCopoeia (Guangzhou, China). MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/well and cultured overnight to reach 70–80% confluence, and then synthetic oligonucleotides (50 nM) or vectors (2 µg) were transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Transfection efficiency was confirmed by western blot at 72 h post-transfection. Knockdown was considered successful when target mRNA expression was reduced by ≥ 70% compared to si-NC control. Overexpression was confirmed by ≥ 2-fold increase in protein expression compared to OE-NC control.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells by using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After quantification by NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer, the RNAs were reverse-transcribed into cDNAs using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). qRT-PCR was manipulated using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The expression was determined through the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the negative control. The primer pairs were exhibited in Table 1.

Western blot

RIPA (Beyotime) was used to extract the proteins in tissues and cells. BCA Protein Quantification Kit (Vazyme) was used to examine the protein concentrations. Next, equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were transferred onto PVDF membranes, segregated with SDS-PAGE and blocked in 5% slim milk in TBST for 20 min at room temperature. The proteins were maintained overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies. After three washes with TBST (10 min each), membranes were incubated with Goat Anti-Mouse IgG-HRP Secondary Antibody (1:5000; ab6729; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional washes (10 min each), protein bands were visualized using an ECL kit (Beyotime) and detected with the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

The primary antibodies against PARP9 (1:1000; ab53796), NSUN2 (1:1000; ab259941) or YBX1 (1:1000; ab255606), and loading control GAPDH (1:5000; ab128915) were purchased from Abcam.

5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

To explore BC cell proliferation, EDU assay reagent (RIBOBIO, Guangzhou, China) was utilized. In brief, BC cells (MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7) in the logarithmic growth phase were plated onto 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per well and cultured overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ to allow cell attachment. After 48 h of transfection, cells were cultured with EdU reagent (10 µM) for 2 h at 37 °C. Following EdU labeling, the medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with PBS, then immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 20 min. After fixation, cells were washed twice with PBS and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton-X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at room temperature. Afterward, cells were washed twice with PBS and stained with Aollo fluorescent staining solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark followed by interaction with Hoechst 33,342 solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. The positive cells were estimated with an Olympus IX73 fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The number of EdU-positive cells and total Hoechst-positive cells were counted using ImageJ software. The proliferation rate was calculated as: (number of EdU-positive cells/total number of Hoechst-positive cells) × 100%. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated independently three times.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cell apoptosis was analyzed by utilizing Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime). After 72 h of transfection, MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells were harvested by trypsinization (0.25% trypsin-EDTA) without EDTA to avoid membrane damage. Cells were washed twice with cold PBS and then resuspended in binding buffer at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Cell suspension (100 µL) was transferred to a 5-mL flow cytometry tube and stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI for 15 min at room temperature in a dark condition. After staining, 400 µL of 1× binding buffer was added to each tube. Samples were analyzed within 1 h using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). FlowJo software version v10.8 (BD Biosciences) was used for data analysis. For each sample, at least 10,000 events were acquired.

Apoptotic cells were identified as: Early apoptotic cells: Annexin V-FITC positive / PI negative (lower right quadrant).

Late apoptotic cells: Annexin V-FITC positive / PI positive (upper right quadrant).

Total apoptotic cells = early apoptotic cells + late apoptotic cells.

Gating strategy: Debris was excluded based on forward scatter (FSC) vs. side scatter (SSC) gating. Doublet discrimination was performed using FSC-H vs. FSC-A gating.

Transwell assay

The 24-well transwell chambers with 8.0 μm pore size polycarbonate membranes pre-covered with Matrigel (Solarbio, Beijing, China) were adopted to explore cell invasion. In short, after 48 h of transfection, BC cells were detached with trypsin, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free medium at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL 200 µL of the cell suspension was added into the upper chamber and 600 µL complete culture medium containing 10% FBS was added into the bottom chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. Invading cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min at room temperature, then stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio) for 20 min at room temperature. The stained membranes were washed three times with distilled water and air-dried. Stained cells were observed and photographed using a light microscope (Olympus Corporation) at 200× magnification. For each insert, five random fields were captured. The number of invading cells per field was counted manually using ImageJ software. The average number of invading cells per field was calculated for each insert.

Tube formation assay

The angiogenesis capacity of BC cells was assessed by tube formation assay. Ice-cold Matrigel (50 µL/well) was added into a 96-well plate and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C to allow solidification. After transfection for 48 h, BC cells were trypsinized, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free DMEM. 150 µL of cell suspension containing 2 × 10⁴ cells was gently added onto the solidified Matrigel in each well. The plate was incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 6 h. Tube formation was monitored periodically under a microscope to identify the optimal time point for imaging. Tube structures were photographed using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Zeiss Axiovert 200 M, Carl Zeiss, Germany) at 100× magnification. For each well, at least five random fields were captured. Tube formation was quantified using ImageJ software with the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin (developed by Carpentier G.).

Sphere formation assay

This assay was used to evaluate cell stemness. After 48 h of transfection, BC cells were trypsinized, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF), 10 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), 1× B27 supplement, 5 µg/mL insulin and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Then cells were seed onto the low-attachment 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well in 2 mL of the above medium. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 14 days. Half of the medium was replaced with fresh complete sphere formation medium every 2 days to maintain nutrient supply and growth factor concentrations. After 2 weeks, the size and number of suspended cell spheres were observed using an inverted microscope (Olympus Corporation) at 100× magnification. For each well, the entire well was scanned, and all spheres with a diameter ≥ 50 μm were counted. A sphere was defined as a non-adherent, rounded colony of cells with clear boundaries.

ELISA

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donor blood (from the Blood Bank of Xijing Hospital) by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation. Briefly, whole blood was diluted 1:1 with PBS, layered over Ficoll-Paque PLUS (400×g, 30 min, at room temperature). The mononuclear cell layer was carefully collected, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Isolated PMBCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁶ cells/mL and stimulated with 5 µg/mL PHA for 48 h to activate T cells. Then the stimulated PBMCs were collected, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in fresh RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS. The transfected BC cells were trypsinized, washed, and resuspended. PBMCs and BC cells were co-cultured at a ratio of 10:1 (1 × 10⁵ BC cells and 1 × 10⁶ PBMCs per well) in 24-well plates for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Next, the supernatants in the co-culture medium were collected and subjected to TNF-α ELISA kit (ab181421; Abcam) and IFN-γ ELISA kit (ab46025; Abcam) for the detection of TNF-α and IFN-γ concentrations.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assay

The m5C MeRIP reagent (BersinBio, Guangzhou, China) was used to determine m5C enrichment of PARP9. In brief, total RNA in si-NC or si-NSUN2-transfected BC cells was extracted. For each MeRIP reaction, 50 µg of total RNA was fragmented into ~ 100–200 nucleotide fragments by incubation in RNA fragmentation buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.0, 10 mM ZnCl₂) for 6 min at 70 °C, followed by immediate termination with 10 mM EDTA on ice. Fragment size was verified by denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis. Next, 30 µL of Protein G magnetic beads were conjugated with 5 µg of anti-m5C antibody or 5 µg of normal mouse IgG (negative control) in IP buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA) for 1 h at 4 °C. Beads were then blocked with 100 µg/mL yeast RNA and 100 µg/mL BSA for 30 min. Thereafter, fragmented RNA was incubated with antibody-conjugated beads for 3 h at 4 °C. After incubation, beads were washed 3 times with low-salt wash buffer (150 mM NaCl) and 1 time with high-salt wash buffer (500 mM NaCl). A 1% input control was saved before IP. RNA was eluted using elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, 200 mM NaCl) at 65 °C for 15 min, then purified using RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo Research). Purified RNA was reverse transcribed and analyzed by qRT-PCR.

RNA integrity (RIN) > 7.0, fragment size 100–200 nt confirmed by gel electrophoresis, and IgG enrichment < 0.5% of input.

RIP assay

The Magna RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation kit (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) was used for RIP assay. The transfected MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (1 × 10⁷ cells per IP reaction) were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed in 100 µL of complete RIP lysis buffer. The magnetic beads were conjugated with 5 µg anti-IgG (ab190475; Abcam), anti-m5C (AT15002; Engibody) or anti-YBX1 (ab255606; Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. Next, cell lysates were incubated with the beads overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, beads were washed twice with low-salt RIP wash buffer (150 mM NaCl) and twice with high-salt RIP wash buffer (500 mM NaCl). RNA was purified by Proteinase K digestion followed by phenol-chloroform extraction. A 10% input aliquot was saved before IP for normalization. Purified RNA was reverse transcribed and PARP9 enrichment was detected by qRT-PCR. Enrichment was calculated as %Input = 2^((Ct_Input − 3.32) - Ct_IP) × 100%.

RNA pull-down assay

The Pierce Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-Down Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to analyze the interaction between NSUN2 and PARP9. Biotinylated RNA probes targeting the PARP9 mRNA region spanning the predicted m5C modification sites were chemically synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) with 3’-end biotin labeling (length: ~200 nt). A biotinylated non-specific scrambled RNA (Bio-NC, ~ 200 nt, random sequence) was used as negative control. After 48 h of si-NC or si-NSUN2 pre-transfection, MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (1 × 10⁷ per reaction) were transfected with 50 nM Bio-PARP9 or Bio-NC using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). After 24 h, cells were lysed in (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 0.5% NP-40). Streptavidin magnetic beads (50 µL per reaction) were blocked with BSA and yeast RNA, then incubated with cell lysates for 3 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times with low-salt buffer (150 mM KCl) and twice with high-salt buffer (500 mM KCl). Bound proteins were eluted in SDS loading buffer by boiling at 95 °C for 10 min and analyzed by western blot. NSUN2 enrichment was quantified as fold change over Bio-NC control.

Actinomycin D (Act D) assay

BC cells transfected si-NC, si-NSUN2 or si-YBX1 were plated into 6-well plates (3 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates) and then treated with 5 µg/mL Act D (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in DMSO for 0 h, 3 h and 6 h. Then total RNA was extracted and subjected to qRT-PCR for PARP9 expression. The percentage of mRNA remaining at each time point was calculated relative to the 0-hour time point using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. mRNA half-life (t₁/₂) was estimated by fitting decay curves to a first-order exponential decay model using GraphPad Prism.

Murine xenograft model

The BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old; Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animals Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were divided into 3 groups: sh-NC, sh-NSUN2 and sh-NSUN2 + OE-PARP9. Lentiviral particles encoding sh-NSUN2 (sh-NSUN2), sh-NC (negative control), or sh-NSUN2 together with OE-PARP9 (sh-NSUN2 + OE-PARP9) were purchased from GeneCopoeia (Guangzhou, China). MCF-7 cells were transduced with the above lentiviral particles at an MOI of 5 in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. After 24 h, cells were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 72 h and knockdown efficiency (> 70%) was confirmed by western blot. Stable cells (1 × 10⁶ in 100 µL PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks, n = 5 per group). From the 9th day, xenograft tumors were monitored every 5 days and tumor volume was calculated with the formula: (length×width2)/2. After 29 days, the mice were euthanized and tumors were weighed and analyzed by western blot and IHC. The animal study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital (20240780). Animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

IHC assay

The xenograft tumor tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich), embedded in paraffin and cut into Sect. (4 μm). After deparaffinization, antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 30 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-PARP9 (1:200; ab53796, Abcam) and anti-Ki-67 (1:200; ab16667, Abcam). After washing, sections were incubated with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200) for 1 h, followed by HRP-streptavidin (1:200) for 30 min. Staining was visualized using the DAB peroxidase staining kit, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured using a microscope (Olympus Corporation) at 200× magnification (5 fields per section). Ki-67 index was calculated as the percentage of brown-stained nuclei (≥ 200 nuclei counted per field). PARP9 expression was assessed using the H-score method (intensity 0–3 × percentage of positive cells, range 0-300).

Data analysis

The data were derived from 3 duplicates and analyzed by GraphPad Prism v7.05. The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was used for testing normal distribution. The results were presented as mean ± SD. The linear correlation between the levels of NSUN2 and PARP9 in BC tissues was analyzed by Spearman’s correlation coefficient analysis. Difference analysis was conducted by Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s test. P < 0.05 was deemed as significant.
