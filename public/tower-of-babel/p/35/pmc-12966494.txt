Maternal-Fetal Ultrasouno Video Dataset for End-to-end Intrapartum Biometry and Multi-task Learning
Authors: Ming Niu, Jieyun Bai, Yunbo Gao, Yitong Tang, Yaosheng Lu, Zhenyan Han, Hongying Hou, Yuxin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntrapartum biometry is of vital significance in monitoring labor progress. However, the realization of AI-based end-to-end intrapartum biometry and labor progress assessment requires intrapartum ultrasound video datasets with multi-category annotations, and currently, there is no public video dataset available for multi-category fine-grained classification. While several image datasets exist for related tasks (e.g., JNU-IFM, PSFHS, IUGC), a dedicated benchmark in the video domain remains unavailable. To bridge this gap, we have publicly released, for the first time, a multi-center, multi-device, and multi-category labeled intrapartum ultrasound dataset. This dataset comprises 774 videos / 68,106 images, along with corresponding standard plane classification labels, multi-class segmentation labels of pubic symphysis and fetal head, and two ultrasound parameter labels that characterize labor progress. This dataset can facilitate research on multi-task learning methods and the development of end-to-end automated approaches, especially in the automation of obstetric processes and auxiliary decision-making.Subject terms: Translational research, Anatomy

Background & Summary

Intrapartum ultrasound, recognized for its cost-effectiveness and potential to reduce fetal mortality, addresses a critical global health challenge1. Alarmingly, 45% of maternal and neonatal deaths occur during the intrapartum period, highlighting an urgent need for improved monitoring solutions2,3. Professional guidelines from leading organizations such as the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG)4, the World Association of Perinatal Medicine (WAPM), the Perinatal Medicine Foundation (PMF)5, and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE)6 underscore this need, both by standardizing the diagnosis of critical intrapartum conditions7 and by highlighting ultrasound parameters to assess fetal head descent.

These practices are supported by detailed technical guidelines, covering aspects from precise cervical length measurement8 to the standardized assessment of fetal biometry and growth9. These parameters function as essential predictive indicators for determining delivery methods, offering the possibility of decreasing non-indicated cesarean sections and enhancing maternal and fetal outcomes. The overarching goal of such detailed biometry and anatomical assessment, conducted in both the first and second trimesters, is the effective screening for potential fetal structural anomalies10.

The manual assessment of intrapartum ultrasound videos is subjective and time-consuming, requiring clinicians to capture standard planes and extract key biometric parameters like the angle of progression (AoP) and head-symphysis distance (HSD) to evaluate fetal descent11,12. This labor-intensive process, dependent on high clinical proficiency, underscores the pressing need for automation to improve efficiency and consistency.

Several international research groups are dedicated to the automation of fetal ultrasound image analysis to improve the consistency and efficiency of measurements. This is particularly important for standard mid-trimester scans, where the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) consensus guidelines define standardized imaging planes and methods for key structures such as the fetal head13. The clinical value of such standardization is profound, with the adoption of the three-vessel view alone significantly improving the detection of critical conotruncal anomalies14. Similarly, the American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine (AIUM) updated its practice parameter to include standardized views like the 3-vessel and trachea view for the second-trimester fetal anatomic survey15. These guidelines provide crucial clinical benchmarks and validation standards for developing automated algorithms. The clinical impact of standardizing ultrasound views is clear, having historically boosted detection rates for major congenital heart disease16.

The automated measurement of parameters from ultrasound videos is a multi-task process that involves screening standard sections for measurement, identifying maternal-fetal structures in these sections, and extracting key points for parameter calculation17–19. While prior research has centered on individual tasks in ultrasound analysis, such as segmenting maternal-fetal structures, classifying standard planes, and detecting key points, multi-task learning applied to videos remains largely unexplored. For example, Zhou et al. developed segmentation algorithms for maternal-fetal structures20, Qiu et al. focused on classification of standard sections21, and Zhou et al. worked on key point detection algorithms22. However, these studies were limited to static images and cannot fully address the clinical needs for fully automated workflows using ultrasound videos23–32. Notably, although there are some semi-automated video-based methods for measuring intrapartum parameters33, these approaches still rely on manual intervention and are subject to thresholds that may be challenging for doctors with insufficient ultrasound experience. This challenge mirrors the evolution in other security domains, such as prototype pollution in JavaScript, where the mitigation of straightforward exploitation paths necessitates the discovery of more complex, chained gadgets to achieve a malicious intent34.

Conversano et al. proposed a semi-automated real-time algorithm for AoP monitoring during the second stage of labor, using image targets and pixel gray levels for template-based auto-segmentation35,36. Despite that some Challenges such as A-AFMA (ISBI 2021), FUGC (ISBI 2025), and ACOUSLIC-AI (MICCAI 2024) have promote the progress of the filed, focusing on the automatic measurement of length and (or) circumference promote. Such methods are far from fully automated, highlighting the need for a more robust solution.

The development of a comprehensive intrapartum ultrasound analysis system faces three primary challenges: first, the substantial workload required to create a video dataset with annotations for classification, segmentation, and measurement; second, most existing studies address either standard plane identification or biometric segmentation in isolation, failing to form a complete pipeline; lastly, despite relevant research efforts, the corresponding datasets are often not publicly available, which severely hinders reproducibility and fair benchmarking in the field. This data scarcity directly impacts clinical translatability, as evidenced by systematic reviews highlighting the gap between guideline development and practical implementation in areas like preterm birth prevention37.

To address this gap, a comprehensively labeled ultrasound video dataset is crucial for developing truly automated parameter measurement in obstetrics and gynecology. obstetrics and gynecology ultrasound image analysis. In 2022, our team publicly released a single-center, single-device intrapartum ultrasound image dataset to support research on algorithms for standard section identification and automatic segmentation of maternal-fetal structures38. To further support the development of generalized segmentation algorithms, we also released a multi-center, multi-device dataset and organized a challenge on automatic segmentation of maternal-fetal structures at MICCAI 202339. The creation of such benchmark resources is a recognized catalyst for progress in the field. However, these image-based datasets lack the necessary video data and corresponding multi-category labels required for video-based automated parameter measurement. To overcome this challenge, we have now collected a multi-center, multi-device intrapartum ultrasound video dataset with comprehensive multi-category labels, facilitating rapid advancements in the development of fully automated intrapartum ultrasound parameter measurement. Methods

Subject characteristics

The retrospective dataset comprised ultrasound videos and images from 774 pregnant women. The study enrolled women with single pregnancies at or beyond 37 weeks’ gestation carrying fetuses in cephalic position. Participants were excluded for the following reasons: gestational age below 37 weeks, non-cephalic fetal positioning, twin or multiple pregnancies, congenital uterine malformations, history of uterine surgical procedures, abnormal fetal heart rate patterns during labor, or declined participation. The dataset is from three medical institutions, and the information on the number of videos, images, and device model is shown in Table 2.

Dataset split for model development

To rigorously evaluate the model’s generalization capability across different ultrasound devices, the dataset was partitioned based on a device-centric strategy. The training set comprised 434 scans sourced exclusively from a single device (ObEye), while the internal validation set contained 40 scans from the same device to ensure a consistent benchmark during model development and hyperparameter tuning. The test set, totaling approximately 300 scans, was constructed to assess cross-device robustness by combining around 100 scans from each of the three distinct ultrasound systems (ObEye, Voluson P8, and Esaote MyLab). This split is designed to simulate a real-world scenario where a model trained on data from one device must perform reliably on images acquired from different institutions and equipment.

This study was approved by the institutional review boards of the participating centers (Approval Nos. JNUKY-2022-019, 2023-SYJS-023, and [2021] 20-367). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and the use of fully de-identified clinical video data (Fig. 1).

The processing flow involves first classifying the ultrasound images, based on these classification results, segmenting regions such as the fetal head (FH) and pubic symphysis (PS); and finally, on the basis of segmentation, calculating biometric indicators like the angle of progression (AoP) and head-symphysis distance (HSD).

Data acquisition

In this investigation, all ultrasound scans were acquired by certified medical professionals utilizing three different ultrasound systems: the Esaote MyLab (Italy), GE Voluson P8 (USA), and ObEye (China). The imaging protocol adhered to the standards established by ISUOG. For each examination, the ultrasound transducer was covered with a sterile latex glove containing conductive gel. Following the application of additional coupling gel, the probe was gently placed between the labia minora below the pubic bone to obtain a mid-sagittal view. The operator made subtle medial-lateral adjustments to the probe to achieve optimal visualization. This technique clearly displayed key anatomical structures, including the maternal pubic symphysis and fetal cranial bones, while avoiding acoustic shadows from the pelvic bones.

The typical two-dimensional transmit frequency was set between 3–5 MHz (utilizing lower frequencies when necessary for adequate penetration), and the imaging depth was adjusted from approximately 12 cm to 22 cm to fully encompass the relevant maternal and fetal anatomy, including the pubic symphysis and fetal head.

By device, the distribution was as follows: 560 videos from ObEye, 121 from Voluson P8, and 93 from Esaote MyLab. All ultrasound videos were acquired following a standardized clinical protocol across the three participating institutions. The videos have an average duration of 2 seconds and were captured at a constant frame rate of 24 frames per second using the MJPG codec. The original grayscale images, with a resolution of 720 × 564 pixels and an 8-bit color depth, were subsequently standardized to 512 × 512 pixels for model input, ensuring data homogeneity. Annotation protocol and delineation criteria

To ensure the consistency and reproducibility of all manual annotations, a standardized delineation protocol was strictly followed by all expert annotators specializing in obstetric ultrasound. The fetal head (FH) boundary was defined as the outer contour of the most prominent visible cranial bone cortex in the mid-sagittal view. The pubic symphysis (PS) boundary included the entire superior surface of the pubic bone that was clearly visible, with careful exclusion of regions obscured by significant acoustic shadows or reverberation artifacts.

Image annotation details

To ensure consistency across different ultrasound devices, all video recordings were in AVI format and underwent two processing steps. First, they were anonymized, and then they were automatically resized to 512×512 pixels by stripping device-specific headers. Data were acquired by specialized experts teams comprising three experts: the sonographer, the obstetrician, and the technologist with over seven years of professional experience. The annotation protocol involved: 1) identifying intrapartum ultrasound standard planes by selecting high-quality images with intact PS and FH from videos; 2) free-hand manual segmentation of PS and FH; 3) measuring ultrasound parameters (i.e., AoP and HSD). To ensure label consistency, a rigorous cross-annotation protocol was followed. Each expert annotated an additional 100 videos (50 from each of the other two experts’ initial sets). The final ground truth for disputed cases was determined through consensus review among the three experts.

Definition of anatomical landmarks and coordinate system

AoP is defined as the angular measurement between the longitudinal axis of the pubic symphysis and a line tangent to both the inferior pubic border and the most prominent bony point of the fetal cranium. HSD refers to the perpendicular distance from the inferior margin of the pubic symphysis to the nearest fetal head point along the pubic axis, which were converted from pixels to millimeters using the pixel spacing values (0.30 mm/pixel).

To ensure clear and reproducible calculation, four key anatomical landmarks were automatically identified from the segmentation results (Fig. 2). The calculation of the Head-Symphysis Distance (HSD) used the Euclidean distance between Point 2 and Point 3, as defined in formula (2). The Angle of Progression (AoP) was computed based on the geometric relationship involving Points 1, 2, and 4. This implementation is consistent with the public IUGC 2024 evaluation code.

The coordinates of these points are stored in the landmarks.json file (in supplementary File 1) under specific keys: Points 1 and 2 are in the “ps_points” array ([“y1”, “x1”], [“y2”, “x2”]), Point 3 corresponds to “hsd_point”: [“y3”, “x3”], and Point 4 to “aop_tangency”: [“y4”, “x4”].

The standard digital image coordinate system was employed, with the origin (0, 0) at the upper-left corner, the X-axis extending right, and the Y-axis extending downward. The specific scripts (cal_aop.py and supporting modules) are available in the IUGC 2024 repository: https://github.com/maskoffs/IUGC2024/tree/main/eval.

Data anonymization & privacy

To ensure patient privacy and ethical compliance, all videos underwent a comprehensive de-identification process. This included the digital removal of all on-screen text and graphical overlays containing protected health information (PHI), such as patient names, IDs, and examination dates. Furthermore, the dataset contains no audio tracks, and all associated metadata were scrubbed prior to public release. The resulting dataset is fully anonymized and poses no risk of patient re-identification.

Data Records

Data labeling schema

The dataset described in this paper, titled “Maternal-Fetal Ultrasound Video Dataset for End-to-end Intrapartum Biometry”, is publicly available and hosted on the Zenodo repository under the [CC BY 4.0] license and can be accessed via 10.5281/zenodo.1765518340. It comprises 774 videos / 68,106 images, accompanied by comprehensive annotations for automated analysis.

The dataset is structured into standard training, validation, and test splits. To support our multi-task learning framework and ensure clarity and reproducibility, annotations within each split are organized by task type. Specifically, each split contains the original ultrasound video sequences (videos/), the corresponding segmentation masks and landmark annotations (seg/), and the classification labels (cls/). Comprehensive metadata files (e.g., *_info.csv) accompany each split to provide detailed frame-level indices and annotation references.

Classification Task: Classification labels are stored in CSV files within the cls/ directory (e.g., cls/class_label.csv). The schema implements multi-task binary classification. Each column in the CSV file represents an independent clinical judgment category (e.g., “filename”, “frame_count”, and pos_index), and each row corresponds to a video sample. Labels for each category are binary (NONE/ALL), indicating whether the specific condition is met for that video. Thus, models learn multiple independent binary decision tasks concurrently during classification.

Segmentation and Biometry Tasks: Segmentation annotations are stored as per-frame mask images in the “.png” format. The coordinates of key anatomical landmarks (e.g., PS midpoint, FH contour points) and their derived biometric parameters (AoP, HSD) are recorded in the “/landmark.json” file. AoP and the Head-Symphysis Distance HSD can be automatically calculate from the coordinates of the key points.

Technical Validation

Framework for inter-rater reliability analysis

To assess potential annotation variability, we conducted an inter-rater reliability analysis. Three annotators, one from each participating institution (Jinan University, Sun Yat-sen University, and Southern Medical University), independently analyzed a randomly assembled and blinded set of 150 videos (50 from each institution’s data pool). The analysis focused on three task types:

Classification

Metrics (Accuracy-ACC, F1-score, Area Under the ROC Curve-AUC, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient-MCC) were calculated at the video level.

Segmentation

Frame-level agreement was quantified using the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), Average Symmetric Surface Distance (ASSD), and Hausdorff Distance (HD).

Biometry mearsurement

Derived ultrasound parameters (ΔAoP and ΔHSD) were also assessed at the frame level.

Both annotators demonstrated consistently high performance across all task categories when evaluated against the consensus standard. In classification tasks, ACC exceeded 0.86 and F1-score surpassed 0.87. For segmentation quality, DSC were notably high, exceeding 0.92 for the PS and 0.95 for the FH, indicating excellent boundary agreement. In biometric measurements, the mean errors were approximately 5° for ΔAoP and 5-6 pixels for ΔHSD, with relatively stable standard deviations across metrics. Notably, metrics such as ASSD, HD, ΔAoP, and ΔHSD exhibit high standard deviations. This is primarily due to two reasons: first, these metrics are inherently sensitive to variations along segmentation boundaries; second, our heterogeneous clinical dataset contains a number of challenging cases (e.g., those with low contrast or complex anatomy), which can cause large fluctuations in the measured values. Together, these factors underscore the inherent complexity of medical image segmentation evaluation on real-world data.

The high level of performance combined with consistent results across all task categories demonstrates that both annotators delivered high-quality and stable annotations throughout the 150-video dataset.

ICC for inter-annotator agreement

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement model (ICC(2,1)) in Python’s pingouin package, with 95% confidence intervals generated via bootstrapping (1000 iterations).

The overall ICC results across all three institutions are summarized in Table 3. Following standard ICC interpretation thresholds— < 0.2 (poor), 0.2–0.4 (fair), 0.4–0.6 (moderate), 0.6–0.8 (strong), and 0.8–1.0 (excellent)—the pooled analysis showed ICC > 0.4 across all metrics, indicating acceptable reliability. Specifically, classification performance exhibited excellent agreement (ICC > 0.8). Segmentation consistency was higher for PS than for FH. This differential reliability stems from fundamental differences in their imaging characteristics. The PS appears as a well-defined, hyperechoic structure with relatively consistent positioning, providing a clear boundary for annotation. In contrast, FH segmentation is challenged by several factors: (1) acoustic shadowing often obscures parts of the skull boundary; (2) its position and shape vary significantly with fetal movement and descent; and (3) the interface with maternal tissues frequently exhibits low contrast.

Among ultrasound-derived measurements, ΔAoP (ICC = 0.53) demonstrated slightly better reliability than ΔHSD (ICC = 0.46). This difference directly affects the derived biometric measurements. The angle of progression (AoP) relies primarily on the well-defined PS contour, explaining its relatively higher reliability. Conversely, the head-symphysis distance (HSD) depends on accurate localization of both structures, making it more susceptible to errors in FH segmentation. Thus, the moderate agreement in FH segmentation emerges as the primary factor limiting HSD measurement consistency. Overall, classification metrics and PS segmentations showed strong-to-excellent reliability (ICC > 0.7), while FH segmentation and ultrasound measurements showed moderate agreement (ICC = 0.4–0.6). No negative values were present in AoP or HSD measurements, as both are positive physical quantities.

The high consistency among annotators, as evidenced in Fig. 3 and Table 3, validates the reliability of the manual annotations used as the consensus standard in this study. Following the reliability analysis, the annotators collaboratively reviewed all cases to resolve discrepancies and produce a single, cross-validated set of manual annotations. This final consensus was subsequently used as the ground truth for all downstream models.

Usage Notes

In order to make it easier for researchers to use this dataset, we provide a baseline method for achieving end-to-end automated ultrasound parameter measurement using the dataset (https://github.com/maskoffs/IUGC-2024-Submit/tree/master/baseline). The foundational architecture operates as a dual-output system, concurrently generating both segmentation and classification results. The segmentation task employs a conventional UNet structure, whereas the classification module utilizes feature representations derived from the UNet’s encoding pathway, which are then processed by a classification head.

The segmentation network is optimized with a hybrid Cross-Entropy and Dice loss, whereas the classification module relies solely on Cross-Entropy. This protocol comprises two stages: the segmentation network is first trained autonomously, after which the classification network is trained with the UNet parameters frozen. The dataset was partitioned into training and validation sets in an approximate 4:1 ratio at the patient level. This ensured that all data from any individual patient were contained within a single split, preventing data leakage. For the segmentation task, the model was trained on single frames from the patient-level training split, using only frames with segmentation labels. Optimal segmentation weights were determined by maximizing the average DSC on the patient-level validation set for PS and FH structures. For segmentation and biometry tasks, only anatomically plausible augmentations were applied, including random rotation within a limited range ( ± 10°), scaling (0.9–1.1×), and translation. Vertical flipping was explicitly excluded for these tasks to preserve the critical spatial relationships and anatomical consistency required for accurate segmentation mask generation and biometric measurement derivation. Data augmentation for segmentation includes random vertical flipping, rotation, and normalization of non-zero pixel regions. The model optimization employs AdamW with the following hyperparameters: initial learning rate 1e-4, momentum coefficients β₁ = 0.9 and β₂ = 0.999, L2 regularization strength of 0.1. Training proceeds for 80 epochs with mini-batches of 4 samples.

For the classification task, positive and negative samples were allocated to the training and validation sets following the same patient-level partitioning scheme. Data augmentation includes vertical flipping (probability 0.5) and non-zero pixel normalization. For the classification task, data augmentation included vertical flipping (probability 0.5). This transformation was specifically employed to enhance model invariance to potential variations in probe orientation during clinical examination, where the ultrasound transducer might be rotated during acquisition. While this creates anatomically atypical views, it improves the model’s robustness to such technical variations in real-world settings. Optimal classification weights are determined by maximizing accuracy (ACC) on the validation set. The classification network uses the same AdamW hyperparameters but with a batch size of 8. During inference, a single frame is first classified. Frames deemed to deviate from established measurement criteria (i.e., low-confidence classifications) are skipped, while others are processed by the segmentation pipeline. While this strategy enhances efficiency, it may introduce selection bias by excluding challenging frames, potentially inflating the reported segmentation performance. Future work will therefore focus on developing more robust methods to process these difficult cases rather than excluding them. After obtaining the segmentation results, we derive AoP and HSD through the following procedures. HSD measurement involves identifying two key landmarks on the segmented image: first, we locate the two farthest points on the PS contour, designating the rightmost point in the image as the first reference point for HSD. Next, using this rightmost point as a benchmark, we find the point on the PS contour that is closest to the FH contour, which serves as the second reference point. The linear distance between these two points constitutes the HSD. For AoP measurement, we construct two lines using three landmarks: starting with the two farthest points on the PS contour, we then draw a tangent line through the rightmost point of the PS to define the boundary of the FH region. The intersection of this right-side tangent line with the FH contour establishes the third landmark. The angle formed by these three points—two on the PS and one on the FH boundary—comprises the AoP.

Potential limitations

The automated measurements of head-symphysis distance (HSD) and angle of progression (AoP) are geometrically derived from specific anatomical landmarks, as illustrated in the Fig. 2. The HSD is the distance between Point 2 and 3, and the AoP can directly calculated based on the coordinates of Point 1, 2 and 4. Consequently, the precision of HSD and AoP is inherently tied to the consistent identification of these points. Minor variations in their selection, which can occur even in manual annotation due to image clarity, may lead to corresponding minor variations in the final biometric values.

The inter-rater reliability assessment was conducted with three expert annotators. While this sample size is a common practical constraint in expert-driven medical image analysis, the strong statistical agreement observed across all tasks provides confidence in the consistency of the annotations. Nevertheless, future work aimed at broader clinical validation would benefit from involving a larger and more diverse group of clinical raters to further generalize these reliability estimates.

A key limitation is the moderate inter-rater reliability for fetal head segmentation and biometry, which inherently constrains the performance ceiling for models trained on this data. First, the inter-rater reliability analysis revealed moderate agreement (ICC < 0.6) for the segmentation of the fetal head (FH) and for the ultrasound-derived biometry measurements (ΔAoP and ΔHSD). This level of annotator disagreement must be acknowledged as it directly impacts the model training and evaluation process. During training, the model is optimized against a ‘noisy’ ground truth for these structures, which may hinder its ability to learn their precise boundaries and biometry. Consequently, during evaluation, the model’s performance on FH-related tasks is benchmarked against a standard that has inherent variability, potentially placing an upper limit on the achievable metrics like Dice score for segmentation or accuracy for biometry. Future work would benefit from more extensive training for annotators on these challenging structures or the implementation of an iterative consensus-building process to refine the ground truth for FH and biometry, thereby providing a more stable target for automation.

Data Availability Statement

The dataset generated and analyzed in this study, titled “Maternal-Fetal Ultrasound Video Dataset for End-to-end Intrapartum Biometry and Multi-task Learning (Version V3)”, is publicly available on Zenodo repository at 10.5281/zenodo.17655183. The dataset can be accessed freely for research purposes, and its use should comply with the terms specified in the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. The complete dataset, including all segmentation masks, landmark coordinate files (landmarks.json), and associated videos, has been compiled into a single archive (17655183.zip) for convenient access and verification. This archive is provided as Supplementary Material. To ensure data integrity, the SHA-256 checksum of the archive is: 0D14E96DF712666B6324780AB6C7BFB3BF87DAB0D72DBF77AD3CD636012A0A1E.

The code used to generate the dataset from raw clinical videos is not publicly available due to institutional data governance policies that protect patient privacy and clinical data processing workflows.

However, The custom code used to calculate the Angle of Progression (AoP) is based on the publicly available IUGC 2024 evaluation scripts cal_aop.py and ellipse.py, accessible at: https://github.com/maskoffs/IUGC2024/tree/main/eval.
