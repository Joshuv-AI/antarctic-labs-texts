Clarifying fasting-based dietary strategies: toward alignment with emerging consensus terminology
Authors: Salah Mhamdi, Helmi Ben Saad, Amine Ghram
Published: 2025

Introduction

Fasting-based dietary strategies, particularly those categorized as intermittent fasting, have garnered significant attention in both research and clinical practice [1]. These interventions, ranging from time-restricted eating to alternate-day fasting, have demonstrated potential health benefits in managing type 2 diabetes mellitus, reducing cardiovascular risk, and biological aging [2,3]. In addition, fasting-based dietary strategies offer a cost-effective and accessible approach to improve cardio-metabolic health [2,3]. However, the literature is characterized by inconsistent terminology [4]. Researchers, clinicians, and even guideline developers frequently use terms such as intermittent fasting, time-restricted eating, and alternate-day fasting interchangeably, despite key differences in protocols, physiological targets, and clinical applications [5,6]. The lack of standardization is further complicated by growing media and public interest in fasting practices, which has amplified definitional confusion and carries the potential for misapplication or even harmful implementation in practice [4]. Individuals often adopt fasting strategies based on popular media, without adequate understanding of the critical implications of fasting durations, caloric intake, or intended physiological outcomes [5], raising concerns about safety and efficacy.

In this opinion piece, we aimed to address the ongoing confusion surrounding fasting-related terms and call for alignment with the 2024 international consensus on standardized definitions [4]. Additionally, we support an earlier update of this framework, well before the five-year timeline suggested by the consensus panel, due to the urgency and increasing importance of this field. By establishing a unified terminology, the scientific and medical communities could enhance communication, increase reproducibility, and ensure the safer and more effective translation of fasting interventions into research and clinical practice.

Terminological inconsistencies in the literature

As of late July 2025, the literature reveals pervasive inconsistencies in the naming and classification of fasting modalities [4]. A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis [7] used time-restricted feeding and time-restricted eating interchangeably. Time-restricted feeding typically refers to animal models and time-restricted eating is generally applied to human trials [8]. This distinction is critical, as differences in timing alignment (e.g.; nocturnal feeding in rodents versus daytime eating in humans) may significantly influence some physiological outcomes related to peripheral clock, de novo lipid synthesis [9,10]. One example of definitional overlap was noted in the study by Pellegrini et al. [7], where search strategy included overlapping and ambiguous terms such as ‘time restricted feeding’, ‘time restricted eating’, ‘time restricted fasting’, ‘time restricted diet’, and ‘time restricted meal’, without clear justification for their use. This terminological ambiguity and variability may complicate literature synthesis, obscure meaningful comparisons across studies, contribute to the lack of clarity in clinical translation, and lead to erroneous conclusions. An additional example of definitional overlap can be seen in the review by de Cabo and Mattson [2], which referenced a 1997 definition of caloric restriction when discussing the effects of intermittent fasting, implicitly treating caloric restriction and intermittent fasting as synonymous [11]. These dietary interventions differ in terms of energy intake patterns, metabolic effects, and underlying mechanisms [11]. Caloric restriction refers to a chronic reduction in daily caloric intake [4]. Failing to differentiate these distinct approaches could lead to misinterpretations of mechanistic pathways and clinical effects [11]. These inconsistencies underscore the urgent need for standardized terminology in fasting research, accurate clinical translation, and clear public communication regarding fasting-based dietary strategies. In the literature, we identified several examples of confusing or inconsistent terminology about intermittent energy restriction [12]. Common types of intermittent energy restriction diets include alternate-day fasting, 5:2 diet, week-on-week-off diet, and time-restricted feeding [12]. In this context, intermittent energy restriction is used as an umbrella term encompassing both frequency-based strategies (e.g.; alternate-day fasting, 5:2 diet, week-on-week-off diet) and timing-based strategies (e.g.; time-restricted feeding), thereby conflating distinct approaches. Furthermore, Wei et al. [12] used the term time-restricted feeding to refer to human trials, where time-restricted eating would be more appropriate. Such interchangeable usage reflects a broader trend of terminological inconsistency among researchers [12].

Terminological inconsistencies in fasting-related research undermine reproducibility, complicate cross-study comparisons, and hinder the development of accurate clinical guidelines. Clear, standardized definitions are essential to ensure scientific rigor, facilitate translational clarity, and prevent misapplication of fasting protocols. Unintended caloric intake during fasting periods could compromise intended physiological outcomes and lead to erroneous conclusions. Establishing a universally adopted terminology is necessary for the future advancement of this field, which may jeopardize intervention efficacy and patient safety. Ambiguity in fasting-related terminology leads to inconsistent study comparisons, complicates data synthesis, and obscures critical distinctions such as dose-response relationships. Time-restricted eating and alternate-day fasting are often inaccurately grouped under the broad term of intermittent fasting [13]. This conflation blurs critical differences between frequency- and circadian-based interventions. For the public, unclear language may lead to the adoption of inappropriate fasting regimens, potentially resulting in suboptimal outcomes or poor adherence. Imagine someone hoping to trigger autophagy (i.e., a cellular cleaning process) through fasting, but he/she mistakenly choose a short-duration fast that is not long enough to achieve that effect. This kind of confusion can lead to people choosing the wrong fasting method for their goals, potentially resulting in suboptimal health outcomes or simply giving up on fasting altogether because it does not seem to work for them.

ii)Collapsing diverse fasting durations into one category: Certain terms, such as prolonged fasting and extended fasting remain ambiguously defined. Additionally, grouping fasting from four days to weeks under one term (i.e.; prolonged fasting) ignores metabolic tipping at three points [13]: 1) Autophagy peaks at 72 h, 2) Muscle catabolism accelerates after 96 h, and 3) Refeeding syndrome escalates beyond five days. Despite these limitations, the 2024 consensus [4] remains a landmark effort in fostering consistency within a rapidly evolving field. However, its practical impact depends on continued refinement. A second iteration should aim to could streamline terminology, anchor definitions to physiological and clinical mechanisms (e.g.; metabolic switching, autophagy, circadian alignment), and prioritize translational clarity to improve implementation in both research and clinical contexts. By aligning definitions with functional outcomes, such as improvements in metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular health, neuroprotection, and even cancer prevention, the field can move toward more effective, evidence-based fasting interventions.

Call for action and future refinement

Adoption of the 2024 consensus terminology [4] by journals, researchers, clinicians, and health educators is strongly encouraged to enhance standardization and improve the quality of communication and evidence synthesis. This alignment will reduce confusion, enhance study comparability, and provide better guidance for patients. However, we also recognize that the 2024 consensus [4] is not a final solution. We propose a second round of refinement to address remaining ambiguities and explore a taxonomy based on physiological mechanisms. A classification system focused on specific biological outcomes, such as autophagy, metabolic flexibility, or longevity, could help tailor fasting regimens to clinical goals more effectively. Although expert panels have updated guidelines on fasting therapies [17], limited progress has been observed in their implementation. Therefore, we believe it is essential to revisit and refine the existing consensus [4] to facilitate meaningful change.

Conclusion

The field of fasting-based strategies stands at a pivotal moment. As scientific interest grows and public adoption accelerates, a unified language is essential. The 2024 consensus [4] has laid critical groundwork, but further refinement is needed to ensure clarity, precision, and utility. We urge journals, guideline committees, and public health communicators to adopt and help evolve this shared terminology, thereby paving the way for evidence-based implementation and future innovation.

Take-home message: Although the health advantages of fasting-based dietary modalities seem promising, scientific advancement, clinical application, and public comprehension are hampered by the inconsistent and overlapping nomenclature used. Although the 2024 international consensus offers a useful starting point for standardized definitions, more work is necessary to guarantee clarity, enhance reproducibility, and direct the safe, efficient application of fasting interventions. This work must be anchored in physiological mechanisms and clinical objectives.
