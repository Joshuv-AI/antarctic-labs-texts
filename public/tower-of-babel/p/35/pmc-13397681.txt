Decisional Conflict Among Adolescents and Young Adults Before and After Facilitated Decision-Making About Learning Personal Genomic Information
Authors: Jennifer S Duann, Lisa J Martin, William B Brinkman, Cynthia A Prows, Michelle L McGowan, Melanie F Myers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:To determine if decisional conflict differed between adolescent (ages 13–17) and young adult (ages 18–21) research participants making decisions about learning actual genomic testing results, and whether decisional conflict changed after a facilitated decision-making (FDM) session.Methods:Participants between the ages of 13–21 (n = 483) completed decisional conflict scale (DCS) surveys after using an electronic decision tool to make independent choices about learning genomic testing results (T1) and again following FDM with a study team member and parent if one was involved (T2). Participants had the option to change their choices at T2. We compared DCS scores of adolescents and young adults at T1 and T2.Results:Median total DCS scores were significantly higher for adolescents than young adults at T1 (20.3 vs. 7.8; p < .0001) and T2 (11.7 vs. 1.6; p < .0001). There were significant decreases in median DCS scores from T1 to T2 among adolescents (20.3–11.7; p < .0001) and young adults (7.8–1.6; p < .0001).Discussion:Although DCS scores were low overall, adolescents experienced higher decisional conflict than young adults. Decisional conflict levels decreased among both cohorts after FDM. Researchers and clinicians should facilitate discussions to engage adolescents and young adults in values-centric informed decisions about genomic testing.Keywords: Genomic testing, Decisional conflict, Facilitated decision-making, Return of results, Adolescent, Young adult

Genomic testing for children has historically been limited to diagnostic purposes unless predictive testing results are actionable in childhood [1]. This stance is aligned with the pediatric bioethical imperative to preserve the rights of children to an open future [2,3]. However, reflecting the proliferation of predictive genetic testing, the American College of Medical Genetics recommends allowing parents to opt in or out of learning secondary findings from clinical exome or genome sequencing for their minor children [4] and advises engaging adolescents in genomic testing decisions, given adolescents’ increasing maturity along the trajectory toward independent medical decision-making [5]. Best practices for engaging adolescents in such decisions are underexplored.

It is unclear whether and how decision-making changes upon reaching the age of majority when young adults in the United States gain the legal right to give or withhold consent for personal genomic testing. Earlier research into medical decision-making suggests that the cognitive components of decision-making are in place by age 13, with the abstract thinking, risk and benefit weighing, and mentalizing among the last to develop [6]. Socioemotional development occurs at a different pace from cognitive development, which may also affect how adolescents and young adults weigh risks and benefits in medical decision-making [7], including those associated with learning personal genomic test results. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that clinicians look for evidence of stable, internalized values in adolescent medical decision-making [8].

A shared decision-making model with a facilitator can bridge the gap between adolescent desire for full decision-making power and the reality of parents or guardians having the legal authority to make health-related decisions for their minor children [9]. When given the chance to consult their parents on which genomic testing results to receive, adolescents typically found shared decision-making to be a helpful process [10]. Shared decision-making has also been found to decrease decisional conflict [11].

Decisional conflict is a state of uncertainty experienced when feeling uninformed about the alternatives, benefits and risks, unclear about personal values, or unsupported in making a choice [12]. Over the past 30 years, hundreds of studies [13] have used a validated scale to measure decision conflict. Decisional conflict scale (DCS) scores [12] below 25 have been associated with implementing decisions [13], while elevated decisional conflict was associated with postponing decisions, anxiety, changing one’s mind, and decisional regret [14–16]. Although decisional conflict may resolve over time without intervention, decision support interventions can decrease DCS scores [12] and prolong the effect of decreased decisional conflict [13]. This prolonged effect of decision support intervention is especially relevant in the context of genetic testing, where there is a time lag of weeks between genetic sample collection and return of results during which patients might change their minds, experience anxiety, or regret their choice.

Previous research has shown that adolescents want to be involved in choosing which of their genomic testing results to learn [9,17,18]. However, there may be important differences between adolescent preferences and those of their parents. For example, in a site-specific Electronic Medical Records and Genomics (phase III) research study, adolescents were less likely than their parents to choose to learn all possible genomic testing results [19] and more likely to exclude unpreventable and adult-onset conditions [18,19] after independent decision making. Notably, adolescents also experienced significantly higher decisional conflict than their parents [20]. However, these previous studies did not explore whether adolescent decisional conflict decreased after facilitated decision-making (FDM) with their parents.

There is little research on the associations between decisional conflict and age or FDM. The primary aims of this study were to determine if decisional conflict among research participants making decisions about learning actual genomic testing results differed between adolescent and young adult participants, and whether decisional conflict changed after an FDM session. We hypothesized that decisional conflict levels would differ between assenting adolescents (13–17 years of age) and consenting young adults (18–21 years of age), and that decisional conflict among participants would change after FDM.

Materials and Methods

The Engaging Adolescents in Decisions About Return of Genomic Research Results study was approved by the Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC) Institutional Review Board, IRB #2020–0517. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment in the study.

Participant recruitment and study design

Participant recruitment and study design have been described in greater detail elsewhere [21]. Briefly, participants between the ages of 13–21 were recruited from the Greater Cincinnati community, CCHMC primary care clinics, and an institutional biobank between July 2021 and June 2024. Adolescents between the ages of 13–17 enrolled in the study with a parent or legal guardian. Young adults between the ages of 18–21 had the option of inviting a parent to participate. Participants were not eligible if they had a molecular diagnosis (except for carrier results), were followed in a genetics clinic, had an intellectual or developmental disability that prevented them from making independent decisions, or were a full sibling of another participant. Participants had to be proficient in English and reside within 100 miles of CCHMC. Quota sampling ensured similar distributions of participants by age and gender with no more than 50% of participants self-identifying as White, non-Hispanic, although sampling quotas did not aim to meet national population proportions for racial and ethnic identification [22].

Participants assented (13–17 year olds) with parental permission or consented (18–21 year olds) to participate in the study and completed demographic and baseline surveys [21]. Adolescents and young adult participants were separated from their parents (if involved) and used an electronic decision tool (https://demo.mygenechoices.com/client/) to make independent choices (time point 1, hereafter referred to as T1), about learning 4 categories of conditions for the participant: preventable, treatable, adult-onset, and carrier status. After making independent choices, participants completed the DCS [12].

Facilitated decision-making

After the DCS was completed at T1, participants joined an FDM session with the study facilitator and a parent if one was involved. Study facilitators included clinical research coordinators, genetic counseling graduate students, an advanced practice registered nurse, an ethicist, and a genetic counselor who were trained to facilitate the FDM using a standardized facilitator guide [10] (Appendix A). The facilitator prompted adolescent and young adult participants, followed by the parent (if involved), to discuss the reasons for their choices. If independent choices were discordant between an adolescent and their parent, both were asked what they thought of the other’s explanation and given the chance to ask each other questions. Participants then had the opportunity to ask questions, change, or confirm choices immediately after FDM (time point 2, hereafter referred to as T2). The DCS was again completed at T2. Participants who decided to learn at least one genomic result provided a saliva or blood sample for DNA collection and sequencing. DNA samples were shipped to the Broad Institute for sequencing and then to the Laboratory of Molecular Medicine for variant analysis based on participants’ choices.

Measures

Score on the DCS was our primary outcome. The DCS was validated among health science students, health employees at a teaching hospital, patients with cardiorespiratory disorders, and a random community sample of women (n = 909) [23]. The validated DCS includes 5 subscales [23].

Participants report their agreement with 16 statements on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (strongly agree) to 4 (strongly disagree). Raw scores are converted to a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating higher decisional conflict and more uncertainty about the decision the participant made [12]. Total DCS scores equally weight all 5 subscales. DCS scores 25 and higher indicate clinically significant decisional conflict (CSDC) [24].

Independent predictor variables of decisional conflict included participant age by year, age stratified by cohort (adolescent vs. young adult), and genetics knowledge. Genetics knowledge was measured by a 15-item quiz comprised of true/false statements covering content from the decision tool and embedded videos, the consent form, and understanding synthesized from content found in the decision tool. Knowledge scores were converted to a 0–100 scale. Genetics knowledge was measured before participants used the electronic decision tool and made independent choices (time point 0, hereafter referred to as T0) and at T2.

Statistical analysis

Non-normality of the DCS scores was confirmed through visual assessment and the Shapiro–Wilk test, so data are described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Data are presented for 2 cohorts: adolescents (under the age of 18) and young adults (aged 18 or older). In preparation for the primary analyses, we compared the DCS scores of young adults who did not involve a parent and those who did and found that the total DCS scores did not differ (Table S1). Because only a small proportion of young adults chose to involve a parent, we did not have sufficient power to analyze the 2 groups of young adults separately. Further analyses combined all young adults into one group. For consistency, all analyses were restricted to individuals who completed the DCS at both T1 and T2 time points.

Our primary analyses were to evaluate whether decisional conflict was associated with age (dichotomized into adolescent and young adult, aligning with reaching the legal age for independent decision-making) and whether decisional conflict decreased after facilitated decision making. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare total and subscale DCS scores between adolescents and young adults at T1 and T2. Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare whether DCS scores decreased among adolescents between T1 and T2 and among young adults between T1 and T2. We also explored whether DCS scores were associated with participants’ choices about which results to learn, whether participants changed their independent T1 decision at T2, participant gender, participant race (tested using Wilcoxon rank sum), and participant knowledge of genetics (tested with Spearman correlation). Decision changes were collapsed into the aggregate choices to “change” or “not change” between T1 and T2. Because very few participants chose to learn no genetic test results at T1 (n = 25) or T2 (n = 12), we collapsed the quantity of results learned into “all” and “some or none.” In addition, as differences in DCS scores may be influenced by other factors, we performed regression to test whether age (continuous), gender (woman/girl vs. man/boy), race (White vs. racial minority group), and genetics knowledge were jointly associated with DCS scores, evaluated by time point.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the software, JMP v. 18.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and p ≤ .05 was considered significant for primary and secondary analyses. For secondary analyses, p values are adjusted by the number of comparisons (2 time points, 5 predictors) yielding a Bonferroni corrected significance threshold p ≤ .005.

Changes in DCS scores between T1 and T2 among adolescent and young adult participants.

Within both cohorts, there was a statistically significant decrease in median total DCS scores at T2 after FDM (Table 3). For adolescents median total DCS scores decreased from 20.3 at T1 to 11.7 at T2 (p < .0001). Among young adults, median total DCS scores decreased from 7.8 at T1 to 1.6 at T2 (p < .0001) (Table 3).

Clinically significant decisional conflict: changes between T1 and T2.

CSDC occurred more often in adolescents than young adults, and adolescents were less likely to resolve CSDC after FDM (Figure S1). There were 114 adolescents with CSDC at T1 and of these, 34 adolescents (29.82%) no longer had CSDC scores at T2. Median DCS scores among these 34 decreased from 27.3 (IQR 26.6–34.8) to 12.5 (IQR 3.1–16). The remaining 80 participants who had CSDC at T1 continued to have CSDC at T2, though the median DCS scores for these 80 participants decreased from 34.4 (IQR 29.7–39.1) to 28.1 (IQR 25–33.2). There were 10 adolescents who reported CSDC at T2 but not at T1, with median DCS scores increasing from 16.4 (IQR 7.8–18.8) to 25 (25–32.8).

There were 53 young adults with CSDC at T1 and of these, 26 (49.06%) did not have CSDC at T2 (Figure S1). Median DCS scores in this latter group decreased from 29.7 (IQR 26.2–32.8) to 18.8 (IQR 7.8–20.7). The remaining 27 who had CSDC at T1 continued to have CSDC at T2 with a small decrease in median DCS scores from 29.7 (IQR 26.6–35.9) to 28.1 (IQR 25–34.4). Only 3 reported CSDC at T2 but not T1, with median DCS scores increasing from 14.1 (IQR 0–20.3) to 25 (IQR 25–31.3).

Decisional conflict and quantity of results learned.

Participants who chose to learn all genomic testing results had lower DCS scores than those who chose to learn some or no results. Specifically for adolescents choosing to learn all results, the median total DCS score at T1 was 14.06 (IQR 0–26.6; n = 162) compared to 28.13 (IQR 17.2–37.5; n = 101) for adolescents who chose to learn some or no results (p < .0001). For young adults, the median total DCS scores at T1 was 6.25 (IQR 0–21.9; n = 181) for those who chose to learn all results compared to 21.88 (IQR 7.8–31.3; n = 28) for those who chose to learn some or no results (p = .0002). There was a similar pattern at T2. Among adolescents choosing to learn all results at T2, the median total DCS score was 9.38 (IQR 0–25; n = 199) compared to 20.31 (IQR 8.6–26.6; n = 64; p = .0030) for adolescents who chose to learn some or no results. Among young adults, total median DCS scores at T2 were 1.6 (IQR 0–12.5; n = 178) and 18.75 (IQR 3.1–28.1; n = 31; p < .0001) for those choosing to learn all compared to some or no results, respectively.

Decisional conflict and decision change.

A higher percentage of participants who changed their choices had CSDC at T2 (Figure 1). Higher decisional conflict at T1 was linked to decision changes at T2 across cohorts. Adolescents who changed their decisions at T2 had a median total DCS score at T1 of 28.1 (IQR 18.8–37.5; n = 93), while those who maintained their decisions at T2 had a median total DCS score at T1 of 14.1 (IQR 0.8–26.6; n = 169; p < .0001). At T2, the median total DCS score for adolescents who changed their initial decisions was 25 (IQR 9.4–28.1; n = 93) and the median total DCS score for adolescents who maintained their initial decisions was 6.3 (IQR 0–21.9; n = 169; p < .0001) (data not shown).

The median total DCS score at T1 for young adults who changed their decision at T2 was 27.3 (IQR 21.5–32.8), while the median total DCS scores at T1 for young adults who maintained their initial decisions was 6.3 (IQR 0–21.9; p < .0001). At T2, the median total DCS score for young adults who changed their initial decisions at T2 was 19.5 (IQR 7.4–30.1; n = 22) and the median total DCS score for young adults who maintained their initial decisions at T2 was 1.6 (IQR 0–12.5; n = 187; p < .0001) (data not shown).

Decisional conflict, genetics knowledge, and participant characteristics.

There was a weak negative but significant correlation between decisional conflict and knowledge scores at baseline prior to using the electronic decision tool (T0) (−0.30, p < .0001) and at T2 after FDM (−0.29, p < .0001). Knowledge scores were significantly higher at T2 for both cohorts, although young adults had higher scores than adolescents (Table S2). When considering a multivariable regression model, including age (continuous), gender (woman/girl vs. man/boy), race (White vs. racial minority group), and knowledge as predictors and DCS as the outcome, we found that both age and knowledge remained significant at both T1 and T2 (Table S3).

Discussion

Decisional conflict is common in medical decision-making and may reflect a typical decision-making process [13]. Decisional conflict is typically resolved when individuals make a well-informed decision that aligns with their preferences [13]. When decision support interventions are used, intervention effectiveness needs to be measured by outcomes other than patient choice, as the patient’s final decision may be affected by many factors (e.g., perceived utility, provider recommendation, cost, etc.) even if decision support improves the quality of the decision-making process [25]. The DCS is a validated outcome measure when evaluating decision aid effectiveness [25] and assessing decision-making needs [26], so we used DCS scores as the primary outcome for this study. We evaluated DCS scores before and after FDM among adolescent and young adult research participants making decisions about whether to undergo and learn the results of personal genomic testing without clinical indication. We found differences in DCS scores associated with age, timing in relation to FDM, decision change, and quantity of results to learn.

We found that DCS scores tended to be low overall, but were significantly higher for adolescents than young adults, both before and immediately after FDM time points. Young adults may have more experience in autonomous health-related decision-making, greater developmental maturity, or more exposure to genetics concepts than younger adolescents, as reflected in the higher genetics knowledge scores of the young adult cohort. The lower decisional conflict experienced by young adults in comparison to adolescents may also reflect self-selection bias, as adolescents may have been influenced by a parent’s desire to enroll in a genomic decision-making study. The DCS scores of adolescent and young adult participants gradually decrease with age, rather than a distinct threshold effect at age 18. This pattern suggests that decision-making about genetic testing may not always need to be delayed until young people reach the age of majority [1].

Both adolescent and young adult cohorts had decreased DCS scores after FDM. This finding aligns with previous studies showing reduction in decisional conflict among adult and adolescent cardiac patients following shared decision-making with health-care providers [27] and adolescents’ perceived helpfulness of joint decision-making with parents [10]. Genetics knowledge scores among participants increased following FDM, suggesting that the FDM process may have provided new information about options, which can help adolescents and young adults feel more confident that they are making an informed decision that aligns with their values. Additionally, allowing participants the opportunity to revisit their decision as part of FDM could have contributed to decreased decisional conflict. Unexpectedly, a few participants experienced an increase in decisional conflict after FDM. While this proportion was small, it is important to understand why decisional conflict might increase. Some studies have shown that decisional conflict can increase after new information about options or values are presented, although this increase may be temporary [13]. Parental persuasion or the need to reduce parental or personal anxiety may also have influenced participant decisions [21]. Our findings support American College of Medical Genetics’ recommendation to inform adolescents and young adults about the goals and implications of genomic testing and explore its personal benefits and limitations for each patient [5].

In our study, CSDC occurred more often among participants who did not choose to learn “all” results at T1. This is consistent with Pillai’s findings that those with DCS scores above the clinically significant threshold of 25 were less likely to choose to learn “all” personal genomic test results [20]. Higher DCS scores may indicate that participants felt less confident about dealing with genetic test results and preferred to learn only actionable results, such as preventable or treatable conditions [20]. CSDC also occurred more often in participants who made decision changes at T2, which supports previous research showing that higher decisional conflict is associated with decisional delay [14,15]. Decision changes at T2 may reflect lingering uncertainty even after FDM since participants who made decision changes were more likely to retain CSDC from T1 to T2. This finding supports the American Academy of Pediatrics’ recommendation that clinicians and parents look for stability in values as a sign of young people’s mature decision-making capabilities [8].

In all age groups, decisional conflict decreased after FDM with and without parental involvement. Our findings suggest that parents should not make unilateral decisions about genomic testing on behalf of their adolescent children, but rather that parents and providers should facilitate discussions with adolescents and young adults to support confident and informed decision-making. FDM may be especially important for younger adolescents as our findings suggest they experienced higher decisional conflict than older adolescents and young adults. In this study, higher decisional conflict was associated with choosing to learn fewer results. This association aligns with previous findings that parents experienced lower decisional conflict than their children [20] and the broader literature showing that those making decisions for others have lower DCS than those making decisions for self [13,24]. Higher decisional conflict, such as that experienced by adolescents in this study, has also been linked to decision postponing and decision regret [14–16], which suggests that, at minimum, parents and providers should seek adolescents’ preferences and assent when they express a desire to learn less information than their parents. More radically, if not necessary to inform medical decision-making in childhood, personal genomic decision-making may be deferred until the adolescent can decide autonomously, as has been suggested as a strategy to promote a child’s right to an open future [3]. FDM can help preserve an adolescent’s right to not learn more than they want to before they are able to decide for themselves.

Limitations

Although quota sampling ensured proportionate distributions of age and gender and a cohort where no more than 50% of the study population identified as White, non-Hispanic [22], the population for this study remains a convenience sample and may not be generalizable to all adolescents and young adults making decisions about genomic testing. Participants enrolled without clinical indication for genomic testing, so these results may not reflect the decisional conflict experienced by patients for whom the implications of testing may differ. Decisional conflict was low overall, which could be related to the process of allowing decision changes. In a clinical setting, there may not be a built-in opportunity for adolescents to change their decisions regarding genomic testing. Our study is also unique in allowing participants to make choices about which specific results to learn. In a clinical setting, adult patients and parents of minors opt in or out of all secondary findings [4], so decisional conflict may differ from what was seen in a research setting. In addition, enrolling in a research study may indicate a higher baseline level of information-seeking, which may manifest as less uncertainty about learning results. The study was limited to English speakers and differences in decisional conflict have been reported to vary by language spoken [28], so the results may be less generalizable to non-English speakers. While no significant difference in total DCS scores was found between young adults who participated with and without a parent, too few young adults chose to involve parents in decision-making to allow for sufficiently powered analysis between young adults who did and did not involve a parent.

Implications for future research

Future studies may examine decisional conflict before and after FDM in a clinical setting where genomic test results may have different implications for medical management. Future studies may also explore whether there are associations between decisional conflict and choosing to learn specific result types (e.g., adult-onset conditions or preventable conditions), as well as the impact of learning results.

Conclusion

This study provides a measure of decisional conflict in adolescents and young adults before and after FDM about whether to learn personal genomic test results in a research context. As the use of genomic testing increases in research and clinical settings, engaging adolescents in FDM could help reduce decisional conflict as they choose which genomic test results to learn. The results of this study suggest that younger adolescents experience more decisional conflict but FDM can reduce decisional conflict even among those with higher initial levels. Higher levels of decisional conflict in adolescents signal an opportunity to improve decision-making facilitation so that adolescents can be involved in making satisfactory choices about genomic testing alongside their parents and legal guardians.

IMPLICATIONS AND CONTRIBUTION

Among adolescents and young adults making decisions about learning personal results of genomic testing, decisional conflict decreased after facilitated decision-making with and without parental involvement. As the use of genomic testing increases in research and clinical settings, facilitated decision-making could be an effective decision support intervention for adolescents and young adults choosing which genomic test results to learn.
