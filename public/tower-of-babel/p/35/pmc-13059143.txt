Compliance with outpatient nebulization inhalation therapy in preschool children: implication for nursing care
Authors: Ying Bi, Zhifang Song, Jufang Zhang, Yinhua Chen, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNebulization inhalation therapy is widely utilized in pediatric clinical practice, and treatment compliance plays a critical role in determining clinical outcomes. This study aimed to assess the compliance of preschool children with outpatient nebulization inhalation therapy, with the goal of providing actionable insights for clinical nursing practice.MethodsPreschool children who had received nebulization inhalation therapy in the pediatric outpatient department of our hospital from February 2025 to July 2025 were included. Selfe designed Preschool Children’s Outpatient Nebulization Inhalation Therapy Compliance Scale were used for survey. Clinical trial number: not applicable.ResultsA total of 528 preschool children undergoing nebulization inhalation therapy were included in the study. The overall mean score for nebulization therapy compliance was (51.36 ± 9.82), with only 142 cases (26.9%) demonstrating high compliance. The scores across the four dimensions, ranked in descending order, were: treatment cognition and attitude, treatment persistence, treatment behavior norms, and treatment cooperation. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that being 5-<6 years old (P = 0.001, OR = 2.28, 95%CI = 1.45–3.58), having guardians with good cognition of nebulization therapy (P = 0.001, OR = 2.81, 95%CI = 1.72–4.59), experiencing a comfortable treatment environment (P = 0.002, OR = 2.21, 95%CI = 1.34–3.65), and receiving good doctor-patient communication (P = 0.005, OR = 1.98, 95%CI = 1.22–3.22) were independent protective factors for high compliance (all P < 0.05).ConclusionPreschool children receiving outpatient nebulization inhalation therapy exhibit moderately low treatment compliance, a phenomenon shaped by multiple interrelated factors such as chronological age, guardians’ understanding of nebulization therapy, and ambient treatment conditions. While nursing interventions involving individualized psychological support, caregiver education initiatives, and environmental adjustments hold potential to enhance compliance, their efficacy requires further verification through dedicated interventional studies to ensure reliable therapeutic effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04493-3.Keywords: Compliance, Nebulization, Preschool children, Nursing, Care, Pediatric, Clinical

Introduction

Nebulization inhalation therapy occupies a central role in the management of pediatric respiratory diseases due to its distinctive pharmacological advantages. By dispersing medications into fine aerosol particles via a nebulizer, this modality enables direct drug deposition on the respiratory mucosa, achieving high local concentrations while minimizing systemic adverse effects [1]. These characteristics render it particularly suitable for preschool children (aged 3–6 years), whose hepatic and renal functions are not yet fully mature [2]. The clinical importance of nebulization therapy is underscored by the substantial and growing burden of pediatric respiratory conditions worldwide. According to the Global Asthma Report 2022, approximately 339 million individuals are affected by asthma globally, with prevalence rates among children ranging from 5% to 20% across different regions [3, 4]. In China, the prevalence of childhood asthma has increased by more than 50% over the past two decades, reaching 3.3% in children aged 0–14 years, with notably higher rates in urban centers. This rising disease burden has been accompanied by increased utilization of nebulization therapy, which remains the cornerstone of acute asthma management and is increasingly employed for other respiratory conditions such as bronchiolitis and pneumonia. Indeed, the utilization rate of nebulization inhalation therapy during acute asthma exacerbations reaches 92.3%, while its application in common respiratory diseases including bronchitis and pneumonia exceeds 75%, establishing it as a preferred administration route in clinical practice [5].

The efficacy of nebulization therapy in real-world settings, however, depends critically on treatment adherence. Recent multicenter studies have demonstrated that appropriate nebulization techniques can reduce hospitalization rates by 30–40% in pediatric asthma exacerbations [6–8], highlighting the clinical significance of proper treatment delivery. Conversely, studies [9, 10] have emphasized that the real-world effectiveness of nebulization is frequently compromised by poor compliance, particularly among younger children who lack the cognitive capacity to comprehend treatment necessity. This challenge is especially pronounced in preschool children, who are in a distinct phase of physical and psychological development. Their cognitive functioning remains largely concrete and image-based, their understanding of medical procedures is limited, and they often exhibit innate fear responses to unfamiliar environments and equipment [11].

Despite the clinical significance of this issue, existing research on pediatric nebulization compliance has predominantly focused on school-aged children or specific disease populations such as asthma. Systematic investigations targeting preschool children in outpatient settings remain limited by three critical gaps: the absence of standardized compliance assessment tools capable of robust quantitative comparison, a predominant focus on single-factor analyses that fail to capture complex interactions among multiple determinants, and the proposal of nursing interventions lacking specificity and empirical validation. Against this backdrop, the present study enrolled preschool children undergoing outpatient nebulization inhalation therapy at our institution to evaluate current compliance levels using a psychometrically validated instrument and to identify key influencing factors through multifactor analytical approaches. By elucidating the determinants of treatment adherence in this vulnerable population, we aim to inform the development of targeted nursing strategies and ultimately enhance the quality of pediatric nebulization therapy delivery.

Methods

Design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to investigate the compliance of preschool children with outpatient nebulization inhalation therapy and identify its influencing factors. Data collection was conducted between February 2025 and July 2025 in the pediatric outpatient department of a tertiary children’s hospital, where eligible participants receiving nebulization treatment for respiratory diseases were consecutively recruited. The sample size was calculated based on a preliminary pre-survey with a compliance rate of 27%, setting α = 0.05, β = 0.2, and a 5% tolerance error; a final sample size of 528 was determined after accounting for a 10% non-response rate (to account for potential incomplete questionnaires or refusal to complete the survey after consent) to ensure statistical power. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Study population

Inclusion criteria: (1) Aged 3 ~ 6 years (calculated based on date of birth, accurate to the month); (2) Clinically diagnosed with respiratory diseases requiring nebulization inhalation therapy such as acute bronchitis, bronchiolitis, bronchopneumonia, and acute attack of bronchial asthma, and conforming to the relevant diagnostic criteria; (3) Having received nebulization inhalation therapy in our hospital for the first time (no previous similar treatment in our hospital or other medical institutions); (4) The child’s guardian had basic reading and comprehension skills and could cooperate in completing the questionnaire survey and scale assessment; (5) Voluntarily participated in this study and signed the informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Complicated with severe organic diseases such as congenital heart disease, chronic kidney disease, and liver failure; (2) Having cognitive or behavioral abnormalities such as mental retardation and autism spectrum disorder; (3) Occurrence of severe allergic reactions, aggravated dyspnea, or other situations requiring termination of treatment during nebulization therapy; (4) Transfer to inpatient treatment or loss to follow-up due to changes in the condition during the study period.

Research tools

We collected factors that may affect compliance, including general information of children (age, gender, disease type, etc.), relevant information of parents (educational level, cognitive level of nebulization therapy, etc.), and medical environment factors (treatment environment, doctor-patient communication, etc.). Among these factors, the nurse-patient communication level was assessed using a self-designed single-item questionnaire embedded in the general information collection module, rather than being a sub-dimension of the compliance scale. This questionnaire asked guardians to rate the quality of communication between medical staff and their family as “Good” or “Fair” based on their actual experience during the nebulization treatment process.

We conducted the survey using the self-designed Preschool Children’s Outpatient Nebulization Inhalation Therapy Compliance Scale (supplementary file 1). This scale was independently developed by the researchers based on social cognitive theory and pediatric nursing practice, and has gone through 3 rounds of revisions. The scale development process is as follows: (1) 28 potential items were selected through literature review; (2) Three associate chief nurses with more than 10 years of pediatric nursing experience conducted content validity evaluation. These three experts were specifically selected for their specialized background in pediatric respiratory nursing and their proven experience in developing multiple nursing scales, thereby ensuring sufficient professional expertise to evaluate the content validity of the scale targeting preschool children’s nebulization compliance. Items with content validity ratio (CVR) < 0.78 were deleted, finally retaining 20 items; (3) A pre-survey was conducted on 100 preschool children, and item discrimination was further tested through item analysis (critical ratio method and correlation coefficient method). The results showed that all items had a CR value > 3.0, and the correlation coefficient with the total score was > 0.4, indicating good item quality; (4) Reliability test showed that the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.86, the Cronbach’s α coefficients of each dimension were between 0.78 and 0.83, and the test-retest reliability (with a 2-week interval) was 0.82, suggesting that the scale has good internal consistency and stability.

The scale includes 4 dimensions:

Scoring method: Each item is scored on a 1–4 scale, where 1 point indicates “complete non-compliance” (such as always refusing to wear a mask), 2 points indicate “partial non-compliance” (such as wearing after persuasion but frequently removing it), 3 points indicate “partial compliance” (such as occasional resistance but able to cooperate to complete), and 4 points indicate “complete compliance” (such as active cooperation without resistance). The total score ranges from 20 to 80 points. To classify compliance levels, we first used ROC curve analysis to determine the optimal cut-off point for distinguishing high compliance from moderate/low compliance. The ROC analysis yielded an optimal cut-off score of 61 (sensitivity = 0.82, specificity = 0.79, AUC = 0.87). Based on this empirically derived threshold, scores ≥ 61 were classified as high compliance. For descriptive purposes, the remaining score range (20–60) was further divided into moderate compliance (41–60) and low compliance (20–40) based on clinical consensus and distribution characteristics, ensuring that the primary classification (high vs. non-high) remained anchored in empirical ROC analysis.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted based on the Helsinki Declaration. This study had been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Ethics No.: 202512025-1). Written informed consents had been obtained from all the included guardians of children.

Procedure

The recruitment of research objects was conducted by two pediatric nurses who had received standardized training in research protocols and pediatric communication skills. Prior to the child’s initial nebulization therapy session, these nurses scheduled individual meetings with the guardians in a dedicated consultation room—an environment designed to minimize distractions and ensure privacy. During these interactions, they employed a structured yet conversational approach to explain the study’s objectives, emphasizing its focus on improving pediatric nebulization experiences; detailed the methodology, including the timeline for questionnaire completion and scale assessments; and explicitly outlined potential risks (such as minimal time investment) and benefits (including personalized feedback on their child’s treatment cooperation). To enhance comprehension, the nurses used simplified language supplemented with visual aids, such as flowcharts illustrating the research process, and encouraged guardians to ask questions, addressing each query with evidence-based responses. For guardians who expressed interest in participating, the nurses provided printed informed consent forms written in plain language, reviewing each clause verbatim to ensure full understanding—particularly regarding data confidentiality protocols and the right to withdraw from the study at any stage without impacting their child’s clinical care. Concurrently, they distributed the baseline questionnaires and compliance assessment scales, offering brief instructions on completion requirements (e.g., marking responses based on the child’s typical behavior) and clarifying ambiguous items to reduce response bias. A contact card with the research team’s contact information was also provided to address any follow-up questions, thereby fostering trust and ensuring a high-quality data collection process aligned with clinical research best practices.

Data analysis

SPSS 26.0 statistical software was used for data processing and analysis. First, data cleaning was performed. For items with missing values < 5%, multiple imputation was used. Outliers were identified through Z-test (|Z| > 3.29) and retained or not based on clinical judgment. Normality test (Shapiro-Wilk test) was performed on measurement data. Those conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and independent sample t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for inter-group comparison, with LSD-t test for pairwise comparison; measurement data not conforming to normal distribution were expressed as median (quartile) [M (P25, P75)], and non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test) were used for inter-group comparison. Count data were expressed as frequency [n (%)], and χ² test was used for inter-group comparison. Fisher’s exact probability method was used when the theoretical frequency was < 5. Binary Logistic regression model was used for multi-factor analysis, with compliance level (high compliance = 1, moderate and low compliance = 0) as the dependent variable, and variables with P < 0.1 in the single-factor analysis as candidate independent variables. The best model was selected through stepwise regression (entry criterion α = 0.05, removal criterion α = 0.10). This approach was adopted because the candidate independent variables covered multiple dimensions (child-related, guardian-related, and environmental factors), and stepwise regression helps screen out variables with significant predictive value, thereby avoiding model overfitting and improving the stability and interpretability of the final regression results. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of each factor were calculated. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 528 preschool children undergoing nebulization inhalation were included. Among the 528 participants, 279 (52.8%) were male and 249 (47.2%) were female. In terms of age distribution, 198 children (37.5%) were aged 3–<4 years, 203 (38.4%) were aged 4–<5 years, and 127 (24.1%) were aged 5–<6 years. Regarding disease types, 203 cases (38.4%) were diagnosed with bronchitis, 165 (31.2%) with pneumonia, 82 (15.5%) with asthma, and 78 (14.8%) with other respiratory diseases. Among the guardians, 246 (46.6%) had an educational level of high school or below, while 282 (53.4%) had a college degree or higher. In terms of guardians’ knowledge of nebulization therapy, 368 (69.7%) had good cognition, and 160 (30.3%) had poor cognition. Detailed information is presented in Table 1.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that only 26.9% of preschool children exhibited high compliance with outpatient nebulization inhalation therapy, placing the overall compliance at a moderately low level. This finding is generally consistent with most relevant studies—for example, Li [12] reported a compliance rate of 28.3% in preschool children receiving nebulization therapy, which is closely aligned with our results. However, our data is slightly lower than the 35.1% high compliance rate reported by Zhang et al. [13]. This discrepancy may stem from the uniqueness of the sample in this study: all participants were receiving nebulization therapy for the first time. In contrast, the sample in Zhang et al.’s [13] study included children with repeated nebulization experience, who may have developed adaptive acceptance of the treatment. Children experiencing nebulization for the first time, lacking relevant experience, tend to develop fear and resistance toward unfamiliar equipment, thereby reducing their compliance [14, 15].

From the perspective of scores across the various dimensions of the scale, the treatment cooperation dimension yielded the lowest score, suggesting that operational procedures—such as mask wearing and physical coordination requirements—are the primary sources of resistance among preschool children. This finding is closely associated with preschoolers’ instinctive rejection of contact with unfamiliar objects [16]. In contrast, the treatment cognition and attitude dimension exhibited a relatively higher score, indicating that children’s acceptance of nebulization treatment is to a certain extent malleable [17]. Targeted intervention measures may therefore help improve their treatment cognition and attitude, thereby laying a foundation for enhancing overall compliance.

The multifactorial nature of our findings suggests several potential targets for intervention, though these should be interpreted as hypotheses requiring empirical validation rather than evidence-based recommendations. The observed age-related differences in compliance align with developmental literature indicating that cognitive and emotional maturation between ages 3 and 6 years substantially influences children’s capacity to cooperate with medical procedures [18–20]. Gender differences were also observed, with girls showing better compliance than boys, possibly linked to the group characteristic that girls are relatively more docile and more likely to follow instructions. In terms of disease types, children with asthma demonstrated better compliance, presumably due to the need for long-term standardized treatment for asthma—parents attach greater importance to the treatment and provide more adequate guidance to their children during medical visits [21, 22]. Parental factors exerted a particularly significant impact on children’s compliance: children whose parents had higher educational levels and a good understanding of nebulization therapy showed better compliance. This is because parents with higher education levels are more likely to comprehend the importance of treatment and can more effectively convey positive information to their children, thereby reducing the children’s fear [23–25]. Additionally, treatment environment and doctor-patient communication, as key external factors, cannot be ignored in their impact on compliance: a comfortable treatment environment can reduce children’s sense of strangeness and fear, while good communication from medical staff can establish trust, alleviate children’s tension, and ultimately improve treatment cooperation [26]. For younger children (3-<4 years), whose resistance likely stems from fear of unfamiliar objects rather than intentional non-cooperation, distraction-based approaches such as gamified nursing models—decorating nebulizers with cartoon imagery or playing favorite cartoons during treatment—may prove more effective than explanatory approaches that exceed their cognitive capacity. This hypothesis is consistent with prior work demonstrating the efficacy of distraction techniques in reducing procedural distress among toddlers; For children aged 4-<5 years, who are developmentally transitioning toward symbolic thinking, concrete explanatory methods using picture books or visual aids may help bridge the gap between their emerging cognitive abilities and the need to understand treatment purpose [27, 28].

Empowerment-based educational approaches—combining one-on-one demonstration, video modeling, and hands-on practice [29] —address the mechanisms through which parental cognition influences child behavior: parents with better understanding are better equipped to manage child resistance, convey positive messages about treatment, and correctly execute technical procedures. Illustrated educational materials [30]; and peer support groups may reinforce these effects by providing accessible reference tools and social reinforcement, though the optimal combination and intensity of these components require systematic investigation [31].

Child-friendly treatment spaces incorporating cartoon decorations, dedicated pediatric areas, and age-appropriate furnishings [32] may reduce the novelty and threat value of the treatment setting. Similarly, communication training for healthcare providers [33] —emphasizing developmentally appropriate language, gentle approach, and allowing children to familiarize themselves with equipment prior to procedure—may enhance the therapeutic alliance and reduce procedure-related anxiety. The observation that waiting time reduction may improve compliance suggests that operational factors influencing child and parent stress levels deserve attention alongside direct clinical interactions [34]. The complex interplay among child, parental, and environmental factors identified in our multivariate analysis suggests that multimodal interventions addressing multiple levels simultaneously may be more effective than single-component approaches. Future research should systematically compare the incremental benefits of different intervention components and examine potential interactions—for example, whether the effectiveness of age-specific child interventions varies according to parental cognition levels or treatment environment quality.

Strengths of this study

First, we developed a self-designed Preschool Children’s Outpatient Nebulization Inhalation Therapy Compliance Scale with rigorous psychometric validation (Cronbach’s α = 0.86, test-retest reliability = 0.82), filling the gap of standardized assessment tools specifically for preschool children’s nebulization compliance. Most existing studies use subjective descriptive evaluations [9, 10], which are difficult to compare quantitatively. Second, we adopted a comprehensive multi-factor analysis using logistic regression, identifying four independent protective factors (5–<6 years of age, parental good cognition, comfortable treatment environment, and good nurse-patient communication) that can provide precise targets for clinical interventions. Third, the proposed nursing countermeasures are grounded in solid empirical evidence from our study results, with clear differentiation according to child age and intervention levels, enhancing their clinical applicability.

Limitations of this study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, the sample was recruited from a single tertiary children’s hospital, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our results to other healthcare settings or geographic regions. Multicenter studies with diverse populations are warranted to validate and extend our findings. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes observation of dynamic changes in compliance over time and prevents evaluation of the long-term effectiveness of potential interventions. Prospective cohort studies would be valuable for examining how compliance evolves with repeated nebulization experiences and for identifying causal relationships. Third, while our multifactor analysis identified several independent predictors of compliance, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits our ability to elucidate the complex interaction mechanisms among these variables. Future research employing structural equation modeling or path analysis could help clarify the direct and indirect pathways through which child-related, parental, and environmental factors jointly influence treatment adherence. Fourth, although the self-designed Preschool Children’s Outpatient Nebulization Inhalation Therapy Compliance Scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86) and content validity through expert evaluation, it has not undergone comprehensive psychometric validation. Specifically, confirmatory factor analysis has not been performed to verify the hypothesized four-dimensional structure, and normative data across diverse populations remain unavailable. Additionally, while the cut-off score for high compliance was empirically derived using ROC analysis (score ≥ 61, sensitivity = 0.82, specificity = 0.79), the categorization of moderate (41–60) and low (20–40) compliance incorporated clinical judgment and requires further validation in independent samples. The content validity assessment was conducted by only three experts; although these experts possessed extensive experience in pediatric respiratory nursing and scale development, a larger and more interdisciplinary panel—including child psychologists, respiratory physicians, and methodologists—would strengthen the robustness of future scale refinement. Comprehensive validation studies, including assessment of measurement invariance across different age groups, disease conditions, and clinical settings, are therefore essential prior to widespread adoption of this instrument.

Building upon these limitations, future research should pursue several avenues. Large-scale, multicenter prospective cohort studies are needed to confirm the stability of identified influencing factors and to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions over time. Qualitative research methods, such as in-depth interviews or focus groups with children and their parents, would complement quantitative findings by elucidating the psychological experiences, concerns, and coping strategies that shape treatment adherence. Such insights could inform the development of more nuanced and person-centered intervention strategies. Furthermore, the integration of information technology into compliance management warrants exploration; for instance, child-friendly health management applications incorporating interactive games, rewards, and educational content could enhance engagement for both children and caregivers. Finally, future instrument development should involve a broader panel of interdisciplinary experts to optimize content validity, alongside rigorous psychometric testing including confirmatory factor analysis, test-retest reliability across diverse samples, and establishment of normative data. These efforts would collectively advance both the measurement and enhancement of nebulization therapy compliance in preschool children.

Conclusion​

This study demonstrates that treatment compliance among preschool children undergoing outpatient nebulization inhalation therapy remains moderately low, with only 26.9% of participants achieving high compliance. By focusing specifically on the understudied preschool population (aged 3–6 years), our investigation reveals that compliance is a multifactorial phenomenon shaped by the complex interplay of individual, familial, and environmental determinants. Multivariate analysis identified four independent protective factors: older age within the preschool range (5-<6 years), adequate parental cognition regarding nebulization therapy, a comfortable treatment environment, and positive nurse-patient communication. These findings suggest several potential avenues for intervention development. Developmentally tailored approaches—such as gamified distraction for younger children (3-<4 years), concrete explanatory methods using visual aids for those aged 4-<5 years, and incentive-based strategies for older preschoolers (5-<6 years)—may address age-specific barriers to cooperation. Structured parental education programs aimed at enhancing knowledge and caregiving competencies, coupled with environmental optimization including child-friendly treatment spaces and improved provider communication, represent additional modifiable targets. It must be emphasized, however, that these proposed strategies are derived from observed associations rather than interventional evidence; their effectiveness in improving compliance requires rigorous evaluation through randomized controlled trials before clinical implementation can be recommended. Future research should prioritize multicenter prospective studies to validate the generalizability of our findings, examine the durability of intervention effects over time, and explore potential interaction effects among identified determinants. Additionally, investigation of digital health tools—such as interactive applications incorporating educational gaming elements—may offer innovative approaches to engaging both children and caregivers in compliance promotion. Such efforts will be essential for translating the correlational insights from this study into evidence-based strategies that can meaningfully improve clinical outcomes in this vulnerable population.
