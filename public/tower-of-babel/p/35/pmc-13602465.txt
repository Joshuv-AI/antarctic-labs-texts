Surgical repair of radial meniscus tears yields favorable healing rates: a systematic review
Authors: Felipe Moreira Borim, Cindy Zeng, Jan Rechsteiner, Timothy Lording
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report on current surgical techniques, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), and quantify healing rates following repair of radial meniscus tears.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines using the PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase and Scopus databases up to November 15, 2025. Studies included reported ≥ 6-month mean follow-up on radial meniscus tear repair, were published in English (or translated) after January 1, 1990. Technical reports and biomechanical or cadaveric research were excluded. Search terms included MeSH and keywords for “meniscal/meniscus AND radial/vertical tears” and repair concepts. Quality was assessed using both the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) and the Modified Coleman Methodology Score (MCMS).ResultsEighteen studies (316 patients, 321 radial repairs) met criteria. Mean follow-up was 36.1 ± 6.4 months (range 6.2–71). Lateral tears predominated (n = 279, 87%). Inside-out and all-inside techniques were most common. Postoperative PROMs demonstrated statistically significant improvement from pre- to postoperative assessments (p < 0.01 for Lysholm, IKDC, Tegner scores), generally indicating good to excellent functional outcomes and activity levels. Satisfactory healing (complete or partial) was reported in 82.5% of assessed tears, with a low complication rate (3.7%). Quality assessment indicated predominantly medium risk of bias according to MINORS criteria, and moderate quality based on MCMS scores (mean 55 ± 8.7).ConclusionsDespite variations in repair techniques, postoperative subjective outcomes indicated significant patient improvement following radial tear repair, with high reported healing rates.Clinical relevanceAs concern for post-meniscectomy osteoarthritis grows, meniscal preservation via radial tear repair offers a promising approach to improve clinical outcomes.Level of evidenceLevel IV, systematic review of Level III and IV studies.Keywords: Knee, Meniscus, Radial tear, Meniscus repair, Systematic review

Introduction

With a growing recognition of the detrimental effects of meniscectomy, there is increasing interest in meniscal repair whenever possible [1–4]. Radial tears pose a particularly unique challenge due to the disruption of circumferential fibers, which can impair the ability of the meniscus to transmit load, absorb shock, and maintain hoop strength [5, 6]. This disruption reduces meniscal contact and thereby increases tibiofemoral dynamic contact pressures, accelerating joint degeneration [7–9].

Recent studies highlight the underestimated prevalence of radial tears, which constitute a large subset (10–23%) of meniscus tear patterns in adults [10]. These tears, common in younger active individuals, and often trauma-related rather than degenerative, are frequently associated with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and multi-ligament knee injuries [1, 11]. The surgical repair of both medial and lateral meniscus tears can yield positive outcomes [12]. However, lateral meniscus tears are often considered to have greater healing potential and demonstrate better outcomes compared to medial meniscus tears [1, 13, 14].

Techniques for repairing radial meniscus tears include inside-out (IO), outside-in (OI), and all-inside (AI) repairs, each using various suture configurations such as single, double, or crossed horizontal/oblique mattress sutures, reinforced vertical “rip stop” sutures, and transtibial techniques [10, 15, 16]. Appropriate selection of a repair method, along with its strength and associated outcomes, is important as it directly influences healing potential and impacts postoperative rehabilitation protocols [15–19]. Therefore, it is important to investigate current surgical techniques, assess patient-reported outcome scores (PROMs), and quantify healing rates following the repair of radial meniscus tears.

A systematic review was conducted to summarize the current literature on radial meniscus tears and to report the available levels of evidence. The primary objective was to evaluate patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and quantify healing rates following radial meniscus repair. It was hypothesized that repair would result in improved PROMs and high healing rates. Additionally, current surgical techniques were investigated, and healing rates between medial and lateral radial meniscus tears were compared, with the hypothesis of superior healing in lateral tears.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

A systematic search of the medical literature was conducted in the PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase and Scopus databases on November 15th, 2025, in accordance with the guidelines set for standardized reporting of systematic reviews by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). Search terms associated with “meniscus”, “radial” and “tear”, with the use of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were used to identify studies assessing outcomes following radial meniscus repair. The detailed search strategy for each database can be found in Appendix 1. To avoid missing publications, reference lists of included studies and related reviews were also reviewed. All studies generated from the search criteria were independently reviewed for inclusion by two authors, F.M.B. and C.Z., with disagreements on article inclusion mediated by a third author, T.L. These articles were initially screened by title and abstract based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and subsequently underwent full-text review to ensure appropriateness. Studies included met the following criteria: levels of evidence IV or greater, studies using human subjects, reporting on radial tears of the lateral and/or medial meniscus, follow up of at least 6 months, published in English (or with available English translation) and publication date from January 1, 1990 onwards. Due to the limited number of randomised trials on this topic, non-randomised studies were included to provide a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence. Technical notes, biomechanical, animal and cadaveric studies were excluded from this review.

Study selection and data extraction

An initial search of PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus November 15th, 2025 identified 559 unique articles (Fig. 1). After initial screening, 512 studies were excluded, leaving 47 for full-text analysis. Applying inclusion and exclusion criteria at the full-text stage, 29 studies were excluded. In cases where medial and lateral radial tears were reported as a single group, authors were contacted for further information; responses providing differentiated data were received from three authors [20–22]. Ultimately, data from 18 studies were independently extracted by F.M.B. and C.Z. and analyzed for outcomes following radial meniscus repair. Disagreements at the full-text level were resolved by a third reviewer T.L. Extracted data included demographics (age, sex), tear characteristics (location, laterality), surgical techniques (approach, suture configuration, augmentation), concurrent procedures (e.g. ACL reconstruction), PROMs used, pre- and postoperative scores), complications, reoperations, and status of repair assessed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or second-look arthroscopy (including criteria for healing).

Methodologic quality assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed independently by two authors (F.M.B., C.Z.) using two established instruments: the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) and the Modified Coleman Methodology Score (MCMS). Both tools were selected to provide a comprehensive evaluation, with MINORS focusing on standard criteria for non-randomized surgical studies and MCMS offering a detailed assessment of study methodology specific to orthopaedic literature. MINORS scores range from 0 to 16 for non-comparative and 0–24 for comparative studies, categorized as high risk of bias (≤ 8 / ≤ 12), medium risk (9–12 / 13–18), or low risk (≥ 13 / ≥ 19). MCMS scores range from 0–100 and were categorized as excellent (≥ 85), good (70–84), moderate (50–69), or poor (< 50). Discrepancies in scoring were resolved by consensus involving a third author (T.L.).

Statistical analysis

Preoperative and postoperative PROMs were analysed using R software (version 4.2.3). Due to study heterogeneity, evaluated using the I2 statistic (I2 ≥ 70% for all analyses), and risk of bias, quantitative pooling was not performed. Forest plots of individual study data were generated without summary estimates using the meta package for common PROMs (Lysholm, IKDC, Tegner scores), and outcomes were synthesized descriptively using ranges.

Results

Study characteristics and risk of bias assessment

The literature search yielded 18 studies meeting inclusion criteria (Table 1), 5 prospective and 13 retrospective studies, with 14 of these studies being case series. Overall, these studies included 316 patients with 321 radial meniscus tear repairs (42 medial, 13%; 279 lateral, 87%). The methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using MINORS and MCMS. Using MINORS criteria, observed scores ranged from 6 to 11 for non-comparative studies and 12 to 19 for comparative studies. Based on predefined thresholds, this classified one study [23] as low risk of bias, sixteen as medium risk, and one [24] as high risk. Applying the MCMS (0–100 scale), the mean score was 55 ± 8.7 (range 31 to 62). This categorized five studies as poor quality (< 50 points) and thirteen as moderate quality (50–69 points), with no studies achieving good or excellent ratings. Detailed scores are available in Appendix 2 Tables 7 and 8.

Repair technique and biologic augmentation

Tear location was specified for 251 tears across fourteen studies, with midbody tears being most common (n = 179, 71%), followed by posterior horn (n = 61, 25%) and anterior horn tears (n = 11, 4%). Various arthroscopic techniques for repair were employed (Table 3). Inside-out (IO) was the most frequent approach (140 cases, 43.6%), followed by all-inside (AI) techniques (122 cases, 38%). AI repairs included both device-based [21, 30, 33, 36] (e.g., FastFix) and suture-shuttling/hook methods [28, 34, 35]. Outside-in (OI) techniques were used in 36 cases (11.2%), and hybrid approaches in 8 cases (2.5%). Horizontal mattress sutures were common, with several studies utilising reinforced constructs like the tie-grip/hashtag/rebar technique [20, 24, 29, 31, 35, 36].

Healing rates and complications

Meniscal healing was objectively assessed in 200 of 321 tears (62%) at final follow-up, using MRI in seven studies and second-look arthroscopy in six studies (Table 4). Definitions of healing varied across studies; however, based on reported categories (Table 5), 106 tears (53%) demonstrated complete healing, 59 (29.5%) partial healing, and 35 (17.5%) were classified as failures (re-tear or lack of healing). Overall, 82.5% of objectively assessed repairs showed either complete or partial healing. Among the seven MRI-based studies (77 tears), 61 tears (79.2%) achieved complete healing, 10 (13.0%) partial healing, and 6 (7.8%) failed. In contrast, six studies employing second-look arthroscopy (122 tears) reported 53 complete healings (43.4%), 50 partial healings (41.0%), and 19 failures (15.6%). The difference in distribution of healing outcomes between MRI and second-look arthroscopy is statistically significant (p < 0.001), with MRI reporting much higher complete healing, but fewer partial healings when compared to second-look arthroscopy.

Minor complications were infrequent, reported in three studies affecting 12 patients (3.7%), and typically involved transient symptoms (pain, stiffness, swelling, mechanical clicking) that resolved non-operatively. When reported, management of repair failures [21, 23, 29–31, 35, 37] involved either revision repair or partial meniscectomy. Among 189 patients across seven studies, 35 underwent one of these procedures, representing 18% of the cohort.

Discussion

The primary finding of this systematic review is that radial meniscus repair results in consistent improvements in PROMs and activity levels, with a low failure rate of 17.5% as evaluated by MRI or second-look arthroscopy. Improvements were observed across various PROMs used in the included studies, such as the VAS, Lysholm Score, IKDC Score and Tegner Activity Scale, indicating meaningful recovery for patients following surgical repair. These results were consistent even where patients undergoing concomitant ACLR were excluded, indicating functional benefit from the meniscal repair alone. While a horizontal suture configuration was traditionally preferred for radial repairs, recent studies included in this review suggest a growing use of tie-grip (hashtag or rebar) reinforcement techniques [20, 24, 29, 31, 35, 36]. Finally, subgroup analysis suggested clinical benefits for both medial and lateral tear repairs, though data limitations and heterogeneity precluded a conclusive comparison between the two.

Techniques for repairing radial meniscus tears include inside-out (IO), outside-in (OI), all-inside (AI) repairs, and the transtibial two-tunnel technique [10, 11, 15, 16], with inside-out (43.6%) and all-inside (38%) approaches being most common in this systematic review. The increasing use of reinforced suture constructs, such as the tie-grip/hashtag technique reported in several included studies [20, 24, 29, 31, 35, 36], reflects biomechanical evidence suggesting improved repair strength and reduced suture pull-out risk compared to simple horizontal repairs [10, 16, 38–41]. However, due to the heterogeneity in techniques, concomitant procedures, and outcome reporting across the reviewed clinical studies, this review could not definitively conclude whether these reinforced techniques yield clinically superior healing rates or functional outcomes compared to non-reinforced methods. Similarly, while biologic augmentation (fibrin clot, BMAC, marrow venting) was used in a subset of cases, its clinical impact could not be determined from the available comparative data. Furthermore, transtibial tunnel drilling has been proposed to enhance healing by providing bone marrow elements [42–44]. However, Cinque et al. (2017) was the only study in this review to report using a two-tunnel transtibial pullout technique for radial tears.

Complementing the clinical findings, biomechanical studies such as the study by Alentorn-Geli et al. have explored the mechanical properties of different radial tear repairs. Their work on lateral midbody tears found no conclusive biomechanical advantages for any single technique regarding load-to-failure, stiffness, or displacement, leading them to suggest surgeon preference as the main reason for choosing one configuration over another [15]. This aligns with our review's observation that diverse techniques can achieve successful clinical results, implying that factors beyond initial biomechanical strength likely contribute to overall patient outcomes.

The management of radial tears frequently overlaps with concomitant procedures, particularly ACLR, which was present in 37% of repairs in this review. Subgroup analyses of studies excluding ACLR patients suggested improvements in postoperative PROMs, though the heterogeneity between studies precludes a clear understanding of the full magnitude of benefit. Despite overall favorable results, debate persists regarding whether all lateral meniscus tears identified during ACLR should be repaired or left in situ. While some advocate repair given the low risk and potential benefits [44, 45], others argue the evidence remains insufficient to support intervention over observation for stable tears [46–50]. Although this review could not directly compare outcomes based on ACLR status and focused exclusively on radial tears, which are generally unstable, the consistently positive outcomes reported across studies support repair of radial tears encountered in the setting of ACLR.

It is also important to consider the nature of the tear itself. Radial tears may range from those confined to zones 2–3 to those extending into zones 0–1, the latter carrying significant biomechanical consequences. While minor tears might preserve some function, potentially making debridement an option in select avascular zones, full-width tears completely disrupt meniscal hoop stresses [10, 11, 51]. This disruption is strongly linked to accelerated joint degeneration and the onset of osteoarthritis [7], underscoring the rationale for repairing substantial or full-width radial tears whenever technically feasible. Reporting of tear characteristics was inconsistent across studies, making it impossible to accurately assess their influence on outcomes.

Although overall failure rates did not differ significantly between MRI and second-look arthroscopy, the distribution of healing outcomes varied markedly. MRI reported a higher proportion of complete healing and fewer partial healings compared with second-look arthroscopy (79.2 and 13% vs. 43.4 and 41%), suggesting a tendency of MRI to overestimate repair integrity. This likely reflects the limited sensitivity of MRI in distinguishing partial from complete healing. In contrast, second-look arthroscopy provides direct visualization and a more detailed grading of repair. These findings highlight the need for caution when interpreting healing rates across studies that use different assessment methods.

The question of whether tear location influences outcomes remains pertinent. Some evidence suggests lateral meniscus repairs might have lower failure rates than medial repairs, possibly due to differences in mobility, loading [52–54], and vascularity [55]; although other studies find no significant effect of laterality [56–58]. A meta-analysis noted a non-significant trend favoring lateral repairs in all-type meniscus tears [59]. Steadman et al., in a study with a minimum 10-year follow-up, also found no significant difference in failure rates between medial and lateral meniscus repairs (though not specific to radial tears) [12]. Our review, focusing specifically on radial tears, was limited in addressing this comparison due to the preponderance of lateral tears (87%) and significant heterogeneity, making our subgroup analysis inconclusive.

This review updates and expands upon previous systematic reviews by Moulton et al. (2016) and Milliron et al. (2021), which also reported promising outcomes after radial tear repair. By incorporating six additional studies published since 2020 [20, 24, 34–37], our analysis includes data from 80 more repairs, providing a more current perspective on techniques and results. Furthermore, our findings are consistent with a recent review by Dzidizishvili et al. (2024) which compared IO and AI techniques, finding improved PROMs with both but potentially higher failure rates with IO repairs. While our review similarly found both IO and AI techniques contributing to positive outcomes, inherent study heterogeneity prevented a direct comparison of their failure rates within our dataset.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Considerable heterogeneity existed across the included studies in surgical techniques, augmentation use, rehabilitation protocols, and particularly in the methods and criteria used for assessing postoperative healing. The variety of PROMs used, while consistently showing improvement, precludes direct comparison between many studies. Due to the heterogeneity and bias risk as assessed by MINORS and MCMS, meta-analysis of these non-randomised studies (Level II and IV evidence) was deemed not appropriate, and therefore only descriptive synthesis was performed. Furthermore, objective healing was assessed in only 62% of tears, and the small number of medial tears (13%) limited subgroup analyses. Interpretation of reported healing rates is limited by heterogeneity in healing definitions across studies. The criteria for complete and partial healing were not explicitly described in most studies, particularly those relying on postoperative MRI, which may overestimate true structural healing. Among studies performing second-look arthroscopy, Tsuji et al. defined complete healing as the absence of a visible surface defect with complete synovial coverage, partial healing as a small residual defect with synovial coverage over more than 50% of the torn area, and failure to heal as a large residual defect with synovial coverage over less than 50% of the torn area. Future studies should be prospective and should explicitly state the location and characteristics of the tear, as well as clarify their definitions for complete and partial healing.

Conclusion

The current evidence supports the repair of radial meniscus tears when feasible. Despite variations in techniques and biomechanical approaches used across the included studies, clinical outcomes consistently show significant patient improvement in pain, function, and activity levels, highlighting the effectiveness of repair. Additionally, objective assessments, including MRI and second-look arthroscopy, report complete or partial healing in over 80% of cases evaluated, with a low complication rate. This alignment between clinical and imaging data reinforces the shift toward meniscal preservation for suitable radial tears.
