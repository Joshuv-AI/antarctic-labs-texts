Effects of gestational weight gain based on weight-recommended guidelines (2022) in China on maternal and neonatal outcomes: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tao Wei, Jing Huang, Bowen Li, Dehui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational weight gain (GWG) is a modifiable factor associated with maternal and neonatal outcomes. However, whether recommended weight gain ranges are appropriate across different pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) categories remains uncertain, particularly in Asian populations. This study examined the associations between GWG and maternal and neonatal outcomes across pre-pregnancy BMI categories using gestational weight management recommendations developed for the Chinese population.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted in a public general hospital in Beijing, China. Pregnant women with singleton pregnancies who attended routine antenatal care and delivered between January 2021, and December 2024 were included. Women with incomplete data were excluded. GWG was calculated as the difference between self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and weight recorded before delivery. Weight gain was classified as inadequate, adequate, or excessive within each pre-pregnancy BMI category according to Chinese gestational weight management guidelines. Maternal outcomes included Caesarean section, non-elective caesarean, mode of birth, gestational diabetes, and gestational hypertension. Neonatal outcomes included preterm birth, low birthweight, and macrosomia, neonatal asphyxia. Multivariable logistic regression analyses stratified by pre-pregnancy BMI category were performed.ResultsA total of 3,734 women were included, of whom 66.1% had normal pre-pregnancy BMI. Among women with normal BMI, excessive GWG was associated with higher odds of caesarean section (adjusted odds ratio 1.38, 95% confidence interval 1.15–1.66) and non-elective caesarean Sect. (1.56, 1.16–2.11). Inadequate weight gain in this group was associated with increased odds of preterm birth (2.60, 1.46–4.49) and low birthweight (2.48, 1.37–4.32). Excessive GWG was also associated with higher odds of macrosomia among women with normal BMI (3.08, 1.98–4.92) and those who were overweight (2.96, 1.31–7.96). Among women with obesity, inadequate weight gain was associated with increased odds of preterm birth (6.28, 1.70–26.30). Other associations were limited or outcome-specific.ConclusionAssociations between GWG and maternal and neonatal outcomes varied across pre-pregnancy BMI categories. Women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI demonstrated the most consistent associations with adverse outcomes when gestational weight gain fell outside recommended ranges. These findings suggest that gestational weight management should not focus solely on women at the extremes of BMI, and support BMI-stratified approaches to antenatal weight monitoring and counselling using population-specific guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27154-1.Keywords: Gestational weight gain, Pre-pregnancy body mass index, Maternal outcomes, Neonatal outcomes, Pregnancy

Introduction

Gestational weight gain (GWG) is a well-recognised modifiable factor associated with maternal and neonatal outcomes [1]. Both inadequate and excessive GWG have been linked to adverse outcomes, including preterm birth, caesarean section, and abnormal birthweight [2–4]. Consequently, optimising GWG is considered a key component of antenatal care aimed at improving pregnancy and birth outcomes.

Although GWG has been widely studied, much of the existing literature has focused on identifying associations between GWG and adverse outcomes, rather than evaluating whether current GWG targets are appropriately tailored across pre-pregnancy BMI categories and populations. Evidence relating GWG to neonatal outcomes is relatively consistent. For example, inadequate GWG is associated with an increased risk of low birthweight and preterm birth, while excessive GWG is associated with macrosomia [3, 5, 6]. In contrast, findings for maternal outcomes, including caesarean sections and metabolic complications such as GDM and gestational hypertension, appear more variable and may differ across BMI groups. A retrospective study in the United States reported that GWG is a risk factor for GDM in the overweight and obese population, but not among underweight women or those with normal BMI [7]. Another study based on Korean women, found that early GWG was associated with GDM and pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH) in women with normal BMI, but not in underweight women; among obese women, early GWG was related to PIH [8].

Although current GWG recommendations are supported by substantial observational evidence, questions remain regarding whether existing ranges are optimal across all BMI categories and populations [9]. This is particularly relevant for women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI, who constitute the majority of pregnant populations but are often assumed to be at comparatively low risk.

The GWG guidelines released by the Institute of Medicine in 2009 have been among the most widely adopted recommendations internationally [10]. These guidelines provide BMI-specific GWG ranges and have informed clinical practice and research for over a decade. However, they were largely derived from populations residing in the United States with limited ethnic diversity and may not fully account for differences in body composition, cardiometabolic risk, and obstetric characteristics observed in other populations. As a result, the applicability of these recommendations to non-Western settings remains uncertain.

Evidence from Asian populations remains comparatively limited. This is despite well-documented differences in anthropometry and metabolic risk at equivalent BMI levels, including higher body fat percentage and greater susceptibility to GDM and hypertensive disorders at lower BMI thresholds [11–13].

Previous research on gestational weight gain has largely focused on women at the extremes of pre-pregnancy BMI, particularly those who are underweight or obese [14–17]. Although women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI constitute the largest proportion of pregnant populations and are routinely included in such studies, they are often treated as a reference group rather than being examined in detail. Emerging evidence suggests that inappropriate GWG among women with normal BMI may still be associated with increased maternal and neonatal risks [18, 19], highlighting the need for more nuanced, BMI-stratified evaluation within this group.

In China, gestational weight management guidelines reflecting local anthropometric characteristics were released in 2022 [20]. However, evidence evaluating the associations between GWG defined by these guidelines and maternal and neonatal outcomes remains limited. A recent study compared the IOM guidelines with the newly released Chinese gestational weight gain (GWG) guidelines in identifying adverse foetal outcomes, including small for gestational age, large for gestational age, low birthweight, and macrosomia [21]. The findings suggested that the Chinese GWG guidelines may better identify pregnant women at risk of large for gestational age and macrosomia. However, this study primarily focused on GWG, with pre-pregnancy body mass index treated as a confounding variable.

BMI-stratified analyses using these context-specific recommendations are needed to assess whether current GWG targets effectively identify women at increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the associations between overall gestational weight gain and maternal and neonatal outcomes across pre-pregnancy BMI categories, using gestational weight management recommendations adapted for the Chinese population. The findings are intended to inform the appropriateness and implementation of BMI-specific gestational weight gain targets and to support more targeted antenatal counselling and risk stratification in clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective cohort study of pregnant women with a singleton pregnancy giving birth in XX hospital, Beijing, China between 2021 and 2024. The reporting of this study followed The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [22]. This study was approved by the institutional review board of XX hospital (Approval number: 2024BJYYEC-KY214-04; Date:25 Feb,2025) with waived informed consent.

Setting

This study was conducted in XX hospital, located at Beijing, China. XX hospital is a government-administered public general hospital that provides comprehensive medical care across a wide range of health needs. The hospital has an estimated annual birth rate of approximately 1,300.

Participants

This study collected maternal characteristics, maternal, and neonatal outcomes of all women who attended regular prenatal visits and gave birth in XX hospital between Jan 2021 to December 2024. Women with incomplete data were excluded from this study.

Variables

The currents study reported variables related to maternal characteristics, maternal and neonatal outcomes. Maternal characteristics included educational level, age, pre-pregnancy BMI, total gestational weight gain, early pregnancy weight gain, and parity. Maternal outcomes included mode of birth, and reasons for caesarean sections, rates of GDM, and rates of gestational hypertension.

In this study, pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated using the self-reported pregnancy height and weight data recorded at the first antenatal visit and categorised as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.0 kg/m2), overweight (24.0–28.0 kg/m2), obese (> 28.0 kg/m2), in accordance with BMI guidelines for Chinese adults. Early gestational weight gain refers to weight gain during the first trimester, measured at the end of 13 weeks of gestation. Total gestational weight gain was calculated as the difference between self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and the last measured weight before delivery.

According to the modified gestational weight gain guidelines in China, the recommended total gestational weight gain for underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese women is 11.0–16.0 kg, 8.0–14.0 kg, 7.0–11.0 kg, and 5.0–9.0 kg, respectively (Table 1). The recommended early gestational weight gain is 0–2 kg for all pregnant women, regardless of pre-pregnancy BMI category.

Modes of Birth included spontaneous vaginal births, assisted vaginal birth, and caesarean sections. Reasons for caesarean were also recorded and categorised as five categories: (1) Elective caesarean section before labour onset due to scarred uterus, breech presentation, placenta previa, placental abruption, funnel-shaped pelves; (2) labour arrest; (3) foetal indications; (4) suspected foetal macrosomia; (5) failed trial of labour.

A diagnosis of GDM was established using a 2-hour Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) conducted between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, with The International Association for Diabetes in Pregnancy Study Group criteria (IADPSG). Gestational hypertension was defined as elevated blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg), occurring after 20 weeks of gestation in women with previously normal blood pressure.

Neonatal outcomes included preterm birth, birth weight, and neonatal asphyxia. Preterm birth was defined as birth occurring before 37 weeks of gestational. Birth weight was categorised as very low birth weight (< 1500 g), low birth weight (< 2500 g), normal birth weight (2500–4000 g), and macrosomia (≥ 4000 g). Neonatal asphyxia was defined as Apgar score at 5 min < 7.

Data collection

In this study, data were extracted from electronic medical record system of XX hospital and entered into statistical software by two independent nurses. Data inconsistencies were identified and resolved through case-to-case verification. All participant data were pseudonymised using numerical codes.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants’ sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Participants were categorised according to pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) into underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–23.99 kg/m²), overweight (24.0–27.99 kg/m²), and obesity (≥ 28.0 kg/m²). Chi-square analyses were conducted to compare participants’ characteristics across pre-pregnancy BMI categories.

Gestational weight gain was classified as inadequate, normal (reference), or excessive within each pre-pregnancy BMI category. Caesarean section was analysed as a binary outcome (caesarean section vs. spontaneous vaginal birth and operative birth). Indications for caesarean section were initially recorded in five categories and were collapsed into two groups (elective and non-elective) for analysis.

Univariate associations between overall gestational weight gain and maternal and neonatal outcomes were assessed within each pre pregnancy BMI category using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate, depending on cell counts.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between overall gestational weight gain and adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes within each pre-pregnancy BMI category, adjusting for maternal age and parity. The inclusion of maternal age and parity as confounders in the multivariable analyses was informed by previous literature [23–27] and supported by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Appendix 1). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values were reported. In analyses affected by sparse data or quasi-complete separation, bias-reduced estimates were obtained using Firth penalised logistic regression.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2.

Patient and public involvement

This study did not involve patient and public involvement.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 3,734 women were included in the analysis (Table 2). The majority of participants were aged 25–34 years (69.2%), followed by 35–45 years (28.9%). Most women had a normal pre-pregnancy BMI (66.1%), while 8.4% were underweight, 18.6% were overweight, and 6.9% were obese. Approximately half of the participants were experiencing their first pregnancy (50.3%), and 68.6% were primiparous. Deliveries were distributed relatively evenly across the study period from 2021 to 2024.

Univariate analyses demonstrated that among women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI, overall GWG was significantly associated with caesarean section, non-elective caesarean section, GDM, and macrosomia (Table 3). In contrast, no significant associations were observed among underweight or obese women in univariate analyses.

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence on the association between GWG and adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes using modified gestational weight management guidelines tailored to Chinese women. Unlike previous studies based on Western-derived recommendations, our findings demonstrate pre-pregnancy BMI-specific and outcome-specific risk patterns, highlighting the importance of population-appropriate gestational weight gain targets. These results indicate the need for stratified and individualised gestational weight management strategies in Chinese maternity care settings.

In this retrospective cohort of 3,734 women, GWG was differentially associated with maternal and neonatal outcomes across pre-pregnancy BMI categories. Notably, women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI, who constituted the majority of the study population, demonstrated the most consistent associations between inappropriate GWG and adverse outcomes. In this group, excessive GWG was associated with increased odds of caesarean section and non-elective caesarean section, while inadequate GWG was associated with increased risks of preterm birth and low birthweight. In contrast, associations among underweight, overweight, and obese women were more limited and outcome specific. These findings highlight the importance of BMI-stratified evaluation of GWG and suggest that women with normal BMI should not be considered a low-risk group with respect to gestational weight management.

A novel key finding of this study is that women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI exhibited the strongest and most consistent associations between GWG and both maternal and neonatal outcomes. Clinically, women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI are often perceived as having lower obstetric risk and may therefore receive less intensive weight monitoring and counselling during pregnancy. However, our findings suggest that deviations from recommended GWG in this group are associated with substantial risks, including increased caesarean delivery, preterm birth, and abnormal birthweight. This challenges the assumption that normal pre-pregnancy BMI confers protection against adverse outcomes and underscores the need for careful GWG monitoring across all BMI categories, not only among women at the extremes of BMI.

The associations observed between GWG and neonatal outcomes in this study are broadly consistent with existing literature [3, 19, 28]. Among women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI, inadequate GWG was associated with increased odds of preterm birth and low birthweight, while excessive GWG was associated with increased odds of macrosomia. Similarly, among overweight women, excessive GWG was associated with macrosomia. These findings align with established evidence linking insufficient maternal weight gain to impaired foetal growth and shortened gestation, and excessive weight gain to foetal overgrowth [2–4]. The consistency of these associations supports the relevance of GWG as an important determinant of neonatal outcomes and the applicability of the Chinese GWG guidelines in identifying neonatal risk.

In contrast to neonatal outcomes, associations between GWG and maternal outcomes were more heterogeneous and appeared to vary by pre-pregnancy BMI category.

Among women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI, excessive GWG was associated with increased risk of both overall and non-elective caesarean section. The findings in this study may reflect a combination of biological factors, such as increased foetal size, and clinical decision-making during labour, including thresholds for intervention. In other BMI groups, associations between GWG and maternal outcomes were less consistent after adjustment. This heterogeneity is in line with previous studies [19, 29] and highlights the complex pathways through which GWG influences maternal outcomes, which may be mediated by baseline metabolic risk, obstetric management practices, and timing of weight gain.

Interestingly, excessive GWG among women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI was associated with reduced risk of GDM. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as GDM is often diagnosed mid-pregnancy and may lead to subsequent dietary restriction and altered weight gain patterns, raising the possibility of reverse causality. Residual confounding related to timing of GWG could not be fully accounted for in this study. Similarly, previous research has not identified total GWG as a predictor for GDM [30].

In the current study, among overweight and obese women, those with inadequate or excessive GWG were not at increased risk of most of the adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes examined, compared with those with adequate GWG. This finding is similar to a large study conducted in the United States based on 515,148 births [31], which reported that GWG above the IOM guidelines was not associated with severe maternal morbidities in this population, such as eclampsia, blood transfusion, and shock. It is worth noting that the pre-pregnancy BMI criteria used in that study differed from those applied in the present study.

The relatively limited associations observed among overweight and obese women may have several explanations. Women in these BMI categories may already have elevated baseline risks for adverse outcomes [32], potentially attenuating the incremental effect of GWG. In addition, overweight and obese women may receive closer clinical monitoring, dietary counselling, or early intervention during pregnancy, which could modify the relationship between GWG and outcomes. Furthermore, the narrower recommended GWG ranges for these groups may reduce variability in weight gain, and the smaller sample size, particularly among obese women, may have limited statistical power to detect associations for some outcomes.

Overall, our findings suggest that both insufficient and excessive GWG are associated with adverse perinatal outcomes, with the magnitude and direction of risk varying by pre-pregnancy BMI. The application of Chinese-specific gestational weight gain guidelines offers improved risk discrimination compared with Western-based recommendations and may better support clinical decision-making in this population.

Taken together, these findings suggest that gestational weight management strategies should not focus exclusively on women who are underweight or obese before pregnancy. Instead, proactive monitoring and counselling regarding GWG should be extended to women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI, who represent the largest proportion of pregnant populations and appear particularly sensitive to deviations from recommended GWG ranges. The use of Chinese gestational weight management guidelines in this study provides context-specific evidence to support their implementation in clinical practice and highlights the importance of BMI-specific approaches to antenatal care.

Strength and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size, the use of BMI-stratified analyses, and the application of gestational weight gain recommendations tailored to the Chinese population. The distinction between elective and non-elective caesarean section and the use of bias-reduced regression methods in the presence of sparse data further strengthen the analysis.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated based on self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and height data, which may introduce recall bias. However, pre-pregnancy BMI data are rarely recorded in medical records, and self-reported data are often used as a proxy. The retrospective design limits causal inference. This study did not account for several potential confounders, including maternal smoking status, drinking status, and maternal education and other relevant socioeconomic characteristics, which are known to be associated with gestational weight gain and pregnancy outcomes. Data were derived from a single centre, which may limit generalisability. Logistic regression models were used to estimate associations, and the results are presented as ORs. As some outcomes in this study, such as caesarean section and GDM, were relatively common, odds ratios may overestimate the magnitude of the association compared with risk ratios. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, this study focused on overall GWG and did not examine trimester-specific weight gain trajectories, which may provide further insight into timing-dependent effects. The relatively small number of obese women may also have reduced power to detect associations in this group.

Future studies should prospectively evaluate GWG trajectories across pregnancy, explore the timing of weight gain in relation to maternal and neonatal outcomes, and validate these findings in multi-centre cohorts across diverse Asian populations. Intervention studies targeting optimal GWG among women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI may be particularly valuable in informing clinical practice.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, GWG was associated with maternal and neonatal outcomes in a BMI-specific manner. Women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI demonstrated the most consistent associations, with both inadequate and excessive gestational weight gain linked to increased risks of adverse outcomes, including caesarean delivery, preterm birth, and abnormal birthweight. In contrast, associations among underweight, overweight, and obese women were more limited and outcome specific.

These findings suggest that gestational weight management should not be restricted to women at the extremes of BMI. Instead, careful monitoring and counselling regarding gestational weight gain are warranted for women with normal pre-pregnancy BMI, who represent the majority of pregnant populations.
