Spatiotemporal heterogeneity and multiscale social influencing factors of syphilis in the mainland of China, 2005–2022
Authors: Ling Jia, Huixian Zhou, Jiani Mao, Jin Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study analyzes the spatiotemporal patterns and social determinants of syphilis in the mainland of China (2005–2022) to inform targeted resource allocation and control strategies. Using provincial-level syphilis incidence analyzed via joinpoint regression, spatial clustering, and multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR). We found the incidence significantly increased from 9.73 to 31.27 per 100,000 (AAPC = 6.81%), evolving through distinct “rapid increase–slow increase–decline” phases. Spatially, five distinct patterns emerged. Hotspots were concentrated in Fujian/Jiangxi, then shifted to Tibet/Xinjiang, and later spread to the central-southern regions. MGWR revealed significant spatial non-stationarity in social drivers: per capita GDP exerted a positive global effect, whereas the density of health technicians and medical institutions showed significant negative global effects, with the urbanization rate exhibiting suppressing effects globally but promoting effects locally. These findings demonstrate profound spatiotemporal heterogeneity in syphilis distribution, underscoring the necessity for differentiated prevention strategies prioritizing high-incidence border regions (e.g., Xinjiang and Tibet) and emerging risk areas (e.g., Yunnan and Guizhou).

Introduction

Syphilis is a sexually and vertically transmitted bacterial infection caused by the bacterium Treponema pallidum.1,2 It can lead to damage and pathological changes in nearly all tissues and organs of the body.3,4 Although syphilis is curable, its primary symptoms are often difficult to detect and may not appear at all.5 If not promptly identified and treated, the infection can result in serious consequences,6 such as infant mortality,7 an increased risk of HIV infection and transmission,8 and a greater economic burden of disease.9 The harm to individual health and societal development can be irreversible.

Despite the implementation of extensive public health measures, such as testing, treatment, and health education, syphilis remains a global public health concern, with incidence rates continuing to rise in most countries and regions.10 According to the latest data from the World Health Organization, in 2022, there were over 8 million prevalent cases of active syphilis among adults aged 15–49 worldwide, and approximately 230,000 deaths were related to the disease.11 In China, syphilis was nearly eradicated in the 1960s, but it re-emerged in the early 1980s and has surged dramatically since the 1990s12 By 2023, the number of reported syphilis cases had become the highest among all sexually transmitted diseases in mainland China.13 The widespread transmission of syphilis across various regions of China has been linked to factors such as economic development, population mobility, and increasingly open sexual attitudes.14,15,16,17 From a biological perspective, spatial diffusion is a critical yet often overlooked aspect of disease transmission,18 and it plays a particularly distinctive role in the spread of syphilis.19 The transmission of syphilis also tends to exhibit marked temporal trends over time.20,21 According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, surveillance data can be used to estimate the scale of the problem, identify disease distribution, generate hypotheses, and monitor changes.22 The analysis of surveillance data enables a deeper understanding of risk factors and is particularly well-suited for comparing the epidemiological patterns of infectious diseases across different times and regions.23 Therefore, conducting an in-depth analysis of the spatiotemporal characteristics of syphilis based on surveillance data is essential for identifying key factors influencing its incidence and has important implications for understanding and controlling the disease.

A substantial body of research has employed various spatial statistical methods to comprehensively and systematically reveal the spatiotemporal distribution patterns of syphilis and its influencing factors. In the context of time series analysis, Joinpoint Regression (JPR) has been widely used in cancer and epidemiological studies to examine temporal trends and characteristics, as well as to evaluate the effects of interventions on incidence rates.24,25 Zhang et al. applied JPR to long-term surveillance data on syphilis incidence, identifying its temporal distribution patterns and dynamic trends, thereby providing a solid foundation for developing prevention strategies tailored to different stages.26 For spatial analysis, Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have been employed to examine the spatiotemporal characteristics of diseases, aiding in the monitoring and prevention of infectious diseases. Spatiotemporal analysis enables the description of the geographic distribution of infectious diseases, the identification of high-risk spatiotemporal clusters, and the exploration of associated variables.21,27,28 Oliveira et al. employed the spatiotemporal scan statistical method to accurately identify high-risk clusters of congenital syphilis, providing a scientific basis for defining key targets for prevention and control.29 Raimundo et al. conducted global and local spatial autocorrelation analyses to identify high-incidence clusters of syphilis, laying the groundwork for investigating spatial heterogeneity and the geographic patterns of transmission.30 Analysis of driving factors for transmission mechanisms of sexually transmitted diseases, Birri Makota et al. applied the multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR) model to examine regional variations in the relationships between covariates and HIV incidence.31 Cruz et al. employed a spatiotemporal Bayesian model to evaluate the associations between congenital syphilis in São Paulo, Brazil, and socioeconomic, demographic, and environmental factors.32 In summary, spatiotemporal analysis serves as a vital tool for clarifying the temporal distribution, spatial clustering, and driving factors of syphilis. A comprehensive spatiotemporal approach can offer valuable insights for the development of targeted intervention policies.33

However, we found that more studies on syphilis trends were conducted before 2022.20,24,34 Few have systematically examined the developmental patterns of syphilis in China over the past two decades.27,35 Research on the spatial distribution of syphilis has often been limited to individual regions rather than covering the entire country.10,21,36 In light of this, the present study analyzes the reported incidence rates of syphilis from 2005 to 2022 (18 years) across all provincial-level regions in the mainland of China. Multiple spatiotemporal analytical methods are employed to investigate the spatiotemporal patterns of syphilis incidence and its associated social determinants. The goal is to generate more comprehensive and actionable insights to support the development of more effective and scientifically grounded strategies for the integrated prevention and control of syphilis in China.

Results

Description of spatiotemporal distribution

From 2005 to 2022, the incidence of syphilis in the mainland of China showed an overall upward trend, increasing from 9.73 per 100,000 in 2005 to 31.27 per 100,000 in 2022 (Figure 1A). Between 2005 and 2011, the incidence rose sharply (from 9.73 to 29.47 per 100,000), with significantly higher incidence rates observed in provincial-level regions such as Shanghai, Zhejiang, Fujian, and Guangxi compared to others (Figure 1B). From 2011 to 2016, the incidence of increase slowed considerably compared to the previous period, though the overall trend remained gradually upward. Starting in 2016, the incidence of syphilis began to rise more rapidly again, peaking in 2019 at 38.37 per 100,000. After 2019, the incidence began to decline, showing a fluctuating downward trend from 2019 to 2022. Joinpoint regression analysis

The fitting accuracy of the JPR model for syphilis incidence rates in the mainland of China from 2005 to 2022 showed that the highest MSE was observed in Tibet (0.11), followed by Guangxi (0.04). The MSEs for all other provincial-level regions were significantly below 0.05, indicating strong statistical significance. These results suggest that the JPR model performed well in capturing the trends in syphilis incidence and in identifying segmented phases (Table S2).

During the study period, the overall incidence of syphilis in the mainland of China exhibited an upward trend (AAPC = 6.81%, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). With the exception of Beijing, Shanghai, Zhejiang, and Guangxi—where the AAPC was not statistically significant—the remaining 27 provincial-level regions showed positive AAPC values, indicating increasing trends consistent with the national pattern. Among these, 21 regions had AAPC values exceeding the national average, with Tibet experiencing the most rapid increase (AAPC = 31.52%, p < 0.001). In contrast, provincial-level regions in the northeastern region, such as Jilin, Heilongjiang, and Tianjin, as well as those in the eastern coastal region, including Fujian, Guangdong, and Jiangsu, had AAPC values below the national average. Trend analysis of syphilis incidence in the mainland of China and 31 provincial-level regions reveals significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity in the mainland of China, the years 2010 and 2019 serve as notable joinpoints in the incidence trend, with the APCs for the three periods recorded at 22.25%, 2.94%, and −4.75%, respectively, indicating an overall pattern of “rapid increase–slow increase–decline.” The provincial-level regions were categorized into three groups based on the number of joinpoints: (1) 10 regions, including Beijing, Tianjin, and Liaoning, had only a single joinpoint, which predominantly appeared around 2013. This group exhibited a clear two-phase pattern: before the joinpoint, the incidence of syphilis increased rapidly (6.60% ≤ APC ≤ 52.41%); after the joinpoint, a declining trend was observed in five regions, while the rate of increase slowed significantly in the others (Figure 2B). (2) 11 regions, including Hebei, Heilongjiang, and Shanghai, followed a dual-joinpoint pattern (Figure 2C), with the first joinpoint occurring between 2008 and 2013, and the second between 2013 and 2019. The trend consistently showed an increase during the first two periods, followed by either a decline or a slower increase after the second joinpoint. (3) 10 regions, including Guangdong, Sichuan, and Henan, exhibited the most complex fluctuation patterns (Figure 2D), with typical joinpoints in 2011 and 2019. After 2019, the incidence of syphilis consistently shifted to a downward trend. In terms of spatial distribution, provincial-level regions with low volatility are primarily concentrated in the North China and Northeast regions, where socioeconomic development is relatively advanced. In contrast, provincial-level regions with high volatility are mainly found in the central-western and border areas, which are characterized by high population mobility. This pattern highlights a strong correlation between prevention and control pressures and factors such as economic development, population mobility, and resource allocation.

Cluster analysis

The Silhouette Coefficient was used to determine the optimal number of clusters for the 20 time series curve features across 31 provincial-level regions. The Silhouette coefficient reached its highest value (0.25) when the number of clusters was set to 5. Therefore, this study set the number of clusters to 5 and performed hierarchical clustering on the curve characteristic values. Cluster I included three coastal regions—Beijing, Tianjin, and Jiangsu—with relatively stable incidence rates. Cluster II consisted of nine provincial-level regions: Henan, Hubei, Shaanxi, Shanxi, Hebei, Shandong, Jilin, Heilongjiang, and Gansu. Cluster III comprised 15 regions: Liaoning, Inner Mongolia, Guangdong, Hainan, Fujian, Jiangxi, Anhui, Hunan, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Chongqing, Qinghai, Ningxia, and Tibet. Cluster IV included Shanghai and Zhejiang in East China, as these were the only two provincial-level regions with an AAPC <0 (Table S2). Cluster V consisted of Guangxi and Xinjiang, both located in border regions, where incidence rates were high and fluctuated significantly. The overall development pattern of syphilis demonstrated a spatial gradient characterized by “higher in the west, lower in the east” and “more volatile in border regions, more stable in coastal areas” (Figure 3). This pattern is closely associated with multiple factors, including regional economic development, allocation of medical resources, population mobility, and disease surveillance capacity.Figure 3Clustering results of syphilis development patterns of 31 provincial-level regions

Hotspot analysis

Based on the spatial analysis of syphilis incidence in the mainland of China from 2005 to 2022, the global Moran’s I showed a significant positive correlation (p < 0.05) in all years except 2006–2007, 2011–2012, and 2014, indicating a notable global spatial pattern of syphilis over a 13-year period. Accordingly, a hotspot analysis was conducted, revealing a three-stage dynamic migration of hotspots. Before 2010, hotspots were consistently clustered in the southeastern coastal regions (Fujian and Jiangxi), while cold spots were concentrated in the northwestern inland areas (Shaanxi and Ningxia). Between 2011 and 2014, most provincial-level regions were identified as non-significant areas, although the northwestern cold spots remained significant, and the hotspots shifted toward Tibet and Xinjiang. After 2015, hotspots became persistently concentrated in the border regions (Tibet and Xinjiang), and in 2019, extended southward into Guangxi and Guangdong. Meanwhile, cold spots continued to spread northward and eastward, gradually expanding by 2019 into North China, Northeast China, Shandong, and the Yangtze River Delta regions. As the number of cold spot areas increased, the significance of low-value clustering also intensified (Figure 4). After 2019, low-value clusters were mainly concentrated in the northern mainland of China, while Tibet and Xinjiang remained identified as significant high-value clusters. Influencing factors analysis

Comparison of three regression models

Among the models applied across the three study periods, the MGWR model demonstrated the best overall fit. In the first period (2005–2010), it achieved an adjusted R2 of 0.880, outperforming the other two models and accounting for 88.0% of the spatial variation in syphilis incidence (Table 1). It also recorded the lowest AICc value, further confirming its superiority. In the second period (2011–2019), the GWR and MGWR models exhibited nearly identical performance, both significantly outperforming the OLS model. In the third period (2020–2022), the MGWR model again showed the best fit, with the highest adjusted R2 and the lowest AICc value. Local regression analysis

The distribution maps of MGWR model regression coefficients across the three periods (Figures 5A–5C) illustrated the effects of various factors on the incidence of syphilis. From 2005 to 2010, X1 had a consistently positive effect, indicating that rising economic levels intensified the upward trend in syphilis incidence. After 2010, its influence became bidirectional, with spatial heterogeneity gradually becoming more evident. X2 and X3 had similar effects on syphilis incidence, both showing bidirectional influences before 2019. This suggested that, before the COVID-19 pandemic, the impact of healthcare resources on syphilis incidence was not uniform across regions but was highly dependent on local conditions, highlighting spatial non-stationarity. After 2019, their effects shifted to entirely negative, indicating that the suppressive impact of healthcare resources on syphilis incidence became more widespread in the post-COVID period. X4 consistently had a positive effect on syphilis incidence throughout the study period, with its influence gradually increasing. Notably, this finding contrasted with the negative effect indicated by the OLS model. X5 had a bidirectional influence in the second period, while all other influences are negative, with the most pronounced polarization, suggesting that the impact of population density on syphilis incidence varied considerably across different regions. Figures 5D–5F presented the characteristics of the residual distribution for the syphilis incidence in the three respective periods. It could be observed that the residual points in each period generally closely follow the diagonal line, indicating that the residuals basically conform to the normality assumption. Discussion

This study employs the JPR model, hierarchical clustering, hotspot analysis, and the MGWR model to conduct a multidimensional spatiotemporal analysis of syphilis development patterns in the mainland of China. The JPR model effectively captures the temporal trends in incidence rates and reveals shifts in these trends. The hierarchical clustering algorithm identifies the spatial evolution of syphilis incidence across different regions, while hotspot analysis detects spatial clustering patterns of syphilis cases. Finally, using syphilis incidence as the outcome variable, the OLS and MGWR models are applied to examine the influencing factors at both global and local scales. The findings indicate that the spread of syphilis in China does not follow a uniform diffusion pattern but is shaped by regional disparities in public health management and socioeconomic structures, resulting in marked spatial heterogeneity.

From 2005 to 2022, the incidence of syphilis in the mainland of China exhibited an overall upward trend, with an AAPC of 6.81%, which was lower than the average annual growth rate of 11.88% observed from 2004 to 2019.20 This difference is primarily attributed to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2019, during which the reallocation and reprioritization of medical resources led to a reduction in syphilis testing, thereby affecting the reported incidence.25 The years 2010 and 2019 were identified as significant joinpoints, with the temporal trend characterized by a pattern of “rapid increase–slow increase–decline” (APC = 22.25%, 2.94%, −4.75%). The 2010 joinpoint is closely linked to the release of the National Program for Prevention and Control of Syphilis in China (2010–2020). The plan significantly curbed the natural growth trajectory of the syphilis epidemic37 through measures such as enhanced surveillance and testing, comprehensive interventions targeting high-risk behaviors, provision of standardized medical services, and strengthened screening and treatment for pregnant women.38 The decline in population mobility and the reduced frequency of sexual activity among sexually active individuals caused by the COVID-19 outbreak may have been key factors contributing to the negative growth in syphilis incidence observed in 2019.15

In hierarchical clustering, cluster I (Beijing, Tianjin, and Jiangsu) consists of economically developed regions. The high-level allocation of public service resources, such as health technicians, provides a material foundation for the prevention and control of infectious diseases,39 contributing to a relatively stable and mildly fluctuating trend in syphilis incidence. Cluster II includes nine geographically adjacent provincial-level regions, such as Shandong, Henan, and Hebei, which are among the most populous in the country. Additionally, provincial-level regions such as Hubei, Henan, and Shaanxi contain several major international transportation hubs.40 Large-scale interprovincial population mobility has led to the formation of regional sexual behavior networks,41 facilitating the spread of syphilis along transportation corridors. Among the 15 provincial-level regions in Cluster III, with the exception of Guangdong, most (such as Hunan, Chongqing, and Jiangxi) have moderate economic development levels. These regions lack both the high population mobility and diagnostic capacity for syphilis found in southeastern coastal regions, as well as the extreme poverty and absence of control measures seen in the northwest. Shanghai and Zhejiang are classified under Cluster IV. Their “high incidence followed by decline” pattern underscores the effectiveness of optimized public health resource allocation in disease control in the economically advanced eastern regions.42 Guangxi and Xinjiang, as ethnic minority autonomous regions, are characterized by insufficient intervention targeting high-risk behaviors and inadequate diagnostic and treatment protocols. As a result, patients in these regions face higher economic burdens and generally exhibit poor treatment adherence compared to those in other provincial-level regions.43 These shared characteristics account for their classification under Cluster V.

The clustering results reflect similarities in the shapes of incidence curves, rather than uniformity in absolute incidence levels. Therefore, hotspot analysis was conducted to identify high-risk areas with elevated absolute syphilis incidence rates. The findings reveal that the spatiotemporal variation in syphilis incidence displays marked regional disparities. In terms of spatial patterns, incidence rates vary gradually along the southwest-northeast axis, whereas a “U-shaped” distribution is evident along the northwest-southeast direction, with higher rates at both ends and lower rates in the middle. This distribution is closely linked to regional levels of economic development.44 In the southeastern coastal regions, advanced economic conditions, frequent population mobility, and stronger diagnostic capabilities for syphilis facilitate the timely and accurate detection and reporting of cases. However, the northwestern regions, predominantly inhabited by ethnic minority populations, continue to experience high incidence rates due to limited awareness of disease prevention and control, as well as a higher prevalence of risky behaviors.34 From a dynamic perspective, the eastern coastal provincial-level regions have undergone a fluctuating pattern, marked by an initial decline followed by a resurgence. In the early stages of economic reform during the 1980s, the expansion of commercial industries may have contributed to a rise in syphilis incidence.45 Subsequent improvements in healthcare resources effectively interrupted transmission chains, but a recent syphilis resurgence has been observed due to the influx of sex workers from inland regions.46 In contrast, inland provincial-level regions - particularly those in the northwest—have shown a continuous upward trend. This is primarily attributed to weak surveillance capacity resulting from poverty, which has left a large number of historically accumulated cases undetected.47 The situation has been further exacerbated by a shortage of medical resources, limited dissemination of sexual health knowledge, and delayed implementation of intervention measures, allowing latent transmission chains to persist and spread over time.48,49 The recent increase in reported cases largely reflects the uncovering of previously hidden infections and highlights gaps in the prevention and control system. This spatiotemporal heterogeneity is fundamentally driven by the combined effects of disparities in socioeconomic development, the effectiveness of public health policy implementation, and patterns of population mobility.

Building upon the revealed spatiotemporal heterogeneity of syphilis incidence, this study further employs global (OLS) and local (GWR/MGWR) regression models to decipher the influence mechanisms of socioeconomic factors on its spatial variation patterns. A comprehensive comparison of model performance across three time periods shows that the MGWR model performs best (Δ Adjusted R2 ≥ 0.48, Δ AICc ≥68). The analysis of influencing factors confirms the geographic non-stationarity of syphilis transmission mechanisms. The economic level indicator (X1) demonstrates a positive effect on the global regression, consistent with the findings of Zhang et al.50 and Zhu et al.51 However, MGWR results indicate that after 2010, the effect of per capita GDP shifted from a uniformly positive influence to a locally bidirectional one, with the direction and strength of local effects depending on each provincial-level region’s socioeconomic context and capacity for disease prevention and control. In contrast, the medical resource indicators (X2, X3) exhibited the opposite trend, shifting from bidirectional effects to consistently negative effects after 2019. This suggests that the allocation of medical resources during COVID-19 prevention and control efforts gradually achieved a nationwide equilibrium.52 These findings highlight the necessity for regionally tailored prevention strategies based on local epidemiological characteristics, rather than adopting a uniform national standard.

Moreover, this study reveals that global statistics may obscure local realities. The urbanization rate (X4) showed a significantly negative correlation in the OLS model during 2020–2022 (β = −0.551, p = 0.004), suggesting that urbanization suppresses incidence. However, the MGWR model revealed that the local regression coefficients for all provincial-level regions during this period were positive (β_local = 0.297–0.374), indicating that urbanization may continuously elevate transmission risk through population aggregation.53 This contradiction essentially arises from the “ecological fallacy”: in more developed regions (e.g., Shanghai), superior healthcare resources enable higher case detection capacity. As urbanization accelerates, improvements in diagnostic efficiency may outpace the actual increase in infections, creating a statistical illusion of “diluted incidence.”54 In reality, the transmission mechanism consistently follows the epidemiological pattern of “urban centers as transmission hubs.”55 The results for the population density factor (X5) further demonstrate that national-level patterns may not apply at the local level. While the OLS results showed that X5 was not significant throughout the study period, the MGWR model revealed that population density had a significant impact on syphilis incidence in certain provincial-level regions. Additionally, the ENP_j value of X5 dropped sharply from 6.699 to 2.246, indicating that as patterns of population mobility become more homogeneous, population size alone is no longer sufficient to explain differences in risk. Instead, social vulnerability is emerging as the primary indicator of risk.48 Based on these findings, this study strongly suggests that national-level trends may fail to accurately reflect actual local risks and may even lead to prevention and control decisions that are entirely counterproductive.

Syphilis prevention and control efforts in the mainland of China are not conducted in isolation but are deeply integrated into the national infectious disease prevention and control master plan and public health system, and are safeguarded by relevant laws and regulations. This work is systematically advanced based on two national-level plans: the National Program for Prevention and Control of Syphilis in China (2010–2020) (referred to as the Syphilis Control Program) and the Implementation Plan for the Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission of HIV, Syphilis and Hepatitis B. The former establishes clear epidemiological objectives such as reducing syphilis incidence and increasing testing coverage, while constructing a comprehensive prevention and control system encompassing health education, testing and counseling, standardized diagnosis and treatment, prevention of mother-to-child transmission, and monitoring and evaluation.38 Studies have confirmed that the implementation of this program has achieved significant results.37,56 The latter further strengthens syphilis screening for pregnant women, standardized interventions, and preventive measures for congenital syphilis.57 Furthermore, the Measures for the Prevention and Treatment of Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs), revised and promulgated by the former Ministry of Health in 2012, have also strengthened the comprehensive prevention and control capabilities for STDs such as syphilis at the institutional level. Regarding resource allocation, the Syphilis Control Program explicitly outlines guiding principles such as “tailored approaches based on local conditions” and “tiered management with defined responsibilities.” For instance, the program states: “STDs departments should be strengthened, or specialized personnel trained in medical institutions at the county-level and below,” and “specialized prevention and control funds should be established in high-prevalence regions.” These measures underscore the necessity of differentiated allocation of healthcare resources. In other words, prevention resources should be prioritized for epidemic hotspots to effectively curb primary transmission sources. As the critical link for implementing prevention strategies, enhancing syphilis screening and treatment capabilities at county-level and below healthcare institutions is particularly crucial. Recently, the Implementation Plan for the Project to Strengthen Primary Healthcare Services was approved by the State Council. This plan provides crucial policy support for enhancing the prevention and control capabilities of primary healthcare institutions and improving the syphilis surveillance network,58 and is expected to further advance the long-term management of syphilis.

In summary, we propose the following four suggestions for prevention and control. Firstly, the National Health Commission should take the lead in developing a tiered prevention and control plan. During periods of sharp increases in incidence, an interdepartmental emergency response should be activated; during periods of gradual increase, a long-term governance mechanism should be implemented. Secondly, in provincial-level regions where syphilis incidence continues to rise due to inadequate medical resources, the central government should provide targeted fiscal transfer payments. Efforts should focus on improving the ratio of health technicians per 10,000 people to meet the national average and on establishing a telemedicine collaboration network that covers county-level Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDCs). Thirdly, border hotspot areas (such as Xinjiang and Tibet) and emerging high-risk regions (such as Yunnan and Guizhou) should be included on the national key supervision list. Provincial-level regions identified as hotspots should be required to report regularly on their progress in syphilis prevention and control. Border regions should also enhance screening and management of syphilis among cross-border mobile populations. Finally, for regions with high economic heterogeneity, a “one province, one policy” approach should be adopted. For example, special economic zones should prioritize the regulation of entertainment venues, while border regions should strengthen health screening in the tourism sector. In regions where medical resources are broadly constrained, an interprovincial medical resource allocation platform should be established to enable intelligent redistribution and balanced allocation of healthcare resources across regions.

This study, based on nationwide syphilis surveillance data spanning 31 provincial-level regions over an 18-year period, pioneers the application of a time-decomposed MGWR model to examine the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of syphilis prevalence. Compared to traditional models (OLS/GWR), this spatial analytical approach—incorporating features such as the dynamic evolution of bandwidth parameters and the heterogeneity pattern of ENP_j—significantly improves the model’s explanatory power. Notably, the study reveals that influencing factors such as the urbanization rate may exert entirely opposite effects at different spatial scales (macro and micro), thereby offering a practical analytical framework for avoiding ecological fallacies.

In future work, we will focus on finer spatial scales (such as municipal or county levels), employing more advanced spatial modeling methods—such as distance decay functions or population-characteristic weighted matrices—along with necessary sensitivity analyses, to develop a multivariate analytical model that integrates spatiotemporal features and social factors. A comparative analysis will be carried out between inland and coastal cities with significant urbanization disparities to identify differences in key social characteristics, thereby further investigating the root causes of ecological fallacies.

Limitations of the study

However, several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. Underreporting in primary healthcare institutions, particularly in border regions, may introduce information bias and lead to an underestimation of the actual incidence, due to factors such as patients’ healthcare-seeking behavior and the diagnostic accuracy of physicians. The absence of behavioral data for high-risk populations and detailed demographic characteristics limits the ability to conduct micro-level analyses of transmission chains. More sophisticated spatial weight matrices and comprehensive sensitivity analyses should be utilized. Analyses at the provincial level may mask intra-provincial disparities, highlighting the need for more granular research units.

Method details

The socioeconomic indicator data we used were obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook and the National Bureau of Statistics. Area data were collected from the official websites of the provincial governments. Geographic information was sourced from the National Geomatics Center of China. The reported incidence of syphilis (number of confirmed cases per 100,000 population per year) for the 31 provincial-level regions in the mainland of China (excluding Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan) were retrieved from the China Public Health Science Data Center. The disease classification codes for syphilis (A50-A53) follow the standard International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). As of the time of writing, the database contained syphilis incidence data from 2005 to 2020. Additional syphilis incidence data for 2021-2022 were obtained from the China Health Statistics Yearbook. Finally, we constructed an 18-year dataset (2005-2022) containing provincial syphilis incidence for 31 administrative divisions in the mainland of China. Provincial-level regions were used as the units of analysis, and the reported syphilis incidence (encompassed all clinical stages) served as the outcome variable for the study of spatiotemporal patterns and social determinants. Since only aggregated data were used and no personal or identifiable information was involved, ethical approval was not required.

Based on the reported incidence of syphilis in the mainland of China from 2005 to 2022, we first analyzed the trend in the incidence using Joinpoint 5.0 software. Subsequently, 20 feature values of the time series (Table S1) were calculated with R 4.1.2 software, and the optimal number of clusters was determined through the Silhouette Coefficient. A hierarchical clustering method was then applied to identify the spatial aggregation patterns of syphilis incidence. Following this, hotspot analysis was conducted using ArcGIS 10.8 software to clarify the spatial distribution of cold and hot spots in the incidence of syphilis. Finally, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression, Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) and Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR) model were used to analyze the influence mechanism of socioeconomic factors such as per capita GDP (X1), number of health technicians per 10,000 population (X2), number of medical institutions per 10,000 population (X3), urbanization rate (X4) and population density (X5) on the incidence of syphilis using MGWR 2.2 software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis

Joinpoint Regression (JPR) is primarily used to analyze trend changes in time series data. The modeling process employs the Grid Search Method (GSM). The core idea of this method is to exhaustively evaluate all possible combinations of joinpoints in piecewise functions using GSM. For each candidate model, the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) and the Mean Squared Error (MSE) are calculated. By systematically comparing the MSE across all candidates, the optimal set of joinpoints is selected. Parameter estimation is then performed based on the identified segmented intervals. The specific model is as follows:(Equation 1)E[y|t]=eβ0+β1t+δ1(t−τ1)++⋯+δk(t−τk)+Where k is the number of joinpoint points, τk is unknown joinpoint points, β0 is the invariant parameter, β1 is the regression coefficient, and δk is the regression coefficient of the segment k. When (t - τk) > 0, (t - τk)+ = t - τk, otherwise (t - τk)+ = 0.

In the Joinpoint trend analysis, the default permutation test was employed, with 4,500 permutations and a significance level of α = 0.05. The primary outcome indicators of the JPR model include the Annual Percent Change (APC), the Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC), and their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). The APC represents the annual percentage change in incidence, reflecting localized trends within each segmented interval. The AAPC characterizes the overall average trend across multiple intervals. An APC or AAPC greater than 0 indicates an increasing trend, while a value less than 0 indicates a decreasing trend. When no joinpoint is present, the APC and AAPC are identical, indicating a single consistent trend in incidence. The specific calculation formulas are as follows:(Equation 2)APC=(eβ−1)×100%(Equation 3)AAPC=(∑i=1k(APCi×ωi)∑i=1kωi)Where k is the number of joinpoint points, APCi is the APC for the i th segment, β is the maximum likelihood estimate of the coefficient of each segment interval, ωi is the length of each segment in the range of years.

Cluster analysis

Clustering algorithms are generally divided into two main approaches: agglomerative and divisive. The agglomerative approach follows a bottom-up strategy, where each data point is initially treated as an individual cluster. Inter-cluster distances are then calculated iteratively, and the closest clusters are merged step by step until all data points are combined into a single cluster. In contrast, the divisive approach uses a top-down strategy, starting with one cluster that contains all data points. This cluster is recursively split, based on predefined criteria, by identifying and dividing the most heterogeneous cluster until a stopping condition is met.59 Among these methods, agglomerative algorithms are more widely used due to their higher computational efficiency. In this study, the agglomerative hierarchical clustering algorithm was employed.

The Silhouette Coefficient is used to evaluate clustering performance by quantifying intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation,60 with values ranging from -1 to 1. Its value close to 1 indicates excellent clustering performance, while a value near -1 suggests poor clustering quality. Due to its computational efficiency and strong interpretability, this metric was selected as the evaluation criterion for clustering in this study. 20 curve feature values were extracted from the time series of syphilis incidence in 31 regions from 2005 to 2022 to quantitatively characterize syphilis incidence patterns (Table S1). Based on these feature values, hierarchical clustering was performed to analyze the development patterns of syphilis across 31 regions in the mainland of China. The calculation of feature values, determination of Silhouette Coefficients, and implementation of hierarchical clustering were all performed using R 4.1.2 software, with detailed code provided in Document S1.

Hotspot analysis

Global spatial autocorrelation analysis is used to determine whether there is an overall spatial clustering pattern within the study area. In this study, Moran’s I was employed to measure global spatial autocorrelation, with values ranging from -1 to 1. A Moran’s I value greater than 0 indicates positive spatial autocorrelation, with values closer to 1 signifying stronger clustering, while a value of 0 suggests that spatial autocorrelation is random.61 When global spatial autocorrelation is statistically significant, local spatial autocorrelation can be used to more precisely identify specific clustering areas. Hotspot analysis, based on the Getis-Ord Gi∗ statistic, reveals the clustering of high or low values of variables across spatial regions, thereby identifying hotspot and cold spot areas. A hotspot refers to a region where both the variable value and the values in neighboring areas are high. Conversely, a cold spot refers to a region where both the variable value and the values in surrounding areas are low. The specific calculation formula is as follows:(Equation 4)Gi∗=∑j=1nωij(xj−x¯)Sn∑j=1nωij2−(∑j=1nωij)2n−1x¯=∑j=1nxjn,S=∑j=1nxj2n−x¯2Where n is the number of regional points, xi and xj are the values of variables (e.g., morbidity) for regional points i and j, respectively, and x¯ is the mean of all observations, and ωij is the proximity of regional points i and j. At the α = 0.05 test level, Gi∗>0 suggests that the region is a high-value spatial aggregation, which is called a hotspot region; if Gi∗<0 suggests that the region is a low-value spatial aggregation, which is called a coldspot region. The larger the absolute value of Gi∗, the more significant the clustering effect of high or low values.

Following the common practice in spatiotemporal analysis of sexually transmitted diseases at the provincial level in China,62,63,64 we used the fixed distance band method (default option) to construct the spatial weight matrix. This method has been validated in multiple similar studies. This method automatically calculates the threshold distance while ensuring that each feature has at least one neighbor. Features within this threshold are assigned a weight of 1 and included in the calculation of the target feature, whereas features beyond the threshold are assigned a weight of 0 and excluded from the calculation.65 Euclidean Distance (default option) was used as the distance metric to represent straight-line proximity in geographic space. The specific operational procedure of our application of ArcGIS 10.8 software was as follows: the “Spatial Statistics Tools” → “Mapping Clusters” → “Hot Spot Analysis” tool was sequentially accessed within the ArcToolbox.

Influencing factors analysis

GWR is a method based on local regression analysis. Its core principle is to capture spatial heterogeneity through a varying-parameter model, and it is widely applied in the study of spatial non-stationarity. Built upon non-parametric regression techniques, this method integrates spatial location information into the parameter estimation process and uses locally weighted least squares to independently fit each location, thereby uncovering how regression relationships vary across geographic space. The calculation formula is:(Equation 5)yi=∑rxirβr(ui,vi)+εiWhere (ui, vi) denotes the centroid coordinates of the i-th grid, yi indicates the incidence of syphilis in the i-th grid (dependent variable), xir refers to the r-th influencing factor in the i-th grid (independent variable), βr is the regression coefficient corresponding to the r-th independent variable in the i-th grid, εi represents the random error term.

Although GWR enhances the global regression model by allowing parameter variation, it assumes that all variables operate at the same spatial scale, which restricts the model’s flexibility. To address this limitation, MGWR was proposed.66 In this model, the optimal bandwidth for each independent variable can be determined using the Golden Section method. The calculation formula is:(Equation 6)yi=∑j=1kβbwj(ui,vi)xij+εiWhere bwj denotes the bandwidth used for the regression coefficient of the j-th variable, yi represents the syphilis incidence in the i-th grid, xij denotes the j-th influencing factor in the i-th grid, βbwj refers to the regression coefficient of the j-th variable in the i-th grid under the bandwidth bwj, k denotes the sample size.

MGWR is essentially a spatial non-stationarity model designed to capture local variations in spatial relationships and is more commonly applied to cross-sectional data. To overcome this limitation, the study period was divided into three intervals (2005–2010, 2011–2019, and 2020–2022) based on the results of Joinpoint regression analysis, allowing for a synchronized investigation of whether the influencing factors change dynamically over time. Firstly, OLS regression was conducted to analyze the global relationship with syphilis incidence. To avoid multicollinearity, independent variables with a Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) greater than 5 were excluded. Consequently, the variables included in the final model were the aforementioned X1 -X5. We utilized MGWR 2.2 software to perform local regression analyses on each influencing factor, successively applying both the GWR and MGWR models. The analysis was configured with an Adaptive bandwidth and Bisquare kernel function for the Spatial Kernel, “Golden Section” for Bandwidth Searching, and Gaussian as the Model Type. Model performance was evaluated and compared using the adjusted goodness of fit (adjusted R2) and the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc). A higher adjusted R2 value indicates that the independent variables explain a greater proportion of the variance in the dependent variable, reflecting a better model fit, while a lower AICc value suggests a superior goodness-of-fit.
