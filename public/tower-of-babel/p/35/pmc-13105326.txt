Entering the ring: circular RNA in pulmonary macrophage antibacterial responses
Authors: Brian T Campfield
Published: 2025

The lung is a uniquely complex environment, wherein mucosal biology, cardiopulmonary dynamics, and environmental exposures (including commensal and pathogenic organisms) interact with an immune milieu to maintain tissue homeostasis. Central to pulmonary immune homeostasis lies the macrophage. As a tissue-resident immune sentinel, lung macrophages incorporate multiple input signals to maintain a poised readiness response: tolerance to innocuous cues and inflammation when threatened by pathogens.1 The cellular mechanisms regulating this response occur at the genetic, transcriptional, protein, metabolic, and signaling levels.2,3

The role of circular RNA (circRNA), a distinct noncoding RNA product, in regulating pulmonary immune homeostasis is poorly understood. First described nearly 50 years ago, circRNAs were identified in humans a decade later and are widely distributed in eukaryotic cells.4,5 The advent of RNA sequencing renewed broad interest in circRNA as more than a missplicing artifact.6 Owing to their circular structure, lacking 5′ caps and polyadenylated-3′ tails, circRNAs are resistant to ribonuclease, often highly abundant, and more stable than linear messenger RNA.7 Circular RNAs function as transcriptional regulators, microRNA sponges, RNA stability modulators, and even translated peptides, where they have been implicated in a number of diseases, including cancer, autoimmunity, pulmonary hypertension, and several others.7-9 Several immune cells have a profile of detectable circRNAs (circRNA-ome), and macrophages have been shown to have distinct circRNA profiles under proinflammatory or anti-inflammatory conditions.10

In this issue of the Journal, Zhang and colleagues11 pilot an investigation of the role of circRNAs in pulmonary immunity to the opportunistic lung pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In a mouse model of acute pulmonary infection with the P. aeruginosa strain PA01, the authors identify thousands of expressed circRNAs, including 123 abundantly and differentially expressed circRNAs, the most highly upregulated being circSlc7a11. Pursuing this circRNA further, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) sorting to isolate lung cells identifies myeloid cells as producing the highest quantities of circSlc7a11, and murine cell line studies using MH-S (alveolar macrophages) and MLE-12 (lung epithelium) confirm it to be induced in macrophages but not epithelial cells. In a ­separate line of study, immortalized bone marrow–derived ­macrophages (iBMDMs) are subjected to circSlc7a11 knockdown using a lentiviral–short hairpin RNA (shRNA) construct, and this tool determines the impact on inflammatory gene expression. Compared with controls, sh-circSlc7a11 iBMDMs have increased Il1b expression, yet phagocytosis and cell survival are reduced, suggesting that this circRNA regulates macrophage inflammatory responses.

At the molecular level, Zhang and colleagues11 report that circSlc7a11 localizes to the nucleus, and using biotinylated pulldown techniques, they show that components of the spliceosome machinery are interactants, with PUF60 (60 kDa poly(U)-binding-splicing factor) being the strongest. Further validation in iBMDMs identifies circSlc7a11-mediated Il1b transcriptional inhibition via PUF60. Returning to an in vivo model of P. aeruginosa acute lung infection, the authors employ 2 techniques to understand the role of circSlc7a11 in acute PA01 infection: (1) an intratracheal adeno-associated virus (AAV)-shRNA approach that identifies circSlc7a11 suppression increases Il1b expression and neutrophil recruitment, and (2) intratracheal adoptive transfer of shRNA-edited iBMDMs corroborates that circSlc7a11 suppression increases Il1b transcription and reduces lung bacterial burden, together suggesting that circSlc7a11 functions to limit macrophage expression of Il1b and acute lung injury. The conclusion here is that circSlc7a11 is a novel molecular modulator of pulmonary immune poise, providing evidence that circRNAs can enter the arena of mucosal bacterial immunity.

Although it is a notable step forward for the field to identify a mechanistic role for circRNAs in regulating innate immune responses in lung cells, the evidence provided for circSlc7a11 as a critical regulator of macrophage activation in the lung warrants additional substantiation. Although valid tools for studying macrophage biology, MH-S and iBMDM cells may not represent the unique and diverse population of myeloid cells in the lung. To this end, single-cell sequencing of AAV5-sh-circSlc7a11 could have revealed the cell-specific transcriptomic circSlc7a11-dependent signature in lung cells, data that may be broadly informative in the field of respiratory biology. This work also provides paradoxical findings that warrant explication—specifically, how sh-circSlc7a11 yields increased Il1b production but decreased phagocytosis and reduced macrophage viability. Enticingly, these observations may suggest a fine regulatory role for circRNA in lung macrophages that deserves further mechanistic evaluation, including whether circSlc7a11 interaction with other components of the spliceosome may fine-tune inflammatory signaling pathways.

Obviously, pulmonary host-pathogen responses have bacteria-specific mechanisms and impacts. As such, comparing the circSlc7a11 impact on P. aeruginosa infection with that of more common, virulent bacterial pathogens, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Hemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, or Klebsiella pneumoniae, are essential to understand generalizability and translational potential of this work. Further, understanding the circRNAs differentially induced by pulmonary bacteria may provide further insight into their role in pulmonary immune homeostasis.

In sum, this work by Zhang and colleagues11 opens a path of rational investigation into the role of circRNAs in pulmonary host defense, myeloid cell immune function, and an implied link to the inflammasome activation. Like most high-quality research, these findings address several important questions while begging many more, the answers to which will be critical to moving forward the fields of respiratory biology, immunology, and pulmonology.
