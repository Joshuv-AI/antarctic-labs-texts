Predictive Value of Life's Crucial 9 for Cardiovascular and All‐Cause Mortality: A Prospective Cohort Study From the NHANES 2007 to 2018
Authors: Jinzhuo Ge, Wenyao Peng, Jiapeng Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe predictive value of Life's Crucial 9 (LC9), a recently proposed cardiovascular health risk score combining psychological health and Life's Essential 8 (LE8), remains unclear.Methods and ResultsIn this cohort study, we included 16 290 adults without cardiovascular disease from the 2007 to 2018 cycles of NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey). The LC9 was the mean of the LE8 score and the depression score, which represented a dimension of psychological health. The study outcomes were cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality. Cox proportional hazard models were fitted to estimate the association of LC9 and LE8 scores with outcomes. The differences in Harrell's concordance index, net reclassification improvement index, and integrated discrimination improvement were calculated to assess the predictive ability of the depression score in addition to the LE8 score. During a median follow‐up of 7.08 years, 879 (5.40%) participants died, and 242 (1.49%) died from cardiovascular disease. The adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of per LE8 10‐score increase for cardiovascular mortality was 0.80 (95% CI, 0.72–0.88; P<0.001) and the adjusted HR of per LC9 10‐score increase was 0.77 (95% CI, 0.69–0.86; P<0.001). Adding the depression score to the LE8 score, the improvement in concordance index for cardiovascular mortality was 0.001 (95% CI, −0.001 to 0.003; P=0.30), the net reclassification improvement index was 10.6% (95% CI, −7.6% to 18.9%; P=0.073), and the IDI was 0.002 (95% CI, 0.000–0.007; P=0.033). The results for all‐cause mortality showed similar patterns.ConclusionsCompared with the LE8, the improvement in the predictive value of LC9 was negligible. It may not be necessary to add a depression score to the current cardiovascular health score.Keywords: all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, Life's crucial 9, NHANES, predictive valueSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Lifestyle

In 2010, the American Heart Association defined cardiovascular health (CVH) and created Life's Simple 7, an accessible and operational metric for improving CVH. 1 In 2020, as more evidence about CVH emerged, the American Heart Association introduced an enhanced approach to assessing CVH: Life's Essential 8 (LE8). 2 In the LE8 score, the scoring algorithm has been updated and sleep health has been incorporated into the Life's Simple 7. Recent evidence has consistently demonstrated that LE8 was associated with various health outcomes including cardiovascular outcomes, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, 7 chronic kidney disease, 8 dementia, 9 life expectancy, 10 and all‐cause mortality. 3 , 4 , 6

Depression is a major global mental health issue, affecting an estimated 300 million people worldwide. 11 Depression has become the leading cause of disability among all mental disorders. 12 World Health Organization projected that major depression would represent the top contributor to chronic disease burden worldwide by 2030. 13 Emerging evidence has demonstrated that depression is an independent non‐traditional risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD). 14 Although the American Heart Association writing group acknowledged the role of psychological health in promoting CVH, they did not include psychological health in the CVH metric due to a lack of agreement on how to combine psychological health factors and concerns about the indistinct role of psychological health on CVH. 2 A recent perspective has further emphasized the importance of psychological health in preventing CVD and introduced a new metric called Life's Crucial 9 (LC9), which integrates psychological health into LE8. 15 However, to date, the predictive performance of LC9 for health outcomes has not been explored.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate the associations of LC9 with cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality. Furthermore, we examined the additional predictive value of depression in addition to LE8 for cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality.

METHODS

All data are publicly available and can be accessed at the NHANES (the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx). Relevant SAS and R code is available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

The first author had full access to all data in the study and takes responsibility for its integrity and the data analysis.

Study Population

In this analysis, data were derived from NHANES, an ongoing, national, and periodic health survey program designed to assess individuals' health and nutritional status in the United States. The design and operation of NHANES have been described previously. 16 The NHANES was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We used NHANES data collected from the 2007 to 2018 cycles as the baseline. In these cycles, 59 842 participants were initially screened. We included participants >20 years old (n=34 770), and excluded those who were pregnant (n=372), had cancer (n=3363), had CVD (n=3056), had incomplete CVH score data (n=10 442), had missing data on covariates (n=1228), and were lost to follow‐up (n=19). Finally, a total of 16 290 adults were included in this study (Figure 1).

Measurement of CVH Scores

The LE8 score consisted of 4 health behaviors (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep duration) and 4 health factors (body mass index, non‐high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, blood glucose, and blood pressure). Detailed algorithms for calculating the LE8 scores for each of the metrics have been previously published. 2 In brief, each of the 8 CVH metrics was scored ranging from 0 to 100 points, assigned by a modified Delphi approach among the expert panel members based on health outcomes and risk associations. The overall LE8 score was calculated as the mean of the 8 metrics.

Diet metric was assessed by the Healthy Eating Index 2015. 17 The diet information collected from 2 24‐hour dietary recalls, which were combined with the US Department of Agriculture food patterns equivalents data to construct and calculate the Healthy Eating Index 2015 score. 3 The simple Healthy Eating Index scoring algorithm method (by individual) was used to compute the Healthy Eating Index 2015 score using an official SAS code provided by the National Cancer Institute. 18 Physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep duration, history of diabetes, and medication history were obtained through standardized self‐reported questionnaires. Height, weight, and blood pressure were measured during physical examinations. The body mass index was calculated as the weight in kilograms divided by the height in meters squared. Blood samples were collected and sent to central laboratories for the determination of blood lipids, fasting blood glucose, and glycated hemoglobin levels.

The depression score was calculated based on the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) score, which is a validated structured questionnaire for depression screening. The higher PHQ‐9 scores indicate higher levels of currently present depressive symptoms. The depression score was assigned as 100, 75, 50, 25, and 0 corresponding to 0 to 4, 5 to 9, 10 to 14, 15 to 19, and 20 to 27 in PHQ‐9 score, respectively. 19 The LC9 score was calculated as the mean of the 8 metrics in the LE8 score and the depression score.

Outcomes

The study outcomes were cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality. The mortality data were obtained from National Death Index death certificate records through December 31, 2019. The follow‐up time was defined as the interval from the interview date to the date of mortality or through December 31, 2019 for alive participants. The underlying cause of mortality was identified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision. Cardiovascular mortality was defined as death from heart diseases (codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51) or cerebrovascular diseases (codes I60–I69).

Covariates

The covariates included age, sex, race or ethnicity, education level, marital status, and the ratio of family income to poverty, which were collected through standardized questionnaires. Race and ethnicity were categorized as Mexican American, other Hispanic, non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and other race including multiracial. The education level was categorized as less than high school, high school graduate/general educational development or equivalent, and more than high school. The marital status was categorized as never married, married/living with partner, and divorced, separated, or widowed. The ratio of family income to poverty was categorized as <1.3, 1.3 to 3.5, and >3.5.

Statistical Analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study participants were described as mean±SD for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. We divided the participants into 4 groups according to the LC9 score quartile (<61.11, ≥61.11 to <71.11, ≥71.11 to <80, ≥80). The characteristics were compared using the analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test for categorical variables.

We fitted Cox proportional hazard models to assess the association of PHQ‐9 score as categorical and continuous variables with outcomes of cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality after adjusting for LE8 score and covariates. The associations between 2 CVH score quartile and per 10‐score increase and outcomes of cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality were also estimated by Cox proportional hazard models. Two models were constructed: (1) model 1, an unadjusted model; and (2) model 2, a model adjusted for age (as a continuous variable), sex, race or ethnicity, education level, marital status, and the ratio of family income to poverty. We examined the subgroup interactions of age (as a categorial variable), sex, race or ethnicity, education level, marital status, and the ratio of family income to poverty in the association of the LC9 score with outcomes of cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality by fitting Cox models. We did the sensitivity for the outcome of cardiovascular mortality by constructing a competing risk model accounting for noncardiovascular mortality as a competing risk. The proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using the Schoenfeld residuals method, and no violations were observed.

Furthermore, we employed the LE8 score and covariates as the base model. We calculated the differences in Harrell's concordance index, the continuous net reclassification improvement index, and the integrated discrimination improvement to evaluate the improvement in discrimination and reclassification for depression score or PHQ‐9 score in addition to the base model with cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality as outcomes. 20 , 21 The model prediction statistics were calculated for the median duration of follow‐up.

In our analyses, we did not apply survey weights because we focused on internal comparisons within the study sample. We considered 2‐sided P<0.05 as statistically significant. Analyses were conducted with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and R software, version 4.1.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics and Mortality

The baseline characteristics of the participants were summarized by the LC9 score quartile in Table 1. Overall, the mean age was 46.50±16.33 years, 51.85% were women, and 41.66% were non‐Hispanic White. The mean LE8 score was 67.70±14.50, and the mean LC9 score was 70.31±13.44. Compared to those with the lower LC9 score, participants with the higher LC9 score were younger; more likely to have higher education; less likely to be divorced, separated, or widowed; less likely to have depression; and have higher wealth. During the median follow‐up of 7.08 years (interquartile range, 4.17–10.00 years), 879 (5.40%) participants died, and 242 (1.49%) died from CVD. The rate of all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality decreased with an increasing CVH score (Tables 2 and 3).

Association of PHQ‐9 Score or CVH Scores With Mortality

After adjusting for LE8 score and covariates, PHQ‐9 score ≥10 was marginally significantly associated with 57% (hazard ratio [HR], 1.57 [95% CI, 1.02–2.44]; P=0.042) higher risk of cardiovascular mortality and significantly associated with 39% (HR, 1.39 [95% CI, 1.10–1.75]; P=0.005) higher risk of all‐cause mortality than PHQ‐9 score <10 (Table S1).

Both the LE8 score and the LC9 score were significantly associated with the risk of cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality in each model. Compared with the lowest LE8 score quartile, the adjusted HR for the second quartile was 0.92 (95% CI, 0.68–1.24; P=0.58), for the third quartile was 0.55 (95% CI, 0.38–0.80; P=0.002), and for the highest quartile was 0.51 (95% CI, 0.32–0.81; P=0.004) for cardiovascular mortality in model 2. The adjusted HR for per LE8 10‐score increase in the association with cardiovascular mortality was 0.80 (95% CI, 0.72–0.88; P<0.001). In terms of the associations between the LC9 score and cardiovascular mortality, compared with the lowest quartile, the adjusted HRs decreased from 0.90 (95% CI, 0.67–1.21; P=0.50) for the second quartile, to 0.53 (95% CI, 0.36–0.78; P=0.001) for the third quartile, and further to 0.50 (95% CI, 0.32–0.79; P=0.003) for the highest quartile in model 2. The per LC9 10‐score increase was associated with a 23% reduced risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.69–0.86]; P<0.001). The competing risk model showed consistent results (Table 2). These patterns were similar for all‐cause mortality (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis showed significant interactions between the LC9 score and age, sex, and education level subgroups with the risk of cardiovascular mortality, and age and race and ethnicity subgroups with the risk of all‐cause mortality. The associations for both cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality were stronger in the younger subgroups. The HR for cardiovascular mortality was stronger in the female subgroup than in the male subgroup. The HR for cardiovascular mortality was stronger in participants with higher education level. The HR for all‐cause mortality was close to 1 in Mexican American and weaker than other ethnicities (Figures 2 and 3).

Improvement of Discrimination and Reclassification by the Depression Score

Compared with the LE8 score covariates (base model), the improvement in concordance index for the LE8 score and depression score for cardiovascular mortality was 0.001 (95% CI, −0.001 to 0.003; P=0.30). Adding the depression score to the base model, the net reclassification improvement index for cardiovascular mortality was 10.6% (95% CI, −7.6% to 18.9%; P=0.073) and the integrated discrimination improvement was 0.002 (95% CI, 0.000–0.007; P=0.033). As for all‐cause mortality, the improvement in concordance index for the depression score was 0.002 (95% CI, 0.000–0.003; P=0.13), the net reclassification improvement index was 8.8% (95% CI, 3.7%–13.1%; P<0.001), and the integrated discrimination improvement was 0.002 (95% CI, 0.000–0.006; P=0.013).

Similarly, the improvement in concordance index for PHQ‐9 score for cardiovascular mortality was 0.002 (95% CI, 0.000–0.003; P=0.13) and for all‐cause mortality was 0.002 (95% CI, 0.000–0.004; P=0.042) compared with the base model. The net reclassification improvement index for PHQ‐9 score for cardiovascular mortality was 18.4% (95% CI, −5.0% to 26.1%; P=0.053) and for all‐cause mortality was 14.2% (95% CI, 7.8%–18.2%; P=0.007). The integrated discrimination improvement for PHQ‐9 score for cardiovascular mortality was 0.002 (95% CI, 0.000–0.007; P=0.013) and for all‐cause mortality was 0.003 (95% CI, 0.001–0.006; P=0.013) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Using nationwide data from NHANES 2007 to 2018, we first demonstrated that the associations of the LC9 score with cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality were statistically significant. However, in addition to the LE8 score, the depression score provided a small improvement in predicting cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality. Given the complexity of depression assessment, these findings indicate that adding depression to LE8 may have limited impact for CVH risk assessment.

A couple of meta‐analyses reported that depression was associated with the risk of CVD incidence and mortality independent of traditional risk factors. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 In a meta‐analysis involving 893 850 participants, depression was associated with an 30% increased risk of myocardial infarction and coronary heart disease with adjusted for demographic information and health behaviors. 26 Another meta‐analysis revealed that depression was an independent risk factor for stroke after pooling the results of 28 prospective cohort studies. 25 These 2 meta‐analyses included general population aged 18 years or older without specific exclusion criteria. A separate meta‐analysis including 198 589 community residents aged 60 years or older showed that late‐life depression was independently associated with a significant increase in the risk of cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality. 28 Although there was some difference among the population in our study and in these meta‐analyses, we found the consistent results in participants aged 20 years or older without CVD. The results in our study showed that PHQ‐9 score was independently associated with higher risk of mortality. The associations of the LC9 score with outcomes were slightly stronger than those of the LE8 score, but the improvement was negligible (HR for the LE8 score, 0.80 for cardiovascular mortality, 0.82 for all‐cause mortality versus HR for the LC9 score, 0.77 for cardiovascular mortality, 0.80 for all‐cause mortality). Furthermore, after adding the depression score to the LE8 score, the discrimination and reclassification enhanced a little. Overall, the addition of the depression score slightly increased the predictive ability of the CVH score, but this improvement may not be clinically impactful.

Based on existing evidence about the bidirectional association between depression and cardiovascular risk factors, we could speculate that the relation of depression with traditional risk factors may undermine the additional predictive value of depression. On the one hand, depression could influence the traditional risk factors through various pathways. Positive psychological health creates a foundational context for maintaining or improving health behaviors. 14 Reversely, depression lead to poorer diet quality, lower levels of physical activity, smoking, sleep disorders, and being overweight or obese. 29 , 30 , 31 In addition, depression triggers the activation of nervous‐humoral regulation and increasing levels of inflammation, which consequently increases the levels of blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 On the other hand, longitudinal studies demonstrated that significant direct associations of traditional risk factors led to an increased risk of depression. 37

Another potential reason for the limited predictive ability of the depression score in our study may be inaccurate responses to the PHQ‐9 due to the different subjective understanding among participants. The distinguishing between the 2 intermediate answers in PHQ‐9: 2 (several days) and 3 (more than half the days) was vague. 38 A previous study in people with spinal cord injury found that the third category (more than half the days) was rarely chosen and led to rating scale inversions. 39 The PHQ‐9 missed some important symptoms of depression such as the ability to make changes. Moreover, the PHQ‐9 focused only on the frequency of symptoms and neglected the intensity of the respondent's symptoms. 40 Additionally, the spectrum of depression severity in our study sample was relatively mild so that the low event rates in participants with the low depression score could limit the statistical power to estimate the unbiased improvement of depression for prediction. Moreover, the outcome of this study was mortality rather than incidence. In addition to CVH factors, other factors such as medical treatment levels, care quality, and present morbidity could contribute to mortality compared with incidence, potentially diluting the predictive ability of depression.

This study has the following limitations. First, we evaluated the additional predictive ability of depression only. Future studies could investigate other individual or combined psychological health factors, such as anxiety, loneliness, chronic and traumatic stress, and social integration. Second, the study sample was obtained from only the American population and was highly selected from the entire NHANES cohort, which may influence the generalizability of our findings. Third, some CVH factors including diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep duration, were collected through self‐reported questionnaires, which may lead to measurement and memory biases. Fourth, although we adjusted for all measured covariates, unmeasured factors (eg, end stage renal disease, autoimmune diseases, or other chronic comorbidities) may confound the results.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, LC9 score was associated with cardiovascular mortality and all‐cause mortality. However, after adding the depression into the LE8, the predictive ability was not remarkably enhanced. Based on the findings, it may not be clinically impactful to include depression in the CVH score. Further studies are warranted to investigate the additional predictive value of other psychological health factors.
