A multidisciplinary management model for severe gestational hypertriglyceridemia: using plasmapheresis to prevent pancreatitis in pregnancy
Authors: Xixi Zheng, Peng Xia, Cai Yue, Jinghua Xia, Yue Zhou, Liting Chen, Yingna Song, Jinsong Gao, Naishi Li, Xuemei Li, Limeng Chen, Yan Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere gestational hypertriglyceridemia can lead to life-threatening pancreatitis with high maternal and fetal mortality, yet there are no established guidelines for managing these patients. We report two cases of severe gestational hypertriglyceridemia managed through a multidisciplinary approach. With the collaboration of obstetricians, endocrinologists, and nephrologists, we utilized a novel plasmapheresis circuit—combining centrifugation and filtration plasmapheresis (CFPP)—and performed regular sessions via peripheral venous access without the use of plasma or albumin. This treatment effectively lowered triglycerides in an attempt to prevent gestational pancreatitis, with no major maternal or fetal complications. We also conducted a systematic review of published reports on regular apheresis for severe gestational hypertriglyceridemia, identifying twenty-one additional cases. In these cases, treatment was generally administered twice weekly or every other week, with therapeutic plasmapheresis being the most frequently employed modality. The mean triglyceride reduction rate was 45.1%, and CFPP achieved the highest reduction rate (68.3% ± 7.2%). No maternal deaths were reported. Despite regular apheresis, four out of 23 patients (17.4%) experienced acute pancreatitis. The average triglyceride level at the onset of pancreatitis was 45.20 ± 4.65 mmol/L. Case reports support the use of regular apheresis for severe gestational hypertriglyceridemia. Multidisciplinary collaboration is essential for the care of these patients. Introduction

Severely elevated triglyceride (TG > 10 mmol/L) affects 1 to 2 in 1000 [1]. In late pregnancy, the increase in estrogen and human placental lactogen promotes lipogenesis and inhibits lipoprotein lipase activity, which, when combined with polygenic or monogenic susceptibility, can result in severely elevated TG of over 100 mmol/L [2].

The major complication of severe hypertriglyceridemia is life-threatening acute pancreatitis [3, 4]. Incidence of pancreatitis increases from 5% to 10–20% when TG level rises from 11.3 to 22.6 mmol/L [5]. Hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis often has a more virulent clinical course with a mortality rate as high as 30% [6].

In pregnancy, severe hypertriglyceridemia can lead to multiple episodes of pancreatitis and result in poor obstetric and neonatal outcomes [7]. Therefore, lowering the TG level in a timely manner is crucial for gestational hypertriglyceridemia. Apheresis is a potential treatment for hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis [8]. However, such treatment involves collaboration across disciplines and is not regularly carried out and there is no estabilshed protocol for the regular use of apheresis for the prevention of hypertriglyceridemia pancreatitis. More information on the technical details, duration, frequency of apheresis, and the safety and efficacy of such treatment is needed to guide clinical practice.

In most reported cases of gestational hypertriglyceridemia, therapeutic apheresis is only initiated when there is an acute pancreatitis attack. Even after the resolution of the first pancreatitis, few starts regular apheresis despite the established association of elevated TG and recurrence of pancreatitis. The main concerns of regular apheresis include complications associated with central venous catheters and the infusion of large volumes of albumin or fresh frozen plasma as replacement fluid.

We present 2 cases of severe gestational hypertriglyceridemia successfully treated prophylactically with modified apheresis circuit by combining centrifuge and filtration plasmapheresis (denoted as CFPP in this article) in which neither central venous access nor plasma replacement was needed. We also systematically reviewed the literature for studies that applied apheresis prophylactically in severe gestational hypertriglyceridemia to identify the most effective strategy for the prevention of pancreatitis in pregnancy and proposed a multidisciplinary collaboration strategy for the management of these pregnancies.

Method

Case presentation

Case 1

A 30-year-old primigravid female at 28-week gestation presented to our hospital with hypertriglyceridemia. She was diagnosed 1 year earlier on a routine medical examination and was treated with fenofibrate and a low-fat diet. The fibrate was stopped since pregnancy.

She had no family history of hypertriglyceridemia. Her oral glucose tolerance test and thyroid function were normal, and she denied a history of alcoholism. On presentation, her height and weight were 162 cm and 49 kg (BMI 18.67 kg/m2). She did not exhibit any stigmata of hyperlipidemia.

The initial TG and cholesterol level was 32.78 mmol/L (normal range 0.45–1.70 mmol/L) and 11.51 mmol/L (normal range 2.85–5.70 mmol/L). After a multidisciplinary consultation with obstetricians, endocrinologists, nephrologists, and dieticians, we decided to start therapeutic apheresis to control her TG level and prevent pancreatitis.

To avoid using central vein and minimize the need for replacement fluid, we devised a circuit combining centrifuge and filtration plasmapheresis, denoted as cascade combined centrifuge and filtration plasmapheresis (CFPP, see later for description).

Apheresis every two weeks was started at 28-week gestation. Her average pre- and post-treatment triglyceride was 35.87 ± 5.49 and 13.15 ± 4.29 mmol/L, respectively. After 34-week gestation, we increased the frequency of plasmapheresis to once a week to control the TG level. This adjustment was guided by the physiological TG surge in late pregnancy, the recurrent weekly rebounds in TG despite dietary management, and the high maternal and perinatal risk associated with acute pancreatitis. This weekly schedule was aimed at attenuating TG peaks and maintaining more stable levels approaching delivery.

In total, 7 sessions were administeredand she did not have acute pancreatitis.

She underwent elective cesarean section at 37 weeks ' gestation with a TG level of 9.45 mmol/L (post apheresis). A healthy baby girl weighing 2360 g was delivered. Two days after delivery, her TG level dropped to 9.35 mmol/L without medication. The patient had an uncomplicated postoperative course and was discharged with a low-fat diet. Her TG level was 9.68 mmol/L 2 months post-delivery.

Case 2

A 26-year-old primigravid female at 23-week gestation presented with hypertriglyceridemia. She was diagnosed with familial chylomicronemia 1 year earlier in our endocrinology clinic and was treated with fenofibrate 200 mg once daily and her TG level was 2 to 7 mmol/L. The fibrate was stopped during pregnancy.

Upon presentation, her height and weight were 170 cm and 50 kg (BMI 17.3 kg/m2). No special sign, including eruptive xanthomas or palmar crease xanthomas, was found.

Her family history was significant for familial hypertriglyceridemia. Her parents were consanguineous (her mother and grandmother from her father’s side were first cousins). Genetic sequencing revealed that she was homozygous for a missense mutation in exon 6 of the LPL gene (NM_000237.2: c.836T > G, p.Leu279Arg). The mutation has previously been reported to cause a decrease in lipoprotein lipase activity and hypertriglyceridemia [9].

The triglyceride level rose to 19.11 mmol/L at 22-week gestation despite a low-fat diet and omega-3 fatty acid supplementation under the supervision of our dietitians. After multidisciplinary consultations, we decided to start therapeutic apheresis to control her TG level and prevent pancreatitis. CFPP every two weeks was initiated at 23-week gestation and increased to a weekly treatment after 30 weeks. A total of 11 sessions were performed. Her pre and post-treatment triglyceride level was 29.19 ± 6.48 and 8.48 ± 2.96 mmol/L. She had no acute pancreatitis during pregnancy. She had an induced vaginal delivery at 38-week gestation. A healthy baby weighing 2640 g was delivered. Her TG level dropped to 8.21 mmol/L without medication on postpartum day 4. She recovered uneventfully and was encouraged to breastfeed the baby.

Plasmapheresis procedure

We modified the plasmapheresis procedure by combining centrifugal separation of plasma and membrane filtration. The procedure is denoted as combined centrifuge and filtration plasmapheresis (CFPP) in this article. The circuit of CFPP was shown schematically in Fig. 2.

Schematic drawing of combined centrifuge and filtration plasmapheresis (CFPP) circuit. Blood drawn from the patient was separated centrifugally, and the separated plasma was sent to a membrane (plasma fractionator) with a pore size of 0.03 μm. Plasma components larger than 0.03 μm were discarded, and the remaining plasma was returned to the patient. No replacement fluid was needed. The anticoagulation of the extracorporeal circuit was achieved by regional citrate anticoagulation

CFPP was performed using COM.TEC® (Fresenius Kabi AG) apheresis platform. At each session, plasma was separated centrifugally, and a membranous plasma filter with a pore size of 0.03 μm (EC-50 W, Asahi Medical, Tokyo, Japan) was used to filter out chylomicrons and other TG-rich lipoprotein particles.

Peripheral veins were used as access because a low blood flow rate of 30-50 ml/min was sufficient for the centrifuge separation method.

During each session, 1.5–2.5 plasma volume was processed, and approximately 300 ml of highly concentrated lipid-rich plasma was discarded. (See Fig. 3 for circuit detail and the discarded lipid-rich plasma).

The circuit connecting the second membrane (Left) and the discarded lipid-rich plasma (Right). The plasma came from below. The lipid-rich component was separated and came out from above, while the remaining plasma was returned via a line on the side

Due to the big pore size of the filter membrane, only a small volume of plasma was removed, and no replacement fluid was needed. Three hundred milliliters of normal saline were infused during each session to prevent hypotension.

Anticoagulant citrate (4% sodium citrate) was used as an anticoagulant in proportion to blood flow rate and calcium gluconate was infused to maintain plasma ionized calcium level.

Review of literature

We searched MEDLINE and EMBASE using “hypertriglyceridemia” “pregnancy” and “apheresis” or a combination of relevant medical subject heading (MeSH) terms.

The search included studies published till January 2022. Reviews, commentaries, conference abstracts, and non-English papers were excluded. The literature search and selection flowchart is shown in Fig. 4.

The flowchart for systemic review of literature

Patient information including gestational age, etiology of hypertriglyceridemia, maternal and fetal outcome and technical details of apheresis treatment were gathered from the literature review. Triglyceride reduction rate for apheresis treatment was calculated by . Data were reported as means ± SD and frequencies (percentages), where appropriate. Student t test was used for comparing triglyceride reduction rate between methods. Values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

We went through the publication and supplemented materials for all the parameters extracted. Missing information on case report was denoted as N/A.

Result

A total of 125 reports were identified. After screening, fourteen case reports and two retrospective cohorts were included in our review. In total, we identified 21 pregnancies complicated with hypertriglyceridemia treated regularly with therapeutic apheresis. The etiology for hypertriglyceridemia was familial hypertriglyceridemia in all but two cases. Type I hypertriglyceridemia (also known as familiar chylomicron syndrome) with mutations in the LPL gene resulting in a diminished activity of lipoprotein lipase is identified by genetic sequencing in 5 cases including one of our cases.

The indication for starting apheresis was either as a continuation of treatment after a hypertriglyceridemia-induced acute pancreatitis (n = 11) or as treatment for refractory hypertriglyceridemia without pancreatitis (n = 12). The median gestation age at the start of apheresis was 26 weeks and the average triglyceride level to initiate apheresis was 36.3 mmol/L.

The median number of apheresis sessions performed during each pregnancy was 13. As many as 40 sessions were conducted in one case. The frequency of treatment varied from every two weeks to twice per week. Treatment frequency increased with gestation age in 8 (25%) cases. In the third trimester, weekly or twice per week treatment was needed.

Therapeutic plasmapheresis (TPE) was used as the sole treatment modality in 14 cases and double filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP) in 3 cases. One case used both PE and DFPP and 5 cases used PE and lipid immunoadsorption (IA). One to 2.5 plasma volumes were processed during a single treatment session.

The venous access was determined mainly by the plasma separation method. Peripheral veins were used for most centrifugal separations, and central venous accesses were required for membranous plasma separators.

Lipid-IA and DFPP did not require replacement fluid because only small volumes of the plasma were discarded. In contrast, TPE required the infusion 2–4 L of replacement fluid, which was either 5% albumin solution or fresh frozen plasma. Both heparin and anticoagulation citrate were used as anticoagulation. Lipid-IA required additional anticoagulation.

The average triglyceride reduction rate for any type of apheresis was 45.1%.

The highest TG reduction rate was 68.3% ±7.2% and was achieved using CFPP by our group. It was 47.7% ±17% for a single TPE session (derived from 55 sessions), 36.1% ±8.1% for DFPP (derived from 10 sessions), 29.6% ±4.3% for lipid IA (derived from 44 sessions) and 41.9% ±0.7% for IA in tandem with TPE (derived from 45 sessions). The TG reduction rate was higher in TPE than in DFPP (P = 0.02).

We compared the TG reduction rate in our CFPP cases with cases reported in the literature. The TG reduction rate in CFPP was significantly higher than both TPE and DFPP (Both P < 0.001) in the literature (see Fig. 5). Between-study differences in processed plasma volume (our cases 1.5–2.5 PV vs. many TPE reports ~ 1.0–1.5 PV) likely contribute to the observed efficacy. This suggests that our circuit can achieve effective clearance of a larger plasma volume within an acceptable timeframe; however, as the comparison is not adjusted for processed volume, it does not constitute a direct TPE versus CFPP comparison.

In all the cases, despite exceedingly high triglyceride levels, the maternal and fetal outcomes were favorable. No death was reported. Four out of 23 patients (17.4%) experienced acute pancreatitis despite regular apheresis treatment. The average TG level at the onset of pancreatitis was 45.20 ± 4.65 mmol/L. One of them resulted in placental abruption, fortunately, the mother and baby both survived. The elective caesarian section was the choice of delivery in most cases (n = 13, 56.5%) and the average gestation age at delivery was 37 weeks.

Discussion

We reported the multidisciplinary care and the use of regular apheresis treatments in 2 pregnancies with severe hypertriglyceridemia. An apheresis circuit that combined a centrifugal plasma separator with a membranous plasma fractionator was applied to achieve high TG reduction rate with peripheral venous access without replacement fluid. With every two weeks to weekly CFPP treatments, both pregnancies went into full term and were uncomplicated by pancreatitis.

We also systematically reviewed the literature to identify the optimal treatment strategy utilizing apheresis in severe gestational hypertriglyceridemia.

Severe hypertriglyceridemia is predominantly a polygenic disease with the accumulation of common genetic variants or heterozygous rare variants. In our review, we found that 5 out of the 23 cases (21.7%) had genetically proven familial chylomicron syndrome, which is higher than the 1.1% prevalence in the non-pregnant population [27].

Familial chylomicronemia syndrome results from mutations in the lipoprotein lipase gene. Traditional drugs that rely on a functional lipoprotein lipase to exert their lipid-lowering functions are not effective [28] and therapeutic apheresis is a powerful tool, along with antisense oligonucleotide against apolipoprotein C III, in treating these patients. Women with familial chylomicron syndrome were often dissuaded from pregnancy due to the high risk of severe hypertriglyceridemia and pancreatitis. However, with the advent of prophylactic apheresis treatment, as in our patient 2, pregnancy is still possible.

In the literature, severe hypertriglyceridemia is often defined as TG > 11.3 mmol/L. The triglyceride level for initiating apheresis treatment is often higher. But the optimal cut-off level is unknown. In our review, the average triglyceride level at the initiation of apheresis was 36.3 mmol/L. The target triglyceride level varies and the average post-treatment TG was 16.35 ± 8.57 mmol/L. In one case (Basar et al. 2013) the TG target level was set arbitrarily at 33.9 mmol/L. The focus of TG-lowering therapy is to prevent acute pancreatitis while balancing the cost and potential adverse effects of apheresis. In a systematic review of 1340 patients with hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis, the median serum TG level at presentation was 29.36 (range 13.11-110.69) mmol/L [29]. In our review, the TG level for the 4 cases that had pancreatitis despite regular apheresis was 45.20 ± 4.65 mmol/L. Keeping TG levels below that commonly seen at AP presentation (often around or above ~ 29 mmol/L) appears reasonable; however, no validated preventive threshold exists and targets should be individualized. More cases and interventional studies are needed to set the best TG level.

The treatment frequency also varied. The median starting time was 26-week gestation. Most cases reported weekly to twice per week treatments during the 3rd trimester due to elevated TG levels. Post-treatment TG rebound is common, especially in late pregnancy due to hormonal changes and increased lipid metabolism. Close monitoring of TG levels is therefore essential, and in some cases, increasing the frequency of apheresis during the third trimester, as in our two cases, may help maintain safer TG levels during this high-risk period. However, more evidence is needed to establish the preventive threshold for TG during pregnancy.

Different apheresis modalities have been investigated. TPE is most widely used followed by DFPP. The TG reduction rate were slightly higher for TPE compared with DFPP in pregnant patients. However, in the 2 cases we reported, we employed a hybrid circuit called CFPP and was able to achieve the highest TG reduction rate of 67.7%, similar to the reported TG removal rate for non-pregnant patients (which was around 60%) [2, 30]. The CFPP circuit can be employed via the peripheral veins with no replacement fluid. This spares the patient of the complications associated with central venous access and makes outpatient treatment feasible. This is especially relevant in prophylactic treatments where regular apheresis is needed. In our implementation of CFPP—using centrifugal separation plus a secondary plasma fractionator (EC-50 W, 0.03 μm)—only a small fraction of lipid-rich plasma was discarded and replacement fluid was not required. This advantage relates to the specific filter characteristics and circuit configuration employed here, rather than to the DFPP/CFPP concept per se.

In managing pregnancies complicated by severe hypertriglyceridemia, collaboration between endocrinologists, obstetricians, nephrologists, and other allied professionals is important. And these patients should be referred to centers with expertise in these fields. A flowchart was proposed in Fig. 6, stressing the multidisciplinary management model we employed in our patients.

The major complication of severe hypertriglyceridemia is pancreatitis, which leads to a 4-fold increase in the chance of preterm (< 37 weeks) delivery and intrauterine fetal death [31]. In a recent retrospective study from a large center in China, the fetal death rate is as high as 33% for hypertriglyceridemia-related pancreatitis in pregnancy [32]. Severe hypertriglyceridemia can also lead to hyper-viscosity syndrome and pre-eclampsia [33]. No maternal or fetal death was reported in our review. There is one case of placental abruption and one case of intrauterine growth retardation in those who had pancreatitis despite apheresis treatment. The triglyceride level in these patients were not well controlled (45.20 mmol/L at onset). This stresses the importance of more frequent apheresis treatment.

Our work tried to synthesize the best available data. However, the evidence level is still low and is Limited to case reports and retrospective case series. Technical details are lacking in some reports and publication bias may limit our evaluation of the complications associated with extracorporeal treatment. This emphasizes the importance of more data in the field of therapeutic apheresis during pregnancy. Given that 4/23 patients developed AP despite regular apheresis and the lack of controlled data, prophylactic apheresis should be viewed as a feasibility and risk-mitigation strategy rather than a proven preventive intervention. Barriers to implementing our work in daily practice include the need for specialized equipment, technical expertise, and high costs. Hopefully, with the advances in technology, the treatment we employed here can reach more people with needs.
