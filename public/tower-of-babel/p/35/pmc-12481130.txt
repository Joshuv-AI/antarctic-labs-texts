Navigating dual risks: Ovarian cancer prevention and cardiovascular health in patients with hereditary cancer syndromes☆
Authors: Laura Keenahan, Muhammad Danyal Ahsan, Alicia Mecklai, Melissa K Frey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Surgical removal of ovaries and fallopian tubes is recommended for many hereditary cancer syndrome carriers before menopause.•Premature surgical menopause is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease.•Clinicians caring for patients with premature surgical menopause should consider cardiovascular health implications.

Patients with pathogenic germline variants (PGVs) in BRCA1 and BRCA2 have a substantially increased lifetime risk of developing ovarian cancer, estimated at up to 40 % and 18 %, respectively (Kuchenbaecker et al., 2017). Risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy has been demonstrated to decrease the risk of ovarian cancer by as much as 96 %, making it a cornerstone of cancer prevention in this high-risk population (Rebbeck et al., 2002). Per the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy is recommended between ages 35 and 40 years for individuals with BRCA1 PGVs and between ages 40 and 45 years for those with BRCA2 PGVs, or upon completion of childbearing (National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 2025). Beyond BRCA1/2, risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy is also recommended for carriers of BRIP1, RAD51C, and RAD51D PGVs, and should be considered for those with PALB2 PGVs at age 45–50 years. With increasing awareness and access to genetic testing, identification of PGV carriers is expected to rise, further contributing to the growing number of patients undergoing risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy for cancer risk reduction.

When risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy is performed at the guideline-recommended ages, patients may undergo abrupt onset of menopause 10–15 years earlier than the average age of natural menopause. This surgical menopause has significant implications for both quality of life and long-term health, including adverse effects on cardiovascular health (Gordon et al., 1978). Menopause—whether natural or surgically induced—is a well-established risk factor for the development of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in women (Gordon et al., 1978, Zhu et al., 2019, Ley et al., 2017, Muka et al., 2016, Wellons et al., 2012, Hu et al., 1999, Freaney et al., 2021, Honigberg et al., 2019, Zhu et al., 2020, Parker et al., 2009). Importantly, CVD remains the leading cause of death among women in the United States, as reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, underscoring its critical importance as a public health concern (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2024).

A substantial body of literature has demonstrated that women who undergo premature menopause face a significantly elevated risk of developing CVD—with risk estimates as high as two to three times greater than those who experience menopause at the average age (Gordon et al., 1978, Zhu et al., 2019, Ley et al., 2017, Muka et al., 2016, Wellons et al., 2012, Hu et al., 1999, Freaney et al., 2021, Honigberg et al., 2019, Zhu et al., 2020, Parker et al., 2009). The Framingham Heart Study was among the first to establish this association, revealing that women with premature menopause, whether spontaneous or surgical, had a 2.7-fold increased risk of developing CVD compared to those who reached menopause at the average age of 51 (Gordon et al., 1978). Subsequent studies have consistently corroborated these findings, as summarized in Supplementary Materials 1 (Gordon et al., 1978, Zhu et al., 2019, Ley et al., 2017, Muka et al., 2016, Wellons et al., 2012, Hu et al., 1999, Freaney et al., 2021, Honigberg et al., 2019, Zhu et al., 2020, Parker et al., 2009). For example, a pooled analysis performed by Zhu et al. found that each year menopause occurred earlier was associated with a 3 % increased risk of incident CVD [95 % CI 1.02–1.04] (Zhu et al., 2019). Similarly, Ley et al., using data from the Nurses’ Health Study, found that women who experienced menopause before age 40 had a relative risk of 1.32 for CVD compared to those who entered menopause after age 50 [95 % CI, 1.16–1.51] (Ley et al., 2017). Other large cohort studies have further supported a nearly two-fold increased risk of coronary heart disease among women with premature menopause (Muka et al., 2016, Wellons et al., 2012, Hu et al., 1999, Freaney et al., 2021).

There is some evidence that premature surgical menopause confers a higher risk of CVD compared to naturally occurring premature menopause (Honigberg et al., 2019, Zhu et al., 2020, Parker et al., 2009). Honigberg et al. found that both natural and surgical menopause occurring prior to age 40 years is associated with an increased risk of the development of coronary artery disease, heart failure, aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation, atrial fibrillation, ischemic stroke, peripheral artery disease, and venous thromboembolism (Honigberg et al., 2019). Additionally, when compared to natural premature menopause, surgical premature menopause was associated with a higher rate of cardiovascular disease, with a hazard ratio of 1.87 [95 % CI, 1.36–2.58], compared to a hazard ratio of 1.36 [95 % CI, 1.19–1.56] (Honigberg et al., 2019). An updated analysis from Zhu et al. supports these results, finding that premature surgical menopause was associated with a 20 % higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease compared to premature natural menopause [95 % CI 1.16–1.28] (Zhu et al., 2020). Additionally, data from the Nurses’ Health Study found that the risk of CVD following surgical menopause before age 45 years was higher when compared to patients who experienced surgical menopause between 45 and 54 years old, with a hazard ratio of 1.26 [95 % CI 1.04–1.54] and 1.07 [95 % CI 0.84–1.37], respectively (Parker et al., 2009). Although the underlying pathophysiology is not yet fully elucidated, one proposed mechanism is that surgical menopause results in a more abrupt and pronounced decline in endogenous estrogen levels compared to the gradual hormonal changes observed in natural menopause (Zhu et al., 2020).

Despite growing evidence, current guidelines lack specific recommendations for CVD screening in patients who experience premature menopause. In 2011, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) introduced sex-specific guidelines for CVD risk reduction; however, menopause was notably absent as a recognized contributor to increased CVD risk in women (Mosca et al., 2011, El Khoudary et al., 2020). This omission has raised concern that postmenopausal women—particularly those with premature surgical menopause—may be under-screened and inadequately offered preventive interventions (El Khoudary et al., 2020). The most recent ACC/AHA guidelines in 2019 on the primary prevention of CVD acknowledged early menopause (defined as menopause before age 40) as a “risk-enhancing factor” for CVD development (Arnett et al., 2019). These guidelines recommend that early menopause be considered during clinician–patient discussions regarding cardiovascular risk. Nonetheless, there remain no specific, evidence-based guidelines for CVD screening or preventive therapy tailored to this high-risk population (Arnett et al., 2019).

In the absence of effective screening or early detection methods for ovarian cancer, risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy remains a cornerstone of care for those at significantly elevated genetic risk. However, it is essential that clinicians recognize and communicate the potential long-term health implications of premature surgical menopause—including the increased risk of CVD. This risk–benefit discussion is particularly critical for patients with moderate-penetrance ovarian cancer genes, such as ATM and PALB2, where the absolute risk of developing ovarian cancer is considerably lower than that associated with high-penetrance genes like BRCA1/2. This aligns with the NCCN Guidelines which recommend that “[providers should] address bone health, cardiovascular health, psychosocial health, neurologic health, sexual health, and generalized quality-of life aspects of undergoing RRSO (risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy) (National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 2025). In addition, the guidelines state to “consider preoperative menopause management consultation if patient is still premenopausal at time of RRSO (risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy) (National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 2025).” However, current guidelines do not outline a standardized screening or prevention strategy for patients at increased risk of cardiovascular disease.

For the primary prevention of CVD, current ACC/AHA guidelines recommend clinicians assess the 10-year risk of developing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) using a pooled cohort equation routinely for women 40 to 75 years of age, to help classify as low (10 year risk <5 %), borderline (5 % to <7.5 %), intermediate (≥7.5 % to <20 %) and high (≥20 %) risk (Arnett et al., 2019). The ASCVD risk calculator components include age, sex, race, total cholesterol levels, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels, systolic blood pressure, blood pressure lowering medication use, diabetes status and smoking status (Grundy et al., 2019). For those aged 20 to 39, it is recommended to assess traditional ASCVD risk factors every 4 to 6 years (Arnett et al., 2019). While the ASVCD risk calculator notably does not include many established risk-enhancers, including premature menopause, it remains a valuable tool (El Khoudary et al., 2020, Arnett et al., 2019). The North American Menopause Society currently recommends that all menopausal women undergo CVD risk assessment using this calculator to guide prevention efforts (Shifren and Gass, 2014). This quantitative assessment of an individual’s 10-year risk of developing CVD can aid in identifying candidates for primary prevention strategies such as statin therapy as well as more intensive lifestyle modifications such as diet changes and exercise counseling.

The ACC/AHA provides guidelines on the initiation of preventative statin therapy based on 10-year ASCVD score and patient risk factors (Grundy et al., 2019). For individuals aged 40 to 75 who have experienced premature menopause, statin therapy is recommended if their 10-year ASCVD risk is greater than 7.5 % (Grundy et al., 2019). For individuals with premature menopause with a borderline 10-year ASCVD risk (5 % to <7.5 %), it is reasonable for a patient and provider to have a risks and benefits discussion about statin therapy, and considering whether obtaining a coronary artery calcium score can help further stratify patient’s CVD risk (Arnett et al., 2019, Grundy et al., 2019). Patients with a calcium score of zero can typically forego statin therapy, while patients with elevated calcium scores are at higher risk of developing CVD and should be strongly encouraged to initiate statin therapy (Grundy et al., 2019). However, calcium scores do not evaluate for the presence of noncalcified atherosclerosis. Therefore, a CT coronary angiogram to evaluate for the presence of noncalcified atherosclerosis could be considered based on clinical judgement (Grundy et al., 2019).

While hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is not currently recommended for the primary prevention of CVD based largely on findings from the Women’s Health Initiative, substantial evidence supports its cardioprotective effects (Rossouw et al., 2002, Stampfer et al., 1991, Hodis and Mack, 2022, Salpeter et al., 2006, Boardman et al., 2015, Hodis et al., 2016). In the Nurses’ Health Study, the age-adjusted risk of CVD among HRT users was approximately half that of non-users (relative risk [RR], 0.51; 95 % CI, 0.37–0.70) (Stampfer et al., 1991). The benefits appear most pronounced when HRT is initiated in younger women and in close temporal proximity to the onset of menopause (Hodis and Mack, 2022). A meta-analysis by Salpeter et al. demonstrated a 32 % reduction in CVD risk when HRT was initiated in younger postmenopausal women (RR, 0.68; 95 % CI, 0.48–0.96) (Salpeter et al., 2006). Similarly, a Cochrane review found that initiation within 10 years of menopause was associated with a significantly lower risk of CVD (RR, 0.52; 95 % CI, 0.29–0.96) (Boardman et al., 2015). A randomized controlled trial further confirmed that women starting HRT within six years of menopause experienced less CVD progression than those initiating therapy more than 10 years after menopause (Hodis et al., 2016). These findings suggest that patients undergoing premature surgical menopause may derive cardioprotective benefit from initiating HRT after risk-reducing surgery, and such therapy should be discussed and offered when appropriate.

This evidence supports the potential cardioprotective benefits of initiating HRT after risk-reducing surgery in patients who experience premature surgical menopause, and such therapy should be offered when appropriate. However, decision-making is more complex in individuals with BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants who also have a history of breast cancer. Although studies have suggested that HRT may be safe in breast cancer survivors, the HABITS trial reported an increased risk of recurrence (Fahlén et al., 2013, Coronado et al., 2024, Holmberg et al., 2008). Consequently, current consensus generally advises against HRT use in patients with a history of breast cancer. Nevertheless, this remains an area of ongoing debate, and further research is critical to determine whether certain subgroups of breast cancer survivors may safely derive both quality-of-life and long-term health benefits from HRT (Bluming, 2022).

To further reduce CVD risk in this population, clinicians should counsel patients on evidence-based lifestyle modifications, including dietary and physical activity interventions. The ACC/AHA recommends reducing sodium intake, increasing consumption of plant-based proteins, and adopting a Mediterranean-style diet, all of which have demonstrated CVD risk reduction (Arnett et al., 2019). Additionally, at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise per week is advised (Arnett et al., 2019). Collaboration with nutrition and exercise specialists can facilitate implementation of these strategies and provide ongoing support for patients after premature surgical menopause.

Given the established association between premature surgical menopause and increased CVD risk—coupled with the absence of clear management guidelines—early referral to cardiology should be considered for appropriate risk stratification and personalized prevention planning. Additional research is needed to determine the optimal timing for cardiovascular evaluation and to inform the development of standardized guidelines for both CVD prevention and risk mitigation in patients undergoing risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy. As cancer prevention and CVD prevention are both national public health priorities, there is a critical opportunity to address the long-term health implications of surgical menopause in individuals with hereditary cancer syndromes. Multidisciplinary collaboration between gynecologists, oncologists, surgeons, and cardiologists may enhance patient outcomes by ensuring comprehensive care.

In summary, early detection and prevention of CVD in patients with premature surgical menopause is essential. Patients should receive counseling on evidence-based dietary and lifestyle modifications and, if there is no medical contraindication, be offered HRT following risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy. Calculation of the 10-year ASCVD risk is recommended, with referral to a cardiologist for patients who may benefit from further evaluation, including coronary artery calcium scoring, and consideration of statin therapy for primary prevention. A coordinated, multidisciplinary approach incorporating gynecologic oncology, cardiology, nutrition, and exercise medicine has the potential to substantially improve long-term cardiovascular and overall health outcomes in women experiencing premature surgical menopause after risk-reducing surgery.
