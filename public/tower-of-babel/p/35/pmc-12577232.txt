Gellan gum/gelatin Schiff-base hydrogel for ocular drug delivery of timolol maleate
Authors: Golnaz Shajari, Hamid Erfan-Niya, Marziyeh Fathi, Nazanin Amiryaghoubi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In ocular drug delivery, increasing the retention time of the drug by using in situ forming hydrogels can be effective. In the current study, hydrogels with suitable gelling properties were obtained using oxidized gellan gum as a green crosslinker and gelatin via Schiff-base linkages. In the first step, oxidation of gellan gum was done to obtain aldehyde groups, and in situ gelling hydrogels were formed based on a simplistic Schiff-base reaction between oxidized gellan gum and amine groups of gelatin in the presence of β-glycerophosphate disodium salt as a pH adjustment agent and gelation accelerator. The calculated aldehyde content was about 64.5% by the titration method. By optimizing the concentration of gelatin, oxidized gellan gum, and β-glycerophosphate disodium salt, hydrogels with a fast gelation time were obtained. The synthesized hydrogels were investigated via Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, scanning electron microscope (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), swelling degree, and rheological properties. The effect of oxidized gellan gum and gelatin concentration on the physical and chemical characteristics of the obtained hydrogel system were studied. The results of in vitro and ex vivo research indicated that the prepared hydrogel with a high concentration of oxidized gellan gum and gelatin had a higher release rate, stronger network, and homogeneous morphological structure. The synthesized hydrogels have adequate porosity for drug loading (around 98–99%). Also, the MTT cytotoxicity test on bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) confirmed that the developed hydrogels with green crosslinker do not have cytotoxic effects. In vitro and ex vivo drug release tests demonstrated a facile preparation strategy for fast-gelling hydrogels that can be considered as an appropriate candidate for timolol maleate delivery in glaucoma treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-025-01056-3.

Introduction

Ocular drug delivery is a remarkable and challenging subject in pharmaceutical sciences due to the unique structure of the eye [1]. Local drug delivery through eye drops is a common way to treat eye diseases [2]. The eye’s cornea is the eye’s main site for drug penetration in topical ocular drug delivery. Epithelium, stroma, and endothelium are the three foremost layers of the human eye cornea. The epithelium, which is the outermost layer and consists of dense and compact cells, acts as a tight barrier for drug penetration into the eye. For the transfer of the most hydrophilic drugs from the surface of the cornea, a high concentration of the drug is required to pass through the corneal barrier [3]. Regrettably, the contact time of the drugs with the cornea is very short, and this is because the solution of the introduced drop is quickly diluted with the tear fluid of the eye and is immediately drained from the pre-cornea area with the release of tears, and finally, less than 5% of the drugs can penetrate through the cornea and reach the tissues inside the eye. Many strategies have been developed to increase the penetration and effectiveness of eye drops on the cornea [4, 5]. Reducing drug loss before the cornea, decelerating drug release from the drug delivery structure, and increasing drug retention and contact time are among the strategies to increase the effectiveness of topical ocular drug delivery [4, 5].

Controlled drug delivery systems (DDSs) offer several advantages over traditional methods, including enhanced drug stability, sustained therapeutic effects, improved patient adherence, and reduced need for frequent medical supervision [6, 7]. Hydrogels, as a prominent class of these systems, have been extensively studied for their ability to provide spatial and temporal control over drug release [8]. Their tunable physical properties, controllable degradability, and capacity to protect labile drugs from degradation make them an attractive platform for various therapeutic applications [6]. Hydrogels are a type of three-dimensional polymer network with a high capacity to absorb water and liquids [9, 10]. The polymers that are used to synthesize hydrogels can be hydrophilic natural or synthetic polymers. However, natural polymers are a more suitable option for medical applications due to their safety, low cost, and availability [11]. Recently, hydrogels have indicated a high potential for drug delivery, considering interesting characteristics such as high absorption capacity, desired biocompatibility, and transparency [12, 13]. The bioavailability and active duration of ophthalmic drugs could be improved with the development of new DDSs [12, 14, 15]. Recently, DDSs based on in situ gelling hydrogels were introduced to solve ocular drug delivery problems [16].

Gellan gum (GG) is one of the natural polymers that has received much attention in recent years in the synthesis of hydrogels. The GG-based hydrogels have been widely used in biomedical applications due to temperature stability, excellent water-holding capacity, oxygen permeability, biodegradability, nontoxicity, rapid gelation, and biocompatibility. Also, GG as a raw material seems attractive for making new drug carrier formulations based on Schiff-base reactions because it has diverse functional groups in its structure [17]. However, the physical hydrogels of GG have very low mechanical strengths and stability in aqueous environments. Therefore, the synthesis of hydrogels containing chemical bonds using GG seems to be useful [18, 19].

Gelatin (GEL) as a natural polymer has been widely used in DDSs due to its specific physical and chemical properties. Hydrogels synthesized with GEL are biocompatible, biodegradable, and non-toxic and seem to be a suitable option for ocular DDSs [20]. On the other hand, the presence of various functional groups in the structure of GEL provides its chemical modification and can be used to synthesize hydrogels with adjustable and tunable gelation. The presence of amine functional groups in the gelatin structure makes it attractive for the synthesis of hydrogels by the Schiff-base reaction [21].

The Schiff-base reaction can be used even at room temperature to form in situ gelling hydrogels with high mechanical strength [22]. Schiff-base reaction is a rapid and facile crosslinking route for the preparation of chemically cross-linked hydrogels [23, 24]. Schiff-base is formed by the formation of an imine bond between carbonyl and amine groups. Hydrogels synthesized by this reaction are sensitive to chemical stimuli and may be degraded in different physiological conditions [25]. Schiff-base reaction proceeds under mild physiological conditions without the need for toxic catalysts, making it highly suitable for sensitive applications such as ocular drug delivery. Moreover, due to the reversible nature of the formed imine bonds, this method imparts self-healing properties to the hydrogel and allows for injectability and rapid in situ gelation. These features are critical for maintaining network integrity against mechanical stress and enhancing drug retention time on the ocular surface with controlled release behavior [26]. β-glycerophosphate disodium salt hydrate (β-GP) can support the formation of in-situ hydrogels by physical interaction and also can act as pH adjusting agent near the physiological range [27, 28]. A common medication used to treat glaucoma and regulate intraocular pressure is timolol maleate (TM) [29]. Ocular TM delivery faces numerous challenges [29, 30]. The low bioavailability of TM is mainly due to rapid precorneal elimination and limited corneal penetration, necessitating frequent dosing that may reduce patient compliance. Additionally, systemic absorption through the nasolacrimal duct can cause undesirable side effects. Conventional eye drops also lack sustained release capability, leading to fluctuations in drug concentration and suboptimal intraocular pressure control [31]. Therefore, it is essential to create a formulation that can boost the TM’s bioavailability while also having a stable release.

To the best of our knowledge, no research has been done in the field of hydrogel synthesis based on oxidized GG and GEL via Schiff-base reaction. Therefore, the hydrogel synthesis based on oxidized GG and GEL containing TM without adding any other toxic crosslinker could be considered as a new formulation for ocular drug delivery.

To this end, in the first step, the chemical modification of (GG) was done by an oxidation reaction to synthesize the oxidized gellan gum (OGG). GEL was used as an amine-containing polymer to form a Schiff-base reaction with aldehyde functional groups of OGG in the presence of β-GP. The modification of GG and the hydrogels’ structure were evaluated by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR). The effect of the concentration of GEL and OGG on the rheological behavior, surface microstructure morphology, in vitro swelling ratio/degradation, texture properties, and porosity of the hydrogels were investigated. The toxicity evaluation of hydrogels was investigated too. In addition, the TM release of optimized hydrogels was evaluated in vitro and ex vivo.

Experimental section

Materials

Gellan gum (low acyl) (GG), sodium periodate (NaIO4), β-glycerophosphate disodium salt hydrate (β-GP), and 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were purchased from Sigma Chemical Co (St. Louis, USA). Gelatin (GEL), ethylene glycol, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were supplied from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were obtained from the Iranian Biological Resource Center (Tehran, Iran). Timolol maleate (TM) was supplied by Darou Pakhsh (Tehran, Iran). All other reagents were of analytic reagent grade.

Preparation of OGG

Preparation of OGG was performed according to the previously reported method with some modifications [23, 32]. Briefly, GG (2 g) was dissolved in distilled water (200 mL) at 90 °C for 1 h. Then, the solution was cooled to 40 °C, and NaIO4 (2 g) was added to this solution under continuous stirring for 7 h in the dark under nitrogen gas. Ethylene glycol (1.345 mL) was added to stop the oxidation reaction. After 30 min, the obtained solution was purified by dialysis membrane (MWCO 12,000) for 3 days using distilled water and was then lyophilized and stored in vacuumed vials at -20 °C until further use.

Determination of the degree of oxidation of OGG

The oxidation degree of OGG was measured by transforming the dialdehyde agents to oximes via a Schiff-base reaction with hydroxylamine hydrochloride [33]. In summary, the specified amount of OGG was dissolved in 20 mL of distilled water with a pH setting of 5. Then, the hydroxylamine hydrochloride solution (20 mL, 0.1 M) was added to the solution. The obtained mixture was stirred at 40 °C for 40 min. Finally, the obtained mixture was titrated with NaOH solution (0.5 M). As a control, a blank sample titration was performed in the same way. The volume of NaOH utilized (in liters) for the titration of the OGG solution and blank sample was noted as V1 and V2, respectively. Preparation of OGG/GEL hydrogels

Different amounts of OGG were dissolved in 500 µL of PBS and different amounts of GEL were dissolved in 1500 µL of deionized water separately. Then, the OGG solution was mixed with the GEL solution at 37 °C with stirring, and 700 µL of β-GP solution (57%w/v) was added. To load the drug into the hydrogel, 2 mg of TM was dissolved in an OGG solution (before hydrogel synthesis) [32]. Table 1 represents different synthesized hydrogels that were produced in this work. The volume ratio of GEL solution to OGG solution was 3:1 in all samples.

Measurement of the gelation time of the OGG/GEL hydrogels

The gelation time of the prepared hydrogels was determined by the inverted tube test method [35, 36]. In summary, OGG/GEL hydrogel solutions (Sect. “Preparation of OGG/GEL hydrogels”) were prepared in vials and immediately incubated at 37 °C. The test vials are inverted every 30 s to measure the sol-gel transition time of the gel samples. The gelation time was determined by timing when the gel stopped flowing.

FT-IR studies

Samples were first freeze-dried to remove residual moisture. The dried samples were then finely ground into powder. The infrared spectra of GG, OGG, and OGG/GEL hydrogels were recorded by an FT-IR (Tensor27, Bruker Co., Ettlingen, Germany) using KBr disks in a range of 4000–500 cm− 1 [34].

XRD of the OGG/GEL hydrogels

The X-ray diffraction patterns of OGG/GEL hydrogels were measured using an X-ray diffractometer (X’Pert PRO MPD, PANalytical Co, Netherlands). The freeze-dried form of hydrogels was used to determine the XRD spectrum patterns. The scattering angle (2θ) was analyzed over a diffraction angular range of 2θ = 5–70° at a scan rate of 5°/min [35]. The X-ray diffraction patterns of the produced hydrogels were examined with a radiation measurement parameter of Cu-Kα filtered with nickel.

Morphological study of the OGG/GEL hydrogels

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; LEO 1455VP, Zeiss, Germany) was used to observe and check the morphology of OGG/GEL hydrogels. The microstructure of the freeze-dried hydrogels was imaged at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV [35]. Imaging was done after coating the freeze-dried samples with a gold layer (at ×300 magnification).

Rheological study of OGG/GEL hydrogels

The synthesized hydrogels were subjected to oscillatory rheological tests using an MCR 300 rheometer (Anton Paar, Austria) with a parallel plate sensor (25PP, 25 mm in diameter). The distance between the parallel plates where the hydrogels were placed was 0.2 mm. At a frequency of 1.0 Hz and a temperature of 37 °C, a strain sweep was performed from 0.1 to 100%. To comprehend the rheological behavior of the hydrogel samples, frequency sweeps in the linear viscoelastic area of each sample were carried out in the frequency range of 1–100 Hz [29].

Swelling study of OGG/GEL hydrogels

Swelling tests of the OGG/GEL hydrogels were conducted based on the methods previously mentioned [36, 37]. Briefly, the pre-weighed prepared hydrogels (W0) were put in 30 mL of simulated tear fluid (STF: NaCl 0.68 g, NaHCO3 0.22 g, CaCl2⋅2H2O 0.008 g, and KCl 0.14 g in 100 mL distilled deionized water) solution with pH = 7.4 and placed in an incubator set to 37 °C. The swollen gels were carefully taken from the solution at regular intervals, and any extra solution was gently blotted away using filter paper. The samples were then returned to the same container after being weighed (Wt). Texture analysis of OGG/GEL hydrogels

The textural characteristic of the OGG/GEL hydrogels was evaluated through a texture analyzer (Brookfield CT3 Texture Analyzer, London, UK). Analytical probe TA-41 was derived at a speed rate of 1 mm/s. A 10-mm diameter analytical probe (TA-41) was compressed into the hydrogels and redrawn. The speed rate of the crosshead was 1 mm/s. All measurements were performed in triplicate and indicated in terms of hardness, cohesiveness, resilience, chewiness, adhesiveness, springiness, and gumminess [39].

In vitro drug release study

The profile of in vitro drug release from OGGH2 and OGGH3 was studied through the dialysis bag method. TM-loaded hydrogels were transferred into the dialysis bag containing 1 mL of STF (pH = 7.4), and then the dialysis bags were immersed in 30 mL STF and placed in the shaker incubator at the rate of 70 rpm at 37 °C. At definite time points (0.16, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 12, and 24 h), 2 mL of release medium was taken out and replaced with 2 mL of fresh STF to keep the sink condition. Finally, the concentration of TM was calculated using a calibration curve prepared at 295 nm by a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Cecil CE 7500, UK). It is noteworthy that blank hydrogels were considered as control samples for absorbance correction. Each sample was tested in triplicate and the results were used as their average [41].

Cytotoxicity assessment

Following the international standard ISO 10,993, MTT was used to examine the cytotoxicity of the OGG/GEL hydrogels on BMSCs. The hydrogel samples were sterilized with UV for 20 min, then placed in DMEM containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 10% FBS and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Under the conditions of cell culture (5% CO2 and 37 °C), BMSCs were cultivated at a seeding density of 5 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate. After 24 h, cells were treated with the extracted medium of hydrogels and were incubated for 24 and 48 h. Following the durations, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL diluted in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for a further 4 h to induce the production of purple formazan crystals. After this period, the environment of MTT was emptied, and the generated formazan crystals were then dissolved in each sample by adding 200 µL of DMSO solution. After that, an ELISA reader operating at a wavelength of 570 nm was used to measure the optical density (OD) of the supernatant solution [41].

Ex vivo study on corneal permeation

The amount of drug penetration through the cornea plays an important role in ocular drug delivery. Consequently, employing the isolated cow corneal tissue, the TM penetration released from the OGG/GEL hydrogels was investigated [41]. A modified Franz vertical diffusion cell method was utilized to examine the corneal permeability of the synthesized compositions. To achieve this, fresh cow eyes were collected from the butcher and placed in an ice bath containing STF (pH = 7.4). After that, the cornea was gently removed and cleaned with STF. The cow cornea’s excised epithelium was oriented toward the supply chamber and was positioned suitably midway between the supply and receiver tanks. The receiving pool was filled with STF and continuously stirred (the temperature was set at 37 °C during the process). About 1 g of TM-loaded hydrogel was placed on the surface of the cornea. After that, 2 mL of medium was taken out of the receiving pool for evaluation and replaced with fresh STF at predetermined times. Hydrogels alone were used as blanks. At the wavelength of 295 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Cecil CE 7500, UK), the permeation efficiency of TM was measured [16, 42].

Hemolysis assay

The blood compatibility of the OGG/GEL hydrogels was systematically evaluated using a hemolysis assay, following the ASTM F756–08 standard protocol, which is based on direct contact with whole human blood [35]. In this method, 300 µL of freshly collected human blood was added to 3 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.5 g of the hydrogel sample in sterile test tubes. For comparison, negative and positive controls were prepared by mixing 300 µL of blood with 3 mL of PBS and 3 mL of distilled water, respectively. All samples were gently agitated to ensure homogeneity and subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 3 h to simulate physiological conditions. Then, the test tubes were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min to separate the plasma from intact red blood cells. The absorbance of the supernatants was then measured at 540 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to quantify the concentration of free hemoglobin released due to red blood cell lysis.

Statistical analysis

All tests were run in triplicate using the GraphPad PRISM program version 6.0 for the statistical analysis. A one-way ANOVA was used, and a statistically significant p-value was defined as less than 0.05.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of OGG/GEL hydrogels

For the synthesis of OGG/GEL hydrogels, in the first step, GG was chemically modified with NaIO4 (Fig. 1A). Hydroxyl groups in GG were oxidized with NaIO4 and turned into aldehyde groups. The oxidation degree of OGG was determined as 64.5%. The Schiff base reaction was carried out through the reaction between the aldehyde groups of OGG and the amine groups of GEL, which led to the formation of imine bonds. Generally, β-GP is used to create physical gels via the interaction between the negative charges of the β-GP group and the positive charges of the amino groups. In the current study, β-GP was used to adjust the pH of the prepared hydrogel to physiological amounts. However, by β-GP addition, the gelation speed and in situ gel formation rate were increased, too. The dynamic nature of Schiff-base linkages allows reversible bonding, which helps maintain responsiveness to pH changes [43, 44]. Also, several reactive moieties, such as free amine, carbonyl, and hydroxyl groups, remain unreacted and are retained within the hydrogel matrix. β-GP induced in situ gelation of chitosan solutions by raising pH and promoting gelation at body temperature. In the current study, the addition of β-GP reduced the gelation time to an acceptable range (less than 120 s). β-GP enhances the gelation kinetics, mechanical properties, and structural integrity of hydrogels [45, 46]. β-GP promoted gel network formation by ionic interactions between its negatively charged groups and the positively charged amino groups of polymers [47]. Thus, β-GP played a dual role in both optimizing the gelation kinetics and maintaining the appropriate pH for hydrogel synthesis. A schematic of the oxidation reaction and formation of OGG/GEL hydrogels with two possible physical and chemical networks is shown in Fig. 1. Through physical interactions in the presence of phosphate groups, it resulted in a physical network and a stable gel. Creating a physical network in the hydrogel structure increased the density of the hydrogel.

FT-IR study

FT-IR analysis is one of the fastest and lowest-cost methods for identifying functional groups and diagnosing the correctness of the synthesis. The FT-IR spectrum can confirm the oxidation and Schiff-base reactions. Figure 2A shows the FT-IR spectra of GG and OGG. The characteristic peaks for OH stretching (3250–3550 cm− 1), C-H2 stretching (2922 cm− 1), asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibration of carboxyl groups (1550 –1450 cm− 1), and C-O stretching (1037 cm− 1) were observed in the GG absorption spectra [48]. The peak at around 1730 cm− 1 was observed in the FT-IR spectrum of OGG as a result of the production of aldehyde groups during the oxidation process of GG and its conversion to OGG (Fig. 2A) [49]. OGGH1- OGGH5 hydrogels showed a peak around 1630 cm− 1 that can confirm the covalent reaction and amide bond formation (Fig. 2B) [50]. The peak at around 980 cm− 1 and peaks at 1030–1120 cm− 1 arise from the P-O-H and the P–O groups that are formed in the presence of β-GP (Fig. 2B) [27].

The XRD patterns of OGG/GEL hydrogels are shown in Fig. 3 in the wide-angle range of 2θ (5° < 2θ < 70°). The XRD patterns indicated that OGG/GEL hydrogels have very low crystallinity. Morphology of OGG/GEL hydrogels

The morphology, porosity, and internal structure of synthesized hydrogels are important in their biomedical applications [53]. SEM images were used to assess the microstructural traits of the OGG/GEL hydrogels. SEM images of OGG/GEL hydrogels are displayed in Fig. 4, which support their porous structure and the development of a homogeneous porous structure with regular interconnected pores (Fig. 4A, B, C, D, E). It has been shown that the concentration of GEL (amine group) and OGG (aldehyde group) affects the internal morphological characteristics of hydrogels [42, 54]. OGGH3 (GEL 30%- OGG 30%) showed the most homogeneous morphology structure (Fig. 4C). This is probably the result of a strong cross-linked network formation and decreased distance between polymer chains in the hydrogel. It can be established that a higher concentration of OGG and GEL results in the formation of a homogeneous porous structure, which is likely a result of the relatively adequate imine bond between aldehyde and amine groups. After 50% degradation, SEM images of hydrogels OGGH2 and OGGH3 are shown in Fig. 6F and G, respectively, which reveal significant structural changes in the hydrogels’ structure. In the same scale bar, the network becomes noticeably more porous, with enlarged and more irregular pores. The overall morphology shows reduced integrity and increased fragmentation, indicating partial collapse of the crosslinked structure. These morphological changes confirm the progressive breakdown of the hydrogel matrix consistent with the degradation data.

Gelation time and rheological studies of OGG/GEL hydrogels

The gelation time of OGGH1-OGGH5 was 120, 60, 60, 120, and 90 s, respectively. The OGG/GEL hydrogels with higher concentrations of amino and aldehyde groups had an accelerated Schiff-base reaction rate [55]. Rheological investigation using sweeping frequencies was performed on OGG/GEL hydrogels in the range of 0.1–100 Hz at 37°C to assess their viscoelastic capabilities (Fig. 5). The hydrogels’ elasticity and viscosity are represented by G’ (storage modulus) and G” (loss modulus), respectively. Hydrogel OGGH3 with the highest concentration of OGG and GEL was stronger than the other hydrogels. The number of functional groups taking part in the Schiff-base reaction increases with an increase in GEL and OGG concentration, and a stronger hydrogel is produced with an increase in cross-linking density and low gelation time. This is consistent with the SEM images. Taking all, by increasing the concentration of aldehyde and amine functional groups, the rheological properties of the hydrogel synthesized by the Schiff-base reaction are improved [54].

Textural analysis of OGG/GEL hydrogels

The texture parameter values of hydrogels (hardness, cohesiveness, resilience, chewiness, adhesiveness, springiness, and gumminess) are shown in Fig. 6. These properties benefit the development of products with desirable features that support patient acceptability and compliance, as they can be directly correlated with sensory metrics in vivo. For example, a formulation intended for ophthalmic use should have good spreadability on the ocular surface and stick to the mucous layer to extend the retention period [56]. In general, these texture characteristics play a significant role in the formulation and design of hydrogels for efficient delivery of medication. The concentration of GEL and OGG significantly affected the hardness (Fig. 6A), cohesiveness (Fig. 6B), resilience (Fig. 6C), chewiness (Fig. 6D), adhesiveness (Fig. 6E), and gumminess (Fig. 6G) values of the hydrogels (p < 0.0001). Changing the concentration of GEL and aldehyde did not have much effect on the springiness of hydrogels (Fig. 6F). The springiness measure indicated that all OGG/GEL hydrogels had about the same ability to revert to the original design following deformation [57]. The degree of hardness, chewiness, and gumminess showed similar behavior with the change of GEL and aldehyde concentration (Fig. 6A, D, and G). With the increase of GEL from 20% to 25%, these parameters increased and, with the further increase of GEL concentration (from 25% to 30%), decreased and reached their lowest value. The force needed to compress a hydrogel is referred to as its hardness. In drug delivery, the hardness of a hydrogel can affect its disintegration and dissolution properties, which in turn can impact drug release kinetics and bioavailability. Hydrogel OGGH3 had the lowest hardness parameter value, which was determined to be superior to the other values since it indicates the force required to compress the formulation to its maximum level of resistance. For a topical application where the formulation must be distributed over the skin with a slight push, OGGH3 more closely matches the intended profile [57].

At the highest GEL concentration, the textural characteristics of hydrogels were dominated by GEL. In high concentrations of GEL, maybe due to the increase in crosslinking density, the mentioned properties decreased. By changing the concentration of aldehyde from 30% to 25%, these parameters did not show much change, and with the further decrease of aldehyde, the hardness, chewiness, and gumminess reached their maximum value at the concentration of 20%. Cohesiveness, or the physical stability between molecules, is the product’s capacity to cling to itself. The hydrogel’s resilience parameter describes its capacity to regain its original shape following deformation. High-resilience hydrogels can withstand deformation and return to their former shape when the deforming force is eliminated. A hydrogel’s crosslinking density and polymer composition can affect how resilient it is [58]. Cohesiveness in drug delivery can affect the hydrogel’s capacity to preserve its structural integrity when applied to mucosal surfaces. Hydrogel cohesiveness and resilience parameters showed similar behavior with the change of GEL and aldehyde concentration (Fig. 6B and C). These parameters decreased with the decrease of GEL concentration, and there was no regular trend in the change of aldehyde concentration, as the highest value of these parameters was recorded at 25% concentration of aldehyde. The chewiness and gumminess increase with increasing hardness. The effectiveness and duration of medication release in the eye are significantly influenced by adhesiveness. To achieve prolonged drug release and improve the delivered medication’s bioavailability, a hydrogel’s adhesiveness to the ocular surface is crucial when used as an ophthalmic drug delivery system. The adhesiveness of hydrogels increased with the decreasing GEL concentration, and the hydrogel with 30% GEL had less adhesiveness (Fig. 6E). By decreasing the concentration of aldehyde from 30% to 25%, the adhesiveness increased. Hydrogel OGGH3 with the highest concentration of GEL and aldehyde was springy, cohesive, and resilient compared to other hydrogels, and its adhesiveness, chewiness, gumminess, and hardness were the lowest compared to other hydrogels. Hydrogel OGGH4 had the most chewiness, hardness, resilience, cohesiveness, and gumminess. Hydrogel OGGH1 had the highest adhesiveness (p < 0.001 and p < 0.0001). Hydrogel OGGH5 had the lowest chewiness and gumminess (Fig. 6D and G). In general, the obtained hydrogels have higher mechanical properties (e.g. hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, etc.) in comparison to some recently reported GG-based hydrogels that could be attributed to the chemical crosslinking of the developed OGG/GEL hydrogels [59].

Porosity and density of OGG/GEL hydrogels

Hydrogel characteristics like mechanical features, loading capacity, drug release behavior, and swelling ratio are significantly influenced by the degree of hydrogel porosity [53, 60]. Porosity plays various roles in nutrient transfer, cell attachment, growth, differentiation, and proliferation. Therefore, it is crucial in biological applications [60]. Hydrogels with larger pores and higher porosity exhibit a more open structure and respond more quickly to external stimuli. They also provide greater contact surfaces and display faster and higher swelling compared to hydrogels with lower porosity [61].

Accordingly, porosity was determined using the liquid displacement method, and the results are summarized in Table 2. According to the obtained results, synthesized hydrogels have sufficient porosity for drug loading (around 98–99%), and with the increase in the concentration of GEL and OGG, the porosity and density of OGG/GEL hydrogels increased. The density of hydrogels is an important factor in ocular drug delivery because it influences drug loading, release rate, swelling behavior, and residence time. Furthermore, the density of hydrogels influences their degradation rate. Higher-density hydrogels are likely to degrade more slowly due to their higher cross-linking density, resulting in longer retention in the eye and sustained drug release.

In vitro swelling/degradation of OGG/GEL hydrogels

The swelling was due to an increase in the distance between cross-linked points of polymer chains, which allowed drug molecules to be released [61–63]. It was demonstrated that the concentration of GEL affected the swelling behavior of hydrogels [64]. The swelling percentage of hydrogel increased with increasing GEL concentration [51]. The hydrogel derived from hydroxyapatite and GEL showed an increase in swelling percentage with increasing GEL concentration [65]. All OGG/GEL hydrogels showed swelling behavior before degradation (Fig. 7A). The graph of the swelling ratio for OGGH3 with high GEL and OGG concentration had a higher swelling rate (p < 0.001, p < 0.0001). GEL had hydrophilic functional groups in its structure, which can be a likely reason for this behavior. On the other hand, as the amount of porosity increased, the swelling increased too. The swelling behavior of the OGG/GEL hydrogels was consistent with the porosity and SEM results.

One of the ways to promote the biodegradability of natural polymers such as alginate and GG is to use an oxidized derivative [33, 66–68]. The imine group is a water-sensitive structure, and the hydrogel synthesized by the Schiff-base method was susceptible to hydrolysis in aqueous media due to the imine bond in its structure [69, 70]. In biological fields, biodegradability is one of its most crucial characteristics. The biodegradability and swelling of hydrogels are critical since the release of hydrogels depends on their decomposition. To test hydrogel degradability and swelling, an STF environment was used. The results of this study showed that the hydrogels (OGGH1, OGGH4, and OGGH5) started to degrade after 5 h when they reached the maximum swelling and were completed in 24 h (Fig. 7B). Hydrogels OGGH2 and OGGH3 had more strength than the other three hydrogels and their degradation started after 24 h and was completed within 48 h. This could be related to the density of the imine bond in OGGH2 and OGGH3 hydrogel, which may be higher than other synthesized hydrogels.

In vitro TM release from OGG/GEL hydrogels

Examining the drug release behavior of hydrogels is essential for their application in drug delivery. TM was loaded in OGGH2 and OGGH3 to investigate their in vitro drug release test. OGGH2 and OGGH3 were selected as the optimized formulations for drug loading due to their superior mechanical strength (as confirmed by rheological analysis), uniform and homogeneous internal morphology (as revealed by SEM), and overall favorable physicochemical characteristics. Furthermore, these formulations exhibited significantly shorter gelation times, which is critical for in situ ocular applications requiring rapid gel formation. Furthermore, stability assessments in STF demonstrated that OGGH2 and OGGH3 maintained structural integrity over time, indicating enhanced resistance to degradation compared to other hydrogels. Figure 8 shows the TM release behavior from the hydrogels (OGGH2 and OGGH3) in STF with pH = 7.4 at 37 °C for 24 h. It was assumed that the larger porosity of the OGGH3 made it possible to absorb high amounts of water. The high water content of hydrogels resulted in the easy transport of drug molecules from the swollen matrix [71]. In the first 5 h, the amount of drug released from the hydrogel OGGH3 was 69.66%, and from the hydrogel OGGH2 was 47.13%. At the end of the first 24 h, the hydrogels OGGH2 and OGGH3 released 89.88% and 94.33% of the loaded TM, respectively. Several variables, including the hydrogel’s structure, drug’s solubility, cross-linking method, material’s composition, external stimuli like pH or temperature, and potential interactions between the drug and the polymeric matrix components, can influence the release processes [72]. Different hydrogel matrices can affect the drug release profiles [32, 41]. The difference in drug release behavior between OGGH2 (GEL 25%–OGG 30%) and OGGH3 (GEL 30%–OGG 30%) highlights the significant role of gelatin concentration in modulating the hydrogel structure and drug diffusion (p < 0.05). The higher gelatin content in OGGH3 provided more amine functional groups for Schiff-base crosslinking, resulting in a more homogeneous network. This improved matrix structure increased the water uptake and porosity of the hydrogel, allowing faster diffusion of TM from the swollen gel. Furthermore, it could be speculated that gelatin’s hydrophilic nature enhances swelling behavior, resulting in an increased release rate in OGGH3. These results demonstrate that adjusting the polymer composition, especially gelatin concentration, is an effective strategy to control the drug release rate in the OGG/GEL hydrogel system.

TM release kinetics from OGG/GEL hydrogels were evaluated using various mathematical models. The results of the regression coefficients of the models are shown in Table 3. The Korsemeyer-Peppas model with an R2 value of 0.933 (OGGH2) and 0.9644 (OGGH3) was the best model for TM release from these hydrogels. The Korsmeyer-Peppas model is a widely used semi-empirical equation to describe the kinetics of drug release from polymeric DDSs, including hydrogels [73]. It should be noted that the release mechanisms were classified based on the release exponent, n, as follows: for n < 0.5, the mechanism is identified as pseudo-Fickian diffusion; for n = 0.5, it is classified as Fickian diffusion; for 0.5 < n < 1, it is termed non-Fickian diffusion; for n = 1, it corresponds to case II transport (zero order release); and for n >1, it is referred to as super case II transport [74]. The TM release mechanism was revealed to be pseudo-Fickian-type diffusion because the Korsmeyer-Peppas release exponent (n) values were less than 0.5 for the OGG/GEL hydrogels [73].

Cell viability studies of OGG/GEL hydrogels

In general, GEL and GG-based hydrogels are biocompatible, biodegradable, and non-toxic, and hence are suitable choices for ophthalmic DDSs [75]. To evaluate the cytotoxicity of the prepared hydrogels, the MTT test was used. As shown in Fig. 9A and B, a high survival rate and cell viability of BMSCs was observed after 24 and 48 h, respectively, when exposed to the extracted solution of OGG/GEL hydrogels. These results confirm the biocompatibility of the hydrogels and indicate no in vitro toxicity [41, 76]. Furthermore, the biocompatibility of synthesized hydrogels based on OGG [32] and GEL [77] has been reported in our previous works. As a result, these hydrogels may be a good choice for ocular medication administration.

Ex vivo investigations on corneal permeation

The main way to absorb the drug in ocular DDS is through the cornea. By controlling the release rate of the drug from the hydrogel or reducing the loss of the drug through conjunctival absorption and drainage of tear fluid, the amount of drug absorbed through the cornea can be optimized. Diffusion of drug across the conjunctiva and drainage of drug with tears in the precorneal region are responsible for precorneal drug loss. With high adhesion and viscosity, the drainage speed decreases. Therefore, for optimal drug absorption from the cornea, it is necessary to control the speed of drug release and drug elimination via nasolacrimal drainage. When using topical ocular drug delivery, the cornea acts as a barrier to ocular drug delivery and, therefore, corneal permeability is a key factor in the effectiveness of topical ocular medication administration [42, 76, 78]. Drug-loaded OGGH2 and OGGH3 were utilized according to in vitro drug release results to investigate ex vivo ocular permeability via the Franz vertical diffusion cell method (Supplementary 1). From Fig. 10, the percentage of drug release from the applied cornea with OGGH2 and OGGH3 was 46.12% and 67.82% in the first 8 h, respectively. After 24 h, the permeation efficiency of TM for OGGH3 and OGGH2 was 75.89% and 68.52% respectively (p < 0.001). A comparison of the permeation efficiency with the TM control solution (commercial product) showed that hydrogels can help to increase the drug permeation efficiency from the cornea significantly (p < 0.0001) [32, 42]. The ex vivo results showed that TM released from OGGH2 and OGGH3 hydrogels could cross the corneal barrier, and these results were consistent with the TM release process and pattern from OGG/chitosan hydrogel [32]. However, in vivo testing is required to ensure the effectiveness of the developed hydrogels.

Hemolysis assay of OGG/GEL hydrogels

The hemocompatibility of the synthesized OGG/GEL hydrogels was evaluated to determine their blood tolerance and potential hemolytic effects. Hemolysis, which occurs due to the disruption of erythrocyte membranes, leads to the release of intracellular hemoglobin into plasma. Visual inspection of the supernatants from blood samples treated with OGG/GEL hydrogels revealed clear solutions, comparable to the negative control (Fig. 11A), indicating negligible hemolytic activity. Quantitative analysis showed that the hemolysis percentage induced by the hydrogels was below 2% in PBS, which is well within the safe hemolytic threshold of 5% as defined by ISO/TR 7406 (Fig. 11B). Conversely, the positive control exhibited complete hemolysis (100%), confirming the disruption of red blood cell membrane integrity. Considering the intended application of these hydrogels as topical DDSs for the corneal surface, the formulations must be isotonic to minimize adverse effects [42]. The observed minimal hemolytic activity further supports the suitability of the developed OGG/GEL hydrogels for ocular applications.

Conclusion

In this study, in situ gelling hydrogels were constructed through a Schiff-base reaction based on GEL and OGG as a green crosslinker. Investigations were conducted on the effect of GEL and OGG concentrations on the physical and chemical properties of hydrogels. Increasing the concentration of aldehyde and amine-containing polymers improves the mechanical properties of OGG/GEL hydrogels. Hydrogels OGGH2 and OGGH3 with the highest concentration of OGG were selected as the optimal percentage with the lowest gelation time. The in vitro and ex vivo drug release behavior of the developed hydrogels indicated a sustained release, which could be desirable for ophthalmologic treatments. The OGG/GEL hydrogels indicated no obvious cytotoxicity impacts on BMSCs as a model cell line. Taking all, the developed OGG/GEL hydrogels could be considered a good candidate for the ocular delivery of TM in the treatment of glaucoma. In the next step, for the application of the OGG/Gel hydrogels as a suitable alternative to the traditional ophthalmic drug delivery methods, more research, especially in vivo studies such as the Draize ocular irritation test, should be conducted.
