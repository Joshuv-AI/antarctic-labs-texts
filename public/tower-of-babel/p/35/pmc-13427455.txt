Reframing the value of reserve antibiotics: how ultra-orphan reimbursement pathways could offer fair and equitable access to new antibiotics
Authors: Jennifer Quinn, Jutta Amersdorffer, Meindert Boysen, Orla Duffy-Roger, Charles Hewitt, Mohit Jain, David McKinney, Zrinka Ivezic-Schoenfeld, Mondher Toumi, Morgane Vanbiervliet, Jurgen Wasem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance is a significant public health challenge. Antibiotic development pipelines are insufficient to tackle this threat, with unfavourable pricing and reimbursement a key factor deterring the development of novel compounds. New antibiotics are priced against generic comparators, and practical and ethical constraints make it challenging to demonstrate superiority in clinical trials. New antibiotics are often classified as reserve antibiotics for a very select group of closely monitored patients with multidrug-resistant infections. The broader value of antibiotics is not routinely considered. ‘Pull’ incentives have been implemented to encourage development, but are not universally available, and some companies may not qualify.While it may seem counterintuitive at first, many parallels can be drawn between novel antibiotics and orphan/ultra-orphan drugs. Like orphan drugs, novel antibiotics often target small, high-unmet need patient groups, with potential for considerable clinical benefit. Several countries have established specific reimbursement pathways for orphan drugs, allowing higher prices and flexibility for clinical data uncertainty; thus, potentially addressing some of the challenges faced by antibiotics in the absence of dedicated reimbursement pathways.A multidisciplinary advisory board was convened to explore the feasibility of this further and provide recommendations to companies on leveraging orphan drug pathways for antibiotics. This paper presents its recommendations on evidence-generation activities to demonstrate added clinical value and economic savings, along with effective advocacy and collaboration to raise awareness. Key recommendations include conducting additional single-arm trials in last-line patients, generating a natural history control arm, and strengthening collaboration to build advocacy around the threat of AMR.Keywords: Antibiotics, Access, Ultra-orphan reimbursement pathways

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most pressing global health emergencies of our time. The World Health Organisation (WHO) has declared it one of the top 10 global public health threats, and many countries have developed national action plans to combat it [1], [2]. AMR is associated with 4.71 million deaths globally, and 1.14 million of these were directly attributable to AMR [3]. Nevertheless, the mortality rates associated with AMR are significantly underestimated. Cases are often attributed to another condition, such as cancer, and in the EU are estimated to be at least 2.5 times higher than what is reported in the literature [4].

The financial and economic impacts of AMR on healthcare systems are substantial. In countries within the European Union (EU) and European Economic Area (EEA), the direct annual costs to healthcare systems and societal costs (e.g., productivity losses) due to AMR are estimated to be approximately €1.1 billion [5]. Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) hold the majority of the AMR product portfolio worldwide [6], [7]. Nearly 93% of clinical-stage antibacterials and 87% of pre-clinical-stage antibiotic development is conducted by SMEs. However, these manufacturers face significant commercialisation challenges. There is a high risk of market failure and low profitability, which has resulted in several manufacturers going bankrupt [8]. The accumulated deficit of recent manufacturers involved in antibiotic development ranged from €152 to €847 million in 2024 (Table 1) [9].

Price and reimbursement challenges are a significant factor in novel antibiotic market entry failures.

The pricing and reimbursement environment for antibiotics is a key factor that deters manufacturers from investing in their development. Non-inferiority trials are often required when developing new antibiotics, as demonstrating superiority poses practical and ethical challenges [10], [11], [12]. Bacterial infections evolve rapidly, constraining manufacturers' capacity to pre-select appropriate comparators and identify patients, especially in cases involving multi-resistant pathogens. It is also unethical to withhold effective treatments from patients to substantiate superiority. Furthermore, existing pricing and reimbursement frameworks do not consistently consider the broader value elements of antibiotics, such as their benefit in reducing the spread of bacterial infections and the development of resistance [13], [14], [15]. For example, in recent HTA decisions in Germany and France for cefiderocol and ceftazidime-avibactam, the assessment frameworks did not explicitly incorporate transmission value, enablement value, or AMR prevention as separate wider value elements in the final appraisal decisions. In Germany, the reserve-antibiotic framework goes further than a standard appraisal by linking reimbursement to quality-assured use and surveillance obligations, which indirectly acknowledges the need to preserve activity against resistant pathogens. Even so, this does not amount to a formal incorporation of broader societal value elements in the appraisal process. Overall, these cases suggest that although German and French HTA bodies recognise the special status of last-resort antibiotics, current approaches stop short of explicitly valuing their broader public health benefits.

In many markets, low-cost generic comparators further constrain pricing flexibility unless manufacturers can show clear added benefit in narrowly defined high-need populations. . Additional challenges stem from funding issues, as antibiotics that are last resort options are usually hospital-prescribed and therefore funded based on diagnosis-related group (DRG) codes, which constrain the price potential of new antibiotics [16], [17]. This can be a greater issue than with other novel drugs, which often benefit from separate funding routes and greater willingness to pay [18], [19]. .,. Finally, government policy interventions have been implemented to reduce the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, including restrictions on reimbursement and prescribing requirements. While these stewardship measures are important for preserving antibiotic effectiveness, they can narrow real-world use well below the broad licensed population assessed in HTA submissions. For reserve antibiotics, this creates a mismatch between the population used to establish value and the much smaller subgroup in whom use is clinically appropriate, a scenario reflective of an ultra-orphan indication. Collectively, these challenges create a commercial paradox: society benefits most when novel antibiotics are conserved and used sparingly, yet current pricing and reimbursement systems continue to reward volume-based utilisation, undermining the economic case for antibiotic innovation.

Currently, there is no specific reimbursement route for antibiotics, although various ‘pull’ incentives have been implemented in a few countries, providing a post-launch financial incentive for antibiotic development. Notable pull incentives include annual revenue guarantees and subscription models, which may be fully or partially delinked from sales volume., where manufacturers are guaranteed a yearly income, contingent upon meeting specific contractual stipulations, such as delivering the product within a reasonable timeframe and adhering to stewardship requirements. Sweden's revenue-guarantee model is therefore an access and availability safeguard rather than a stand-alone research and development incentive. [20] The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and NHS England in the UK have implemented a fully delinked payment model that provides fixed annual payments to manufacturers irrespective of sales volume [21]. However, this is currently limited in scope and primarily covers antibiotics targeting WHO priority pathogens. Antibiotic manufacturers are required to provide assurance that they will always have supply, ensuring antimicrobial product availability at the right time and right place to meet need and demand. This is impossible for smaller manufacturers with limited financial resources and is reflected in the absence of such manufacturers in recent NICE appraisals for antibiotics. Hence, small and medium enterprises were not eligible for the recently concluded antibiotic procurement process in August 2024 [22]. Conversely, incentives in France, Germany, and Korea enable high-price negotiations for antibiotics, provided that they satisfy strict evidence requirements. On closer inspection, it was observed that an 3.3 fold increase in volume of antimicrobials used or prices for these drugs would be needed for Germany to produce similar impact on antimicrobial revenues as a delinked pull incentive [9]. AIFA in Italy has recently introduced a pull incentive, redefining the criteria for assessing a drug's innovation status, where drugs classified by WHO as reserve antibiotics are automatically classified as innovative and included in the Innovative Medicines Fund . [23] Moreover, in the update to the EU pharmaceutical legislation, the European Commission proposed transferable exclusivity vouchers that grant manufacturers an additional year of data exclusivity for priority antimicrobials. Strict conditions on the transferable exclusivity vouchers scheme are applicable, limiting their use to the fifth year of a product's regulatory data protection period and restricting these vouchers to products with annual gross sales below €490 million in any of the previous four years [23]. Some commentators have nevertheless argued that vouchers alone are unlikely to solve the underlying commercial problem [24], [25]. .Overall, while promising, pull incentives are not universally available. The entry and award criteria tend to favour large pharmaceutical manufacturers, even though most antimicrobial manufacturers are small or medium-sized.

The global peak-year sales required to enable antibiotics to be commercially viable are estimated at €1.99 billion [26]. However, based on publicly available data, only two antibiotics launched since 2000 have achieved over €1 billion in peak sales – Zyvox (linezolid) with €1.4 billion in 2015 and Cubicin (daptomycin) with €1.37 billion in 2016 (all figures reflect 2025 Euros after adjustment for inflation) [27], [28]. This highlights that higher prices or alternative payment models that delink revenue from sales volume are necessary to ensure the commercial viability of novel antibiotics [24], [29], [30]. Due to antimicrobial stewardship, novel antibiotics are likely to be used only in a very small number of multi-resistant patients. Consequently, prices that are more aligned with other orphan drugs are necessary for commercial viability in situations where revenue remains linked to volume.

Current (ultra-) orphan drug reimbursement criteria address several common challenges faced by novel antibiotics.

Many European countries have established reimbursement pathways specific to orphan or ultra-orphan drugs. These routes often have strictly defined eligibility criteria, typically limited to indications where there are no alternate therapies and where the target patient population is no greater than 5 in 10,000 people for orphan drugs and ≤ 1 in 50,000 for ultra-orphan drugs. Orphan and ultra-orphan reimbursement pathways differ from standard reimbursement pathways by offering greater flexibility for challenges in evidence generation, considering disease severity and its impact on patient and carer quality of life, as well as offering higher price potential through more generous cost-effectiveness thresholds. For example, the Highly Specialised Technologies (HST) pathway in England considers broader value elements such as societal and carer benefits and allows for greater stakeholder engagement and a cost-effectiveness threshold up to 10× higher than a standard appraisal [31]. The Swedish Dental and Pharmaceutical Benefits Agency (TLV) recently introduced a new “staircase model” that links the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) threshold to disease rarity, allowing higher prices for drugs for ultra-rare conditions. Other similar bespoke reimbursement routes for orphan and ultra-orphan drugs are available in several European countries [31], [32], [33].

Treatments appraised through (ultra-) orphan reimbursement pathways often offer long-term (potentially lifelong) benefits after a very limited treatment duration. However, limitations in the clinical evidence, including single-arm trials, small patient numbers and reliance on real-world evidence, mean that the flexibility offered by (ultra-) orphan pathways is crucial to recognise the value that these innovative treatments bring to healthcare systems. Dedicated reimbursement pathways for orphan drugs have also normalised the use of managed access agreements, post-launch evidence development, and broader deliberation on unmet need when evidence is necessarily uncertain. Several parallels can be drawn between new orphan/ultra-orphan drugs and novel antibiotics. Like many orphan drugs (e.g., gene therapies), novel antibiotics are one-time therapies, delivered over a short timeframe (typically 1 to 2 weeks or measured in days to weeks) and have the potential to cure a patient of their infection and associated morbidity and mortality. Orphan drugs and novel antibiotics address small, high-unmet-need patient groups. Like orphan drugs, the clinical evidence supporting novel antibiotics is often based on small sample sizes, surrogate outcomes and single-arm or non-inferiority study designs.

In the UK, the NICE HST program was introduced with the broad aims of encouraging research and innovation in ultra-rare disease treatments and ensuring equitable access for very small populations. The NICE HST pathway and dedicated antibiotic ‘subscription model’ both provide a useful framework for highlighting common features and differences in evidence submissions and pricing/reimbursement outcomes for orphan/ultra-orphan drugs and novel antibiotics (Table 2). While target population size, unmet needs in treatment and challenges with generating high-quality evidence for novel antibiotics share many similarities with orphan drug development, current reimbursement pathways for antibiotics do not sufficiently recognise long-term benefits to patients and health systems. This is evident from the stark differences between per-patient list prices for novel antibiotics and orphan drugs even though the treatable populations for a reserve antibiotic and ultra-orphan product are likely to be a similar size.

Materials and methods

A multi-disciplinary advisory board of seven members comprising of industry experts, representatives from the AMR advocacy community and health technology agencies, and professors with extensive knowledge of AMR policy and market access was convened. The objective of this advisory board was to explore how existing (ultra-) orphan drug reimbursement pathways could be leveraged to facilitate antibiotic reimbursement in situations where pull mechanisms are either unavailable or not applicable. The advisory board scrutinised these reimbursement pathways, assessing key strengths, weaknesses, and critical success factors for manufacturers. The scope of this discussion was limited to developed nations with established reimbursement systems, excluding low- and middle-income countries. The panel ultimately concluded that (ultra-) orphan pathways are viable for reserve antibiotics without a dedicated antibiotic reimbursement pathway. The advisory board also emphasised the importance of integrating broader value elements into health technology assessments (HTAs) to capture the unique benefits of antibiotics fully. This policy paper is an outcome of qualitative advisory board discussions, authored by members of the advisory board to stimulate debate and discussion. No formal, structured expert elicitation methodologies or formal review methodologies were employed.

Recommendations for leveraging current reimbursement pathways and enhancing access and uptake of novel antibiotics

The advisory board recommended manufacturers focus on three areas to leverage (ultra-) orphan reimbursement pathways for antibiotics: 1) Generate evidence of clinical benefit over comparators; 2) Demonstrate potential cost savings; and 3) Strengthen advocacy and collaboration to raise awareness of AMR and the societal value of novel antibiotics.

Recommendation 1: Evidence generation activities to demonstrate added clinical value

The advisory board agreed that a non-inferiority trial in a broad patient population is unlikely, on its own, to support reimbursement terms that make investment in antibiotics attractive relative to other therapeutic areas through (ultra-) orphan pathways. . Non-inferiority trials are not designed to demonstrate a potential significant additional efficacy benefit or superiority. Although they may satisfy regulatory requirements, they are unlikely to meet payer expectations without supplementary evidence…

That evidence should be generated specifically in a high-unmet-need population, for example in patients with multidrug-resistant infections who have limited or last-line treatment options. A key recommendation from the advisory board was for manufacturers to conduct more single-arm trials in small, more severe patient populations (20 to 30 patients) to support reimbursement submissions. An important consideration when designing such studies would be the inclusion of patient-relevant endpoints, including mortality, serious comorbidities, clinical failure rate, time to clinical response and quality of life outcomes.

Robust, prospective longitudinal natural history studies in patients with multidrug-resistant infections will provide valuable evidence for indirect treatment comparisons. This data would also allow disease burden and unmet need to be clearly characterised and enable a mechanism to link pharmacokinetic data, such as microbial load, to hard clinical efficacy outcomes. The experts noted that syndicated prospective natural history studies for diseases like cystic fibrosis have demonstrated the importance and viability of gathering such data.

These recommendations correspond with the level of clinical evidence typically required in reimbursement submissions for orphan drugs (Table 2, Table 3). Furthermore, the advisory board highlighted the examples of Libmeldy and Brineura, both ultra-orphan drugs that successfully used single-arm clinical trial data alongside natural history data in reimbursement applications. The efficacy of these treatments was evaluated by establishing naïve or matched indirect comparisons with natural history data, given the absence of a direct comparator arm in clinical studies. For instance, the Libmeldy evidence package included data from open-label, single-arm studies involving 30 patients. A naïve comparison was made with a natural history cohort (n = 31), comprising untreated patients. Despite some criticisms regarding gaps in baseline data, this evidence was accepted by the NICE committee. Similarly, the G-BA appraisal for Libmeldy resulted in a hint of major added benefit rating in the pre-symptomatic late-infantile/early-juvenile subgroup, where the committee found clinical improvements in outcomes compelling despite the high risk of bias. Brineura's HTAs were based on data from an open-label, single-arm trial, combined with complex statistical models showing a decline in clinical functions within a natural history cohort. The G-BA awarded Brineura noted that, since the disease is invariably fatal without treatment, the slower decline in the treatment arm was clinically relevant [[32], [33], [50], [51], [52], [53]].

Recommendation 2: Evidence generation activities to demonstrate potential for economic savings

The advisory board noted that traditional cost-effectiveness frameworks do not fully capture the value of antibiotics, particularly the broader societal value and impacts of AMR. Many of the benefits from improved antibiotic use, such as reducing the length of hospital stays and health care costs associated with infectious diseases, are realised indirectly or in the long term [[54], [55]]. The use of non-inferiority data further complicates the challenge. Ultimately, uncertainties in economic modelling pose a significant obstacle in evaluating the benefits of antibiotics, and thus, economic evaluation methods must be adapted. Manufacturers should collaborate with academic institutions to develop novel economic models that capture the broader societal value of antibiotics (e.g., incorporating transmission value into budget impact models). Certain countries, such as Italy, Belgium and Spain, consider public health benefits in their HTA processes, providing an opportunity to test alternative models.

The panel proposed utilising real-world evidence (RWE) to support modelling the long-term benefits of antibiotics in mitigating the risk associated with AMR. Leveraging RWE registries can help demonstrate reductions in transmission rates and provide estimates of insurance value. This argument can be supported by a recent study which highlighted the importance of assessing antibiotics at the population level, emphasising the consideration of transmission value to demonstrate the societal value of antibiotics [56]. Other societal and health impacts of antibiotics can be illustrated by analysing changes in hospital stays and intensive care unit admissions, which, in turn, will yield estimates for improvements in productivity and reductions in healthcare strain (e.g., closing down of hospital wards). Importantly, exploring the impact on mortality and morbidity in patients with advanced cancers is essential for illustrating the disease burden of AMR. Previous research has indicated that bacterial infections were the root cause of a significant number of deaths in patients with advanced cancer [57]. Moreover, modelling the evolution of the disease landscape without timely access to appropriate antibiotics will highlight the impact of antibiotic market failures and help accurately estimate the future cost burden of AMR.

In addition to RWE data collection, conducting discrete choice experiments (DCE) can ensure that manufacturers gain a better understanding of societal willingness to pay. DCEs assist in identifying the most relevant health-related preferences or patient-relevant outcomes valued by patients. This enables manufacturers to quantify preferences and values associated with healthcare treatments, ensuring that new antibiotics developed align with the most important outcomes for patients.

Delays in reimbursement remain a persistent barrier to antibiotic access. The average time to reimbursement for novel antibiotics varied across the four largest economies in the EU, ranging from 267 days in Germany to 579 days in Spain [58]. Additionally, delays before manufacturers seek EMA approval or market launch in an EU Member State and extended waiting periods for decisions can significantly impact company operations and financial outcomes.

The establishment of managed access agreements (MAAs) and price-volume agreements (e.g., price-volume caps) can provide payers with greater confidence in funding higher-cost antibiotics. HTA bodies often use commercial arrangements to facilitate access to orphan drugs while managing uncertainties related to clinical evidence. This is exemplified in the HTA of orphan drugs such as Zolgensma, Brineura, Kanuma, Lamzede, and Imcivree (Table 2), where a patient access scheme or MAA is in place to support reimbursement. The panel noted that such commercial arrangements are prevalent in oncology and rare disease pathways. They ensure the disease burden is appropriately characterised, which, in turn, can help improve the time to reimbursement. These agreements would facilitate addressing payer concerns regarding budget impact and uncertainties surrounding treatment uptake.

Recommendation 3: More effective advocacy and collaboration between stakeholders to build awareness of the threat of AMR and the societal value of novel antibiotics

Multi-stakeholder engagement is crucial for effectively building support for reimbursement pathway adaptations and positioning antibiotics as high-priority interventions. A unified community is paramount, including industry bodies (e.g., IFPMA, the BEAM Alliance, EFPIA), governments and policymakers to shape and influence antibiotic policy. The COVID-19 response framework, alongside the development of the NICE/NHS England subscription model, was cited as a potential model for future collaboration. The impact of AMR poses a serious societal threat, necessitating a public health/political discussion to drive more tangible change.

To drive effective policy change, advocacy efforts must be grounded in robust evidence generation, including RWE and success stories from other therapeutic areas to strengthen the urgency of addressing AMR, potentially leading to more favourable reimbursement outcomes for antibiotics. To construct a compelling advocacy narrative, any messaging campaigns (supported by multistakeholder engagement) should focus on the human and economic impact of delayed access to new antibiotics. The advisory board emphasised that vulnerable populations, such as children, patients with cancer (where ∼50% of deaths are due to infections) or immunocompromised patients, must be central to these efforts. Greater engagement with the cancer community can also enhance awareness of the impact of secondary bacterial infections on mortality and strengthen the perception of unmet medical needs. A multifaceted approach is crucial to effectively communicate the impact of AMR in a clear, understandable and actionable manner, thereby facilitating recognition within policy frameworks.

The advisory board deemed advocating for cross-stakeholder collaboration essential for harmonising antibiotics assessment across public health and payer perspectives. As previously discussed, some access challenges antibiotics face stem from a lack of alignment with evidence needs and trial designs. The advisory board emphasised that clinicians are integral to driving this effort, in the absence of high patient advocacy for orphan drugs. For instance, manufacturers can collaborate with academic institutions to address data gaps, including validating novel evidence generation models for antibiotics. To further strengthen the economic argument for antibiotics, the Sheffield Elicitation framework and multi-criteria decision analysis can also be explored to limit uncertainty and showcase pragmatism in the allocation of spend on antibiotics, respectively. Ultimately, this would enhance the credibility of novel methods with HTA bodies and increase payer acceptance.

Increasing collaborative efforts with clinical stakeholders, patient advocacy groups and HTA bodies (e.g., Beneluxa, Nordic joint HTA), and the WHO is essential in establishing severity-based ICER thresholds within reimbursement pathways. This collaborative approach to tackling AMR has been noted throughout the Nordic regions, where a shared purchasing model is under consideration. The collaboration to streamline medicine assessments among Nordic HTA bodies (Nordic joint HTA) has been extended for an additional 5 years, highlighting the potential for more effective antibiotic HTAs. Beyond regional collaboration, adjusting ICER thresholds will help better reflect the high societal value of preserving effective antibiotics. Additionally, antibiotic-specific designations should account for the public health threat of resistant infections.

Another key recommendation for future lobbying efforts was to integrate additional value elements associated with the vaccine pathway into HTAs, ideally in the context of a dedicated antibiotic-specific reimbursement pathway. A parallel was drawn with reimbursement pathways for vaccines, which allow consideration of broader value elements, such as herd immunity. Vaccine assessors adopt a pragmatic mindset when evaluating new products, and a shift in perspective would be advantageous to facilitate any future dedicated antibiotic-specific reimbursement pathway. Future work could also examine how antibacterial diagnostics and complementary push incentives interact with reimbursement reforms, because pull and push mechanisms are likely to be most effective when designed together. Conclusions

HTA bodies should apply selected criteria already used in (ultra-) orphan reimbursement pathways to reserve antibiotics and permit reimbursement outcomes that better reflect their small treated populations, high unmet need, and broader social value. . To do so effectively will require additional evidence generation by manufacturers to meet HTA evidence requirements and a shift in mindset from policymakers and HTA bodies to treat antibiotics with a similar level of flexibility as orphan drugs and potentially consider wider value elements within individual technology appraisals. Effective collaboration, lobbying, and consistent evidence-based campaigns are vital to drive these recommendations forward.
