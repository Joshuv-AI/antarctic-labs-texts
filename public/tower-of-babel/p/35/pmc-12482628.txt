Mapping multimorbidity cluster-specific intrinsic capacity impairment patterns and mortality risks in a community-dwelling aging population cohort
Authors: Fei Song, Jagadish K. Chhetri, Mengjin Hu, Boyu Li, Jinggang Xia, Chunlin Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing evidence indicate that chronic diseases have distinct clusters, each potentially influencing intrinsic capacity (IC) through unique pathological pathways. This raises two critical questions: (1) How do different multimorbidity clusters preferentially impact specific IC domains? (2) Which multimorbidity cluster would have the highest risk of mortality in individuals with IC impairment?MethodsWe used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort study in China. Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of two or more chronic conditions. IC impairment was assessed across six domains: cognition, locomotion, vitality, psychology, hearing and vision. We identified multimorbidity clusters using latent class analysis (LCA). Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between identified multimorbidity clusters and domain specific IC decline, while Kaplan-Meier analysis and cox regression analysis were used to assess the mortality risk.ResultsAmong the 4333 participants with multimorbidity, 48.6% were male, with mean age of 59.5 years. LCA method identified four distinct multimorbidity clusters: arthritis-metabolic (24.8%), stomach-arthritis (29.4%), respiratory (16.5%), metabolic-vascular (29.4%) clusters. Multimorbidity cluster-specific patterns of IC impairment revealed markedly elevated risks of decline in the cognitive, psychological, hearing, and vitality domains within the respiratory cluster. Similarly, the stomach-arthritis cluster was associated with significantly higher risks of impairment in the psychological, visual, hearing, and vitality domains. In contrast, the arthritis-metabolic cluster demonstrated significantly increased risks specifically in the cognitive and psychological domains. Survival analysis revealed significant mortality differences across multimorbidity clusters (p < 0.001). After multivariate adjustment, intrinsic capacity impairment remained significantly associated with increased mortality in the respiratory cluster (HR = 1.74, 95%CI:1.06−2.87, p = 0.029), demonstrating pattern-dependent prognostic value of IC.ConclusionsOur findings revealed a significant heterogeneity in IC impairment patterns across different multimorbidity clusters, showing cluster-specific IC impairment and cluster-dependent risk of mortality according to IC impairment.

Introduction

Population aging is now almost inevitable globally. A large portion of older adults are known to present with multiple chronic conditions as they age. Multimorbidity - defined as the co-occurrence of two or more chronic conditions in an individual possess a severe challenge in geriatric healthcare [1]. Multiple chronic conditions are difficult to manage and severely impact the quality of life in older adults. Epidemiological studies have shown the prevalence of multimorbidity to increase sharply from 30% in middle-aged adults (45–64 years) to 65% in young-older adults (65–84 years), reaching up to 82% among the oldest-old (≥85 years) [2,3]. The conventional single-disease management approaches have shown significant limitations in addressing multimorbidity in older adults, necessitating an urgent transition toward more integrated healthcare models [4,5].

The Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) care model proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO) is an example of a personalized, holistic and integrated care model [6]. The ICOPE care model focuses on optimizing intrinsic capacity (IC) to prevent functional decline in older adults and thus promoting healthy aging [7]. WHO defines IC as "the composite of physical and mental capacities of an individual". There is a growing interest among researchers in the concept of IC as it shifts away from the traditional disease-based approach to a function-centered approach. Past studies have shown impaired IC to serve as a robust predictor of adverse health outcomes, including disability and mortality [[8], [9], [10]]. However, studies investigating IC in the context of multimorbidity are lacking.

The impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes is influenced not only by the number of coexisting conditions, but also by the specific patterns of multimorbidity [11]. Emerging evidence suggests that chronic diseases do not coexist randomly but rather present specific clustering patterns [[12], [13], [14]]. These disease clusters may differentially affect the IC domains through unique pathological pathways, which is not yet understood.

The aim of this study was: (1) to elucidate the different multimorbidity clusters preferentially impacting the specific IC domains (2) identify the individuals at high-risk of mortality according to specific multimorbidity cluster and with IC impairment. This study is an attempt towards elucidating the pathway from disease patterns to functional targets to clinical outcomes, promoting the integration of function with multimorbidity. The findings of this study might provide an evidence-based foundation for precision geriatric health management.

Methods

Study population

We used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort, which is a nationally representative longitudinal cohort study of individuals aged 45 years old and above in China. The design and cohort profile of the CHARLS study have been extensively documented in previous publications [15]. The CHARLS study received approval from the Institutional Review Board at Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participating in the study. All fieldwork staff received systematic and professional training and conducted face-to-face interviews using standardized questionnaires. The datasets used in this study are publicly available from the official CHARLS website (https://charls.charlsdata.com).

Our analysis of multimorbidity clusters and their relationships with IC domains utilized the 2011 baseline data from the CHARLS cohort. For survival analysis, follow-up assessments conducted in 2013, 2015, and 2018 were used. The flowchart (Fig. 1) shows the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study. Among the 17,385 participants enrolled in the 2011 baseline study, 7661 participants with missing data on IC, 217 participants with missing information on chronic conditions were excluded. Additionally, 5174 patients without multimorbidity were also excluded in our study, leading to a inclusion of 4333 participants in the final analysis.Fig. 1Flowchart of this study.Fig. 1

Measures

Intrinsic Capacity (IC): IC was operationalized based on the original criteria proposed by the WHO, assessed through six domains: cognition, locomotion, vitality, psychology, hearing, and vision [16,17]. Although, WHO considers hearing and vision as a single sensory domain, we have separated them as two individual domains, as hearing and vision loss occur through different processes and require different clinical approach. In detail, locomotion impairment was defined as the inability to complete five independent sit-to-stand tests in 14 s [6]. Vitality impairment was indicated by a BMI ≤ 18.5 kg/m2 [6,18,19]. A self-reported impairment in hearing and vision was considered a decline in these domains [20]. Cognition was assessed by immediate and delayed recall and orientation. Cognitive capacity was considered intact if neither memory nor mental status was impaired. Psychology was assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), and a score of ≥12 was considered impairment in the psychological domain. The CES-D cut-point of ≥12 is well-validated and widely used for screening depressive symptoms in community-dwelling Chinese older adults, with multiple studies confirming its good sensitivity and specificity in this population [[21], [22], [23]].

Multimorbidity: Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of two or more chronic conditions in a single individual. We included 14 chronic conditions from the CHARLS dataset, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart problems, stroke, kidney disease, stomach or other digestive disease, emotional/nervous/psychiatric problems, memory-related disease, arthritis/rheumatism, and asthma. The medical conditions were primarily confirmed through a self-reported history of a diagnosed disease or a clinical diagnosis during the interview. For e.g., hypertension, dyslipidemia, kidney disease and diabetes or high blood sugar were further confirmed through physical examinations and blood tests. Those participants who reported a history of hypertension or were receiving specific treatment for hypertension, as well as those with SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg at baseline, were defined as hypertensive. Individuals who reported a history of diabetes or were undergoing treatment for this condition, as well as those with HbA1c≥ 6.5% at baseline [24], were considered to have diabetes. Patients who reported a history of CKD or those with eGFR < 60 were considered to have CKD. eGFR was calculated using the CKD-EPI formula (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula) equation and was classified into CKD stages according to Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [25].

Statistical analysis

Multimorbidity patterns were identified using latent class analysis (LCA) with the poLCA package. We fitted models with two to six latent classes and determined the optimal number based on Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and clinical interpretability. The four-class solution was selected as the optimal model (BIC = 46,382.29; AIC = 46,006.22). Model quality was satisfactory, with an entropy-based classification quality of 0.735 and 68.6% of participants assigned to a class with posterior probability > 0.8. The stability of the model was confirmed through multiple runs with different random seeds, showing minimal variation in BIC (coefficient of variation = 0.03%). Participants were subsequently assigned to the latent class for which they had the highest posterior probability. Each subgroup was characterized by chronic condition prevalence rates and visualized in Fig. 2, demonstrating clinically significant divergence in the chronic disease patterns.Fig. 2Disease profiles of the four multimorbidity clusters derived from latent class analysis. Disease profiles of the four multimorbidity clusters derived from latent class analysis. The bar plot displays the relative probabilities for 14 chronic conditions across the four latent classes. Each cluster is represented by a distinct color.

Baseline characteristics of patients by clusters were compared. Continuous variables with a normal distribution ware expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and compared using the Student t-test. For continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution, the median and interquartile ranges were utilized for statistical description and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. The frequency of categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test. Subsequently, logistic regression analysis was used to estimate the association between multimorbidity clusters and impairment in IC domains. Odds ratios (OR) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Results were visualized using forest plots for comparisons. Additionally, we calculated and presented the prevalence of functional decline for each capacity domain across clusters using a color-gradient heatmap. Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox regression analysis was used to examine the association between multimorbidity clusters and mortality risk. First, we generated overall survival curves stratified by the four identified multimorbidity clusters, followed by cluster-specific analyses evaluating the impact of impaired intrinsic capacity on survival within each subgroup.

Results

Multimorbidity clusters

Hierarchical clustering identified four distinct multimorbidity clusters among the included 4333 participants with multimorbidity. Fig. 2 illustrates the conditional probability distribution of each chronic disease within the four identified clusters. Cluster 1 exhibited a higher prevalence of arthritis than other clusters, along with high rates of hypertension and dyslipidemia, hence labeled as “arthritis-metabolic” cluster. In Cluster 2, the prevalence of stomach disease and arthritis stood out significantly compared to the other clusters, leading to the designation of “stomach-arthritis” cluster. Cluster 3 exhibited a notably higher prevalence of chronic lung disease and asthma compared to the other clusters, hence, it was classified as “respiratory” cluster. In Clusters 4, the prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes was notably elevated compared to the other clusters, along with elevated rates of heart disease, stroke, and chronic kidney disease. Thus, it was designated as the “metabolic-vascular” cluster. In summary, the four multimorbidity clusters comprised of arthritis-metabolic (1073, 24.8%), stomach-arthritis (1273, 29.4%), respiratory (713, 16.5%), metabolic-vascular (1274, 29.4%) clusters. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the clusters. Additionally, we compared the baseline characteristics between individuals excluded due to missing IC (n = 3425) and those ultimately included in the analysis (n = 4333) among patients with multimorbidity (Supplement Table 1). The results showed that the included group was younger (mean difference: 2.8 years) with a slightly higher proportion of males (absolute difference: 8.5%). However, the two groups exhibited similar distributions in most disease burden indicators and blood biochemical measures.

Association between multimorbidity patterns and domains of IC

The heatmap analysis revealed distinct patterns of IC impairment across the multimorbidity clusters (Fig. 3). Visual, psychological, and cognitive domains were the most vulnerable domains across all multimorbidity clusters. Different cluster types exhibited distinct patterns of IC impairment. As shown in Fig. 4 significant heterogeneity in the risk profiles were observed across the four clusters. Compared to the metabolic-vascular cluster (reference group), the respiratory cluster demonstrated a significantly higher risk of intrinsic capacity (IC) impairment in the following domains: cognitive (OR = 1.43, 95%CI = 1.17–1.74), psychological (OR = 2.05, 95%CI = 1.67–2.51), hearing (OR = 1.31, 95%CI = 1.00–1.71) and vitality (OR = 6.07, 95%CI = 4.02–9.41) domains. The stomach-arthritis cluster was associated with significantly elevated risks of impairment in the psychological (OR = 2.26, 95%CI = 1.90–2.70), visual (OR = 1.27, 95%CI = 1.09–1.50), hearing (OR = 1.33, 95%CI = 1.06–1.68) and vitality (OR = 3.34, 95%CI = 2.23–5.16) domains. Impact of IC impairment at baseline on mortality risk in different multimorbidity clusters

We subsequently conducted stratified Kaplan-Meier survival analyses to examine the association between baseline IC impairment and mortality across the four multimorbidity clusters (Fig. 6). Significant heterogeneity in survival outcomes was observed. No significant mortality difference was found between those with impairment in IC and intact IC in arthritis-metabolic cluster (Fig. 6A, P = 0.070) and metabolic-vascular cluster (Fig. 6D, P = 0.071). However, IC impairment was significantly associated with increased mortality risk in both the respiratory cluster (Fig. 6C, P = 0.003) and stomach-arthritis cluster (Fig. 6B, P = 0.028). After multivariate adjustment, IC impairment remained significantly associated with increased mortality in the respiratory cluster (HR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.06–2.87, p = 0.029). Discussion

Our findings demonstrate a significant heterogeneity in IC impairment patterns across various multimorbidity clusters, with distinct IC domain specific vulnerabilities, differential risk magnitudes, and cluster-specific prognostic value of IC for risk of mortality. These findings also highlight the potential necessity of multimorbidity specific IC management approaches in clinical practice.

With the rapid increase in global aging, multimorbidity has become a critical public health concern [1]. Growing evidence reveals that the co-occurrence of multiple chronic diseases follows non-random patterns, potentially being driven by common underlying pathophysiological pathways that ultimately manifest as distinct disease clustering phenotypes [12,14]. Our findings have important implications for elucidating disease progression. In this study, we applied LCA—a model-based clustering method particularly suited for identifying unobserved subgroups in population health data—to delineate multimorbidity patterns [26,27]. Our analysis identified four clinically interpretable cluster. The respiratory, metabolic-vascular and stomach-arthritis align with multimorbidity patterns consistently reported in previous studies [12]. Notably, arthritis emerged in two distinct clusters: one characterized by co-occurrence with metabolic conditions, and another primarily associated with gastric diseases. These associations likely reflect distinct etiopathological mechanisms that cannot be meaningfully merged. The arthritis-metabolic cluster may be driven by systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, which are common underpinnings of both osteoarthritis and metabolic disorders [28]. In contrast, the stomach-arthritis cluster may arise from shared autoimmune pathways or NSAID-induced mucosal injury in patients using anti-inflammatory medications [29]. These differential mechanistic underpinnings suggest that combining the two clusters would obscure important heterogeneity in etiology.

A recent study demonstrated that multimorbidity was associated with sustained impairment in IC among older adults [30]. Indeed, decline in IC could be a result of certain health-related stressors, which also links IC with the physical resilience concept [31] and a proxy of underlying reserves that are impacted by stressors [32]. Crucially, the domain-specific effects of particular multimorbidity patterns on IC components have not been systematically investigated. We identified differential IC impairment profiles across multimorbidity clusters, revealing domain-selective vulnerabilities. The respiratory cluster was the dominant cluster demonstrating particularly striking vulnerabilities, showing over 6 fold increased risk for vitality loss, along with elevated risks for cognitive, hearing and psychological impairment. Similarly, the stomach-arthritis cluster exhibited almost 3 fold risk of vitality loss and comparable psychological and hearing impairment. In contrast, the arthritis-metabolic cluster was primarily associated with declines in cognitive and psychological domains. These cluster-specific associations likely reflect the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms linking functions with diseases. Previous studies have shown hypoxia, smoking, infections, and systemic inflammation to constitute the key pathogenic pathways linking respiratory cluster diseases to neurodegeneration, depression and mobility impairment [[33], [34], [35]]. While the gastrointestinal-arthritis/arthritis-metabolic cluster's impairments may stem from pain-induced sleep disruption, chronic stress pathways, and gut-mediated nutrient absorption issues [36,37]. These findings may carry significant clinical implications for personalized precision health management approach. For patients within the respiratory cluster, guideline-based care for respiratory conditions could be supplemented by adjunct strategies addressing functional decline, such as supervised pulmonary rehabilitation, targeted nutritional support, and cognitive screening given the observed vulnerability in this domain. Similarly, for the stomach-arthritis cluster, integrated interventions aligned with multimodal pain management complemented by mental health support and dietary modifications may help mitigate the biopsychosocial burden suggested by this cluster’s profile. Although the assignment of cluster membership does not directly dictate new treatments, it may help identify subgroups more likely to benefit from specific adjunctive interventions that target shared mechanisms. These insights highlight the potential for future research to develop more tailored adjunctive strategies that extend beyond current condition-specific guidelines, particularly for older adults with complex multimorbidity patterns that may not be fully addressed by siloed care approaches.

Given the strong association between multimorbidity clusters and IC impairment, we investigated the prognostic impact of IC status at baseline in patients with multimorbidity. Through Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with stratification by IC status at baseline, we identified cluster-specific prognostic patterns. Notably, IC impairment was significantly associated with increased mortality risk specifically in the respiratory cluster, even after multivariate adjustment. In contrast, no statistically significant differences in mortality based on IC status were observed in the other clusters. The differential prognostic significance of IC across disease clusters may be explained by distinct clinical trajectories. Patients in this cluster are prone to acute and critical conditions such as respiratory failure and severe infective exacerbations [38,39], where patients' reserve (represented by IC) becomes the determining factor for survival [40]. On the other hand, clusters such as the metabolic, arthritis- metabolic and stomach-arthritis groups clusters demonstrated more stable chronic disease progression patterns, with mortality being primarily dependent on long-term management of underlying conditions rather than IC. These findings demonstrate the cluster-dependent prognostic value of IC assessment. Hence, it is apparent that precision medicine in geriatrics must integrate both disease clusters and IC profiles to optimize risk stratification.

Our findings have important clinical implications for the management of patients with multimorbidity. Firstly, the identified cluster-specific IC impairment profiles enabled stratified approaches to consider in geriatrics. Secondly, the vulnerability of emotional, and cognitive domains across all clusters highlights the need for integrated mental health and rehabilitation services in multimorbidity management. Thirdly, the stratified Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed how impaired IC influences mortality risk across the four multimorbidity clusters, suggesting that the prognostic value of IC assessment may be fundamentally shaped by an individual's underlying disease pattern.

Several limitations of our study should also be acknowledged. Firstly, the exclusion of cases with missing data, particularly IC measures, may have resulted in selection bias. To address this, we conducted a comprehensive comparison of baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants and further adjusted for significantly different variables in multivariate models. Additionally, multiple imputation was performed for missing covariates in the Cox regression analyses to enhance the robustness of the findings. Secondly, the reliance on self-reported or registry-based disease diagnoses may introduce reporting bias. In our study, we employed a multi-modal case identification approach to mitigate this concern wherever possible. Such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease were objectively verified through rigorous physical examinations, blood pressure measurements, and laboratory tests as detailed in our methods. Thirdly, due to database limitations, certain chronic conditions could not be included, which may have impacted the completeness of cluster characterization. Additionally, the assessment of vitality using BMI and sensory domains via self-report, while pragmatic and consistent with major aging studies, represents a trade-off between feasibility and precision. Nevertheless, these widely adopted measures remain valuable for population-level insights. Lastly, as an observational study, our findings do not permit definitive causal conclusions, and residual confounding may persist. Future studies should validate clusters in diverse cohorts and explore biological mechanisms underlying IC decline. Additionally, intervention studies are needed to examine whether targeted improvement of specific IC domains can mitigate cluster-specific mortality risk.

Conclusion

Our study revealed multimorbidity cluster-specific patterns of IC impairment, characterized by distinct domain vulnerabilities and differential mortality risks. Our findings underscore the clinical necessity for tailored IC management strategies based on multimorbidity profiles.
