Hypothalamic obesity: Epidemiology in rare sellar/suprasellar tumors—A German claims database analysis
Authors: Julian Witte, Bastian Surmann, Manuel Batram, Markus Weinert, Mathias Flume, Nicolas Touchot, Julia Beckhaus, Carsten Friedrich, Hermann L. Müller
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypothalamic obesity (HO) is defined as abnormal weight gain resulting in severe persistent obesity due to physical, tumor‐ and/or treatment‐related damage to the hypothalamus. HO epidemiology is poorly understood. We developed a database algorithm supporting the standardized identification of tumor/treatment‐related HO (TTR‐HO) patients. The algorithm is used to estimate incidence rates of TTR‐HO patients in the German healthcare context from a representative claims database (n = 5.42 million) covering 2010–2020. Patients were identified based on surgery/radiotherapy procedures and HO‐associated tumor diagnoses (n = 3976). HO was defined by incident obesity and validated based on incident diabetes insipidus diagnoses and desmopressin prescription within a 12‐month period after surgery/radiotherapy. Uncertainty due to algorithm definitions is explored in sensitivity analyses. Estimated annual incidence of TTR‐HO in Germany is between 0.7 and 1.7 cases per 1,000,000 persons (2019 prevalence: n = 1262 patients). With observed cases in all age groups, two HO‐incidence peaks are identified: children/young adults aged 10–24 years and adults aged 40–44 years. Most frequent HO‐validated tumor diagnoses are benign sellar/suprasellar tumors (6.1/1,000,000 persons over 9 years), including tumors of the craniopharyngeal duct (1.3/1,000,000), neoplasms of the pituitary gland (4.1/1,000,000), and nonspecific brain tumors of endocrine glands (2.4/1,000,000). This is the first real‐world database analysis of TTR‐HO epidemiology, refining current estimates of HO epidemiology and early patient identification. A more comprehensive characterization of patients with HO as well as a better understanding of clinical implications will be crucial in developing optimal treatment strategies to improve patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Survivors of hypothalamic and sellar/suprasellar tumors are at risk for significant morbidity with impacts on health and health‐related quality of life. Hypothalamic obesity (HO) is most commonly described in the context of craniopharyngioma and its treatment, but can also occur due to sellar/suprasellar tumors of different histology, irradiation, trauma, inflammation or surgical insult to the hypothalamus. 1 , 2 , 3 The most common causes of acquired hypothalamic damage are space‐occupying lesions, such as craniopharyngiomas and pituitary macroadenomas, with sellar/suprasellar extension and invasion of the hypothalamic nuclei. 3 Recent evidence implies that there may be differences in HO prevalence depending on lesion location and size. 4 Hypothalamic damage can be caused by the tumor or subsequent surgical and/or radiooncological treatment. 5 , 6

HO is a rare disease; data detailing its epidemiology are scarce and limited. Published epidemiological estimates of HO are based on the most common diagnosis associated with HO: craniopharyngioma, which accounts for ≥50% of all HO cases. 7 , 8 Annual incidence of craniopharyngioma is reported to range from 0.5 to 1.9 per 1,000,000 persons per year and can vary depending on age at diagnosis. 6 , 8 General obesity and eating disorders are prevalent in 40%–50% of children with craniopharyngioma, 8 , 9 , 10 but without a precise HO diagnosis, the epidemiology of tumor/treatment‐related HO (TTR‐HO) remains uncertain.

Along with endocrine dysfunction resulting from tumor treatment, patients with TTR‐HO present with hyperphagia and reduced energy expenditure. 6 Rapid weight gain usually begins during the first weeks after tumor treatment, leading to morbid obesity, impaired quality of life, and increased mortality and morbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, circadian rhythm disturbances, sleep‐disordered breathing, non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease, and reduction in functional capacity. 11 , 12 , 13 Cardiovascular risk factors are highly prevalent and often inadequately treated in adult patients with hypothalamic–pituitary disease, 14 and aggressive treatment is essential to reducing mortality and morbidity in HO patients. 13 , 15 Despite standard treatment options like medication and lifestyle modifications, clinical management of HO remains challenging. 11 , 16 Better patient characterization and enhanced understanding of the epidemiology of TTR‐HO may facilitate improvement of treatment strategies. A transparent and reproducible algorithm aimed at identifying TTR‐HO patients and related tumors will enable refinement of the epidemiology of this rare endocrine disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and database

Our study uses a retrospective cohort design and has been conducted following applicable subject privacy requirements and the guiding principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964. Analyses are based on anonymized routinely collected claims data from German Statutory Health Insurance (SHI), a system which insures 88% of the German population. 17 GWQ ServicePlus AG, a joint venture of medium‐sized SHI funds in Germany, provided the data. The dataset comprises information on 5.42 million people insured at 19 SHI funds, representing 6.3% of the total German SHI population. Based on a comparison with official statistics published by the German Health Ministry on SHI (“KM6”), the data set is representative of the German SHI population in terms of age and gender distribution. 18 Analyses were based on data covering the period 2010–2020. We used the years 2011–2019 for patient identification, allowing for a 1‐year wash‐in and 1‐year post‐observational period.

Diagnostic data include all diagnoses documented during physician outpatient contacts and patient hospital stays. German claims data do not include laboratory results, anthropometric data, MRI findings, detailed tumor characteristics, or detailed information on physician contacts within inpatient stays. While prescription data is available, prescriptions and their specific indications are not directly linked. A general description of claims in the German setting is available from Swart et al. 19 Furthermore, the German healthcare system is characterized by high involvement of general practitioners, many of whom also practice internal medicine. Outpatient access to specialists, such as endocrinologists, can be associated with long wait times.

Study population

The initial data set represents all persons in the SHI database aged between 0 and 100 years in the years 2010–2020. We only considered patients with at least 24 consecutive months of observational period (1 year before index hospitalization for incidence validation, 1 year after index hospitalization for HO validation) in the data set. Individual information on the birth year was provided using 5‐year intervals. We identified patients with TTR‐HO using an algorithm approach. First, we selected all patients hospitalized with an incident primary‐ or secondary‐discharge tumor diagnosis known to potentially lead to TTR‐HO (“index hospitalization,” for applied inclusion criteria see Table S1) and incident inpatient brain surgery/radiotherapy. Incidence was validated based on the preceding 12 months without respective tumor diagnosis and brain surgery/radiotherapy. Second, we applied an incident obesity diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, German Modification [ICD‐10‐GM] E66.x, E67.x, E68, R63.2) within 12 months of index hospitalization as a mandatory criterion for TTR‐HO‐patient definition. Incident obesity was validated based on the preceding 12 months without an obesity diagnosis. Third, we applied central diabetes insipidus (ICD‐10‐GM E23.0, E23.2, E23.3, E23.6, E23.7) and a desmopressin prescription (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical‐Classification [ATC]‐code H01BA02) within the post‐observational period as HO‐validation criteria. ICD‐10 codes such as E67.x and E23.x were included based on their likely association with HO in this context.

We selected central diabetes insipidus as a TTR‐HO‐validation criterion, as these patients are more likely to have postsurgical weight gain and more significant hypothalamic damage on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly in the posterior hypothalamus. Central diabetes insipidus does not directly cause HO but can serve as an endocrine marker of hypothalamic damage. Given our algorithm approach, using incident inpatient treatment and incident obesity diagnosis, the hypothalamic damage and diabetes insipidus are likely attributable to the surgery or radiotherapy. However, it cannot be assumed that central diabetes insipidus is always accurately documented in claims. As all patients with diabetes insipidus need treatment, desmopressin is defined as a validation criterion to capture hormone replacement therapy. Although it is possible that diabetes insipidus occurs transiently in some cases, it is likely that diabetes insipidus following TTR‐HO is persistent. Once desmopressin medication has been started, there is generally no return to the situation before and the continuous substitution reflects a permanent chronic deficit.

To differentiate between acquired and genetic risk for HO, patients with a history of Prader–Willi syndrome (ICD‐10‐GM Q87.1) were excluded from the study. Other rare genetic syndromes, such as Bardet–Biedl syndrome or Alström syndrome, are not explicitly excluded, as obesity in such cases would likely pre‐date TTR‐HO and thus not be considered an incidence of TTR‐HO according to our identification algorithm.

Definition of TTR‐HO and other relevant patient identification criteria were made based on literature 16 , 20 , 21 following review of medical records of TTR‐HO patients treated in a large German pediatric clinic with >100 cases of TTR‐HO per year. HO definition and validation criteria can be related to different physician contacts or hospitalizations.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was annual HO incidence rate. Results were differentiated by age, sex, and underlying tumor entity. Secondary outcome measures were cumulative HO incidence over 9 years, prevalence, and site of HO diagnosis validation. We distinguished between “hospital validation,” that is, inpatient incident follow‐up treatment of obesity and central diabetes insipidus (index or subsequent hospitalization), and diagnosis by outpatient physicians (Table S2).

Statistical analysis

Based on the study population and study design explained in the preceding sections (see also Figure S1), we calculated incidence rates using the midterm population‐at‐risk. We calculated prevalence as a function of incidence and survival rate. We conservatively assumed a disease duration of 20 years, following the results of a longitudinal analysis of craniopharyngioma registry data showing a 20‐year survival rate of nearly 90% in 280 patients with childhood‐onset craniopharyngioma. 13

We explored uncertainty associated with epidemiologic estimate of HO by varying TTR‐HO validation criteria: validation of HO based on patients with and without desmopressin prescription, validation of HO based on patients with prevalent obesity diagnoses before index hospitalization, and validation of HO without central diabetes insipidus diagnosis.

Reporting of study results followed STROBE criteria. 22 We performed analyses with R (Version 4.1.3), a standard and open‐access statistical computing programming language.

RESULTS

Study population

Based on the database of 5.42 million persons from 2011 to 2019, between 3.93 and 4.41 million persons met the inclusion criteria annually. Over this time period, we identified a total of 3976 patients with index hospitalization for treatment of tumors potentially leading to TTR‐HO. Of these, a total of 37 patients (3–7 patients annually, see Table 1) could be validated with TTR‐HO following our algorithm (Figure 1). Patients were more often female (59.5%), with a mean age of 38 years at index hospitalization (standard deviation [SD]: 18.0 years).

HO epidemiological estimates

The estimated 9‐year incidence of TTR‐HO is 6.8 cases per 1,000,000 persons (data: 2011–2019, Table 1). Extrapolated to the total German population, this is approximately 80 incident TTR‐HO cases per year. The estimated and extrapolated 2019 prevalence is 1262 cases in Germany (203 persons age <20 years, 784 persons age 20–64 years, 274 persons age 65+). The estimated annual incidence of TTR‐HO in Germany is 0.7–1.7 cases per 1,000,000 persons. After index hospitalization, we observed incident obesity and diabetes insipidus diagnosis within 3 months in 90% of patients and within 6 months in 95% of patients.

HO patient characteristics and treatment pathways

We observed two TTR‐HO incidence peaks, in children and young adults age 10–24 years and adults age 40–44 years (Figure 1). Diagnosis codes differentiate between neoplasms of benign behavior and neoplasms of unknown behavior. The most frequent TTR‐HO diagnoses are benign sellar and suprasellar tumors, including tumors of the craniopharyngeal duct (1.3/1,000,000 over 9 years) and neoplasms of the pituitary gland (4.1/1,000,000). Nonspecific brain tumors of unknown behavior of endocrine glands (2.4/1,000,000) were also frequent (Table 2). The overall 9‐year incidence of TTR‐HO‐validated benign sellar/suprasellar tumors was 6.1/1,000,000 (controlling for multiple coding of the same tumor). Benign sellar/suprasellar tumors were more common among patients age <20 years, whereas pituitary gland neoplasms were most common among adults age 20–64 and 65+ years at index hospitalization (Table 2).

By definition, all patients had a desmopressin prescription in the year after hospitalization. To approximate pituitary involvement: 97% of patients had a hydrocortisone prescription, 86% of patients had a levothyroxine prescription, and 27% of patients had a testosterone prescription in the 2 years following TTR hospitalization.

Various multidisciplinary groups were involved in the treatment of TTR‐HO patients in hospital settings and in outpatient care. 40% of TTR‐HO patients met the HO definition‐ and validation‐criteria during index hospitalization or follow‐up hospitalization, while 34% were diagnosed at an outpatient general practitioner (GP) visit. Endocrinologists were less likely to be involved in diagnosing obesity or diabetes insipidus after HO‐related tumor surgery/radiotherapy (Table S3; Figure 2).

Sensitivity analysis

Extending the HO‐validation period to prevalent obesity patients did not significantly affect incidence estimates, whereas removing the criterion of mandatory desmopressin prescription lead to substantially more patients. Among patients age ≥40 years, the estimated number of patients with TTR‐HO was 5.7 times higher when no validation by desmopressin prescription was applied (Figure 3). Altering HO validation by not requiring a central diabetes insipidus diagnosis did not affect incidence estimates (Table S4; Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

HO epidemiology is poorly understood because incidence and prevalence are related to several very rare underlying diseases. Currently, the most‐cited epidemiological approximation for HO is based on two US population‐based cancer registries. These registries include brain tumors of benign and borderline malignancy and report an incidence estimate of craniopharyngioma of 1.3 per 1,000,000. 23 In this study, we focused on identifying TTR‐HO‐specific incidence, and used a wash‐in period to account for prevalent obesity. We identified TTR‐HO patients using an algorithm approach. To be considered an incident TTR‐HO patient, following the wash‐in period, patients must have been hospitalized for a tumor‐related diagnosis, been diagnosed with obesity, and been given a desmopressin prescription in the follow‐up period. Based on our results, the estimated annual incidence of TTR‐HO in Germany is 0.7–1.7 cases per 1,000,000 persons. Furthermore, incident obesity and diabetes insipidus diagnosis were observed within 3 months in 90% of patients and within 6 months in 95% of patients in our study.

Our algorithm approach allows us to assess not only the development of obesity but also the incidence of diabetes insipidus requiring treatment, allowing for a better clinical understanding of TTR‐HO and associated risk factors. For example, Sterkenburg et al. reported that prevalent overweight/obesity is one risk factor for developing severe obesity in pediatric patients with different sellar/suprasellar tumors. 13 Our results indicate that most TTR‐HO patients developed incident obesity and did not have prevalent obesity before tumor‐related surgery/radiotherapy. Other studies, based on selected retrospective medical records review, report high HO prevalence rates among patients with tumors involving the hypothalamic–pituitary region, with 57% of patients being obese (body mass index [BMI] ≥30 kg/m2) and 15% morbidly obese (BMI ≥40 kg/m2). 12 Yet, these patients were reported to be older at the time of diagnosis compared with our study cohort (mean age at tumor diagnosis: 43 years vs. 38 years at tumor‐surgery/radiotherapy). Craniopharyngiomas, as nonmalignant sellar/parasellar tumors, exhibit a well‐known bimodal age distribution also observed in our analysis. 23 , 24 While most studies report a peak in adolescence (age 10–20 years), many studies differ in the second peak among older adults with reported age groups ranging from 40 to 65–74 years. 23 , 25 Our observed low incidence in persons aged 15–19 years may be due to small sample‐sizes.

Our study expands the spectrum of TTR‐HO tumor diagnoses in addition to craniopharyngioma. Our analysis identified TTR‐HO cases related to all tumor entities described in the patients' self‐reported data of the International Registry of Hypothalamic Obesity Disorders (IRHOD) 16 except for the diagnosis of “other specified congenital malformations of the brain” (ICD‐10‐GM Q04.8). TTR‐HO cases with benign neoplasm of the pituitary gland are significantly more frequent in our claims data analysis when compared with craniopharyngioma. It cannot be excluded that a certain number of cases diagnosed with benign neoplasm of the pituitary gland have a histological diagnosis of craniopharyngioma but were not coded accordingly. No reported cases at the IRHOD register were related to neoplasm with uncertain or unknown behavior or other histiocytosis syndromes, while 36% of all TTR‐HO cases in our analysis come from these tumor entities. As tumor entities in the IRHOD registry are based on patient‐reported data, it can be speculated that TTR‐HO patients with nonspecific tumor diagnoses are underrepresented in registry data. Alternatively, it is conceivable that the observed rate of nonspecific tumor entities is influenced by administrative miscoding. This could be clarified by a validation study, which would collect administrative diagnosis documentation in parallel to a clinical examination.

The trends in site of diagnosis can be explained by the German setting and data. Most tumor diagnoses are made in the hospital; however, we cannot observe which specialists were involved in the diagnosis (see Table S3). Although it is not explicit in the data, it is highly likely that endocrinologists are involved with inpatient care and diagnosis. Furthermore, in Germany, many general practitioners are also practicing internists, which could explain the high number of incidences that come from outpatient general practitioner diagnoses. In addition, outpatient access to endocrinologists is sometimes limited or associated with long wait times, which could explain the relatively low level of outpatient endocrinologist involvement observed in this data.

In addition, a significant effect on the number of patients with TTR‐HO is the additional validation by a desmopressin prescription as a confirmation of diabetes insipidus. 26 Diabetes insipidus is associated with hypothalamic obesity because vasopressin is produced in the hypothalamus, transported via the pituitary stalk to the posterior lobe of the pituitary gland, and released into the bloodstream. A diabetes insipidus diagnosis without medication is not considered a valid confirmation, as we cannot disregard missing diagnosis documentation or misclassification. Furthermore, using desmopressin to validate TTR‐HO after index hospitalization yields a low risk of false‐positives.

Obesity, diabetes insipidus, and desmopressin prescription occur rapidly after surgery/radiotherapy and show patterns expected from theory in terms of frequency and age distribution. Other studies have shown that incident or worsened obesity might follow diabetes insipidus requiring treatment. 12 Our results suggest that the treatment of diabetes insipidus with desmopressin, a synthetic analogue of vasopressin, might be an important factor for early identification of TTR‐HO patients. This is important, as HO often goes unnoticed initially as patients, families, and medical teams focus on oncological therapies and the management of panhypopituitarism. 27 Early recognition of TTR‐HO is necessary for prevention and intervention before substantial weight gain occurs. 11 , 28 Our results emphasize the relevance of the GP's involvement in communication around occurrence of HO, early obesity‐prevention, and HO‐related treatment initiation.

While available treatments showed limited efficacy and considerable side effects of pharmaceutical agents for obesity treatment, new treatment options for HO are currently under development. Setmelanotide, a melanocortin‐4 receptor (MC4R) agonist, is currently in a phase 3 clinical trial for patients with lesional hypothalamic obesity, following robust phase‐2‐trial data showing a statistically significant proportion of TTR‐HO patients achieving ≥5% reduction in BMI. 29

Our study has some limitations unique to claims data. Claims data are collected primarily for administrative and billing purposes, rather than for epidemiological research. As a result, analyses using claims data are often subject to certain assumptions and inherent uncertainty. Routine data do not include lab results, anthropometric data, MRI findings, detailed tumor characteristics, or information on specialist contacts during inpatient treatment. This leads to four specific limitations. First, although it is not explicit in the data, we assume that all patients have an inpatient contact with an endocrinologist. Second, as no clinical parameters such as BMI are available in claims data, available diagnosis codes of obesity limit the analysis of weight gains. Poor sensitivity of ICD‐10 diagnosis data for administrative diagnosis of overweight/obesity has been reported in the literature. 30 However, it can be assumed that documentation might be more accurate because of the high patient relevance of rapid weight gain in TTR‐HO. Third, the potential for upcoding must be considered for some validation criteria 31 ; this effect should, however, be small due to our strict case definition (i.e., in the primary analysis, all validation criteria need to be documented within 4 quarters after index hospitalization). Fourth, evaluating indication‐specific prescriptions in German routine data is complex, especially in multimorbid patients. For this reason, we are not able to exclude subjects receiving desmopressin specifically for enuresis. However, it is possible that desmopressin treatment for enuresis is a rare and generally reserved to pediatric settings. Despite its limitations, the dataset represents one of this study's primary strengths. Its large size leads to a relatively high number of patients with TTR‐HO and provides a comprehensive database for the validation of TTR‐HO through incident obesity, central diabetes insipidus diagnosis, and desmopressin prescription. The large dataset allows us to observe relevant clinical patient constellations that may not have been possible via case reports.

The study design and methods face other general limitations as well. First, there is no available information on the history of TTR‐HO‐applied definition and validation criteria prior to the observational period for defining incident cases. If, for example, a TTR‐HO patient had a 2‐year interval between TTR‐related hospitalizations, 1‐year wash‐in period would be too short, and the prevalent case would be misclassified as an incident case. Second, self‐selection of patients is possible; for example, patients with severe weight gain might be more likely to seek treatment after TTR‐HO (and be documented in claims data). However, in the German setting, all patients are closely monitored in follow‐up visits after brain tumor treatment; in addition, the ubiquity of associated neuroendocrine disorders and their treatment implies that all patients are likely to be under active medical care during this period. In this study, we focus on the analysis of incident TTR‐HO specifically, whereas future research could examine the epidemiology of other acquiredHO diseases, such as systemic non‐tumorous disorders like sarcoidosis and hypophysitis, or the sequelae of HO.

This is the first real‐world database analysis of TTR‐HO epidemiology. Analysis of epidemiology of rare diseases such as HO is highly important not only to understand the disease, but also to inform clinical and health‐economic decision‐making. Due to lack of definitional standards for clinical documentation, epidemiological estimation is associated with uncertainty. We refined previously reported definitions and estimates of HO epidemiology. The results indicate that TTR‐HO can develop at all ages and following surgery or radiotherapy of multiple different sellar/suprasellar tumor entities besides craniopharyngioma, leading to an incidence slightly higher than previously estimated. Our results also imply that a diabetes insipidus diagnosis and desmopressin prescription after tumor‐related surgery/radiotherapy may be valid indicators of TTR‐HO in patients with obesity. Considering how quickly these indicators manifest after tumor‐surgery/radiotherapy, post‐operative planning should include reviewing the indication for desmopressin.
