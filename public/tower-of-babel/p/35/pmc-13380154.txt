A multidisciplinary FEVAR and stent graft repair to exclude mycotic aneurysms by interventional radiology and vascular surgery: Case report
Authors: Nitya Kumar, Shawn Sarin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycotic pseudoaneurysms are rare arterial wall disruptions caused by infectious or inflammatory destruction of the vessel wall. They account for a small percentage, about 0.7%, of all aortic aneurysms. Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) therapy for nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer has been associated with rare vascular complications, including infectious aortitis and pseudoaneurysm formation. We present a 69-year-old male with a history of intravesical BCG therapy who presented with severe bilateral flank and abdominal pain and was found to have multifocal pararenal aortic and left common iliac pseudoaneurysms with extensive necrotic para-aortic and iliac lymphadenopathy. Given concern for an underlying infection and poor candidacy for open surgery, a multidisciplinary team consisting of vascular surgery and interventional radiology performed complex in situ laser fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair with preservation of the superior mesenteric and bilateral renal arteries. The procedure successfully excluded the pseudoaneurysms. Although microbiologic confirmation of BCG infection was not obtained, the temporal relationship to prior intravesical BCG therapy and imaging findings raised suspicion for a BCG-associated infectious source. This case highlights the technical feasibility of multidisciplinary endovascular management of complex paravisceral pseudoaneurysms and emphasizes the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients presenting with vascular abnormalities following BCG therapy.

Introduction

Intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) therapy remains one of the most effective adjuvant treatments for high-risk nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). BCG consists of a live attenuated strain of Mycobacterium bovis and is administered following transurethral resection to reduce tumor recurrence and progression. Although most adverse effects are localized to the urinary tract, rare systemic complications have been reported, including disseminated infection, infectious aortitis, and mycotic aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm formation [1].

Mycotic pseudoaneurysms are uncommon vascular lesions resulting from infectious or inflammatory destruction of the arterial wall and account for a small percentage of all aortic aneurysms. BCG-associated vascular complications remain rare but potentially life-threatening because of the risk of aneurysm expansion and rupture. According to the American Heart Association, rupture is the most serious fatal complication of mycotic aneurysms, occurring in 50%-85% of cases; there is a significantly higher mortality rate compared to nonmycotic aneurysms [2]. Diagnosis is often challenging because patients may present months to years after treatment, clinical symptoms are frequently nonspecific, and microbiologic confirmation is not always obtained.

Management of BCG-associated pseudoaneurysms remains controversial because of the rarity of the condition and the absence of standardized treatment guidelines. Both open surgical reconstruction and endovascular repair have been described, frequently in conjunction with prolonged antimycobacterial therapy [3]. We present a case of presumed BCG-associated multifocal paravisceral aortic and iliac pseudoaneurysms treated with multidisciplinary in situ laser fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair (L-FEVAR), highlighting the diagnostic challenges, imaging findings, and technical considerations of this complex intervention.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old male with a past medical history of nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) treated with intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) therapy, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, benign prostatic hyperplasia, and nephrolithiasis presented to the emergency department with severe bilateral flank pain and lower abdominal pain of 1 day’s duration. The pain was more pronounced on the left side and radiated from the left flank across the abdomen to the suprapubic region. Associated symptoms included gross hematuria, subjective fevers, chills, and rigors.

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated inflammatory markers concerning for an underlying infectious or inflammatory process. Given the patient’s prior history of bladder cancer and constitutional symptoms, recurrent malignancy, infectious lymphadenitis, and vascular pathology were considered.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated extensive necrotic para-aortic and left iliac chain lymphadenopathy with mass effect upon adjacent vascular structures. Dedicated CT angiography (CTA) revealed a 1.3 × 1.4 × 2.1 cm broad-based pararenal aortic pseudoaneurysm adjacent to the left renal artery (Fig. 1) and a second 0.9 × 1.3 × 1.8 cm pseudoaneurysm arising from the distal left common iliac artery (Fig. 2). Additional findings included complete encasement of the left renal artery by inflammatory tissue and compression of the left common iliac vein secondary to necrotic iliac chain lymphadenopathy. The combination of multifocal pseudoaneurysms, extensive periaortic inflammatory change, necrotic lymphadenopathy, and prior intravesical BCG exposure raised concern for an infectious vascular process. Given the paravisceral location of the lesions and risk of rupture, vascular surgery and interventional radiology were consulted for multidisciplinary evaluation.

Surgical intervention

The patient was deemed to be a poor operative candidate due to the suspected underlying infectious process. Four days after admission, interventional radiology and vascular surgery were consulted for possible stenting; a multidisciplinary plan for in situ laser fenestrated aortic endovascular repair (L-FEVAR) was scheduled. Under general anesthesia, ultrasound-guided bilateral common femoral artery access was obtained using a preclose technique. Initial abdominal aortography confirmed a para-renal aortic pseudoaneurysm adjacent to the left renal artery and a second pseudoaneurysm involving the distal left common iliac artery (Fig. 3). The celiac artery, superior mesenteric artery (SMA), and bilateral renal arteries were selectively catheterized and pre-stented to facilitate subsequent laser fenestration.Fig. 3Digital subtraction angiogram taken during laser fenestrated endovascular aortic repair (L-FEVAR) which shows the saccular suprarenal and left iliac pseudoaneurysms.Fig 3 – dummy alt text

A 14 × 120 mm Ovation iliac limb stent graft was first deployed to exclude the left common iliac pseudoaneurysm. An Endologix abdominal aortic endograft was then deployed, followed by placement of a Medtronic Valiant thoracic stent graft extending from the supraceliac aorta to the infrarenal abdominal aorta. Because preservation of visceral perfusion was required, in situ laser fenestration was subsequently performed using a Spectranetics 2.0 excimer laser system. Fenestrations were successfully created for the superior mesenteric artery and both renal arteries, after which covered Viabahn VBX stent grafts were deployed through the fenestrations into the target vessels. Renal ischemia time remained less than ten minutes.

Laser fenestration of the celiac artery was attempted but ultimately abandoned because of severe vessel angulation and inability to safely deploy a bridging covered stent. Further attempts were deferred because of increasing fluoroscopy and anesthesia times. Completion angiography demonstrated successful exclusion of the pararenal pseudoaneurysm, marked improvement of the left common iliac pseudoaneurysm, preserved perfusion of the superior mesenteric artery and bilateral renal arteries, and no evidence of endoleak (Fig. 4). Total fluoroscopy time was 114 minutes, and 360 mL of iodinated contrast was administered during the procedure. Rifampin-soaked grafts were utilized because of concern for a possible infectious etiology. The patient tolerated the procedure without immediate complications.

Postoperative recovery/biopsy

The patient did not have any perioperative complications and was placed on a multimodal pain control regimen. Coronal CT imaging postoperatively showed proper placement of the aortic stent graft (Fig. 5). Interventional radiology was consulted for a routine follow-up for a left para-aortic lymph node biopsy to rule out active malignancy. The sample taken was irregular, tan-white, and mainly necrotic, with minimal lymphoid tissue. Immunohistochemistry stains were conducted (AE1/AE3, CD68, CD45, and synaptophysin) and all results were nondiagnostic/inconclusive. Discussion

Etiology and demographics

BCG-associated vascular complications are rare but potentially life-threatening manifestations of intravesical BCG therapy. Proposed mechanisms include hematogenous dissemination of M. bovis with subsequent arterial wall infection, as well as chronic granulomatous inflammation resulting in progressive weakening of the vessel wall. The abdominal aorta is the most frequently reported site of involvement, although multifocal disease affecting multiple vascular territories has also been described. Because of the rarity of these complications, most available evidence is derived from individual case reports and small case series. Mycotic pseudoaneurysms typically arise as a complication due to a combination of factors, such as systemic infection and atherosclerosis. As for the route of infection, mycotic aneurysms can occur due to septic emboli, direct invasion, or systemic bacteremia. The most common pathogen for mycotic pseudoaneurysms is Staphylococcus aureus [2].

The populations that are most at risk include elderly men, median age 65, with atherosclerosis or immunosuppression [1]. This subtype of aneurysm is incredibly rare; the incidence is from 0.7% to 3% of all aortic aneurysms. Additional risk factors include smoking or diabetes mellitus. The most common arteries for infectious aneurysms are the femoral arteries and aorta [4]. BCG-induced mycotic aneurysms represent a rare subset of mycotic aneurysms. In this case, the abdominal aorta is the most common site of ballooning [5].

Diagnostic challenges and differential diagnoses

Diagnostic confirmation of BCG-associated vascular disease remains challenging. Unlike many previously reported cases, definitive microbiologic confirmation was not obtained in the present case. Blood cultures demonstrated growth of Staphylococcus hominis in 1 of 4 culture bottles, and para-aortic lymph node biopsy yielded predominantly necrotic tissue without identification of a causative organism. Consequently, the diagnosis should be interpreted as presumed rather than microbiologically confirmed BCG-associated disease.

Several alternative diagnoses were considered. Malignancy-associated vascular involvement was investigated because of the patient’s history of bladder cancer and extensive para-aortic and iliac lymphadenopathy. However, biopsy failed to demonstrate recurrent malignancy. Additional considerations included bacterial mycotic aneurysm, inflammatory aortitis, and penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer disease. The patient’s prior BCG exposure, constitutional symptoms, multifocal pseudoaneurysm formation, periaortic inflammatory changes, and absence of another clearly identified etiology collectively supported the working diagnosis of presumed BCG-associated vascular disease.

Clinical and imaging findings

The clinical presentation of BCG-associated vascular complications is often nonspecific and may delay diagnosis. Patients frequently present months to years following intravesical BCG therapy with constitutional symptoms including fever, chills, weight loss, or localized pain resulting from aneurysm expansion and mass effect upon adjacent structures. In the present case, the patient presented with severe bilateral flank pain, lower abdominal pain, hematuria, fevers, and chills, raising concern for an underlying infectious, inflammatory, or malignant process.

Cross-sectional imaging was critical in establishing the diagnosis and guiding treatment. Contrast-enhanced CT and CTA demonstrated multifocal vascular abnormalities consisting of broad-based pseudo-aneurysm outpouchings involving the suprarenal abdominal aorta and distal left common iliac artery. Unlike degenerative atherosclerotic aneurysms, which typically demonstrate fusiform dilation and gradual enlargement of the vessel lumen, these lesions appeared as focal saccular pseudoaneurysms associated with extensive surrounding inflammatory change and necrotic lymphadenopathy.

The suprarenal pseudoaneurysm was located immediately adjacent to the left renal artery and was associated with complete encasement of the vessel by inflammatory soft tissue. Extensive para-aortic lymphadenopathy produced mass effect upon adjacent vascular structures, while iliac chain lymphadenopathy resulted in compression of the left common iliac vein. The combination of multifocal pseudoaneurysm formation, periaortic inflammatory change, vascular encasement, and necrotic lymphadenopathy suggested a systemic infectious or inflammatory process rather than isolated degenerative aneurysmal disease.

Several imaging characteristics supported the diagnosis of a mycotic pseudoaneurysm. Previous studies have demonstrated that infected aneurysms frequently appear as saccular or multilobulated arterial outpouchings associated with perivascular soft tissue thickening, inflammatory stranding, adjacent lymphadenopathy, and rapid interval progression. In contrast, uncomplicated atherosclerotic aneurysms are typically fusiform, demonstrate less surrounding inflammatory change, and are not commonly associated with extensive necrotic lymphadenopathy. The imaging appearance in this case therefore favored an infectious or inflammatory etiology.

The radiologic differential diagnosis included bacterial mycotic aneurysm, inflammatory aortitis, penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer with pseudoaneurysm formation, and malignancy-associated vascular invasion. Malignancy was considered because of the patient’s history of urothelial carcinoma and the extensive para-aortic and iliac lymphadenopathy. However, the multifocal pseudoaneurysms, constitutional symptoms, periaortic inflammatory changes, and lack of evidence for recurrent malignancy on biopsy favored a presumed BCG-associated vascular complication.

Beyond diagnosis, CTA played a critical role in procedural planning. Detailed evaluation of the relationship between the paravisceral pseudoaneurysm and the celiac artery, superior mesenteric artery, and bilateral renal arteries demonstrated that conventional endovascular exclusion would compromise visceral perfusion. Preoperative imaging therefore directly informed the decision to pursue complex in situ laser fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair with preservation of critical branch vessels. This case highlights the essential role of CTA not only in detecting pseudoaneurysms, but also in characterizing branch vessel involvement, narrowing the differential diagnosis, and facilitating individualized endovascular treatment planning.

Treatment and prognosis

Although there are no treatment guidelines for BCG-induced mycotic (pseudo)aneurysms, literature shows that patients who are treated with a combination of surgical intervention and antibiotics have an increased survival rate and a decreased likelihood of rupture. The surgical interventions include FEVAR (fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair) or open repair, along with stent grafting to exclude and cut off blood supply to the pseudoaneurysms. In literature, it has been shown that FEVAR is associated with increased survival postsurgery compared to open repair, especially when focusing on abdominal aortic aneurysms [6]. For our patient, FEVAR with stent grafting was conducted. This was a complex procedure that required laser fenestration, deployable stents, and stent grafts. Since the pseudoaneurysm was also presumed to be BCG (M. bovis)-induced, antimycobacterium pharmacotherapy such as rifabutin, isoniazid, pyridoxine, and ethambutol were given.

Teaching point

While rare, BCG therapy related aortic mycotic pseudoaneurysms occur. If the patient is felt to be a poor operative candidate, a multidisciplinary approach may include in situ laser fenestration to exclude pseudo aneurysms that do not have ideal “seal zones.”
