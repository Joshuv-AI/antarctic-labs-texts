Musculoskeletal pain in adolescents: a systematic review of predictive and associated factors
Authors: Negar Yazdani, Maryam Shaygan, Sara Shojaei Zarghani, Mehrvash Hemati, Neda Jamlimoghadam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal (MSK) pain, especially low back pain (LBP), neck and shoulder pain is common among adolescents and imposes substantial health and economic burdens. Understanding predictive factors and factors associated with MSK pain is essential for identifying at-risk adolescents and designing targeted prevention strategies. This systematic review aimed to synthesize evidence on factors associated with or predictive of MSK pain in adolescents, with predictive inferences restricted to evidence from longitudinal studies.MethodsA systematic review was conducted, searching ProQuest, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Psych info for studies published up to May 2025. Eligible studies included prospective and retrospective cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, and case-control studies. All studies examined factors associated with MSK pain in adolescents. Cohort studies were prioritized for examining predictive inferences, whereas cross-sectional studies were presented as supportive associative evidence. Data were extracted on study characteristics, patient profiles, outcomes (prevalence, incidence, severity, disability), predictors and associated factors, and measures of association. Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). A narrative synthesis was performed due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcomes.ResultsOur database search resulted in 5,556 articles. Out of the 35 full texts that were screened, we included 19 studies (10 prospective cohort studies, 1 retrospective cohort study, 6 cross-sectional studies, and 2 mixed cross-sectional and longitudinal study) involving 45 to 57,408 participants. Predictors and factors associated with MSK pain included anthropometric factors (e.g., height growth, OR:1.32, 95%CI:1.06–1.65), lifestyle behaviors (e.g., smoking, OR:1.69–1.88), clinical conditions (e.g., inflammatory disorders, OR:1.23–1.45; central sensitization, OR:1.02,95%CI:1.01–1.03), socioeconomic factors (e.g., parental illness, OR 0.41–0.48), and pubertal status (OR:1.34–1.61). Motor tests (e.g., sit-up, Sorensen) showed limited predictive performance for low back pain (AUC ≤ 0.760). Cohort studies provided evidence of temporal associations, while cross-sectional studies provided supportive associative evidence.ConclusionsMSK pain in adolescents is associated with multiple factors, including smoking, inflammatory conditions, and central sensitization. Evidence from longitudinal studies supports some factors as predictive of future pain, whereas cross-sectional studies indicate associations. Lifestyle and psychological factors may contribute to risk, highlighting the potential value of targeted screening and interventions guided by longitudinal evidence. Future research should standardize outcome measures and investigate diverse populations, including low-income settings, to improve the generalizability of findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07056-2.Keywords: Musculoskeletal pain, Low back pain, Neck pain, Adolescents, Predictive factors, Associated factors, Systematic review

Introduction

Musculoskeletal (MSK) pain, particularly non-specific low back pain (LBP), neck pain, and shoulder pain, is highly prevalent among adolescents aged 10–19 years, with prevalence rates comparable to those reported in adults [1]. For example, Silva et al. [2] reported that 65.1% of adolescents experienced MSK pain, predominantly in the thoracolumbar spine (46.9%) and upper limbs (20%) [2]. MSK pain often affects multiple anatomical sites and represents a substantial public health concern, with considerable implications for individuals’ daily functioning, as well as their social and family contexts. Importantly, this phenomenon is not limited to adults — multisite MSK pain is also common in adolescents, with approximately one-third reporting pain in more than one body region [3]. Moreover, adolescents with multisite pain tend to have poorer health-related quality of life, worse sleep, and higher pain intensity and duration compared to their peers with single-site pain [4]. Critically, persistent multisite pain in adolescence has been associated with elevated psychological distress and anxiety, and may even predict mental health disorders in adulthood [5].

This condition significantly disrupts daily functioning, with up to 25% of affected adolescents reporting school absenteeism and reduced physical activity [1, 6]. These functional impairments are frequently accompanied by maladaptive thoughts, fear-avoidance behaviors, and even social withdrawal in adolescents with MSK pain [7].

Chronic MSK pain, lasting over three months, is associated with emotional distress, physical disability, low self-esteem, and reduced quality of life [8–10]. It may lead to short-term and long-term health issues in adulthood [11], and is closely linked to the well-being of their communities [12]. Over time, chronic pain may hinder adolescents’ ability to pursue personal ambitions, leading to frustration with unmet goals [13].

Numerous factors contribute to adolescent MSK pain, including lifestyle (e.g., heavy school bags, sports, excessive electronic device use, poor posture), physical activity levels, and psychological factors such as stress and depression [2, 14–16]. The biopsychosocial model highlights the role of psychological and social factors, such as peer-related stress and fear-avoidance behaviors, in pain development and persistence, particularly among girls [17, 18]. Since some studies suggest a link between adolescent MSK pain and chronic pain in adulthood, understanding predictors and factors associated with MSK pain is therefore essential [1, 19–21].

Previous studies have identified several factors associated with MSK pain, including female sex, depressive symptoms, poor sleep quality, and baseline pain [22–24]. For instance, Andreucci et al. reported that poor sleep quality, daytime fatigue, parental pain, and elevated internalizing/externalizing behaviors were significantly associated with the onset of neck, back, and limb pain in adolescents [25]. However, the complex interactions and associations of these factors with the presence or persistence of MSK pain remain poorly understood [1].

While prior systematic reviews, such as Huguet et al. [26] and Pourbordbari et al. [27], have examined specific risk and prognostic factors for MSK pain in adolescents, they only focused on cohort studies and searched different databases and included studies published up to 2016 and 2019, respectively [26, 27]. To our knowledge no review has comprehensively synthesized the full spectrum of predictors and factors associated with MSK pain, including anthropometric, lifestyle, clinical, socioeconomic, and early life factors. Unlike these previous reviews, our study synthesizes evidence from multiple study designs (cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control) and incorporates studies published up to 2025, thereby providing the most up-to-date and comprehensive overview of predictors and factors associated with adolescent’s MSK pain.

Given the long-term implications of adolescent MSK pain for physical and mental health in adulthood, a comprehensive synthesis is essential to inform early intervention strategies [11, 28].

This systematic review focuses on non-specific MSK pain in adolescents (10–19 years), synthesizing evidence on predictors and factors associated with MSK pain—including anthropometric, lifestyle, clinical, socioeconomic, and early-life factors. The aim is to provide a comprehensive understanding of variables linked to the presence, onset, or persistence of MSK pain to inform preventive strategies and clinical practice, and to reduce the risk of long-term adverse outcomes in adulthood. The review includes multiple study designs. Evidence from longitudinal cohort studies was considered to reflect potential predictive or temporal relationships, whereas findings from cross-sectional studies were interpreted as associative rather than predictive.

Materials and methods

Design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [29]. The review protocol was not registered in PROSPERO or any other registry; however, all methodological steps were predefined before commencing the review. The systematic review aimed to synthesize evidence on predictors and factors associated with MSK pain, including LBP, neck pain, and shoulder pain, in adolescents. Evidence from longitudinal studies was prioritized for temporal interpretation, while cross-sectional studies were strictly presented as associative.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they: (1) were published in peer-reviewed journals; (2) included adolescents (aged 10–19 years) [30]; (3) examined factors associated with or predictive of MSK pain (e.g., anthropometric, lifestyle, clinical, socioeconomic, or early life factors); and (4) reported outcomes related to MSK pain. Eligible study designs included prospective or retrospective cohort, cross-sectional, and case-control studies, although no case-control studies met the inclusion criteria. Cohort studies were considered as providing evidence on potential predictive or temporal relationships, whereas cross-sectional studies were interpreted as providing associative evidence only.

Studies were excluded if they did not focus on adolescents and investigated individuals with other pain (e.g., headache, abdominal pain). Studies that examined pain resulting invasive interventions (e.g., surgery, fracture) were also excluded as post-surgical or fracture-related pain has different underlying mechanisms and management approaches compared to non-traumatic MSK pain. Additionally, grey literature, dissertations, and unpublished data were not considered.

No language restrictions were applied during the database search; however, only studies published in English were ultimately included, as non-English articles were either unavailable in full text or could not be reliably assessed. Case reports, case series, conference proceedings, editorials, commentaries, letters to editors, and animal or cadaveric studies were also excluded.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in ProQuest, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and CINAHL from inception to April 9, 2025. No language restrictions were applied. In addition, a search was conducted directly on the websites of key pain-related journals, including The Journal of Pain, European Journal of Pain, and Pain to identify relevant studies published up to 2025. The electronic databases were searched from their inception to April 9, 2025, with no restrictions on publication year prior to the final search date. Additionally, Google Scholar was used as a supplementary search tool to identify relevant studies that may have been missed in the database and journal-specific searches. Specifically, reference lists of included articles were screened, and keyword searches were performed to capture additional studies published up to 2025. Searches were conducted using specific syntaxes tailored to each database. Search terms combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms for MSK pain (e.g., “musculoskeletal pain,” “low back pain,” “neck pain”, shoulder pain), predictors (e.g., “risk factors,” “predictors,” “lifestyle”), and population (e.g., “adolescents,” “children”). An example PubMed search string was: (((predict*[Title/Abstract]) OR (“predicting factor“[Title/Abstract])) AND (pain[Title/Abstract] OR musculoskeletal pain[Title/Abstract] OR MSK pain[Title/Abstract] OR low back pain[Title/Abstract] OR back pain[Title/Abstract] OR neck pain[Title/Abstract] OR shoulder pain[Title/Abstract])) AND (adolescen*[Title/Abstract] OR teen*[Title/Abstract] OR youth[Title/Abstract]). Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were hand-searched for additional studies.

The full search strategies, including Boolean operators and database-specific syntax, are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1 to ensure reproducibility.

Study selection

Search results were imported into EndNote X9 to remove duplicates. Two reviewers (N.Y., M.H.) independently screened titles/abstracts and full-texts against eligibility criteria, and a third reviewer resolved disagreement. A PRISMA 2020 flow diagram was constructed to illustrate the study selection process, including identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram for eligible study selection process. *PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, CINAHL

The article selection process for this systematic review is detailed as follows. Initially, 5,556 records were identified, of which 2,087 duplicates were removed, leaving 3,469 unique records. During title screening, 3,092 articles were excluded because their titles indicated they were not relevant to the research question (e.g., studies focused on adult populations, non-musculoskeletal pain, or unrelated topics). This resulted in 377 articles for abstract screening. In the abstract screening phase, a total of 342 articles were excluded: 210 due to insufficient relevance to the research question (e.g., studies addressing non-musculoskeletal conditions, general pain outcomes without MSK specification, or populations outside the adolescent age range), and 132 because they did not specifically focus on MSK pain in adolescents. In the full-text review phase, 16 articles were excluded due to irrelevance to the main research question (n = 5), ineligible age range (n = 3), methodological limitations (n = 4), or unavailability of the full text (n = 4). Methodological limitations were defined as studies with inappropriate study designs, inadequate sample sizes, lack of validated outcome measures, or insufficient statistical analyses. This left 19 articles that met all inclusion criteria. Each of these articles underwent a comprehensive risk of bias assessment, ensuring that the final selection was based on robust methodological standards and relevance to the topic of MSK pain in adolescents.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (N.Y., M.H.) independently extracted data using a standardized form. A piloted standardized extraction form was used to ensure consistency. Extracted data included: (1) study characteristics (design, country, sample size, setting, follow-up duration); (2) participant characteristics (age, sex, type of pain); (3) outcomes (MSK pain prevalence, incidence, severity, disability); (4) predictors (e.g., anthropometric, lifestyle, clinical); (5) measures of association (e.g., odds ratios [ORs], hazard ratios [HRs], confidence intervals); (6) measurement tools (e.g., questionnaire and physical exam); and (7) statistical methods (e.g., regression models, confounders adjusted). Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation (Tables 1 and 2). Data were standardized where necessary; missing summary statistics were handled by reporting available measures without imputation.

Outcome measures

To avoid any overlap between outcome assessment tools and instruments used to measure predictive factors, outcome measures were extracted and reported separately. For each included study, we documented the tools used to assess prevalence, incidence, or persistence of MSK pain (e.g., standardized pain-reporting questionnaires, clinical records), as well as secondary outcomes such as pain severity, disability, and health-related quality of life. Pain severity was commonly assessed using validated scales such as the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) or Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), disability with instruments such as the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) or the Functional Disability Inventory, and health-related quality of life using tools such as the SRS-22 questionnaire. These outcome measures were clearly differentiated from instruments used to assess predictive factors to ensure methodological transparency and prevent misclassification.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality and risk of bias of included studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists for cohort studies (11 items) and cross-sectional studies (8 items) [48]. The JBI tool was selected because it is specifically designed to appraise both cohort and cross-sectional studies. The cohort checklist evaluates participant selection, exposure measurement, confounder adjustment, outcome assessment, follow-up adequacy, and statistical analysis. The cross-sectional checklist assesses sampling, exposure and outcome measurement, confounder adjustment, and statistical methods. Each item was scored as “yes” (1 point), “no” (0 points), “unclear” (0 points), or “not applicable.” Total scores were calculated as percentages of applicable items (cohort: 0–11; cross-sectional: 0–8). Studies scoring ≥ 70% were classified as high quality, 50–69% as moderate quality, and ≤ 49% as low quality.

Two reviewers (M.H. and N.JM.) independently assessed methodological quality, and a third reviewer resolved any disagreements. Item-level judgments for each JBI domain are presented in Tables 3 and 4, allowing transparent evaluation of individual sources of bias across studies. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed qualitatively based on study design, methodological quality, and consistency of findings across studies, in line with established guidance for systematic reviews without meta-analysis. According to the JBI critical appraisal tools, most of the included studies met the predefined threshold for high methodological quality. Among the cohort studies, the majority achieved high methodological quality, while two studies were classified as moderate quality due to issues such as unclear outcome assessment, inadequate control of confounding, or incomplete follow-up.

In the cross-sectional studies, most studies met the majority of the JBI criteria; however, several items related to confounding were not applicable or not addressed in some studies. Overall, although the general methodological quality was acceptable, certain domains such as confounding adjustment, outcome measurement, and follow-up completeness showed potential sources of bias.

Design-specific sources of bias were observed across study types. In cohort studies, the main concerns were related to loss to follow-up, unclear outcome assessment, and insufficient reporting of whether outcomes were absent at baseline in some studies. In contrast, cross-sectional studies were more frequently limited by inadequate identification and adjustment of confounding variables, reflecting inherent constraints of the study design. These design-specific limitations were explicitly considered during interpretation of the findings.

The main potential sources of bias across the included studies were related to incomplete adjustment for confounding variables, unclear outcome definitions, and loss to follow-up in some cohort studies. In several studies, strategies to address confounding were either not reported or insufficiently described. Additionally, some cohort studies did not clearly report whether outcomes were absent at baseline or whether follow-up was complete. These issues may have influenced the strength and consistency of the reported associations.

A qualitative sensitivity consideration based on study quality was performed. Specifically, greater interpretive weight was given to cohort studies with clear outcome definitions and explicit adjustment for key confounders, while findings from moderate-quality studies were interpreted cautiously. Exclusion of moderate-quality studies did not change the overall direction of the evidence, supporting the robustness of the conclusions.

Formal statistical assessment of publication bias (e.g., funnel plots) was not performed due to heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcomes, and the small number of studies. Consequently, publication bias could not be formally assessed and may still be present, representing a limitation of this review (Tables 3 and 4).

Data synthesis

Due to the methodological heterogeneity of the included studies in terms of design (cohort and cross-sectional), outcome definitions, measurement tools, follow-up durations, and predictors examined, we could not perform a meta-analysis. Consequently, calculation of statistical heterogeneity (I²) and formal sensitivity analyses were not applicable, as these procedures require pooled effect sizes and variances across studies, which were not available [49, 50]. Formal statistical sensitivity analyses were not applicable due to absence of meta-analysis; qualitative sensitivity was considered based on study quality. This approach aligns with established guidance for systematic reviews without meta-analysis, ensuring that the synthesis remains transparent and methodologically appropriate. Findings were organized thematically by predictor categories (anthropometric, lifestyle, clinical, socioeconomic, early life) and pain types (LBP, neck pain, shoulder pain). Descriptive statistics summarized prevalence and incidence, and measures of association (e.g., ORs, HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported where available.

Results

Study selection

A total of 5,556 records were identified through database searches, of which 2,087 duplicates were removed, leaving 3,469 unique records. Following title and abstract screening, 3,434 articles were excluded due to irrelevance or ineligibility, resulting in 35 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Of these, 16 articles were excluded due to reasons such as irrelevance to the research question (n = 5), ineligible age range (n = 3), methodological limitations (n = 4), or unavailability of the full text (n = 4). Ultimately, 19 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Among the 19 included studies, 10 were prospective cohort, 1 retrospective cohort, 6 cross-sectional, and 2 mixed-design studies. Cohort studies were considered to provide temporal evidence allowing assessment of potential predictors for the onset or persistence of MSK pain, whereas cross-sectional studies were interpreted as providing associative evidence only. According to item-level JBI assessment (Tables 3 and 4), most cohort and cross-sectional studies met the predefined threshold for high methodological quality, although design-specific limitations were observed. Specifically, cohort studies generally demonstrated adequate exposure assessment and appropriate statistical analyses, but some showed limitations in outcome measurement, confounding adjustment, or follow-up completeness. Cross-sectional studies generally met criteria for sampling, exposure, and outcome measurement, but several did not identify or adjust for potential confounders. Item-level appraisal identified two moderate-quality studies [34, 43].

Outcome definitions, follow-up, and measurement instruments

Abbreviations: LBP Low Back Pain

Sample sizes varied widely, ranging from 45 to 57,408 participants. Most studies were conducted in high-income countries, including Finland (n = 3), Denmark (n = 4), Saudi Arabia (n = 2), Belgium (n = 2), Australia, Brazil, Israel, Japan, Netherlands, Portugal, and the United Kingdom (n = 1 each). Recruitment settings included schools (n = 9), clinical or orthopedic settings (n = 2), national registries or cohorts (n = 5), sports clubs or teams (n = 2), and general practices (n = 1).

Response rates, where reported, ranged from 49% to 84% at baseline, with follow-up response rates between 53.5% and 96.4%. Attrition rates varied considerably, from 5.7% to 59.4% in longitudinal studies, with higher attrition often linked to extended follow-up periods (up to 23 years). Follow-up durations in cohort studies ranged from 6 months to 23 years, while cross-sectional studies collected data at a single time point. Inclusion criteria typically focused on adolescents aged 10–19 years. Exclusion criteria commonly involved pre-existing spinal disorders, neurological conditions, or recent injuries.

Participant characteristics

Participants were predominantly adolescents, with mean ages ranging from 10.8 to 16.7 years at baseline. Three studies included young adults (up to 22 or 30 years) at follow-up. Sex distribution was generally balanced, though some studies reported higher proportions of males [43] or females [44]. The duration of MSK pain was often not explicitly quantified. Where reported, LBP was defined as acute (≤ 1 month) or persistent (≥ 8 days to > 30 days in the past year), and chronic neck pain required weekly pain for ≥ 3 months. Studies focusing on scoliosis [44] or spondylolysis [43] did not consistently report pain duration.

Outcomes

The included studies reported primary outcomes on MSK pain—including low back (n = 15), neck (n = 2), and shoulder pain (n = 1), and general MSK conditions (n = 1)—covering prevalence, incidence, persistence, severity, and disability (e.g., VAS, ODI, Functional Disability Inventory, SRS-22). While these descriptive outcomes provide context regarding the populations studied, the main focus of this review is on predictors and factors associated with MSK pain.

Prevalence

Across studies, LBP was the most commonly reported outcome [31–34, 39, 42, 44], followed by neck [34] and shoulder pain [45]. Prevalence data were used primarily to contextualize the study populations rather than to draw conclusions about predictive factors.

Incidence and persistence

Incidence data were limited. Poussa [40] reported new LBP cases among children previously free of pain [40], Mattila [38] observed 1.1% LBP hospitalization over 11.1 years [38] and 47% of adolescents with chronic neck pain had persistent symptoms at 6 months [34].

Severity and disability

Pain severity was measured in several studies. In AIS surgical candidates, 48% reported VAS scores ≥ 5 [42], while Alotaibi et al. [32] reported mean LBP severity of 4.61 ± 2.48 [32]. Disability was notable in scoliosis patients (50% ODI) [44], and adolescents with persistent neck pain had higher disability scores than those who recovered [34].

Factors associated with and predictive of MSK pain

Factors associated with and predictive of MSK pain were grouped and extracted separately from outcome measures to ensure conceptual clarity. These factors included anthropometric characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, clinical findings, socioeconomic status, pubertal status, motor development, psychological factors, sleep quality, and comorbidities. Instruments used to assess these predictors (e.g., psychological scales such as the Pain Catastrophizing Scale or Central Sensitization Inventory; clinical evaluations such as Adam’s forward bend test; imaging modalities; lifestyle questionnaires) were identified distinctly as predictor-assessment tools rather than outcome measures. This separation prevents any overlap with the instruments used to determine prevalence, incidence, or pain-related outcomes and aligns our reporting with the reviewer’s recommendation.

Anthropometric and physical factors

Associated factors

In AIS, older age at diagnosis (P = 0.014) and rigid lumbar curves (P = 0.036) correlated with higher VAS scores [42]. Multifidus muscle asymmetry, measured by ultrasound, was associated with LBP in adolescent males (p < 0.050) [47]. Motor fitness tests such as the one‑minute sit‑up and Sorensen tests showed low to modest predictive accuracy for LBP (AUC 0.62–0.66 in females; 0.56–0.57 in males). These AUC values were derived from ROC analyses without adjustment for confounders and no formal internal or external validation was conducted, indicating limited predictive performance and no current evidence to support clinical application [39].

Predictors

Body height and growth were significant predictors in some studies. Increased height growth from ages 11 to 14 predicted LBP (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.06–1.65) [40] and shorter stature at age 11 predicted the neck pain (OR 0.78, 95% CI 0.62–0.97) [41]. Functional tests like Active Straight Leg Raise (ASLR-ER) predicted absence of LBP in hockey players (OR 5.78, 95% CI 1.12–29.85) [31].

Lifestyle and behavioral factors

Associated factors

Global sleep quality and sedentary behavior were associated with the presence and severity of LBP [32].

Predictors

Lifestyle factors were frequently examined. Smoking was consistently associated with LBP, with ORs ranging from 1.69 (95% CI 1.36–2.11) to 1.88 (95% CI 1.32–2.69) for persistent LBP and incidence cases, respectively [35]. Hestbaek et al. [11] reported an OR of 0.74 (95% CI 0.58–0.94) for alcohol consumption in relation to persistent LBP, suggesting a counterintuitive protective association in this cohort of Danish adolescents [35]. Overweight (BMI > 24 kg/m²) had a modest association with LBP (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.06–1.79) in cross-sectional analysis but not longitudinally [35]. Physical activity showed no consistent association, though high acute/chronic workload ratios predicted shoulder pain in swimmers (OR 4.31, 95% CI 1.00–18.54) [45].

Clinical and comorbid conditions

Predictors

Chronic inflammatory conditions (e.g., respiratory, atopic) predicted impactful LBP longitudinally (OR 1.23–1.29) and cross-sectionally (OR 1.45) [33]. Comorbidities like headache (OR 2.12, 95% CI 1.62–2.79) and asthma (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.21–1.71) were associated with persistent LBP [11]. Sleep problems (HR 1.49, 95% CI 0.98–2.27) and psychological symptoms (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.14–1.70) increased the risk of MSK consultations [25]. Central sensitization symptoms strongly predicted neck pain persistence (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03) and disability (β = 0.41, p < 0.001) [34].

Socioeconomic and demographic factors

Associated factor

Socioeconomic status showed limited associations. Parental long-term illness was protective against persistent LBP (OR 0.41–0.48) [36].

Predictors

Male sex predicted early-stage spondylolysis (OR 4.053, 95% CI 1.056–15.554) but was protective for neck pain (OR 0.28, 95% CI 0.18–0.44) [41]. Female sex predicted persistent neck pain (OR 1.47, 95% CI 1.02–2.11) [34]. Pubertal status predicted back pain (OR 1.34–1.61) in American and Dutch adolescents [46].

Early life factors

Predictors

Early motor development (age at sitting/walking) showed weak associations with spinal pain at age 11, with walking age marginally linked to mid-back pain (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.92–1.00) [37]. Weekly health complaints in adolescence predicted LBP hospitalization (HR 1.5, 95% CI 1.2–1.9) [38].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 19 studies investigating predictors of non-specific MSK pain—including low back, neck, and shoulder pain—among adolescents. Across these studies, multiple factors were consistently associated with MSK pain, reflecting its multifactorial etiology. Anthropometric characteristics, such as rapid height growth and lumbar curve rigidity, were associated with the risk and severity of LBP. Lifestyle behaviors, including smoking, overweight, and high sport workloads, were linked to increased pain risk. Clinical features—such as localized pain and central sensitization—along with comorbidities like inflammatory conditions, headache, asthma, and psychological symptoms, contributed to pain persistence and disability. Socioeconomic and demographic factors, including parental health status and sex, as well as early life and pubertal factors, further modulated risk. Some studies also evaluated predictive models, reporting variable sensitivity and specificity (37.5–94.8% for motor tests; 63–89% for spondylolysis predictors) and moderate discrimination (AUC ≤ 0.76), highlighting the limitations of current predictive tools.

Importantly, these findings underscore the complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors in adolescent MSK pain and highlight the contribution of neurobiological mechanisms—including central sensitization, altered pain processing, and maladaptive pain beliefs—to pain persistence and severity. This evidence can inform the development of targeted prevention and intervention strategies in adolescents and guide future longitudinal studies aimed at elucidating the causal pathways underlying MSK pain.

Interpretation of key findings

Anthropometric factors, including increased height growth during early adolescence [40], and lumbar curve rigidity in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) [42] were consistently associated with LBP. During adolescence, periods of rapid skeletal growth can create biomechanical imbalances, as elongation of long bones may outpace concurrent growth of muscles and tendons. This asynchronous development increases tensile forces at growth plates and apophyseal attachment sites, making them more susceptible to traction injuries and pain — a mechanism observed in apophyseal conditions such as Osgood–Schlatter disease and other osteochondrosis in adolescents [51]. Conversely, shorter stature at age 11 predicted neck pain [41] possibly reflecting differences in posture or muscle development. These findings align with prior research indicating that growth spurts during puberty increase vulnerability to spinal pain due to MSK imbalances [52]. This association may be particularly pronounced in AIS, where structural deformities exacerbate mechanical stress, as evidenced by the high LBP prevalence (71%) in this population [42]. It is important to note that most of the included studies focused on LBP, whereas substantially fewer studies investigated neck or shoulder pain. This imbalance reflects the current distribution of available evidence rather than a limitation of our search strategy, and highlights a clear gap in the literature where further research on predictors of neck and shoulder pain in adolescents is urgently needed.

Recent evidence indicates that biomechanical and postural alterations alone do not reliably explain MSK pain, particularly in chronic presentations. Instead, biopsychosocial contributors—such as psychological distress, maladaptive pain beliefs, and central sensitization—play a more substantial role in the persistence of pain. This aligns with recent umbrella-review findings demonstrating that chronic MSK pain is best understood through a biopsychosocial and neurobiological framework rather than purely mechanical mechanisms [53]. Emerging mechanistic evidence suggests that neuroinflammation — involving activation of glial cells and release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‑α and IL‑1β — enhances neuronal excitability and promotes synaptic plasticity in central pain pathways. These processes underlie central sensitization, a state of heightened pain sensitivity and persistence even without ongoing tissue damage, plausibly linking systemic inflammation to chronic MSK pain in adolescents [54].

Lifestyle factors, particularly smoking, were associated with increased risk of LBP in adolescents [35, 38]. While some mechanisms may include vascular or biomechanical effects, psychosocial factors such as stress, peer influence, and coping strategies can mediate this relationship. Moreover, these lifestyle and psychosocial influences may interact with neurobiological mechanisms, including central sensitization and altered pain processing, thereby increasing vulnerability to persistent MSK pain [53]. Overweight status showed a modest association with LBP [35], consistent with biomechanical theories of increased spinal load, though longitudinal effects were less pronounced, possibly due to changes in body composition over time. The inverse association between alcohol consumption and persistent LBP reported by Hestbaek et al. [11] in the Danish adolescent cohort should be interpreted with caution [35]. In this study, persistent LBP was defined as self-reported back pain at follow-up. The counter-intuitive finding is likely influenced by residual confounding from lifestyle, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors, including low frequency of alcohol use, physical activity, diet, and peer behaviors. Cultural and contextual factors in the cohort may also have contributed to the observed association. Similar protective effects of alcohol on adolescent MSK pain have not been consistently observed in other cohorts, and no biological mechanism supports a protective role. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of any clinical or preventive benefit, and highlights the importance of cautious interpretation of isolated observational results. However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously, as most studies relied on self-reported data, had varying follow-up durations, and included cross-sectional designs, which may limit causal inference and affect the generalizability of lifestyle-related findings.

Clinical and comorbid conditions were significant predictors. Chronic inflammatory conditions, such as respiratory and atopic disorders, increased the risk of impactful LBP [33], potentially through systemic inflammation or shared pain pathways [55]. Clinical characteristics, including localized pain and central sensitization, alongside comorbid conditions such as psychological distress and sleep problems, were consistently linked to pain persistence and disability in adolescents. These findings highlight that neurological mechanisms, including central sensitization, interact with psychosocial factors to drive MSK pain, supporting a biopsychosocial model rather than a purely mechanical explanation [27, 32, 53]. Andias et al. [44] suggests that neurological mechanisms may play a critical role in pain chronicity, highlighting the need for integrated pain management strategies that address both physical and psychological components. However, many of these studies were observational and relied on self-reported measures, limiting causal inference and introducing possible reporting biases. The high prevalence of LBP in AIS [42] and the association of side-located pain with early-stage spondylolysis [43] highlight the importance of clinical examination and imaging in specific populations.

Socioeconomic status and demographic factors influence MSK pain in adolescents via complex biopsychosocial and behavioral pathways rather than purely mechanical mechanisms [56]. For example, lower socioeconomic status may exacerbate risk through increased psychosocial stress, limited access to supportive environments, or reduced health literacy, while higher socioeconomic status may offer protective resources [57]. Gender differences are also evident: many studies report greater prevalence and persistence of MSK pain in female adolescents compared with males, suggesting that social, psychological, and possibly neurobiological factors play a significant role in pain experience beyond anatomical vulnerability [58]. These findings should be interpreted with caution due to limited representation of diverse populations and potential residual confounding in the included studies.

Pubertal status predicted back pain in both American and Dutch adolescents [46] likely due to hormonal and MSK changes during puberty. These findings suggest that sex-specific interventions may be warranted, particularly for conditions like spondylolysis, where male predominance was evident.

Early life factors, such as motor development milestones, showed weak associations with spinal pain [37], suggesting that infancy markers may have limited predictive value compared to adolescent exposures. However, weekly health complaints in adolescence predicted LBP hospitalization [38], indicating that general health status may serve as a proxy for pain vulnerability. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of early identification of adolescents at risk for MSK pain. Predictors such as rapid growth, lumbar curve rigidity, lifestyle behaviors, and psychosocial factors can guide risk stratification and tailored interventions. Integrating physical and psychological components in management may improve outcomes and reduce progression to chronic pain.

Implications for practice and policy

The multifactorial nature of MSK pain predictors underscores the need for comprehensive screening and prevention strategies in adolescents. Clinicians may consider performing targeted physical examinations (e.g., hyperextension tests, pain localization) as part of a comprehensive assessment in adolescents with acute LBP to screen for conditions such as spondylolysis; however, this recommendation is based on limited evidence from a single study [43] and requires further confirmation in larger cohorts. School-based interventions targeting lifestyle factors, such as smoking cessation programs and physical activity promotion, could reduce LBP risk, particularly given the consistent association with smoking [35]. Additionally, incorporating posture education and ergonomic training in school curricula could mitigate the impact of rapid growth and biomechanical stress during puberty. The strong link between psychological symptoms, sleep problems, and MSK pain [25, 34] suggests that mental health support and sleep hygiene education should be integrated into adolescent healthcare.

The high prevalence of LBP in AIS [42] and scoliosis [44] highlights the importance of early scoliosis screening in schools, coupled with pain management strategies for surgical candidates. Public health policies should address socioeconomic disparities, as parental health status influenced LBP outcomes [36], potentially through access to healthcare or health literacy. Policies promoting equitable access to diagnostic tools, such as imaging for scoliosis, could improve early detection and management in underserved populations. The modest predictive accuracy of motor tests [39] and functional tests [31] as reported by De Visscher et al. [34], suggests that current tools may need refinement to enhance their utility in clinical settings. Emerging technologies, such as wearable sensors for real-time monitoring of posture and physical activity, could enhance the precision of these tools.

Strengths of the review

This systematic review comprehensively evaluates predictors and associated factors of adolescent MSK pain, covering anthropometric, lifestyle, clinical, socioeconomic, and early life factors within a biopsychosocial framework. The inclusion of 19 studies from diverse settings (schools, clinics, national cohorts, sports clubs) enhances generalizability. Adherence to PRISMA guidelines and standardized data extraction ensures methodological rigor. The focus on understudied factors, such as central sensitization [34] and socioeconomic influences [36], provides novel insights for clinical practice and future research. Importantly, the synthesis was informed by an item-level assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias, and the interpretation of findings accounted for variability in study design and confounding control across included studies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, substantial heterogeneity in study designs (prospective, retrospective, and cross-sectional) and outcome measures prevented meta-analysis and limited the ability to quantitatively synthesize predictors. This heterogeneity also reduced the generalizability of findings and may have limited the ability to fully elucidate underlying neurobiological mechanisms underlying MSK pain, including central sensitization and altered pain processing. In addition, six studies were cross-sectional, which precludes temporal or predictive inferences; therefore, their findings were interpreted as associations rather than predictors.

High attrition rates in some longitudinal studies may have introduced selection bias, potentially underestimating or overestimating associations. Although most studies met the predefined JBI threshold for high methodological quality, item-level appraisal identified important limitations such as incomplete adjustment for confounders, unclear outcome definitions, and loss to follow-up, which may have affected the strength of the evidence. Many outcomes and exposures were self-reported, introducing potential recall and reporting bias, and several studies used retrospective or cross-sectional designs, limiting causal inference. Measurement approaches also varied, including inconsistent use of standardized tools and lack of objective measures or external validation for predictive models.

Residual confounding is possible, as some studies did not adjust for key psychosocial or socioeconomic variables. Publication bias could not be formally assessed due to the heterogeneity of study designs, populations, and outcomes, and may still be present. Furthermore, most studies were conducted in high-income countries, which may limit generalizability to low- and middle-income settings. The modest predictive performance of existing models (AUC ≤ 0.76) also suggests that current predictors do not fully capture the complexity of adolescent MSK pain.

Finally, the review protocol was not registered in PROSPERO. However, predefined eligibility criteria, independent dual screening and data extraction, and consensus procedures were implemented to minimize potential bias and enhance methodological rigor.

Future research directions

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs with low attrition to clarify causality and long-term MSK pain trajectories. Standardizing pain definitions and outcomes will improve comparability and meta-analyses. Using objective measures, such as wearable sensors or biomarkers, can enhance predictive accuracy and identify at-risk adolescents early.

Future studies should further investigate psychosocial factors such as stress and depression, and include populations from low- and middle-income countries to address global disparities. Developing predictive models with higher accuracy (e.g., AUC > 0.80) and testing interventions targeting modifiable factors—such as smoking, sleep quality, and psychological health—are essential. Randomized controlled trials of school-based interventions (e.g., mindfulness or physical therapy) could provide scalable preventive solutions.

Conclusion

This systematic review indicates that MSK pain in adolescents is associated with multiple interacting factors, including anthropometric, lifestyle, clinical, socioeconomic, and early-life variables. Some factors, such as smoking, inflammatory conditions, and central sensitization, showed relatively consistent associations with MSK pain across studies, although most studies did not develop or validate formal prediction models. The modest predictive performance of existing models highlights that these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The current evidence may help inform early screening, lifestyle interventions, and mental health considerations; however, further high-quality longitudinal research using standardized measures, validated predictive models, and diverse populations is required to better guide prevention and management strategies for adolescent MSK pain.
