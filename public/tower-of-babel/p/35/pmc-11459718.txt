Examining multidimensional health poverty and determining factors among rural women of childbearing age in ningxia, china
Authors: Kexin Chen, Qi Hu, Ning Xu, Ximin Ma, Jiahui He, Wenlong Wang, Jiancai Du, Jiangwei Qiu, Yongxin Xie, Hui Qiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe multidimensional health poverty afflicting rural women of reproductive age bears profound implications for the sustainable development of families, societies, and healthy villages. Elucidating vulnerable groups at risk of multidimensional health poverty and delineating its determinants can inform refinements and implementation of health-poverty alleviation policies.MethodsBased on the 2022 "Health Status and Health Service Utilization" survey data in rural Ningxia, China, this study analyzes the dimension of health status, capacity for health service utilization, health expenditure and security using the A-F dual-threshold method, logit model, and probit model. It comprehensively evaluates multidimensional health poverty and analyzes its determining factors among rural women of childbearing age.ResultsWith k set at 0.3, the health multidimensional poverty index, incidence, and intensity among rural women of reproductive age were 0.021, 0.053, and 0.392, respectively. Chronic illness, capacity for free gynecological examination utilization, health examination utilization, and borrowing due to illness contributed significantly to multidimensional health poverty. Risk factors for multidimensional health poverty among rural women of childbearing age include age, unemployment, family size, and lack of sanitary toilets. Protective factors include being married, educational level, non-farm or student occupation, receiving government support, separate housing and kitchen, owning a television and computer.ConclusionsEven with the elimination of absolute poverty, the multidimensional health poverty situation among rural women remains important from a gender perspective. It is recommended to strengthen economic assistance and health service support for these groups, improve the level of rural health services from a gender perspective, and enhance housing structure and sanitation toilet usage in rural areas. Efforts should be made to narrow the digital divide in rural areas and promote digital health education. Pre-interventions on multidimensional health poverty can provide new pathways for the development, empowerment, and well-being of rural women of childbearing age.Keywords: Childbearing-age Women, Rural, Multidimensional health poverty, Measurement, Influence factors

Introduction

Poverty continues to be one of the world's most pressing economic challenges, attracting significant attention from all sectors of society. As a result, poverty alleviation has become a central goal for countries worldwide. In 2020, China achieved a historic milestone by eliminating absolute poverty in rural areas according to current standards. However, relative poverty is expected to persist in the long term and will remain a key focus for ongoing poverty reduction efforts [1]. As a perennial social issue intrinsic to economic and social development, relative poverty alleviation requires systematic efforts across the economic, political, cultural, social, ecological, and health spheres [2]. Studies reveal that ill health is a primary driver of impoverishment in rural areas [3]. Between 2017 and 2019, the proportion of rural poor households falling into or returning to poverty due to illness reached alarming highs of 41.7%, 40.7%, and 38.4% respectively [4]. The privation precipitated by poor health remains a pressing concern of health equity, especially for economically disadvantaged populations [5]. Their lack of access to healthcare and unfavorable health conditions engender susceptibility to debility and destitution [6]. Relative impoverishment precipitated by affliction arises when malady inflicts economic loss, or when poor health depletes one's capacity for labor [7]. This engenders burdensome expenditures alongside heightened vulnerability. This underscores the need to advance health justice by extending care and buttressing wellbeing among marginalized communities, for whom vulnerability all too often proves the harbinger of hardship [8]. Health poverty emerges as the cardinal trait and catalyst of poverty among rural populations, both presently and in the foreseeable future [9]. Health poverty refers to the state of vulnerability where individuals and families fall into or revert to poverty due to illness [9].

The feminization of agriculture, combined with the rural exodus of young adults, has made women the backbone of the labor force in rural households. This shift has made addressing female health deprivation crucial for both familial and societal progress [10]. While reproductive-aged women in China have witnessed notable improvements in health service utilization over the past decade, those dwelling in rural areas continue to experience suboptimal health service utilization, a phenomenon particularly perceptible across western rural regions [11, 12]. Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, situated in northwest China with its southern mountainous counties among the first recognized as profoundly deprived by the State Council, had a substantial proportion of its population mired in poverty before comprehensive poverty elimination efforts, with disease a primary driver of such destitution [13]. With underdeveloped rural economies and deficient healthcare infrastructure, rural women of childbearing age, laden with twin burdens of agricultural and domestic labor, are besieged by tremendous health pressures and unfulfilled needs, rendering them vulnerable to more pervasive health impoverishment [14]. Thus, urgent heed must be accorded to health poverty amongst women of reproductive age, optimizing pro-health anti-poverty strategies to propel health equity in China and cement gains in poverty alleviation.

Multidimensional Health Poverty refers to a complex condition where individuals experience poverty due to various interconnected dimensions of health and well-being. Multidimensional health poverty refers to a condition where individuals experience economic hardship and diminished quality of life due to health-related issues. It encompasses two main aspects: the economic losses incurred from illness, which include direct medical expenses and indirect costs such as lost income; and the reduction in labor capacity resulting from poor health, leading to further financial strain and increased health risks. This concept highlights the interplay between economic disadvantage and health status, illustrating how health problems can exacerbate poverty and impact overall well-being [15]. In retrospective appraisals, most scholars approach health poverty from two dimensions. Firstly, impoverishment precipitated by surging medical expenditures, typically gauged via catastrophic (CHE) or impoverishing health expenditures (IHE), overlooking populations with lower income and poorer health unaffected by CHE or IHE [16, 17]. Secondly, health poverty conceptualized as lack of personal health capital and inadequate health capability, grounded in human capital and capability theories. The multidimensional essence of health poverty necessitates measurement and governance to encompass health status, service utilization, expenditures, and protection, transcending the confines of income or health spending [18]. This holistic consideration can effectively identify populations genuinely experiencing health impoverishment. However, there is a lack of relevant research on multidimensional health poverty among rural women of childbearing age.

Effectively identifying populations experiencing genuine health poverty and determining key contributing factors is a significant research priority and challenge. Using data from the 2022 Ningxia Rural Residents Health Survey, this study constructs a quantitative framework to identify multidimensional health poverty among rural women of reproductive age. The A-F double boundary method is employed to measure and decompose multivariate health poverty, with logistic and probit regression used for comparative analysis to pinpoint key determinants. The findings will accurately identify high-risk groups and assess the scale of health poverty, informing targeted strategies to address multidimensional health deprivation. This research provides essential theoretical and empirical foundations for managing health poverty and advancing gender equity in the post-poverty alleviation era.

Methods

Data source and sampling method

The data were sourced from the 2022 Ningxia Rural Residents Health Status and Healthcare Utilization Survey. The respondents were rural residents across Haiyuan, Yanchi, Xiji, and Pengyang counties in Ningxia. Multistage stratified cluster random sampling was implemented, surveying all townships in each sample county. Firstly, all townships in each sample county were enumerated and their villages stratified into high, medium and low levels by economic development. 40% of natural villages were then randomly selected from each stratum as sample villages. Applying systematic sampling, 33 households were chosen from the roster of each sample village. In this study, we selected 15–49 years old rural women of childbearing age who lived in the area for more than one year as the research object. A total of 8262 rural women of childbearing age were sampled in this survey. Among these, 62 individuals were unable to complete the survey due to relocation or employment away from their residence. Additionally, 43 individuals were excluded due to extreme or invalid variable values. After rigorous data cleaning, the final analytical sample comprised 8,157 rural women of childbearing age. The survey encompassed key determinants including individual characteristics, health status, healthcare utilization, familial and social support, and environmental attributes. Questionnaires were administered by well-trained teachers and graduate students.

Multidimensional healthy poverty measurement

In multidimensional poverty research, the dual cutoff method proposed by Alkire and Foster in 2011 has been widely adopted for identifying impoverished populations [19–21]. We apply the A-F double cutoff approach to measure and analyze multidimensional health poverty among rural women of reproductive age. The A-F double cutoff method encompasses four steps—determining measurement parameters, identifying multidimensional health poverty, aggregating multidimensional health poverty, and decomposing multidimensional health poverty [19, 22].

Measuring parameters determination

Key parameters for the multidimensional health poverty index include deprivation cutoffs for individual indicators, dimension weights, and the multidimensional health poverty cut-off k. Deprivation cutoffs were established by comprehensively referencing validated results from previous empirical studies, practical experience, and data availability. An indicator system encompassing the three core dimensions of "health status—ability to utilize health services—health expenditures and security" was constructed to measure the multidimensional health poverty index or rural women of childbearing age. Subsequently, equal weights were assigned to each dimension, following the universal standard for multidimensional poverty measurement. Although potentially obscuring the relative importance of different dimensions regarding overall health poverty conditions, this minimally impacts overall health poverty trends, while ensuring index comparability [23, 24]. This breaks inherent cognitive frameworks of solely measuring health poverty through economic poverty or physiological health from a single dimension, and measures health poverty from a multidimensional equal perspective of inherent mechanisms [22]. Multidimensional health poverty identification

Let N be the number of rural women of childbearing age samples, assessed across D indicators of multidimensional health poverty. Let YN×D denote the matrix formed by all sample values, and let yij∈YN×D denote the value of rural resident i on dimension j (i = 1, 2, L, N; j = 1, 2, L, D). First, set a poverty standard for each dimension Zj. If yijZj, then rural resident i is poor in dimension j; Otherwise, there is no poverty. To facilitate the calculation, make gij0=1,yij<zj0,other, So the sample matrix g0=[gij0] can be transformed into a deprivation matrix. In addition, the deprivation matrix g0 can be compensated by the normalized gap deprivation matrix g0=[gij1], gij1=gij0zj-yijzj. Secondly, the total deprivation score of rural residents i in all dimensions was calculated, ci=∑j-1Dwjgijα. Finally, a critical value k is set to determine whether rural residents are multidimensional healthy poor. If cj≥k, the rural resident is in multidimensional healthy poverty; Otherwise, it is not multidimensional healthy poverty. Further, let ci(k)=ci,ci≥k0,other, the deprivation matrix g0 can then be transformed into a censoring deprivation matrix gα(k)=[gijα(k)] and gijα(k)=gijαci(k).

Calculation of the multidimensional health poverty index

On the bedrock of identifying multidimensional health poverty among rural denizens, we have erected a composite index that appraises this very phenomenon. The calculated indicators include the following:

Incidence of multidimensional health poverty (H): Represents the rate of multidimensional healthy poverty in the population. Calculation formula is H=∑i=1Nci(k)/N=q/N. Where, q is the number of people with multidimensional healthy poverty and N is the total sample number.

Multidimensional Health Poverty Index (MHPI): Also known as the adjusted incidence of multidimensional health poverty, its formula is MHP0=HA=g0(k)/q=∑j=1D∑i=1Ngij0(k)/N, Where A is the average missing share, and indicates the depth of multidimensional health poverty, also known as the poverty occurrence intensity index.

Calculation of the contribution rates of dimensions and indicators of the MHPI

The MHPI decomposition formula for this study is as follows: Let MHPαj be the multidimensional health poverty contribution rate of dimension j, then MHPαj=(qjα×wj)/N. qjα is the multidimensional poverty incidence of dimension j in the case of multidimensional poverty, and wj is the weight of each index. Then the contribution of dimension j to the multidimensional poverty index wj is φjα=MHPαj/MHPα=(qjα×wj)/(N×MHPα)α=0,1,2.

Modeling and variable selection of determinants of multidimensional health poverty

Logistic and probit models were designed to meticulously examine the determinants of multidimensional health poverty among rural women of reproductive age in Ningxia. While logistic models emphasize whether the outcome variable occurs given changes in explanatory variables, probit regressions focus on how the probability of a positive outcome changes with explanatory variables, providing more precise probability distributions [25]. Using both models synergistically leverages their strengths. Employing logistic and probit models jointly enhances result robustness and provides more comprehensive analytical perspectives to uncover distinct information [26]. Measurement of multidimensional health poverty

Figure 1 reports the the multidimensional health poverty index, incidence rate, and average missing share of multidimensional health poverty for different values of k (ranging from 0.1 to 0.6). The results showed that as the value of k increased from 0.1 to 0.6, both the multidimensional health poverty index and incidence rate decreased continuously, while the depth of multidimensional health poverty increased. When k was greater than 0.2, the incidence rate of multidimensional health poverty significantly decreased. For k = 0.3, the incidence rate of multidimensional health poverty was 0.053. When k = 0.6, both the multidimensional health poverty index and incidence rate were 0.000. The larger the value of deprivation parameter k, the more indicators considered in evaluating multidimensional health poverty. However, this failed to capture the differences among residents in multidimensional health poverty [18]. Therefore, we selected a value of k = 0.3 as the deprivation threshold for identifying multidimensional health poverty in the subsequent analysis.

The analysis of factors influencing single-dimensional health poverty shows that the risk factors for health poverty include age (OR = 0.1083), unemployment (OR = 2.866), housing year (OR = 1.020), and lack of sanitary toilets (OR = 1.503). Protective factors for health poverty include primary school, middle school, and high school education levels (OR = 0.670, 0.374, 0.260), non-agricultural occupation, and student status (OR = 0.560, 0.396), receiving government support (OR = 0.632), and owning a mobile phone (OR = 0.597). The risk factors for health service utilization poverty include primary school education (OR = 1.188), receiving government support (OR = 1.170), family size (OR = 1.110), and per capita housing area (OR = 1.004). Protective factors for health service utilization poverty include age (OR = 0.984), married status (OR = 0.845), having a childbirth history within the past five years (OR = 0.720), and owning a mobile phone (OR = 0.764). The risk factors for health expenditure and security poverty include non-agricultural occupation (OR = 1.393) and family size (OR = 1.101). Protective factors for health expenditure and security poverty include age (OR = 0.986), education level of middle school and above (OR = 0.661, 0.630), student status (OR = 0.646), receiving government support (OR = 0.774), separation of housing and kitchen (OR = 0.680), and owning a television and computer (OR = 0.567, 0.701).

The regression robustness of the results regarding the factors influencing the multidimensional health poverty of rural women of childbearing age is examined by constructing a Probit regression model. Table 6 presents the model results, which indicate that in the Probit model, the regression coefficients still confirm that the independent variables age, unemployment, household size, and non-sanitary toilets at home have a significant negative impact on multidimensional health poverty. Moreover, being married, having primary, middle, or high school education, non-agricultural worker or student, receiving government support, and having separate housing and kitchen, as well as having a television and mobile phone at home, have a significant positive impact on multidimensional health poverty. The direction of the results for the remaining variables is consistent with the original model, indicating a reasonably robust regression result.

Discussion and conclusion

This study comprehensively assesses the multidimensional health poverty of childbearing-aged women in rural Ningxia, China, from three dimensions: health status, capacity for healthcare service utilization, and health expenditure and protection. Furthermore, at the regional level, we conduct a contribution rate decomposition analysis of the multidimensional health poverty of childbearing-aged women in the region to explore important contributing factors. At the individual level, logistic regression models are employed to explore the key factors of multidimensional health poverty among rural childbearing-aged women.

Even with the elimination of absolute poverty, the health poverty situation among rural women remains dire from a gender perspective. At k = 0.3, research found that the multidimensional health poverty index, incidence, and intensity among rural women of childbearing age in Ningxia were 0.021, 0.053, and 0.392, respectively. Some rural women of childbearing age still face multidimensional health poverty, with a relatively high risk of falling into or returning to poverty due to illness. Notably, utilization of the national free cervical and breast cancer screening services remains low among women of reproductive age. This may be related to their lack of awareness of gynecological health risks and unwillingness to actively undergo gynecological examinations [27]. Additionally, traditional attitudes in some rural and ethnic minority areas view gynecological checks as private matters, reducing their willingness to utilize relevant services [28, 29]. Strengthening rural women's cognition and capability to utilize health services for the prevention and treatment of gynecological diseases will help mitigate multidimensional health poverty risks. Furthermore, our research reveals that income and chronic disease remain a key risk factor for multidimensional health poverty, related policies should increase support for the treatment and aid of chronic diseases among rural women. This could both alleviate economic stress from illness-related borrowing and mitigate the negative impacts of diseases on their working capacity, thereby helping improve health poverty.

Our research reveals that older age and unemployment are associated with an increased risk of multidimensional health poverty among rural women of childbearing age. Higher education levels offer a protective effect on health status, health expenditures, and security. Notably, married women and those who have given birth in the past five years demonstrate better health service utilization capabilities. This may be attributed to the health education received during childbirth and a heightened awareness of the importance of maintaining good health. Additionally, China's policies prioritizing maternal and child health services likely enabled better access to medical resources for parous women, reducing their health poverty risks [30]. Those with higher education levels also tend to have greater health knowledge and ability to actively acquire medical resources [31, 32]. Overall, improving rural women's education and family planning services will likely strengthen their capabilities for health service utilization, spending, and security, thereby alleviating multidimensional health poverty.

Our research findings indicate that an increasing household population raises the multidimensional health poverty risk for women of childbearing age in rural areas. It also has a negative impact on their ability to utilize health services and incur health expenditures. This may be due to the fact that with a larger household population, women of childbearing age bear heavier responsibilities in terms of child-rearing and elderly care, which reduces the time they have available to take care of their own health [33]. At the same time, the disposable income is shared among more people, resulting in a reduced budget for health expenditures for women of childbearing age [33]. We found that rural women of childbearing age who have already received relevant government support face lower risks of multidimensional health poverty and risks of health poverty in specific dimensions. This may be because the vulnerable groups that are included in the government support program find it easier to obtain medical expense subsidies and medical services [3]. This suggests that the government should continue to optimize the effectiveness of existing poverty alleviation funds and increase economic assistance and health service support for vulnerable groups of rural women of childbearing age, which will help further alleviate the current situation of multidimensional health poverty.

Regarding the housing environment characteristics of rural women of childbearing age, we found that having a separate bedroom and kitchen and using a sanitary toilet in the household have a mitigating effect on multidimensional health poverty. Previous studies have demonstrated that the absence of a separate kitchen confers over twice the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes [34]. Moreover, non-independent kitchens lacking ventilation exacerbate indoor air pollution, heightening risks of respiratory illnesses in women [35]. Some prior studies have also found that women are more vulnerable to the adverse health effects of unsanitary toilets and public hygiene facilities [36]. The improvement of rural living conditions has a positive impact on health. Notably, reforms related to rural toilets can significantly reduce the medical and healthcare expenses of rural residents [37]. Therefore, continuing to enhance the structure of rural housing and promoting the use of sanitary toilets are crucial measures to create a healthier living environment for rural women of childbearing age and alleviate multidimensional health poverty.

Regarding the characteristics of the digital divide among rural women of childbearing age, our findings indicate that the use of televisions and computers alleviates multidimensional health poverty. This may be due to these devices facilitating access to health education information, enhancing knowledge about health, and helping women understand government public health service policies [38]. Such access improves their ability to obtain medical resources and utilize telemedicine services conveniently [31]. However, it is essential to consider the broader social realities that contribute to the gender gap in digital literacy and access. Many rural women face systemic barriers, such as limited education and economic opportunities, which can hinder their ability to effectively use digital devices. Studies have suggested that individuals in health poverty can generate endogenous strength through increased confidence and digital literacy, potentially helping them escape the health poverty trap [39]. Moreover, digital device usage can reduce social isolation among rural women, allowing them to receive more social support, which positively impacts their mental health [40]. Acquiring skills related to digital devices can expand job opportunities for rural women and enhance their capacity to invest in their health. However, addressing the digital divide requires a multifaceted approach that considers these social barriers. Strategies should not only focus on providing technology but also on improving educational opportunities and economic conditions for women [41].

To improve digital health literacy among these women, targeted training programs should be implemented, focusing on practical skills such as using smartphones and accessing online health resources. Additionally, community-based workshops that integrate digital training with health education can empower women to confidently navigate health information and services online [42]. By bridging the digital divide, we can empower rural women of childbearing age, enhance their access to health resources, and ultimately alleviate multidimensional health poverty. Therefore, it is crucial to develop targeted interventions that consider the specific social realities faced by these women, promoting both digital health education and broader socio-economic empowerment.

Strengths and limitations

Our research has conducted a comprehensive assessment and analysis of the determinants of multidimensional health poverty among rural childbearing women by integrating health status, health service utilization capacity, health expenditure, and security into a multidimensional health poverty identification index. This study provides reference points for further studying the challenges of multidimensional health poverty among rural childbearing women and interventions. Although this study is of great significance, there are also limitations. The types of health poverty among rural childbearing women are complex, and despite taking into account the characteristics of rural childbearing women, the dimensions and contents of the indicator system need further supplementation and demonstration due to limitations such as sample data and other relevant objective factors.

Moreover, this study does not fully account for other potential influencing factors, such as changes in community resources and healthcare policies, which may also impact health poverty. Future research should address these gaps by incorporating a broader range of variables and employing longitudinal designs to better establish causal relationships. Finally, integrating qualitative research methods could provide a richer and more nuanced understanding of multidimensional health poverty among rural women of childbearing age. This approach can help uncover underlying factors and contextual subtleties that may not be fully captured by quantitative methods. These efforts will help overcome the current limitations and offer more comprehensive guidance for decision-making and strategies aimed at alleviating health poverty.
