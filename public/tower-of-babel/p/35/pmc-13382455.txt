High-throughput phenotypic screening identifies novel antimalarial scaffolds and target-associated chemotypes
Authors: Japheth Kibet Ng'etich, Normalita Eka Pravitasari, Takeshi Ishikawa, Naoya Kadofusa, Ayato Sato, Shinjiro Hamano, Takaya Sakura, Daniel Ken Inaoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The emergence and spread of artemisinin-resistant Plasmodium falciparum threaten recent progress in malaria control and highlight the urgent need for antimalarial agents with new mechanisms of action. Here, we performed a high-throughput phenotypic screening of the Nagoya Chemical Library, comprising 36,160 compounds. This yielded 1391 (3.8%) primary hits, further reduced by cytotoxicity profiling. Differential screening against wild-type 3D7 and transgenic 3D7-yDHODH parasites expressing yeast dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) was done to identify mitochondrial electron transport chain inhibitors. This approach identified a compound with modest activity against parasite DHODH, confirmed by enzymatic assays with recombinant PfDHODH. A subset of 441 potent compounds with half-maximal concentration (EC50) < 6.5 μM was selected and profiled against the Dd2-derived multidrug-resistant panel. This analysis identified compounds likely targeting key parasite pathways, including phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase (PfPI4K) and P-type ATPase 4 (PfATP4). Chemical clustering of a selected subset of 350 compounds (<10 μM) revealed novel scaffolds with antiplasmodial potency that bear no structural resemblance to known antimalarial compounds. Collectively, these findings highlight novel chemotypes associated with validated antiplasmodial targets (PfDHODH, PfPI4K, and PfATP4) and uncover previously uncharacterized scaffolds with unknown targets. These compounds provide a foundation for further investigation of their mechanisms of action and optimization as potential antimalarial agents.

Introduction

Despite being both preventable and treatable, malaria remains a global public health burden. The World Health Organization (WHO) reported at least 282 million cases in 2024, representing an increase of 9 million from 2023. Resistance of Plasmodium falciparum to the frontline drug artemisinin and its partner drugs has been implicated in this increase (WHO Malaria World malaria report, 2025). Moreover, insecticide resistance in anopheline vectors, together with behavioral adaptations that increase the frequency of infectious bites, has contributed to sustained malaria transmission (Hitz et al., 2021). To make matters worse, the recent discovery in the sub-Saharan region of Anopheles stephensi (Taylor et al., 2024), a malaria vector species endemic in South Asia and the Persian Gulf, further undermines malaria control efforts in a region that already bears the heaviest burden of the disease (WHO Malaria World malaria report, 2025).

In keeping with the WHO goal for malaria elimination and the speed with which the parasite develops resistance to existing medicines, the need for new drugs with new targets and mechanisms of action (MoA) cannot be overstated (Bansal et al., 2024). The Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV), in collaboration with academic and industry partners, established target candidate profiles (TCPs) and target product profiles (TPPs) to guide antimalarial drug discovery and development efforts. Among these, TCP1 focuses on the identification of new small-molecule antimalarials with emphasis on the rapid clearance of the asexual stage parasitemia and robust activity against drug-resistant strains, thereby addressing the ongoing and evolving threat of drug resistance (Paonessa et al., 2022; Burrows et al., 2017).

For this reason, at least 5 million compounds have been phenotypically screened in the last decade against P. falciparum, yielding over 25,000 hits at low to sub-micromolar concentrations (Bopp et al., 2023). Phenotypic screening remains a powerful approach for the discovery of first-in-class antimalarial compounds (Dechering et al., 2022), particularly when compared with target-based strategies. Indeed, most currently used antimalarial drugs and many clinical-stage candidates originated from phenotypic discovery efforts, including natural product screening and large-scale high-throughput screening (HTS) campaigns. Landmark examples include artemisinin, discovered through phenotypic screening of natural products (Miller and Su, 2011), as well as more recent HTS-derived clinical candidates such as Cipargamin (KAE609) (Diagana, 2015), and Ganaplacide (KAF156) (Siqueira-Neto et al., 2023), underscoring the sustained impact of phenotypic screening on the antimalarial drug pipeline.

Although the complete sequence of the Plasmodium genome has enabled the identification of numerous essential parasite proteins as potential drug targets (Agamah et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018), the lack of high-resolution three-dimensional structures for many of these proteins continues to hinder structure-based drug design (Godinez-Macias et al., 2025). This challenge arises from the size and complexity of parasite proteins (Pizzi and Frontali, 2001) and from difficulties in recombinant expression due to the extreme AT richness and codon-usage bias of the parasite genome (Weber, 1987). Collectively, these factors continue to constrain the broad application of target-based drug discovery approaches in malaria research and delay the identification of novel targets for antimalarial drug discovery.

While concerns have been raised that large-scale HTS campaigns may preferentially rediscover compounds acting on previously validated targets, the continued clinical success of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) highlights the importance of continually replenishing the antimalarial pipeline with new chemical entities, regardless of prior target validation (Godinez-Macias et al., 2025). Furthermore, the latest clinical success of Ganaplacide/Lumefantrine (GanLum; KLU156), the first non-ACT therapy to reach clinical development, marks a major milestone in malaria chemotherapy (Novartis website, accessed in 2025). The present study sought to expand the chemical space of antimalarial compounds by phenotypically screening the Nagoya Chemical Library (NCL) against P. falciparum. The NCL comprises a diverse set of compounds, including commercially available molecules, in-house synthesized compounds, and scaffolds derived from natural products. The library was prepared to maximize chemical diversity by incorporating a broad range of chemical scaffolds and structural classes, thereby increasing the likelihood of identifying novel bioactive chemotypes. We report a set of new antimalarial chemotypes with no prior antimalarial reports, as well as candidates targeting the P. falciparum sodium efflux pump PfATP4, the phosphatidylinositol-4-OH kinase (PfPI4K), and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) identified via testing against the asexual blood stage (ABS) of the parasite.

Materials and methods

Primary screening

The NCL containing 36,160 compounds was provided as 2.5 mM stocks (100 nL in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in 384-well plates (#781086, Black Polystyrene CELLSTAR; Greiner Bio-One, Tokyo, Japan). The primary screening campaign against the asexual blood stage (ABS) of the P. falciparum 3D7 strain was performed at a single dose of 10 μM. We utilized a fluorescence-based P. falciparum lactate dehydrogenase (PfLDH) system that measures parasite viability by coupling the reduction of nitrobenzyl-umbelliferone 1 (NCOU1) by the Escherichia coli nitroreductase (NTR) enzyme to yield a highly emissive fluorophore, umbelliferone (Sakura et al., 2024). The parasite was cultured using standard procedures (Trager and Jensen, 1976). Briefly, the parasite was maintained in RPMI 1640 containing 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), supplemented with 0.5% (w/v) Albumax II (Gibco, Nihonbashi, Japan) and 2% human erythrocytes to achieve approximately 3% parasitemia. Ring-stage-synchronized parasites were prepared in 5% (w/v) sorbitol. Plates containing 100 nL of test compound per well were pulsed with 25 μL of parasite at 0.3% parasitemia and 1% hematocrit using a Multidrop Combi machine with a small metal cassette (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Nihonbashi, Japan). An equal concentration of DMSO was used as a negative control, while a cocktail of atovaquone and artemisinin at a fixed concentration of 1 μM was used as a positive control. In addition, an 8-point serial dilution of atovaquone was used as a reference in duplicate on every plate. Plates were then incubated for 72 h at 37°C in sealed flasks with a 90% N2, 5% CO2, 5% O2 gas mixture. The antiplasmodial activity of a compound was then determined using the NTR-NCOU1 assay (Sakura et al., 2024). A reaction mixture consisting of PfLDH buffer (150 mM lactate, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and 0.25% Triton X-100 supplemented with 100 μM Acetylpyridine-adenine Dinucleotide (APAD+), 100 μM NCOU1, 10 μg/mL NTR, and 0.015% (v/v) KM-70 defoaming agent (Shin-Etsu, Japan) was dispensed into the assay plates at 35 μL/well and incubated for 20 min. The fluorescence of umbelliferone was then measured (λex/em = 360/465 nm) using a PHERAstar Plus microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany). The percentage growth inhibition of all compounds was calculated as described earlier (Xu et al., 2013).

Cytotoxicity against the HepG2 cell line

A cell-counting kit (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was used to assess the cytotoxic profile as previously described (Bayih et al., 2016), with minimal modifications. Briefly, HepG2 cells (ATCC, Manassas, USA) were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Invitrogen, New York, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma, Tokyo, Japan), 4 mM L-glutamine (Wako, Osaka, Japan), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic (Wako) in a standard tissue culture incubator (37°C; 5% CO2; PHCbi, Tokyo, Japan). Cells were harvested at over 70% confluence using trypsin and diluted to a cell density of 1.1 × 105 cells/mL. Subsequently, 90 μL of cell culture was seeded into 96-well plates (IWAKI tissue-treated Microplate) using the Multidrop Combi machine with a standard metal cassette (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The compounds were tested at a single final concentration of 10 μM. DMSO was used as a negative control, while DMEM was used as a blank. Plates were then incubated for 72 h in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Thereafter, 10 μL of cell-counting reagent (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was added, and plates were incubated for an additional 3 h. Absorbance was then recorded using the Spectramax plate reader (Molecular Devices, California, USA) at 450 nm.

Hit confirmation and identification of compounds targeting the mitochondrial electron transport chain (MtETC)

Hit compounds that passed cytotoxicity triage were further evaluated in a dose-dependent manner against P. falciparum 3D7 (wild type) and 3D7-yDHODH parasite line. This step was performed not only to confirm the antiplasmodial activity of selected compounds but also to identify compounds with known mechanisms of action for prioritization within the screening cascade while distinguishing compounds that may act through novel targets or pathways The 3D7-yDHODH parasite expresses a cytosolic yeast DHODH that utilizes fumarate as an electron acceptor, bypassing the parasite's endogenous ubiquinone-dependent DHODH and reducing its dependence on the mitochondrial electron transport chain (MtETC) for pyrimidine biosynthesis. Consequently, this transgenic line can survive exposure to compounds targeting the MtETC. Since the MtETC contains validated antimalarial targets, including DHODH and cytochrome bc1, comparison of compound activity between 3D7 and 3D7-yDHODH parasites provides a useful approach for identifying compounds with potential MtETC-dependent mechanisms of action (Komatsuya et al., 2022). A total of 896 hit compounds exhibiting >80% cell viability against HepG2 cells were tested in 8-point dilutions from 40 μM to 0.02 μM against 3D7 and 3D7-yDHODH in duplicate. Parasite culture and assay conditions were as previously described for primary screening.

Enzyme inhibition assay against recombinant plasmodial DHODH (PfDHODH)

The MtETC comprises two validated targets for antimalarial drug development: DHODH and the cytochrome bc1 complex. Compounds that show efficacy against 3D7 but diminished activity against the 3D7-yDHODH strain could target either the DHODH or cytochrome bc1 protein of the MtETC (Supplementary Fig. 2). To determine the specific target of the selected compound, the PfDHODH recombinant protein was expressed in E. coli, purified, and assayed as described previously (Hartuti et al., 2021). Seven compounds showing over 100-fold shift in EC50 between the 3D7 parent strain and 3D7-yDHODH strain were considered to target the MtETC and were assessed kinetically against the recombinant PfDHODH.

Assessment of hit compounds against a panel of resistant Dd2 parasites

To determine the MoA of selected hits apart from the MtETC, a panel of resistant parasites carrying gene mutations in validated plasmodial target proteins was used. To obtain a sufficient shift in EC50 values to determine the MoA, hit compounds were organized into different test groups based on EC50 values obtained from the dose-response assay against the 3D7 strain. Compounds were then assayed against the Dd2-resistant panel alongside the Dd2 wild-type strain using the NTR assay method, as described earlier (Sakura et al., 2024). Initially, mutant parasites were screened against the respective tool compounds to confirm the expected shift in EC50, which serves as a yardstick to confirm that the compound is likely targeting the protein affected by the point mutation carried by the mutant parasite (Supplementary File 1). Assays were completed in 384-well plates, as described before. The shift in EC50 between wild-type and resistant parasite strains was determined compared to the tool compounds.

Chemical clustering

Chemotype clustering was performed in collaboration with the Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV). Compounds were clustered and prioritized according to the MMV in-house multiparameter optimization (MPO) framework (Dechering et al., 2022). Briefly, this framework selects compounds with balanced drug-like properties based on physicochemical and pharmacokinetic criteria, including the absence of structural alerts, logP/logD values between 2 and 4, ≤3 hydrogen-bond donors, ≤8 hydrogen-bond acceptors, molecular weights of 200-400 Da, and ≤10 rotatable bonds. The 350 hit compounds were clustered using a Tanimoto similarity threshold of 0.8 in combination with the average linkage method to define distances between clusters. Compounds were subsequently prioritized based on chemical diversity and physicochemical properties. Moreover, MolPrint2D fingerprints and Tanimoto similarity indices were used to compare NCL hit compounds against the MMV Public Dataset of compounds previously reported to exhibit antiplasmodial activity (https://www.mmv.org/mmv-open/malaria-box/malaria-box-supporting-information). This comparison generated a similarity matrix with values ranging from 0 to 1.

Molecular docking

We obtained the predicted protein structure files of PfATP4 and PfPI4K from the AlphaFold2 database (A0A143ZZK9 and Q8I406, respectively). The protein structures were processed before docking using the AutoDock Tools (Morris et al., 2008) (Olson Laboratory of the Scripps Research Institute, California, USA) by the addition of polar hydrogen atoms, calculation of specific net charges, and generation of PDBQT files. The search space for docking calculations was set to cover the entire protein. Docking calculations were performed using a cubic search space of 22.5 × 22.5 × 22.5 Å centered on Gly385 for PfATP4, and a cubic search space of 30.0 × 30.0 × 30.0 Å centered on S1320 and Y1356 for PfPI4K. All docking calculations were performed using AutoDock Vina (Trott and Olson, 2010), which has been reported to provide a reliable estimation of binding affinity (scoring power) (Wang et al., 2025). Two-dimensional structure data of compounds in the NCL were obtained in SDF format and converted into 3D structural data using Open Babel (Louisiana State University, New Orleans, US) (https://openbabel.org/index.html). Structural data were then converted to Protein Data Bank, Partial Charge (Q), & Atom Type (T) format (PDBQT) using AutoDock Tools.

Data analysis

Data analysis and visualization, including correlation and density plots and hit rate calculations, were performed in R (version 4.4.3). The inhibition curves and EC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression analysis by fitting data to the log (inhibitor) versus response equation Y = Bottom + (Top − Bottom)/(1 + 10(X−LogIC50)) in GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.3, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, California). EC50 shifts were calculated as the ratio of the EC50 values obtained against the respective parasite strains. Molecular docking structure visualization and alignments were performed using PyMOL software version 3.0.1 (Schrödinger).

Results

Phenotypic screening cascade and hit identification

The NCL, comprising 36,160 compounds, was screened against the asexual blood stage of P. falciparum 3D7 using a high-throughput 384-well assay format (Fig. 1). We used a screening system recently developed in our laboratory, which couples the PfLDH to NTR, leading to the reduction of nitrobenzyl umbelliferone probe, yielding a highly fluorescent product. (Sakura et al., 2024). Excellent assay performance was observed, with a mean Z’ factor of 0.80 ± 0.06 across 226 screened plates (all with Z factor >0.5; Fig. 2a), together with robust signal-to-background (S/B) and signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios (Supplementary Fig. 1a and b), confirming the fidelity of the assay for hit selection. Furthermore, duplicate data across separate plates showed a high linear correlation, with an R2 of 0.8 (Fig. 2b). The primary screening dataset exhibited an approximately normal distribution, with a slight deviation in the hit compounds. Hits were defined using an inhibition cut-off ≥68.7%, corresponding to three standard deviations above the mean (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 1c). Consequently, 1391 compounds were selected, corresponding to a 3.8% hit rate. Dose-response assay for identification of compounds targeting the mitochondrial electron transport chain (MtETC)

The activity of selected compounds was confirmed in a dose-dependent manner at 8-point serial dilutions against 3D7 and 3D7-yDHODH parasites, to identify inhibitors of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (MtETC). The plasmodial MtETC contains well-validated targets for antimalarial drug discovery, including DHODH and cytochrome bc1 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The efficacy of selected hits was confirmed by EC50 values ranging from sub-micromolar to micromolar. We considered compounds that exhibited >100-fold shifts in EC50 between the two strains to potentially target the MtETC (Fig. 2d). This threshold was adopted as a pragmatic prioritization criterion based on our previous screening experience, in which compounds exhibiting smaller EC50 shifts rarely inhibited recombinant PfDHODH in follow-up biochemical assays. We recognize, however, that a small EC50 shift does not exclude activity against other components of the MtETC, including cytochrome bc1 (Pfcytb), and therefore such compounds may still warrant further investigation despite not being prioritized in the present study. Consequently, seven compounds meeting this criterion were selected. To identify the target protein in the MtETC, the PfDHODH recombinant protein was purified and tested against these seven selected compounds. Of the seven, only one, compound 1, demonstrated modest activity, inhibiting the recombinant enzyme with an IC50 of 4.6 μM (Supplementary Fig. 3a and b). The remaining compounds did not exhibit any inhibition activity against the protein, suggesting a different target in the parasite MtETC, for example, the cytochrome bc1 (Supplementary Fig. 2). A subset of 441 compounds with confirmed antiplasmodial activity against 3D7 (EC50 < 6.5 μM; hit rate, 1.2%) was prioritized for follow-up assays, consistent with the 1% hit-rate requirement for compound structure disclosure by the supplier (Fig. 1).

Resistance profiling using mutant parasite lines reveals compounds linked to PfATP4 and PfPI4K

Given the broad range of potencies among the 441 selected hits, compounds were first stratified by EC50 values from the efficacy confirmation assay. This stratification enabled detection of EC50 shifts between the mutant parasite strain and the parental line, facilitating target inference through resistance profiling. For each resistant strain, the corresponding tool compound against which the strain exhibits established resistance was included as a control (Supplementary Fig. 3c), providing a benchmark for assessing whether the test compounds shared a similar or distinct MoA. While most hit compounds showed no significant EC50 shift between resistant and parental lines, a subset exhibited reduced potency against specific resistant strains, suggesting potential targeting of PfPI4K (compound 5) and PfATP4 (compounds 2, 3, and 4). Chemical clustering reveals novel antiplasmodial chemical scaffolds

. Clustering of the 350 NCL compound set yielded 43 clusters comprising 174 compounds and 176 singletons (Fig. 3 and Supplementary File 1). Several clusters corresponded to established antimalarial scaffolds, confirming the robustness of our screening pipeline and validating the successful first application of our laboratory-developed NTR assay for antiplasmodial screening (Sakura et al., 2024). The analysis also revealed previously unreported chemotypes with antiplasmodial activity, including compounds exhibiting sub-micromolar potency. Among these, clusters 4, 71, and 155 represent scaffolds that, to the best of our knowledge, have not been previously associated with antiplasmodial activity. Cluster 4, the largest cluster identified, comprised 35 compounds sharing a common core scaffold. Structural variations involving three-to four-membered ring substitutions appear to modulate potency, resulting in activities spanning sub-micromolar to micromolar ranges (Fig. 3). Cluster 71 exhibited a similar substitution-dependent potency trend. However, the compounds were only modestly potent, suggesting this scaffold as a promising starting point for further optimization. Cluster 155, composed of three compounds, was not only potent but also met the MMV molecular-weight threshold for hit prioritization, providing a suitable scaffold with potential to improve potency into the nanomolar range. Comparison of the NCL library against the MMV database of approximately 19,000 reported antiplasmodial hits revealed that 17 singleton compounds shared >80% structural similarity with previously reported MMV compounds, whereas only two compounds were exact matches (Supplementary File 1). Cluster 176 includes compound 5 (C176a) (Fig. 3), which showed a fivefold EC50 shift against the Dd2_048 line, suggesting PfPI4K as a potential target. However, according to MMV internal prioritization criteria, this cluster consists of lipophilic polybasic compounds, a chemotype commonly associated with inhibition of β-hemozoin formation and a chloroquine-like mechanism of action. Activity in this class often correlates with the pKa of basic centers, which can complicate drug metabolism and pharmacokinetic (DMPK) optimization due to poor membrane permeability (Kjellin et al., 2014). Consequently, this chemotype is typically deprioritized by MMV despite its frequent identification in phenotypic malaria screening campaigns (Siqueira-Neto et al., 2023). Consistent with this observation, compounds 2, 3, and 4, predicted to target PfATP4 (Fig. 4a–d), and compound 5 (Fig. 5a–c), predicted to target PfPI4K, were identified as singletons. Compound 2 is structurally related to a scaffold in the MMV database (MMV1061666) and shows similarity to kinesin Eg5 inhibitors based on SciFinder analysis. However, the scaffold contains several metabolic liabilities, including ether groups and a thioether moiety. Compound 3 presents additional concerns, including nitro and thioether functionalities, two chiral centers, and a strongly basic center (predicted pKa about 8), with similar liabilities observed for compound 5.

Molecular docking of hits linked to PfATP4 and PfPI4K reveals putative binding conformations within the active sites

Compounds that exhibited a shift in EC50 relative to the parental strains, of a magnitude comparable to that observed with the tool compounds used to generate the resistant parasites, were subjected to molecular docking against the corresponding protein structures encoded by the resistance-conferring genes. We identified compounds displaying substantial EC50 shifts consistent with potential targeting of the corresponding resistance-associated proteins, PfATP4 and PfPI4K. In the absence of parasite crystal structures for PfATP4 and PfPI4K, AlphaFold-predicted models were used to predict the binding conformations of the selected compounds within their respective binding sites. The PfATP4 protein is a validated target, with two frontrunner inhibitors, Cipargamin and (+)-SJ733, having undergone extensive testing in humans. PfATP4 is responsible for maintaining low levels of Na+ by exporting it from the parasite cytosol, whilst importing H+ equivalents. Inhibitors of PfATP4 cause a spike in Na+ levels within the parasite cytosol, eventually leading to parasite elimination (Spillman et al., 2013). The G358S mutation in PfATP4 has been widely reported to decrease the sensitivity of ATP4 to inhibition by Cipargamin and (+)-SJ733, hence protecting parasites from disruption of Na+ regulation. This mutation has been reported to cause steric clashes between the serine side chain in the putative Na+-active site against Cipargamin and (+)-SJ733, being the mechanistic mode of parasite resistance to the two inhibitors (Qiu et al., 2022). Using the AlphaFold-predicted structure, we defined the docking search space around this region. Compounds that showed markedly reduced activity against the Dd2_DHIQ strain (except compound 4), docked within 2.5 Å of the G358 residue (Fig. 4), and exhibited interactions with amino acid residues located within the putative Na + binding site. Compound 2 formed mostly hydrophobic interactions with residues at the binding site. The G358 residue is 2.9 Å away from the binding site (Fig. 4a). Compound 3 has a unique extended binding conformation due to its structure, forming a strong hydrogen bond with residue P408 (2.1 Å) and weaker polar bonds with N207 (3.3 Å) and Q172 (3.5 Å) while also forming many hydrophobic interactions with many residues in the putative binding site (Fig. 4b). Compound 4 primarily forms hydrophobic interactions within the binding site, adopting a somewhat perpendicular conformation relative to the protein helices (Fig. 4c). Although these docking conformations are predictive, the AlphaFold model of PfATP4 aligns well with the recently reported cryo-EM structure (Haile et al., 2025) of PfATP4, supporting its suitability for structure-based studies (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Furthermore, superposition of all docked conformations of PfATP4-targeting compounds, including Cipargamin, reveals consistent binding within the active site (Fig. 4d), thereby providing additional validation of the docking protocol.

On the other hand, PfPI4K catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylinositol to phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate (PI4P), which is important for signal transduction, membrane trafficking, and cytokinesis during blood -stage development (McNamara et al., 2013). KDU691 adopts a similar orientation within the binding pocket and forms weaker hydrogen-bond interactions (yellow dashed lines) with Tyr1342 and Val1357, contributing to its anchoring within the active site. Compound 5 binds within the predicted active site of PfPI4K, forming hydrogen bonds with Ser1362 and Ser1365, alongside multiple hydrophobic interactions within the putative ATP-binding pocket (Fig. 5a and b). Structural superposition with KDU691 reveals strong alignment, particularly across the central heteroaromatic core, suggesting a conserved binding orientation. Like KDU691, Compound 5 adopts an extended conformation within the binding cavity, projecting into adjacent pockets of the PfPI4K ATP-binding site and engaging additional residues. Such an extended binding mode has been associated with increased susceptibility to resistance-conferring mutations in PfPI4K inhibitors (Dziwornu et al., 2025). To further assess structural conservation, the docked conformations of Compound 5 and KDU691 were superposed onto the human orthologous X-ray structure (PDB ID: 8Q6F) of PI4KIIIβ co-crystallized with a reference inhibitor (orange, Supplementary Fig. 4b). The central aromatic scaffolds of Compound 5 and KDU691 align consistently with the human inhibitor-bound structure, supporting conservation of the ATP-binding architecture. However, the HuPI4KIIIβ inhibitor adopts a slightly distinct overall conformation compared to both KDU691 and Compound 5, underscoring potential structural determinants of parasite selectivity. Residues previously reported to undergo resistance-associated point mutations are highlighted as orange sticks. KDU691 forms hydrogen-bond interactions with Tyr1342 and Val1357, contributing to anchoring within the active site. This hinge interaction with Val1357 is atypical for ATP inhibitors, as it mimics the canonical hydrogen bonds formed by the adenine moiety of ATP with the kinase hinge region (McNamara et al., 2013). Notably, residues Tyr1356 and Tyr1357, known to confer resistance when mutated, are positioned proximal to the ligand-binding region, further supporting the validity of the docking protocol and the biological relevance of the predicted binding mode.

Discussion

The continued emergence and spread of parasite resistance to frontline antimalarial drugs underscore the urgent need to discover new antimalarial chemotypes for the drug development pipeline (Casas et al., 2025). Phenotypic screening remains the most widely used approach in antimalarial drug discovery and has led to the identification of most antimalarial drugs currently in clinical use (Siqueira-Neto et al., 2023). Guided by the MMV prioritization strategy, we identified four novel scaffolds from the NCL library that, to our knowledge, have not previously been reported to exhibit antiplasmodial activity. For example, cluster 155 comprises small, compact compounds with low molecular weights (on average about 270 Da), favorable calculated lipophilic ligand efficiency (LLE; LLE = pIC50- logP/D), and no evidence of cross-resistance. These observations suggest that Cluster 155 represents a promising starting point for further chemical optimization to improve potency and efficacy. Similarly, although Cluster 51 contains compounds with an average molecular weight of approximately 450 Da, slightly above the MMV selection criterion of 400 Da, the cluster shows strong potential for further SAR optimization. For analogues with scaffolds similar to those in the MMV database, our assay showed retained activity comparable to their reference scaffolds, providing useful insights for SAR. However, several of these compounds, structurally related to known antimalarial chemotypes in the MMV portfolio, did not align with their previously reported targets. For example, compounds in cluster 96 resemble scaffolds previously associated with PfATP4 inhibition (Ashton et al., 2023); however, no shift in EC50 was observed against the PfATP4-resistant Dd2_DHIQ line carrying the G358S mutation. While this finding does not exclude PfATP4 as the target, it suggests that these compounds may interact differently with PfATP4 and therefore be susceptible to alternative resistance mutations or may act through a distinct molecular target. Similarly, compound 1 (Supplementary Fig. 3b and C29a in cluster 29) exhibited moderate inhibition of recombinant PfDHODH, whereas a structurally related compound C29b (Fig. 3) showed no comparable inhibitory activity, indicating that compound 1 may interact with an additional biological target. We have also identified new scaffolds 2, 3, and 4 that may target PfATP4, whereas Compound 5 may primarily target PfPI4K, with possible additional activity against PfDHODH, which warrants further investigation. These targets, PfATP4, PfPI4K, and PfDHODH, have been intensely studied, and reports of inhibitor resistance exist both in vitro and in the field (Mandt et al., 2019; Qiu et al., 2022). However, since future antimalarial drugs will be administered in combinations to impede parasite resistance (Daily and Parikh, 2025), continuous addition of molecules to the drug discovery pipeline in these targets is warranted. The identification of scaffolds not previously classified as antiplasmodial in the NCL, together with known antimalarial scaffolds bearing functional group substitutions, expands the chemical space of potential antimalarial agents and provides promising starting points for further SAR optimization and mechanism-of-action studies. Most clusters and singletons overlapping with the MMV database correspond to compounds from the GlaxoSmithKline Tres Cantos Antimalarial Compound Set (TCAMS) or the Novartis GNF dataset.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Target assignment was primarily inferred from resistance-shift profiling and in silico studies, and therefore requires orthogonal biochemical or genetic validation. In addition, in vivo efficacy, pharmacokinetic characterization, and broader selectivity assessments were beyond the scope of the present work. Addressing these points will be important in future lead-optimization studies. Nonetheless, the chemical and biological profiling data generated here provide a valuable foundation for future target deconvolution and medicinal chemistry optimization.

Conclusion

The rise and spread of artemisinin-resistant malaria underscores the urgent need for accelerated drug discovery efforts aimed at identifying new chemotypes with novel mechanisms of action. Phenotypic screening of chemical libraries against parasite strains has proven highly effective in expanding the chemical space available for new drug development. In this study, we identified three novel clusters, C4, C71, and C155, with promising properties that warrant further development through SAR studies, MoA validation, and in vivo efficacy evaluation, supported by their favorable predicted pharmacokinetic profiles. Furthermore, we reveal new chemical entities with potential activity against PfATP4, PfDHODH, and PfPI4K. Despite the emergence of resistance-associated mutations in these targets, the recent development of GanLum, a combination of ganaplacide and lumefantrine, as a non-artemisinin-based antimalarial therapy further underscores the value of identifying and developing novel inhibitors of validated parasite targets. The resistance profiling approach employed here, utilizing a panel of Dd2-resistant parasite strains in conjunction with chemical clustering, provides an effective framework for compound prioritization in early drug development. Overall, the screening data generated in this study expand the repertoire of antimalarial starting points and provide a foundation for multiple downstream medicinal chemistry campaigns, highlighting the broader impact of phenotypic screening in advancing antimalarial drug discovery.
