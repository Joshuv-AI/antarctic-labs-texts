Oxymetazoline as a predictor of turbinate reduction surgery outcomes: Objective support from a prospective, single‐blinded, computational fluid dynamics study
Authors: Zachary T Root, Zhenxing Wu, Thomas J Lepley, Aspen R Schneller, Robbie J Chapman, Veronica L Formanek, Kathleen M Kelly, Bradley A Otto, Kai Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA patient's subjective response to topical nasal decongestant is often used to screen for turbinate reduction surgery suitability. However, this anecdotal strategy has not been objectively and quantitatively evaluated.MethodsProspective, longitudinal, and single‐blinded cohort study employing computational fluid dynamic modeling based on computed tomography scans at baseline, 30 min postoxymetazoline, and 2 months postsurgery on 11 patients with chronic turbinate hypertrophy.ResultsNasal obstruction symptom evaluation (NOSE) and visual analogue scale (VAS) obstruction scores significantly improved from baseline to postoxymetazoline and again to postsurgery (NOSE: 71.82 ± 14.19 to 42.27 ± 25.26 to 22.27 ± 21.04; VAS: 6.09 ± 2.41 to 4.14 ± 2.20 to 2.08 ± 1.56; each interaction p < 0.05), with significant correlation between the latter two states (r∼0.37–0.69, p < 0.05). Oxymetazoline had a broader anatomical impact throughout inferior and middle turbinates than surgery (many p < 0.05); however, the improvement in regional airflow is similar (most p > 0.05) and predominantly surrounding the inferior turbinate. Strong postoxymetazoline to postsurgery correlations were observed in decreased nasal resistance (r = 0.79, p < 0.05), increased regional airflow rates (r = ‐0.47 to −0.55, p < 0.05) and regional air/mucosa shear force and heat flux (r = 0.43 to 0.58, p < 0.05); however, only increasing peak heat flux significantly correlated to symptom score improvement (NOSE: r = 0.48, p < 0.05).ConclusionWe present the first objective evidence that the “topical decongestant test” can help predict turbinate reduction surgery outcomes. The predictive effect is driven by similar improvementin regional airflow that leading to improved air/mucosa stimulations (peak heat flux) rather than through reduced nasal resistance.Keywords: adrenoceptor agonist, computational fluid dynamics modeling, oxymetazoline, turbinate hypertrophy, turbinate reduction, α1 and α2‐adrenogeneic

INTRODUCTION

Nasal obstruction is a commonly encountered symptom that may result from turbinate hypertrophy. Turbinate hypertrophy can occur on its own, or can be accompanied by a deviated septum, where the inferior turbinate on the concave side of the deviation will swell to compensate for the enlarged passage on that same side. 1 , 2 Turbinate edema may also result in enlarged turbinates as a consequence of allergic rhinitis, due to an Immunoglobulin E (IgE)‐mediated response to inhaled allergens that triggers histamine and cytokine influx. 3 , or vasomotor rhinitis, where the hypertrophy is usually bilateral and unrelated to an IgE‐mediated mechanism. 4 , 5 Because of Poiseuille's law, which states that the flow rate through a tube is directly proportional to the fourth power of the radius of the tube, rather small changes in turbinate size can drastically constrict nasal airflow and result in nasal obstruction. When medical management of vasomotor or allergic rhinitis through corticosteroids and antihistamines fail to restore perceived airflow patency, turbinate reduction surgery may be required, sometimes along with a septoplasty. These procedures are among the most commonly performed nasal sinus surgeries by otolaryngologists with over 250,000 cases performed in the United States annually. 6

The “decongestant test” is often anecdotally employed to screen for turbinate reduction surgery candidacy. The decongestant test consists of in‐office spray of nasal decongestant (oxymetazoline) to the nasal cavity of a patient with turbinate hypertrophy followed by observation of their subjective response. Alternatively, the patient may report their subjective response to home use of the medication. Oxymetazoline, the active ingredient in many over‐the‐counter medications, is an imidazoline derivative that acts as nasal decongestant due to its agnostic activity on receptors that control blood flow in the turbinate capillaries. 7 Since both oxymetazoline and turbinate reduction surgery result in the reduction of the inferior and (sometimes) middle turbinates, it can be intuitively argued that a patient who responds well to nasal decongestant will also respond well to turbinate reduction surgery. The contrary may also be argued, since perceived nasal obstruction may also result from a decrease in airflow sensation or a lack thereof due to dysfunction of the maxillary and/or ophthalmic branches of the trigeminal nerve, 8 , 9 such that the effect of nasal decongestant and surgery on perceived obstruction may be identical. However, these arguments have never been objectively or quantitatively assessed.

Our objective is to build on previous work detailing the effects of oxymetazoline compared with baseline and extend the followup to include the postoperative state. 10 Computed tomography (CT) scan‐based computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is used to objectively compare the baseline, postoxymetazoline, and postsurgical nasal cavities and airflow while at the same time correlating subjective symptom scores to variables derived from CFD simulations in order to uncover clinical relevance. If the decongestant test is proven valid, surgeons may confidently consider it during their clinical decision making as a quick in‐office tool to screen for suitable turbinate reduction candidates. This analysis may also lead to better understanding of the underlying factors contributing to subjective nasal obstruction complaints and their alleviation, which are still not well understood.

METHODS

Subjects

Eleven subjects aged 21–63 years (median: 41 years, five male and six female) with chronic nasal obstruction for >3 months and a nasal obstruction symptom evaluation (NOSE) score >40, who were candidates for turbinate reduction surgery were recruited from a tertiary medical center over a 14‐month period. The presence of inferior turbinate hypertrophy with or without septal deviation as the primary contributor to the subject's nasal obstruction was determined by attending otolaryngologists based on clinical assessment, CT scan, and endoscopic evaluation. Subjects with nasal trauma, nasal valve dysfunction, history of neurological disorders, pregnancy, chronic rhinosinusitis, or other medical conditions, which in the opinion of the investigator could confound the patients’ symptomology, were excluded. Included patients must have also failed 4–6 weeks of topical steroid therapy and must have had at least a 7‐day period without medical therapy prior to enrollment.

The study was approved by the Ohio State Biomedical Sciences Institutional Review Board (approval number: 2015H0262) and was conducted in accordance with the principals laid out in the Declaration of Helsinki. All subjects signed consent and authorization forms for participation and to enable their clinical data to be included in this study.

Study protocol

The study has a prospective, longitudinal, and single blinded design. After a routine head and neck physical and clinical examination during which the clinician determined the suitability of the candidate for this study, subjects rated their baseline symptoms via the NOSE 11 and visual analogue scale (VAS) of nasal obstruction (VAS: 0 being completely clear and 10 being completely obstructed). 8 The VAS is assessed unilaterally, where a subject would gently block one naris and rate their degree of obstruction while breathing through the contralateral side. Next, a baseline CT scan was obtained using an in‐office cone beam CT scanner (3D Accuitomo; J. Morita USA, Inc., Irvine, CA). To account for placebo effect, a sham test was performed prior to the oxymetazoline spray where the subjects were blindfolded and told that they would receive two treatments in randomized order, though the first treatment administered was always saline spray. Subjects were then unblindfolded and re‐assessed symptom scores after 30 min of rest. The sham test results were published in our prior studies, 10 , 12 which showed some placebo effect, although it was not statistically significant. Subjects then received two sprays of oxymetazoline in each naris while blindfolded and sitting upright (Afrin no drip oxymetazoline hydrochloride 0.05% spray bottle, Bayer Healthcare LLC, Whippany, NJ). After 30 min of rest to allow for medication to take its effect, subjects again reported NOSE and VAS scores, after which a second CT scan was obtained. The saline and Afrin bottles were primed to ensure similar spray volumes between subjects. All patients then received submucous inferior turbinate resection using a microdebrider, with eight patients additionally receiving concurrent septoplasty. Depending on the patients’ elective decision as well as operating room availability, patients received surgery between 15 and 67 days after baseline evaluation (mean 42.6 ± 17.1 days). All subjects, except one, had no prior sinus surgery and allergic rhinitis was present in four subjects who were on allergy treatment, though inferior turbinate hypertrophy was still determined as the primary contributor to nasal obstruction in these subjects. A CT scan, NOSE score, and VAS score were obtained 2 months after surgery. Subjects’ CT scans at three time points: baseline, 30 min postoxymetazoline, and 2 months postsurgery, were then used for CFD analysis. Throughout the process, the clinical providers performing the surgery were blinded to the patients’ subjective nasal decongestant treatment outcome, and the team performing CFD analysis team was blinded to the patients’ clinical outcomes.

CFD modeling

The procedures for nasal CFD modeling based on cone‐beam CT scan have been widely described in the literature. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Briefly, CT scans are imported into the commercial software AMIRA (Visualization Sciences Group, Hillsboro, OR), where the interface between the nasal mucosa and air is delineated and a 3D model is created after necessary smoothing, artifact correction, and extensive inspection. A second software package (ICEM CFD; Ansys, Inc, Canonsburg, PA) is then used to fill the 3D volume with tiny tetrahedral elements and prism layers; the application of the latter at the mucosal surface serves to model more accurately the rapidly changing near‐wall air velocity. The final volume mesh contains 1.1–3.6 million elements. Next, the CFD software ANSYS Fluent 19.2 (Ansys, Inc, Canonsburg, PA) is used to solve the governing equations for airflow, the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, and simulate inspiratory quasi‐steady laminar nasal airflow. A pressure drop of 15 Pa was applied between the nares and the nasopharynx to represent restful breathing, a state that is most relevant to patients’ symptoms during daily life. 18 The walls of the nasal cavity were assumed to be ridged with a no‐slip boundary condition; the numerical solutions of the continuity and momentum equations were determined using the finite‐volume method, and continuous pressure and velocity fields were discretized using a second‐order upwind scheme. The converged simulation results were determined once the residual of each variable was less than 10−5. The numerical method and meshing protocol applied in this study has been previously validated against experimental measurements. 19 Nasal resistance was calculated based on ratio of pressure drop over total nasal flow rate.

To investigate the regional impact of oxymetazoline and surgery, airflow rate and nasal airway cross‐sectional area were analyzed in three separate inferior, middle, and superior regions, selected to best contain their respective turbinate structure and remaining constant for each subject from baseline to post‐treatments, as well as at three separate coronal cross‐sections at 3/10, 4.5/10, and 7/10 of the length from the nasal vestibule to the nasopharynx. Additionally, an “anterior” region was defined for each patient, containing the nasal vestibule and the nasal valve regions (see Figure 1A).

(A) To investigate the regional impact of oxymetazoline and nasal obstruction surgery, CFD variables were analyzed in three separate transverse plane regions (inferior, middle, and superior regions) and three separate coronal cross‐sections, from the apex to 3/10, 4.5/10, and 7/10 of the total length of the nasal cavity (the anterior, middle, and posterior section, respectively). (B) The effect of oxymetazoline and surgery on regional nasal airway cross‐sectional area (cm2) and (C) flow rate (mL/s).

Since previous reports have indicated that sensory perception of nasal cooling may impact subjective nasal obstruction symptoms, 8 , 9 we examined regional heat flux and wall shear stress (WSS) according to previous published methods. 9 , 20 Heat transfer was evaluated by solving the decoupled energy equation in ANSYS Fluent following convergence of the momentum simulation. The temperature at both nares was set to 20°C to represent ambient room temperatures. The temperature at the mucosal wall was set to 35°C, based on experimental measurements in the literature. 21 The energy equation was discretized using a first‐order upwind scheme for numerical solutions with the energy convergence residual set to 1e−15. We considered two additional variables from these literatures that have some success in predicting nasal obstruction symptoms: the peak heat flux posterior to the nasal vestibule 9 (after smoothing with a 4 mm diameter median filter) and area of the nasal cavity where the heat flux exceeds 50 W m−2. 22

Statistical analysis

Repeated measures ANOVA with post‐hoc Fisher's least significant difference test were used to compare changes of variables between baseline, postoxymetazoline, and postsurgery. Nonparametric Spearman correlations were performed between all objective variables and symptom scores as well as symptom score improvement. p Values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

NOSE and VAS scores significantly improved (i.e., decreased) from baseline to post‐oxymetazoline and again from post‐oxymetazoline to post‐surgery (NOSE: from 71.82 ± 14.19 to 42.27 ± 25.26 to 22.27 ± 21.04, all interactions p < 0.05; VAS: from 6.09 ± 2.41 to 4.14 ± 2.20 to 2.08 ± 1.56, all interactions p < 0.05; see Figure 2 and Table 1). Most importantly, significant correlations were found in NOSE and VAS between improvements from baseline to post‐oxymetazoline and from post‐oxymetazoline to post‐surgery (NOSE: r = 0.37, VAS: r = 0.69, p < 0.05). Compared with baseline (0.24 ± 0.18 Pa/mL s), significant reductions in nasal resistance were found both post‐oxymetazoline (0.13 ± 0.05 Pa/mL s) and post‐surgery (0.12 ± 0.04 Pa/mL s) with no significant difference between the postoxymetazoline and postsurgery states. A significant correlation was found in the reduction of nasal resistance between postoxymetazoline and postsurgery (r = 0.79, p < 0.05). These results provide the first confirmation that patients who responded well to the decongestant test also responded well to turbinate reduction surgery when considering both subjective and objective outcomes.

Subjective symptoms scores significantly decreased from baseline to post‐oxymetazoline and from post‐oxymetazoline to pos‐tsurgery and significantly correlated between the post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgical improvements. Reductions in nasal resistance as compared with baseline were significant yet quite similar between the two treatments, and also significantly correlated between the post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgical improvements.

The anatomical effects of oxymetazoline versus surgery on the nasal turbinates (i.e., cross‐sectional area) and their functional impact on nasal airflow (i.e., regional flow rate) are depicted in Figure 1B,C and Table 1. Oxymetazoline had a broader anatomical impact within the nasal cavity than surgery, significantly affecting both inferior and middle turbinates (both p < 0.05 to baseline). As expected, changes associated with inferior turbinate reduction surgery are limited to the inferior turbinate, and there were no significant anatomical or functional differences between baseline and post‐surgery at the middle turbinate. Along the inferior turbinate, oxymetazoline and surgery produced similar regional airflow changes in that they were both significantly higher than baseline (p < 0.05). However, along the middle turbinate, no significant changes in regional airflow rate were found between post‐oxymetazoline versus baseline, except at the posterior end of the middle region where the nasal airway begins to merge with the nasopharynx. Thus, despite the broader anatomical impact of oxymetazoline, the functional regional airflow changes are quite similar between oxymetazoline and surgery. Superior cross‐sectional area was largely unaffected by either oxymetazoline or surgery, and thus were omitted from further analysis.

WSS represents the shear force of airflow exerted onto a unit area of nasal mucosa, and heat flux represents the amount of cooling occurring over a unit area of nasal mucosa when a subject breathes in cooler room air. Both can be indicative of the level of air/mucosal stimulation during breathing. Peak mucosal WSS as well as mucosal area integrated WSS in the anterior region significantly increased both post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery (see Table 1 and Figure 3; p < 0.05). Mucosal area‐integrated heat flux (cooling) significantly increased in the inferior turbinate regions both post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery (p < 0.05), but only postsurgery in the anterior region (Table 1 and Figure 3). Overall peak heat flux and mucosal area where heat flux > 50 W/m2 significantly increased both post‐oxymetazoline and post‐urgery.

Area‐integrated heat flux and wall shear stress (WSS) saw no significant differences in the middle turbinate region. Significant correlations were seen between treatment improvements from baseline for both overall heat flux (r = 0.583, p < 0.05) and overall WSS (r = 0.432, p < 0.05).

Table 2 shows that several variables, including nasal resistance, regional airflow rates, airway cross‐sectional area, area‐integrated WSS and heat fluxes, and peak WSS and heat fluxes, all significantly correlated between the postoxymetazoline and postsurgery states. This indicates that these variables experience a similar trend of improvement following the two treatments. However, among these variables, only peak heat flux significantly correlated with symptom score improvements (in this case, NOSE score) postoxymetazoline versus postsurgery (Figure 4). Peak heat flux may be the candidate objective variable that links the observed symptom improvement trends across the two treatments.

DISCUSSION

Herein, we tested the anecdote that a patient's subjective response to oxymetazoline may predict his or her response to turbinate reduction surgery. We confirmed that patients who responded well to the decongestant test also responded well to turbinate reduction surgery in terms of both subjective (VAS and NOSE scores) and objective (e.g., nasal resistance, heat flux, etc.) outcomes. Overall, 10 out of 11 (90.9%) of subjects had similar subjective responses to oxymetazoline and surgery, extending the findings from a previous case report. 12

CT scans were taken at three time points (baseline, postoxymetazoline, and postsurgery) and CFD simulations were conducted to quantify the detailed structural and aerodynamic profiles at each time point (Figure 5). Our previous work showed that oxymetazoline broadly affects the full length of the inferior and middle turbinates, which is contrary to the common notion that the anterior portion of the turbinates in the primary site of vasoconstriction. In this study, we confirmed this observation and further showed that oxymetazoline's anatomical effect is broader than inferior turbinate reduction surgery due to its effect on adjacent tissue, including the middle turbinate. However, despite its broader anatomical effect, the regional airflow changes postoxymetazoline were actually quite similar to that of postsurgery, which is contained along the inferior turbinate, with no significant changes in the middle turbinate (Figure 1C). Correspondingly, total nasal resistance, multiple regional WSS variables, and multiple regional heat flux variables changed in similar fashion and correlated significantly between post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery.

Visualizing the effect of oxymetazoline and nasal obstruction surgery through CT scan and CFD modeling in two subjects. Interestingly, subject 1 (BA001) showed both improved subjective nasal patency (NOSE: from 80 to 35 and to 30) and improved nasal aerodynamics post‐oxymetazoline and post‐urgery, while subject 2 (BA0023) did not report significant symptom relief (NOSE: 80‐90‐75) despite significantly improved airflow and reduced nasal resistance post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery. However, peak heat flux in the nasal valve region for subject 2 did not improve much post‐oxymetazoline nor post‐surgery, which may explain their poor clinical outcome, whereas the peak heat flux for subject 1 was significantly improved post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery, corroborating the observed significant correlation between NOSE score improvement and peak heat flux (but not nasal resistance) in Figures 3 and 4. As the nasal vestibule region is covered with a stratified squamous epithelium that is moderately keratinized and histologically resembles skin, nasal mucosal heat flux was only analyzed and plotted posterior to the nasal vestibule.

In order to determine which set of objective variables best explain subjective symptom improvement, as well as the correlation between post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery, we screened variables based on three criteria: (1) first, since both oxymetazoline and surgery are effective in relieving patient symptoms, the variable or set of variables must be significantly improved both post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery as compared with baseline (Table 1); (2) the degree of change of the variables must significantly correlate between both post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery (Table 2), which is necessary to explain why post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery outcomes correlate with each other; (3) finally, the improvement of variables must significantly correlate to subjective symptom improvement (Table 2).

One consideration is nasal resistance, which was significantly reduced both postoxymetazoline and postsurgery, and these reductions significantly correlated with each other (Figure 2). However, improvement in resistance did not significantly correlate with symptom score improvement across the two treatments. Previously, Malik et al. 23 also found that CFD‐derived nasal resistance does not significantly correlate with the 22‐item sino‐nasal outcome test, NOSE, or VAS scores in both deviated septum and healthy control cohorts. Hsu et al. 24 also presented evidence that total nasal resistance does not significantly correlate with NOSE or VAS scores preoperatively or postoperatively. In the current study, figure 3 did show that if all three time points are analyzed together, nasal resistance does correlate significantly with NOSE score (not NOSE score improvement); however, this correlation could be artificially driven by a grouping effect, as baseline nasal resistance and NOSE scores are all significantly lower than post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery states.

The only objective variable in Table 2 meeting all three criteria was peak heat flux (bold font). Heat flux represents mucosal cooling as the cooler ambient air is inhaled and warmed by the nasal turbinates. It is indicative of the amount of air‐mucosal stimulation perceived during breathing, a sensory mechanism mediated by transient receptor potential melastatin family member 8 (TRPM8) activation. 25 Patient perception of obstruction has previously been shown to depend on this mechanism rather than physical obstruction. 8 Our group was among the first to provide evidence that subjective nasal patency ratings significantly correlated to peak heat flux among healthy controls 9 and among patients who underwent nasal valve treatment. 26 In a separate study investigating which aspects of nasal septal deviation (NSD) result in obstructive symptoms, only anterior averaged heat flux on the deviated side and inferior turbinate peak heat flux on the nondeviated side were able to significantly differentiate symptomatic NSD from asymptomatic NSD cohorts. 23 In the present study, peak heat flux significantly increased both post‐oxymetazoline and post‐surgery. Additionally, the degree of increase in peak heat flux (but not in the area where heat flux surpassed 50 W/m2) significantly correlated between treatments and correlated well with VAS score improvement. This suggests that changes in peak heat flux, representing maximum airflow stimulation perceived through activation of the TRPM8 pathway, may be the primary predictor of patient response to both oxymetazoline and turbinate reduction surgery. With the rapid evolution of CFD techniques and models resulting in improved processing efficiency, 27 future clinical evaluations could include obtaining CT imaging after oxymetazoline administration and using CFD to predict or optimize improvements in peak heat flux post‐surgery. This could be a future point of care test to capture those who would not do well with turbinate reduction surgery or those with a preexisting tendency for empty nose syndrome.

Surveying the literature, a potential weakness in previous nasal obstruction outcome and CFD studies is that they often combine diseased patients and healthy controls or combine pre‐ and posttreatment data within the same regression analysis. 22 , 28 However, patients’ nasal airway structures can significantly differ from those of the healthy population. Treatments such as surgery or medical management can make extensive changes to the nasal airway and impact numerous variables simultaneously (e.g., resistance, airway volume, surface area, WSS), often in the same direction. For example, in this study, the inferior meatus showed a significant increase in airflow rate and cross‐sectional area between both postoxymetazoline and postsurgery versus baseline, which pushed a wide range of variables to change in the same direction after both treatments. While significant differences or correlations are often found when combining data (see Figures 2 or 3), the correlation may be driven by large cluster changes between treatment states that do not necessarily contribute or correlate to symptom changes (see Figure 5 for examples of two patients, one who responded well and one did not respond well). We have designed our analysis to carefully screen variables based on three strict criteria. Our findings, while still preliminary due to the nature of a pilot study, demonstrate that rigorous analysis must be used to yield findings that corroborate well with existing peer‐reviewed literature.

Limitations of the present study include potential individual variability in the agnostic activity of oxymetazoline between subjects, despite our efforts to control for consistent drug delivery via bottle priming, and the lack of a surgical control group, for example, patients undergoing pituitary surgery. The data presented demonstrate an association, but association does not necessarily mean causation. Future research may aim to directly assess the relationship between turbinate bone and tissue volume to oxymetazoline‐induced improvement. The presence of concurrent septoplasty as well as allergic rhinitis in a small subset of subjects may confound turbinate reduction results. Additionally, one subject was prescribed fluticasone propionate at the time of postsurgery follow‐up. Last, septoplasty and turbinate reduction were conducted blindly by two rhinologists, and while there were no significant differences in symptom scores and improvements between each surgeon's cohort, minor differences in their surgical approach could be a confounding variable.

CONCLUSION

Overall, patients who subjectively respond well to oxymetazoline also respond well to inferior turbinate reduction surgery. Our findings indicate that a patient's response to nasal obstruction surgery depends on the improvement in regional airflow rates and regional cooling stimulation to nasal mucosa, which may be indexed through CFD‐calculated peak heat flux. Future work may aim to incorporate CFD analysis into clinical evaluations as an objective predictor of nasal obstruction surgery outcome.
