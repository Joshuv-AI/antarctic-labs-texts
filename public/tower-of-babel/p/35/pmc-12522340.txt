Mediating role of sleep quality in the relationship between smartphone addiction and academic performance among nursing students in Morocco: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdelhadi El Haddaouy, Hicham Blaak, Jaouad Elkhalladi, Meryem Essaidi, Hayat Iziki, Fatima Es-Sabir, Fatima El Omari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic performance (AP) is a crucial indicator of success among nursing students, directly influencing their professional development and the quality of patient care they will deliver. While smartphone addiction (SA) and poor sleep quality (SQ) have each been linked to diminished AP, the mediating role of SQ in this relationship remains poorly understood, especially in North African contexts.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the mediating role of SQ in the relationship between SA and AP among nursing students in Meknes, Morocco.MethodA cross-sectional quantitative design was employed, involving 451 nursing students recruited through a census approach at a public higher institute in Meknes. Data were collected between January and July 2024 using validated instruments: the Smartphone Addiction Scale – Short Version (SAS-SV) for smartphone addiction, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) for sleep quality, and grade point average (GPA) for academic performance. Structural relationships were tested using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM).ResultsThe prevalence of SA was notably high (81.15%), and 86.467% had a poor SQ. PLS-SEM revealed that SA had a significant direct negative effect on AP (β = -0.460, p < 0.001) and a positive effect on poor SQ score (β = 0.587, p < 0.001). Poor SQ score, in turn, negatively predicted AP (β = -0.227, p < 0.001). SQ partially mediated the relationship between SA and AP, with the indirect effect accounting for 22.4% of the total effect of SA on AP.ConclusionThese findings highlight the importance of integrating educational interventions that address both digital behavior and sleep hygiene to improve the academic performance of nursing students. Future research should investigate this dynamic in greater depth across various sociocultural contexts.Keywords: Academic performance, Sleep quality, Smartphone addiction, Nursing students, Mediating role, Structural equations modeling

Introduction

Academic performance (AP) serves as a critical indicator of student success, capturing their capacity to meet the learning objectives established through assessments, grades, and overall engagement in the educational process [1]. This metric holds particular significance for nursing students, as their AP not only shapes their professional development but also directly influences the quality of care they will ultimately deliver [2]. Among the numerous factors that can affect AP, such as cognitive abilities, intrinsic motivation, and overall well-being [3], smartphone addiction (SA) is attracting growing interest.

Characterized by excessive, poorly controlled, and often compulsive use, SA is frequently associated with impairments in concentration, working memory, and time management, as well as increased procrastination and reduced academic engagement [4, 5]. This dynamic is theoretically grounded in the Techno-Stress Theory, which highlights how prolonged exposure to digital technologies can generate cognitive overload, disrupt attentional resources, and ultimately undermine AP [6]. Numerous international studies have confirmed a negative relationship between SA and AP [4, 5, 7, 8]. In Morocco, data indicate that 41.2% of nursing students report excessive smartphone use, with 21% acknowledging that this directly impacts their academic outcomes [9].

Beyond its impact on AP, SA also exerts a significant influence on SQ. Intensive nighttime use of smartphones disrupts circadian rhythms, delays sleep onset, and fragments overall sleep architecture, mainly due to the blue light emitted by screens and the prolonged cognitive stimulation they induce [10, 11]. This phenomenon can be interpreted through the lens of the compensatory use theory, which posits that individuals often turn to digital technologies to regulate emotions or cope with stress, thereby extending their nighttime screen exposure and exacerbating sleep disturbances [12]. Such mechanisms appear to be particularly pronounced among nursing students, who face substantial academic and clinical demands, as evidenced by a Moroccan study indicating that nearly 46.3% of students with elevated SA levels also report impaired SQ [13].

Sleep quality itself emerges as a significant determinant of AP. Poor SQ undermines cognitive functions essential for learning, such as sustained attention and memory consolidation [14, 15]. These effects can be further understood through the lens of neurocognitive models of sleep, which emphasize the role of sleep in supporting neural plasticity and the integration of newly acquired knowledge [16]. Nursing students, in particular, face substantial stress due to long hours of study, demanding clinical placements, and intense academic pressure, all of which contribute to the deterioration of their sleep quality [5, 17, 18]. In Morocco, more than 60% of nursing students report experiencing sleep disturbances [19], a figure that aligns with global trends observed among university populations, 64.81% [20].

Emerging evidence highlights the mediating role of sleep quality in the relationship between digital technology overuse and academic performance. Specifically, poor sleep quality functions as a central pathway through which smartphone addiction undermines academic performance [21]. On the one hand, intensive smartphone use disrupts sleep patterns, leading to insufficient rest and cognitive fatigue [10, 22]. On the other hand, sleep disturbances have been shown to mediate not only the link between excessive technology use and diminished academic performance but also the relationship between smartphone addiction and broader mental health problems such as anxiety and depressive symptoms, which in turn negatively affect learning capacities and academic engagement [21, 23, 24]. This dual mediating role positions sleep quality as a pivotal variable, integrating cognitive, behavioral, and psychological mechanisms that collectively account for the detrimental impact of digital overuse on educational achievement [25, 26].

Despite the extensive body of research that has independently examined the effects of SA and SQ on AP, few studies have investigated how SQ might mediate the relationship between SA addiction and AP, particularly within North African contexts. However, understanding this dynamic is crucial for developing targeted educational interventions. Accordingly, the present study aims to analyze how SQ mediates the relationship between SA and AP among nursing students in Meknes, Morocco. Method

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional quantitative design to examine the structural relationships among SA, SQ, and AP in nursing students. The survey was conducted at a public nursing training institute in Meknes, Morocco, involving all undergraduate students. The primary variables in the model included SA as the independent variable, SQ as the mediating variable, and AP as the dependent variable.

Study setting

This study was conducted at the Higher Institute of Nursing and Health Techniques (HINHT) in Meknes, a public institution operating under the auspices of the Moroccan Ministry of Health and Social Protection. Unlike traditional university systems, HINHT is distinguished by its professional orientation and its institutional alignment with the national strategy to modernize and streamline higher education in the health sector. It forms part of a structured national network comprising seven central institutes and sixteen affiliated institutes distributed across the entire Moroccan territory.

As an affiliated institute, the HINHT of Meknes plays a strategic role in strengthening regional healthcare capacities by offering training programs focused on developing practical and clinical competencies. The institution provides degree pathways at the levels of professional bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral studies, all centered on nursing disciplines. Its entire educational framework is aligned with national standards for professional health training, thereby ensuring coherence between field requirements and academic benchmarks.

Sample/participants

Participants were nursing students aged 18 to 23 years, recruited through direct contact in classroom settings. A census-based sampling strategy was adopted, whereby all students enrolled at the HINHT in Meknes, Morocco (n = 472), regardless of their year of study or area of specialization, were invited to participate, thereby maximizing representativeness and minimizing selection bias. Students who did not own a smartphone (n = 8) or who declined to participate (n = 13) were excluded from the study. The final sample thus comprised 451 participants. This strategy preserved the natural diversity of the nursing student population in terms of gender, academic level, and geographical origin, consistent with national trends observed in Moroccan nursing schools. The final sample size exceeded Cohen’s (1988) recommendations for mediation analysis [27] and was consistent with or larger than those used in similar PLS-SEM studies [28, 29]. Nevertheless, as acknowledged in the Limitations section, the single-site setting and gender imbalance may restrict broader generalizability, particularly to male students.

Measurement of variables

Academic performance was assessed using the students’ grade point average (GPA), obtained directly from the Examinations and Admissions Unit of the HINHT in Meknes. These official records, expressed as overall grade point averages on a 20-point scale, were treated as objective indicators of academic achievement. To ensure a representative reflection of the target population and to correct for potential imbalances related to the distribution of students across academic levels or programs, weighting adjustments were applied during the statistical analyses. This approach provided a robust and unbiased estimation of overall academic performance.

Sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) developed by [30]. This standardized and validated instrument measures sleep quality over the past month across seven components: subjective sleep quality, latency, duration, efficiency, disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. The global PSQI score ranges from 0 to 21, with scores below 5 indicating good sleep quality, while scores of 5 or higher reflect poor sleep quality. The scale has demonstrated excellent psychometric validity across diverse populations, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 [30]. In the present sample, the PSQI also showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.70).

Smartphone addiction was measured using the Smartphone Addiction Scale – Short Version (SAS-SV) developed by [31]. This abbreviated version of the original scale includes 10 items rated on a 6-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 6 = strongly agree), yielding a total score ranging from 10 to 60. Cutoff scores established by the authors, 31 for males and 33 for females, were used to identify problematic smartphone use. The scale has demonstrated excellent psychometric validity among various student populations, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91 [31]. In this study, the scale also exhibited strong reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.88).

The SAS-SV and PSQI were administered in their original English versions to avoid potential biases related to translation, as students demonstrated sufficient proficiency in this language through their training. The data collection process was supervised directly by the researcher, who obtained administrative approval and ensured clarity during administration. The robustness of this approach was confirmed by psychometric analyses, which demonstrated satisfactory reliability, validity, and the absence of methodological biases.

Data collection

Descriptive analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25, calculating means and standard deviations for quantitative variables, as well as frequencies and proportions for qualitative variables, to characterize the sociodemographic and academic profile of the sample. Spearman correlation analyses were performed to explore unadjusted relationships among the primary variables of interest: SA, SQ, and AP.

To ensure the validity of the structural model, diagnostic procedures were additionally performed for both multicollinearity and common method bias. Multicollinearity among predictor variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance values, following methodological recommendations [32]. Potential common method bias (CMB) was evaluated using Harman’s single-factor test [33]. At the measurement level, the psychometric properties of the SAS-SV and PSQI were evaluated using partial least squares confirmatory factor analysis. Specifically, indicator reliability (outer loadings), internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha, rho_A, and composite reliability, CR), convergent validity (average variance extracted, AVE), discriminant validity (heterotrait–monotrait ratio of correlations, HTMT), and item-level collinearity diagnostics (outer VIF) were assessed in accordance with established guidelines. These measurement-level analyses were conducted in SmartPLS version 3.0, while descriptive and preliminary analyses (correlations, multicollinearity at the construct level, and CMB) were conducted in SPSS version 25.

Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was employed to simultaneously test the hypothesized relationships among these variables and assess the mediating role of SQ in the relationship between SA and AP. The conceptual model tested (Fig. 1) included three variables: two exogenous variables (SA and SQ) and one endogenous variable (AP). In addition to the hypothesized paths, sociodemographic variables (gender, study level, and origin) were entered as control variables to examine their potential effects on AP. The mediating effect of SQ was evaluated using SmartPLS version 3.0, with a bootstrap procedure applied to 2,000 samples. Path coefficients, t-values, and p-values were computed to determine significance, and model fit was evaluated using R² and Q². Effect sizes (f², q²) were also calculated to quantify the relative contribution of predictors to the explained variance. p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Mohammed V University in Rabat, Morocco (ref. CERB 62 − 23). Informed verbal consent was secured from all participants prior to data collection. Anonymity, confidentiality, and voluntary participation were ensured throughout the research process.

Measurement model assessment

Reliability and convergent validity

The reliability of the instruments was supported by Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR), which exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70 for both SAS-SV and PSQI. In addition, rho_A coefficients were consistent with these results, reinforcing the internal consistency of the constructs. Convergent validity, assessed through the Average Variance Extracted (AVE), reached acceptable values (0.50–0.56), in line with the criteria of Fornell and Larcker [34]. Factor loadings ranged from 0.58 to 0.84, indicating satisfactory indicator reliability and justifying the retention of all items. Although some loadings were below 0.70, their inclusion is supported by methodological recommendations when global reliability indices are adequate. Collinearity diagnostics confirmed the robustness of the model, as all outer VIF values remained below the conservative cut-off of 3.3. Given the reflective nature of the SAS-SV and PSQI, VIF values are interpreted only as an additional diagnostic tool and not as a primary validation criterion. These findings collectively demonstrate the absence of problematic multicollinearity among items. Taken together, the results provide robust evidence of both reliability and validity of the SAS-SV and PSQI in this sample. Consequently, the measurement model meets established quality standards in psychometric evaluation (Table 2).

Assessment of multicollinearity and common method bias

To ensure the robustness of the structural model, multicollinearity among the predictor variables was first assessed. VIF and tolerance values were calculated. The results showed that SAS-SV scores had a VIF of 1.091 with a tolerance of 0.917, while PSQI scores had a VIF of 1.112 with a tolerance of 0.899. All values were well below the commonly accepted VIF threshold of 5, confirming that multicollinearity was not a concern in this study.

In addition, Harman’s single-factor test was performed to examine the potential influence of CMB. Exploratory factor analysis without rotation revealed that the first factor accounted for 34.02% of the total variance, a percentage below the critical threshold of 50%. This indicated that no single factor dominated the variance structure and that common method bias was not likely to compromise the validity of the study results.

Structural model estimation

To test the structural model’s hypotheses, a PLS-bootstrapping procedure was conducted to estimate the path coefficients and determine the significance levels of the three hypothesized relationships. The number of bootstrap subsamples was set at 2,000.

The results from the bootstrapping analysis indicate that all three hypothesized relationships are statistically significant. Specifically, SA exerts a positive and significant effect on the mean SQ score (β = 0.587; t = 20.050; p < 0.001), suggesting that problematic smartphone use is associated with poorer SQ. Additionally, SA demonstrates a direct, negative, and significant effect on AP (β = −0.460; t = 11.777; p < 0.001), indicating a detrimental impact of SA on AP. Furthermore, SQ negatively influences AP (β = −0.227; t = 5.733; p < 0.001), implying that lower SQ leads to a significant reduction in academic outcomes.

The mediation analysis reveals a significant indirect effect of SQ in the relationship between SA and AP. Both segments of the indirect pathway (SA → SQ and SQ → AP) are significant, confirming the presence of a mediating effect. The indirect effect is estimated at β = 0.587 × (− 0.227) = − 0.133. Consequently, the total effect of SA on AP, calculated by summing the direct and indirect effects, amounts to β = −0.460 − 0.133 = − 0.593. In practical terms, this means that a one-point increase in the SAS-SV score (reflecting higher SA) results in a 0.593-point decrease in the student’s cumulative grade average. This model corresponds to partial mediation, as both direct and indirect effects coexist and jointly contribute to the decline in AP (Fig. 3; Table 4).

Control variables analysis

In addition to the hypothesized structural paths, control variables were examined to assess whether sociodemographic factors contributed significantly to AP.

Table 5 presents the results regarding the integration of control variables into the structural model. The bootstrapping procedure was applied to the model to estimate the effect of each control variable. The analyses show that study level exerts a negative and statistically significant effect on AP (β = − 0.080, p = 0.037), suggesting that progression through the curriculum may be accompanied by increased academic and clinical demands leading to a slight decline in performance. In contrast, gender did not show a significant association with AP (β = − 0.025, p = 0.518), indicating that sex-related differences are not a major explanatory factor in this context. Similarly, origin (urban vs. rural) was not significantly related to AP (β = − 0.090, p = 0.059), although the trend observed, close to the significance threshold, may suggest a potential influence in certain subgroups. Thus, only study level appears to be a relevant factor, while gender and origin do not account for variations in AP. These results confirm that most of the variance in AP is explained by the model’s main variables rather than by sociodemographic characteristics. Overall, the inclusion of control variables demonstrates that the mediation model remains robust and valid, regardless of these additional factors.

Quality of model fit

To assess the model’s quality of fit, several PLS-SEM indices were employed. The model demonstrated acceptable explanatory power (R² = 0.383) and moderate predictive relevance (Q² = 0.380), indicating an overall satisfactory performance. Regarding the contribution of predictors, SA exhibited a stronger effect size (f² = 0.226) compared to SQ (f² = 0.183), suggesting a greater influence on the dependent variable in terms of predictive strength. Conversely, SQ showed higher predictive relevance (q² = 0.219) than SA (q² = 0.175), underscoring its superior utility in explaining variance within the endogenous construct. These results highlight the complementary roles of SA and SQ in enhancing the model’s predictive validity for AP.

Importance–performance map analysis (IPMA) for the mediation analysis

To complement the mediation analysis, an IPMA was performed with AP as the target construct. The results indicate that SA has the strongest negative contribution to AP (|β| = 0.593), whereas SQ also shows a meaningful impact (|β| = 0.227). However, SQ exhibited a lower performance index (54.3) compared to SA (61.0), highlighting greater room for improvement in SQ. This suggests that interventions targeting better sleep hygiene may yield substantial benefits for AP, while continued efforts to reduce problematic smartphone use remain necessary (Table 6).

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to examine the mediating role of SQ in the relationship between SA and academic performance (AP) among nursing students in Morocco. To achieve this, PLS-SEM was employed to model and test the structural relationships among the variables. The analysis revealed both a significant direct effect of SA on AP and a partial mediating effect of SQ, highlighting the complexity of interactions between behavioral factors and students’ academic outcomes.

The study revealed a particularly high prevalence of smartphone addiction, with 81.15% (366/451) of students classified as dependent according to the SAS-SV. This figure far exceeds that reported in a previous Moroccan study, 46.3% [13], confirming the worrying rise of this phenomenon among nursing students. These results are consistent with those observed in similar sociocultural contexts: 78% in Saudi Arabia [7], 87.8% in Egypt [35], 80.15% in Turkey [36], and 87.8% in Sudan [37]. Such variability across studies can be attributed to methodological differences (e.g., measurement tools, diagnostic thresholds) as well as contextual factors such as academic pressure and culturally specific technology use, further underscoring the relevance of the present local investigation.

Furthermore, our findings indicate that study level also exerts a small but statistically significant negative effect on AP. This suggests that as students progress through successive years of training, their academic outcomes may slightly decline, likely reflecting the cumulative burden of academic workload, clinical practice requirements, and psychological stressors. Similar patterns have been observed in medical and health sciences education, where advanced years are associated with increased distress, fatigue, and reduced academic efficiency [38, 39]. Comparable results have also been reported in secondary and higher education contexts, where heightened academic expectations in higher levels or later years often negatively influence performance, particularly in the absence of adequate coping strategies and institutional support [40, 41]. These findings highlight the need to account for curricular stage as a relevant contextual factor when analyzing predictors of student performance.

Analytically, SA emerged as a significant negative predictor of AP. This relationship, although complex, can be understood through technological stress theory, which suggests that excessive exposure to digital technologies leads to cognitive overload, disrupts working memory, and impairs time management, thereby diminishing academic outcomes [6]. This trend is corroborated by previous studies reporting a significant decrease in AP among students who are heavily dependent on smartphones [5, 42]. Moreover, specific smartphone usage patterns, such as frequent morning checking, extended daily use, and high interaction frequency, have been found to significantly predict reduced AP [43]. These findings suggest that the negative impact of SA extends beyond mere screen time, involving deeper psychocognitive processes that divert attentional resources away from academic demands.

Despite these significant associations, the literature remains marked by inconsistencies regarding the nature of the relationship between SA and AP. Some studies have reported positive effects, indicating that smartphones used for educational purposes may enhance mobile learning, information access, and peer collaboration [44]. Others have noted that heavy users sometimes outperform their less connected peers due to strategic engagement with digital tools [45]. Conversely, certain studies have found no statistically significant relationship between these variables [46]. Such divergent findings underscore the need for a nuanced interpretation that distinguishes between utilitarian or academic uses of smartphones and recreational or compulsive behaviors.

The analysis of the mediating role of SQ reveals an indirect pathway through which AS influences SQ, which in turn has a detrimental effect on students’ AP. In this sense, the results of our study show a significant positive effect of SA on SQ scores, indicating that students with high levels of SA are more likely to report poor SQ. These results are consistent with recent studies highlighting circadian rhythm disruption, delayed sleep onset, and fragmented sleep as the main consequences of excessive smartphone use at night [11, 47, 48]. In this context, compensatory use theory suggests that individuals sometimes use their smartphones as a means of emotional regulation or stress avoidance. This behavior, motivated by unmet psychological needs, increases cognitive arousal before sleep and delays sleep onset [49]. This behavioral pattern can be worsened in a challenging academic environment, like that faced by nursing students, who encounter a mix of mental workload, clinical pressures, and emotional instability [50]. Supporting this [51], found that over 78% of students using smartphones late at night reported poor SQ, underscoring the systemic nature of this compensatory mechanism.

In the same vein, our findings demonstrate a significant negative effect of SQ score on AP, confirming that students with poorer SQ tend to achieve lower academic outcomes. These results align with recent studies that document how insufficient or poor SQ impairs critical cognitive functions such as attention, working memory, and learning consolidation, capacities particularly vital in rigorous fields like nursing [52–54]. Neurocognitive models of sleep further illuminate these mechanisms by positing that sleep actively supports brain plasticity and memory integration, especially during REM and slow-wave phases [55]. Disrupted sleep, therefore, hinders the reactivation of memory traces and the structuring of knowledge, reducing learning efficiency [56]. Moreover, compromised sleep exacerbates daytime sleepiness, reduces cognitive vigilance, and impairs attentional processes, directly undermining AP [16]. These effects may be even more pronounced among nursing students, who often contend with irregular clinical schedules and substantial intellectual demands.

Taken together, these findings highlight a complex chain of relationships in which SA indirectly impacts AP through the deterioration of SQ. This model enriches traditional frameworks for understanding student success by integrating psychotechnical factors often overlooked in educational research. It also underscores the importance of adopting integrative approaches that draw on theories of academic engagement, techno-stress, and circadian regulation to more effectively address these multifaceted dynamics.

Strengths and limitations

This study is characterized by a large sample size (N = 451), the exhaustive inclusion of nursing students (an underrepresented population in Moroccan research), and the use of internationally validated instruments (SAS-SV, PSQI), which reinforce its methodological rigor. The application of structural equation modeling further enabled a nuanced assessment of both direct and mediated pathways between smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and academic performance. Robustness was additionally ensured through systematic diagnostic checks of reliability, validity, multicollinearity, and common method bias.

Nonetheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The predominance of female participants (68.7%) and the focus on a single nursing institute may restrict generalizability, despite the exhaustive sampling strategy. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference and leaves open the possibility of reverse causality (e.g., poor performance leading to increased smartphone use). Additional confounders such as stress, workload, or socioeconomic status were not assessed, and only sleep quality was tested as a mediator. Academic performance was measured solely via GPA, which may not fully capture practical nursing skills.

Finally, while qualitative methods were not incorporated, this omission does not compromise the validity of the findings, as the primary objective was to quantitatively test mediation using PLS-SEM. However, future research may gain additional depth by adopting longitudinal or mixed-method designs, thereby complementing statistical modeling with insights into students’ lived experiences.

Implications of the study

The findings of this study contribute meaningfully to the advancement of knowledge on the relationships between smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and academic performance in the nursing education context. Theoretically, they confirm and extend existing models in addiction psychology and behavioral health by highlighting the disruptive effect of excessive smartphone use on biological rhythms and cognitive functioning. By demonstrating the mediating role of sleep quality between smartphone addiction and academic performance, the study clarifies a central explanatory mechanism consistent with self-regulation and cognitive overload theories, reinforcing the importance of sleep as a key determinant of educational success and an essential component of integrated behavioral health models. Furthermore, the results provide theoretical support for models of techno-stress and compensatory use, showing how problematic smartphone behaviors indirectly undermine academic outcomes through impaired sleep. By incorporating sleep quality as a mediator, the study advances understanding of the pathways through which digital overuse affects student performance, thereby contributing to both cognitive-behavioral frameworks and educational psychology.

From a practical perspective, the results underscore the need for nursing schools and faculties to implement institutional policies and awareness campaigns promoting more mindful smartphone use, particularly at night, and to design interventions that improve sleep hygiene through workshops, training programs, or time management coaching. In particular, the IPMA results highlight that improving sleep quality may yield greater gains in AP than focusing solely on smartphone use reduction, emphasizing the need for targeted sleep hygiene strategies. Such initiatives, by directly enhancing sleep quality, could indirectly optimize academic performance while providing a strong basis for integrating smartphone use and its impact on sleep into university counseling and psychological support systems.

Ultimately, the study provides both theoretical insights into the mechanisms linking digital behaviors, sleep, and learning, and actionable recommendations that can inform policy, curriculum design, and student counseling services aimed at improving well-being, AP, and professional preparedness in nursing education.

Conclusion

This study confirms that smartphone addiction is a significant risk factor for the academic performance of nursing students, partly through its detrimental impact on sleep quality. The application of the partial least squares structural equation modeling model allowed for the identification of a partial mediating effect of sleep, emphasizing the importance of addressing digital behaviors in strategies aimed at promoting academic success.

The findings highlight the high prevalence of risk behaviors within this population and underscore the need for integrated interventions that combine education on digital well-being, sleep hygiene, and psychosocial support. This work offers concrete avenues for adapting nursing curricula to contemporary challenges in digital health while also laying the groundwork for future longitudinal studies that could provide more robust explanatory insights.
