Serum metabolomics profiling in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma: a multicentre diagnostic study
Authors: Hexin Ma, Xinran Zhao, Zhihui Li, Zhen Zhang, Xinhua Pan, Mengxuan Du, Qingqing Wan, Chaojun Zhang, Xu Wang, Hongyu Zhao, Zhien Feng, Xin Zhang, Wan-Tao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundHead and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages, when outcomes are poor. Biomarkers for HNSCC detection remain needed.MethodsWe performed serum metabolomics profiling in 938 participants, including 457 patients with HNSCC, 451 controls, and 30 patients with oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs). Discovery and external validation cohorts were analysed using LC-MS/MS. Machine learning-based feature selection was used to develop an 8-metabolite diagnostic model. Disease specificity was assessed in an independent third-centre cohort. Multi-omics analyses integrating TCGA transcriptomics and spatial transcriptomics data were used to explore molecular heterogeneity. Study registration: ChiCTR-BRC-17014040.FindingsSerum metabolomics profiling showed altered arginine metabolism, characterised by arginine decrease and ornithine accumulation. The 8-metabolite model achieved an internal validation AUC of 0.990, with 91.3% sensitivity and 97.1% specificity. In the multicentre external validation cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 0.901, with 91.1% sensitivity and 80.2% specificity, and maintained performance in Stage I disease, with an AUC of 0.927. In the independent disease-control cohort, the model distinguished HNSCC from healthy controls with an AUC of 0.980 and showed moderate discrimination between HNSCC and OPMDs, with an AUC of 0.710. Multi-omics analyses identified two candidate molecular subtypes with distinct immune features, prognosis, TP53 mutation status, and predicted treatment sensitivities. Spatial transcriptomics supported an inverse association between metabolic score and immune infiltration.InterpretationThis multicentre study identifies a serum metabolomics signature associated with HNSCC detection and molecular stratification. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Web of Science for articles published before April 2026 using the terms “head and neck squamous cell carcinoma” AND (“metabolomics” OR “metabolite biomarker”) AND (“serum” OR “plasma” OR “blood”). Previous studies identified candidate serum biomarkers but were limited by small sample sizes, single-centre designs without independent external validation, and reliance on relative quantification without analytically validated targeted assays. No prior study combined large-scale multicentre discovery with independent cross-platform validation using isotope-labelled internal standard-based targeted quantification in HNSCC.

Added value of this study

This study enrolled 938 participants across three cohorts from three centres. We identified an 8-metabolite diagnostic signature and validated it in an independent multicentre cohort (AUC = 0.901), with consistent performance for Stage I disease (AUC = 0.927). Critically, a purpose-built targeted LC-MS/MS assay with stable isotope-labelled internal standards confirmed diagnostic generalisability in a third-centre cohort (AUC = 0.980), including discrimination against oral potentially malignant disorders. Paired tissue metabolomics provided mechanistic evidence linking serum alterations to tumour-intrinsic metabolic activity. Exploratory multi-omics integration identified two candidate molecular subtypes with distinct prognoses and therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Implications of all the available evidence

The 8-metabolite panel, validated across independent centres and analytical platforms, supports further evaluation as a candidate minimally invasive triage tool for HNSCC, particularly for early-stage detection. Prospective validation in real-world screening populations encompassing diverse ethnicities and broader disease controls is essential before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) ranks as the sixth most common malignancy worldwide, affecting more than 890,000 patients annually and causing approximately 450,000 deaths.1,2 Notably, the burden of HNSCC is not evenly distributed: incidence is approximately 2.5-fold higher in males than in females, shows a strong socioeconomic gradient that is not entirely explained by tobacco and alcohol exposure,3 and varies substantially by geographic region, with the highest rates observed in South and Southeast Asia, Eastern Europe, and parts of South America.2 Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, 5-year survival remains below 50%, largely because most patients present with locoregionally advanced disease at diagnosis3 and show marked variability in treatment response.4, 5, 6, 7 Prognosis is highly stage-dependent, with 5-year survival exceeding 80% for localised tumours but declining to approximately 40% in advanced stages.8 This variability reflects underlying molecular heterogeneity9 and underscores the urgent need for biomarker-driven approaches that support both earlier detection and more precise therapeutic strategies.

Current diagnostic approaches face critical limitations. Tissue biopsy, whilst the gold standard, requires invasive sampling and may miss intratumoral heterogeneity, especially in anatomically challenging sites.3 Liquid biopsy approaches such as circulating tumour DNA analysis show promise but generally achieve 65–80% sensitivity in advanced HNSCC and suboptimal performance in early disease.10 These constraints highlight the unmet clinical need for innovative, minimally invasive tools that improve early detection and enable patient stratification.

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, shaping tumour growth, progression, and immune evasion.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Metabolomics offers unique advantages as a minimally invasive approach that captures dynamic, systemic metabolic alterations in real time, making it particularly suitable for early detection and for exploring tumour heterogeneity.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Unlike static genomic or transcriptomic profiling, metabolomics directly reflects functional states and may uncover therapeutically actionable vulnerabilities.

Previous metabolomics studies in HNSCC have provided insights into potential biomarkers,24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 but their clinical translation has been limited by several persistent barriers: (i) small, single-centre cohorts; (ii) absence of independent external validation; (iii) reliance on single-omics data without mechanistic contextualisation through multi-omics integration, limiting biological interpretability. To address these gaps, comprehensive studies combining large multicentre cohorts, fully independent external validation, multi-omics integration, and stage-stratified performance evaluation are needed to establish clinically robust diagnostic signatures and uncover biologically meaningful subtypes that explain patient heterogeneity.

In this study, we sought to address these limitations by performing a large-scale serum metabolomics analysis of HNSCC, enrolling 938 participants across discovery and multicentre validation cohorts. Machine learning-based analyses identified an 8-metabolite diagnostic signature with high discriminative accuracy in case-control settings, particularly for early-stage disease, with consistent performance across independent patient populations. Recognising that accurate diagnosis alone does not resolve clinical heterogeneity, we extended the analysis through metabolomics-guided multi-omics integration, identifying candidate molecular subtypes with divergent prognoses, immune landscapes, and predicted therapeutic vulnerabilities. Together, these findings provide a foundation for further development of metabolomics-based diagnostic and stratification approaches in HNSCC, pending prospective clinical validation.

Methods

Study cohorts and sample collection

This multicentre, retrospective study enrolled a total of 938 participants across three independent centres between October 2015 and September 2025, comprising 457 patients with histopathologically confirmed HNSCC and 451 healthy controls (HC). An additional 30 patients with oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) were included in Cohort 3 for disease-control validation.

Cohort 1 was recruited from the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial-Head and Neck Oncology, Ninth People's Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Shanghai, China) between October 2015 and December 2024, and comprised 498 participants: 248 patients with HNSCC (median age: 62 years; 65.3% male) and 250 HC. Tumour staging among HNSCC cases was as follows: Stage I (n = 41), Stage II (n = 65), Stage III (n = 67), and Stage IV (n = 75), according to the TNM staging system. All serum samples were collected on the morning of scheduled surgery, between 7:00 and 9:00 AM after overnight fasting, and before anaesthesia induction. All patients with HNSCC were treatment-naïve at the time of blood collection, having received no prior anticancer therapy. Histopathological confirmation was obtained postoperatively through examination of surgical specimens by two independent pathologists. This cohort was used for biomarker discovery and internal validation.

Cohort 2 was obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Zhengzhou, China) between January 2022 and September 2024, representing an independent patient population, and included 171 participants: 90 patients with HNSCC (median age: 62.5 years; 62.2% male) and 81 HC, serving as the external validation cohort. Tumour staging among HNSCC cases was as follows: Stage I (n = 20), Stage II (n = 21), Stage III (n = 28), and Stage IV (n = 21), according to the TNM staging system. Serum samples were collected following the identical standardised protocol as Cohort 1.

Cohort 3 was assembled from Beijing Stomatological Hospital, Capital Medical University (Beijing, China) between December 2023 and September 2025 as an independent disease-control validation cohort, and comprised 269 participants: 119 patients with HNSCC, 120 age- and sex-matched HC, and 30 patients with OPMDs. The OPMDs group included patients with oral leukoplakia, erosive and non-erosive oral lichen planus, oral papilloma, and oral submucous fibrosis, analysed as a single pooled group owing to insufficient sample sizes for individual subtype analysis. Serum samples were collected following the identical standardised protocol as Cohorts 1 and 2. Serum concentrations of the eight diagnostic metabolites were quantified using the validated targeted LC-MS/MS assay described in the targeted metabolomics validation section. This cohort was used for disease-control validation to assess the specificity of the 8-metabolite signature against clinically relevant non-malignant oral conditions.

Inclusion criteria for patients with HNSCC were: (i) availability of tissue samples obtained via surgical resection or biopsy; (ii) histopathological confirmation of squamous cell carcinoma by two independent pathologists; and (iii) no anti-cancer therapy prior to sample collection. Exclusion criteria included (i) uncontrolled systemic diseases (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, thyroid dysfunction): that could significantly alter metabolic profiles; (ii) other concurrent malignancies; and (iii) absence of written informed consent.

Healthy controls were recruited during routine health examinations. Exclusion criteria were identical to those applied to patients with HNSCC, with the additional requirement of no prior history of malignancy.

Serum sample collection

Peripheral blood samples were collected after overnight fasting. Blood was drawn into vacuum tubes containing separating gel and clot activator. Within 2 h of collection, samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Serum was immediately aliquoted into cryovials and stored at −80 °C until metabolite extraction. Each aliquot underwent no more than two freeze-thaw cycles before analysis, consistent with evidence that limited freeze-thaw cycles have minimal impact on targeted serum metabolite concentrations.31

Metabolite extraction

For each serum sample, 40 μL was mixed with 280 μL of pre-chilled methanol: acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). After vortexing for 1 min and incubation on ice for 10 min, samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling 5 μL of serum from each sample and processed identically. QC samples were inserted at regular intervals (every 10 study samples) throughout the analytical sequence to monitor instrument stability and signal drift.

Widely-targeted metabolomics analysis

Widely-targeted metabolomics profiling was performed using liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS, AB SCIEX QTRAP 4500) following established protocols described in the literature.2,32 Samples were injected in randomised order. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters) at a column temperature of 30 °C and autosampler temperature of 4 °C, with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min and injection volume of 1 μL. Mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile).

Elution gradient: 0–2 min, 100% A; 2.1–14 min, linear to 5% A; 14.1–16 min, 5% A; 16–16.1 min, returned to 100% A; 16.1–20 min, 100% A.

Metabolites were quantified in scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Source parameters were as follows: curtain gas (CUR) 35 psi, collision gas (CAD) 9, ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V (positive ion mode), ion source temperature (TEM) 550 °C, nebuliser gas (GS1) 40 psi, auxiliary gas (GS2) 60 psi, entrance potential (EP) 10 V, and collision cell exit potential (CXP) 10 V. Raw data (.wiff format) were processed using SCIEX OS 1.6 for peak identification and peak area integration. Metabolites with detection rates ≥80% were retained, yielding 183 reliably quantified metabolites. Missing values were imputed with a baseline intensity of 1000, approximating the instrument noise floor, consistent with recommended practice for left-censored data in targeted metabolomics.32, 33, 34

Data normalisation and preprocessing

Pooled QC samples were inserted at regular intervals (every 10 study samples) throughout the analytical sequence to monitor instrument stability. A two-step QC-based normalisation approach was applied32 within each cohort:

(i)Batch effect correction: for each metabolite, the mean intensity across all QC samples within each analytical batch was calculated. Scaling factors were derived as the ratio of the global QC mean to the batch-specific QC mean, and applied to all study samples within that batch to correct for batch-specific signal drift.

This study employs a discovery–validation design: Cohort 1 served as the discovery cohort for biomarker identification, feature selection, and model training, whilst Cohorts 2 and 3 served as fully independent external validation cohorts. The diagnostic model was trained exclusively on Cohort 1 data and applied to Cohorts 2 and 3 without re-training, recalibration, or parameter adjustment. In this framework, cross-cohort statistical harmonisation methods (e.g., ComBat) were not applied, as these are designed for pooled analyses and risk removing genuine biological variation along with technical artefacts. Instead, per-cohort independent Z-score normalisation was applied: for each metabolite, the mean and standard deviation were calculated within each cohort separately, and Z-scores were computed independently. This approach preserves the relative metabolic perturbation patterns within each cohort whilst enabling direct application of the trained model to new cohorts. To assess potential centre-related effects following normalisation, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the normalised metabolomics data using MetaboAnalyst 6.0.

The same sample preparation, LC-MS/MS acquisition, and data processing pipeline was applied uniformly to all samples irrespective of participant sex, age, or disease status.

Metabolite differential analysis

In Cohort 1 (248 HNSCC, 250 HC), 183 serum metabolites were evaluated for differential abundance. Data were log10-transformed and analysed on the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform. Differential metabolites were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and fold change (FC) > 1.2 or < 0.83.

Hierarchical clustering was performed with the ClusterGVis R package (v0.1.2). KEGG pathway enrichment of significantly altered metabolites was conducted using Fisher's exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (P < 0.05).

Diagnostic model construction

A diagnostic model was developed using random forest with LASSO-based feature selection. Cohort 1 (n = 498) was randomly divided into a training set (n = 348) and a test set (n = 150) (set.seed = 123, 7:3 ratio) (Supplementary Data 1).

Feature selection. LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was performed using the glmnet R package (v4.1.8), with family = binomial, alpha = 1, and no intercept. The optimal regularisation parameter (lambda.min) was selected, identifying 43 metabolites with non-zero coefficients. Recursive feature elimination with random forest importance ranking (rfFuncs) was subsequently applied using the caret R package, evaluating subsets of 1–8 features to iteratively refine the feature set, yielding an optimal panel of eight metabolites.

Model training and evaluation. Seven machine learning algorithms, namely Naïve Bayes, AdaBoost classification trees, nearest centroid classifier (cancerclass), random forest, boosted logistic regression (LogiBoost), weighted k-nearest neighbour classifier (kknn), and support vector machines with class weights (svmRadialWeights), were implemented using the Mime R package (v0.13).35 Each algorithm outputs a continuous probability score (range 0–1) representing the predicted likelihood of HNSCC. For model comparison, a default classification threshold of 0.5 was applied, with samples scoring >0.5 classified as HNSCC and those ≤0.5 as controls. Random forest was selected as the final classifier based on superior discriminative performance. The model was trained with 10-fold cross-validation using AUC as the optimisation metric (trainControl: method = cv, number = 10, classProbs = TRUE, summaryFunction = twoClassSummary) and hyperparameter tuning of the mtry parameter via the caret R package.

Validation and performance metrics. Internal validation was performed on the held-out test set from Cohort 1 (n = 150). External validation was conducted using the independent Cohort 2 (n = 171). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and AUC were computed with the ROCR package (v1.0.11). Sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated with the pROC package (v1.18.5). The optimal classification threshold for reporting sensitivity and specificity was determined independently for each cohort by maximising the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1). Feature importance scores were derived from the Gini impurity index using the randomForest package (v4.7.1.2).

Data visualisation. Heatmaps of metabolite expression patterns were generated with ComplexHeatmap (v2.15.4), and additional figures were created using ggplot2 (v3.5.1).

Chemical descriptor computation and feature engineering

A complementary model was constructed using computed molecular descriptors rather than metabolite concentrations, serving as a robustness check for the diagnostic signature. For each of the eight diagnostic metabolites, 263 one-dimensional and two-dimensional molecular descriptors and 1190 molecular fingerprint bits (1024-bit ECFP4 and 166-bit MACCS keys) were computed from canonical SMILES structures using the rcdk R package (v3.8.2),36, 37, 38 yielding 1453 descriptors per metabolite. A chemically informed feature matrix was then constructed by multiplying the Z-score-normalised abundance of each metabolite by its corresponding descriptor values, generating 11,455 features per sample.39

A multi-stage feature selection strategy was applied exclusively to the training set (n = 349): (i) variance filtering (threshold ≤1 × 10−10) retained 1218 features; (ii) random forest importance-based pre-screening retained the top 200 features; (iii) recursive feature elimination with 10-fold cross-validation (RFECV) identified an optimal subset of 100 chemical descriptors.40,41 A random forest classifier (ntree = 500, mtry optimised via grid search, 10-fold stratified cross-validation) was trained on these 100 features using the caret R package (v7.0.1). Internal validation was performed on the held-out test set (n = 149), and external validation on the independent Cohort 2 (n = 171). This descriptor-based model served as a robustness check and does not replace the concentration-based primary model.

Targeted metabolomics validation (cohort 3)

A targeted LC-MS/MS assay with stable isotope-labelled internal standards was developed for absolute quantification of the eight diagnostic metabolites. The assay was performed on an AB SCIEX QTRAP 4500 system operating in scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with electrospray ionisation in positive mode (ESI+). Each of the eight analytes was paired with a stable isotope-labelled internal standard (13C or deuterium-labelled; purity ≥98%). Two MRM transitions (quantifier and qualifier) were monitored per analyte and per internal standard. Metabolite identity was confirmed at Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) Level 1 through retention time matching (±0.2 min),42 MRM transition ratio matching (qualifier/quantifier within ±20%), and standard addition experiments in pooled serum matrix.43

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 100 × 2.1 mm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water containing 5 mM ammonium acetate and 0.3% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile), delivered at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The gradient programme was as follows: 0–1.4 min, 1–2% B; 1.4–4 min, 2–65% B; 4–4.5 min, 65–95% B; 4.5–5.7 min, held at 95% B; 5.8–7.5 min, returned to and held at 2% B for column re-equilibration. The autosampler temperature was set at 15 °C. Injection volumes were optimised per analyte: 2 μL for l-glutamic acid and hypoxanthine, and 5 μL for l-cystine, p-aminobenzoic acid, N-acetylcysteine, l-arginine, choline, and glycerophosphocholine. Needle rinse was performed with 50% methanol between injections. Mass spectrometer source parameters were set as follows: curtain gas (CUR) 25 psi, ion source gas 1 (GS1) 50 psi, ion source gas 2 (GS2) 50 psi, IonSpray voltage (ISVF) 5500 V, source temperature (TEM) 550 °C, collision gas (CAD) 9, entrance potential (EP) 10 V, and collision cell exit potential (CXP) 12 V.

For sample preparation, 30 μL of serum (or calibration standard) was aliquoted into a microcentrifuge tube, followed by addition of 23 μL of mixed isotope-labelled internal standard solution (5 μg/mL). Protein precipitation was performed by adding 60 μL of methanol–acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). After vortexing for 1 min, samples were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials for injection.

Calibration curves were constructed using 1/x2-weighted linear regression with 6–8 calibration points per analyte. Serum samples were prepared by spiking internal standards prior to protein precipitation. Pooled QC samples were inserted every 10 study samples, and solvent blanks were included to monitor carry-over. Complete analytical validation parameters (linearity, LOD, LLOQ, precision, accuracy, matrix effects, and freeze-thaw stability), assessed following established frameworks for targeted metabolomics bioanalytical validation,44,45 are reported in Tables S1, S2 and Supplementary Data 2–6.

The validated assay was applied to the independent Cohort 3 from Capital Medical University (Beijing), comprising 119 patients with HNSCC, 120 HC, and 30 patients with OPMDs. Per-cohort independent Z-score normalisation was applied as described in the Data normalisation and preprocessing section, and the trained random forest model in Diagnostic model construction was applied without re-training or recalibration. For metabolite concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD), per-metabolite median imputation was applied prior to normalisation.

Tissue metabolomic validation

To validate the tissue-level origin of the serum metabolic alterations, we performed targeted metabolomic profiling on 61 paired tumour and adjacent non-tumour tissue samples from Cohort 1 patients. Adjacent non-tumour tissue was obtained at a minimum distance of 2 cm from the tumour margin and confirmed histologically to be free of dysplasia or malignancy. Tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 30 min of surgical excision and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Targeted quantification of the eight diagnostic metabolites was performed using the validated LC-MS/MS assay described in the targeted metabolomics validation section. Metabolite concentrations (μg/mL) were determined using isotope-labelled internal standard calibration. Tissue metabolite content was normalised to tissue wet weight and expressed as ng/mg tissue, calculated as: concentration (μg/mL) × extraction volume (0.423 mL) × 1000/tissue wet weight (mg). Paired comparisons between tumour and adjacent tissue were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing.

Cross-cancer validation of diagnostic metabolites

To evaluate the cross-cancer relevance of the diagnostic signature, the eight metabolites were assessed in publicly available blood metabolomics datasets for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC),46 colorectal cancer (CRC),47 and gastric cancer (GC).32 Performance metrics were calculated using MetaboAnalyst 6.0.48

Consensus clustering of multi-omics data

RNA-seq counts and clinical data for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), ESCC, and cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Data were converted to TPM and log2(TPM+1). Consensus clustering was conducted using ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.54.0),49, 50, 51 with k = 2–6, Ward.D2 linkage, 80% resampling, and 100 iterations. Heatmaps were visualised with pheatmap (v1.0.12). For each validation cohort, the optimal cluster number was independently determined based on consensus matrix stability and CDF delta area curves, allowing for cohort-specific variation in subtype number.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed using GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn)52 and the Kaplan–Meier Plotter tool,53 focussing on candidate genes within TCGA cohorts.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between C1 and C2 were identified with the limma package (v3.64.1) (|log2FC| > 1, adj. P < 0.05). KEGG pathway enrichment was performed using ClusterProfiler (v4.0.3) with BH correction.

Expression analysis of m6A regulators

The expression of m6A RNA methylation regulators was analysed in TCGA-HNSC, based on the gene list from Li et al.54 Expression differences between C1 and C2 were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Mutation profiling

Mutation annotation files (MAF) for TCGA-HNSC were downloaded from the GDC portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Samples were classified into C1 and C2 subtypes as described above. Mutation frequencies of recurrent driver genes were compared using chi-square tests.

Immune dysfunction assessment

Immune evasion was quantified using the Tumour Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm55 across HNSC, LUSC, ESCC, and CSCC cohorts. Subtype differences were compared with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Immune cell infiltration assessment

Immune cell composition across four squamous carcinoma cohorts (TCGA-HNSC, LUSC, ESCC, and CSCC) was estimated using the CIBERSORTx algorithm,56 covering 22 immune cell types. Subtype-specific infiltration profiles were visualised using radar plots to highlight differences in immune landscapes among subtypes.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis

Spatial transcriptomics data for HNSCC (GSE220978; n = 4 tissue samples)57 were analysed with Cottrazm (v0.1.1).58 A metabolic gene panel score was calculated by ssGSEA (GSVA v1.50.5). Immune cell composition was estimated with RCTD in doublet mode. Spatial correlations between metabolic gene expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation.

Chemotherapeutic sensitivity prediction

Chemotherapeutic response (cisplatin, gemcitabine, docetaxel, paclitaxel) was estimated using pRRophetic with TCGA-HNSC transcriptomes and GDSC reference data.59 Ridge regression60,61 was applied to predict IC50 values, with batch effects corrected using ComBat. To experimentally assess the predicted drug sensitivity differences between molecular subtypes, we classified HNSC cell lines using a nearest centroid approach. Transcriptomic data for 23 HNSCC cell lines were obtained from the GDSC and DepMap databases,62,63 and expression profiles of the 16-gene metabolic panel were extracted. C1 and C2 centroid vectors were computed from the TCGA-HNSC consensus clustering results, representing the mean expression of each gene within each subtype. Each cell line was assigned to the subtype whose centroid showed higher Pearson correlation with its expression profile.64

Two representative cell lines, CAL-27 (C1-like) and SCC-25 (C2-like), were selected for in vitro drug sensitivity testing. The human oral squamous cell carcinoma cell lines CAL-27 (RRID: CVCL_1107) and SCC-25 (RRID: CVCL_1682) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). CAL-27 cells were cultured in DMEM (BasalMedia, China) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco-BRL, USA), penicillin (100 units/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL). SCC-25 cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 (BasalMedia, China) supplemented with the same additives. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 and 95% air.

Neither cell line is listed in the ICLAC register of misidentified cell lines. Cell line identity was confirmed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. The STR profiles matched the corresponding reference profiles. Mycoplasma contamination was tested using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector kit (Vazyme, D101-01), and all cell lines tested negative.

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with serial dilutions of cisplatin (0–300 μM), gemcitabine (0–100 μM), paclitaxel (0–300 μM), and docetaxel (0–300 μM) for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, C0038). IC50 values were calculated by fitting dose–response curves to a four-parameter log-logistic model using GraphPad Prism v9.0. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) unless otherwise stated. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test where applicable. For two-group comparisons, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-normally distributed data; for comparisons involving three or more groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied followed by Dunn's post hoc test. Correlations between variables were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method, with statistical significance defined as P or FDR <0.05. No formal sample size calculation was performed. Sample sizes were determined by the availability of eligible participants during the recruitment period. No class imbalance correction was applied, as the discovery cohort had a balanced case-to-control ratio. Model fairness was assessed through pre-specified subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, and tumour stage; no algorithmic fairness constraints were applied as the model input comprised objective metabolite measurements independent of demographic variables.

Diagnostic model performance was evaluated using ROC curve analysis with area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the clinical net benefit of the diagnostic model across a range of threshold probabilities. Positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV) were calculated at disease prevalence rates of 1%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 50% using Bayes' theorem.

Machine learning analyses, including LASSO regression, random forest classification, recursive feature elimination, and consensus clustering, were performed in R (v4.3.2) with the specific packages and parameters detailed in the respective Methods subsections. Multi-omics bioinformatics analyses were conducted on the HOME for Researchers platform (https://www.home-for-researchers.com). Dose–response curves for in vitro drug sensitivity experiments were fitted using a four-parameter log-logistic model in GraphPad Prism (v9.0). All other statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.2). The specific statistical test used for each comparison is indicated in the corresponding figure legend.

Study population and metabolomics profiling quality control

To develop a serum metabolomics-based diagnostic signature for HNSCC, we recruited 938 participants across three independent cohorts: a discovery cohort (Cohort 1: n = 498; 248 HNSCC, 250 HC), and external validation cohort (Cohort 2: n = 171; 90 HNSCC, 81 HC), and a disease-control validation cohort (Cohort 3: n = 269; 119 HNSCC, 120 HC) (Fig. 1). An additional 30 patients with OPMDs were included in Cohort 3 to assess diagnostic specificity against non-malignant oral conditions. Demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of patients with HNSCC across the three cohorts are summarised in Table 1. Although the distributions of sex, age, tumour location, histological grade, and clinical stage differed significantly among cohorts (all P < 0.05), reflecting the distinct referral patterns of each centre, all major tumour subsites and disease stages were represented in each cohort, ensuring adequate coverage for model training and validation. The sex distribution of patients with HNSCC and HC within each cohort is shown in Figure S1A.

To characterise the patterns of metabolic changes across disease stages, we performed clustering analysis, revealing three distinct metabolic profiles (Fig. 3B): progressive decrease (Cluster 1), early elevation followed by decline (Cluster 2), and progressive accumulation (Cluster 3). These patterns suggest stage-dependent metabolic adaptation during tumour development (Figure S2C).

Pathway enrichment analysis identified the major metabolic pathways associated with these alterations. Arginine biosynthesis emerged as the top dysregulated pathway (P < 0.001; Fig. 3C, Supplementary Data 9). Serum l-arginine was decreased whilst ornithine accumulated (Figure S1B), indicating enhanced arginase-mediated arginine consumption. Proteomic validation from the Clinical Proteomic Tumour Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) HNSCC cohort supported this metabolic shift, showing upregulated arginase-1 and downregulated arginine biosynthetic enzymes (ASS1, ASL) in tumour tissues (Figure S2A and B). Additional pathways including pantothenate/CoA biosynthesis and glycerophospholipid metabolism were also perturbed, indicating coordinated metabolic reprogramming beyond arginine metabolism.

Machine learning identifies an 8-metabolite diagnostic signature

To translate these findings into a diagnostic tool, we applied systematic feature selection (LASSO regression and recursive feature elimination), identifying an optimal 8-metabolite panel: choline, p-aminobenzoic acid, l-glutamic acid, glycerophosphocholine, hypoxanthine, cystine, l-arginine, and acetylcysteine (Fig. 4A and B, Figure S3A–C). Random forest modelling achieved the strongest performance among seven algorithms tested (Figure S3D), with 10-fold cross-validation confirming reliability (mean AUC = 0.985; Figure S3E).

To evaluate the model's performance, we first applied the 8-metabolite diagnostic model (8-DM) to the training set (n = 348). The model achieved an AUC of 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000), with a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 100%, accurately classifying 100% of participants (Figure S4A and B). In the internal test set (n = 150), the model maintained high performance (AUC = 0.990, 95% CI: 0.981–1.000, sensitivity = 91.3%, specificity = 97.1%), correctly identifying 93.8% of all participants (Figure S4C and D). Predicted probability distributions demonstrated clear separation between HNSCC and controls across Cohort 1 and Cohort 2 (Fig. 4E and F). In the external validation set (Cohort 2, n = 171), the model achieved an AUC of 0.901 (95% CI: 0.853–0.950, sensitivity of 91.1%, specificity of 80.2%), and accurately classified 86% of participants (Fig. 4C).

Given the critical importance of early detection in HNSCC for improving survival outcomes, we further assessed the 8-DM model's performance in distinguishing Stage I HNSCC from HC. In Cohort 1 (discovery, n = 498), the model achieved strong discrimination (AUC = 1.000, 95% CI: 0.999–1.000; sensitivity = 100%, specificity = 97.1%), correctly identifying 99.3% of patients with Stage I (Figure S5A and C). In Cohort 2 (external validation, n = 171), the model maintained good discriminative performance (AUC = 0.927, 95% CI: 0.878–0.976; sensitivity = 100.0%, specificity = 80.2%) (Figure S5B and D). Individual metabolite patterns showed progressive stage-specific changes consistent with the diagnostic signature (Fig. 5A–H).

Targeted validation and clinical performance of the 8-metabolite signature

To assess the generalisability of the 8-DM across clinically relevant subgroups, we performed stratified analyses in Cohort 2. The model maintained consistent performance across sex (male AUC = 0.901, female AUC = 0.902), age groups (age <60: AUC = 0.927; age ≥60: AUC = 0.882), and tumour subsites (tongue AUC = 0.885, gingiva AUC = 0.898, buccal AUC = 0.925, palate AUC = 0.910, oropharynx AUC = 0.853). Stage-stratified analysis confirmed robust discrimination across all clinical stages (Stage I: AUC = 0.927; Stage II: AUC = 0.837; Stage III: AUC = 0.912; Stage IV: AUC = 0.928) (Figure S5E, Table S3).

To establish an analytically validated quantification method suitable for independent replication, we developed a dedicated targeted LC-MS/MS assay with stable isotope-labelled internal standards for absolute quantification of the eight diagnostic metabolites. All eight analytes demonstrated linearity with R2 > 0.99, with calibration ranges covering the expected physiological concentrations in human serum (Figure S6). Metabolite identity was confirmed at MSI Level 1 through retention time matching, MRM transition ratio matching, and standard addition experiments using certified reference standards (≥98% purity) (Figure S7).

Using this validated targeted assay, we analysed an independent disease-control validation cohort (Cohort 3, n = 269) recruited from a third centre, comprising patients with HNSCC (n = 119), HC (n = 120), and patients with OPMDs (n = 30). The 8-DM model, originally trained on Cohort 1 widely-targeted metabolomics data, was applied to the Cohort 3 targeted quantification data without any retraining or parameter adjustment. The model achieved an AUC of 0.980 (95% CI: 0.965–0.995, sensitivity 91.6%, specificity 95.0%) for discriminating HNSCC from HC, confirming that the diagnostic signature generalises across both independent populations and analytical platforms (Fig. 4D–G). The model also maintained discrimination for early-stage disease, achieving an AUC of 0.988 (95% CI: 0.975–1.000) for Stage I HNSCC versus HC in Cohort 3 (Figure S8A and B), whilst moderate discrimination was observed between HNSCC and OPMDs (AUC = 0.710, 95% CI: 0.611–0.808; Figure S8C and D). Predicted HNSCC probabilities showed a progressive stepwise increase from HC (median 0.154, IQR 0.054–0.329) through OPMDs (median 0.698, IQR 0.501–0.769) to HNSCC (median 0.778, IQR 0.687–0.856; Kruskal–Wallis P < 0.001; Figure S8E), consistent with a metabolic gradient along the oral carcinogenesis continuum.

As an additional test of model robustness, we constructed an alternative classifier using computed molecular chemical descriptors (Chemistry Development Kit, rcdk v3.8.2) rather than metabolite concentrations. SMILES structures for all eight metabolites were retrieved from PubChem and cross-validated against HMDB (Table S4). A chemically informed feature matrix was generated by multiplying normalised abundances by their corresponding 1D/2D molecular descriptors and fingerprint bits, yielding 11,455 initial features. After multi-stage feature selection (variance filtering, importance ranking, and recursive feature elimination with 10-fold cross-validation), a final set of 100 chemical descriptors was retained (Supplementary Data 10, Figure S9A–D). The descriptor-based random forest model achieved an AUC of 0.976 (95% CI: 0.954–0.998) in the internal test set and 0.932 (95% CI: 0.895–0.970) in Cohort 2 (Figure S9E–F). In head-to-head comparison on Cohort 2, the descriptor model yielded higher AUC (0.932 versus 0.901) and specificity (87.7% versus 80.2%), whilst the concentration-based model showed higher sensitivity (91.1% versus 87.8%) (Figure S9G). The concordance between two fundamentally different modelling frameworks, one based on metabolite abundances and the other on computed physicochemical properties, reinforces the biological validity of the 8-metabolite diagnostic signature.

Finally, we evaluated the clinical utility of the 8-DM using prevalence-adjusted predictive values and decision curve analysis based on Cohort 3 targeted quantification data (sensitivity 91.6%, specificity 95.0%). At a screening-relevant prevalence of 5% (high-risk population), the model achieved a PPV of 49.1% and NPV of 99.5%; at a prevalence of 10%, PPV increased to 67.1% with NPV of 99.0% (Table S5). Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the 8-DM provided net clinical benefit exceeding both “treat all” and “treat none” strategies across threshold probabilities from 0.01 to 0.80 (Figure S10), supporting its potential utility as a triage tool (NPV ≥99.0% at prevalence ≤10%).

To investigate whether the serum metabolic alterations captured by the 8-DM signature reflect tumour-intrinsic metabolic reprogramming, we performed targeted metabolomic profiling on 61 paired tumour and adjacent non-tumour tissue samples. Of the eight diagnostic metabolites, three showed statistically significant differences between tumour and adjacent tissue after multiple testing correction (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, BH-adjusted P < 0.05; Figure S11; Supplementary Data 11). l-Arginine was significantly elevated in tumour tissue compared with adjacent normal tissue (median: 1514.61 vs 1210.92 ng/mg tissue; fold change = 1.25; adjusted P = 2.78 × 10−4), as were choline (FC = 1.23; adjusted P = 0.014) and glycerophosphocholine (FC = 1.71; adjusted P = 0.014). l-Glutamic acid showed a borderline trend toward tumour elevation (FC = 1.11; adjusted P = 0.071). Notably, these three significantly elevated metabolites (l-arginine, choline, and glycerophosphocholine) were all decreased in serum of patients with HNSCC, revealing a “tumour retention” pattern characterised by local accumulation within tumour tissue and concurrent systemic decrease. For l-arginine, this is further supported by our proteomic finding of elevated ARG1 expression in tumour tissue (Figure S2). l-Cystine showed a non-significant decrease in tumour tissue (FC = 0.80; adjusted P = 0.349), consistent with rapid intracellular reduction to cysteine via xCT/SLC7A11 for glutathione synthesis. These tissue-level metabolic data provide direct evidence that the serum 8-DM diagnostic signature captures tumour-derived metabolic signals rather than non-specific systemic perturbations (Figure S11; Supplementary Data 11).

Cross-cancer validation and multi-omics integration

We next assessed whether the metabolic signature was applicable to other cancers. The model performed strongly in ESCC (AUC = 0.999) but only moderately in adenocarcinomas (GC AUC = 0.739, CRC AUC = 0.759) (Figure S12A–C). This histology-specific performance suggested conserved metabolic reprogramming among squamous carcinomas, prompting further mechanistic exploration.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying metabolic reprogramming, we developed a systems biology-based multi-omics integration pipeline. We first annotated metabolite-associated genes using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), which yielded 383 candidate genes. Parallel transcriptomic analysis of the TCGA-HNSC cohort identified 1532 significantly upregulated genes in tumour tissues (FDR <0.05, |log2FC| > 1). Cross-comparison revealed 28 overlapping genes (Fig. 6A). Applying functional filters resulted in a 16-gene core metabolic panel (Supplementary Data 12).

Consensus clustering of TCGA-HNSC samples based on this 16-gene signature revealed two stable molecular subtypes: C1 (n = 229, 45.4%) and C2 (n = 275, 54.6%) (Fig. 6B). C2 was associated with significantly worse survival (P = 0.042; Fig. 6C) and distinct expression patterns (Fig. 6D). Similar stratification was observed in other squamous carcinoma cohorts (Figure S13), supporting broader relevance.

Molecular subtype characterisation reveals therapeutic vulnerabilities

To understand the biological basis of the poor prognosis C2 subtype, we performed comprehensive molecular characterisation. C2 tumours exhibited activation of pro-invasive pathways including ECM-receptor interaction and PI3K-Akt signalling (Fig. 6E), consistent with their aggressive phenotype. At the epitranscriptomic level, C2 showed upregulation of m6A machinery (P < 0.001; Fig. 6F), suggesting enhanced mRNA stability and translation.

Genomic profiling showed higher TP53 mutation frequency in C2 (P < 0.001), concentrated in the DNA-binding domain (Fig. 6G, Figure S14A), with no subtype association for other driver mutations (Figure S14B). These genomic alterations were accompanied by immunological changes. C2 tumours displayed elevated immune dysfunction scores (Fig. 6H) and reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration, and enrichment of M2 macrophages (Fig. 6I). These immune patterns were consistently observed across multiple squamous carcinoma types (Figure S15A–F). Spatial transcriptomics analysis of four HNSCC tissue samples (GSE220978) provided supportive spatial evidence consistent with these findings, showing inverse spatial correlations between metabolic gene expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration, along with localised enrichment of M2 macrophage markers in metabolically active tumour regions (Figure S16). Given the limited sample size, these spatial observations should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger spatially resolved datasets.

Importantly, these molecular features translated into distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities. C2 tumours demonstrated increased sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents (cisplatin, gemcitabine, docetaxel) but resistance to paclitaxel (Fig. 6J), providing preliminary evidence supporting subtype-associated therapeutic differences. To experimentally validate the predicted drug sensitivity differences, we classified 23 HNSC cell lines from the GDSC/DepMap databases into C1-like/C2-like subtypes using nearest centroid classification with TCGA-HNSC consensus clustering centroids as reference (Figure S17A). In vitro IC50 assays using representative cell lines CAL-27 (C1-like) and SCC-25 (C2-like) confirmed that the C2-like subtype exhibited enhanced sensitivity to cisplatin (IC50: 5.771 vs 12.31 μM; Figure S17B), gemcitabine (IC50: 0.756 vs 44.37 μM; Figure S17C), and docetaxel (IC50: 1.150 vs 1.317 μM; Figure S17D), but reduced sensitivity to paclitaxel (IC50: 1.618 vs 1.003 μM; Figure S17E), consistent with bioinformatic predictions (Fig. 6J).

Discussion

Our study represents one of the largest serum metabolomics investigations of HNSCC, enrolling 938 participants across three independent cohorts from three medical centres. HNSCC prognosis is profoundly stage-dependent, with 5-year survival exceeding 80% for localised tumours but declining to approximately 40% for advanced disease. Given that approximately 60% of patients present with advanced disease, improving early detection remains a clinical priority. Our work addresses this challenge through an independently validated 8-metabolite diagnostic signature (8-DM) that achieved an AUC of 0.901 (95% CI: 0.853–0.950, sensitivity 91.1%, specificity 80.2%) in multicentre external validation and maintained discriminative performance for stage I disease (AUC = 0.927, 95% CI: 0.878–0.976). Independent cross-platform validation using a dedicated targeted LC-MS/MS assay in a third cohort (Cohort 3, n = 269) further confirmed diagnostic generalisability (HNSCC vs HC: AUC = 0.980, 95% CI: 0.965–0.995). The concordance between a chemical descriptor-based model (AUC = 0.932) and the concentration-based model reinforces the biological validity of the diagnostic signal. Importantly, diagnostic accuracy was maintained across sex, age, and tumour stage subgroups, supporting equitable model performance.

A notable observation from our analysis was the coordinated dysregulation of arginine metabolism, characterised by reduced circulating serum l-arginine levels (FC = 0.65) and increased ornithine (FC = 1.25), consistent with enhanced ARG1 activity. CPTAC proteomic data showing downregulation of ASS1 and ASL in HNSCC tissues support impaired de novo arginine synthesis as a contributing mechanism. This systemic arginine decrease may promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment by limiting T cell metabolic fitness, as Geiger et al. demonstrated that l-arginine availability directly controls T cell survival and anti-tumour capacity,65 and Canale et al. showed that restoring intratumoral arginine reinvigorates anti-tumour immunity and synergises with PD-L1 blockade.66 Interestingly, this pattern contrasts with breast cancer, where elevated serum arginine supports macrophage-driven immunosuppression via polyamine metabolism.67 These tumour type-specific differences suggest that patients with HNSCC may potentially benefit from arginine supplementation strategies to restore immune competence, consistent with prior evidence linking arginine-enriched regimens to improved survival68 and reduced surgical complications.69 To directly address the question of whether serum arginine decrease reflects tumour-intrinsic metabolic activity, we performed targeted metabolomic profiling on 61 paired tumour and adjacent non-tumour tissue samples. l-arginine was significantly elevated in tumour tissue compared with adjacent normal tissue, as were choline and glycerophosphocholine, whilst all three were decreased in serum. This opposite directionality between serum (decreased) and tissue (increased) is consistent with a tumour retention pattern, characterised by local metabolite accumulation within the tumour microenvironment and concurrent systemic decrease. For l-arginine, this tissue-level accumulation is concordant with the ARG1 upregulation and ASS1 downregulation identified through proteomic analysis, indicating active arginine uptake and catabolism within the tumour microenvironment. Nevertheless, our study design cannot definitively establish causality. The tissue metabolomic analysis was performed on a subset of patients (n = 61 pairs), and larger-scale matched serum-tissue studies combined with functional perturbation experiments will be essential to comprehensively establish the directionality of these metabolic alterations and their therapeutic targetability.

The inclusion of a disease-control validation cohort (Cohort 3) provided important insights into diagnostic specificity. Moderate discrimination was observed between HNSCC and OPMDs (AUC = 0.710, 95% CI: 0.611–0.808). This finding is clinically informative: the predicted HNSCC probabilities showed a progressive stepwise increase from HC (median 0.154) through OPMDs (median 0.698) to HNSCC (median 0.778; Kruskal–Wallis P < 0.001), consistent with a metabolic gradient along the oral carcinogenesis continuum. The moderate HNSCC-versus-OPMDs discrimination suggests that the 8-DM captures metabolic changes that accumulate progressively during malignant transformation rather than appearing abruptly at the point of invasion. In a clinical screening context, this characteristic may be advantageous, as it would flag both established cancers and high-risk premalignant lesions for further evaluation. Nevertheless, the 8-DM in its current form is not designed to replace histopathological confirmation and should be positioned as a triage tool to identify individuals warranting further clinical investigation.

This metabolic classification complements prior HNSCC taxonomies derived from distinct molecular perspectives. The TCGA consortium defined four transcriptional subtypes (atypical, basal, classical, and mesenchymal) based on gene expression programmes related to epithelial differentiation and stromal composition.59 Dai et al. refined this framework through single-cell integration to define intrinsic consensus molecular subtypes capturing epithelial-mesenchymal transition states,70 and Li et al. identified immune-centric subtypes driven by B cell-dominated signatures.71 Our C1/C2 classification differs from these systems in its biological entry point: it originates from serum metabolite profiles and captures the interplay between tumour metabolism, immune contexture, and therapeutic vulnerability, a functional dimension not directly addressed by transcriptomic classifications alone. Notably, the immunosuppressive, TP53-mutated phenotype of C2 shares features with the classical TCGA subtype, whilst the immune-active profile of C1 shows parallels with the mesenchymal/atypical subtypes; however, our classification integrates metabolic vulnerability with immune landscape, potentially informing treatment selection in future studies. The connection between the serum metabolite panel and the tissue-level 16-gene signature was established through database-level annotation and pathway mapping rather than through matched serum-tissue analyses from the same patients. Whilst published functional evidence supports the biological plausibility of the key metabolite-gene associations (arginine–ARG1/ASS1, choline–CHKA, cystine–SLC7A11), direct experimental validation of the regulatory relationships was not performed in this study.

The observed inverse association between metabolic activity and immune infiltration in C2 tumours aligns with emerging concepts of metabolic-immune crosstalk in cancer.72,73 Nutrient depletion (e.g., arginine,74 tryptophan75) and metabolite accumulation such as lactate75,76 generate hostile microenvironments that impair T cell effector function. These observations suggest that C2 tumours may have limited response to immunotherapy alone, consistent with the generally modest efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors in immune-cold HNSCC.77 Instead, combined strategies that concurrently target metabolic vulnerabilities and immune dysfunction may be required to restore antitumour immunity. Our in vitro validation demonstrating enhanced C2-like subtype sensitivity to cisplatin and gemcitabine provides initial experimental support for subtype-informed treatment approaches, though this hypothesis warrants prospective evaluation in clinical settings.78,79

Several considerations are relevant for clinical translation. Compared with circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA)-based liquid biopsy approaches, which achieve ctDNA detection rates of approximately 65–80% in advanced-stage HNSCC but substantially lower rates (20–50%) in early-stage disease due to limited tumour DNA shedding,80,81 our serum metabolomics approach captures systemic metabolic perturbations that may precede detectable genetic alterations. The analytical requirements for targeted metabolomics are also less complex and less costly than deep sequencing. However, ctDNA provides tumour-specific genetic information (e.g., actionable mutations) that metabolomics cannot, suggesting that these modalities may be most informative when used in combination rather than as alternatives. For clinical implementation, the 8-DM requires a standard fasting blood sample and approximately 20 min of LC-MS/MS instrument time, with results available within 24–48 h. Our multicentric validation across three geographically distinct populations (Shanghai, Zhengzhou, and Beijing) provides evidence for diagnostic robustness. Future development of simplified detection methods (e.g., targeted assay kits) could further reduce costs and turnaround time, facilitating broader application. Technical harmonisation across LC-MS platforms, standardised quality control procedures, and formal cost-effectiveness analyses will be essential prerequisites for implementation in routine clinical settings.

This study has several methodological strengths, including a large multicentric cohort (n = 938), fully independent external validation without model retraining, cross-platform confirmation using a dedicated targeted assay, and experimental validation of predicted drug sensitivities. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our study employed a case-control design comparing patients with HNSCC to HC, which may overestimate diagnostic performance relative to real-world clinical scenarios where the differential diagnosis includes inflammatory conditions, benign tumours, and other malignancies. Although the inclusion of OPMDs in Cohort 3 partially addresses this concern, validation against a broader spectrum of disease controls is warranted. Second, race/ethnicity was not formally collected; however, given that all three recruiting centres are located in mainland China, the study population is predominantly of Chinese Han ethnicity. Metabolomic profiles can be influenced by genetic polymorphisms affecting metabolic enzymes, dietary patterns, and environmental exposures that vary across populations. Multi-ethnic validation will be an important future direction to evaluate model performance across diverse populations and, if necessary, develop population-specific calibration strategies. Third, chronic dietary patterns, well-controlled comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes), and detailed smoking/alcohol exposure data were not systematically recorded or adjusted for. Although our exclusion criteria removed patients with uncontrolled systemic diseases, residual confounding from medications (e.g., proton pump inhibitors, metformin) or lifestyle factors cannot be excluded. Future studies should incorporate detailed medication records and dietary assessments to enable formal adjustment. Fourth, the cross-sectional design is appropriate for biomarker discovery but should be complemented by longitudinal studies to evaluate metabolite dynamics during therapy and recurrence. Fifth, the spatial transcriptomics analysis was based on four publicly available HNSCC tissue samples (GSE220978), limiting the generalisability of the observed spatial associations. Finally, all reported performance metrics are derived from retrospective case-control settings and require prospective validation before clinical implementation. Of note, clinical implementation requires LC-MS/MS quantification of all eight metabolites from a standardised serum sample; if any analyte is undetectable, the sample should be re-analysed or the patient triaged through standard clinical pathways.

This multicentre metabolomics study provides new insights into HNSCC biology and diagnosis. We developed and independently validated a serum-based 8-metabolite diagnostic signature that achieved an AUC of 0.901 (sensitivity 91.1%, specificity 80.2%) in external validation and maintained discriminative performance for stage I disease (AUC = 0.927). In parallel, metabolomics-anchored multi-omics integration identified two molecular subtypes with distinct prognostic trajectories, immune landscapes, and predicted therapeutic vulnerabilities. Multicentric external validation across three geographically distinct centres supports the potential for further clinical evaluation. Prospective validation in real-world screening populations, including diverse ethnicities and disease-control groups, will be essential to establish the clinical utility of this approach. In summary, we identified a simple blood test based on eight metabolites that can accurately detect head and neck cancer, including early-stage disease, using a minimally invasive approach. If validated in prospective screening studies, this test could help identify patients with cancer earlier, when treatment is most effective.
