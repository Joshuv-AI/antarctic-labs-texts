Messaging Informed by Behavioral Economics and Representativeness in Clinical Research Enrollment
Authors: Alexander C. Fanaroff, Nirali Patel, Casey Whitman, Adina Lieberman, Shira Blady, Colleen Morse Kripke, Nawar Naseer, Joellen Weaver, Modele Ogunniyi, Rachel Kohn, Alanna A. Morris, Alisa Stephens-Shields, Kevin G. Volpp, Scott D. Halpern
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDo messaging strategies incorporating behavioral economic principles increase enrollment fraction in a clinical research study with direct-to-participant electronic outreach?FindingsIn this series of 4 sequential randomized clinical trials, outreach via email plus text message and text message alone (vs email alone) and with an offer of a financial incentive (vs no offer) significantly increased enrollment fraction of Black and Hispanic patients in the Penn Medicine BioBank, a prospective research study. Outreach from the clinical team (vs the research team) and with an appeal to altruism or social proof (vs a control message) did not significantly increase enrollment fraction.MeaningIn this study of direct-to-participant electronic outreach, contacting eligible participants by text message with an offer of a financial incentive increased enrollment fraction of Black and Hispanic patients.

Introduction

Prospective clinical research studies, including randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and prospective cohort studies, are essential for determining the effectiveness and safety of medical interventions.1,2 However, these studies are challenged by low enrollment rates and a lack of representation of racial and ethnic minority individuals.1,2,3,4,5 Investigators increasingly recruit participants through direct outreach via email or text message, but most participants do not respond to such contact.6,7 Black and Hispanic patients may be particularly less likely to respond to unsolicited outreach, reducing representativeness.7 Thus, strategies that increase initial engagement with outreach could improve both enrollment rates and representativeness.

We therefore conducted this series of 4 enrollment RCTs to rigorously test the effectiveness of strategies informed by behavioral economic theory to increase the enrollment fraction—the proportion of individuals who enroll in a study divided by all those contacted with an offer of enrollment—of Black and Hispanic individuals in an ongoing prospective observational research study. In the first recruitment RCT (RCT 1), we tested method of outreach, in the second (RCT 2), we tested source of outreach, in the third (RCT 3) we tested message framing, and in the fourth (RCT 4) we tested the incentive structure.

Methods

This series of RCTs was approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board under a waiver of informed consent per 45 CFR 46, as the study posed no more than minimal risk to participants. The rationale and design have been previously reported,8 and the trial protocol can be found in Supplement 1. This study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline.

Study Population

We embedded this series of recruitment RCTs within existing outreach processes for the Penn Medicine BioBank (PMBB). Details of the PMBB have been previously published and are summarized in the eMethods in Supplement 2.9

Eligible patients had at least 1 encounter with the University of Pennsylvania Hospital System in the 3 months prior to outreach, were able to provide informed consent (aged ≥18 years, with no documented dementia or psychiatric illness), and had a telephone number able to receive text messages or a valid email address on file in the electronic health record (EHR). Participants were ineligible if they had previously consented to or declined enrollment in the PMBB. For RCT 1 and RCT 2, participants were ineligible if they had activated their patient portal account, since the PMBB was conducting outreach to patients with active patient portal accounts via the portal prior to scheduled visits in select clinics. The PMBB stopped outreach via the portal prior to the start of RCT 3, and this exclusion criterion was removed for RCT 3 and RCT 4. A data scientist unaffiliated with the project selected a sample of eligible patients for outreach using a random number generator. Because the primary outcome was enrollment fraction of Black and Hispanic patients, we enriched the proportion of Black and Hispanic patients to at least 50% for each RCT.

Race and ethnicity was captured using data from the EHR, which derived from patient self-report. Participants identified as American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White, more than 1 race, or race unknown or not reported. They also identified as Hispanic or Latino, not Hispanic or Latino, or ethnicity unknown or not reported. Medicaid insurance was used as a proxy for low individual-level socioeconomic status (SES).10 Patients’ addresses on file were linked to Area Deprivation Index (ADI), a composite measure of neighborhood disadvantage measured at the US Census block level.11

Study Procedures

We conducted 4 iterative recruitment RCTs between October 30, 2023, and November 13, 2024, using a rapid-cycle innovation strategy.12 At the conclusion of each RCT, we chose a winning strategy, which became the control arm in the next RCT. In the event of a statistically significant difference between arms, the winning strategy was the arm with the highest enrollment fraction of Black and Hispanic patients. When there was not a statistically significant difference between arms, the winning strategy was selected based on enrollment fraction of Black and Hispanic patients as well as other considerations, including ease and cost of implementation.

Eligible patients’ information was uploaded to an online behavioral intervention platform (Penn Way to Health) used to automate outreach, survey delivery, and data collection.13 Within Way to Health, uploaded participants were randomized using a random number generator and were sent an initial outreach message determined by the arm to which they were randomized with a survey link. Randomization was stratified based on race (Black compared with White compared with Asian) and ethnicity (Hispanic compared with non-Hispanic). Participants received a message from Way to Health on days 1, 3, and 8 or until the consent survey was completed. The messages sent to participants in each of the 4 RCTs are included in eTable 1 in Supplement 2. All study team members were blinded to messages sent to participants.

When participants clicked on the survey link, they were taken to a landing page on the Way to Health platform (eFigure 1 in Supplement 2). In RCT 1, participants were first asked to authenticate their identity by entering their year of birth. Authentication was conducted automatically by the Way to Health platform, and authenticated participants were sent a second link to the PMBB consent form. However, most patients who authenticated did not ultimately click on the consent form link, so we redesigned the process for subsequent RCTs. In RCTs 2 to 4, patients provided consent, confirmed their first name and last initial, and entered their year of birth on the same webpage. Before participants were formally enrolled in the PMBB, study team members manually authenticated participants.

In RCT 1, participants were randomized (1:1:1) to 1 of 3 methods of outreach (text, email, or email plus text). Randomization was conducted without taking into consideration contact information available in the EHR. For example, a participant with only an email address available in the EHR randomized to text message outreach would not receive any outreach; and if that participant were randomized to email plus text message, they would receive only email outreach. This strategy was chosen to inform trade-offs related to availability of specific contact information in the EHR.

In RCT 2, participants were randomized (1:1) to receive a message from the research team compared with a message from a clinic where they had an appointment during the past 3 months. The clinic name was used rather than clinician’s name due to (1) health system regulations requiring clinicians to approve outreach for each patient contacted in their name and (2) concern that this would unduly burden clinicians.

In RCT 3, participants were randomized (1:1:1) to 1 of 3 message frames: an appeal to altruism (“your participation can help members of your community”), an appeal to social proof (“many people like you have participated”), or neither. RCT 3 only recruited patients who were Black or Hispanic, as the appeal to altruism specifically mentioned the importance of research participation to inform the care of members of communities historically underrepresented in clinical research.

In RCT 4, participants were randomized (1:1:1:1:1) to a control arm with no incentive or to 1 of 4 arms with differing structures for providing an incentive with a $25 actuarial value: a $25 guarantee, a $15 guarantee plus a 5% chance at winning $200 (small lottery), a 5% chance at winning $500 (medium-sized lottery), or a 1% chance at winning $2500 (large lottery). Outreach messaging included details of the incentive structure.

Outcome Measures

The primary end point for all RCTs was enrollment fraction of Black and Hispanic patients, defined as the number of Black and Hispanic patients who consented for the PMBB within 30 days of initial outreach divided by the total number we attempted to contact, compared between arms. We also evaluated enrollment fraction overall, among White patients, and by age (<65 or ≥65 years), sex, insurance type (Medicaid or other), and community-level disadvantage, as measured by ADI. ADI was coded by decile, with the highest decile corresponding to the greatest deprivation (lowest SES).

Statistical Analysis

For RCT 1, we estimated that the enrollment fraction would be 10% in the email contact arm. In the absence of data on the minimum clinically important difference in enrollment fraction, we powered our trial to detect a 50% relative increase in enrollment fraction in one of the arms (from 10% to 15%) among Black and Hispanic patients, which required 1338 Black and Hispanic patients per arm (4014 total) for 90% power with α = .017 to account for multiple comparisons in a 3-arm RCT. Because Black and Hispanic patients comprised 50% of the study population in RCT 1, we planned to contact 8028 patients.

After blinded analysis of RCT 1, enrollment fraction was less than 1% in all arms, but approximately 4% of patients who were contacted attempted to authenticate. Because we changed the authentication strategy for subsequent RCTs such that authentication and consent would occur at the same time, we assumed a 3.5% enrollment fraction in the control arm of RCT 2. Calculating sample sizes required to achieve adequate power with enrollment fractions of 3.5% demonstrated that conducting subsequent RCTs would be infeasible with continued adjustment for multiple comparisons. We therefore decided to set α = .05 for all subsequent comparisons and determined that 1069 Black and Hispanic patients per arm would provide 80% power to detect a 75% relative increase in enrollment fraction (from 3.5% to 6.125%). Therefore, RCT 2 (2 arms) planned to enroll 4276 participants (n = 2138 Black or Hispanic patients); RCT 3 (3 arms), 3207 participants (all Black and Hispanic); and RCT 4 (5 arms), 10 690 participants (n = 5345 Black or Hispanic patients).

We described baseline characteristics of patients contacted in each enrollment RCT as well as the enrollment fraction overall and by subgroup. To determine the associations between baseline participant characteristics and the likelihood of enrolling in the PMBB, we used logistic regression with variables of interest (race, ethnicity, insurance type, and ADI decile) entered categorically as variables and further adjusted for RCT and arm. When evaluating the association between enrollment and ADI, we used the Cochrane-Armitage 1-df trend test. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated this analysis excluding RCT 1.

In our primary analyses, we compared enrollment fractions of Black and Hispanic patients between arms using Fisher exact tests. In RCTs with more than 2 arms, we performed pairwise tests between arms without adjustment for multiple comparisons. In secondary analyses, we repeated these analyses for all patients. We assessed differences in recruitment strategies’ effects on enrollment fraction in various subgroups using logistic regression with 2-way interactions between study arm and race and ethnicity, sex, age, insurance type, or ADI in separate models. All randomized patients were included in analyses, consistent with the intention-to-treat principle.

Two-tailed P < .05 was considered significant. This analysis of all 4 RCTs was performed from December 2024 through September 2025 using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute), and R, version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

RCT 1: Method of Outreach

RCT 1 enrolled 8038 patients; 4581 were Black or Hispanic and 2896 were White. A total of 1158 Black or Hispanic patients were contacted by email (n = 2625 patients randomized), 1510 by text message (n = 2568), and 1553 by both email and text message (n = 2580). Among the Black or Hispanic patients in RCT 1, none randomized to email consented compared with 7 (0.5%) randomized to text message outreach and 3 (0.2%) randomized to email plus text outreach (P = .02 by Fisher exact test; P = .06 for text vs email; P = .20 for email plus text vs email; P = .20 for text vs email plus text). Corresponding rates among all patients were 5 (0.2%) for email, 7 (0.5%) for text message, and 3 (0.2%) for both email and text message (P = .03 by Fisher exact test; P = .01 for text vs email; P = .08 for email plus text vs email; P = .37 for text vs email plus text) (Figure 1).

Bar and Whisker Plot of Effect of Method of Outreach on Enrollment Fraction (Randomized Clinical Trial 1 [RCT 1])

Both text message alone and email plus text message led to higher enrollment fractions than email alone. Whiskers indicate 95% CIs.

There were no significant treatment-by-subgroup interactions (eFigure 3 in Supplement 2). More than 80% of patients contacted for RCT 1 failed to authenticate due to a cumbersome process, but the proportion of patients who took any action in response to outreach was significantly higher with text (73 [4.8%]) and email plus text (68 [4.4%]) than email alone (9 [0.6%]) (P < .001 for text vs email and email plus text vs email), both overall and among Black and Hispanic patients (eFigure 4 in Supplement 2).

Both text message alone and email plus text were superior to email alone but there were no significant differences between these 2 strategies, so there was no clear winning strategy. Because there is no incremental cost for adding email to text message outreach and there is a potential benefit, email plus text was carried forward as the method of outreach in all subsequent RCTs.

RCT 2: Source of Contact

RCT 2 enrolled 4271 patients; 1902 were Black or Hispanic and 1901 were White. A total of 923 Black or Hispanic patients received outreach that indicated it was from the Penn Medicine BioBank research team (n = 2102 patients randomized), and 979 received outreach that indicated it was from a clinic the patient had visited in the past 3 months (n = 2169). Among Black and Hispanic patients in RCT 2, 9 (1.0%) randomized to research team outreach consented compared with 18 (1.8%) randomized to clinical team outreach (P = .12) (Figure 2A). Corresponding rates among all patients were 34 (1.6%) for research team outreach and 49 (2.3%) for clinical team outreach (P = .14). There were no significant treatment-by-subgroup interactions, although clinical team outreach was numerically more effective for Black or Hispanic patients and patients aged 65 years or older (Figure 2B).

The enrollment rate was lower than anticipated, and RCT 2 was underpowered. Because the point estimate for the effect of clinical team outreach among Black and Hispanic patients was greater than the minimum difference the trial was designed to detect, lack of statistical significance was attributed to low power, and clinical team outreach was carried forward.

RCT 3: Framing

RCT 3 enrolled 3217 patients (all Black or Hispanic). A total of 1075 patients were randomized to the control, 1098 to an appeal to altruism, and 1044 to an appeal to social proof. There were 34 patients (3.2%) randomized to the control message who consented, compared with 40 (3.6%) randomized to the appeal to altruism and 43 (4.1%) randomized to the appeal to social proof (P = .50 by Fisher exact test; P = .55 for altruism vs control; P = .26 for social proof vs control; P = .57 for altruism vs social proof) (eFigure 5 in Supplement 2). There were no significant treatment-by-subgroup interactions (Figure 3).

RCT 4: Incentive Structure

RCT 4 enrolled 10 689 patients; 8629 were Black or Hispanic and 2009 were White. A total of 1736 Black or Hispanic participants were randomized to no incentive (n = 2149 patients randomized), 1696 to a small guarantee ($25) (n = 2118), 1748 to a smaller guarantee with a small lottery ($15 plus 5% chance at $200) (n = 2150), 1715 to a medium-sized lottery (5% chance at $500) (n = 2102), and 1734 to a large-sized lottery (1% chance at $2500) (n = 2170). Among Black and Hispanic patients in RCT 4, 44 (2.5%) randomized to no incentive consented compared with 423 (6.1%) randomized to any incentive (P < .001) (Figure 4A). Among Black and Hispanic patients receiving an incentive, 115 (6.8%) randomized to the small guarantee consented compared with 96 (5.5%) randomized to the smaller guarantee plus small lottery, 100 (5.8%) randomized to the medium-sized lottery, and 112 (6.5%) randomized to the large lottery (P < .001 by Fisher exact test; P < .001 for each incentive individually vs no incentive; P > .05 for each incentive compared with another incentive). Among all patients, 65 (3.0%) randomized to no incentive consented compared with 521 (6.1%) randomized to any incentive (P < .001). All incentives were superior to no incentive, but no incentive structure was superior to any other (Figure 4B; pairwise comparisons between incentive structures are presented in eTable 6 in Supplement 2).

Discussion

In this series of recruitment RCTs, we found that enrollment fraction of Black and Hispanic patients into a prospective clinical research study was improved with outreach by email and text message or text message alone (vs email alone) and with a small incentive (vs no incentive). We also found that while outreach from a clinical team rather than the research team was numerically more effective for Black and Hispanic patients than for White patients, it was not significantly more effective for either group and there was no statistical interaction between source of contact and race. By contrast, including an offer of an incentive with an expected value of $25 had significantly larger effects on enrollment fraction among Black and Hispanic patients, those with Medicaid insurance (a surrogate for low SES), those living in more disadvantaged communities (as represented by ADI), and patients younger than 65 years, although the structure of the incentive (lottery vs guarantee) did not affect enrollment fraction. Together, these findings provide evidence regarding the effect of the content and structure of outreach for direct-to-participant clinical research studies.

The advent of EHRs to identify potentially eligible participants and online platforms for study enrollment and consent has facilitated direct-to-patient outreach in decentralized clinical trials, which may be less expensive and faster than traditional clinical research. Moreover, direct-to-patient recruiting can counter implicit bias that affects which patients clinicians invite to participate in clinical research studies, as well as barriers to access at smaller or rural hospitals.14,15 However, there has been very limited systematic research into enrollment fraction by race, ethnicity, and SES in studies that enroll in this way. In one study, investigators found that contacting eligible individuals by email with an offer to enroll in a decentralized RCT without regard for race or ethnicity led to lower enrollment fraction and underrepresentation of men and Black, Hispanic, and Asian individuals.7 Our series of 4 enrollment RCTs extends these findings, demonstrating that Black and Hispanic individuals had 23% lower odds of enrollment in a prospective clinical research study after receiving an unsolicited electronic invitation, even after adjusting for demographic characteristics.

The decision to enroll or not enroll in a clinical research study is complex, and patients’ decision-making is limited by incomplete information and time constraints in considering the pros and cons of participation. In the setting of these constraints, behavioral economic theory recognizes that decision-making may be influenced by the context in which the decision is presented, including whether the message feels trustworthy or whether people like them participate. Although there were theoretical reasons to believe that outreach via text message (vs email), from a more trusted clinical source,16,17,18,19,20 and with an appeal to social proof or altruism17,21 would increase likelihood of enrollment, only text message–based outreach significantly increased enrollment fraction. Importantly, outreach came from a clinical team (rather than patients’ personal clinician), and patients’ trust in their physician may not extend to the more generic clinical team. Similarly, references to altruism and social proof increased the length of outreach messages, and the resultant small increase in participant burden may have outweighed any potential effect of message content. The disparate effects on enrollment fraction of these theoretically grounded approaches highlight the need for rigorous testing of clinical research recruitment strategies. The importance of context-dependent factors is further illustrated by the large difference in enrollment fraction between RCT 1 and RCT 2: Real-time authentication requiring navigation to 2 separate web pages (as in RCT 1) introduced substantial friction into the enrollment process. Removing this friction (without changing anything about the risks or benefits of research participation) increased enrollment fraction by an order of magnitude.

Based on prospect theory, which highlights that humans overestimate small probabilities near zero,22,23,24 we had hypothesized that structuring a small incentive as a lottery would increase participation. However, this was not the case, as enrollment fraction was the same regardless of incentive structure. Providing an incentive with an expected value of $25—regardless of how it was structured—approximately doubled participation compared with no incentive, with greater effects on patients from groups historically underrepresented in clinical research. Future work is needed to confirm these findings, as a 2021 study of 2 RCTs of incentives for research participation found that incentives were not more effective among individuals with lower financial well-being.25 By contrast, our finding that incentives may improve racial and ethnic representativeness in prospective research is consistent with prior work.26

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, all participants were recruited from a single academic health system; findings may not necessarily generalize to other settings. Second, recruitment RCTs were conducted in the context of recruitment for the PMBB. Factors influencing participation in low-burden registries differ from those influencing participation in RCTs; however, the largest barrier to enrollment in direct-to-participant RCTs is engagement with initial outreach,7 and many features of the initial response to unsolicited outreach may be shared between higher-burden RCTs and low-burden registries. Moreover, as study participants were not asked to do anything beyond consenting to enroll in the PMBB, we cannot comment on their likelihood of engagement. However, previous studies have found no association between details of outreach messaging and participants’ subsequent engagement.27,28,29 Lastly, enrollment fraction in all trials was less than 5%, consistent with previous trials conducted in similar patient populations with direct-to-patient electronic outreach.6,29 Although absolute differences between tested methods of outreach were small, relative differences in enrollment fraction may be magnified in large research programs.

Conclusions

In this series of RCTs of outreach messaging strategies for clinical research, enrollment fraction among Black and Hispanic patients was improved with outreach by text message and with a small incentive.
