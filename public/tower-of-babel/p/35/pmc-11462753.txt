A case of early-onset congenital syphilitic osteomyelitis of the calcaneus and literature review
Authors: Wang Guo, Zhiqun Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital syphilis (CS) is a sexually transmitted disease caused by Treponema pallidum (TP). When the skeletal system is involved, it often results in multiple, symmetrical bone destruction at the epiphyses of long tubular bones such as the humerus and radius, rarely involving the calcaneus. This article reports a case of calcaneal osteomyelitis caused by TP in a child with no other bone damage and subtle clinical manifestations, No similar cases have been reported.Case presentationA 4-month-old male infant presented with right foot swelling without any obvious cause and no history of trauma. X-ray and CT scans showed bone loss in the calcaneus and surrounding soft tissue swelling. Review of past medical records revealed that the infant had been diagnosed with CS infection during a hospital stay for “pneumonia” at one month old. The parents refused surgery, opting for conservative treatment at an external hospital for three weeks, during which the symptoms of the affected foot showed no significant improvement. Subsequently, the child was treated at our hospital with surgery, including lesion removal and cast fixation, followed by oral antibiotic treatment. The last follow-up showed no swelling or tenderness in the affected foot, with good mobility, and X-rays indicated that the bone had essentially returned to normal.ConclusionsEarly CS rarely involves the calcaneus. When diagnosing unexplained calcaneal osteomyelitis in infants, this rare cause should be considered. A thorough medical history should be taken and a careful physical examination conducted. Once diagnosed, timely surgical debridement and appropriate antibiotic therapy targeting TP infection are required. Early identification and intervention can result in a good prognosis without related complications.

Background

Congenital syphilis (CS), also known as transplacental syphilis, is primarily caused by the vertical transmission of Treponema pallidum (TP) through the placenta [1]. When the disease manifests in fetuses or infants under two years of age, it is referred to as early CS. This condition can affect the nervous, skeletal, hematopoietic, and other bodily systems [2, 3]. When the skeletal system is involved, it often manifests as multiple, symmetrical epiphyseal osteochondritis, periostitis, and osteomyelitis. Severe cases can lead to pathological fractures, commonly affecting long tubular bones such as the humerus, radius, ulna, and tibia, but rarely the calcaneus [4]. This article reports the diagnosis and treatment of a case of calcaneal osteomyelitis caused by CS in a child treated at our hospital. By reviewing relevant literature, it summarizes the clinical and imaging characteristics, differential diagnosis, and treatment prognosis of calcaneal osteomyelitis due to CS to enhance understanding of this disease.

Case presentation

The patient, a 4-month-old boy, presented to orthopedic outpatient clinic with “right foot swelling for three days.” The parents reported noticing the swelling without any history of trauma or recent infection with fever. Physical examination revealed slight swelling and tenderness of the right foot compared to the left, without significant redness or elevated skin temperature. Laboratory tests showed CRP at 17.28 mg/L, WBC at 10.80 × 109/L, neutrophils at 29.3%, and ESR at 38 mm/h (Fig. 1). The results of blood bacterial culture and drug sensitivity tests were negative. X-rays of the right foot showed localized decreased bone density in the calcaneus and surrounding soft tissue swelling (Fig. 2A-C). Further CT scans confirmed localized decreased bone density in the calcaneus with uneven internal density, discontinuous cortex, and visible callus formation (Fig. 3). Initially, the parents refused hospitalization. After three weeks of treatment with cefazolin and erythromycin at a local hospital (Day 24 of the illness), the swelling in the child’s right foot did not subside. The child returned to our outpatient clinic for a follow-up X-ray, which showed no significant improvement in the right calcaneal destruction (Fig. 4). During the second visit, the parents agreed to hospitalization for surgery.

Reviewing the patient’s medical history, it was found that the child had been diagnosed with “neurosyphilis” at one month old (3 months before osteomyelitis of calcaneus), but this crucial information was withheld by the guardian. At that time, the child was admitted to the respiratory department of our hospital due to a 3-day history of fever, with a peak temperature of 38.0 °C. Chest X-ray showed patchy infiltrates in the left lower lung, and the diagnosis was bronchopneumonia. Infectious disease tests indicated positive specific antibodies for syphilis (180.600 COI) and positive results for human cytomegalovirus IgG and herpes simplex virus type I IgG. CSF routine tests showed WBC > 5 × 106/L with normal protein levels and negative culture results. Serum syphilis antibody TRUST and TPPA were positive, while CSF TRUST was negative and TPPA was positive. X-rays of the long bones showed no significant abnormalities (Fig. 5A-B), and no obvious abnormalities were found in ultrasound of brain, heart and abdomen. Due to penicillin allergy, the patient was treated with ceftriaxone and discharged. Unfortunately, the guardian disclosed that the child was adopted, and details regarding the birth circumstances and the parents’ syphilis infection status were unknown.

Upon admission, the physical examination revealed that the child had no jaundice or rash, and the liver and spleen were not palpably enlarged. Repeat blood bacterial cultures and tuberculosis-related indicators were negative, but infectious disease tests still indicated a syphilis infection status. An MRI of the right foot was planned but could not be completed due to the child’s lack of cooperation. Consultation with the infectious disease department was conducted for preoperative preparation. Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent debridement of the osteomyelitis focus in the right foot. During the surgery, a transverse incision was made in the anterior third of the right calcaneus, exposing severely damaged bone cortex with extensive granulation tissue, which was thoroughly debrided and sent for pathological examination and bacterial culture. Postoperatively, the affected limb was immobilized with a plaster cast.

Postoperative treatment included ceftriaxone for infection control. The plaster cast was removed one month post-surgery, and imaging showed uniform bone density in the right calcaneus (Fig. 6A-C). Postoperative bacterial cultures were negative and pathology revealed fragmented bone trabeculae and extensive chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, with marked fibrous tissue proliferation at the bone-cartilage junction, consistent with syphilitic granulation tissue and chronic osteomyelitis (Fig. 7). Eighteen months post-surgery, online follow-up indicated that the right foot showed no swelling or pain, had unrestricted movement, and demonstrated a normal walking gait. (Fig. 8A-B).

Discussion

Syphilis is a chronic, systemic sexually transmitted disease (STD) caused by Treponema pallidum (TP) [1]. Based on the route of infection, syphilis can be classified into congenital syphilis(CS) and acquired syphilis [5]. Congenital syphilis, also known as fetal syphilis, is primarily transmitted vertically through the placenta from the mother and can occur at any stage of pregnancy [6]. A minority of cases result from direct contact during delivery [7]. TP can invade multiple organs and systems, including the nervous system, skeletal system, and hematopoietic system [2], causing significant health hazards to the child. However, over two-thirds of affected infants show no obvious symptoms at birth, complicating early diagnosis and potentially delaying diagnosis and treatment [4]. CS is divided into early and late disease based on results that appear before or after age 2 years [2, 3]. Early-onset CS accounts for about 95% of symptomatic cases, with a mortality rate as high as 21-40% [8].

The skeletal system is a common target for CS [4]. Symptoms usually appear within the first 6 months of life and may be the only clinical manifestation of early-onset CS [4]. TP invades the metaphysis, diaphysis, and periosteum, causing osteochondritis, periostitis, and osteomyelitis [2]. Due to the rich vascularity and slow blood flow in the metaphysis, bacteria can easily colonize this area [9], leading to syphilitic granulomas and bone destruction in long bones such as the humerus, radius, ulna, and tibia. Bone marrow biopsy reveals irregular or discontinuous trabeculae with significant fibrosis at the osteochondral junction [10]. Affected children often present with swelling and pain in the affected limbs, restricted spontaneous movement (Parrot’s pseudoparalysis), and hyperactive responses to touch, such as crying and resistance to being held [11, 12]. Approximately 70% of early-onset CS patients exhibit skin and mucosal lesions, including generalized maculopapular rashes, papules, or desquamation, particularly prominent on the palms and soles [6]. Some children may also develop progressive jaundice, anemia, and varying degrees of hepatosplenomegaly [13].

X-rays and MRI scans have high sensitivity in diagnosing congenital syphilitic osteitis and can evaluate bone healing during follow-up. Bone scintigraphy plays a crucial role in the management of osteoarticular infections in infants. It is particularly valuable for detecting early-stage bone involvement, identifying the extent of infection, and guiding the treatment plan. Approximately 80% of symptomatic and 20% of asymptomatic CS patients exhibit positive findings on long bone radiographs [3]. Syphilitic osteitis predominantly affects the metaphysis, making metaphysitis the most characteristic feature, visible on plain films as indistinct metaphyseal bands, radiolucent bands, irregular edges, and serrated changes. Multiple and symmetrical bone lesions are significant characteristics of congenital syphilis-induced bone damage [4]. When the proximal medial aspects of both tibiae are involved, it can form Wimberger’s sign [14]. Persistent TP stimulation of long bone diaphysis may appear on X-rays as “onion-skin” periosteal thickening [15]. TP can also invade the diaphysis of long bones like the humerus and tibia, forming osteomyelitis, visible on imaging as moth-eaten bone destruction or extensive destructive and proliferative changes, potentially leading to pathological fractures in severe cases [16].

TP-induced osteomyelitis often co-occurs with metaphysitis and periostitis, but osteomyelitis involving the calcaneus is extremely rare. Currently, there are no reported cases of CS leading to calcaneal osteomyelitis. Hematogenous spread is the primary route of infection for pediatric calcaneal osteomyelitis [17]. Since the calcaneus consists mainly of cancellous bone with rich blood supply and is located at the lowest part of the body, where blood flow is slow, TP can colonize through the bloodstream [17]. Based on this case, the characteristics of TP-induced calcaneal osteomyelitis are summarized as follows: ① Slow onset with insidious and nonspecific clinical manifestations. Swelling and pain in the affected limb are not prominent, and infants, often swaddled and not weight-bearing, make early detection difficult for parents; ② No acute inflammatory response like fever or chills during the disease course, with early laboratory tests showing normal white blood cell counts but potentially elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR); ③ Absence of TP infection symptoms at birth, with no anemia, jaundice, skin lesions, hepatosplenomegaly, or other multisystem involvement at disease onset; ④ Lack of bone damage in other areas, with long bone X-rays showing no characteristic metaphysitis or periostitis, presenting calcaneal bone destruction as the sole positive finding. Additionally, during clinical diagnosis and treatment, factors such as the attending physician’s lack of experience, incomplete or deliberately concealed history of TP infection by the parents, and the child’s non-cooperation during the physical examination can easily lead to delays in diagnosis and treatment, resulting in significant calcaneal bone destruction by the time the child seeks medical attention.

When suspecting early-onset syphilitic osteomyelitis of the calcaneus, differential diagnosis should include the following diseases: (1)Pyogenic Calcaneal Osteomyelitis: Typically presents with acute inflammatory responses such as limb immobility, limping, redness, swelling, heat, and pain, with significant swelling. Radiographs show notable bone destruction, often with sequestra and involucrum. Blood cultures and layered puncture bacterial cultures can isolate pathogens. (2)Subacute osteomyelitis is more insidious in clinical presentation and is often caused by less virulent microorganisms (such as bacteria or mycobacteria). Because the symptoms are often not obvious, it may go unnoticed, and laboratory tests may appear normal, leading to delays in diagnosis. Some affected bones may show tumor-like aggressive (lytic) changes on imaging. Chronic pyogenic osteomyelitis may involve sequestra and sinus tract formation, usually with a history of acute onset. (3)Calcaneal Tuberculosis: Tuberculosis commonly affects the spine and long bone metaphyses and rarely involves the calcaneus. CT shows circular or oval bone destruction with clear edges, surrounded by osteopenia with indistinct boundaries, commonly with granular sequestra, and rare periosteal reactions. (4)Idiopathic Heel Swelling and Pain: Conditions such as calcaneal stress fractures, calcaneal apophysitis, and Achilles tendinitis should be excluded. MRI can assist in diagnosis, and biopsy may be required for a definitive diagnosis. (5)Other Diseases Causing Calcaneal Lesions: This includes unicameral bone cysts, fibrous dysplasia of bone, eosinophilic granuloma, and Kaposi-like hemangioendothelioma.Syphilitic infections remain a significant concern in some developing countries, particularly during the early stages when specific signs and symptoms are lacking, making the diagnosis of syphilis-induced osteomyelitis quite challenging. Therefore, when encountering unexplained bone lesions in infants from regions where syphilis is prevalent, clinicians must consider syphilitic osteomyelitis as a differential diagnosis. A thorough inquiry into birth history and exposure to infectious diseases, combined with necessary imaging and serological tests, is essential to determine the cause.

There are no definitive guidelines for treating osteomyelitis caused by early-onset CS. Treatment should address both syphilitic infection and osteomyelitis. Penicillin is the first-line treatment for syphilis [18]. Benzathine penicillin G intramuscular injection is given to patients with normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), while patients with abnormal CSF should receive intravenous aqueous penicillin for 10–14 days. For penicillin-allergic patients, intramuscular ceftriaxone can be used as an alternative [5]. In cases of concurrent osteomyelitis, persistent calcaneal bone destruction should be considered a surgical indication [19]. Syphilitic osteomyelitis of the calcaneus is a severe pathology in infants, necessitating multidisciplinary management in a specialized hospital center. This team should include a pediatric orthopedic surgeon, pediatrician, and infectious disease physician. A comprehensive assessment is essential to identify any potential visceral involvement, ensuring that the full scope of the disease is addressed. Surgical intervention includes thorough debridement of proliferative syphilitic granulation tissue, elimination of dead space, post-operative plaster immobilization, and anti-TP infection treatment. During surgery, the placement of a drain allows for timely drainage of pus, promotes the resolution of inflammation, and prevents the spread of infection. Additionally, post-operative plaster immobilization can reduce the child’s pain and local swelling, promote wound healing, and prevent pathological fractures, especially in cases of severe bone destruction or after extensive debridement. Early treatment of CS generally leads to a good prognosis, with no long-term complications [2]. In this case, after three weeks of conservative treatment at an external hospital without significant improvement in calcaneal radiographs, surgical debridement was performed immediately upon admission. Post-operative radiographs showed that bone structure had essentially returned to normal, and the last online follow-up showed satisfactory appearance and function of the affected limb.

Of course, this article has certain limitations. Firstly, the birth history of the child is unknown, preventing a detailed analysis of the transmission routes of congenital syphilis. Secondly, the follow-up period is too short, and long-term observation is needed to understand the potential impact of syphilis infection on the child’s health in the future. This article is the first to report a case of congenital syphilis involving the calcaneus, with no other systemic clinical manifestations in the child. While individual case reports provide valuable insights into rare pathologies, they should not be solely relied upon as definitive references for treatment planning. Treatment strategies should be guided by broader clinical evidence and guidelines where available, and tailored to the specific circumstances of each patient.

Conclusion

This article focuses on the clinical and imaging features, differential diagnosis, and treatment prognosis of calcaneal osteomyelitis caused by CS. When infants and young children present with destructive calcaneal lesions without clear trauma or other causes, this rare etiology should be considered. It is important to carefully inquire about birth history and infectious disease exposure history during consultations. For children with a history of TP exposure, the possibility of skeletal system damage should be taken seriously. Physical examinations should be comprehensive and meticulous, with bilateral comparisons, routine imaging of long bones in the limbs, and vigilance for bone invasion in other rare locations. Upon diagnosis, surgical debridement should be performed promptly, and appropriate antibiotic treatment for TP infection should be administered. Early identification and intervention can lead to a good prognosis without related complications.
