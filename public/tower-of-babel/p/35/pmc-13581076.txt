Serum Amyloid β Oligomer May Predict Treatment Response in Middle‐Aged and Late‐Life Patients With Depression
Authors: Kentaro Shimizu, Seita Yasuda, Hitoshi Maeshima, Tomoya Ichikawa, Hajime Baba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimLate‐life patients with depression are reportedly less responsive to antidepressant treatment than younger patients. Additionally, patients who have depression comorbid with Alzheimer's disease (AD) and those with an amyloid β (Aβ) burden have shown a poor response to antidepressant treatment, suggesting that AD pathology may contribute to treatment resistance. A recent report indicated that Aβ oligomers in blood have increased in patients with AD and are associated with AD pathology. This study was performed to reveal the relationship between blood Aβ oligomers and the response to antidepressant treatment in middle‐aged and late‐life patients with depression.MethodsIn this observational study, serum levels of Aβ40, Aβ42, and Aβ oligomers were evaluated in 80 inpatients with major depressive disorder aged ≥ 40 years. Depressive symptoms were assessed at admission (baseline) and after 4 weeks of treatment.ResultsThere were significantly fewer treatment responders among patients with than without serum Aβ oligomers (p = 0.016). Serum Aβ oligomers were found to influence the treatment response even after control for age, sex, number of depressive episodes, severity of depression, and Mini‐Mental State Examination scores (p = 0.031).ConclusionsThese results suggest that serum Aβ oligomers may predict a poor response to antidepressant treatment in middle‐aged and late‐life patients with depression. Our findings also lead us to speculate that elderly patients with a poor treatment response may share AD‐related pathophysiological features or neural vulnerability.

Introduction

Late‐life patients with depression are reportedly less responsive to pharmacological therapy than younger patients [1]. Among patients with depression aged ≥ 60 years, meta‐analyses of the treatment response to antidepressants showed a significant effect of antidepressants compared with placebo [2]. However, other meta‐analyses involving patients aged ≥ 65 years showed no significant benefit by antidepressants [3, 4]. A meta‐analysis focusing on the predictors of treatment outcomes in late‐life depression (LLD) also showed that older age is a predictor of a poor treatment response, especially in biological treatments (including antidepressant treatments) [5]. The authors considered that this poor treatment response associated with aging in patients with LLD is caused by age‐related brain changes, such as brain atrophy and decreased serotonin receptor density.

Previous studies have suggested that antidepressants may be less effective for depression comorbid with dementia in general [6]. In particular, randomized controlled trials focusing on Alzheimer's disease have reported limited efficacy of antidepressants in this population [7, 8]. The Health Technology Assessment Study of the Use of Antidepressants for Depression in Dementia (HTA‐SADD) was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled (three‐arm) trial in England [8]. In that study, approximately 300 patients with concomitant AD and depression were randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups (sertraline, mirtazapine, or placebo), and the treatment efficacy was evaluated at 13 and 39 weeks. Neither the sertraline nor mirtazapine treatment group showed significant efficacy compared with the placebo group at either 13 or 39 weeks. Amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in the brain are the two main AD pathologies, and amyloid pathology develops many years before clinical symptoms [9]. Therefore, amyloid plaques deposit in the brain prior to the development of cognitive decline. Evidence regarding the relationship between AD biomarkers and antidepressant treatment response in LLD remains limited. In this context, Li et al. [10] conducted a pilot amyloid PET study in patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and reported that greater cerebral amyloid deposition was associated with a higher degree of antidepressant treatment resistance, with region‐specific increases in amyloid burden particularly evident in patients with moderate‐to‐severe treatment resistance. In a preliminary study, Taylor et al. [11] performed amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) scans in 27 cognitively intact patients with LLD before treatment and then randomized the patients to receive escitalopram or placebo for 8 weeks. The results demonstrated that five patients with LLD who had a cortical amyloid burden as confirmed by PET showed significantly less change in the severity of depression and a lower remission rate than 22 patients without amyloid. These results suggest that the amyloid status may predict the response to antidepressants and that patients with LLD who have amyloid pathology (possibly a prodromal stage of AD) may be less responsive to antidepressant treatment.

Amyloid β protein (Aβ) is a major component of amyloid plaques and has two main isoforms: Aβ40 and Aβ42. Aβ42 is more easily polymerized, and soluble Aβ oligomers are neurotoxic, and can cause synapse failure [12, 13]. Several studies have focused on plasma levels of Aβ oligomers in association with AD pathology. Zhou et al. [14] reported that the plasma levels of Aβ oligomers were significantly higher in patients with AD than in controls and showed weak to moderate negative correlations with cognitive functions. Wang et al. [15] evaluated Aβ oligomer levels using the multimer detection system (MDS), an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for selective measurement of Aβ oligomers, and reported that the plasma levels of Aβ oligomers were higher in patients with AD than in controls and showed modest‐to‐moderate associations with conventional AD biomarkers, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of Aβ42, phosphorylated tau, total tau, and amyloid deposition as assessed by PET. Meng et al. [16] also reported that the plasma levels of Aβ oligomers levels by MDS were significantly higher in patients with AD than in controls and showed moderate correlations with cognitive functions. Pyun et al. [17] investigated the relationship between plasma levels of Aβ oligomers as shown by the MDS and the Aβ burden as shown by amyloid PET. They found that the presence of plasma Aβ oligomers positively assessed by MDS could discriminate amyloid PET positivity with an area under the curve of 0.855. We previously investigated the serum levels of Aβ40, Aβ42, and Aβ oligomers by ELISA in 104 patients with MDD and 132 healthy controls [18]. We found that the serum levels of Aβ oligomers were not different between patients with MDD and control; however, the serum levels of Aβ oligomers in patients with MDD were dependent on the serum Aβ42 levels, regardless of age. This relationship was not observed in the controls, suggesting that Aβ42 may be more prone to aggregation and polymerization in patients with depression than in healthy subjects, based on our prior findings [18].

The aim of the present study was to reveal the relationship between blood Aβ oligomers and treatment response in middle‐aged and late‐life patients with depression. Our hypothesis was that middle‐aged and older patients with depression who have serum Aβ oligomer positivity may show a poor response to antidepressant treatment.

Methods

Participants

In total, 94 inpatients with depression were recruited from Juntendo University Koshigaya Hospital, Saitama, Japan, from November 2010 to August 2016. All patients met the criteria for MDD outlined in the fourth and fifth editions of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. Patients were excluded if they had severe or acute medical illnesses, neurologic disorders, or use of drugs that may cause psychosis or depression (n = 0). Patients were also excluded if they discontinued the initial treatment for any reason, including adverse events (n = 14). After all ineligible patients were excluded, 80 inpatients with MDD (26 men, 54 women; mean age = 60.4 years; age range, 40–80 years) were enrolled in the study. The number of depressive episodes was determined based on information obtained from clinical interviews with the patients and, when available, their family members at the time of admission. Antidepressant Treatment and Identification of Responders

All patients were treated with antidepressants. Because this was an observational study, the classes and doses of the antidepressants were not standardized, and adjuvant medications were not restricted. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM‐D) [19] at admission (baseline) and after 4 weeks of treatment. Treatment responders were defined as patients whose HAM‐D scores improved by ≥ 50% from baseline after 4 weeks of treatment.

Serum Aβ40, Aβ42, and Aβ Oligomer Measurements

Fasting blood samples for measurement of Aβs were drawn the morning after admission. The serum Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels were measured using a sandwich Aβ ELISA kit (WAKO, Osaka, Japan), as described previously [20]. The Aβ (1–40) kit uses the BAN50 monoclonal antibody, which specifically detects the N‐terminal portion of human Aβ (1–16), and the BA27 monoclonal antibody, which detects the C‐terminal portion of Aβ (1–40). The Aβ (1–42) kit uses BAN50 and the BC05 monoclonal antibody, which detects the C‐terminal portion of Aβ (1–42). The sensitivity for Aβ40 was 0.019 pmol/L (dynamic range, 1.0–100 pmol/L), and that for Aβ42 was 0.06 pmol/L (dynamic range, 0.1–20 pmol/L). The intra‐assay coefficients of variation (CVs) for Aβ40 were 4.8% at a mean of 14.2 pmol/L, 4.25% at a mean of 36.0 pmol/L, and 3.6% at a mean of 75.5 pmol/L, and those for Aβ42 were 0.8% at a mean of 3.2 pmol/L, 0.8% at a mean of 7.4 pmol/L, and 1.0% at a mean of 16.4 pmol/L. The inter‐assay CVs for Aβ40 were 3.2% at a mean of 14.8 pmol/L, 1.1% at a mean of 33.6 pmol/L, and 2.5% at a mean of 75.7 pmol/L, and those for Aβ42 were 8.3% at a mean of 3.2 pmol/L, 11.3% at a mean of 7.1 pmol/L, and 5.8% at a mean of 16.4 pmol/L.

Serum Aβ oligomer levels were detected using a Human Amyloid β Oligomers (82E1‐specific) Assay Kit (IBL, Gunma, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions [18]. This ELISA kit detects human Aβ molecules that bind to an antihuman Aβ N‐terminal antibody (82E1) via two or more epitopes (including Aβ oligomers and Aβ bound to other proteins). The sensitivity was 4.41 pmol/L. The intra‐assay CVs were 5.0% at a measurement value of 314.24 pmol/L, 2.9% at a measurement value of 88.32 pmol/L, and 5.4% at a measurement value of 33.66 pmol/L (n = 24). The inter‐assay CVs were 3.1% at a measurement value of 310.02 pmol/L, 5.2% at a measurement value of 76.92 pmol/L, and 10.0% at a measurement value of 27.39 pmol/L (n = 5).

Data Analysis

Among patients with MDD, the age, number of depressive episodes, HAM‐D scores, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores, and doses of antidepressants were compared between those with and without serum Aβ oligomers using the two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐test. The χ 2 test was used to compare the sex. The serum levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test because of the skewed distribution [20]. Multiple regression analyses were conducted with treatment response as the outcome variable and age, sex, number of depressive episodes, HAM‐D scores at admission, MMSE scores, and Aβ oligomer status as independent variables (predictors). A significance level of p < 0.05 was used. Statistical procedures were performed using the Japanese version of SPSS v.21 software (SPSS Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan).

Results

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

Serum Aβ oligomers were detected in 29 patients with MDD. Age, sex, number of depressive episodes, HAM‐D scores at baseline and after 4 weeks, doses of antidepressants, and serum levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 did not differ significantly between the two groups. In contrast, the serum Aβ42/40 ratio showed a trend toward higher values in patients with Aβ oligomers compared with those without (p = 0.05). The MMSE scores were significantly lower in the patients with than without Aβ oligomers (p = 0.016). The changes in HAM‐D scores from baseline to 4 weeks of treatment are shown in Figure 1. The mean changes in the HAM‐D score were not significantly different between patients with and without Aβ oligomers.

Rate of Response to Antidepressant Treatment in Patients With and Without Aβ Oligomers

The proportion of treatment responders was 51.0% (26/51) among patients without Aβ oligomers, and 24.1% (7/29) among patients with Aβ oligomers. The response rate was significantly lower in patients with than without Aβ oligomers (p = 0.016) (Figure 2).

Discussion

In the present study, middle‐aged and late‐life patients with depression who had positive serum Aβ oligomers had a lower response rate to antidepressant treatment than those without serum Aβ oligomers. This negative effect of serum Aβ oligomers on the treatment response was statistically significant even after controlling for confounding factors such as age, sex, number of depressive episodes, severity of depression at admission, and cognitive status. In the present study, although a significant difference in response rates was observed between patients with and without Aβ oligomers, the mean change in HAM‐D scores did not differ significantly between the groups. The mean change in HAM‐D scores is sensitive to the influence of outliers, and as shown in Figure 1, a small number of patients without Aβ oligomers exhibited marked worsening of HAM‐D scores. This may have attenuated the group difference in mean change, resulting in the lack of a significant difference when analyzed using mean values.

The mechanism underlying how the presence of Aβ oligomers in the peripheral blood of patients with depression results in a lower treatment response to antidepressants is unknown. One possibility is that patients with depression who have positive serum Aβ oligomers may share certain pathophysiological features with early AD and are therefore less likely to respond to antidepressant treatment. While the method of Aβ oligomer measurement and the sample type (plasma vs. serum) differed from those in our study, several studies have shown that Aβ oligomers in peripheral blood are increased in patients with AD and that the blood concentration of Aβ oligomers correlates with cognitive function and AD pathology (including Aβs and phosphorylated tau in CSF and amyloid burden found by PET) [14, 15, 16, 17]. Most of these previous studies evaluated Aβ oligomer levels using the MDS, an ELISA for selective measurement of Aβ oligomers [15, 16, 17]. Notably, this method does not directly measure Aβ oligomers; instead, it measures the tendency of Aβ oligomerization [21]. Due to differences in methods used to measure Aβ oligomers, it is unclear whether the oligomers detected by the ELISA used in this study reflect AD pathology, as seen in previous studies. Nonetheless, the oligomers may at least reflect some change in Aβ metabolism that is associated with AD pathology. As mentioned in the introduction, a study by Taylor demonstrated that patients with LLD in whom a cortical amyloid burden had been confirmed by PET scan showed significantly less change in depression severity and a lower remission rate compared with amyloid‐negative patients [11]. Similarly, a pilot amyloid PET study by Li et al. [10] reported that greater cerebral amyloid deposition was associated with a higher degree of antidepressant treatment resistance in patients with MDD, particularly in those with moderate‐to‐severe treatment resistance. Although these studies were preliminary and involved relatively small sample sizes, their findings collectively suggest that LLD with AD‐related pathophysiological features may be less responsive to antidepressant treatment. However, the absence of group differences in serum Aβ42 levels suggests that the present findings cannot be explained solely by amyloid deposition. Although the serum Aβ42/40 ratio tended to be higher in patients with Aβ oligomers, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the borderline significance and variability of serum biomarkers, and it does not necessarily indicate overt amyloid deposition.

It is not clear how AD pathology, particularly Aβ, negatively affects antidepressant treatment. Mahgoub and Alexopoulos [22] reported that amyloid accumulation is an etiologic factor for frontolimbic compromise that predisposes to depression and increases treatment resistance in a subgroup of older adults. Amyloid deposition leads to frontolimbic and frontostriatal abnormalities due to vascular damage, neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and disrupted functional connectivity that impair networks implicated in depression. Taylor et al. [11] focus on the default‐mode network (DMN) as a possible mechanism related to Aβ and treatment resistance. Successful antidepressant treatment in patients with LLD is reportedly associated with changes in resting‐state functional connectivity (rsFC) of the DMN. The earliest accumulation of Aβ plaques occurs within DMN regions, including the posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, and prefrontal cortex. Aβ deposition in turn is associated with alterations in rsFC of the DMN, reported as both network disruptions (decreased rsFC) and network reorganizations (increased rsFC). This evidence suggests that the presence of a greater Aβ burden may decrease the ability of antidepressants to facilitate these critical functional network changes, resulting in treatment resistance. However, recent neuroimaging studies of LLD have suggested that classical AD biomarkers, such as amyloid positivity and tau accumulation, are not necessarily elevated compared with cognitively unimpaired older adults, despite the presence of structural brain changes including reduced gray matter volume, particularly in temporal regions [23]. These findings indicate that the relationship between LLD and Alzheimer's disease is complex and cannot be explained solely by amyloid or tau pathology. In this context, serum Aβ oligomers observed in the present study may reflect altered neural vulnerability or synaptic dysfunction rather than overt amyloid deposition.

The present study has several limitations. First, the ELISA kit used to measure the Aβ oligomers in this study did not contain heterophilic antibody inhibitors. Previous research has suggested that this type of ELISA method is vulnerable to heterophilic antibody interference, suggesting that the detected signals may have contained artifacts due to heterophilic antibody interference [24]. Therefore, it is possible that the current results regarding Aβ oligomers did not indicate pure “pathological” Aβ oligomers. Second, although most previous studies measured plasma Aβ, serum Aβ was measured in the present study. Previous studies have suggested that aggregated platelets release Aβ, as well as adenosine diphosphate and clusterin, which promote Aβ aggregation [25]. Moreover, a previous study comparing plasma and serum Aβ levels reported approximately 2‐fold increases in Aβ levels in serum samples, compared with plasma samples [26]. The relationship between clusterin and Aβ aggregation may have a particular impact on Aβ oligomer levels [27]. Considering the findings of these previous reports, the results of the present study may have been influenced by the use of serum samples. To better understand the kinetics of Aβ levels in patients with depression, the CSF should be monitored in future studies. Third, the classes and doses of antidepressants were not standardized, and adjuvant medications were not restricted. These variations in medication are likely to have affected treatment response. Future studies should standardize the types and doses of antidepressants and adjuvant medications. Fourth, the number of depressive episodes was assessed based on retrospective information obtained from patients and, when available, their family members. Therefore, recall bias cannot be excluded, particularly in patients with LLD with early onset, in whom accurate recollection of lifetime depressive episodes may be difficult.

In conclusion, this is the first report to investigate the relationship between blood Aβ oligomers and the treatment response to antidepressants in middle‐aged and late‐life patients with depression. The results demonstrated that patients with serum Aβ oligomers had a lower response rate to antidepressant treatment than those without serum Aβ oligomers. This negative effect of serum Aβ oligomers on the treatment response was significant even after controlling for confounding factors such as age, sex, number of depressive episodes, severity of depression at admission, and cognitive status. These results suggest that serum Aβ oligomers may predict the treatment response to antidepressants in middle‐aged and late‐life patients with depression. Although the mechanism underlying how the presence of serum Aβ oligomers results in a lower treatment response to antidepressants remains unclear, serum Aβ oligomers may reflect AD‐related pathophysiological processes or neural vulnerability that contribute to treatment resistance.
