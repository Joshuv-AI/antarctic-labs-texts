RASA1 expression highlights Z/W dosage dynamics and gonadal development in chickens
Authors: Ying Xue, Mengqing Lv, JiaHui Li, Ruixin Su, Minmeng Zhao, Tuoyu Geng, Daoqing Gong, Debiao Zhao, Long Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
All identified genes on the avian W chromosome have homologous counterparts on the Z chromosome, with protein-coding genes showing over 90% homology. These W-linked genes are typically dose-sensitive and conserved, ensuring balanced expression between sexes during development. This study explores the sequence characteristics, expression patterns, and potential functions of the RASA1 gene in chicken embryos. In gonads, RASA1-Z was found to be more highly expressed in males, but total mRNA levels (RASA1-C) were higher in females due to RASA1-W compensation. However, RASA1 protein levels were significantly higher in males, suggesting limited contribution of RASA1-W. Knockdown of RASA1-Z reduced both mRNA and protein levels, whereas overexpression of RASA1-W increased mRNA but not protein levels. Sex reversal experiments showed no significant changes in RASA1 expression, indicating it is not involved in gonadal sex differentiation. However, higher expression in the left female gonad suggests a role in gonadal development. Functional studies using CCK8 proliferation assays revealed that RASA1-Z promotes cell proliferation, while RASA1-W has minimal impact. This study indicates that RASA1 primarily functions in cell proliferation and angiogenesis rather than sex differentiation, with RASA1-W contributing to transcript balance but not protein output.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12222-4.

Background

Birds are among the most recognizable and diverse groups of modern vertebrates. Over the past decades, the discovery of numerous primitive bird fossils and advancements in phylogenetic and macroevolutionary studies have substantially improved our understanding of bird origins [1, 2]. Unlike mammals, where SRY on the Y chromosome determines testes development, birds lack a master sex-determining gene on the sex chromosomes. During the evolution of sex chromosomes in birds, recombination between the primitive sex chromosomes progressively ceased, leading to the differentiation of the Z and W chromosomes. In birds, males are homogametic (ZZ), whereas females are heterogametic (ZW), in contrast to the XX/XY system in mammals [3–5]. The degree of divergence between the Z and W chromosomes varies widely among different species of birds, ranging, for example, from two-thirds of the Z chromosome in emus and ostriches still crossing over with the W chromosome in a long pseudoautosomal region to almost complete divergence of the chicken Z and W chromosomes [6].

The chicken Z chromosome contains over 1,000 genes, including 884 protein-coding genes and 348 non-coding sequences, and has undergone androgenic-driven evolution [7]. In contrast, the W chromosome is a highly degenerated counterpart of the Z chromosome, comprising only about 28 protein-coding genes and approximately 116 non-coding RNAs [8]. Dosage compensation in chickens is incomplete, resulting in sex-biased expression of many Z-linked genes [9]. Interestingly, certain W-linked genes have been preserved due to their potential roles in development and sex determination, maintaining a functional balance between males and females despite their small number [10]. These genes are often co-expressed with their Z homologues and exhibit similar expression levels in embryonic and adult tissues of females [3].

RASA1 (Ras p21 protein activator 1) encodes a key regulator of Ras GDP/GTP cycling and is involved in critical physiological processes such as angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and apoptosis [11]. Dysregulation of RASA1 is implicated in vascular abnormalities and tumor formation [12]. In mice, RASA1 also plays a pivotal role in regulating vascular growth, particularly as a negative regulator of tumor-driven angiogenesis in response to factors such as FGF [13]. Despite its well-characterized roles in mammals, the function of RASA1 in chickens remains unclear. Interestingly, as a conserved W-linked gene that has been preserved during W chromosome degeneration, RASA1 may play a role in chicken sex determination and embryonic development.

To address this gap, we investigated RASA1 expression patterns in gonadal and non-gonadal tissues of male and female chicken embryos at multiple developmental stages. Notably, our analysis revealed a discrepancy between mRNA and protein expression levels, with protein expression in gonadal tissues and cells exhibiting an opposite trend compared to mRNA levels. To further explore this phenomenon, we performed targeted knockdown of RASA1-Z and overexpression of RASA1-W in chicken gonadal cells to assess their impact on RASA1 protein levels. Additionally, we employed a sex-reversal model to evaluate the responsiveness of RASA1 under altered sex-determination conditions. These findings provide critical insights into the expression patterns and potential roles of RASA1 in chicken embryonic development and lay the groundwork for future functional studies.

Methods

Egg incubation and sample collection

The eggs were incubated at 37.5 °C and 60% humidity until 6 days (E6) and 12 days (E12), and tissues were collected from the embryos on the 6th day of incubation (six replicates from both sexes) and on the 12th day of incubation (six replicates from both sexes), snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −70 °C for storage.

Fadrozole (FAD) treatment

Chicken embryos incubated for 2.5 days were treated with aromatase inhibitor-FAD (0.1 mg FAD dissolved in PBS was injected into the air sac) to induce female-to-male sexual reversal and gonadal tissue was collected from control and treated groups on day E12.

Isolation and treatment of chicken gonadal cells

E9.5 chicken embryo gonads were collected with cold PBS. After aspirating and discarding PBS, 500 µL of pre-warmed collagenase (Cat. No. 17104019; Gibco) was added, and the tissues were pipetted and heated at 37 °C for 30 s. This step was repeated until the gonads became invisible. Subsequently, 800 µL of 10% bovine serum (FBS)-Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) was added to stop the digestion. After centrifuged at 700 × g for 5 min at room temperature, the supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of complete medium (DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (100 IU/mL), and 10 µL Epidermal growth factor (EGF) (20 ng/mL) was added. The mixture was filtered into a 50 mL conical tube using 70 µm nylon mesh. After counting the cells, a complete medium was added and the cells were cultured at 1 × 10^6 cells/well. 24 h later, the cells were then transfected with RASA1-Z siRNA and RASA1-W overexpression plasmid using jetPRIME transfection reagent. The shRNA targeting RASA1-Z was designed to bind a sequence unique to the Z isoform, avoiding homology with RASA1-W. Specificity was verified by BLAST against the chicken genome to ensure no off-target binding. The RASA1-W overexpression construct contained only the coding sequence (CDS) without the 5’ and 3’ UTRs. The cells were incubated for a further 24 h.

RNA Isolation, cDNA Synthesis, and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA samples were diluted with nuclease-free water and treated with DNase to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the HiScript® qRT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, each reaction included 1 µg of RNA, 4 µL of 4 × gDNA wiper mix, and add RNase-free water to 10 µL, incubated at 42 °C for 2 min. Afterward, 4 µL of 5 × qRT SuperMix was added, and the reaction continued at 25 °C for 10 min, 42 °C for 30 min, and 85 °C for 5 min. The resulting cDNA was stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis. Quantitative PCR was conducted using AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), with HMBS as the internal control and primers listed in Table 1. Each 20 µL qPCR reaction contained 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2 µL of diluted cDNA (equivalent to 100 ng RNA input), and nuclease-free water. The relative expression of RASA1 was calculated using the 2−△△CT method.

Protein extraction and Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues and cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (C1055, APPLYGEN, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, cat. no., P0398S). The relative expression level of RASA1 protein was assessed by Western blotting, following the protocol available at http://biosupport.licor.com. Briefly, equal amounts of protein samples (10 µg) were separated on Bis-Tris gels via electrophoresis and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against RASA1 (Proteintech, 12935-1-AP; 1:2000 dilution) and Tubulin (Cell Signaling Technology, 2144; 1:5000 dilution), followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies (Rabbit IgG (Beyotime, cat. no. A7016) and mouse IgG (Beyotime, cat. no., A7028)). Protein bands were visualized and imaged, and densitometric analysis was performed using Image J. Tubulin served as a loading control.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay

CCK8 (Cell Counting Kit-8) assay was performed using a CCK8 reagent from Vazyme (Nanjing Vazyme Biology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DF-1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10^3 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. After 24 h, cells were transfected with either RASA1-Z siRNA (for knockdown) or RASA1-W plasmid (for overexpression) using jetPRIME transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After transfection, cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. Following the 24-hour incubation period, 10 µL of CCK8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured. The relative cell proliferation rate was calculated by comparing the absorbance values of transfected cells to control cells.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard error (SEM). A Student’s t-test was used to analyze the statistical significance of the difference between the control and treatment groups, and P < 0.05 was set as the criteria for statistical significance.

Results

Characterization of mRNA Sequences and Expression of RASA1 in Chicken Embryonic Tissues at E6 and E12

The RASA1 gene is present on both the Z and W sex chromosomes in chickens, referred to as RASA1-Z and RASA1-W, respectively. According to the NCBI database (GRCg7b), RASA1-Z generates two isoforms (Z1, XM_424907.7, 4023 bp; Z2, XM_046905700.1, 3634 bp), while RASA1-W (XM_040655196.2) produces a single transcript of 3799 bp. Sequence alignment revealed an identity of 89.37% between RASA1-Z and RASA1-W, with RASA1-W containing 24 exons, whereas RASA1-Z1 and RASA1-Z2 contain an additional exon (Fig. 1-A).

Specific primers were designed for conserved regions of RASA1-Z, RASA1-W, and a region common to both Z and W transcripts (RASA1-C) to quantify expression via qPCR in male and female embryonic tissues at E6 and E12. At E6, RASA1-Z expression was significantly higher in the liver, kidney, and heart of females compared to males but lower in the gonads, brain, leg muscles, and tail of females than males. Total RASA1 expression (RASA1-C) was significantly higher in the gonads, kidneys, and leg muscles of females than in males. qPCR analysis of 11 tissues of E12 chick embryos revealed that RASA1-Z expression was higher in all 11 tissues in males compared to females, while RASA1-C gene expression was significantly higher in female gonads and proventriculus, but the difference was not significant in other tissues (except for the brain) (Fig. 1-B, C). RASA1-Z showed a consistent dosage effect, with higher expression in males (ZZ) than females (ZW) across all tissues. RASA1-W was actively transcribed in female gonads and contributed to the total RASA1 mRNA (RASA1-C), partially compensating for the single Z copy, although it had minimal impact on protein levels. However, RASA1-C expression was relatively balanced between the sexes, suggesting partial compensation by RASA1-W in females.

Expression of RASA1 in Chicken Gonads and Gonadal Cells

Given the observed sexual dimorphism of RASA1 in embryonic gonads, its mRNA expression was further examined in the left and right gonads and gonadal cells. In E12 female gonads, RASA1-Z and RASA1-C were significantly upregulated in the left gonads compared to the right (P < 0.01, Fig. 2-A). Similar trends were observed in gonadal cells, with higher expression of RASA1-Z and RASA1-C in females than in males (P < 0.05, Fig. 2-B).

Characterization of Protein Sequences and Expression of RASA1 Protein in Chicken Embryo Gonads and Gonadal Cells

Alignment of chicken RASA1-Z (isoform 1, XP_424907.5; isoform 2, XP_046761656.1) and RASA1-W (XP_040511130.1), and human RASA1 (isoform 1, NP_002881.1) is shown in Fig. 3-A. Notably, the region absent in RASA1-Z2 relative to Z1 corresponds to the SH2 superfamily and PRK07764 superfamily domains, which likely account for the lower amino acid similarity of Z2 with human RASA1. These domain differences may influence protein-protein interactions and functional activity. An antibody against the C-terminal region of human RASA1, which is identical in the chicken sequences, was used for Western Blot (WB) analysis. As the predicted AA sequence of the antibody binding region is identical (Fig. 3-A), our WB analysis should represent the combined protein expression of RASA1-Z and RASA1-W. Despite higher combined RNA transcript levels (RASA1-C) in female gonads, WB analysis revealed higher RASA1 protein levels in male gonads (P < 0.05, Fig. 3-B). Similarly, male gonadal cells exhibited higher RASA1 protein expression than females (P < 0.01, Fig. 3-C).

Expression of RASA1 in Fadrozole-Treated (Sex Reversed) Chicken Embryonic Gonads

As chicken RASA1 was expressed at different levels between female and male gonads (Fig. 2), to evaluate the effect of sex reversal on RASA1 expression, a sex-reversal model was established using Fadrozole treatment. Morphological analysis of E12 gonads confirmed the efficacy of the treatment (Fig. 4-A). However, qPCR analysis showed no significant differences in RASA1 transcripts between the Fadrozole-treated group and the control group (Fig. 4-B, D), indicating that RASA1 expression does not respond to sex-reversal treatment.

Effects of RASA1-Z Knockdown on mRNA and Protein Expression

RASA1-Z was knockdown in gonadal cells and the expression levels of RASA1 mRNA and protein were detected by qPCR and Western Blot. The results showed that the knockdown of RASA1-Z in gonadal cells led to a significant decrease in both RASA1-Z and RASA1-C mRNA levels (P < 0.01, Fig. 5-A). Correspondingly, RASA1 protein levels were also reduced in both female and male gonadal cells after RASA1-Z knockdown (Fig. 5-B, C).

Effects of RASA1-W Overexpression on mRNA and Protein Expression

To assess the functional role of RASA1-W, targeted overexpression was performed in gonadal cells. The results showed that mRNA expression of both RASA1-W and RASA1-C was significantly increased after overexpression of RASA1-W (P < 0.01, Fig. 6-A). However, this did not affect the protein levels of RASA1, as there were no significant differences between the overexpression and control groups (Fig. 6-B, C).

Functional Implication of RASA1 in Cell Proliferation

As RASA1 has been implicated in cell proliferation, we sought to further investigate its role in this process by performing a cell proliferation assay using the DF-1 chicken embryo fibroblast cell line. Specifically, we conducted a knockdown of the RASA1-Z and overexpression of RASA1-W, followed by an assessment of cell proliferation using the CCK8 assay. The results revealed no significant change in cell proliferation following overexpression of the RASA1-W, suggesting that RASA1-W does not exert a substantial effect on cellular growth. In contrast, the knockdown of RASA1-Z led to a significant reduction in cell proliferation, with the Z-knockdown group exhibiting markedly lower proliferation levels compared to the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 7).

Discussion

Like other birds, Chickens possess a Z/W sex chromosome system, with males being homogametic (ZZ) and females heterogametic (ZW). While genes on the W chromosome are homologous to their Z counterparts, they are typically dosage-sensitive and retained during evolution due to their essential developmental roles [3, 5, 6, 14, 15]. RASA1 is among these W-linked genes and is expressed across various tissues, suggesting its widespread physiological importance. However, our findings indicate that RASA1 does not appear to be involved in gonadal sex differentiation, instead playing a more fundamental role in chicken embryonic development.

Our results demonstrated that RASA1 was expressed across all 11 tissues examined in E12 embryos, reflecting its fundamental role. This aligns with its known involvement in key biological processes, such as angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and vascular development in other species [16, 17]. Notably, RASA1 expression levels did not respond to Fadrozole-induced sex reversal, suggesting that RASA1 is independent of estrogen-regulated pathways and not directly involved in sex determination.

The absence of a fully compensatory dosage mechanism for Z-linked genes in birds results in male-biased expression of Z chromosome genes in various tissues [4]. This phenomenon was evident in RASA1, where male expression levels were consistently higher than female levels in most tissues at both E6 and E12, reflecting a dosage effect. However, the total RASA1 expression (RASA1-C) was higher in females in certain tissues, suggesting that the W copy of RASA1 might partially compensate for the single Z copy in females [7, 18, 19]. Interestingly, some tissues, such as the gizzard in E6 embryos and the liver in E12 embryos, showed no sex-based differences in RASA1-Z expression, indicating tissue-specific sensitivity to RASA1 dosage. These findings suggest that certain tissues may rely more heavily on local regulatory mechanisms to maintain functional RASA1 levels, a pattern consistent with other dosage-sensitive genes in birds [15].

The expression patterns in gonadal tissues revealed intriguing dynamics. RASA1-Z mRNA levels were higher in males, while RASA1-C were higher in females, suggesting that RASA1-W compensates for the lower Z expression in females. However, this compensation does not extend to protein levels, as RASA1 protein expression was higher in males. It is important to note that the antibody used recognizes a conserved C-terminal region and cannot distinguish between RASA1-Z and RASA1-W. This limitation should be addressed in future studies using isoform-specific antibodies or mass spectrometry. Our knockdown and overexpression experiments further support this: RASA1-Z knockdown reduced protein levels, whereas RASA1-W overexpression increased mRNA levels without affecting protein expression, which implies that the W copy primarily functions in maintaining mRNA homeostasis without contributing significantly to RASA1 protein production or activity. The discrepancy between mRNA and protein levels may result from differences in 5′ or 3′ UTR sequences that affect translation efficiency, possibly in a sex-specific manner.

Despite the lack of involvement in sex differentiation, RASA1 may still play a critical role in gonadal development. Its higher expression on the left side of female gonads, which undergoes expansion and differentiation, suggests a role in supporting the angiogenesis and cell proliferation required for left-sided gonadal development. This hypothesis is consistent with RASA1’s established role in vascular development in other organisms [13, 20, 21]. The sexual dimorphism observed in gonadal RASA1 expression might be related to the differing developmental trajectories of male and female gonads rather than direct involvement in sex determination.

The results of the CCK8 assay provide significant insights into the functional role of RASA1 in cell proliferation, particularly in the context of its Z- and W-linked isoforms. Our findings suggest a crucial involvement of the RASA1-Z in promoting cell proliferation, while the RASA1-W appears to have minimal impact on cellular growth. The marked reduction in cell proliferation upon knockdown of RASA1-Z supports the notion that RASA1-Z plays an essential role in cellular processes such as growth and proliferation, which are critical for developmental processes in the embryo. This is consistent with the known functions of RASA1 in other species, where it has been implicated in the regulation of Ras signaling pathways that control cell cycle progression and cell survival. In contrast, the lack of significant effect of RASA1-W overexpression on cell proliferation suggests that its contribution at the protein level is limited. This observation aligns with previous findings that, although RASA1-W is involved in dosage compensation, its protein expression contributes minimally to the overall protein pool. These results also highlight a possible discrepancy between mRNA and protein expression, where RASA1-W may regulate transcript levels but does not translate into a functional protein that influences cellular processes like proliferation.

Our study investigates the functional divergence between the Z- and W-linked copies of RASA1 in chickens. While RASA1-W is actively transcribed in female gonads and appears to compensate for mRNA dosage at the transcript level, it does not translate into detectable protein. This decoupling between transcription and translation points to a “pseudo-functional” nature of RASA1-W—a copy that contributes to the transcriptome but not to the proteome. This study highlights an underappreciated layer of sex chromosome evolution in birds: the retention of transcriptionally active but translationally silent W-linked genes. We propose that RASA1-W exemplifies a broader trend among avian W-linked genes—one in which apparent expression masks a loss of functional protein output. This observation expands our understanding of how the W chromosome preserves gene remnants that may serve as molecular placeholders rather than functional equivalents, contributing to sex-specific transcriptomic balance without true dosage parity. Understanding why the W copy is retained despite its limited functional contribution at the protein level remains an open question. Additionally, investigating how male-biased Z expression translates to differences in protein activity will be critical to unraveling the functional dynamics of RASA1. Comparative analyses across avian species with varying degrees of Z/W differentiation will be essential to determine whether the transcription–translation decoupling observed in RASA1-W is a general phenomenon or a lineage-specific feature, thereby shedding light on the evolutionary retention of W-linked genes such as RASA1 [4, 15]. Future studies should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying RASA1 regulation, particularly the interplay between Z and W copies, and their roles in tissue-specific development. The functional significance of RASA1-W in compensating mRNA levels, despite the lack of direct protein contribution, is another intriguing avenue. Comparative studies across avian species with varying levels of Z/W chromosome differentiation could also provide insights into the evolutionary retention of W-linked genes like RASA1.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that RASA1-W, a gene located on the chicken W chromosome, is actively transcribed but minimally translated, suggesting strong post-transcriptional regulation. While RASA1-W contributes to maintaining mRNA dosage balance, our results consistently show that its protein contribution is negligible. By contrast, RASA1-Z plays an essential role in supporting cell proliferation and embryonic development. These findings provide new insights into the functional divergence between Z- and W-linked gene copies and highlight how the avian W chromosome retains transcriptionally active but functionally constrained genes.
