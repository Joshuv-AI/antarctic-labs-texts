Chenodeoxycholic acid redirects the bile acid synthetic pathway to limit pro-inflammatory neutrophil infiltration and alleviate colitis
Authors: Wei Zhang, Xinkai Wu, Ye Feng, Luxi Yin, Jiansheng Xie, Rongjie Zhao, Guimei Wang, Bingru Lin, Zhipeng Fang, Eryun Zhang, Hongming Pan, Wendong Huang, Weidong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) exhibit a dysregulated bile acid pool, characterized by increased primary and decreased secondary bile acids, largely due to gut microbiota dysfunction. However, the impact of colitis on hepatic bile acid synthesis remains poorly understood. In this study, analyses of public datasets, in-house patient samples, and an animal model revealed that colitis enhances flux through the classical bile acid synthesis pathway while suppressing the alternative pathway. Oral administration of chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) redirected bile acid synthesis toward the alternative pathway and alleviated colitis in mice. Single-cell RNA sequencing and adoptive transfer experiments demonstrated that CDCA administration reduced pro-inflammatory neutrophil accumulation in the colon by downregulating epithelial-derived CXCL2, a finding validated by in vitro assays and a transgenic mouse model. Mechanistic studies further demonstrated that lithocholic acid, a CDCA metabolite in the gut, activates colonic epithelial vitamin D receptor, thereby suppressing CXCL2 via NF-κB inhibition. Clinical sample analyses supported these findings, showing that a higher cholic acid to CDCA ratio positively correlates with neutrophil counts and CXCL2 levels in IBD patients. Together, these findings suggest a critical role of hepatic bile acid synthesis pathways in IBD pathogenesis and highlight CDCA as a potential therapeutic candidate.Keywords: chenodeoxycholic acid, bile acid synthesis, inflammatory bowel disease, neutrophil, vitamin D receptor, CXCL2, lithocholic acid, epithelial-immune crosstalk, NFκB signaling

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn disease (CD), is a chronic immune-mediated disorder affecting the gastrointestinal tract.1 Although the pathophysiological mechanisms of IBD remain incompletely understood, alterations in metabolites resulting from host-microbiota interactions play a critical role in IBD pathogenesis.2 Indeed, fecal metabolomic studies of IBD patients have consistently identified dysregulation of bile acid metabolism as a key feature of IBD.3,4

Cholesterol is converted into primary bile acids (PBAs) via two distinct enzymatic pathways. The classical pathway is mediated by cytochrome P450 family 7 subfamily A member 1 (CYP7A1) and cytochrome P450 family 8 subfamily B member 1 (CYP8B1), producing cholic acid (CA) as the primary product. In contrast, the alternative pathway, with the cytochrome P450 family 7 subfamily B member 1 (CYP7B1) as the rate-limiting enzyme, predominantly generates chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) in humans.5 In mice, CDCA is further converted into muricholic acid (MCA) by cytochrome P450 family 2 subfamily c polypeptide 70 (CYP2C70).6 Both CA and CDCA (MCA in mice) are conjugated with either taurine or glycine and further transformed into deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA), respectively, through a series of gut microbiota-mediated reactions. These include deconjugation by bile salt hydrolases, dehydroxylation by bile acid-inducible (bai) genes, as well as dehydrogenation and isomerization by hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases (HSDHs).7,8,9,10 These transformation patterns indicate that the composition of colonic secondary bile acids is influenced not only by the microbial enzymatic activity but also by the relative contributions of the classical and alternative bile acid synthesis pathways. Notably, increased flux through the classical pathway has been implicated in the development of metabolic disorders such as obesity11 and insulin resistance.12 Conversely, suppression of the classical pathway has been associated with the therapeutic benefits of vertical sleeve gastrectomy (VSG),13 while elevated CDCA levels following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass correlate with improved metabolic outcomes.14 However, whether dysregulation of the balance between the classical and alternative pathways also contributes to the altered bile acid profile in IBD remains unclear.

The distribution of metabolic flux between the classical and alternative bile acid synthesis pathways is primarily regulated by the expression levels of key enzymes, including CYP7A1, CYP8B1, and CYP7B1. Hepatic farnesoid X receptor (FXR)-small heterodimer partner (SHP) and ileal FXR-fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF15) signaling pathways are well-established negative regulators of CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 expression.15,16 CDCA, the most potent endogenous agonist of FXR, is an approved therapeutic agent for cholesterol gallstones and cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis.17,18 Recent studies have also suggested potential therapeutic applications of CDCA in acute myeloid leukemia19 and acute pancreatitis.20 Additionally, in healthy individuals, oral administration of CDCA increases brown adipose tissue activity and enhances whole-body energy expenditure.21 However, CDCA’s therapeutic efficacy in patients with IBD remains unexplored.

CDCA is metabolized by gut microbiota into LCA and its derivatives, which are the most potent endogenous agonist of the vitamin D receptor (VDR) among bile acids.22 Notably, intestinal epithelial VDR expression is significantly reduced in IBD patients compared to healthy individuals,23 with colonic VDR levels inversely correlating with the severity of intestinal inflammation.24 Although clinical trials have explored the therapeutic effects of vitamin D supplementation in IBD, a pooled analysis of 1,127 patients revealed that while vitamin D supplementation reduces serum C-reactive protein levels, it does not significantly improve erythrocyte sedimentation rate, disease activity index, or relapse rate in IBD patients.25 Crucially, the clinical utility of high-affinity VDR agonists, such as vitamin D, is often constrained by the risk of systemic hypercalcemia,26 underscoring the need for a localized regulatory strategy that can target intestinal VDR without inducing systemic toxicity. In contrast, LCA is produced locally within the gut through microbiota-mediated conversion of CDCA. In addition, LCA’s lower affinity compared to 1,25(OH)2D3 further minimizes the risk of systemic side effects. Given these factors, it is important to investigate whether CDCA/LCA offer a safer, more targeted approach to mitigating IBD.

Despite these insights, key questions about bile acid synthesis in IBD remain. This study examines whether chronic inflammation shifts metabolic flux between the classical and alternative pathways, and whether redirecting this flux can alleviate intestinal inflammation. It further clarifies how specific BA metabolites regulate interactions between the intestinal epithelium and the immune system.

Results

Colitis promotes classical and suppresses alternative bile acid synthesis pathways in IBD patients and colitic mice

To investigate whether patients with IBD exhibit altered hepatic bile acid synthesis flux between classical and alternative pathways (Figure 1A), we calculated the ratio of CA and its derivatives (CAs) to CDCA and its derivatives (CDCAs) using fecal bile acid profiles from the Integrative Human Microbiome Project (iHMP).27 This dataset includes six primary and thirteen secondary bile acids (Figure S1A). Patients who had undergone bowel surgery were excluded from the analysis due to the potential confounding effects of surgery on bile acid metabolism.28 Our results revealed that the CAs/CDCAs ratio was significantly higher in individuals with IBD compared to healthy controls (Figure 1B). When stratifying by IBD subtype, we found that both CD and UC patients exhibited elevated CAs/CDCAs ratios relative to healthy individuals (Figure S1B). To assess whether this elevation in the CAs/CDCAs ratio persists across disease states, we examined patients in both active and remission phases of IBD. The results demonstrated that CD patients in clinical remission, and UC patients in both active and remission phases, showed significantly increased CAs/CDCAs ratios compared with healthy controls (Figure S1C). There was no significant difference between CD patients in the active phase and healthy individuals, which might be due to the limited samples (Figure S1C). To evaluate systemic bile acid composition, we profiled plasma bile acids from 24 IBD patients and 13 healthy individuals in our hospital, quantifying six primary and eight secondary bile acids. Consistent with the fecal bile acids data, the plasma CAs/CDCAs ratio was significantly elevated in IBD patients compared to healthy subjects (Figures 1C, S1D, and S1E; Table S1).

To further validate the above findings, mice were challenged with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) to induce colonic inflammation, and bile acid profiles from the stool and serum were analyzed (Figures S1F and S1G). Unlike humans, the terminal product of the alternative bile acid synthesis pathway in mice is not CDCA. Instead, a substantial portion of CDCA converted into MCA, which is subsequently conjugated with taurine (Figure 1A). Therefore, to estimate the metabolic flux ratio between the classical and alternative bile acid synthesis pathway in mice, we used the ratio of CA species to the sum of MCA species and CDCA species (CAs/MCAs+CDCAs). This CAs/MCAs+CDCAs ratio significantly increased in the stool and serum of colitis-induced mice compared to controls (Figures 1D and 1E). Correspondingly, the expression of enzymes involved in the classical bile acid synthesis pathway, CYP7A1 and CYP8B1, were upregulated in colitis mice, while the expression of the alternative pathway enzyme CYP7B1 was downregulated at both mRNA and protein levels (Figures 1F and 1G).

These results collectively indicate that colitis is associated with an increased classical bile acid synthesis pathway, accompanied by a suppression of the alternative pathway.

CDCA administration redirects bile acid synthesis toward the alternative pathway and alleviates colitis

The distribution of metabolic flux between the classical and alternative bile acid synthesis pathways is primarily regulated by the expression of their respective key enzymes. Previous studies have shown that the expression of CYP7A1 and CYP8B1,15,16 critical enzymes in the classical pathway, is predominantly suppressed by FXR activation. Moreover, CDCA has been reported to inhibit liver X receptor alpha (LXRα), thereby upregulating CYP7B1 expression via retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor alpha (RORα).29,30,31,32 Based on these findings, we hypothesized that oral administration of CDCA may attenuate hyperactivation of the classical pathway while enhancing the alternative pathway. Furthermore, we aimed to investigate whether this metabolic shift in bile acid synthesis contributes to alleviating colitic symptoms.

To investigate whether oral administration of CDCA could suppress the classical pathway in colitis, we employed a DSS-induced colitis mouse model and administered CDCA or vehicle. Bile acid profiles were analyzed in stool and serum samples collected from colitic mice with or without CDCA treatment. In stool samples, nine major classes of bile acids were identified, with CDCA-treated mice showing a marked increase in CDCA species and a significant decrease in CA species compared to controls (Figure S2A). Similarly, seven major classes of bile acids were detected in serum samples, with the CDCA-treated group showing significant elevations in CDCA and LCA species and a notable reduction in CA species compared to controls (Figure S2B). As anticipated, CDCA treatment significantly lowered the CAs/MCAs+CDCAs ratio in stool and serum samples (Figures 2A and 2B). In line with these findings, hepatic expression of CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 was reduced, and hepatic CYP7B1 was significantly upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in CDCA-treated colitis mice (Figures 2C and 2D). These results demonstrated that CDCA treatment led to a suppression of the classical bile acid synthesis pathway and an enhancement of the alternative bile acid synthesis pathway.

It is well established that hepatic CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 are negatively regulated by hepatic FXR-SHP signaling and ileal FXR-FGF15 pathway. To investigate the involvement of these mechanisms, we first examined the expression levels of hepatic FXR and SHP. Our results showed that colitic mice treated with CDCA exhibited increased hepatic SHP levels, although the expression levels of FXR do not exhibit a significant difference between CDCA-treated and vehicle-treated groups (Figure S2C). Consistent with these findings, the concentration and proportion of hepatic CDCAs were significantly elevated in CDCA-treated colitic mice compared to vehicle-treated controls (Figures S2D and S2E). Notably, the FXR antagonist MCAs did not differ significantly between colitic mice with or without CDCA treatment (Figures S2D and S2E). In the ileum, CDCA treatment significantly upregulated FGF15 expression (Figure S2F). Additionally, CDCA administration increased both the concentrations and proportions of CDCAs and LCAs. Although the concentration of MCAs was elevated in the CDCA-treated group, its relative percentage decreased, likely due to the overall expansion of the bile acid pool induced by exogenous CDCA administration (Figures S2G and S2H). In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that hepatic Cyp7b1 expression is induced by RORα and negatively regulated by LXRα,29,30,32 and CDCA can suppress LXR activation.31 In accordance with these reports, our data showed that CDCA treatment significantly suppressed hepatic LXRα expression while concurrently increasing RORα level (Figure S2I). These results suggested that CDCA administration suppressed hepatic Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1 through activation of hepatic and ileal FXR signaling while enhancing Cyp7b1 expression at least partially via modulation of the LXRα-RORα axis.

IBD is characterized by body weight loss, shortened colon length, and bloody diarrhea.33 As shown in Figure 2E, oral administration of CDCA significantly reduced body weight loss in mice exposed to DSS. In addition, CDCA treatment markedly restored colon length (Figure 2F) and reduced the disease activity index (DAI), a clinical measure of colitis severity (Figure 2G). Histological analysis of colon tissue revealed preservation of normal crypt architecture in CDCA-treated mice, in contrast to the vehicle-treated mice, which exhibited epithelial cell loss and elevated pathological scores (Figure 2H). The expression levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL6, TNFα, and IL1β, were downregulated in CDCA-treated mice (Figure 2I). Similarly, TNBS-induced body weight loss and colon shortening were alleviated by CDCA administration (Figures S2J and S2K). CDCA administration also reduced DAI and epithelial damage, as shown by the HE score (Figures S2L and S2M). In addition, CDCA administration reduced the expression levels of IL6, TNFα, and IL1β (Figure S2N).

To assess the safety of CDCA administration under physiological conditions, we evaluated its effects in the absence of DSS challenge. As shown in Figures 2E–2H, CDCA alone did not significantly alter body weight, colon length, DAI, and intestinal epithelial structure. Given that bile acids can contribute to cholestatic liver injury,34 we measure serum levels of alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) in CDCA- and vehicle-treated mice. No significant differences were observed in ALT and AST levels between the two groups (Figures S2O and S2P). Additionally, histological examination of liver tissue confirmed that CDCA, at the administered dose, did not induce liver injury (Figure S2Q).

These findings suggested that CDCA treatment changed bile acid profiles and the expression levels of bile acid synthesis enzymes, indicating a shift from the classical to the alternative bile acid synthesis pathway. Furthermore, CDCA treatment alleviates colitis symptoms without inducing adverse hepatic or intestinal effects.

Oral CDCA administration modulated the colonic immune microenvironment

It is well established that bile acids influence various aspects of the intestinal microenvironment, including the regulation of intestinal barrier integrity and immune responses.7 However, the mechanisms underlying CDCA’s anti-inflammatory effects remain poorly understood. To investigate this, we first conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on colonic epithelial cells (CECs) isolated from colitic mice treated with CDCA or vehicle. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of immune-related pathways, along with limited signaling pathways associated with intestinal barrier function (Figure 3A).

To further characterize immune modulation, CD45+ cells from the colonic lamina propria (cLP) of colitic mice were isolated and subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Unsupervised clustering of 13,175 cells from four CDCA-treated mice and four vehicle-treated mice revealed six dominant cell populations (Figure 3B), defined based on signature gene expression profiles35 (Figures 3C–3E; Table S2). Although a small number of CD45-negative cells, such as epithelial cells, endothelial cells and fibroblasts, were present, the dominant immune populations consisted of T and natural killer (T&NK) cells, and myeloid cells (Figure 3B).

Notably, CDCA treatment led to a marked reduction in the proportion of myeloid cells, decreasing from 47.63% in vehicle-treated mice to 13.26% in CDCA-treated mice (Figures 3F and 3G). Conversely, the T&NK cell population expanded in CDCA-treated mice, constituting approximately 57.86%, compared to 34.69% in vehicle-treated mice (Figure 3G). These findings suggest that oral administration of CDCA reshaped the immune cell landscape in colitic mice, particularly the myeloid cells and T&NK cells.

Oral CDCA administration attenuates colonic inflammation by reducing pro-inflammatory neutrophil accumulation

Next, we investigated the role of myeloid cells and T&NK cells in mediating the anti-inflammatory effects of CDCA. Within the myeloid compartment, four major subpopulations were identified, monocytes, mast cells, macrophages and neutrophils, based on canonical marker gene expression profiles35,36 (Figures 4A–4C; Table S3). The comparative analysis demonstrated a notable reduction in neutrophils and a modest increase in macrophages in the CDCA-treated group compared to vehicle controls (Figure 4D). To further characterize the dynamics of macrophages and neutrophils during colitis, we performed flow cytometry analysis of cLP macrophages and neutrophil abundances. Mice treated with CDCA for 9 days without DSS challenge were sacrificed on day 0. The remaining mice were administered 2% DSS for 6 days, followed by 3 days of water, with or without CDCA treatment during these 9 days. Additional mice were sacrificed on day 5 (quiescent phase of colitis) and day 9 (active phase) (Figure S3A). There were no significant differences in the overall abundance of CD45+ cells between CDCA- and vehicle-treated groups at any time point (days 0, 5, and 9), suggesting that CDCA did not broadly alter immune cell infiltration (Figure S3B). Longitudinal analysis revealed that oral CDCA administration did not significantly affect the proportion of cLP macrophages over time (Figure S3C). However, neutrophils markedly expanded during the active phase of colitis (day 9), and CDCA treatment significantly reduced both the percentage and absolute number of neutrophils in the cLP (Figures 4E and S3D), consistent with the scRNA-seq findings (Figure 4D). Immunohistochemical staining of colon sections collected on day 9 with the neutrophil marker Ly6G further confirmed this reduction: CDCA-treated mice had significantly fewer Ly6G-positive cells compared to vehicle-treated mice (Figure 4F). To explore the functional properties of these neutrophils, we conducted KEGG enrichment analysis on the neutrophil population using scRNA-seq. The results indicated that the neutrophil population diminished by CDCA treatment was associated with several pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (Figure S3E). Moreover, these neutrophils expressed pro-inflammatory marker genes and lacked markers of exhausted neutrophils (Figure S3F). Together, these results indicated that CDCA treatment reduced pro-inflammatory neutrophils in the colonic lamina propria during colitis.

To investigate whether the anti-inflammatory effect of CDCA depended on the reduction of neutrophils, we performed neutrophil adoptive transfer experiments. Donor mice were treated with 2% DSS for 6 days, followed by 3 days of water, after which Ly6G+ and Ly6G− cells were isolated from spleens with high purity (Figure S3G). Recipient mice received the same DSS treatment, and on day 5, they were injected with either Ly6G+ or Ly6G− cells (Figures S3H and 4G). As shown in Figures 4H–4J, the transfer of neutrophils (Ly6G+ cells) into CDCA-treated mice resulted in DSS-induced colon shortening, elevated clinical colitis scores, and histologic abnormalities comparable to those observed in vehicle-treated mice. In contrast, CDCA-treated mice that received Ly6G− cells exhibited no significant differences in colon length or DAI and histological scores relative to untreated CDCA recipients (Figures 4H–4J).

These findings suggest that the anti-inflammatory effect of oral CDCA administration is, at least in part, mediated through the reduction of pro-inflammatory neutrophils in the colon.

The anti-inflammatory activity of oral CDCA administration is T cell independent

T&NK cells, alongside myeloid cells, represented the predominant immune cell clusters in the colonic lamina propria of colitic mice (Figure 3F). Notably, the proportion of T&NK cells significantly increased, rising from 34.69% in vehicle-treated mice to 57.86% in CDCA-treated mice (Figure 3G). Given the established roles of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and their subsets in regulating colonic inflammation,37,38 as well as the influence of neutrophils on T cell-mediated immune responses,39 we sought to investigate whether T cells contributed to the anti-inflammatory effects observed following CDCA treatment.

Within the T&NK cell cluster, two distinct subpopulations were identified: T cells, characterized by expression of Cd3d, Cd3e, and Cd3g, and NK cells, marked by Ncr1, Klrk1, and Klrb1c. Among these, T cells were the predominant subset (Figures S4A and S4B). To further evaluate T cell dynamics during colitis, we performed flow cytometry on colonic tissues collected at multiple time points. Mice treated with CDCA for 9 days without DSS challenge were sacrificed on day 0. The remaining mice were administered 2% DSS for 6 days, followed by 3 days of water, with or without CDCA treatment, and were sacrificed on day 5 (quiescent phase of colitis) and day 9 (active phase) of colitis. Time course analysis revealed a significant expansion of total CD3+ T cells in CDCA-treated mice during the acute phase (day 9) (Figure S4C). CD8+ and CD4+ T cells exhibited similar patterns, although the increase in CD8+ T cells did not reach statistical significance. CDCA treatment led to an increase in both CD8+ and CD4+ T cell populations on day 9, with no significant difference observed at earlier time points (day 0 and 5) (Figures S4D and S4E). Given the critical roles of CD4+ T cell subsets, including Th17 and Treg cells, in modulating colitis progression,40 we also assess their frequencies. However, the proportions of Treg (CD4+FOXP3+) and Th17 (CD4+IL-17+) cells did not differ significantly between CDCA-treated and vehicle-treated mice (Figures S4F and S4G).

To further assess the involvement of T cells in CDCA-mediated protection, we utilized nude mice, which lack mature T cells.41 These mice were subjected to DSS-induced colitis and orally administered CDCA. Remarkably, despite the absence of T cells, CDCA-treated nude mice exhibit reduced weight loss, less colon shortening, and improved DAI and histological scores compared to vehicle controls (Figures S4H–S4K). Furthermore, during the acute phase of colitis, CDCA treatment led to a significant reduction in colonic neutrophil abundance in nude mice (Figure S4L).

Collectively, these findings indicate that while CDCA may also modulate T cell populations during colitis, its anti-inflammatory effects are independent of T cells.

Oral CDCA administration restricts neutrophil recruitment by downregulating epithelial CXCL2 expression

To investigate the mechanisms by which CDCA treatment limits the accumulation of pro-inflammatory neutrophils in the colon, we first performed GO enrichment analysis on the neutrophil subcluster (Figure 4A). This analysis revealed significant enrichment in the neutrophil chemotaxis pathway (Figure 5A). To identify which chemokines contribute to neutrophil recruitment in the context of CDCA treatment, we measured the expression levels of Mif, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl5, Cxcl7, Cxcl12, Ccl2, Ccl3, Ccl4, and Ccl5 in colon tissues from colitic mice treated with either CDCA or vehicle. Among these chemokines, only Cxcl2 was significantly reduced (∼90%) in CDCA-treated mice compared to vehicle-treated controls, while the others showed no statistically significant differences between CDCA-treated and vehicle-treated groups (Figure 5B). CXCL2 is known to mediate neutrophil migration by binding to its receptor CXCR2.42 Flow cytometric analysis of cLP cells showed that CXCR2+ cells comprised the majority of cLP neutrophils, and all cLP CXCR2+ cells were neutrophils (Figure S5A).

To determine whether CXCL2 reduction is required for CDCA protection, we neutralized CXCL2 with a specific antibody in DSS-treated mice. Administration of the anti-CXCL2 antibody significantly preserved body weight (Figure S5B) and colon length (Figure 5C) and reduced the DAI and histological scores (Figures S5C and S5D). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that CXCL2 blockade markedly decreased infiltrating neutrophils in the colonic lamina propria (Figure S5E). Notably, combined treatment with CDCA and anti-CXCL2 did not yield further improvements in either disease indices or neutrophil recruitment compared to anti-CXCL2 alone (Figures 5C and S5B–S5E). Together, these findings demonstrate that CXCL2 is the key mediator of CDCA’s suppression of neutrophil infiltration and colitis.

Previous studies have identified CECs are an important source of CXCL2.43,44 To verify this, we isolated CECs (EpCAM+) and non-CECs (EpCAM−) from colitis mice with or without CDCA treatment and assessed Cxcl2 levels by qPCR (Figure S5F). In vehicle-treated mice, Cxcl2 levels were significantly higher in CECs compared with non-CECs, confirming CECs as an important source of CXCL2 (Figure 5D). Moreover, Cxcl2 expression in CECs was significantly lower in CDCA-treated mice compared to vehicle-treated mice, indicating that oral CDCA administration inhibited colonic epithelial Cxcl2 production (Figure 5D). To further evaluate whether oral administration of CDCA modulates Cxcl2 expression via changes in colonic contents, we performed an in vitro stimulation assay.45 CECs isolated from healthy C57BL/6 mice were cultured with fecal extracts (FEs) derived from colitic mice treated with CDCA or vehicle. FEs from both groups induced Cxcl2 expression in primary CECs, supporting the idea that colonic contents can stimulate colonic epithelial Cxcl2 production (Figure 5E). Notably, Cxcl2 levels induced by FEs from CDCA-treated mice were significantly lower than those induced by FEs from vehicle-treated mice, suggesting that CDCA alters luminal signals that regulate CXCL2 expression (Figure 5E). To corroborate these findings, colon tissue sections from mice during active colitis were co-stained for CXCL2 and the epithelial marker EpCAM. CXCL2 and EpCAM co-localized, and the CXCL2 signal was markedly stronger in vehicle-treated mice compared to CDCA-treated mice, further supporting that CDCA reduces colonic epithelial CXCL2 expression (Figure 5F).

To determine the requirement of VDR in LCA-mediated immune regulation, we performed Transwell chemotaxis assays. We used conditioned media from Vdr-silenced (siVDR) or control (siNC) MODE-K cells, and the knockdown effect is shown in Figure S5G. In the control group, TNFα stimulation caused a robust increase in neutrophil migration, which was significantly reduced by LCA treatment. Notably, the inhibition by LCA was similar to that achieved by a specific neutralizing antibody against CXCL2 (Figure 5G). Conversely, in VDR-knockdown cells, the anti-migratory effect of LCA was abolished (Figure 5G), whereas the anti-CXCL2 antibody retained its efficacy in suppressing migration. These data showed that LCA requires VDR to suppress CXCL2-mediated neutrophil recruitment.

To determine whether the suppression of epithelial CXCL2 is functionally important for CDCA’s protective effects, we generated intestinal epithelium-specific CXCL2-overexpressing mice by injecting adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors into villin-cre mice (Figure S5H). These mice were then subjected to DSS-induced colitis, with or without concurrent CDCA treatment (Figure S5I). In AAV-control mice, CDCA treatment significantly ameliorated colitis, as evidenced by reduced colon shortening (Figure 5H), preserved crypt architecture (Figure 5I), and lower DAI scores (Figure 5J). However, these protective effects were absent in mice overexpressing CXCL2 in the epithelium (Figures 5H–5J). Additionally, flow cytometric analysis confirmed that CDCA-mediated suppression of colonic neutrophils was diminished in these CXCL2-overexpressing mice (Figure 5K).

Collectively, these findings suggest that CDCA could alleviate colitis by suppressing CXCL2 expression in CECs, thereby limiting neutrophil accumulation in the colon.

Oral administration of CDCA downregulates colonic epithelial CXCL2 in a VDR-dependent manner

To investigate the mechanism by which oral administration of CDCA suppresses colonic epithelial CXCL2 expression, CECs were isolated from colitic mice treated with either CDCA or vehicle. RNA sequencing was performed on the isolated cells to assess gene expression profiles. The fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were analyzed for several bile acid receptors and their downstream target genes. These included Tgr5 (with downstream targets cAMP and Glp1); the pregnane X receptor (Pxr, also known as Nr1l2) with Cyp3a11 and Slco1a4; the Vdr with Cyp24a1 and S100g, the Fxr with Fgf15 and Ostα, the liver X receptor (Lxr, also known as Nr1h3) with Abca1, Abcg5 and Abcg8, and the constitutive androstane receptor (Car, also known as Nr1l3) with Gsta1 and Gsta4. Among these, only the Vdr signaling pathway was activated in the CDCA-treated group (Figure 6A). qPCR analysis of CECs validated the RNA-seq findings (Figure 6B), and western blot analysis further confirmed that CDCA treatment significantly upregulated VDR protein levels (Figure 6C). In addition, the expression of Vdr downstream target genes, S100g and Cyp24a1, was significantly upregulated following CDCA treatment (Figure 6D), supporting the activation of the VDR signaling pathway in response to oral CDCA administration.

To determine whether Vdr mediates the CDCA-induced downregulation of colonic epithelial CXCL2 expression in vitro, we used siRNA to knock down VDR in human colorectal cancer cell lines HT29 and SW620 (Figures S6A and S6B). The cells were then cultured with FEs obtained from CDCA-treated or vehicle-treated mice with active colitis. FEs from CDCA-treated mice significantly reduced Cxcl2 levels in wild-type HT-29 and SW620 cells, but this effect was abolished in VDR-knockdown cells (Figures 6E and 6F). Additionally, CECs from intestinal epithelium-specific VDR knockout mice (VDRΔIEC) and their wild-type littermates (VDRfl/fl) (Figures S6C and S6D) were cultured with FEs from CDCA-treated or vehicle-treated colitic mice. The results showed that Cxcl2 expression was significantly reduced in CECs from VDRfl/fl mice, but not in those from VDRΔIEC mice (Figure 6G). These results demonstrate that the suppression of colonic epithelial CXCL2 expression by CDCA is dependent on VDR signaling.

To investigate whether VDR mediates the CDCA-induced downregulation of colonic epithelial CXCL2 in vivo, VDRfl/fl and VDRΔIEC mice were challenged with DSS and treated with either CDCA or vehicle. In VDRfl/fl mice, CDCA treatment significantly alleviated disease severity, as evidenced by reduced weight loss, less colon shortening, and improved DAI and histological score. These protective effects were not seen in the VDRΔIEC mice (Figures 6H–6K). Consistently, CDCA treatment decreased colonic epithelial CXCL2 mRNA and protein expression in the VDRfl/fl mice, but not in VDRΔIEC mice (Figures 6L and 6M). Furthermore, CDCA reduced the number of cLP neutrophils in VDRfl/fl mice, an effect absent in VDRΔIEC counterparts (Figure 6N). These results indicate that the protective effects of CDCA in colitis are dependent on colonic epithelial VDR signaling.

Previous studies demonstrated that VDR activation can suppress the NF-κB pathway46,47,48 and Cxcl2 is a target gene of the NF-κB pathway.46 To elucidate the mechanisms by which VDR activation downregulates colonic epithelial CXCL2 expression, CECs were isolated from colitic VDRfl/fl and VDRΔIEC mice treated with either CDCA or vehicle and underwent RNA-seq. Consistently, KEGG analysis revealed enrichment of the NF-κB signaling pathway in CDCA-treated VDRΔIEC mice compared to CDCA-treated VDRfl/fl mice (Figure S6E). A heatmap analysis showed upregulation of NF-κB pathway-related genes in CDCA-treated VDRΔIEC mice but not in CDCA-treated VDRfl/fl counterparts (Figure S6F), a finding further supported by gene set enrichment analysis (Figure S6G). To assess whether NF-κB signaling mediated the VDR-dependent suppression of CXCL2, we examined pathway activity in wild-type and VDRΔIEC mice. CDCA treatment significantly downregulated NF-κB pathway genes in VDRfl/fl mice, compared to vehicle-treated controls (Figures S6H and S6I). However, in VDRΔIEC mice, CDCA failed to suppress NF-κB pathway gene expression (Figures S6J and S6K), suggesting that CDCA inhibits NF-κB signaling in a VDR-dependent manner. Further, we assessed the phosphorylation status of NF-κB P65 (P-P65) in primary CECs. CDCA treatment reduced P-P65 levels in CECs from VDRfl/fl mice (Figure S6L), whereas no such effect was observed in VDRΔIEC mice (Figure S6M). Collectively, these findings indicate that CDCA treatment suppresses colonic epithelial CXCL2 expression, at least in part, through the VDR-NFκB signaling axis.

Discussion

In patients with IBD, bile acid dysregulation is characterized by elevated PBAs and reduction in secondary bile acids, primarily due to diminished gut microbiota diversity and activity.50 Although gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in modulating the bile acid pool, it remains unclear whether colitis affects the metabolic flux distribution between the classical and alternative bile acid synthesis pathways in the liver. Recent studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of the alternative bile acid synthesis pathway in metabolic disorders, such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and diabetes, primarily mediated by HDCA and HCA species, which regulate hepatic lipid metabolism.51,52 However, we observed an increased flux through the classical bile acid synthesis pathway, evidenced by elevated CAs/CDCAs in patients with IBD, and increased CAs/MCAs+CDCAs ratio in DSS-challenged mice compared to healthy controls. Consistent with our findings, Chen et al. reported elevated CA levels in both UC patients and DSS-induced colitic mice.53 Previous studies have similarly demonstrated the upregulation of hepatic CYP7A1 and CYP8B1, key enzymes in the classical pathway, in DSS-induced colitic mice.53,54 In agreement, we observed increased expression of both CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 in colitic mice alongside reduced expression of CYP7B1, a critical enzyme in the alternative pathway. Altogether, these findings indicate that colitis enhances the classical bile acid synthesis but suppresses the alternative pathway in both IBD patients and mouse models.

To address the imbalance between the classical and alternative bile acid synthesis pathways, CDCA was orally administered to DSS-challenged mice. This treatment increased CDCA levels in both the liver and ileum, enhancing hepatic FXR-SHP and ileal FXR-FGF15 signaling, which in turn suppressed hepatic CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 expression. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that administration of CDCA, or the FXR agonists GW4064 and obeticholic acid (OCA), markedly represses CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 expression in mice, thereby reducing metabolic flux through the classical bile acid synthesis pathway.55,56 In contrast, our study revealed that CDCA administration significantly upregulated hepatic CYP7B1 expression, accompanied by reduced hepatic LXRα and elevated RORα levels. While direct transcriptional regulation was not examined in the current study, these expression patterns align with established mechanisms where CDCA inhibits LXRα,31 and LXRα is known to suppress CYP7B1 promoter activity, a transcriptional target of RORα.29,30,32 Overall, these results suggest that CDCA treatment shifts the bile acid synthesis pathway toward the alternative pathway in colitic mice, potentially mediated by the modulation of LXRα-RORα axis.

It is well established that CDCA is metabolized into LCA by gut microbiota. Consistent with this, we observed that oral CDCA administration increased colonic LCA levels. Previous studies have shown that UDCA, which is similarly converted to LCA in the colon, or direct LCA administration, alleviates colitis in mice, although the underlying mechanisms remained unclear.57,58 Sinha et al. demonstrated that LCA enemas mitigate DSS-induced colitis by modulating myeloid cells through TGR5 activation.59 In agreement with these findings, our study showed that intrarectal LCA administration exerted anti-inflammatory effects comparable to oral CDCA administration. Moreover, we identified a distinct mechanism in which LCA mitigates DSS-induced colitis via activation of colonic epithelial VDR signaling.

Previous studies have demonstrated that VDR signaling is essential for intestinal homeostasis. VDR deficiency in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) exacerbates colitis, as VDR activation inhibits IEC apoptosis and preserves the mucosal barrier by suppressing the NF-κB pathway.60 Mechanistically, this suppression has been attributed to direct molecular interactions, where VDR physically complexes with the NF-κB p65 subunit to prevent its nuclear translocation61 or stabilizes the inhibitor IκBα and reduces p65 phosphorylation.48 Our current study provides functional support for this model in the context of bile acid treatment. We demonstrate that LCA suppresses Cxcl2 expression, a canonical NF-κB target, in a strictly VDR-dependent manner. This suggests that LCA likely harnesses the established VDR-NFκB interference machinery to dampen inflammation. Moreover, VDR deficiency in Paneth cells has been associated with impaired antimicrobial function, further contributing to intestinal inflammation.62,63 Additionally, bile acid-mediated VDR activation has been shown to increase RORγ+ Treg cells and alleviate DSS-induced colitis.64 These findings highlight epithelial VDR activation as a promising strategy for colitis management.

However, the clinical translation of this concept via vitamin D supplementation faces significant hurdles. Calcitriol is the most potent natural VDR ligand, but it carries a substantial risk of inducing hypercalcemia.26 In contrast, LCA’s relatively lower affinity prevents systemic spillover and hypercalcemic toxicity.65 Meanwhile, oral CDCA is metabolized by the local microbiota into LCA directly at the site of inflammation, ensuring sufficient VDR activation within the colonic mucosa. Thus, CDCA represents a safer and potentially more efficacious strategy than traditional vitamin D supplementation for harnessing VDR-mediated immunomodulation in IBD.

Although our data identify the LCA-VDR-CXCL2 axis as a key driver of reduced neutrophil-mediated inflammation, the overall therapeutic efficacy of CDCA in colitis may not rely solely on this pathway. CDCA also acts as a primary FXR agonist, a receptor vital for intestinal homeostasis. Notably, previous studies have shown that FXR activation suppresses inflammatory signaling via the FGF15 feedback loop54 and promotes epithelial repair by enhancing Lgr5+ intestinal stem cell renewal.53 Thus, CDCA may activate FXR to support barrier reconstruction, meanwhile, allowing its metabolite, LCA, to engage VDR to modulate innate immunity. While our study centers on VDR-mediated immune regulation, potential synergy between FXR-mediated barrier protection and VDR-mediated anti-inflammation needs further investigation. This mechanism may explain CDCA’s effectiveness in the complex pathological environment of IBD.

In summary, our study revealed an increased flux through the classical and a decreased flux through the alternative bile acid synthesis pathway in both IBD patients and DSS-induced colitic mice. To address this imbalance, we investigated the FDA-approved bile acid CDCA, which suppressed hepatic CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 and upregulated CYP7B1 to shift the bile acid synthesis toward the alternative pathway. This metabolic shift was accompanied by significant alleviation of colitis symptoms. Mechanistically, CDCA exerted anti-inflammatory effects by converting to LCA in the gut, which reduced colonic neutrophil accumulation via downregulation of colonic epithelial CXCL2 through the VDR-NFκB pathway. Together, these findings highlight a previously unknown dysregulated hepatic bile acid metabolism in IBD and demonstrate that oral CDCA administration can restore metabolic balance and alleviate colitis by modulating epithelial-immune crosstalk, supporting its potential as a therapeutic agent for IBD (Figure S7).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although we demonstrated the anti-inflammatory activity of CDCA in a preclinical model, its therapeutic efficacy in patients remains untested. Clinically, CDCA is approved for the dissolution of cholesterol gallstones; however, this treatment typically requires more than 6 months to produce noticeable effects.17 The long-term CDCA administration may lead to hepatotoxicity, which is generally reversible upon discontinuation.17 This highlights the necessity of monitoring liver function during CDCA treatment. Second, IBD patients are at a higher risk of developing gallstones, possibly due to cholesterol-supersaturated bile caused by bile acid malabsorption.66,67 Whether CDCA administration could aid in the prevention of gallstones in IBD patients remains unclear and requires further clinical studies. Third, the conversion of CDCA to its downstream metabolite, LCA, depends on the presence of gut bacteria. Specifically, the bai operon, primarily encoded by Clostridium scindens and its relatives, plays a critical role in the conversion of CDCA to LCA.68 However, the complete biosynthetic pathways responsible for generating LCA and its derivatives are not yet fully understood and likely involve multiple bacterial species.

Materials and methods

Mice

6-week-old male C57BL/6 mice and nude mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. (Shanghai, China). The intestinal epithelial cell-specific knockout VDR mice (VDRΔIEC) are a kind gift from Prof. Chaohui Yu (Department of Gastroenterology, the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine). Mice were pathogen-free and kept in the Laboratory Animal Center of Hangzhou Medical College under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 22°C. The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Zhejiang Center of Laboratory Animals (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) under approval document no. ZJCLA-IACUC-20030105.

Mouse treatment

To induce colitis, C57BL/6 mice were administered 2% DSS (160110, MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) in drinking water for 6 days, followed by 3 days of normal water. In nude mice, the regimen was adjusted to 7 days of 2% DSS followed by 3 days of normal water. For the TNBS model, overnight-fasted mice received an intrarectal administration of 100 mg/kg TNBS (P2297, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) in 50% ethanol, while control mice received 50% ethanol alone. In AAV-mediated intestinal epithelium-specific CXCL2 overexpression mice and their littermates, colitis was induced by providing drinking water containing 2% DSS for 4 days, followed by 2 days of normal drinking water. Colitis in VDRΔIEC mice and their littermates was induced by administering drinking water containing 2% DSS for 4 consecutive days, followed by 3 days of regular drinking water.

Colitis severity was assessed by monitoring daily body weight, measuring colon length, and calculating the DAI based on weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding. Distal colon samples were fixed in formalin, paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with H&E. Histological scoring was conducted by pathologists blinded to sample identity.69 Successful model establishment was confirmed by significant clinical and histological signs of inflammation compared to the control group; only samples meeting these criteria were included in downstream bile acid and molecular analyses.

For CDCA treatment, mice were given drinking water containing 4 mg/mL sodium chenodeoxycholate (S817461, Macklin, Shanghai, China) from the start to the end of the experiment. The LCA treatment is referenced from a previous study, with some modifications made.59 LCA (L812392, Macklin, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in saline at a concentration of 80 mg/mL. The solution was sonicated on ice using an ultrasound protocol of 2 s of sonication followed by a 2-s pause, repeated for a total duration of 5 min. On days 2 and 4, 0.1 mL of LCA solution was administered intrarectally to each mouse using a 1-mL Luer Lock syringe attached to a 23G needle and lubricated polyethylene tubing (0.048 inches outer diameter).

For CXCL2 neutralization in vivo, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 25 μg anti-CXCL2 antibody (MAB452, R&D, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) or isotype control (MAB006, R&D, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA). Antibodies were dissolved in sterile PBS and administered on days 0, 2, and 4 of the DSS challenge.

Measurement of bile acid profile

For stool, ileal contents, or liver samples, 576 μL ice-cold methanol-water (1:1, v/v, containing inter standard) was added to 100 mg of stool; steel balls were then added to the mixture, which was ground using a grinder (60 Hz, 1 min). The sample was subsequently sonicated in an ice-water bath for 10 min and centrifuged at 4°C (12,000 rpm) for 10 min. For serum or plasma samples, 150 μL ice-cold methanol-acetonitrile (2:1, v/v, containing internal standard) was added to 50 μL of serum or plasma samples. The samples were then sonicated in an ice-water bath for 5 min, followed by centrifuging at 4°C (12,000 rpm) for 10 min.

Supernatants from the stool or serum/plasma samples were then filtered through a 0.22-μm organic phase pinhole filter for subsequent UPLC-MS/MS analysis. Prepared samples were stored at −80°C until use. A total of 52 bile acid standards were used to generate a calibration curve (Table S7).

Liquid chromatography was performed using an AB ExionLC (AB SCIEX, Framingham, Massachusetts, USA) with a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 2.6 μm). The injection volume was 5 μL. The mobile phase A was water containing 0.1% formic acid, while the mobile phase B consisted of a methanol-acetonitrile-isopropanol mixture (1:1:1, v/v/v), also containing 0.1% formic acid. The gradient conditions were as follows, with a flow rate of 0.45 mL/min: 0 min A/B (80:20, V/V), 0.5 min A/B (80:20, V/V), 1.5 min A/B (62:38, V/V), 12 min A/B (50:50, V/V), 17.5 min A/B (5:95, V/V), 19 min A/B (5:95, V/V), 19.01 min A/B (80:20, V/V), and 20 min A/B (80:20, V/V). All samples were kept at 4°C during the analysis, with the column temperature set at 45°C. Mass spectrometry was carried out using an AB SCIEX API 6500+ Qtrap System with an electrospray ionization source, operating in both positive and negative ion modes. Nitrogen was used as the collision gas. Additional instrumental parameters were as follows: curtain gas: 35 psi; ion spray voltage: −4,500 V or 5,500 V; ion source temperature: 450°C; Gas1: 55 psi; Gas2: 55 psi. Targeted metabolites were analyzed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The MRM pairs, declustering potentials, and collision energies are optimized for each analyte. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using Analyst software, while SCIEX OS-MQ software was used for the quantification of all metabolites.

Flow cytometry

Colons were longitudinally dissected and washed in cold PBS. Then, each colon was cut into 3- to 5-cm sections and incubated in RPMI-1640 containing 1.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, and 10% (v/v) FBS at 37°C for 5 min with stirring at 220 rpm to remove CECs. The tissues were minced into 1-mm pieces and digested in RPMI-1640 containing 2 mg/mL collagenase type IV and 10% (v/v) FBS at 37°C for 30 min with stirring at 220 rpm. The digested tissue was filtered through 40-μm cell strainers and centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min to collect colonic lamina propria cells. Single-cell suspensions were prepared from blood by sieving and pipetting and then treated with ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysis buffer to eliminate red blood cells.

Anti-CD16/CD32 antibody (101303, Biolegend, San Diego, California, USA) and Human TruStain FcX (422302, Biolegend, San Diego, California, USA) were used to block Fc receptors of mouse and human samples, respectively. Intracellular and intranuclear staining was performed with IC fixation buffer (00–8222, eBioscience, California, USA) and the Foxp3 Fixation/Permeabilization Buffer Set (00–5521, eBioscience, California, USA), respectively, according to the technical data sheet. The antibodies used are listed in Table S8. Flow cytometry analysis was performed on a CytoFLEX LX Flow Cytometer (Beckman, Brea, California, USA), and data were analyzed with CytExpert V2.3 software (Beckman, Brea, California, USA).

Single-cell RNA library construction and sequencing

Mouse colons were collected on day 9 (the acute phase of DSS colitis), and cLP cells were isolated as described above. CD45+ cells were selected with CD45 MicroBeads (130-052-301, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). Single-cell suspensions were loaded on a 10× genomics controller to generate gel beads in emulsion (GEMs). cDNA amplification and library preparation were performed according to the instructions of the 10× Genomic Chromium Single-Cell 3′ kit (V3). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (paired-end multiplexing run, 150 bp) by LC-Bio Technology Co. (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China).

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Illumina bcl2fastq software (version 2.20) was used to convert the sequencing results into FASTQ format. Sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, and single-cell 3′ gene counting were performed with Cell Ranger software, which is available from 10× Genomics (https://support.10xgenomics.com/single-cell-gene-expression/software/overview/welcome). The ensemble genome Mus musculus GCRm38 reference genome was used for analysis. The Cell Ranger output was loaded into Seurat (V3.1.1) for further analysis. Cells with fewer than 500 genes or more than 500 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were removed. We retained cells with less than 25% mitochondrial gene expression. After filtering, there were 8,426 cells in the vehicle-treated group and 4,749 cells in the CDCA-treated group.

Single-cell data visualization

Seurat was used to calculate the expression values of genes, and dimensionality reduction was performed by principal component analysis. The top ten PCs were used to perform clustering and t-SNE analysis according to the shared nearest neighbor graph-based clustering method. Marker genes for each cluster were identified with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with default parameters via FindAllMarkers. The selected marker genes were expressed in more than 10% of cells in a cluster, and the average log fold change was greater than 0.74.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Colons were fixed in a 10% solution of formaldehyde in water, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 6-μm-thick sections and stained with Ly6G. For Ly6G analysis, brown-stained cells were counted manually in 5 fields of view. For immunofluorescence staining, primary antibodies against EpCAM (MA5-12436, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and CXCL2 (AF-452, R&D, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) were used, and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, dihydrochloride (DAPI) was used for nuclear staining. Images were scanned using an Eclipse C1 microscope system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). 5–10 representative images of CXCL2 staining in each sample were obtained by CaseViewer 2.1 software with consistent parameters. Quantification of median fluorescence intensity was performed on each sample by ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Neutrophil adoptive transfer

Donor mice were sacrificed during the acute phase of DSS-induced colitis, and neutrophils were isolated from donor mouse spleens with a Neutrophil Isolation Kit (130-097-658, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). The first fraction containing unbound cells was used as a control (Ly6G−). The purity of the isolated Ly6G+ fraction was more than 90% (Figure S3G). A total of 2 × 106 Ly6G+ or Ly6G− cells were injected into each recipient mouse through the retro-orbital sinus on day 5 of the DSS-induced colitis model (Figure S3H).

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA from tissues and cells was extracted using RNA Isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) combined with chloroform, isopropanol and 75% ethanol. A HiScript Ⅱ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (R212-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) was used for cDNA synthesis. qPCR was performed with a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA) with MagicSYBR Mixture (CW3008M, Cwbio, Taizhou, Jiangsu, China). The expression levels of the targeted genes were normalized to the expression level of GAPDH, and the qPCR primers used in this study are listed in Table S9.

Primary colonic epithelial cell isolation and in vitro experiments

Primary CECs were isolated and cultured as described with modifications.45 Mouse colons were opened longitudinally and irrigated with cold PBS. Each colon was cut into 1-cm pieces and incubated in DMEM with 10% FBS, 1.5 mM EDTA, and 1.0 mM DTT for 10 min at 37°C and 220 rpm. The supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min. CECs were isolated with EpCAM MicroBeads (130-105-958, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). In some experiments, CECs were cultured in a complete culture medium for mouse colonic epithelial cells (CM-M159, Pricella, Wuhan, Hubei, China) and stimulated by adding 2% fecal extract. LCA or CDCA was added at the indicated concentration under certain conditions.

Transwell assay

MODE-K cells were transfected with VDR-targeting siRNA (D-Nano Therapeutics, Beijing, China). After transfection, cells were incubated in low-serum medium with or without 20 μM LCA for 24 h. The cells were washed twice with PBS and then stimulated with 50 ng/mL TNFα (315-01A, Pepro Tech, Cranbury, New Jersey, USA) in serum-free medium for 6 h. Culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove cellular debris, producing conditioned medium (CM).

Primary neutrophils were isolated from healthy C57BL/6 mouse bone marrow using a Mouse Neutrophil Extraction Kit (P8550, Solarbio, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For the chemotaxis assay, neutrophils were suspended in serum-free medium and seeded into upper chambers of Transwell inserts (14321, LABSELECT, Beijing, China) at 2 × 106 cells per well. The lower chambers contained the prepared CM. For neutralization, CM was pre-incubated with anti-CXCL2 antibody (MAB452, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) for 2 h at 37°C before adding neutrophils. The migration assay ran for 3 h at 37°C, after which migrated neutrophils in the lower chamber were counted.

Cell line quality control

Human colorectal cancer cell lines HT-29 (RRID:CVCL_0320), SW620 (RRID:CVCL_0547), and mouse intestinal epithelial cell line MODE-K (RRID:CVCL_B4FG) were purchased from ATCC. The cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based assay. Authentication of both cell lines was performed by short tandem repeat profiling upon receipt and periodically thereafter, and the results were consistent with the reference profiles. Cells were regularly monitored for morphology, proliferation rate, and overall growth characteristics; any cultures showing abnormal behavior were excluded from experiments. All experiments were conducted using cells within 20 passages of resuscitation. These quality control measures were implemented to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the data presented in this study.

Fecal extracts preparation

FEs were prepared with modifications as described previously.45 Mice were sacrificed during the acute phase of DSS-induced colitis. Given that the DSS-induced colitis model results in loose stools and precludes the collection of formed pellets, colon contents were collected from the distal colon (terminal 2–3 cm). These contents were pooled from five mice that were DSS-challenged with either vehicle or CDCA treatment and suspended in PBS at a concentration of 100 mg/mL. The mixture was placed horizontally on a vortex oscillator and oscillated for 15 min. The suspension was then filtered through a 70-μm filter to remove aggregates.

Construction of AAV-mediated intestinal epithelium CXCL2 overexpression mice

AAV9-loxp-Cxcl2 constructs were produced by Genomeditech Co. (Shanghai, China). The loxP site was utilized to specifically overexpress CXCL2 in IECs by injecting the constructs into villin-cre mice. For AAV transduction, 5 × 106 particles were administered via retro-orbital injection to 3- to 4-week-old villin-cre mice once a week for 3 weeks prior to the DSS challenge.

Digital RNA sequencing

CECs were isolated from the colons during the acute phase of DSS-induced colitis. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15596018, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). mRNA was purified using Dynabeads Oligo (dT) (61002, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (18-064-022, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA) was used for cDNA synthesis. Then, 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on an Illumina Novaseq6000 system (LC-Bio Technology Co., Hangzhou, China). To remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences, the raw data were filtered with Cutadapt (https://cutadapt.readthedocs.io/en/stable/, version 1.9). The reads of all samples were aligned to the mouse reference genome by using the HISAT2 version 2–2.0.4 package (https://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/), and UMI-tools were used to remove sequence-dependent bias and amplification noise. Transcript expression levels were estimated with StringTie and Ballgown (http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/ballgown.html), and the expression abundance of mRNAs was measured on the basis of the FPKM value.

Alanine transaminase and aspartate transaminase

Mouse serum was collected by centrifugation at 4°C, and ALT and AST levels were determined with an ALT test kit (ALT01, Purebio, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China) and an AST test kit (AST01, Purebio, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China).

Multi-omics data of individuals with IBD

Fecal bile acid relative abundance was determined from the untargeted metabolic study from iHMP-IBD (https://ibdmdb.org/).27 Measurement of fecal bile acid was normalized against total bile acids for analysis. The counts of CXCL2 were obtained from the Host Transcriptomic data from the iHMP-IBD database.

Samples from individuals with IBD and healthy individuals

Blood samples were obtained from subjects diagnosed with IBD at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). Blood samples from healthy individuals from the physical examination center of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China), were used as controls. All blood samples were obtained and analyzed by flow cytometry within 24 h. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, under approval document no. 20210622-33.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism V.9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). The results are presented as the mean ± SEM. The statistical significance of differences was evaluated by an unpaired or paired Student’s t test or one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The significance of correlations was determined by linear regression analysis. The p values are noted in the figures, as follows: ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. The statistical details are provided in each figure legend.
