Evaluation of the clinical utility of poor-quality embryos using an extended in vitro embryo culture system
Authors: Can CHENG, Shuoping ZHANG, Yifan GU, Fei GONG, Ge LIN, Lizhi LENG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is limited consensus among embryologists regarding the decision to freeze or discard borderline-quality blastocysts. This study aims to investigate the intrinsic developmental potential of blastocysts with poor inner cell mass (ICM) or trophectoderm (TE) quality using an in vitro three-dimensional (3D) culture system that excludes maternal factors. A total of 51 one-pronucleus (1PN)-derived blastocysts were obtained from couples undergoing assisted reproduction technology (ART). The embryos were categorized into three groups: 20 high-quality embryos, 17 embryos with poor ICM, and 14 embryos with poor TE. A 3D culture system was utilized to assess the post-implantation developmental potential of blastocysts with varying grades. Embryonic morphology and diameter were measured to evaluate growth characteristics, and immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the differentiation of embryonic lineages. 35% of high-quality 1PN embryos developed into OCT4 + epiblast (EPI), GATA6 + hypoblast (HYP), and CK7 + TE, along with an amniotic cavity and yolk sac. Blastocysts with poor TE quality demonstrate reasonable developmental potential, whereas those with poor ICM quality are already compromised during implantation. This study provides valuable insights for ART and clinical management of low-quality blastocysts.Keywords: Early post-implantation embryonic development, Extended culture system, Human low-quality blastocysts, Poor inner cell mass quality, Poor trophectoderm quality

Assisted reproductive technology (ART) has achieved significant advancements in the treatment of infertility [1]. The Gardner scoring system, a widely accepted method for grading blastocyst morphology, evaluates embryos based on their expansion and hatching status, as well as the quality of the inner cell mass (ICM) and trophectoderm (TE) [2]. A grade of 3BB is typically considered the minimum threshold for a viable blastocyst [3]. Embryo quality plays a pivotal role in determining pregnancy outcomes, as low-quality blastocysts—such as those with grade C-ICM or C-TE—tend to exhibit diminished implantation potential and poorer prognoses [4]. A common clinical challenge lies in the significant inter-observer variability among embryologists regarding the disposition of borderline blastocysts [5, 6]. Embryologists frequently face difficult decisions about whether to discard or utilize low-quality blastocysts through methods such as biopsy, fresh transfer, or cryopreservation [7]. An anonymous survey conducted among 117 embryologists from six European countries and 29 in vitro fertilization (IVF) centers revealed a divided approach, with 38.7% opting to discard low-quality blastocysts, while 61.3% chose to utilize them [8]. Given the potential financial, emotional, and time-related costs associated with the clinical use of low-quality blastocysts, many clinics elect to discard them [9]. However, live births have been reported following the transfer of low-quality blastocysts, with normal obstetric and perinatal outcomes [10]. This persistent clinical controversy largely stems from the fact that the developmental trajectory of a blastocyst in vivo reflects not only its intrinsic quality but also its intricate interaction with the endometrial environment. Consequently, a live birth after transfer cannot be taken as definitive proof of robust autonomous developmental potential, nor does implantation failure necessarily signify a complete lack thereof. Resolving this ambiguity therefore requires a tool capable of directly evaluating the embryo’s intrinsic developmental capacity, independent of maternal factors.

Embryo implantation, a critical step in human reproduction, typically occurs between 7 and 12 days post-fertilization. During this period, the implanting embryo consists of three distinct lineages: the extra-embryonic TE, the hypoblast (HYP), and the embryonic epiblast (EPI) [11]. However, up to 30% of embryos fail to implant during this crucial period, leading to early pregnancy loss [12]. Due to ethical constraints on in vivo studies, the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying human embryo implantation remain poorly understood. Recent advancements in in vitro two-dimensional (2D) [13, 14] and three-dimensional (3D) culture systems [15] have allowed human embryos to develop beyond the implantation stage in the absence of maternal tissues. Notably, embryos cultured in a 3D system closely replicate in vivo embryonic development. The EPI undergoes morphological remodeling to form a pseudostratified columnar epithelium with an amniotic cavity. The HYP gives rise to the yolk sac, while the TE differentiates into three major subpopulations: cytotrophoblast (CTB), syncytiotrophoblast (STB), and extravillous cytotrophoblast (EVT). Critically, a 3D culture system is not influenced by various confounding factors present in the maternal environment, such as congenital anomalies, acquired uterine diseases [16], endometrial thickness [17], hormonal fluctuations, inflammatory processes [18], and the composition of the endometrial microbiota [19]. By removing these variables, an in vitro system uniquely allows for the assessment of an embryo’s intrinsic developmental program directly through its post-implantation morphogenesis and lineage differentiation. Therefore, we hypothesized that applying such a system to grade C-ICM or C-TE blastocysts would reveal their unconfounded developmental fates. The primary objective of this study was to determine whether blastocysts with poor ICM or TE quality have the intrinsic capacity to progress through peri-implantation development by directly comparing their post-implantation growth and lineage segregation with those of good-quality embryos in an established in vitro 3D culture system.

Materials and Methods

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Human subjects, human material, and human data involved in our study all abide by the rules set in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Reproductive and Genetic Hospital of CITIC-Xiangya (LL-SC-2021-023) and conducted in accordance with its guidelines and supervision. Fresh one-pronucleus (1PN) and two pronuclei (2PN)-derived blastocysts were obtained from couples undergoing ART treatment on day 6 post-fertilization, with informed consent obtained from all participating donor couples. No embryos used in this study had been previously cryopreserved. No financial incentives were provided for the donations.

In vitro culture of human embryos beyond implantation

The day when 1PN/2PN zygotes were observed was designated as 0 days post-fertilization (d.p.f.). All embryonic ages referred to in this study are expressed as d.p.f. Blastocysts were evaluated and graded on 6 d.p.f. The morphological grading of 1PN and 2PN blastocysts was performed by two senior embryologists using the Gardner scoring system. An ICM exhibiting tightly packed cells or a TE with numerous cells forming a cohesive epithelium was assigned an A grade. An ICM with loosely grouped cells or a TE with fewer cells forming a loose epithelium received a B grade. An ICM or TE with very few cells was assigned a C grade. The hatching status of blastocysts was evaluated prior to zona pellucida removal. A blastocyst at expansion stage 4 (expanded blastocyst, zona pellucida intact) was classified as unhatched, while a blastocyst at stage 5 (hatching; TE beginning to herniate through the zona pellucida) or stage 6 (fully hatched; blastocyst completely free of the zona pellucida) was classified as hatched. Subsequently, acidic Tyrode's solution (T1788; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used to remove the zona pellucida from blastocysts that still possessed one (i.e., those at stages 4 and 5). Stage 6 blastocysts, which had already completely escaped the zona pellucida, did not require this treatment. Following this, the embryos were transferred to a drop of mIVC1 medium to neutralize the acidic solution. After being washed twice in drops of mIVC1 medium, the embryos were cultured using a 3D extended culture system based on the protocol established by Xiang et al. (2020) [15]. Briefly, blastocysts were placed in a low-attachment 96-well plate (3474; Corning, Corning, NY, USA) containing 160 μl of pre-equilibrated mIVC1 medium per well, maintained at 37°C under 21% O2 and 5% CO2. At 8 d.p.f., half of the mIVC1 medium was replaced with pre-equilibrated mIVC2 medium. At 9 d.p.f., the entire medium was replaced with mIVC2 supplemented with 10% Matrigel (354277; Corning). Subsequently, the medium was refreshed daily with mIVC2 containing 10% Matrigel (354277; Corning). The detailed compositions of the mIVC1 and mIVC2 media have been outlined in the 3D blastocyst culture system [15]: 20% fetal bovine serum (10100147C; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in mIVC1, 30% knockout serum replacement (10828028; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in mIVC2, 2 mM L-glutamine (25030081; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× ITS-X (51500056; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 8 nM β-oestradiol (E8875; Sigma-Aldrich), 200 ng/ml progesterone (P0130; Sigma-Aldrich), 25 μM N-acetyl-L-cysteine (A7250; Sigma-Aldrich), 0.22% (v/v) sodium lactate (L7900; Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (11360070; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 10 μM Y27632 (S1049; Selleck, Houston, TX, USA). Of note, the Matrigel product used in this study (catalog number 354277) differs from that used in the original protocol (354234; Xiang et al. (2020) [15]). Additionally, the sources of fetal bovine serum and sodium pyruvate were substituted with equivalent products from alternative suppliers, as detailed above.

Embryo frozen sectioning and immunofluorescence staining

Embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at room temperature, washed three times with 1% BSA (V900933; Sigma-Aldrich), and stained with eosin solution (C0109; Beyotime). The samples were then dehydrated with 15% sucrose (V900116; Sigma-Aldrich), embedded in O.C.T. compound (4583; Sakura, Tokyo, Japan), and sectioned using a frozen slicer (CM1950; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 (T8787; Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 30 min and blocked overnight at 4°C in a solution containing 5% donkey serum (017-000-121; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA), 2% BSA (V900933; Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1 M glycine (V900144; Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.01% Triton X-100 (T8787; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies, diluted in the blocking solution, at 4°C overnight. After washing three times with 0.05% Tween-20 (A600560; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) in PBS, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies, diluted in blocking solution, and DAPI (D3571; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. After three additional washes, the sections were imaged using a Celldiscoverer 7 fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The primary and secondary antibodies used were as follows: mouse monoclonal anti-OCT3/4 antibody (1:200 dilution, sc-5279; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), goat polyclonal anti-GATA6 antibody (1:100 dilution, AF1700; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), rabbit monoclonal anti-cytokeratin 7 (CK7) antibody (1:300 dilution, ab181598; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), rabbit polyclonal anti-SOX2 antibody (1:500 dilution, ab97959; Abcam), mouse monoclonal anti-PODXL antibody (1:400 dilution, MAB1658; R&D Systems), rabbit polyclonal anti-Laminin antibody (1:50 dilution, L9393; Sigma-Aldrich), mouse monoclonal anti-human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) antibody (1:100 dilution, ab9582; Abcam), phalloidin (F-actin) Alexa Fluor 647 (1:300 dilution, A22287; Invitrogen), donkey anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:500 dilution, A21202; Invitrogen), donkey anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:500 dilution, A21207; Invitrogen), donkey anti-goat IgG secondary antibody (1:500 dilution, A21447; Invitrogen). The amniotic cavity was identified by the presence of a central lumen surrounded by radially organized, OCT4-expressing EPI cells that exhibited apical polarization, as evidenced by the apical localization of PODXL. The yolk sac was identified as a second cavity, distinct from the amniotic cavity, that was surrounded by GATA6-expressing HYP cells and located adjacent to the EPI compartment. Bilaminar disc formation was defined by the presence of an OCT4+ EPI layer and a GATA6+ HYP layer, separated by a continuous laminin-positive basement membrane.

Statistical analysis

The diameters of 1PN and 2PN embryos from 6 to 12 d.p.f. were measured using ImageJ software. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and SPSS (version 27.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Qualitative data are presented in contingency tables and analyzed using the Fisher’s exact test. Quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of the data was analyzed using a D’Agostino-Pearson test, and Pearson correlation coefficient was computed. Differences between groups were evaluated using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparison test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Establishment of the delayed human blastocyst culture system

To establish a 3D delayed culture platform, we cultured 9 2PN blastocysts with good-quality (A/B-grade ICM and TE) at 6 d.p.f. and allowed them to develop for an additional six days. These embryos exhibited blastocoel expansion followed by collapse at 8 d.p.f., with subsequent gradual growth until 12 d.p.f. (Figs. 1A and B). At 12 d.p.f., the embryos were fixed for immunofluorescence analysis. Notably, 33.33% of the embryos developed OCT4 + EPI, GATA6 + HYP, and CK7 + TE, along with the formation of both the amniotic cavity and yolk sac, indicating bilaminar disc formation (Fig. 1C). Additionally, the expression of the anti-adhesion molecule PODXL in the apical domains facing the amniotic cavity suggested EPI polarization and lumen formation (Fig. 1D). A basement membrane, visualized through laminin staining, was observed between the EPI and HYP (Fig. 1E). To investigate TE differentiation, we used CK7 and hCG to distinguish CTB and STB lineages, respectively, and F-actin to delineate cell boundaries. Strong filamentous CK7 staining was predominantly detected in mononucleated cells, identifying them as CTB. In contrast, all multinucleated cells were positive for hCG, confirming their identity as STB (Fig. 1F). In summary, 33.33% of embryos exhibited all three lineages and a bilaminar disc structure, reflecting significant post-implantation developmental potential. Furthermore, the 2PN embryos successfully recapitulated key post-implantation structures, including the amniotic cavity, yolk sac, basement membrane, and TE differentiation by 12 d.p.f. Thus, we successfully established a delayed blastocyst culture system.

Impact of ICM and TE quality on early post-implantation embryonic development

Next, we investigated how ICM and TE quality influence early post-implantation embryonic development. For this analysis, we collected 20 1PN embryos with good-quality, 17 1PN embryos with poor ICM (C-grade ICM and A/B-grade TE) and 14 1PN embryos with poor TE (A/B-grade ICM and C-grade TE). No significant difference was observed in the blastocyst hatching rate among the groups (Supplementary Figs. 1A and B). At 12 d.p.f., we classified the embryos into two distinct morphological categories: “developed,” characterized by spherical morphology and tight cell-to-cell connectivity, and “arrested,” characterized by anomalous bulges and a loose, disorganized structure (Fig. 2A). The rate of developed embryos showed no significant difference among the good-quality, poor ICM, and poor TE embryos (Fig. 2B). We monitored the diameters of developed embryos in each group until 12 d.p.f. Poor ICM embryos exhibited a growth rate similar to that of good-quality 1PN blastocysts, whereas poor TE embryos displayed a significantly smaller diameter after 10 d.p.f. (Fig. 2C). Immunofluorescence staining of morphologically developed embryos at 12 d.p.f. revealed two distinct categories of lineage differentiation (Fig. 2D): “All lineages,” comprising embryos that developed OCT4 + EPI, GATA6 + HYP, and CK7 + TE, and “No EPI/HYP,” comprising embryos that lacked EPI or both EPI and HYP lineages. Among these developed embryos, 42.9% (3/7) of 2PN good-quality embryos exhibited all lineages, while 57.1% (4/7) were classified as “No EPI/HYP.” In the 1PN groups, 43.8% (7/16) of good-quality embryos displayed all lineages and 56.2% (9/16) were “No EPI/HYP.” In contrast, none of the poor ICM embryos established all lineages (0/8; Fig. 2E). Among poor TE embryos, 77.8% (7/9) exhibited all lineages. These findings indicate that TE quality predominantly affects embryonic growth, whereas poor ICM quality has a profound negative impact on post-implantation lineage differentiation.

Altered cell composition in poor TE embryos that retained all lineages

We further examined the cell composition of embryos that successfully established all three lineages, comparing 7 good-quality 1PN embryos and 8 poor TE embryos. Cell numbers of maximum cross section of each embryo were counted. Notably, poor TE embryos exhibited significantly higher numbers of EPI (92 ± 15.84) and HYP (32.25 ± 6.11) cells compared to good-quality embryos (EPI: 39.71 ± 15.05; HYP: 16.57 ± 4.79) (Figs. 3B and C). However, no significant differences were observed in total cell numbers (347 ± 70.95 vs. 344 ± 53.25) or TE cell numbers (222.75 ± 67.31 vs. 287.71 ± 62.35) between the two groups (Figs. 3A and D). Furthermore, a negative correlation was observed between the number of TE cells and the number of EPI or HYP cells (Supplementary Figs. 2A and B). These data reveal an inverse relationship between TE cell abundance and ICM-derived lineage cell numbers at this developmental stage.

Discussion

To evaluate the intrinsic developmental capacity of blastocysts with poor ICM or TE quality independent of maternal factors, we used an in vitro 3D extended culture system that excludes the complex influences of the endometrial environment. Our findings demonstrate that embryos with poor TE exhibited early post-implantation developmental potential similar to that of good-quality embryos. However, retrospective studies suggest that higher TE quality is associated with improved pregnancy and perinatal outcomes. Clinical pregnancy and live birth rates progressively decline as TE quality decreases from grade A to grade C, in both fresh and cryopreserved cycles [4, 20]. Kovacic et al. [21] reported significantly higher implantation and live birth rates for optimal blastocysts compared to those with poor TE quality. In clinical settings, the outcomes of transplanted embryos are uniquely influenced by the maternal uterine environment and the interaction between the embryo and the endometrium in vivo. For instance, blastocysts with higher TE quality reportedly secrete greater levels of serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) during early pregnancy [22], which promotes implantation by stimulating TE invasion and enhancing endometrial receptivity [23, 24]. The contrasting environmental conditions between in vitro and in vivo settings may impact embryonic development. In vitro culture systems provide embryos with nutrients, growth factors, and hormones such as β-estradiol and progesterone to support growth and energy metabolism, while minimizing external maternal influences. These systems rely on embryo-autonomous patterning signals [13, 14]. In vivo, endometrial cells modulate the growth of trophoblast cells, maintaining balance. In contrast, in vitro systems lack the regulatory influence of the endometrium, potentially allowing trophoblast cells in poor TE embryos to proliferate more rapidly, resulting in developmental rates comparable to those of high-quality embryos. Our study highlights the value of extended in vitro culture system in evaluating the clinical utility of embryos, devoid of maternal influences. We specifically applied this platform to investigate the developmental potential of blastocysts with poor ICM or TE quality. In the future, this extended culture system could evaluate the developmental potential of blastocysts with varying morphological grades by integrating time-lapse monitoring and artificial intelligence-based grading, such strategies could be employed to establish more reliable selection criteria for embryo transfer, ultimately supporting improved decision-making in clinical ART.

In the present study, we observed an inverse relationship between TE cell number and ICM-derived lineage cell numbers among embryos that successfully established all three lineages. Specifically, poor TE embryos contained significantly more EPI and HYP cells than good-quality embryos, and a negative correlation was noted between TE cell abundance and EPI/HYP cell counts. The mutual interactions between embryonic and extra-embryonic tissues during peri-implantation remain largely unexplored. Molè et al. [25] reported that FGF2 and FGF4, secreted by the EPI, are essential for the proliferation of EPI, HYP, and TE lineages during human peri-implantation development. Furthermore, a subset of HYP cells functions as an anterior signaling center, mediating EPI patterning through the secretion of inhibitors of BMP, NODAL, and WNT signaling pathways. The polar TE plays a critical role in regulating EPI morphology during embryo implantation, exerting mechanical stretching forces that facilitate the transition of the EPI into a disc-like structure [26]. The composition and concentration of the extracellular matrix significantly influence the coordinated post-implantation development of embryonic and extra-embryonic cells in vitro. In extended in vitro cultures, a high concentration of Matrigel has been shown to promote TE survival and differentiation, but it inhibits EPI development, frequently leading to EPI loss in most embryos [15]. Similarly, Zhai et al. [27] suggested that excessive growth of extra-embryonic tissue may impede the development of the inner embryo by depleting nutrients from the culture medium. It is also possible that the expansion of the TE compartment influences the growth of ICM-derived lineages through physical or metabolic constraints, as previously suggested [14]. These observations may partially account for the inverse correlation observed in our study; however, whether a higher EPI/HYP cell number reflects enhanced lineage functionality remains to be determined, and further studies are required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

One significant limitation of our study is the indiscriminate use of 1PN embryos without conducting ploidy analysis. The occurrence of 1PN zygotes is likely due to factors such as parthenogenetic activation, failure in male or female pronucleus formation, asynchronous development, or abnormal pronuclear fusion [28]. Studies have shown that 36.0% of 1PN-derived blastocysts was biparental diploid, 13.4% lower than that in 2PN blastocysts [29, 30]. However, haploid and uniparental diploid chromosomal constitutions have also been observed in 27.2% and 26.7% of 1PN-derived blastocysts, respectively. The parental genomes play complementary and distinct roles during mammalian development. The paternal genome exhibits a bias for the trophoblast lineage, and the maternal genome for embryonic tissues [31]. Consequently, uniparental embryos compromise the development of the ICM and TE. In this study, embryos categorized into three groups (high-quality, poor ICM, poor TE) by morphology showed a skewed chromosomal constitution, potentially affecting lineage differentiation in poor ICM embryos and poor TE embryos.

In summary, this study highlights the impact of poor ICM or TE quality on post-implantation morphological characteristics and lineage differentiation. Embryos with poor TE demonstrated significant developmental potential, underscoring their clinical importance. In contrast, embryos with poor ICM were compromised during implantation, emphasizing the need to inform patients about the increased risk of pregnancy failure associated with transferring poor ICM embryos. Importantly, our findings provide valuable insights for ART and the clinical management of low-quality blastocysts.
