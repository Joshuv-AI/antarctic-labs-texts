Prevalence, associated factors and treatment prescriptions for community-acquired pneumonia: a cross-sectional study at the largest children’s hospital in the Ashanti Region, Ghana
Authors: Manuella Hayford, Henry Kyeramateng Acheampong, Richmond Gorman, Annie Animwaa Sarpong, Abigail Akoto, Constance Adu-Gyamfi, Jesse Addo Asamoah, Sherihane Naa Ayeley Aryeetey, Emmanuella Nyarko-Afriyie, Henrietta Dede Tetteh, Portia Boakye Okyere, Veronica Barnor, Richmond Yeboah, Michael Owusu, Richard Odame Phillips, Augustina Angelina Sylverken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViral respiratory agents have increasingly become a major cause of paediatric community-acquired pneumonia (vCAP) globally, with a reported prevalence of 25.7% in Ghana. Despite improved access to molecular diagnostics in Ghana, its limited use has led to underdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment prescriptions of viral respiratory agents in paediatric vCAP. We determined the prevalence of viral respiratory agents, their association with associated factors and vCAP, and assessed the treatment prescriptions for the management of vCAP at Asokwa Children’s Hospital, the largest children’s hospital in the Ashanti Region of Ghana.MethodsThe study was conducted from July to August 2023 using a cross-sectional design and convenience sampling approach among children under 18 years who met WHO Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) criteria for pneumonia such as cough with phlegm, difficulty breathing, and fever. Socio-demographic and clinical information was collected using case report forms. Oropharyngeal swabs from the participants were screened for common respiratory viruses, including influenza A and B, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), SARS-CoV-2, human rhinovirus (HRV), and human metapneumovirus (HMPV), using real-time polymerase chain reaction. Viral CAP (vCAP) was defined as RT-PCR-confirmed detection of at least one respiratory virus. Logistic regression analysis compared the association between associated factors and vCAP cases using STATA SE version 14.ResultsAmong 305 participants, viral respiratory agents were detected in 31 cases, representing a prevalence of 10.2%, with 28 (9.2%) single viral detections and 3 (1.0%) double viral detections. Influenza A was the most frequently detected virus, in 17 (5.57%) participants, with 6 (1.97%) testing positive for RSV, 2 (0.7%) for Influenza B and 3 (1.0%) for SARS-CoV-2. Being a female (AOR: 2.6, 95% CI:1.2–5.7) and having fever (AOR: 2.8, 95% CI:1.3–6.2) were significantly associated with vCAP. Furthermore, antibiotics were administered to 58.1% of viral positive detections, with penicillin being the most prescribed (35.3%).ConclusionInfluenza A was the most frequently detected virus among children with vCAP in this cohort. Fever (≥ 38 °C) and female sex were identified as associated factors. Although antibiotics were prescribed in 58% of viral-positive cases, this likely shows empiric management in a setting where bacterial co-infection cannot be reliably excluded.

Introduction

Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP), caused primarily by bacteria and viruses, is a leading cause of illness and death worldwide, especially among children under five years of age [1, 2]. In 2019, pneumonia accounted for nearly 15% of all deaths in this age group globally, with viral CAP (vCAP) representing a significant proportion of cases [3].

Following the COVID-19 pandemic, viral respiratory infections have gained increased attention as major contributors to pneumonia cases, likely due to heightened surveillance and their more pronounced impact on vulnerable populations [4]. This rise in CAP due to viral agents is particularly concerning, as it often leads to acute inflammation of the pulmonary parenchyma [5].

Children are often among the most affected groups due to their immature immune systems, making them vulnerable to this infection [6]. Globally, the prevalence of vCAP is high in children, ranging from 8.6% to 56.2%, with 21% to 28% of hospitalised children requiring admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) [7]. Children account for 27.8% of recorded deaths in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) as a result of vCAP [8]. In Ghana, vCAP prevalence has been reported to be 25.7% among children under five years of age [9].

Although molecular diagnostics such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) were previously limited in SSA, including Ghana, the COVID-19 pandemic has significantly improved access to these platforms. However, their use for detecting viral respiratory agents in vCAP cases remains constrained due to challenges such as cost, infrastructure, and trained personnel [10, 11]. In the absence of rapid viral diagnostics, antibiotics are frequently prescribed as first-line treatment due to diagnostic uncertainty and high suspicion of bacterial infection [12, 13]. In Ghana, the Standard Treatment Guidelines recommend empiric antibiotic therapy [14] for suspected pneumonia, largely because rapid viral diagnostics are not routinely available. Although antibiotic stewardship initiatives exist, they remain limited at the primary-care level, resulting in continued reliance on empirical antibiotic use in paediatric vCAP cases.

This reliance on empirical treatment contributes significantly to the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), as the widespread use of antibiotics in cases of viral pneumonia, where they offer no therapeutic benefit, drives the selection of resistant bacterial strains. This practice is particularly concerning, as specific treatments for respiratory viruses that cause pneumonia are not included in the Ghana Standard Treatment Guidelines [14]. Consequently, the limited data on vCAP among Ghanaian children has likely led to an underestimation of their contribution to childhood morbidity and mortality.

This underestimation has contributed to the low prioritisation of viral respiratory agents among researchers, funders, and policymakers, resulting in limited data on their prevalence in Ghana [15]. Hypothetically, viral respiratory agents play a role in community-acquired pneumonia among children in the Ashanti Region of Ghana. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated factors of viral community-acquired pneumonia and to evaluate treatment prescription patterns among paediatric patients in the Ashanti Region of Ghana.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study from the beginning of July 2023 to the end of August 2023. The study employed a convenience sampling method, selecting participants based on their availability and willingness at the time of presentation. Although the sampling period was limited to two months and may not fully capture seasonal variation in viral circulation, paediatric vCAP cases were consistently reported at the facility. These cases occurred during one of the peak clinical windows for paediatric pneumonia.

Study area

The study was carried out at the Asokwa Children’s hospital in the Ashanti region of Ghana as shown in Fig. 1. It is the largest and sole facility in the Ashanti Region specialising exclusively in paediatric health. It is located in the central part of the Ashanti Region, covering about 25.31 square kilometres [16]. It forms part of the Asokwa Municipal District, serving 18 towns representing the Asokwa, Ahinsan, and Atonsu zonal councils. While major public hospitals handle tertiary cases, Asokwa Children’s Hospital supports primary paediatric care from Kumasi and referrals from outlying zones during seasonal disease outbreaks in children.

Inclusion criteria

Study participants under 18 years old who presented to the Outpatient Department of Asokwa Children’s Hospital was the first inclusion criteria. In accordance with WHO classification of pneumonia [18], which categorises disease severity based on clinical signs, this study investigated outpatients who showed signs and symptoms suggestive of viral CAP such as cough with phlegm, fever, pleuritic chest pain, runny nose, sore throat and sputum colour change as determined by an attending physician. All participants included in the study met WHO IMCI clinical criteria for pneumonia, defined as the presence of cough or difficulty breathing accompanied by age-specific fast breathing, with symptom onset occurring prior to hospital presentation [19].

Exclusion criteria

Children presenting with isolated cough, coryza, or upper respiratory symptoms without WHO-defined pneumonia were not included in the study. Unconscious children and those undergoing antiviral or antibiotic treatment, were excluded from the study. Also, participants were withdrawn from the study when consent was not provided by parents or guardians, samples were inadequate for analysis, and clinical data were incomplete.

Sampling method

Children were first evaluated at the Outpatient Department (OPD) unit where vital information such as temperature, and respiratory rate were obtained by the attending nurse. Each child was then taken to the consulting rooms where children presenting with symptoms and signs of pneumonia in line with the WHO IMCI criteria as judged by the attending physician were enrolled in the study. Before enrolment, the aim, benefits and possible risks of this study were explained to the child’s parent or guardian in the presence of the attending physician as a witness. Afterwards, the parent/guardian was required to give a written informed consent by either signature or thumbprint before their ward(s) was recruited for the study. Eligible participants were consecutively enrolled until the required sample size was reached.

Data and sample collection

A standardized case report form (see Supplementary File) was administered to study participants to obtain socio-demographic information and to capture information on probable risk factors associated with vCAP. This was done by trained research scientists who had received prior instruction on the study protocol. The case report form was written in clear and basic English language. For parents who could not read, the attending physician interpreted information on the case report form in their local dialects. The demographic data collected included the age, gender of the child as well as the educational level of care givers among other variables.

Oropharyngeal swabs were taken from each participant by a trained laboratory worker using sterile swabs. Each child was asked to tilt his or her head slightly backward, and the tip of the swab stick, was gently inserted into the posterior pharynx and tonsillar areas about 1/2 to 3/4 of an inch (1–1.5 cm) into the oropharyngeal area, avoiding contact with the tongue and cheeks. The swab was rubbed there for a minimum of four circles or at least 15 s and inserted into the filled extraction tube and swirled approximately five times, lasting at least 30 s. Samples were immediately placed into viral transport medium, RNAlater (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Each sample was labelled with a unique participant identification (ID) and maintained at approximately 4 °C during transport to ensure cold chain integrity and standard infection prevention. Samples were promptly transported to the Kumasi Centre for Collaborative Research in Tropical Medicine’s virology laboratory, Ghana for analysis.

Laboratory analysis

Samples were screened in the laboratory for common respiratory viruses including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2), Influenza A&B, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human metapneumovirus (HMPV) and human rhinovirus (HRV).

Initial inactivation and lysis of viral particles were conducted in a biosafety level III laboratory to release the viral RNA. Viral RNA was then extracted using the RADI PREP Swab and Stool DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (KH Medical Co., Ltd, Republic of Korea) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were eluted in a 70 µl volume.

Viral nucleic acid detection was done by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using the Invitrogen Superscript III One Step RT-PCR system (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Amplification and analysis were carried out using the Bio-Rad CFX96 C1000 thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., USA). Cycling conditions for each virus consisted of an initial reverse transcription, and polymerase activation. This was followed by repeated cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension, with fluorescence measured during the annealing step. Viral CAP (vCAP) was defined as the detection of at least one respiratory virus by real-time PCR among children meeting the WHO IMCI criteria for pneumonia. This definition reflects viral detection but does not exclude the possibility of concurrent bacterial infection, as bacterial testing was not performed. All samples with a threshold cycle (Ct) above 40 were considered negative. All samples with a threshold cycle (Ct) less than 40 were considered positive based on manufacturer recommendations and standard laboratory practice for respiratory virus detection.

Co-detections (i.e., detection of more than one virus in a sample) were classified as viral-positive cases. Cases without viral detection were classified as viral-negative CAP; however, these were not assumed to be bacterial, as no bacterial cultures or testing for other pathogens were conducted in this study. Given that previous Ghanaian studies have predominantly focused on bacterial aetiology [2, 20–22] of community-acquired pneumonia, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, our study sought to address the relatively under-researched gap in data on the role of viral respiratory agents contributing to community-acquired pneumonia in this population.

Antibiotic prescriptions in this study were administered based on the attending physicians’ clinical judgment, in accordance with Ghana Health Service guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Data collected were manually double entered into a Microsoft® Excel 2019 and analysed using STATA SE (version 14.0; StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the distribution of sociodemographic information, such as age and sex, as well as respiratory virus detection in tables and treatment prescriptions in figures.

Associations between associated factors and vCAP were evaluated using both univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. Associated factors, expressed as ratios with corresponding 95% CIs, were used to examine the association between age, temperature, clinical symptoms and medical conditions. A p-value of less than or equal to 0.05 (p≤0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of three hundred and five (305) participants were enrolled in the study, ranging from below 1 year to 15 years, with a mean age of 7 years. Children aged 1 to 4 years constituted the largest age group, accounting for 190 participants (62.3%). Of the total participants, 173 (56.7%) were males. The majority, 290 participants (95.1%), were managed as outpatients by the attending physician (Table 1).

Prevalence of viral agents in CAP

Out of the 305 samples tested, viral respiratory agents were detected in 31 participants, representing a 10.2% prevalence of vCAP. Among the positive detections, 28 (9.2%) were single virus detections, while 3 (1.0%) were co-detections (Table 2). The most frequently identified virus was Influenza A, detected in 17 (5.6%) participants. This was followed by RSV in 6 (2.0%) participants. Influenza B and SARS-CoV-2 were detected in 2 (0.7%) and 3 (1.0%) participants, respectively. HMPV and HRV were not detected in any of the samples screened.

Associated factors associated with vCAP

Children aged 1 year and below had reduced odds of vCAP (OR = 0.5, CI: 0.2, 1.9) compared to children aged 1–4 years (OR = 0.8, CI: 0.3, 2.1). However, these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.332 for children ≤ 1 year; p = 0.710 for children aged 1–4 years). In both univariate and multivariate analyses, female sex was significantly associated with an increased risk of vCAP. The odds ratio (OR) for females was 2.6, CI:1.2, 5.7, (p = 0.014) in univariate analysis and 2.9, CI:1.2, 7.0, (p = 0.017) in multivariate analysis. Fever, defined as a body temperature of ≥ 38 °C, was also found to be a significant risk factor. The odds of vCAP among children with fever were 2.8, CI: 1.3, 6.2 (p = 0.010) in univariate analysis and 3.1, CI: 1.2, 7.9 (p = 0.016) in multivariate analysis (Table 3).

Treatment prescriptions for the management of vCAP

Viral respiratory agents were detected in 31 of 305 participants, representing 10.2% of the total study population. Among these viral-positive cases, 18 (58.1%) participants were administered antibiotics for the management of viral community-acquired pneumonia (vCAP), while 13 (41.9%) did not receive antibiotics as shown in Fig. 2.

In the viral-positive cases, penicillin was the most frequently prescribed antibiotic class, administered to 11 participants (35.3%), followed by macrolides in 4 (12.9%) and cephalosporins in 3 (9.7%) of cases. In single viral infections, Influenza A cases accounted for the highest proportion of penicillin prescriptions (6/17, 35.3%). In co-infected cases, penicillin and macrolides were each prescribed in 1 (100.0%) of the cases as shown in Fig. 3.

Discussion

This study is one of the few in Ghana to investigate the viral aetiology and treatment prescriptions for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) at the largest paediatric hospital in the Ashanti Region.

A prevalence of 10.2% for viral respiratory agents was detected among children presenting with CAP symptoms. This estimate should be interpreted as a short-term, facility-based estimate, given the limited two-month sampling period. The prevalence is lower than previously reported (25.7% in hospitalized children under five [9] and 50.4% in similar settings [23]. Differences may reflect variations in patient populations, disease severity, sampling duration, and the panel of viruses tested. Lower detection in the current study may be attributed to health-seeking behaviours commonly associated with viral community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), whereby caregivers are more likely to seek hospital care only when symptoms become severe. Moreover, vCAP often presents with milder symptoms compared to hospital-acquired pneumonia resulting in fewer hospital visits or delayed testing, and limiting virus detection in this study. Extended surveillance and testing a broader viral panel could improve understanding of seasonal and regional vCAP patterns Ghanaian children.

Influenza A&B viruses were the most frequently detected respiratory agents in this cohort, with a combined prevalence of 6.3% among children with vCAP. This finding aligns with a 6% prevalence reported in Cambodia [24] but lower than reports from Ghana (10%) [23] and South Africa (11%) [25]. Higher prevalence in these studies may reflect inclusion of hospitalized or immunocompromised children. Although the World Health Organisation reported a 30% influenza detection rate in Ghana in 2023, this figure included all age groups [26]. Although Influenza A (5.6%) was the most frequently detected viruses in this study, their prevalence was relatively low compared to other studies. This may reflect sampling during a peak influenza period, and as such, should not be overgeneralized beyond the study timeframe.

RSV was the second most commonly detected respiratory agent, identified in 6 (2.0%) participants. Although RSV is widely reported as a leading cause of CAP in children, this prevalence is lower than the 12.9% reported by [2] in Ghana, and 22.0% rate reported in Mali [27]. This higher prevalence in these studies could be likely due to the inclusion of hospitalised patients, use of multiple sample types, and radiographic imaging for diagnosis. The lower prevalence in this study could be due to the use of only oropharyngeal swabs and sampling outside the peak season of RSV in Ghana, which typically occurs between September and October [28]. Also, RSV is most prevalent in children aged 2–6 months, where the relatively older age distribution of our study participants may explain the low detection rate.

SARS-CoV-2 was detected in only 0.9% of participants, consistent with low paediatric prevalence in Italy [29], and France [30], where no SARS-CoV-2 cases were detected among paediatric CAP patients. While severe COVID-19 cases in children are rare, they tend to occur in those with underlying health conditions, and overall disease severity remains lower in children than in adults [31]. A higher prevalence of 4.4% was observed in a Russian study, primarily among adolescents aged 16 years and above, suggesting a possible age-related increase in susceptibility [32]. SARS-CoV-2 transmission rates were generally low [33] and the widespread vaccination against SARS-CoV-2 post the COVID-19 pandemic could have accounted for this low prevalence.

This study also explored various demographic and clinical factors in relation to vCAP. Of the variables assessed, female sex (p = 0.017) and fever (p = 0.016) were significantly associated with vCAP. Females were found to have a higher risk of infection (OR = 2.6, CI:1.2, 5.7) compared to males which contrasts with findings from [34,35] and [36], who reported a higher risk in males. Given the relatively small number of viral-positive cases (n = 31), these findings should be considered exploratory, as the estimates may be subject to overfitting and as such, should be interpreted with caution. On the other hand, Temperature rise (≥ 38 °C) served as a marker for fever and was a key clinical indicator of vCAP, likely reflecting the host’s immune response to respiratory infections as it has been previously associated with vCAP in children [23]. These associations, though preliminary, may inform clinical decision-making and triage strategies in paediatric outpatient settings, especially where viral diagnostics are limited. Future studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate these findings and clarify potential sex- and fever-related factors in viral CAP.

Despite the detection of respiratory viruses in 31 (10.2%) participants, 18 (58.1%) were prescribed antibiotics. While this may suggest overuse in cases likely caused by viral agents, bacterial co-infection was not assessed, and clinical severity indicators were not fully captured. Empiric antibiotic use may be clinically justified, particularly in resource-limited settings where bacterial infections cannot be reliably excluded [9]. Penicillin was the most commonly prescribed antibiotic, accounting for 35.5% (11/31) of cases. This may reflect its broad-spectrum activity and its historical use as a first-line treatment, particularly in settings where bacterial co-detections cannot be ruled out [12, 22, 37]. Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid was also frequently administered, reflecting its effectiveness against common respiratory agents and widespread availability. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such antibiotics are inexpensive [38] and has been linked to rising levels of antimicrobial resistance [39]. These findings highlight the need for improved viral diagnostics to guide rational antibiotic use and strengthen stewardship without compromising patient care [39].

The lack of effective antivirals in many LMICs limits targeted treatment options for vCAP [40]. In line with WHO guidelines, empiric antibiotic therapy is recommended due to challenges in distinguishing viral from bacterial infections [18]. Specific antiviral treatments are rarely used, except in cases of confirmed influenza, where oseltamivir may be indicated. As a result, supportive care, including oxygen therapy, fever management, adequate hydration, and nutritional support, remains the mainstay of treatment for vCAP [18]. WHO further emphasises preventive strategies such as vaccination, exclusive breastfeeding, hand hygiene, and reduction of indoor air pollution as critical measures to reduce the burden of viral pneumonia, particularly in resource-limited settings [41]. Moreover, existing age restrictions on certain vaccines leave younger children especially vulnerable to respiratory viral infections and subsequently at higher risk of receiving inappropriate antibiotic therapy [42]. Addressing these issues by investing in the development and distribution of affordable antivirals could provide a more targeted and effective approach to managing vCAP, especially in LMIC settings [43, 44].

While a combination of clinical signs (e.g., cough, difficulty breathing, chest indrawing), radiological imaging (such as chest X-rays), and laboratory tests (including blood cultures and inflammatory markers) are commonly used to confirm pneumonia, this study focused primarily on molecular detection of respiratory viruses, highlighting gaps in diagnostic capacity. Pneumonia diagnosis was based on clinical assessment following WHO guidelines [18], which strengthens the reliability of the case classification in our study. The results provide evidence to inform empirical treatment protocols and support national policy reforms that prioritize diagnostic-guided, rational management strategies for paediatric CAP in Ghana and similar settings.

Our study had some limitations that should be acknowledged. The short two-month sampling period may not capture seasonal variations in viral circulation, which is particularly relevant for viruses such as RSV and influenza that exhibit seasonal patterns. The use of convenience sampling could introduce selection bias, potentially affecting the representativeness of the detected viral infections relative to the broader population. We acknowledge that relying solely on oropharyngeal swabs may have underestimated the prevalence of some viral agents, particularly those better detected using nasopharyngeal specimens such as RSV, HRV, and HMPV. This may partly explain the absence of HRV and HMPV detection in this study. Consequently, the reported prevalence of 10.2% should be interpreted with caution, as it may represent an underestimate due to the sampling methodology. This limitation may also affect the generalizability of the findings beyond the study setting. Oropharyngeal swabs were selected as they are better tolerated in young children and help reduce distress and discomfort during sample collection. Also, the relatively small sample size limits the statistical power for subgroup analyses, including the assessment of double virus detections, as reflected in wide confidence intervals. Additionally, the multivariate model included several predictors relative to only 31 viral-positive events, which may increase the risk of overfitting and unstable estimates; variables were selected based on clinical relevance and prior evidence, and larger studies with more positive cases are needed. Importantly, the definition of viral CAP (vCAP) in this study was based solely on PCR detection of respiratory viruses and did not account for possible viral–bacterial co-infections. Evidence [45–48] suggests that a substantial proportion of viral pneumonia cases ranging from 10% to 70% may involve concurrent bacterial infection. This limitation may affect the interpretation of prevalence estimates and associated risk factors, which should therefore be interpreted as relating to virus-detected pneumonia rather than exclusively viral aetiology.

Despite its limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the aetiology of viral community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) among children in Ghana, a context where such evidence is scarce. The use of molecular diagnostics (PCR) allowed for accurate identification of viral pathogens, and linking these findings to antibiotic prescribing patterns offers important information on routine clinical decision-making. Conducted in a major regional paediatric hospital, the study reflects real-world clinical practice, enhancing the relevance of its findings. With full ethical approval and adherence to standard research procedures, the assessment of multiple respiratory viruses offers a comprehensive view of viral CAP and shows the importance of expanding molecular diagnostics to support rational management of viral respiratory infections.

Conclusion

This study highlights the role of viral respiratory agents, in paediatric community-acquired pneumonia in Ghana.

Influenza A was the most frequently detected virus in this cohort of children with vCAP. Fever and female sex emerged as significant risk indicators for vCAP. Although antibiotics were prescribed in 58% of viral-positive cases, this likely reflects appropriate empiric management in a setting where bacterial co-infection cannot be reliably excluded, while highlighting the need for improved diagnostics to support more targeted use.

Integrating molecular diagnostics and antigen-based rapid diagnostic tests into routine care will enhance case management and reduce inappropriate antibiotic use. Future studies should aim for broader surveillance across seasons to strengthen the evidence base for public health interventions.
