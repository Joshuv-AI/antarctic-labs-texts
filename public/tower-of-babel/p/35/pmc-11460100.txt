Ectatic vertebral artery associated medullary compression treated with microvascular decompression: a case report and review of literature
Authors: Jorrdan N Bissell, Brandon Edelbach, Vadim Gospodarev, Kenneth M De Los Reyes, Promod Pillai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVertebral artery compression syndrome is a rare condition defined as the clinical presentation of dizziness, vertigo, ataxia, dysarthria, dysphagia, progressive or acute paralysis, hemisensory loss, and cervical myelopathy secondary to compression of the medulla and upper spinal cord by the vertebral artery.Case descriptionHere we describe a 57-year-old Mexican–American male with bulbar symptoms, positional vertigo, and diplopia. The patient’s symptoms were managed through microsurgical decompression of the medulla utilizing a far lateral transcondylar approach. The patient reported clinical improvement on follow-up.ConclusionThis case underscores the clinical complexity and heterogeneity of this rare neurovascular pathology and the importance of elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the symptomatic presentation of vertebral artery compression syndrome.Keywords: Vertebral artery compression syndrome, Far lateral partial transcondylar approach, Microsurgical decompression, Ectatic Vertebral Artery

Introduction

Compression of the medulla can result from various factors or conditions, leading to a spectrum of clinical manifestations. Common causes of vertebral artery compression syndrome include tumors, aneurysms, inflammatory conditions, congenital malformations, and syringobulbia [1–4]. Clinical symptoms typically include difficulty swallowing, speaking, walking, and coordination issues, as well as cardiac and respiratory manifestations [1–5]. While these pathologies are often the primary culprits of medullary compression, the anatomical relationship between the medulla and surrounding structures can also contribute to clinical presentations.

Case report

Clinical description

A 57-year-old Mexican–American veteran male presented with bulbar symptoms of dysphasia and dysarthria, diplopia, and headaches. The patient had a history of right sided Bell’s palsy, hypertension, major depressive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and substance abuse. Subsequently, the patient had a computed tomography (CT) angiogram, which demonstrated bilateral vertebral artery compression of the medulla; although, the right vertebral artery had more significant involvement than the left as seen in Figs. 1 and 2.

Surgical approach

Microsurgical decompression of the medulla was planned via a far lateral partial transcondylar approach to optimize the view of the ventral aspect of the brainstem. The patient was brought to the operating room (OR), prepped, and placed in the standard lateral oblique position with the head laterally flexed and rotated. An incision was made exposing the suboccipital region. Dissection was then carried down to expose the lamina of C2 and the ipsilateral portion of the posterior arch of C1. To obtain a corridor into the vertebral canal, the posterior arch of C1 was followed laterally to the sulcus arteriosus. Whereupon the adjacent transverse process and vertebral artery were localized. Upon localization, the posterior arch of C1 was removed from the posterior tubercle to the sulcus arteriosus, thereby exposing the lip of the foramen magnum. Then, the craniotomy was performed and extended medially and laterally in which a portion of the occipital condyle was removed.

An incision was made into the dura in a curvilinear fashion exposing the lower cranial nerves to C2. From this stage the arachnoid layer was then opened accessing the spinal canal and permitting visualization of the vertebral arteries. The vertebral artery was found to be bulbous, tortuous, stiff, and atheromatous causing severe compression of the adjacent medulla. The vertebral artery was then separated from the medulla and Teflon felts were placed in between the medulla and vertebral arteries, confirming adequate decompression.

Follow up

Postoperatively, the patient had good functional mobility and reported a significant improvement in diplopia. Imaging revealed a reduction in mass effect of right vertebral artery on the medulla as illustrated on Fig. 3. The patient’s postoperative course, however, was complicated by the development of pseudomeningocele and hydrocephalus, which was treated via a right ventriculoperitoneal shunt. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient reported significant improvement in his balance, swallowing in addition to improvements in vision.

Discussion

Vertebral artery ectasia is an uncommon neurovascular pathology causing compression of the medulla oblongata. Typically, it manifests on imaging as unilateral dominance of the intracranial vertebral arteries, often accompanied by contralateral hypoplasia or reduction in arterial lumen caliber. The ectatic vertebral artery typically exerts pulsation and mechanical pressure on the lateral surface and pyramid of the medulla oblongata, aligning with its native anatomical position.

This phenomenon prefaces a wide spectrum of nonspecific neurological sequelae. Lateral medullary syndrome (LMS) results from a disruption in the laminar flow of the vertebral artery or posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) and is one of the most common strokes affecting the posterior aspect of the intracerebral circulation [6]. Manifestations of LMS are closely linked to the neuroanatomy of the lateral medulla. For instance, vertigo and Horner syndrome are associated with sympathetic fibers, while dysphagia and dysphonia likely stem from disturbances to cranial nerves IX and X [7]. Additionally, impaired facial sensation may result from involvement of fibers from the descending spinal trigeminal complex [7]. Impaired pain and temperature sensation in the limbs are attributed to spinothalamic tract involvement [5].

Medial medullary syndrome (MMS) occurs due to disruption at the distal portion of the vertebral artery or associated subsidiary arteries. Impaired perfusion to the pyramidal decussation leads to characteristic clinical manifestations, including contralateral arm and leg paralysis. Nystagmus and ipsilateral tongue paralysis are also associated with MMS, reflecting the anatomical relevance of cranial nerves XII and the longitudinal fasciculus [8].

Hemi medullary syndrome (HMS) and bilateral medial medullary syndrome (BMMS) are rare diseases resulting from various combinations of LMS and MMS. HMS is characterized by contralateral hemiparesis, hemisensory loss, ipsilateral ataxia, and Horner’s syndrome, along with nonspecific manifestations [9]. BMMS is characterized by face-sparing quadriplegia, hypoglossal nerve palsy, and respiratory failure [10].

An additional pathology involving the interaction between the vertebral artery and the medulla is the ectatic vertebral artery. This rare condition, recently termed “vertebral artery compression syndrome” (VACS) by Li et al., has limited description in medical literature [11]. VACS describes a constellation of symptoms associated with vertebral artery ectasia. VACS is anatomically defined as imaging suggestive of compression of the medulla and upper spinal cord by the vertebral artery, accompanied by corresponding clinical presentation suggesting medullary compression [11]. Symptoms of VACS often include dizziness, vertigo, ataxia, dysarthria, dysphagia, progressive or acute paralysis, hemisensory loss, and cervical myelopathy [11].

The first reported case of suggestive of VACS in the literature appeared in 1985 when Kim et al. described a case of hemiparesis secondary to vertebral artery compression of the medulla oblongata, which was surgically treated in the same manner as this case report [12]. According to Kim et al. the left vertebral artery was described as thick and medially displaced causing displacement and disfiguration of the medulla. Savitz et al. reported a case series of nine patients with symptoms secondary to vertebral artery compression of the anterolateral surface of the medulla. These symptoms included both ipsilateral and contralateral motor weakness, vertigo, ataxia, dysphagia, dysarthria, abnormal palate elevation, tinnitus, and headaches, which bear significant overlap with the symptoms ascribed to VACS [13]. A case report by Younus et al. described a patient with a dilated vertebral artery presenting with bulbar symptoms as well as cerebral ischemia secondary to the vascular pathology [14]. Lastly, Sadashiva et al. reported a case series of three patients with VACS who had significant pyramidal involvement [15]. While some variations exist between individual cases, the symptoms described in this case report bear significant resemblance to these cases. A summary of these cases is illustrated in Table 1.

Medical treatment of VACS has been reported to successfully treat several (n = 8) patients. Medical intervention either consists of anticoagulation (aspirin, warfarin, dipyridamole) or acetazolamide [13, 16]. Medical management was often employed when the patient either had contraindications to surgery, refused surgery, or had a mild symptomatic presentation.

The majority of authors have described treating VACS via microvascular decompression [12, 15, 17, 18], which was generally associated with a reduction in symptoms. A recent literature review describing treatment of VACS indicated that guidelines for effective treatment were still necessary and furthermore, long term complications related to the procedure were not well described [19]. Of interest, Takahashi et al. described a case of cervical myelopathy secondary to bilateral vertebral artery compression of the upper cervical spinal cord. Microsurgical decompression was completed and then both vertebral arteries were anchored to the dura. A similar strategy of dural anchoring was also described by Tomasello et al. [20] Since then, several authors have noted postoperative improvement of patients symptoms and proposed that the size of the vertebral arteries necessitated not only decompression but also further stabilization [20–23].

It is important to note that not all patients with imaging demonstrating vertebral artery compression of the medulla oblongata will be symptomatic. Cierpiol et al. demonstrated that up to 25% of patients may have asymptomatic brain stem compression, suggesting that this may represent an anatomical variation present in a significant proportion of the population [24]. However, a significant number of patients with VACS presented with hypertension and it has been proposed that a symptomatic presentation arises in the context of a pulsatile environment secondary to hypertension [12]. In our case, the vertebral artery was associated with significant rigidity secondary to atheromatous plaques which could further contribute to the symptomatic presentation and suggests that symptomatic VACS may present secondary to long standing hypertensive conditions.

Conclusion

This report contributes to the expanding body of knowledge on vertebral artery compression syndrome, emphasizing the significance of recognizing its diverse clinical presentations and interactions with comorbidities. The case underscores the importance for ongoing research to further elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms and optimal therapeutic approaches for this intriguing neurovascular condition.
