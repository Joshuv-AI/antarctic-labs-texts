Effects of Various Anti-Diabetic Drugs on the Risk of Fractures in Older Women with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Seong Hee Ahn, Kyoung Jin Kim, So Young Park, Su Jin Kwon, Ha Young Kim, Kyoung Min Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the fracture risks associated with anti-diabetic drugs in older women with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), who are particularly susceptible to skeletal fragility.MethodsUsing data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service, this nested case-control study included 10,104 older women with T2DM and osteoporotic fractures (aged 66.5±3.4 years) matched in a 1:3 ratio with controls by birthdate, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and cohort entry date. We analyzed the odds of major osteoporotic fracture (MOF), vertebral fracture (VF), and non-VF (NVF) in users of sulfonylurea, thiazolidinedione (TZD), dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor, and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i), compared to metformin (Met)-only users using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsDuring a follow-up period of 3.8±2.8 years, TZD users had a higher risk of MOF than Met-only users (odds ratio [OR], 1.35; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.19–1.53; P<0.001). Risks of VF and NVF were also increased in the TZD group (OR, 1.21; 95% CI 1.03–1.42; P=0.022 and OR, 1.32; 95% CI 1.14–1.52; P<0.001, respectively). No significant differences were observed in other drug groups. The increased risk of VF and NVF in the TZD group were particularly pronounced in patients with normal or osteopenic bone mineral density (BMD) and in those with normal body mass index (BMI), respectively.ConclusionsIn older women with T2DM, TZD use was associated with increased VF and NVF risks, particularly among those with normal or osteopenic BMD and normal BMI. SGLT2i showed no increased risk, but further large-scale studies are needed to confirm its skeletal safety.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

The global incidence and prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are increasing,[ 1,2] leading to increased scrutiny of its well-documented complications, such as retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, and vascular disease.[3] Recent data also suggest that skeletal fragility should be recognized as a complication of diabetes, given the disproportionately high risk of fractures in individuals with T2DM.[4,5] It is particularly concerning that fractures occur more frequently in these patients even though their bone mineral density (BMD) is often preserved or even elevated, presenting a paradox that underscores the critical nature of this complication.[6] Moreover, mortality following fractures is significantly greater in patients with T2DM than in non-diabetic individuals with similar injuries.[7–9]

Although the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying diminished bone health in T2DM are complex and not fully understood, the contributing factors may include hyperglycemia, obesity, hyperinsulinemia, accumulation of advanced glycation end products, and micro- and macrovascular complications.[10,11] Certain diabetes-specific characteristics seem to define subgroups with heightened fracture risk, including long duration of diabetes, poor glycemic control, concomitant retinopathy or neuropathy, and poor functional status. Furthermore, numerous studies have investigated the relationship between fracture risk and anti-diabetic drugs, particularly highlighting the increased risk associated with insulin [12,13] and thiazolidinedione (TZD).[14] The impact of newer anti-diabetic agents, such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i),[15,16] on fracture risk continues to be debated.

However, these studies often have limitations owing to the high correlation between diabetes-related characteristics and the use of various anti-diabetic drugs, which can introduce potential selection bias and confounding variables. Furthermore, diabetes-focused cohorts often include patients with T2DM from diverse demographic groups, such as men and younger individuals whose fracture risks differ from those of women and older individuals who are more vulnerable to fractures.[11,17] This demographic variability limits the ability to draw conclusions regarding the effects of different anti-diabetic drugs on bone health. There is a lack of research specifically examining the impact of various anti-diabetic drugs on fracture risk in older women with T2DM.

To address this gap, a nested case-control design was employed using national claims data from Korea to investigate the fracture risks associated with various anti-diabetic drugs in this population.

METHODS

Data source

This study used data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS), which provides mandatory health coverage for nearly all residents in Korea. The KNHIS database contains extensive health information for approximately 50 million individuals, encompassing sociodemographic details, diagnostic codes, medical procedures, and prescribed medications, as referenced in sources. [18,19] Diagnoses in this database were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). Additionally, the database includes the results of general health screenings that are mandated biannually for all insured employees, self-employed individuals, and their dependents. These screenings aim to enhance public health through early detection and prevention of diseases.[20] Furthermore, the database incorporates findings from the National Screening Program for Transitional Ages (NSPTA), specifically designed to assess the risk of osteoporotic fractures in women aged 66 years. [21] This program employs a variety of BMD measurement technologies, such as dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), peripheral DXA, ultrasound, and computed tomography. In this database, BMD results are reported as categorized values (normal, osteopenia, or osteoporosis) rather than continuous measurements, and detailed information on measurement devices or anatomical sites is not available.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Yongin Severance Hospital and ensured compliance with the ethical standards outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki (IRB No. 9-2022-0095). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB of Yongin Severance Hospital because this study used de-identified data from the KNHIS cohort and had a retrospective design.

Study population

Among women who underwent BMD examination at age 66 by the NSPTA between January 2011 and December 2017 (N=778,719), those diagnosed with T2DM between January 2010 and December 2018 (N=213,428) were included. T2DM is defined as (1) having a diagnostic code for T2DM (ICD-10 E11-E14) with a prescription of anti-diabetic drugs, including metformin (Met), sulfonylurea (SU), TZD, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP4i), or SGLT2i, or (2) having two or more diagnostic codes for T2DM with hemoglobin A1C measurement. This definition was based on approaches used in previous studies utilizing the KNHIS database and was adapted to the available data fields in our dataset.[22] The cohort entry date was defined as the first day after T2DM diagnosis. Participants who had previously been diagnosed with any osteoporotic fracture before the cohort entry date were excluded (N=9,490).

Cases are defined as those who developed osteoporotic fractures at the vertebra, hip, humerus, or wrist after cohort entry (N=23,409) and controls as those who did not (N=180,529). Among the participants in the case group, those with a history of prescription of osteoporosis medication, including bisphosphonate (BP) or selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM), between the year before cohort entry and the occurrence of osteoporotic fractures were excluded (N=11,917). Each participant in the case group was 1:3 matched to those in the control group on the basis of date of birth, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and cohort entry date. The index date for the case participants was the date of osteoporotic fracture, and the index date was randomly assigned for the matched control participants to be the same as that for the case groups. Additionally, among the participants in the control group, those with a history of BP or SERM use were excluded (N=7,127). Finally, the case group included 10,104 patients, and the control group included 18,461 patients (Fig. 1). The follow-up duration between cohort entry and the index date in the study cohort was 3.8±2.8 years.

Exposure assessments

All study participants were classified according to anti-diabetic drug use, such as Met-only, SU, TZD, DPP4i, and SGLT2i groups. The primary anti-diabetic drugs were determined based on prescriptions issued for at least 180 days prior to the fracture date in the case group or prior to the matched index date in the control group, in accordance with definitions used in previous studies.[23–26] Met-only users were defined as those who used Met only for at least 180 days between cohort entry and the index date. Other anti-diabetic drug users were defined as those who used the corresponding anti-diabetic drugs (SU, TZD, DPP4i, and SGLT2i) for at least 180 days during the period from the cohort entry data to the index date. Except for the Met-only group, classification into drug groups was non–mutually exclusive; patients who used multiple anti-diabetic drug classes for ≥180 days were assigned to all corresponding groups. Those who had never received anti-diabetic drugs or had taken any of the anti-diabetic drugs for less than 180 days were included in the non-medication group. We selected Met monotherapy as the comparator group due to its known neutrality or beneficial effects on fracture risk, its common use in combination therapies that could confound results with other agents.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of major osteoporotic fractures, including vertebral, hip, humeral, and wrist fractures. The secondary outcome was the incidence of vertebral and nonvertebral fractures, including hip, humeral, and wrist fractures. The operational definitions of osteoporotic fractures were made using ICD-10 codes and procedure codes using KNHIS data. ICD-10 codes for vertebral fracture were; S22.0 (fracture of the thoracic spine), S22.1 (multiple fractures of the thoracic spine), S32.0 (fracture of the lumbar spine), S32.7 (multiple fractures of the lumbar spine), T080 (fracture of the spine), M48.4 (fatigue fracture of vertebra), M48.5 (collapsed vertebra), and M49.5 (collapsed vertebra). ICD-10 codes of hip fractures were S72.0 (fracture of the femur neck) and S72.1 (trochanteric fracture). ICD-10 codes of humerus fractures were S42.2 (fracture of upper end of humerus), S42.3 (fractured shaft of humerus), and codes for wrist fractures were S52.5 (fracture of lower end of radius) and S52.6 (fracture of lower end of both ulnar and radius).[27]

Covariates

Covariates include age at index date, body mass index (BMI) close to the point of index date, BMD at their NSPTA health examinations, old T2DM, CCI at cohort entry, comorbidities (myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, dementia, Parkinson’s disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic liver disease, rheumatoid arthritis, malignancy, and hyperthyroidism), and concurrent drugs (vitamin D/calcium complex, glucocorticoid, proton pump inhibitor, and anti-convulsants). Old T2DM was defined as a diagnosis of T2DM before the earliest cohort entry in January 2010. Comorbidities and concurrent drugs used were defined as relevant claim codes during the period ranging from one year before the cohort entry date to the index date.

Statistical analyses

The baseline characteristics of the study participants according to fracture status were analyzed using Student’s t-test or the χ2 test. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to determine the odds ratios (ORs) for major osteoporotic, vertebral, and non-vertebral fractures associated with different anti-diabetic drugs, including SU, TZD, DPP4i, and SGLT2i, as well as non-medication, compared with Met alone. The multivariable model included age, BMI, CCI, BMD, old T2DM, comorbidities including myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, Parkinson’s disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic liver disease, rheumatoid arthritis, malignancy, and hyperthyroidism; and concurrent medications including vitamin D/calcium complexes, glucocorticoids, proton pump inhibitors, and anticonvulsants. Subgroup analyses were performed for the OR of vertebral and non-vertebral fractures in the TZD group according to BMD results and BMI, and the P-value for interaction was calculated. These subgroup analyses were not pre-specified in the study design but were conducted after observing a significant association between TZD use and fracture risk in the overall analysis. Covariates were selected based on established risk factors for bone health and fracture, as well as clinical relevance for diabetes severity. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.15 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). For this analysis, a two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of study subjects

The mean age at the index date for all participants with T2DM was 66.0±3.4 years, with 46.7% and 24.4% of them showing osteopenia and osteoporosis, respectively, on BMD examinations performed at age 66. Moreover, 69.1% of participants with T2DM were classified as having old T2DM. Among all participants, the groups treated with Met-only, SU, TZD, DPP4i, SGLT2i, and those without or with less than 6 months of medication included 11.4%, 51.6%, 9.1%, 30.9%, 1.2%, and 13.6% of all participants with T2DM, respectively (Table 1). The mean follow-up duration of study participants was 3.8±2.8 years.

Differences in clinical characteristics between fractured and non-fractured patients

Patients with T2DM with fractures were slightly older (66.5±3.4 vs. 65.8±2.4 years, P<0.001), included more patients with osteopenia or osteoporosis (47.8% vs. 46.1% and 29.7% vs. 21.6%, respectively, P<0.001), and included fewer patients with old T2DM (62.9% vs. 72.5%, P<0.001) compared to those without fractures. The fracture group included more patients with T2DM who were prescribed TZD (10.6% vs. 8.2%, P<0.001) and DPP4i (32.0% vs. 30.3%, P=0.002) or who had been prescribed any medication for less than 6 months (15.0% vs. 12.8%, P<0.001) than the non-fracture group. Conversely, fewer patients with T2DM with fractures were prescribed SU (49.6% vs. 52.7%, P<0.001). The prevalence of comorbidities, including myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, Parkinson’s disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic liver disease, rheumatoid arthritis, malignancy, and hyperthyroidism was higher in the fracture group than in the non-fracture group (P<0.001 to P=0.020). Patients with T2DM with fractures were more often prescribed concurrent medications, including vitamin D/calcium complexes, glucocorticoids, proton pump inhibitors, and anticonvulsants, than those without fractures (all P<0.001) (Table 1).

The associations between anti-diabetic drugs and risk of fractures

The odds for major osteoporotic fractures, including vertebra, hip, humerus, and wrist fractures, were higher in the TZD group compared to the Met-only group (OR, 1.35; 95% CI, 1.19–1.53; P<0.001) after adjusting for all confounders, while there were no significant differences in the SU, DP-P4i, SGLT2i, and non-medication groups (Fig. 2). Moreover, the odds for vertebral fractures were higher in the TZD group compared to the Met-only group (OR, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.03–1.42; P=0.022) after adjusting for all confounders, while there were no significant differences in the SU, DPP4i, SGLT2i, and non-medication groups (Fig. 3A). The odds for non-vertebral fractures were also higher in the TZD group compared to the Met-only group (OR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.14–1.52; P<0.001) after adjusting for all confounders, while there were no significant differences in the other groups (Fig. 3B).

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore the effects of BMD and BMI on the increased risk of fractures with TZD use, which only provided significant associations with fracture risk. In the subgroup analyses, the increased ORs for vertebral fracture in the TZD group compared to the Met-only group were observed in patients with T2DM with normal BMD and osteopenia (OR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.01–1.96; P=0.046 and OR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.10–1.78; P=0.006, respectively) after adjusting for all confounders, while no significant difference was observed in those with osteoporosis (P for interaction=0.004) (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the increased OR for non-vertebral fracture in the TZD group compared to the Met-only group was observed in patients with T2DM with normal BMI (OR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.28–1.94; P<0.001) after adjusting for all confounders, while no significant difference was observed in those with obesity (P for interaction=0.042) (Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION

In this nested case-control study, based on national claims data of older Korean women with T2DM, the risk of major osteoporotic fractures, vertebral fractures, and non-vertebral fractures was found to increase in those treated with TZD compared to those treated with Met alone, whereas other anti-diabetic drugs including SU, DPP4i, and SGLT2i showed no differences. Particularly, among older Korean women with T2DM treated with TZD, those with normal or osteopenic BMD exhibited a significantly higher risk of vertebral fractures than those treated with Met alone, and those with normal BMI showed a significantly higher risk of non-vertebral fractures than those treated with Met alone.

The impact of anti-diabetic drugs on bone density, quality, and fracture risk has received considerable attention for several years owing to the long-term nature of diabetes management. However, results from many previous studies remain contradictory owing to the scarcity of prospective studies exploring the effects of anti-diabetic drugs on bone fragility and their complicated relationship with glycemic control and bone health.[13,28] Furthermore, the small number of fractures in a certain study population, which was set as an outcome of the study, further limits the conclusions drawn. Nevertheless, the generally accepted concepts are that insulin use is associated with an increased risk of fractures,[12] Met use is neutral or favorable,[ 29,30] incretin-based therapies such as DPP4i or glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists are neutral or favorable,[ 31,32] and SGLT2i use is neutral or deleterious for fracture risk.[15,16] The effect of SU is contradictory,[29,30] whereas the detrimental impact of TZD on fracture risk is well-established and consistent with the findings of our study.[14]

The adverse skeletal effects of TZDs are primarily attributed to peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ–mediated changes in gene expression, which shift mesenchymal stem cell differentiation toward adipogenesis at the expense of osteoblastogenesis.[33] Consistent with this mechanism, a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials reported that TZD use nearly doubled fracture risk in women with T2DM (OR, 2.23; 95% CI, 1.65–3.01).[34] The increased fracture risk has been shown to be independent of specific TZD type or patient age,[14] but was partly explained by lower BMD.[35] Moreover, a previous study suggested that fracture risk rises with longer TZDs treatment duration, supporting the biological plausibility of cumulative skeletal effects.[36] In line with these findings, our study of older women with T2DM also demonstrated an increased risk of both vertebral and non-vertebral fractures among TZD users, even after accounting for potential confounding by BMD, comorbidities, and concurrent medications known to influence bone health. The magnitude of this risk (OR, ~1.3) is comparable to that of other moderate clinical risk factors—such as smoking, low BMI, or parental history of hip fracture (OR, ~1.2–1.5)—but smaller than the risk associated with prior fracture or long-term glucocorticoid use (OR, ≥1.5–2.0).[37] Furthermore, unlike many prior studies that primarily examined overall fractures risk, our analysis demonstrated elevated risks for both vertebral and non-vertebral fractures in TZD-treated patients, providing further information on the risk factors for vertebral fractures in patients with T2DM—a topic that has been relatively underexplored.

Moreover, the risk of vertebral fractures among TZD users was particularly high in patients with normal or osteopenic BMD. This pattern may reflect the greater relative impact of TZD in patients with preserved or near-normal BMD, where bone remodeling capacity is intact.[33] In contrast, in those with established osteoporosis, bone fragility may already result from multiple other factors, making the detrimental effect of TZDs less apparent. Although this finding appears counterintuitive, it may be partly explained by treatment selection bias—patients with osteoporosis might have been preferentially prescribed alternative agents with more favorable bone safety profiles. Residual confounding and limited statistical power in subgroup analyses may also have contributed to this pattern. Nevertheless, these findings underscore the importance of considering BMD status when prescribing TZDs. While our study does not define formal thresholds for avoiding TZD use, clinicians should exercise particular caution in patients with preserved or near-normal BMD, who might otherwise be perceived as lower risk. Similarly, the increased risk of non-vertebral fractures in patients with normal BMI treated with TZD indicates that more attention should be paid when prescribing TZD to non-obese women with T2DM, especially since low body weight is a known risk factor for osteoporosis and fractures.

In this study, unlike TZD, other anti-diabetic medications— including SU, DPP4i, and even SGLT2i—did not show an increased risk of fractures compared with Met alone. This finding contrasts with concerns raised by the CANagliflozin cardioVascular Assessment Study (CANVAS) trial, which reported an elevated fracture risk associated with canagliflozin, a type of SGLT2i.[15,38] However, a subsequent real-world, fixed-effects meta-analysis outside the CANVAS program found no material difference in fracture incidence between canagliflozin and comparator groups, [39] and more recent cardiovascular outcome trials [40–42] and meta-analyses of dapagliflozin and empagliflozin have reported largely neutral results.[43–45] Real-world observational studies similarly support the absence of a consistent class-wide effect, though agent-specific differences and patient heterogeneity cannot be excluded.[25,46–49] These findings suggest that the fracture signal observed in CANVAS may not represent a uniform effect across the SGLT2i class. In our study, we specifically examined this association in older female patients with T2DM, who are especially vulnerable to fractures. However, the sample size in this subgroup was insufficient for definitive conclusions, and the evidence regarding fracture risk in frail elderly individuals remains inconclusive. Therefore, further large-scale studies are warranted to assess fracture risk in T2DM patients treated with SGLT2i, with careful consideration of patient characteristics such as sex, age, and frailty status. Additionally, assessing fracture risk prior to initiating SGLT2i therapy remains important, and the long-term effects of these agents on bone quality and fracture incidence require further investigation.

This study has several strengths, including the use of national claims data from the KNHIS involving a large number of older female patients with T2DM, enhancing the reliability of our findings on the association of various anti-diabetic drugs with fracture risk in these patients. Additionally, analyzing older female patients with T2DM provides valuable insights into the association between anti-diabetic drugs and fracture risk in a population particularly vulnerable to deteriorated bone health. This study investigated the risk of fractures not only in the context of major osteoporotic fractures but also by distinguishing between vertebral and non-vertebral fractures. This further highlights the increased risk of vertebral fractures in patients treated with TZD despite the ongoing controversy over the risk of vertebral fractures in patients with T2DM. Furthermore, in this study, BMD data were used as an adjustment factor in the analysis, which allowed us to evaluate the independent effect of anti-diabetic drugs on fracture risk after accounting for the bone-related characteristics of each patient with T2DM. Finally, the effects of various anti-diabetic drugs, including relatively new medications, such as SGLT2i inhibitors, on fracture risk were compared. These results provide comprehensive insights into the effects of anti-diabetic drugs on the risk of fractures in older female patients with T2DM. However, this study has limitations. Our analyses involved multiple statistical comparisons across different drug classes and fracture outcomes. Although we were unable to apply formal multiple comparison corrections, we acknowledge that this limitation may have increased the risk of type I error. Therefore, marginally significant results, such as the association between TZD and vertebral fractures, should be interpreted with caution. Because the index date for controls was assigned only after matching, pre-index exclusion of anti-osteoporosis medication users could be applied solely to cases, which may have introduced a differential exclusion process between groups. This study may have been underpowered for certain analyses, particularly subgroup interaction tests, the relatively small SGLT2i group, and single fracture outcomes with fewer events. Non-significant findings for SU and DPP4i should also not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no effect. These limitations highlight the need for larger, adequately powered studies to confirm our observations. Although we matched and adjusted for multiple covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured or incompletely captured factors cannot be excluded. Especially, we were unable to adjust for certain diabetes-related factors that can affect bone health, including glycemic control levels, exact duration of diabetes, diabetes-related complications, and the precise dosage of diabetes medications. Moreover, our analysis did not account for menopause-related factors such as age at menopause, time since menopause, or hormone replacement therapy use, which were not available in our claims data. Since anti-diabetic drug prescription patterns evolved over the study period, involving complex regimens of monotherapy and combination therapies, we simplified our analysis by defining the primary anti-diabetic drug as the one used for at least 180 days. However, this approach may overlook both the influence of varying drug-exposure durations and potential combined effects from concurrent use of multiple anti-diabetic medications on fracture outcomes. This potential misclassification may have biased the results. BMD was measured using various methods, but our analysis relied on KNHIS-provided classifications (osteoporosis, osteopenia, normal), which may not fully eliminate heterogeneity. Since SGLT2i was introduced in Korea only in 2014, the duration of exposure was relatively short and the number of patients treated with SGLT2i was limited. As the investigated population in this study was limited to older female patients with diabetes, the findings cannot be generalized to assess fracture risk associated with anti-diabetic medications in the entire diabetic population. Lastly, as this study employed a nested case–control design, absolute clinical risk measures (e.g., number needed to harm or treatment thresholds) could not be established. Therefore, our findings are presented in relative terms and should be interpreted with caution.

In this study based on national claims data from older Korean female patients with T2DM, TZD use was associated with increased risks of vertebral and non-vertebral fractures compared with Met, whereas other anti-diabetic drugs, including SU, DPP4i, and SGLT2i, did not show such associations. Given the heightened fracture vulnerability of this population, these findings highlight the importance of considering skeletal health when prescribing TZDs. Given the relatively small number of patients using SGLT2i in this study, further research is needed to clarify the association between SGLT2i use and fracture risk in this population.
