Social and community determinants of living arrangements among older adults: the role of social support and mental health promotion
Authors: Supaporn Sudnongbua, Samran Chuamuangphan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiving arrangements are a key social determinant of health among older adults, influencing well-being, access to care, and social participation. Beyond sociodemographic characteristics, social and community factors—particularly social support and mental health promotion—may play important roles in shaping living arrangements. However, evidence on the associations between these multidimensional factors and living arrangements remains limited. This study examined the relationships between social and community determinants and living arrangements among older adults.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among adults aged 60 years and older (n = 468). Living arrangements were categorized as living alone or living with a spouse and/or children. Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted using three hierarchical models. Model 1 included sociodemographic characteristics; Model 2 added overall social support and overall mental health promotion; and Model 3 examined specific components of social support and mental health promotion. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsGender and age were consistently associated with living arrangements across models. After adjustment for sociodemographic factors, several social and community determinants were significantly associated with living arrangements. In the fully adjusted model, stronger interpersonal relationships and higher financial support were associated with higher odds of living with a spouse and/or children. In contrast, greater accessibility to technology and higher levels of emotional and instrumental support were independently associated with lower odds of co-residence. Regarding mental health promotion, higher levels of community-based mental health promotion were inversely associated with living with a spouse and/or children, while personal- and family-level mental health promotion showed no significant associations in the final model. The final model demonstrated good explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.682).ConclusionsLiving arrangements among older adults are shaped by a complex interplay of sociodemographic, social, and community factors. Different dimensions of social support and mental health promotion show distinct and sometimes contrasting associations with co-residence. These findings highlight the importance of adopting a nuanced, multidimensional approach when designing social and community-based interventions to support appropriate and sustainable living arrangements among older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07280-6.

Introduction

Global population ageing has accelerated in recent decades, with the proportion of older adults increasing worldwide. This demographic transition has heightened attention to living arrangements as a critical social determinant of health in later life. Living arrangements influence multiple dimensions of older adults’ well-being, including psychological health, physical functioning, access to care, social participation, and overall quality of life. Evidence consistently shows that older adults who live alone or outside family settings are more likely to experience loneliness, depressive symptoms, and poorer mental health compared with those who live with a spouse or family members [1–3]. Importantly, living arrangements affect health not only as structural residential patterns but also through underlying psychosocial mechanisms, particularly social support and social connectedness [4].

Social support is a well-established protective factor for mental health and well-being among older adults. Emotional, instrumental, and financial support have been associated with reduced depressive symptoms, greater life satisfaction, and improved coping with age-related stressors [5, 6]. Supportive social environments can buffer the negative effects of stress and loneliness, especially in contexts where community engagement and interpersonal relationships are strong [7]. Conversely, limited or inadequate social support may intensify feelings of isolation and increase vulnerability to adverse mental health outcomes, particularly among older adults living alone [8]. These findings highlight the central role of social support in shaping both psychological outcomes and everyday living conditions in later life.

Beyond social support, mental health promotion plays an increasingly important role in healthy ageing. Mental health promotion encompasses strategies at individual, family, and community levels aimed at strengthening psychological resilience, enhancing social participation, and reducing risk factors for mental illness. Community-based interventions that foster social interaction, meaningful activity, and collective engagement have been shown to improve psychosocial outcomes and reduce social isolation among older adults [9]. Despite growing recognition of the importance of mental health promotion, relatively few studies have examined how mental health promotion interacts with social support to influence living arrangements in older populations.

Theoretical perspectives from social gerontology and public health provide important lenses for understanding these relationships. The Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) framework posits that social and economic conditions—such as financial security, access to information and services, social relationships, and community resources—fundamentally shape health outcomes and life circumstances across the life course [10]. From this perspective, living arrangements are not merely individual choices but reflect accumulated social advantages or disadvantages. Older adults with greater economic stability and stronger social and community resources are more likely to reside in supportive living environments, including co-residence with family members [11].

At the interpersonal level, Social Support Theory further explains how different dimensions of support buffer stress and promote psychological well-being in later life. Emotional, instrumental, financial, and informational support can enhance coping capacity, sustain social roles, and influence the feasibility of shared living arrangements [12, 13]. Strong interpersonal relationships may facilitate continued co-residence within family networks, whereas limited support may increase the likelihood of solitary living. In addition, Activity Theory of Aging suggests that sustained engagement in social roles and meaningful activities contributes to successful ageing and well-being [14]. Older adults who remain socially active and connected within families and communities are therefore more likely to maintain socially integrated living arrangements.

In Thailand, rapid population ageing presents significant challenges for older adults’ well-being. National longitudinal evidence indicates that loneliness, depressive symptoms, socio-economic status, and living alone are key determinants of life satisfaction and mental health among older Thais [15]. In northern Thailand, where community ties and cultural practices remain influential, studies have documented psychosocial challenges among older adults living alone, as well as adaptive coping strategies such as the use of mobile communication and participation in community-based activities to support mental well-being [16]. Furthermore, culturally adapted psychosocial and community-based programs in northern Thailand have shown promise in reducing loneliness and improving social connectedness and mental health outcomes among older adults [17]. These findings underscore the importance of examining living arrangements within their broader social and community contexts.

Despite a growing body of evidence on social support, mental health, and ageing, research examining the combined influence of sociodemographic factors, social support, and mental health promotion on older adults’ living arrangements remains limited globally. In particular, there is insufficient understanding of how both overall and component-specific dimensions of social support and mental health promotion interact to shape living arrangements in later life. Guided by the Social Determinants of Health framework, Social Support Theory, and Activity Theory of Aging, this study seeks to address this gap by examining the associations between sociodemographic characteristics, social support, mental health promotion, and living arrangements among older adults. Elucidating these relationships is critical for informing evidence-based policies and interventions that foster supportive living environments and promote healthy ageing across diverse social and cultural contexts.

Guided by a social determinants’ perspective, this study conceptualized living arrangements as a function of multiple domains, including mental health well-being, social determinants, social support, and mental health promotion. Sociodemographic characteristics were included as covariates in the regression models. The conceptual framework is presented in Fig. 1.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the factors associated with living arrangements (living alone vs. co-residence) among older adults, with a particular focus on mental health well-being, social determinants, social support, and mental health promotion. Guided by a multidimensional social determinants perspective, we conceptualized living arrangements as a structural and social outcome influenced by multiple psychosocial and community-level domains.

Using multivariable binary logistic regression models, we assessed the independent associations between these explanatory constructs and living arrangements while adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics.

This study contributes to the literature in several important ways. First, it integrates mental health, social determinants, social support, and health promotion constructs within a single analytical framework rather than examining them in isolation. Second, it advances the understanding of living arrangements as a key structural dimension of later life. Third, it provides empirical evidence from a middle-income country context, where research on multidimensional determinants of living arrangements remains limited.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This study employed a cross-sectional design to examine associations between sociodemographic characteristics, social support, mental health promotion, and living arrangements among older adults. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered to community-dwelling older adults. The survey gathered information on living arrangements, sociodemographic characteristics, social support, mental health promotion, and related contextual factors.

The study was conducted in rural communities in Chiang Rai Province, Thailand. Participants were selected using a simple random sampling method from a sampling frame of eligible older adults obtained through local health centers and community records. This approach ensured that all eligible individuals had an equal probability of selection and enhanced the representativeness of the study sample.

Eligible participants were aged 60 years and older, resided in the study communities at the time of data collection, and provided informed consent prior to participation. Data collection was carried out by trained researchers to ensure consistency and data quality. For participants with limited literacy, visual impairment, or language barriers, face-to-face interviews were conducted using linguistically appropriate procedures to facilitate comprehension and ensure reliable responses.

Sample size and sampling

A total of 468 participants were included in the study. The sample size was calculated using the formula for estimating a proportion in a finite population, based on standard methodological guidance [18]. The total population comprised 32,685 individuals aged 60 years and above. Assuming a 50% expected prevalence to maximize the required sample size, a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 421 participants. To account for potential non-response or incomplete data, an additional 10% was added, resulting in a final target sample size of 468 participants.

Participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique from the eligible population. The sampling frame consisted of an official registry of older adults obtained from the local administrative office, which included all residents meeting the inclusion criteria within the study area. Each eligible individual was assigned a unique identification number, and participants were selected using computer-generated random numbers produced by statistical software.

In cases of refusal or inability to participate, replacement participants were selected from the remaining eligible individuals using the same randomization procedure to preserve the randomness of the sample.

Measures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire comprising standardized and study-specific instruments. The questionnaire included measures of living arrangements, sociodemographic characteristics, mental health well-being, social determinants, social support, and mental health promotion. An English-language version of the questionnaire is provided as a supplementary file.

Instrument development and validation

The social determinants and mental health promotion scales were newly developed for this study based on an extensive review of relevant literature and established conceptual frameworks related to social determinants of health and geriatric mental health care. Item generation was guided by theoretically derived domains and adapted to reflect the sociocultural context of older adults in Thailand.

The social support scale was adapted from previously published instruments developed among older adults in rural Northeast Thailand [19, 20]. The original items were reviewed and culturally contextualized for the present study, and minor wording modifications were made to improve clarity and relevance to the study population.

Content validity was evaluated by a panel of three experts in geriatric nursing, public health, and mental health. Each item was assessed for relevance, clarity, and cultural appropriateness using a four-point rating scale. Item-level content validity indices (I-CVI) ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.94, indicating excellent content validity. Minor revisions were made based on expert feedback.

A pilot study was conducted among 40 older adults with characteristics similar to the target population. Feedback from the pilot testing was used to refine item wording and enhance comprehensibility prior to the main data collection. No major structural modifications were required.

Reliability

Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The Thai Geriatric Mental Health Assessment Tool (T-GMHA-15), developed by the Department of Mental Health, Ministry of Public Health, Thailand [21], demonstrated excellent reliability in previous research (α = 0.940) [22]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.962 for the T-GMHA-15, 0.880 for the social determinants scale, 0.898 for the social support scale, and 0.886 for the mental health promotion scale.

Living arrangements

Living arrangements were assessed as the primary outcome variable. Participants were asked about their current household composition and classified into two categories: living alone or living with a spouse and/or child/children. For analytical purposes, living arrangements were dichotomized, with living with a spouse and/or child/children coded as 1 and living alone coded as 0.

This categorization was chosen to reflect the presence or absence of immediate family co-residence, which represents a central source of informal support in the Thai sociocultural context.

Sociodemographic variables

Sociodemographic characteristics included gender, age, marital status, educational attainment, employment status, methods of physical care, methods of mental health care, source of health information, mental health well-being, and health benefit scheme. Gender was categorized as male or female. Age was collected as a categorical variable, with participants classified into predefined age groups (60–69 years and ≥ 70 years). Marital status was classified as not married or married. Educational attainment was categorized as low education or secondary education or higher. Employment status was classified as employed or not employed.

Methods of physical health care were categorized as ethnic-belief–based care or modern medical care. Methods of mental health care were similarly classified as ethnic-belief–based care or modern mental health care. Sources of health information were categorized as broadcast or leaflet–based sources and village health volunteers. Health benefit scheme was classified as either the Civil Servant and Social Security Scheme or Universal Health Coverage.

Mental health well-being

Mental health well-being was assessed using the Thai Geriatric Mental Health Assessment Tool (T-GMHA-15), which comprises 15 items covering emotional well-being, social functioning, and perceived family support. Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Not at all”) to 4 (“Extremely”), with higher scores indicating better mental health well-being. Item 3, which assessed worry or anxiety, was reverse-coded prior to score calculation.

A total mental health well-being score was obtained by summing responses across all 15 items, yielding a possible score range of 15 to 60. For analytical purposes, the total score was dichotomized into low and high mental health well-being using an equal-interval approach based on the rating scale. The scale range (60–15 = 45) was divided into two equal intervals. Participants with total scores ranging from 15 to 37 were classified as having low mental health well-being, while those with scores ranging from 38 to 60 were classified as having high mental health well-being. This approach facilitated interpretation and ensured consistency with the measurement scale for use in binary logistic regression analysis.

Social determinants

Social determinants were assessed using a structured questionnaire comprising four domains: financial security, access to technology, availability of mental health services, and interpersonal relationships. The instrument included 47 items. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Always”), with higher scores indicating more favorable social determinant conditions.

Negatively worded items were reverse-coded prior to score calculation so that higher scores consistently reflected more advantageous conditions across all domains. Domain-specific scores were then calculated by summing item responses within each domain.

For analytical purposes, each domain score was dichotomized into low and high levels using an equal-interval classification based on the theoretical score range of the rating scale. The minimum and maximum possible scores for each domain were determined by the number of items multiplied by the lowest and highest scale values, respectively, and the resulting score range was divided into two equal intervals. Participants with domain scores in the lower interval were classified as having low levels of the respective social determinant, whereas those with scores in the upper interval were classified as having high levels.

This scale-based dichotomization approach was applied to facilitate interpretation and ensure suitability for binary logistic regression analysis, while preserving consistency with the measurement structure of the instrument.

Social support

Social support was assessed using a structured questionnaire consisting of 20 items covering four domains: emotional support, financial support, material support, and instrumental support. Participants were asked to indicate how often they had received each type of support during the past 12 months. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Always”), with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support.

All items were positively worded; therefore, no reverse coding was required. Domain-specific scores were calculated by summing item responses within each domain, and an overall social support score was obtained by summing responses across all 20 items.

For analytical purposes, social support was dichotomized into low and high levels using an equal-interval classification based on the theoretical score range of the rating scale. The total possible score ranged from 20 to 100, calculated from the minimum and maximum scale values across all items. The score range was divided into two equal intervals. Participants with scores in the lower interval were classified as having low social support, whereas those with scores in the upper interval were classified as having high social support. This scale-based dichotomization facilitated interpretation and ensured suitability for binary logistic regression analysis.

Mental health promotion

Mental health promotion among older adults was assessed using a structured questionnaire comprising 13 items across three levels: individual, family, and community. Participants were asked to indicate how often they had engaged in or received mental health promotion activities during the past 12 months. Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Not at all”) to 4 (“Very much”), with higher scores indicating greater engagement in mental health promotion activities.

All items were positively worded; therefore, no reverse coding was required. A total mental health promotion score was calculated by summing responses across all items, resulting in a possible score range of 13 to 52.

For analytical purposes, the total mental health promotion score was dichotomized into low and high levels using an equal-interval classification based on the theoretical score range of the rating scale. The total score range (52–13 = 39) was divided into two equal intervals. Participants with total scores in the lower interval were classified as having low mental health promotion, whereas those with scores in the upper interval were classified as having high mental health promotion. This scale-based dichotomization approach facilitated interpretation and ensured suitability for binary logistic regression analysis. A sensitivity analysis was performed using continuous scores for key explanatory variables to assess the robustness of the findings (see Supplementary Table S1).

Data analysis

Some variables originally measured using four response categories were dichotomized for binary logistic regression analysis. Categories were combined based on conceptual similarity and response distribution to ensure adequate cell sizes and interpretability.

All conceptually relevant variables were entered simultaneously into the multivariable logistic regression models to allow comprehensive adjustment for potential confounding and to estimate the independent association of each factor with living arrangements. This theory-driven modeling strategy was adopted to minimize bias associated with data-driven selection procedures (e.g., stepwise methods), which may increase Type I error and produce unstable estimates. By incorporating sociodemographic characteristics, social determinants, social support, and mental health promotion variables, the models were designed to reflect the multidimensional influences on living arrangements.

Prior to model estimation, diagnostic assessments were conducted to evaluate multicollinearity and model stability. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with values > 5 considered indicative of concern. Pairwise correlation coefficients were also examined, with coefficients > 0.70 suggesting high collinearity. All retained variables demonstrated VIF values < 5 and correlation coefficients < 0.70.

Variables with sparse distributions that generated unstable estimates or wide confidence intervals were carefully examined. Where quasi-complete separation was suspected, sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

To enhance transparency regarding model complexity, the number of predictors included in each model was explicitly reported. Model 3 included 17 independent variables and 68 events (older adults living alone), corresponding to an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of 4. Given the relatively small number of events, regression estimates were interpreted with caution. A sensitivity analysis excluding gender was conducted to assess potential instability related to sparse data; the overall pattern and direction of associations remained materially unchanged (Table S1).

Multivariable binary logistic regression analyses are then used to identify independent determinants of living arrangements among older adults, with adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals reported. The sensitivity analysis using continuous scores yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S1).

Slightly more than half were female, and the majority were aged 60–69 years. Over half were not married, and most had low educational attainment. Approximately half of the participants were employed.

Modern medical care was the predominant method of physical health care, whereas the use of ethnic-belief and modern approaches for mental health care was more evenly distributed. Most participants received health information from village health volunteers and were covered under the Universal Health Coverage scheme.

The majority reported high levels of mental health well-being. Living alone was reported by a minority of participants, while most lived with a spouse and/or children.

Regarding social determinants, most participants reported high financial security and strong interpersonal relationships, although accessibility to mental health services was limited for a substantial proportion.

In terms of social support, the majority reported low levels across most support domains, particularly financial and overall social support. Conversely, most participants reported high levels of personal- and family-level mental health promotion, whereas community-level mental health promotion was more evenly distributed.

Table 2 compares sociodemographic characteristics, health-related factors, and social determinant factors between older adults living alone and those living with a spouse and children. Significant differences were observed across most variables.

Older adults living alone were more likely to be female, aged 70 years or older, not married, have lower educational attainment, and be unemployed compared with those living with a spouse and/or children (all p < 0.001). Differences were also observed in methods of physical and mental health care, source of health information, and health benefit schemes.

Regarding social determinants, financial security, accessibility of technology, and accessibility of mental health services differed significantly between groups. In contrast, mental health well-being and interpersonal relationships did not significantly differ by living arrangement.

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive evidence that living arrangements among older adults are strongly associated with a constellation of sociodemographic characteristics, social determinants, social support, and mental health promotion factors. The findings reinforce growing international evidence that living arrangements in later life are not merely residential choices but reflect broader social, economic, and psychosocial contexts that shape ageing experiences globally [23–27].

Interpretation of key associations

Several sociodemographic variables were significantly associated with living arrangements. Gender showed a particularly strong association, with women having substantially higher odds of living with a spouse and/or children compared with men. Similar gendered patterns have been documented across Asia, Europe, and Latin America, where women’s longer life expectancy, caregiving roles, and stronger family ties contribute to co-residential living in older age [28–32]. Age was also associated with living arrangements, with adults aged ≥ 70 years showing lower odds of co-residence, reflecting transitions related to widowhood, declining health, and changing family dynamics, as reported in multiple population-based studies [33–36].

Social determinants and access-related factors

Social determinants—including financial security, access to technology, and accessibility of mental health services—were strongly associated with living arrangements. Financial security was positively associated with co-residential living, likely by reducing economic strain on households and enabling shared living arrangements [37–40]. In contrast, greater access to technology was inversely associated with co-residence. Rather than facilitating family-based living, digital access may enable older adults to maintain independence, manage daily activities, and access services autonomously, thereby reducing reliance on family members for co-residential support [41–45].

Accessibility of mental health services also showed significant associations with living arrangements. While accessible services are often viewed as enhancing social integration, in this context they may function as an alternative support system, allowing older adults to meet psychosocial needs outside the household and potentially substituting for family-based care [46–48].

Social support and mental health promotion

Consistent with previous research, social support—both overall and domain-specific—was closely linked to living arrangements. However, the direction of these associations varied across domains. While financial support was positively associated with co-residential living, higher levels of emotional and instrumental support were inversely associated with living with family members. These findings suggest that certain forms of support may promote autonomy rather than dependence, enabling older adults to maintain functional independence without the need for shared households [49–53].

Mental health promotion, particularly at the community level, also showed inverse associations with co-residence. Community-based psychosocial resources may reduce the necessity for family co-residence by providing alternative sources of social engagement, emotional support, and practical assistance. In this sense, community environments may partially substitute for family support rather than reinforcing traditional co-residential arrangements [40–44].

Some associations observed in the multivariable analysis may appear counterintuitive. Higher levels of mental health well-being, stronger community-level mental health promotion, and greater accessibility of services were associated with lower odds of co-residing with a spouse and/or children. One possible explanation is that older adults who live alone may develop greater autonomy, self-reliance, and adaptive coping strategies over time, contributing to better self-perceived psychological well-being. In addition, individuals living alone may engage more actively with community-based resources and formal support systems outside the household, thereby reporting higher levels of mental health promotion and service accessibility. In contrast, older adults living with family members may rely predominantly on intra-household support and therefore have less need for community engagement.

However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. This pattern was also observed for accessibility of services, suggesting that external support systems may serve as functional alternatives to household-based care. Given the cross-sectional design, the directionality of these associations cannot be determined. It is possible that individuals with better mental health and stronger external support systems are more capable of maintaining independent living arrangements, rather than living alone leading to better well-being. The relationships between living arrangements, social support structures, mental health promotion, and service engagement are likely complex and bidirectional. Longitudinal studies are needed to further clarify these dynamics.

High odds ratios and wide confidence intervals

Notably, several variables in Table 4 demonstrated very large odds ratios accompanied by wide confidence intervals. These included gender, financial security, accessibility of technology, interpersonal relationships, and specific domains of social support (particularly emotional and financial support). Such patterns have been reported in previous studies examining complex social determinants among older populations, particularly in settings where exposures are highly clustered or unevenly distributed [54–58].

Large odds ratios may reflect both substantive underlying associations and methodological factors. In many low- and middle-income contexts, financial security and intergenerational support remain dominant determinants of co-residential living among older adults [59–62]. Similarly, strong interpersonal relationships and high levels of emotional support frequently co-occur with family co-residence [63–66]. When such closely related exposures cluster within specific subgroups, their combined effects may produce amplified estimates in multivariable models.

At the same time, methodological considerations—including dichotomization of continuous variables, sparse data within certain categories, limited variability in reference groups, and a relatively low number of outcome events—may further inflate regression coefficients, particularly in fully adjusted models. In multidimensional social determinants frameworks where predictors are interrelated, exceptionally large odds ratios can therefore emerge. These estimates should be interpreted as indicators of strong directional associations rather than precise measures of effect magnitude.

In contrast, for several variables (e.g., technology access and community-level mental health promotion), the observed inverse associations suggest that these resources may facilitate independent living rather than reinforce family co-residence, indicating potential substitution effects between formal or community-based resources and informal family support.

The association between technology access and a lower likelihood of co-residence aligns with growing recognition of digital inclusion as a structural determinant of healthy ageing. Digital connectivity has been shown to expand social participation, access to information, and service utilization among older adults, thereby reducing loneliness and improving psychosocial well-being, including in Asian and middle-income settings [67]. Evidence from rural Thailand further indicates that digital engagement can strengthen community participation and active ageing processes [68]. Within this framework, technology access may function as an enabling resource that supports ageing in place, rather than signalling social withdrawal. This aligns with emerging research on digital inclusion and healthy ageing across Asian settings [69].

Furthermore, wide confidence intervals may indicate imprecision in effect size estimation, potentially arising from sparse data within certain categories, limited variability in exposures, or strong correlations among social variables [70–73]. Similar patterns have been reported in studies using logistic regression to examine social and behavioral determinants, particularly when modeling multidimensional constructs such as social support and mental health promotion [74–78].

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding gender, which exhibited the most extreme odds ratio and suggested quasi-complete separation in the original model. Following its removal, the remaining variables no longer demonstrated unusually large effect estimates, while the direction of associations remained consistent (see Supplementary Table S1). Although these results should be interpreted cautiously with respect to magnitude, the stability of association directions across models supports their substantive relevance.

Ceiling effects and measurement limitations in mental health promotion

Notably, approximately 97% of participants were classified as having high levels of mental health promotion. This suggests a potential ceiling effect and limited discriminatory capacity of the measure. Such patterns may reflect social desirability bias, particularly in self-reported assessments of socially valued behaviors, as well as the design of the instrument, which was intended for health education and community monitoring rather than fine-grained analytical differentiation. Similar ceiling effects have been reported in studies using community-based mental health promotion tools in low- and middle-income settings, where awareness and normative endorsement tend to be high. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the null or inverse associations observed for mental health promotion in the regression models.

Contextual and substitution effects in social and community resources

Although technology access and community-level mental health promotion are often assumed to reinforce supportive living arrangements, our findings indicated inverse associations with co-residence. This pattern suggests possible substitution dynamics, whereby external social and community resources reduce reliance on family-based support. For instance, digital communication technologies may enable older adults to maintain social connections, access services, and manage daily needs without requiring shared living arrangements. Similarly, community-based mental health promotion programs may provide emotional or instrumental support that partially offsets the need for co-residential family care.

The shift in direction observed in Model 3 may also reflect statistical suppression arising from the simultaneous inclusion of correlated social and structural determinants. When interrelated predictors are entered into a fully adjusted model, shared variance may alter the magnitude or direction of specific associations. Rather than representing inconsistency, these findings likely capture the complex and interdependent ways in which technology access, community engagement, and family support jointly shape living arrangements in later life.

Overall interpretation

Taken together, the findings suggest that living arrangements among older adults are embedded within complex and interrelated social systems. Several access-related and psychosocial resources appear to function as substitutes rather than reinforcers of family co-residence, indicating that independent living may be supported by broader community and structural resources rather than relying solely on intra-household support. While some predictors yielded large odds ratios—highlighting the potential strength of social and economic influences—wide confidence intervals underscore contextual concentration and methodological challenges inherent in modeling social determinants. These patterns call for cautious interpretation and further investigation using longitudinal and multi-contextual designs.

These findings can be more deeply understood through established theoretical frameworks. From a Social Determinants of Health perspective, living arrangements are shaped not only by individual preferences but also by structural and community-level conditions that influence access to material and psychosocial resources. The associations observed between social determinants, service accessibility, and independent living arrangements illustrate how broader social environments structure opportunities for ageing in place.

Consistent with Social Support Theory, different domains of support appear to operate through distinct mechanisms. Emotional and instrumental support may enhance autonomy and self-efficacy, facilitating independent living, whereas financial support may reinforce interdependence within shared households.

Furthermore, the findings resonate with Activity Theory of Ageing, which emphasizes the importance of sustained social and community engagement for well-being in later life. Older adults who remain engaged in community-based mental health promotion and social networks may be better positioned to maintain independent living arrangements, reflecting adaptive and active ageing processes.

Implications for practice, research, and policy

Practice

The findings highlight the importance of integrating assessments of social support and mental health promotion into routine care for older adults. Health and social care providers should systematically consider living arrangements alongside interpersonal relationships, financial support, emotional well-being, and access to community resources. Community-based interventions that strengthen emotional and instrumental support—such as family engagement programs, peer support groups, and outreach services—may help sustain co-residential living where desired. At the same time, improved access to technology and community mental health services may facilitate more independent living arrangements for older adults who prefer autonomy, underscoring the need for flexible and individualized care planning.

Research

Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to better understand causal pathways between social support, mental health promotion, and living arrangements over time. Studies in diverse cultural and socioeconomic contexts are needed to assess the generalizability of these findings across different ageing societies. Further investigation into specific components of social support and mental health promotion may help identify which elements are most influential in shaping living arrangements. Mixed-methods and participatory approaches could also provide deeper insights into older adults’ preferences, lived experiences, and contextual factors influencing residential decisions.

Policy

At the policy level, the findings underscore the importance of ageing policies that extend beyond housing provision to include social and mental health dimensions of later life within community-based systems of care. In rural and resource-limited settings, policymakers should prioritize investments in community infrastructure, social participation programs, and accessible mental health promotion to support both family-based care and independent living options. Policies that strengthen family and community support systems, promote age-friendly environments, and enhance access to information and services may contribute to more adaptive and context-sensitive living arrangements among older adults.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it adopts a comprehensive and hierarchical analytical framework incorporating sociodemographic factors, multidimensional social support, and mental health promotion, offering a nuanced understanding of social and community influences on living arrangements in later life. Second, the relatively large sample size enabled examination of both overall and domain-specific components. Third, multivariable logistic regression allowed adjustment for potential confounders and assessment of independent associations.

This study also has limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The temporal ordering of living arrangements and psychosocial factors cannot be established, and reverse or bidirectional relationships are possible. Second, reliance on self-reported measures may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Third, the study was conducted within a specific cultural and geographic context, potentially limiting generalizability.

Methodological considerations related to model stability should also be acknowledged. The relatively small proportion of participants living alone resulted in an imbalanced outcome distribution and a limited number of events per variable in fully adjusted models. Under such conditions, sparse data bias and estimation instability may occur, contributing to large odds ratios and wide confidence intervals for some predictors. While diagnostic assessments and sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate multicollinearity and model robustness, certain effect estimates should be interpreted cautiously, with greater emphasis placed on the direction and consistency of associations across models rather than their absolute magnitude.

In addition, several continuous variables were dichotomized to enhance interpretability. Although this approach facilitates presentation and policy translation, it may reduce variability, obscure dose–response relationships, and affect precision. Future longitudinal studies with balanced outcome distributions and more flexible modeling strategies are warranted to strengthen causal inference and external validity.

Conclusion

Living arrangements among older adults are shaped not only by sociodemographic characteristics but also by distinct dimensions of social support, mental health promotion, and service accessibility in rural Chiang Rai, Thailand. While stronger interpersonal relationships and financial support were associated with co-residential living, greater access to technology and higher levels of community-based mental health promotion were linked to independent living. These findings suggest that certain community and structural resources may complement or partially substitute for family-based support in later life.

This multidimensional approach extends prior research by demonstrating that social and community resources operate alongside—and in some cases beyond—traditional sociodemographic determinants. The findings provide evidence from a middle-income setting where family-based living remains normative, yet broader community resources are increasingly shaping ageing patterns.

Importantly, these results carry practical implications. Local Thai health systems can leverage established community infrastructures—particularly village health volunteers—to strengthen digital literacy and expand access to community-based mental health initiatives. Integrating digital inclusion efforts within routine primary care and outreach programs may help older adults maintain social connectivity and access services while ageing in place.

Overall, independent living in this context should not be interpreted solely as social isolation, but may instead reflect adaptive engagement with external support systems. Policies that reinforce community-level psychosocial resources alongside family support structures may therefore be critical for promoting autonomy and well-being among older adults in similar low- and middle-income settings. Caution is warranted in generalizing these findings to urban or high-income contexts with different institutional and cultural configurations.
