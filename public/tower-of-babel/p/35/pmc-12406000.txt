Navigating the Muddled Middle: A Case-Based Exploration of Oral β-Lactams for Systemic Gram-Negative Infections
Authors: Jennifer Shif, Hawi Tasissa, Conan MacDougall, Emily L Heil, Jason C Gallagher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral β-lactams are frequently referred to as low bioavailability agents that are inferior in the treatment of systemic gram-negative infections. This notion limits their utility beyond their use. The pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic profiles of oral β-lactams differ among agents, and each agent must be considered individually in the context of the patient. In this review, we describe 3 scenarios where oral β-lactams may play a role in the treatment of systemic gram-negative infections and the decision process to select or avoid these agents. Each case represents a risk-vs-benefit scenario in which the degree of confidence in using an oral β-lactam varies.

The development of novel oral antibiotic agents has not kept pace with that of intravenous (IV) antibiotics. Over time, this disparity has furthered the notion that oral antibiotics are suboptimal choices for the treatment of some infections, including systemic gram-negative infections. Some highly bioavailable agents, such as fluoroquinolones and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX), are accepted as options for these infections. However, their use can be limited by resistance, adverse effects, and patient-specific issues, including comorbidities, allergies, and drug-drug interactions [1–3]. Oral β-lactam antibiotics may be considered to avoid the attendant costs and risks of continued IV therapy [4].

Oral β-lactams are sometimes viewed as a homogenous collection of drugs whose perceived inferior pharmacokinetics make them deemed unworthy for treatment of moderate to severe infections. Assessing oral β-lactams with this narrow perspective limits their utility and puts continued pressure on other agents. In this review, we explore the utility of oral β-lactams for the treatment of systemic gram-negative infections through 3 hypothetical case scenarios common to clinical practice and the decision processes used to select or avoid these agents.

HISTORY

There are conflicting data regarding the use of oral β-lactams in patients with systemic infections caused by gram-negative organisms. While some retrospective studies suggest equivalence between fluoroquinolones or TMP/SMX and oral β-lactams, others had more failure in patients treated with oral β-lactams. These studies are limited by largely urinary sources, heterogeneous β-lactam dosing, a lack of antimicrobial susceptibility results, and different durations of lead-in therapy with IV antibiotics [1–3 , 5–7].

A potential explanation for poorer outcomes seen with oral β-lactams relative to other oral gram-negative agents is suboptimal serum concentrations. These concentrations may be suboptimal by virtue of comparison with similar IV β-lactams or in reference to the excellent bioavailability of oral fluoroquinolones and TMP/SMX [1–3, 5–8]. In addition to potential issues with serum concentrations, the pharmacodynamic profiles of many oral β-lactams for gram-negative infections are not well characterized. Most of these agents have only been evaluated in clinical trials for, and are indicated in, the treatment of community-acquired respiratory tract infections and skin and skin structure infections.

The relationship between antimicrobial exposure and the infecting organism is one of the most significant factors determining the clinical and microbiological outcomes in the patient who is infected. For β-lactams, the unbound (free) drug exposure time above the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of an organism (%fT >MIC) is the pharmacodynamic target associated with positive outcomes. To attain maximal activity for β-lactams, available evidence suggests that %fT >MIC should be at least 30% to 60% [9]. Oral β-lactams are often suboptimally dosed in practice and clinical trials [3, 7, 10, 11]. Higher dosing strategies (ie, increased doses or more frequent administration) than typically used for many oral β-lactams are necessary for sufficient probability of target attainment (PTA) for gram-negative infections, although this may also increase gastrointestinal intolerability and decrease adherence. Targeting even higher pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) targets, such as trough concentrations exceeding 4 times the MIC (100% 4×MIC), might be desirable for the acute phase of treatment of systemic infections, but these targets are not achievable with oral β-lactams (Table 1). Therefore, the role of oral β-lactams is suited for that of “stepdown” therapy, when the bacterial burden has been sufficiently reduced by the initial treatment regimen. In a consensus statement, Heil et al stated that oral β-lactams with high PTA and direct or inferred susceptibility data may be considered alternative treatment options for uncomplicated gram-negative bacteremia. Suggested dosing for select oral antibiotic options can be found in their article, although data for optimal dosing are limited [12].

CASE 1: READY FOR TRANSITION

A 36-year-old woman (5 ft 5 in, 52 kg) presents to the emergency department with dysuria, lower back pain, and a temperature of 101.2 °F. Urine and blood cultures are drawn. She is admitted for acute pyelonephritis and prescribed ceftriaxone (1 g, IV, daily). On day 3, she reports significant improvements in symptoms and is ready for discharge. Urine and blood cultures grew Escherichia coli with identical susceptibilities (Figure 1).

Case 1: urine and blood cultures, Escherichia coli. Abbreviations: MIC, minimum inhibitory concentration; R, resistant; S, susceptible; UTI, urinary tract infection.

Many automated susceptibility platforms used in clinical microbiology laboratories do not include oral β-lactams as part of their portfolio of agents for routine testing. Thus, decisions about whether an oral β-lactam can be considered susceptible are frequently based on the results of testing an IV β-lactam and applying criteria for cross-interpretation. Moreover, the ranges of MICs that are tested on a platform are limited and may hinder decision making [13].

When used for therapy for uncomplicated urinary tract infections (UTIs) due to E coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Proteus mirabilis, cefazolin susceptibility results are recommended as a surrogate to predict results for the oral agents cefaclor, cefdinir, cefpodoxime, cefprozil, cefuroxime, cephalexin, and loracarbef. The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) recommends using a cefazolin MIC ≤16 mcg/mL to predict susceptibility to those oral cephalosporins for uncomplicated cystitis based on predicted high urinary concentrations [14–17]. Notably, cefadroxil is not included, and use of cefazolin as a surrogate may not be appropriate [18]. Outside of uncomplicated cystitis, these recommendations do not apply.

For systemic infections, the cefazolin susceptibility breakpoint is 2 mcg/mL. With the cefazolin MIC of 2 mcg/mL in the example, cefazolin would be considered active for systemic infections, but the CLSI does not provide an interpretation for whether any oral cephalosporin could be considered for a systemic infection. Whether it would be reasonable to do so depends on the PK/PD characteristics of the specific oral β-lactam being considered as well as the site of infection.

The bioavailability of oral β-lactams is heterogeneous. Cephalexin has a bioavailability of 90% to 100%, and when dosed at 1000 mg every 6 hours, it achieves >60% fT >MIC for E coli isolates with MICs up to 4 mcg/mL. However, MIC distributions for cephalexin tested against E coli isolates indicate that nearly all isolates have MICs ≥4 mcg/mL [19]. Cefpodoxime has low bioavailability (29%–53%) and reaches adequate fT >MIC for E coli isolates with MICs ≤0.25 mg/dL only when dosed at 400 mg every 12 hours (Table 1) [8]. Oral cephalosporins do not frequently achieve systemic target exposures against gram-negative isolates, even when reported as susceptible; one must consider the target site of infection as well as the fT >MIC.

Renal elimination of oral cephalosporins is important to consider when treating bacteremia originating from the urinary tract. Although any drug eliminated renally achieves high concentrations in the bladder of patients with renal sufficiency, adequate systemic concentrations and concentrations in the renal parenchyma are necessary to treat infections extending beyond cystitis [20]. Thus, antibiotics with high rates of urinary excretion that achieve adequate %fT >MIC in plasma should be chosen for definitive treatment of pyelonephritis with associated bloodstream infection. Cephalexin is primarily excreted through the urine (>90%) and is distributed widely into most body tissues and fluids, including the kidneys [21]. For cystitis, twice-daily dosing is likely sufficient for susceptible organisms, but for systemic gram-negative infections, higher doses are required [22].

Cefadroxil is pharmacokinetically favorable to cephalexin and is an oral therapy option with less frequent dosing [23]. Cefadroxil exhibits a mildly extended serum half-life (1.4–2.6 hours) and is excreted renally [21]. In a study comparing cephalexin and cefadroxil, the latter had higher and more sustained serum concentrations. Cefadroxil concentrations in the urine are also more sustained [22]. Robust PK/PD analyses and breakpoints or surrogates are lacking, but available evidence favors the utility of cefadroxil [18, 20–22].

As an oral third-generation cephalosporin, clinicians sometimes treat cefpodoxime as “oral ceftriaxone.” However, cefpodoxime has lower bioavailability (50%) and limited renal elimination (30%) in comparison with cephalexin and cefadroxil, and it is unlikely to reach adequate target attainment in most patients for the treatment of systemic gram-negative infections, including complicated UTIs [8, 12, 20, 24]. Urine concentrations are low in comparison with other oral β-lactams (Table 2) [20]. Additionally, it must be administered with food to optimize absorption [8]. Cefdinir is also a poor choice for the treatment of pyelonephritis, as renal elimination is only 12% to 18% and urine concentrations are limited. Cefuroxime has low bioavailability and limited urinary recovery as well [20]. Although second- and third-generation oral cephalosporins may be active in vitro, they are pharmacokinetically limited.

Back to Patient Case

Based on available PK/PD and outcomes data, a fluoroquinolone or TMP/SMX would be most appropriate for oral stepdown therapy, although both are associated with the potential for adverse drug events. These agents show similar outcomes to IV therapy in clinical data, provide similar systemic exposure to their IV forms, and have easily interpretable antimicrobial susceptibility results. Due to resistance, neither of those agents is an option in this case, and oral β-lactams are the only option for an oral transition. While some clinical data support this switch, it is complicated by a lack of direct antimicrobial susceptibility testing, suboptimal exposures, and some evidence of increased recurrence in systemic infections. A shared decision-making approach weighing the risk of increased recurrence with oral β-lactams vs the risks of IV treatment is indicated. An ideal situation may require outpatient parenteral antibiotic therapy, but not all patients are able or willing to complete it. If oral β-lactam therapy is selected, we recommend cephalexin (1000 mg, every 6 hours). Cefadroxil (1000 mg, every 12 hours) is another option. There are less robust PK/PD data supporting its use, but twice-daily dosing improves patient adherence. For the aforementioned reasons, other oral cephalosporins, including cefpodoxime and cefuroxime, are not recommended. Due to its high serum and urinary concentrations, amoxicillin/clavulanate is another potential option (Table 2). Further discussion regarding its utility in systemic gram-negative infections is presented in case 2.

CASE 2: THE MUDDY MIDDLE

A 67-year-old man (5 ft 11 in, 72 kg) presents to the emergency department with complaints of nausea, vomiting, and diffuse abdominal pain. He has a medical history notable for atrial fibrillation (receiving dofetilide), stage 2 chronic kidney disease, and anaphylaxis to a sulfonamide. On physical examination, diffuse abdominal tenderness and rebound tenderness are detected. Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrates a perforated appendix, complicated by the development of multiple fluid collections in the right lower quadrant. He is empirically prescribed piperacillin/tazobactam, and a percutaneous drain is placed in the largest abscess. Cultures of the abscess grow K pneumoniae (Figure 2).

Case 2: abscess, Klebsiella pneumoniae, rare growth. Abbreviations: I, intermediate; MIC, minimum inhibitory concentration; R, resistant; S, susceptible; UTI, urinary tract infection.

Repeat computed tomography is done after placement of the drain, which demonstrates improvement of the primary intra-abdominal fluid collection in the right lower quadrant. Imaging is notable for persistence of undrainable smaller collections. After 5 days of IV antibiotics, he is hemodynamically stable and afebrile, and his leukocytosis has resolved. He is ready for discharge but has difficult IV access. The patient and team both want to avoid home IV access.

The most common mechanism of resistance of Enterobacterales to β-lactam antibiotics is β-lactamase production [25]. Coadministration of a β-lactam with a β-lactamase inhibitor, such as clavulanic acid or sulbactam, can restore activity for many of these isolates. β-Lactamase inhibitors differ measurably in their affinity for different β-lactamase enzymes and in their inhibitory activities. Clavulanate is a more potent inhibitor of broad-spectrum β-lactamases than sulbactam by 14 to 20 times, including inhibition of plasmid-mediated enzymes that are commonly found in E coli, as well as the chromosomally mediated β-lactamases of K pneumoniae and P vulgaris [26]. Thus, amoxicillin/clavulanate is generally more active than ampicillin/sulbactam against ampicillin-resistant strains of E coli, K pneumoniae, and Proteus vulgaris and can be considered stepdown therapy for the treatment of infections caused by ampicillin/sulbactam-resistant, amoxicillin/clavulanate-susceptible strains once appropriate source control is achieved. However, not all laboratories test both of these agents, and amoxicillin/clavulanate susceptibility cannot be assumed in isolates reported as ampicillin/sulbactam resistant.

As with other β-lactams, the efficacy of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid depends on the PTA, which is a function of patient characteristics and the targeted MIC. Clinical data supporting the establishment of clinical breakpoints are usually lacking for older drugs, and in vitro data are frequently the only data available to determine MICs. The CLSI considers Enterobacterales with MICs ≤8/4 mg/L susceptible to amoxicillin/clavulanate [17]. By contrast, the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) considers Enterobacterales with MICs ≤8/2 mg/L susceptible and those with MICs >8/2 mg/L resistant for invasive infections. This leads to significant discrepancies and a higher rate of resistant isolates when EUCAST methodology is used due to differing concentrations of clavulanic acid. Delgado-Valverde et al found that EUCAST-derived MICs were more predictive of outcomes than CLSI-derived MICs. Clinical failures among discrepant isolates occurred with borderline MICs (8/4–16/8 mg/L) according to CLSI criteria [27]. This narrow distinction becomes increasingly important when one considers the lack of precision of MIC measurements. Reported clinical MIC values are less precise than they appear and are affected by several components, such as differences within a strain, among laboratories, and in testing methods [28]. MICs within a strain often vary by 1 to 2 dilutions from the value reported to clinicians. For example, in an E coli strain with a reported MIC of 8/4 mg/L, there may exist organisms with MICs of 4/2 and 16/8 mg/L. Given the increased risk of clinical failure of using amoxicillin/clavulanate in isolates with borderline susceptibility, it is best to use amoxicillin/clavulanate as stepdown therapy when the MIC <8/2 mg/dL. In isolates with borderline MICs, one must consider whether the patient has received sufficient source control and prior therapy with agents having likely pharmacodynamic target attainment (eg, IV β-lactams) when amoxicillin/clavulanate is used as stepdown therapy.

Amoxicillin is rapidly absorbed after oral administration with a bioavailability of 77% to 93% [29]. Although the saturable absorption of amoxicillin results in disproportionately smaller increases in peak concentrations with increased doses, pharmacokinetic studies show that more frequent doses increase the PTA [29, 30]. In a study by de Velde et al, investigators found that despite nonlinear absorption, increased doses did result in a larger %fT >MIC. However, the benefit of increasing the daily dose has limitations. For most patients, utilizing higher doses, such as 1000 mg given every 8 hours, can achieve only 90% PTA for an organism with an MIC ≤2 mcg/mL [30]. The PK/PD driver of clavulanate appears to be %fT above a threshold concentration, but this has not been evaluated in combination with amoxicillin [31]. High doses also increase the risk of adverse events and may more significantly disturb intestinal microflora. The frequency of diarrhea increases at higher daily doses of amoxicillin, likely due to higher amounts of unabsorbed antibiotic [29, 30, 32, 33]. Oral clavulanic acid increases the risk of gastrointestinal side effects, particularly diarrhea, as compared with amoxicillin alone; as such, the maximum recommended daily dose of clavulanic acid is 500 mg [29].

Data that compare the efficacy and safety of different ratios of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid are scarce, but for respiratory tract infections, available data suggest that higher doses of amoxicillin do not improve outcomes but increase adverse effects [32, 33]. The amount of β-lactamase inhibitor required for treatment of respiratory tract pathogens, such as Haemophilus and Moraxella spp, may differ from that required for other gram negatives. Additional studies are warranted to evaluate the impact of strategies based on higher clavulanic acid ratios (eg, 1000/250 vs 875/125 mg) in the setting of infections caused by Enterobacterales [34].

Back to Patient Case

The fluoroquinolone MICs indicate resistance or intermediate susceptibility for the K pneumoniae grown, making these agents suboptimal for the treatment of this intra-abdominal infection. The patient's age, renal function, and contraindicated drug interaction with dofetilide further increase the risk of fluoroquinolone-associated side effects. Finally, the severe sulfonamide allergy eliminates TMP/SMX as a potential stepdown option. Amoxicillin/clavulanate may not be pharmacodynamically optimal for this patient given the breakpoint MIC of 8/4 mcg/mL but may have a favorable risk-benefit tradeoff and be the best choice for this patient. It also possesses activity against intra-abdominal anaerobes that are likely involved in the infection.

Without an ideal agent, relying on amoxicillin/clavulanate alone does increase the risk of treatment failure in this setting, and a weighing of the “optimal” vs “convenient” course is necessary. An optimal regimen requires completion of the treatment course intravenously, but the patient does not want that. In these scenarios, it may be best to intensify the dosing regimen and use 875/125 mg administered every 8 hours to increase the likelihood of target attainment, even though robust data regarding optimal dosing for infections caused by Enterobacterales are lacking. Treatment with high-dose amoxicillin/clavulanate may be the best of unpalatable options. Close patient follow-up and monitoring for signs of treatment failure are essential.

CASE 3: HECK NO, CEFPODOXIME MUST GO

A 39-year-old man (5 ft 2 in, 60 kg) is admitted for sickle cell crisis. He has a medical history significant for alcohol use disorder (sober for 5 years) complicated by an episode of seizures from withdrawal. Four days into his course of pain medications, he develops systemic signs of infection with chills, leukocytosis, and a fever (101.4 °F). Blood cultures become positive for Klebsiella aerogenes (née Enterobacter aerogenes). His line is removed and replaced 48 hours after negative blood cultures, and the patient is treated with ceftriaxone (2 g daily for 3 days). He is now ready for discharge, and the primary team plans on transitioning him to cefpodoxime based on the ceftriaxone susceptibility demonstrated in his culture result (Figure 3). The team wishes to avoid fluoroquinolones given his history of seizures.

Case 3: blood culture, Klebsiella aerogenes. Abbreviations: MIC, minimum inhibitory concentration; R, resistant; S, susceptible; UTI, urinary tract infection.

K aerogenes is one of the Enterobacterales spp containing chromosomal AmpC genes most likely to overexpress these enzymes. Production of AmpC enzymes leads to resistance to many β-lactams, including second- and third-generation cephalosporins [35]. Potent inducers of the AmpC production pathway lead to resistance being detected in vitro on standard tests; these include aminopenicillins, first- and second-generation cephalosporins, and clavulanate. Additionally, while third-generation cephalosporins, aztreonam, and piperacillin/tazobactam are weak substrates of AmpC and frequently test susceptible when expression is low, increased production of AmpC can occur after exposure to these agents and can lead to clinical resistance and thus therapeutic failure [35, 36].

Although emerging data have suggested that ceftriaxone may be a more accurate predictor of cefpodoxime susceptibility than cefazolin, these data are in vitro and define susceptibility to cefpodoxime based on concentrations that may not be achieved in vivo when MICs approach the breakpoint, particularly for systemic infections [37, 38]. As noted in case 1, extrapolation of cefazolin results to oral cephalosporins (including cefpodoxime) is valid only for uncomplicated cystitis caused by E coli, K pneumoniae, and P mirabilis. Additionally, cefpodoxime has poor bioavailability and leads to low serum concentrations (Table 2) [8, 20]. A surveillance study by Knapp et al found an MIC50 of 4 mg/L for cefpodoxime and 2 mg/L for ceftriaxone among 32 isolates of K aerogenes, which is above the breakpoints of both drugs [39]. While a correlation exists between cefpodoxime and ceftriaxone MICs, we require more data, including clinical studies, to use ceftriaxone susceptibility as a surrogate for cefpodoxime for any gram-negative organism [40]. Even if cefpodoxime susceptibility were documented in this case based on a cefpodoxime-specific test, the drug would be a poor choice given the potential for emergent resistance and the low PTA.

Back to the Patient Case

Although ceftriaxone is not an ideal therapy for this isolate, the patient has improved on it. Cefepime would have been a better initial therapy. The likely source of infection was removed, and there is no evidence of metastatic disease. An oral cephalosporin would not be appropriate in this case for the reasons previously discussed. The concern about the seizure history and fluoroquinolone therapy is noted, but the risk-benefit ratio of selecting a fluoroquinolone for a patient with a remote seizure history is favorable.

CONCLUSION: SHOULD WE USE AN ORAL β-LACTAM? IT DEPENDS

Use of oral β-lactams for systemic gram-negative infections is challenging, but they are favorable options for select patients. The disparity in exposures achieved with IV and oral regimens of β-lactams complicates the decision process. The scenario is clouded by situations where IV antibiotics “do the hard work” before an oral antibiotic is used—and most data from clinical trials and cohort studies represent this scenario. Better understanding of oral β-lactam PK/PD, the pairing of more potent β-lactamase inhibitors with oral β-lactams, and ongoing trials of gram-negative bloodstream infections being treated with oral agents, including optimally dosed oral β-lactams, may help reinvigorate the use of oral β-lactams for systemic gram-negative infections (ie, NCT06080698). In the meantime, we hope that the considerations illustrated in the cases presented here can help clinicians reason through the many considerations for use of these agents.
