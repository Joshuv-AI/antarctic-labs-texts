Thylakostasis: key factors in thylakoid membrane organization with emphasis on biogenesis and remodeling proteins in vascular plants
Authors: Wataru Sakamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe thylakoid membrane (TM), a defining feature for almost all oxygen-evolving photosynthetic organisms, serves as the structural foundation for light-driven energy conversion. In vascular plants, the TM evolved into a complex architecture composed of single-layered stroma thylakoids and stacked grana thylakoids, enabling the spatial organization of two photosystems (PSII and PSI) to optimize light capture and energy transfer. In addition, two membrane regions, one connecting these two compartments (grana margin) and the other corresponding to the curvature domain in grana, function in dissipating excess energy, balancing electron transfer, and maintaining functional PSII. Recent advances in electron microscopy imaging and proteome analysis of membrane subcompartments have provided new insights into the structure and dynamic adaptations of the TM in response to diverse environmental conditions. To describe the mechanisms that govern TM architecture, dynamics, and integrity, I am introducing the concept of “thylakostasis” (thylakoid homeostasis). Here, I provide an overview of the molecular components and processes central to thylakostasis, including the biosynthesis of lipids, chlorophyll, and proteins. I focus particularly on the membrane remodeling proteins whose functions have been elucidated recently, such as VIPP1, a member of the evolutionarily conserved PspA/ESCRT-III superfamily; FZL, a dynamin-like GTPase; and CURT1, a curvature-inducing protein unique to photosynthetic organisms. Together, these factors orchestrate TM biogenesis, remodeling, and adaptive flexibility that is essential for photosynthetic efficiency.

Introduction

Thylakoid membranes (TMs) serve as the structural foundation for the light reactions of photosynthesis, the most important biological process that converts light energy into chemical energy and sustains our global ecosystem. Nearly all organisms that perform oxygen-evolving photosynthesis have this characteristic membrane system. The presence of chloroplasts and the TM system has been recognized since the seventeenth century, following the development of the microscope by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek. Early observations of plant chloroplasts and their granular structures (grana) captured researchers’ attention, as described in the review by Staehelin and Paolillo (2020). While the components of the TM—including protein complexes, pigments, lipids, and cofactors—have been elucidated at the atomic level, recent advances in electron microscopy have provided unprecedented insights into their spatial organization (McCafferty et al. 2024).

In vascular plant chloroplasts, two-dimensional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images reveal TM as comprising grana thylakoids (stacked, disc-like membrane structures) and stroma thylakoids (unstacked membranes that interconnect the grana) (Fig. 1a). However, it was not until the twenty-first century that electron tomography allowed for a detailed three-dimensional reconstruction of these membranes, demonstrating their intricate connectivity (Adam et al. 2011, Austin and Staehelin 2011, Daum and Kuhlbrandt 2011, Shimoni et al. 2005, Wietrzynski et al. 2024). These findings challenge conventional textbook depictions of chloroplast structure, which often fail to accurately represent TM organization (Mustardy and Garab 2003, Mazur et al. 2021). Furthermore, thylakoid morphology exhibits significant variability across species, developmental stages, and environmental conditions, underscoring the complexity of both their architecture and dynamic spatial arrangement (e.g. Charuvi et al. 2012, Kirchhoff 2019). For example, in the chloroplasts of conifer species, well-developed grana stacks are commonly observed. However, unlike annual herbaceous plants, conifers exhibit the seasonal optimization of light energy conversion, accompanied by dynamic changes in TM architecture (Demmig-Adams et al. 2015). During the transition from autumn to winter, a marked reduction in grana stacking is observed, which coincides with enhanced energy dissipation mechanisms. The term “thylakoid membrane” itself encompasses diverse structural forms, with significant differences observed between cyanobacteria, algae, and vascular plants (Dekker and Boekema 2005, Rantala et al. 2020, Ostermeier et al. 2024, Perez-Boerema et al. 2024). Unlike the highly ordered grana stacks found in vascular plant chloroplasts, prokaryotic TM generally lacks such layered organization. Notably, even within cyanobacteria, thylakoid architecture varies among species (Mares et al. 2019).

The increasing complexity of TM architecture in terrestrial photosynthetic organisms, such as grana stacking, suggests an evolutionary adaptation to fluctuating light environments (e.g. Chow et al. 2005, Anderson et al. 2012, Terashima et al. 2025). A representative example of the co-evolution of grana stacking with terrestrial adaptation can be found in basal land plants such as mosses. In the model moss Physcomitrium patens, the TM architecture exhibits an intermediate degree of stacking compared to that observed in green algae and vascular plants (Pinnola et al. 2015, Gerotto et al. 2019). These observations raise a fundamental question: How do TMs establish, differentiate, and maintain their intricate structures? In this review, I define the entire process of TM formation and maintenance as “thylakostasis” (thylakoid homeostasis) and provide an overview of its key components (Fig. 1b).

As a foundation for understanding thylakostasis, it is first necessary to dissect current knowledge on TM architecture. I begin by introducing recent structural models, along with proteomic data that reveal the lateral distribution of protein complexes across distinct TM subcompartments. Next, for a comprehensive understanding of thylakostasis, I describe the biosynthesis and maintenance of the core building blocks of the TM—lipids, chlorophyll, and proteins. Proteins are assembled into functional complexes involved in electron transport and ATP synthesis, and their biogenesis is intimately linked to the process of TM formation. Since all major thylakoid lipids originate from the chloroplast envelope, understanding the mechanisms governing TM formation and maintenance is also essential. Recent findings related to these processes are discussed. Finally, I highlight how mature TMs exhibit dynamic behavior through protein post-translational modifications and membrane remodeling activities, allowing the system to respond resiliently to environmental fluctuations such as changes in light. This review particularly focuses on emerging insights into TM remodeling activity, exploring the structural and functional characteristics of key remodeling factors.

TM Organization and Subcompartments

The helical model

Understanding the three-dimensional architecture of TM observed through conventional electron microscopy presents both fundamental and technical challenges (e.g. Adam et al. 2011, Staehelin and Paolillo 2020). When visualizing cellular structures with an optical microscope, their approximate shape can be inferred by adjusting the focal plane. Confocal laser microscopy further enables the acquisition of serial Z-axis images, providing 3D structural information; however, its resolution is relatively low (~1 μm). In contrast, electron tomography (ET) allows for high-resolution 3D segmental analysis of membrane structures (comprehensively reviewed by McCafferty et al. 2024). Since electron beam penetration requires thin sections (150–300 nm thick), entire TM networks—such as grana stacks (~4 μm)—are typically reconstructed from serial sections. High-pressure freeze-fixation and resin embedding have been employed for tomographic analyses of TM in various plant species, including lettuce, Arabidopsis, and spinach (Shimoni et al. 2005, Mustardy et al. 2008, Daum et al. 2010, Austin and Staehelin 2011, Bussi et al. 2019). Based on these methodologies, a structure-based helical model has been proposed.

Using 20 tomograms reconstructed from ~ 300 serial sections, in the study by Austin and Staehelin (2011), they generated a 3D segment measuring 4 μm × 4 μm × 1.5 μm (Fig. 2). This model revealed three cylindrical grana thylakoids, each exhibiting distinct stacking patterns. The grana discs measured ~1 μm in diameter. Serial tomographic slices of the interconnecting stroma thylakoids demonstrated that these membranes adopt a right-handed helical arrangement (oriented top-right to bottom-front) at an angle of ~ 20° relative to the cylindrical grana. This helical network wraps around the grana stacks and forms junction sites (slits) that connect individual discs within the stacks. While the width of these slits typically measures ~ 150 nm, some have been reported to exceed 500 nm (Daum et al. 2010). Additional evidence supporting a helical model was provided by Bussi et al. (2019), who demonstrated that stroma thylakoids can also adopt a left-handed helical orientation, indicating a more complex spatial arrangement than previously thought. A recent study with cryo-ET also showed that spiral organization of stroma thylakoids exists in purified spinach chloroplasts (Wietrzynski et al. 2024). Collectively, these findings suggest that grana discs and stromal thylakoids form a continuous membrane network, sharing a common lumen through these slit-like junctions (Daum et al. 2010, Austin and Staehelin 2011). This region, commonly referred to as the “grana margin,” is thought to play a crucial role in photosynthetic electron transport and adaptation to fluctuating light conditions. However, as discussed in later sections, its functional implications warrant careful consideration.

Among early models describing TM connectivity, the helical model aligns with the proposal by Paolillo (1970). Alternative models, including the fenestrated membrane model, also suggest the presence of slit-like structures, while Y-shaped bifurcated and branched models have been proposed based on different interpretations of membrane organization (Brumfeld et al. 2008, Adam et al. 2011, Bussi et al. 2019). Further high-resolution studies will be necessary to clarify the existence and prevalence of these alternative structures. Importantly, the widely used schematic representation of a single-layered stromal thylakoid extending from one grana stack to another does not accurately depict the complex TM architecture in vascular plant chloroplasts (Mustardy and Garab 2003, Mazur et al. 2021). Instead, each grana stack is integrated into a highly interconnected and dynamic membrane network.

TM subcompartments

As aforementioned, the TM in vascular plants can be structurally divided into four distinct regions of grana thylakoids, stroma thylakoids, grana margins, and curvature regions (Rantala et al. 2020) (Fig. 3). Each of these subcompartments can be biochemically isolated and are well characterized in Arabidopsis (e.g. Puthiyaveetil et al. 2014, Wang et al. 2016, Koochak et al. 2019, Trotta et al. 2019, Nishioka et al. 2021, Chotewutmontri and Barkan 2024). Trotta et al. (2025) employed sequential ultracentrifugation of digitonin-solubilized TM to fractionate these regions, followed by mass spectrometry to determine the localization of > 800 proteins within each membrane domain. Characteristics of each subcompartment in terms of membrane appression and protein enrichment, together with potential challenges in biochemical analyses, are discussed here.

Grana thylakoids

Grana stacks are a defining feature of vascular plant chloroplasts, whereas algae exhibit appressed membrane regions, although they are generally less elaborate (Kirchhoff et al. 2011, Engel et al. 2015). In C4 plants, grana stacks are usually observed in mesophyll cells but are largely absent from bundle sheath cells (Fig. 1a). Grana discs are relatively uniform in diameter (~1 μm), although their stacking number varies depending on environmental and physiological conditions (Pribil et al. 2014, Kirchhoff 2019, Rantala et al. 2020). The vertical spacing between the TM, lumen, and stromal regions remains relatively constant, with no protruding protein complexes due to the tight intermembrane spacing (Wietrzynski et al. 2020).

The formation of 3–4 nm stacked TMs is driven by a balance of van der Waals forces, electrostatic repulsion, and affinity interactions between membranes (Puthiyaveetil et al. 2017), although this principle should be carefully interpreted (Moazzami Gudarzi et al. 2021). Within these stacks, the light-harvesting complex II (LHCII)-PSII supercomplex is localized densely, facilitated by its flat, stroma-exposed surface (Wood et al. 2018, Albanese et al. 2020, Wietrzynski et al. 2020). LHCII-PSII is highly abundant in proteome fractions corresponding to grana thylakoids (Koochak et al. 2019, Chotewutmontri and Barkan 2024, Trotta et al. 2025). In grana stacks, the C₂S₂M₂ supercomplex—comprising a PSII core dimer (C₂) associated with two S2 and two M2 LHCII trimers—exists in various states, contributing to the appressed membrane organization (Dekker and Boekema 2005, Pribil et al. 2014, Albanese et al. 2020). LHCII aggregation plays a key role in generating surface charges, which facilitate membrane stacking. LHCII is encoded by multiple gene families, with the Lhcb1, Lhcb2, and Lhcb3 isoforms forming the primary heterotrimer in land plants (van Bezouwen et al. 2017, Cao et al. 2018, Rantala et al. 2020). The interactions between LHCII molecules are influenced by positively and negatively charged amino acid residues on the N-terminal region of the stroma-exposed Lhcb proteins (Albanese et al. 2020). This region undergoes phosphorylation and acetylation in response to the light environment, dynamically altering TM morphology (Pesaresi et al. 2011, Koskela et al. 2018). These reversible modifications regulate the association state of the supercomplex, allowing for structural flexibility that optimizes photosynthetic efficiency.

Functionally, grana stacks are thought to optimize photosynthetic electron flow in terrestrial environments by spatially segregating PSI and PSII, thus minimizing spillover effects (Chow et al. 2005, Terashima et al. 2025). Responding to light conditions, grana thylakoids underdo spaciotemporal alteration involving redistribution and/or aggregation status of LHCII antennae, which is orchestrated by protein post-translational modification, at least in part (see below section “Protein complexes”). Under high light conditions, lumen expansion and LHCII association with PSII undergo dynamic rearrangements, contributing to energy dissipation (Johnson et al. 2011, Kirchhoff et al. 2011, Wood et al. 2018). Under extreme light stress, LHCII detaches from PSII, exposing the PSII core complex for photodamage repair (Pesaresi et al. 2011). Although the grana core region is relatively homogeneous and dominated by PSII-LHCII supercomplexes and the cytochrome b6f complex (Cyt b6/f), the grana periphery, particularly the stromal-facing non-appressed regions, shares molecular components with stroma thylakoids. Notably, this region has been found to harbor components involved in chloroplast gene expression, including the plastid-encoded RNA polymerase complex, highlighting its role beyond photosynthesis (Tomizioli et al. 2014, Trotta et al. 2025).

Stroma thylakoids

The stroma thylakoids, which constitute the non-appressed TM domains, are enriched in PSI and the ATP synthase (ATPase). Unlike PSII, these complexes contain bulky protrusions on the stromal side and are unlikely to be accommodated in the grana core. In contrast, the Cyt b6/f and the NADH dehydrogenase-like (NDH-1) complex are detected in the stroma thylakoid (Trotta et al. 2025). It also contains components in RNA maturation (RNA editing and processing) and translation (ribosomes), pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, and membrane-remodeling proteins such as vesicle inducing protein in plastid 1 (VIPP1), which forms large complexes that appear to associate with both stromal thylakoids and the inner chloroplast envelope (Zhang et al. 2012). When TMs are solubilized under conditions that disrupt Mg2+-mediated interactions, PSI and PSII become more uniformly distributed (Anderson and Vernon 1967, Trotta et al. 2025), indicating that their lateral segregation within the membrane is structurally regulated, although membrane fluidity allows them to move.

Grana margins (slit/connecting domain)

In this review, I refer to the slit-like junctions between grana and stroma thylakoids as grana margins. However, caution is needed when interpreting biochemical fractionation data, as grana margins are often co-isolated with membrane curvature regions due to their similar sedimentation properties (Tomizioli et al. 2014, Rantala et al. 2020, Trotta et al. 2025). Some studies instead refer to this region as the connecting domain (Trotta et al. 2025). The grana margin is functionally significant in dynamic photosynthetic adaptations. This region is enriched in proteins involved in PSII-LHCII conformational changes in response to light conditions, including the PSI-LHCII supercomplex and the state transition 7 (STN7) kinase, which mediates state transitions, as well as filamentous temperature-sensitive H (FtsH) and related proteases involved in PSII repair (Puthiyaveetil et al. 2014, Nishioka et al. 2021, Chotewutmontri and Barkan 2024, Trotta et al. 2025). Translational machineries are also found in this region, although predominant in stroma thylakoids. Stromal processing peptidase A (SppA) involved in the cleavage of transit peptides is also enriched in this region. In addition, the NDH-1 complex, which facilitates cyclic electron flow, is localized here (Trotta et al. 2025). These findings underscore the grana margin’s importance in regulating membrane dynamics and photosynthetic efficiency.

Curvature regions (CURT1-rich region)

The curvature regions, which typically form at the periphery of grana stacks, have been isolated as the lowest sedimentation density membrane fraction enriched in curvature thylakoid 1 (CURT1) proteins. CURT1 plays a crucial role in inducing and stabilizing membrane curvature, allowing for the dynamic remodeling of TMs (see below). However, biochemical fractionation of this region requires careful interpretation, as detergent-solubilized samples may contain micelle-associated protein complexes rather than native curvature-enriched membranes (Trotta et al. 2025). Fuzzy onion-like (FZL), another membrane-remodeling protein, has been reported in the curvature fraction (Patil et al. 2018, Ogawa et al. 2023). While FZL is implicated in TM organization, it is also associated with the inner envelope membrane and has been linked to chloroplast division (Gao et al. 2006), suggesting a broader functional role beyond membrane curvature maintenance. Given these properties, it remains unclear whether other proteins than these membrane remodeling proteins exist in this region.

Plastoglobules

Although not included in the models depicted in Figs 2 and 3, plastoglobules (PGs) are introduced here as a subcompartment of TMs, as they are lipid–protein microcompartments that are physically connected to the TM. Structurally, PGs are monolayer-bound spherical droplets that extend from the outer monolayer of thylakoids, often at grana margins or curvature regions (Austin et al. 2006). They are composed of neutral lipids such as triacylglycerols, prenylquinones (plastoquinone, tocopherols), carotenoids, and fatty acid phytyl esters, surrounded by a lipid monolayer enriched in galactolipids (van Wijk and Kessler 2017). PGs are densely packed with specific proteins, including members of the fibrillin family (FBN1a, FBN1b), PG-associated ABC1 kinases, tocopherol cyclase, and NAD(P)H dehydrogenases (Lundquist et al. 2012). Functionally, PGs serve as dynamic hubs for lipid metabolism and redox regulation, playing roles in the storage, synthesis, and remodeling of lipid-derived molecules, especially under stress conditions such as high light, senescence, and oxidative damage.

Building Blocks of TMs: Lipids, Chlorophylls, and Proteins

Lipids

The TM is predominantly composed of glycolipids, including monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG), digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG), and sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol (SQDG). In cyanobacteria, glucosyldiacylglycerol (GlcDG) is also present (Petroutsos et al. 2014). In vascular plant chloroplasts, MGDG, DGDG, and SQDG make up ~50%, 30%, and 10% of the total lipids, respectively, while phospholipids, primarily phosphatidylglycerol, account for the remaining ~ 10% (Dorne et al. 1990, Sakurai et al. 2006). In addition, trace amounts of phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidic acid, and phosphatidylethanolamine have been identified. The spatial and temporal distribution of these lipids plays a critical role in organizing membrane compartments and maintaining thylakostasis. MGDG, the most abundant thylakoid lipid, is essential for TM function whereas it has a small neutral head and forms a cone-shaped molecular structure (hexagonal II) that prevents it from forming stable bilayers and instead promotes non-lamellar (Adam et al. 2011, Ostermeier et al. 2024). However, when MGDG interacts with LHCII, it facilitates the formation of a lamellar bilayer (Simidjiev et al. 2000). In addition, although negatively charged phospholipids, such as phosphatidylinositol phosphates, are present in only trace amounts, they may contribute to membrane remodeling through interactions with regulatory proteins (Schroda 2020). Whether specific lipid-driven membrane domains exist in TMs remains poorly understood.

Thylakostasis can be influenced not only by lipid biosynthesis, which varies in response to light and other environmental conditions, but also indirectly by lipid metabolism and degradation (Kobayashi 2016, Higashi et al. 2018). While these topics are not discussed in detail here, readers are referred to other review articles for further information (e.g. Ostermeier et al. 2024). In addition, since glycolipid biosynthesis occurs in the chloroplast envelope, these lipids must be transported to the TM. A recent study suggested that the proteins involved in membrane lipid transport and metabolism enriched at the contact site between TM and inner envelope membranes may play roles in TM organization (LaBrant et al. 2025). Studies on mutants lacking specific glycolipids have demonstrated their essential role in photosynthesis and membrane integrity (e.g. Kobayashi et al. 2013). In cyanobacteria, MGDG synthesis begins with the enzyme MdgA, which catalyzes the addition of glucose from UDP-glucose to diacylglycerol (DAG), forming GlcDG, which is then isomerized by MdgE to produce MGDG (Awai et al. 2006, Awai et al. 2014). In contrast, in chloroplasts, MGDG is synthesized in a single-step reaction catalyzed by MGD1, which transfers a galactose moiety from UDP-galactose to DAG (Shimojima et al. 1997). The second galactose addition to form DGDG is performed by DgdA in cyanobacteria and DGD1 in chloroplasts, though these genes originate from different evolutionary backgrounds (Awai et al. 2007, Kelly et al. 2016). SQDG biosynthesis also varies between the two systems. These observations suggest that while glycolipids are universally essential for photosynthetic membranes, their origins diverged during endosymbiosis (Sato and Awai 2016). In any case, both glycolipids and phospholipids are critical for maintaining TM architecture and ensuring proper photosynthetic function.

Chlorophylls

Chlorophyll biosynthesis is tightly coordinated with TM development and is a critical factor of thylakostasis during chloroplast development (Tanaka and Tanaka 2006). Etioplasts in dark-grown seedling offer an excellent example of this process in vascular plants (Liang et al. 2018, 2022); etioplasts contain prolamellar bodies (PLBs), a paracrystalline lattice of membrane tubules enriched in protochlorophyllide and its reductase (LPOR). Upon illumination, LPOR catalyzes the photoreduction of protochlorophyllide to chlorophyllide, triggering the rapid conversion of PLBs into lamellar TMs. Recent structural analysis using cryo-ET visualized the prothylakoids enriched with ATPase and ribosomes (Floris and Kuhlbrandt 2021), and the PLB with LPOR developing into thylakoid sheets and grana stacks (Liang et al. 2022). Involvement of TM remodeling proteins during this transition period has also been implicated (Sandoval-Ibanez et al. 2021). This light-dependent chlorophyll biosynthesis is coupled with the assembly of photosynthetic protein complexes and the expansion of membrane surface area, ensuring the synchronized formation of functional thylakoids. Disruptions in chlorophyll biosynthesis not only impair light harvesting but also stall TM development, highlighting its integral role in maintaining membrane biogenesis and photosynthetic competence.

Due to the potential cytotoxicity of chlorophyll molecules, its regulated degradation is equally important for maintaining thylakostasis, particularly during leaf senescence, high light stress, or developmental transitions. Inappropriate degradation or retention of chlorophyll during thylakoid disassembly can impair membrane remodeling, disturb the balance of photosynthetic complexes, and trigger oxidative damage (Kuai et al. 2018). Controlled chlorophyll breakdown thus contributes not only to nutrient remobilization but also to the safe dismantling and turnover of TMs, a key process in thylakostasis under both physiological and stress conditions.

Proteins

Thylakostasis is ensured by the continuous and coordinated supply of nuclear- and plastid-encoded proteins that constitute photosynthetic complexes and membrane-shaping factors. Most thylakoid-localized proteins are encoded in the nucleus, synthesized in the cytosol, and imported post-translationally into chloroplasts via the Translocons at the outer and inner envelope membrane of chloroplasts (TOC/TIC) (Gao et al. 2023). Once inside, stromal targeting is followed by guided translocation into or across the TM mediated by the Sec, Tat, or SRP pathways. Meanwhile, proteins encoded by chloroplast genomes are synthesized by membrane-bound ribosomes enriched in stroma thylakoids (Fig. 3). The fidelity and timing of protein import are critical for assembling functional photosystems and maintaining the stoichiometry of membrane protein complexes. Equally important is proteostasis: damaged or misassembled proteins are selectively degraded by chloroplast-localized proteases such as FtsH, caseinolytic protease (Clp), and degradation of periplasmic proteins (Deg) protease families, which prevent the accumulation of non-functional proteins and allow remodeling in response to environmental changes (Nishimura et al. 2016). Particularly, the involvement of FtsH in TM biogenesis has been extensively elucidated by Arabidopsis mutants lacking major isoforms (FtsH2 and FtsH5) of FtsH heterocomplexes, because these mutants exhibit leaf-variegated phenotypes (Wang et al. 2025). Numerous studies focusing on suppressor screening revealed that leaf variegation can be suppressed by impaired protein synthesis in chloroplasts (e.g. Miura et al. 2007), underscoring the importance of proteostasis in chloroplasts. Optimized proteostasis appears to be prerequisite for proper thylakostasis.

Protein Complex Assembly and Post-Translational Modifications

Although the TM has a lipid bilayer structure, 75% of its mass consists of proteins, which are embedded in or associated with the membrane (Kirchhoff et al. 2008). In addition to proteostasis, the spatial arrangement of protein complexes within the membrane is a key determinant of thylakoid architecture. Among them, LHCII is the most abundant protein, and its interaction with PSII dimers contributes directly to grana stacking and thylakoid organization (Dekker and Boekema 2005, Albanese et al. 2020, Trotta et al. 2025). Also, chlorophylls are considered to be inserted properly to the antenna and photosystems, concomitantly with the assembly steps.

PSII assembly occurs in a stepwise manner, a process intricately linked to TM biogenesis. In cyanobacteria, these events initiate at the convergent zone (CZ), a region where the PratA protein accumulates, facilitating PSII assembly (Nickelsen and Rengstl 2013). Both in cyanobacteria and vascular plants, the process begins with the formation of a PSII core intermediate, consisting of the D2/cyt b559 complex bound to the reaction center protein D1, which has been processed by CtpA and stabilized by HCF136/ycf48. This intermediate sequentially integrates core antenna proteins (CP47 and CP43), followed by the oxygen-evolving complex on the lumenal side, forming the PSII monomer. During this assembly, proteins such as Psb27 and Psb28 stabilize intermediates and prevent cytotoxic redox reactions. The dimerization of PSII and its association with light harvesting antenna contribute to maturation of the TM, suggesting a strong functional link between PSII biogenesis and membrane formation (Heinz et al. 2016a). PSII assembly is also influenced by a protein complex composed of one helix protein 1 (OHP1), OHP2, and HCF244, which presumably assist in cotranslational insertion of nascent D1 polypeptides (Maeda et al. 2022). The absence of PSII assembly factors results in defective TM formation, underscoring the essential role of PSII in chloroplast thylakoid biogenesis. Some of the assembly factors pleiotropically affect PSII repair. Assembly factors have also been identified for PSI (Rolo et al. 2024), Cyt b6/f (e.g. Sandoval-Ibanez et al. 2022, Li et al. 2023), and ATP synthase (e.g. Zhang et al. 2016a, Zhang et al. 2018), though their direct roles in TM biogenesis are less well understood.

TMs exhibit structural and functional plasticity in response to environmental conditions, such as changes in light intensity. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) of protein complexes are a key mechanism underlying this adaptability (reviewed by Rantala et al. 2020). Phosphorylation of LHCII and PSII core proteins, mediated by STN7 and STN8 kinases, respectively, is a well-characterized modification that influences TM dynamics (Bellafiore et al. 2005, Bonardi et al. 2005, Wood et al. 2018). STN7 is activated by reduction of the plastoquinone pool, leading to phosphorylation of the LHCII N-terminal region. This modification promotes LHCII dissociation from PSII, triggering state transitions that optimize light harvesting. STN8, on the other hand, phosphorylates PSII core proteins, leading to supercomplex disassembly, increasing PSII mobility, and enhancing D1 repair at the grana margins (Tikkanen et al. 2008, Kato and Sakamoto 2014, Puthiyaveetil et al. 2014). These phosphorylation-dependent changes enable the dynamic remodeling of TMs to adapt to varying light environments, playing a central role in thylakostasis (Chuartzman et al. 2008, Pesaresi et al. 2011). Beyond phosphorylation, recent studies suggest that acetylation may also regulate TM function (Koskela et al. 2018). The plastid-localized GNAT2 acetyltransferase has been implicated in state transitions, highlighting the potential for multiple layers of PTMs in regulating thylakoid dynamics (Rantala et al. 2022). Further research into acetylation and other PTMs will provide deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms governing TM homeostasis. Besides these reversible PTM, oxidative modification of PSII core subunits was recently shown to have PSII disassembly and repair, though its involvement in TM organization remains elusive (Kato et al. 2023, McKenzie and Puthiyaveetil 2025).

TM Biogenesis and Possible Factors Involved

TMs in vascular plants emerge during the differentiation of proplastids that lack well-developed thylakoids into chloroplasts, providing an excellent example to dissect TM biogenesis (Charuvi et al. 2012, Liang et al. 2018). Two widely accepted models have been proposed: the membrane protrusion or extension and the vesicular transport from the inner envelope (plasma membrane in case of cyanobacteria) (Morre et al. 1991, Vothknecht and Westhoff 2001, Adam et al. 2011, Kobayashi et al. 2013, Rast et al. 2019, Ostermeier et al. 2024). Liang et al. (2018) used ET to observe chloroplast formation from proplastids 120 h after seed imbibition. They documented granule stacking from tubulovesicular membrane structures and analyzed the process in 3D segments. The presence of membrane-bound polysomes during granule formation suggests the crucial role of coordinated photosynthetic protein synthesis. In addition, tomographic analysis of chloroplast differentiation during the different growing conditions has provided further insights. Observations of vesicle accumulation under low-temperature conditions or in the presence of various inhibitors have helped identify candidate factors involved in dynamic membrane transport during TM formation (Morre et al. 1991, Vothknecht and Westhoff 2001) (Table 1).

Potential thylakoid membrane remodeling molecules

As for possible protein factors involved in thylakoid biogenesis, the first candidate was reported in 2001. A high-chlorophyll fluorescence mutant hcf155 in Arabidopsis exhibited abnormal TM development, and was shown to lack vesicles that typically accumulate under low-temperature (Kroll et al. 2001). This suggested that the protein encoded by HCF155 plays a role in TM formation via vesicular transport, and was termed VIPP1. The Arabidopsis VIPP1 protein corresponded to IM30 in pea (Li et al. 1994), the novel 30-kDa protein identified as co-localized in both inner envelopes and TMs, which implicated its function associated with chloroplast membranes (Westphal et al. 2001, Vothknecht et al. 2012). However, direct evidence for VIPP1’s involvement in vesicular trafficking remains lacking, and VIPP1 is rather considered to play a fundamental role in membrane integrity (Vothknecht et al. 2012, Zhang and Sakamoto 2015, Junglas and Schneider 2018). Beside such genetic studies, attempts have been made to identify factors involved in membrane transport via vesicles (Karim and Aronsson 2014). In chloroplasts, vesicles accumulate under low-temperature conditions or upon the addition of various inhibitors, including a phosphatase inhibitor, microcystin LR, possibly due to inhibited fusion with the target membrane—a feature similar to plasma membrane trafficking. Subsequently, bioinformatic analysis of membrane trafficking factors potentially localized in chloroplasts identified > 50 candidate proteins, which resemble components of the COPII pathway, a well-known cytoplasmic membrane trafficking pathway (Andersson and Sandelius 2004, Khan et al. 2013, Lindquist et al. 2014). Among these, CPSAR1 and CPRabA5e, homologous to the GTPases Sar1 (involved in vesicle formation) and Rab (involved in vesicle fusion with target membranes), respectively, have been experimentally analyzed (Garcia et al. 2010, Karim et al. 2014). CPSAR1 belongs to the Obg family, which is associated with ribosome function rather than Sar1-related membrane trafficking (Chigri et al. 2009, Bang et al. 2012). Arabidopsis cpsar1 mutants fail to develop TMs and exhibit abnormal embryogenesis. Immunostaining has shown that, similarly to the cytoplasmic Sar1, CPSAR1 was abundantly localized to vesicles, implicating its possible role in membrane transport. In contrast, the mutants lacking CPRabA5e exhibit normal growth and only a mild phenotype; increased chloroplast vesicle accumulation under low-temperature conditions and oxidative stress has been discussed in relation to vesicle transport, but it remains uncertain whether the observed phenotype is a direct effect or an indirect consequence of stress responses.

Another distinct factor, chloroplast-localized Sec14-like protein (CPSFL1), has been identified as a candidate for chloroplast membrane trafficking, based on bioinformatic analysis to search proteome data for proteins with lipid-binding domains and confirmed chloroplast localization (Garcia-Cerdan et al. 2020, Hertle et al. 2020). CPSFL1 is homologous to the yeast membrane trafficking factor Sec14. Unlike cpsar1 mutants, Arabidopsis cpsfl1 mutants show normal embryonic development but impaired TM formation. CPSFL1 is the only known chloroplast membrane transporter that (1) lacks vesicle accumulation in cpsfl1 mutants under low-temperature conditions and (2) localizes to vesicles. Therefore, CPSFL1 is currently the strongest candidate for a chloroplast vesicle transporter. In cpsfl1 mutants, underdeveloped TMs are in direct contact with the inner envelope, suggesting that in the absence of vesicular transport, a direct linkage between the two membranes may serve as a compensatory mechanism. However, the precise role of Sec14 in vesicle transport remains unclear. Since both CPSFL1 and Sec14 are involved in lipid (hydrophobic molecule) transport, they may have additional functions beyond vesicle trafficking.

TM Dynamics and Membrane Remodeling Proteins

Over the past decade, research on thylakostasis has unveiled factors involved in membrane formation, namely, proteins with broad remodeling functions, such as membrane deformation, fusion, and fission. This section introduces some of these key proteins associated with TM remodeling.

VIPP1

Homology analyses suggest that VIPP1 originates from the bacterial membrane stress response factor phage shock protein A (PspA) (Westphal et al. 2001, Vothknecht et al. 2012, McDonald et al. 2015). Both PspA and VIPP1 are rich in alpha-helical structures (H1–H7, Gupta et al. 2021), and have been shown to form large oligomeric complexes (Aseeva et al. 2004, Zhang et al. 2012, Otters et al. 2013, Jovanovic et al. 2014), which were shown to associate with membranes despite the lack of transmembrane helices. As VIPP1 is widely conserved in photosynthetic organisms, it is inferred that VIPP1 evolved from PspA and became specialized for TM function (Vothknecht et al. 2012, Zhang et al. 2016b, Ostermeier et al. 2024). While many algal and plant chloroplasts possess multiple homologs (Nordhues et al. 2012, Vothknecht et al. 2012, Gao et al. 2017, Micol-Ponce et al. 2020, Theis et al. 2020, Gachie et al. 2025), Arabidopsis is an exception, containing only one VIPP1 gene (AtVIPP1). Since its discovery, VIPP1 has been implicated in pleiotropic functions, including PSI and PSII activity and protein transport to the TM (Lo and Theg 2012, Nordhues et al. 2012, Zhang et al. 2014, Walter et al. 2015). In Arabidopsis, the AtVIPP1 has been linked to chloroplast membrane maintenance under heat and osmotic stress conditions (Zhang et al. 2012, Zhang et al. 2016b, Zhang et al. 2016c). Functional studies have shown that a GFP-tagged version of VIPP1 retains its function, with its oligomeric structure dynamically responding to stress, further supporting its role in membrane maintenance and repair (Bryan et al. 2014, Gutu et al. 2018, Gachie et al. 2025).

In vitro studies reveal that Chlamydomonas VIPP1 (CrVIPP1) forms helical tubes and binds membranes (Theis et al. 2019), while structural analyses of cyanobacterial VIPP1 (cyanoVIPP1) in 2021 demonstrated its formation of ring structures (Gupta et al. 2021, Liu et al. 2021) (Fig. 4a–c). VIPP1 monomer has the N-terminal amphiphilic H1 helix, arranged perpendicularly to the hairpin formed by H2 and H3 helices. Toward its C-terminus, H4–H6 are connected by the flex amino-acid stretches that act as a hinge, allowing flexibility of the ring oligomers (Fig. 4b). The hydrophobic surface of H1 faces to the interior of the ring, which binds to lipids and guides membrane curvature toward the lumen, facilitating membrane deformation (McDonald et al. 2017). Furthermore, VIPP1 oligomers have been reported to contain nucleotide-binding sites and NTP hydrolytic activity (Ohnishi et al. 2018), though their involvement in membrane remodeling remains unclear (Junglas et al. 2020, Ohnishi et al. 2022). Interestingly, VIPP1 shares structural similarities with endosomal sorting complex required for transport complex III (ESCRT-III) proteins, which play critical roles in endomembrane remodeling in eukaryotic cytoplasms, as well as with the helical structure of PspA (Gupta et al. 2021, Junglas et al. 2021, Liu et al. 2021). These similarities reinforce the hypothesis that VIPP1 is a membrane-remodeling molecule derived from prokaryotic PspA but widely conserved across different biological systems.

Further in vitro studies have demonstrated that cyanoVIPP1 self-assembles into filaments on liposomes, forming spiral structures with a pitch of 54 Å, as observed via atomic force microscopy (Naskar et al. 2025). These structures preferentially associate at the highly curved edges of membranes, recognizing membrane stress regions (Junglas et al. 2025). Similar self-association has been reported for CrVIPP1, further drawing parallels with ESCRT-III (Pan et al. 2025). Notably, AtVIPP1-GFP expressed in tobacco forms bundled filament-like structures in vivo (Fig. 4d–f), and dynamic morphological changes have been observed in response to heat stress (Gachie et al. 2025). These findings strongly suggest that VIPP1 contributes to membrane remodeling through higher-order structural assemblies (Fig. 4g). Although VIPP1 oligomers are observed in vivo at the interface between thylakoids and inner envelopes/plasma membranes (Rast et al. 2019, Gupta et al. 2021, Gachie et al. 2025), their direct involvement in TM biogenesis remains uncertain. Further investigations are necessary to clarify VIPP1’s role in this process.

The C-terminal amino acid sequence corresponding to H7 is intrinsically disordered and appears to be crucial for VIPP1’s self-association (Zhang et al. 2016b, Naskar et al. 2025). Initially, H7 was considered specific to photosynthetic organisms, ensuring a specialized function for the TM (Vothknecht et al. 2012). However, similar α-helical structures are also present in non-photosynthetic bacteria (Theis et al. 2020, Ma et al. 2025), suggesting that its function extends beyond photosynthesis. While H7 is necessary for VIPP1 activity, its deletion leads to excessive oligomerization, reducing protein dynamics and negatively affecting high-temperature tolerance (Zhang et al. 2016b, Gachie et al. 2025, Ma et al. 2025). Comparable results have been observed in vitro for synVIPP1, where C-terminal truncation resulted in excessive self-association (Naskar et al. 2025). These findings suggest that H7 functions as a negative regulator, maintaining the dynamic nature of VIPP1’s membrane remodeling activity.

ESCRT-III requires interactions with AAA proteins like Vps4 for disassembly (Mierzwa et al. 2017, McCullough et al. 2018), and similar protein interactions have been proposed for VIPP1. Heat shock protein (HSP) 70 has emerged as a potential interacting partner, with in vitro studies showing that a chaperone complex involving HSP70B, the J-domain protein CDJ2, and the GrpE homolog CGE1 binds to CrVIPP1, potentially regulating its oligomerization (Liu et al. 2005, Liu et al. 2007). In vascular plants, AtVIPP1 has also been reported to interact with HSP70, reinforcing the idea that VIPP1 activity is chaperone-regulated (Gachie et al. 2025, Li et al. 2025). In addition, proteins interacting with VIPP1 have been identified through proximity-labeling experiments, leading to the discovery of the VIPP1 proximity labeling (VPL) protein family (Kreis et al. 2023). Among them, VPL3, known as VIPP1-associated protein 1 (VIA1) in Arabidopsis, has been implicated in TM formation and may play a role similar to ESCRT-II in eukaryotic endomembrane systems (Yilmazer et al. 2023). Future studies exploring the relationship between these interacting proteins and VIPP1 protein will be crucial for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying thylakostasis.

CURT1

CURT1 encodes TM proteins featuring an N-terminal amphiphilic helix followed by two putative transmembrane domains (Armbruster et al. 2013, Heinz et al. 2016b). It is well conserved in oxygen-evolving photosynthetic organisms, and the Arabidopsis genome contains four CURT1 members (CURT1A, B, C, and D), all of which localize to the TMs. This small (~10–15 kDa) protein family is conserved across photosynthetic organisms, including cyanobacteria, green algae, and land plants (Armbruster et al. 2013). Initially identified as uncharacterized genes co-expressed with photosynthetic genes, CURT1 was later demonstrated to play a critical role in thylakoid architecture. In Arabidopsis, loss of CURT1 leads to distorted thylakoid architecture, characterized by elongated, unstacked membranes, and enlarged, flattened grana layers. Conversely, overexpression of CURT1A results in more highly stacked grana with reduced diameters (Armbruster et al. 2013). Immunogold labeling has confirmed the localization of CURT1 at the curvature region of grana, and the experiments with liposomes have shown that CURT1A can induce membrane curvature, supporting its role in shaping the grana margins.

Although CURT1 has not yet been elucidated structurally, its predicted domain organization provides insight into its role in membrane curvature (Armbruster et al. 2013). The N-terminal amphipathic helix, characterized by the positioning of hydrophobic and polar residues along opposite faces of the helix, is considered to insert shallowly into the membrane lipids and align parallel to its surface. This configuration generates an angular orientation relative to the C-terminal transmembrane helices, thereby promoting membrane bending. CURT1 in TM is therefore considered a key curvature-inducing membrane remodeling factor, acting at the grana margins and curvature regions where it localizes and oligomerizes to facilitate the formation of grana thylakoids (Pribil et al. 2014). Notably, proteins containing N-terminal amphipathic α-helices with membrane bending activity are also found in mitochondria, highlighting CURT1 as part of a broader class of proteins that mediate membrane curvature and remodeling across different organelles (reviewed by Konnel et al. 2019).

From a physiological perspective, CURT1 optimizes thylakoid architecture and regulates photosynthesis by influencing PSII repair cycles and LHCII movement between PSII and PSI under fluctuating light conditions (Pribil et al. 2018). It also modulates plastocyanin-mediated electron transport by controlling grana diameter (Hohner et al. 2020). Regarding these functions, CURT1 has been reported to undergo post-translational modifications, notably in CURT1B, in which the N-terminal amphipathic helix is subject to phosphorylation and acetylation (Trotta et al. 2019). Phosphorylation of CURT1B appears to occur in parallel with the phosphorylation of the PSII core, strongly suggesting regulation by the STN8 kinase. This raises the possibility that phosphorylation of the N-terminal helix modulates TM curvature, thereby indirectly influencing the distribution of excitation energy from PSII or facilitating PSII repair. Such regulatory mechanisms likely contribute to plant acclimation to fluctuating light environments (Trotta et al. 2019). Beyond its role in mature chloroplasts, CURT1 is also essential for PLB maintenance in etioplasts, supporting the transition to functional chloroplasts during de-etiolation (Sandoval-Ibanez et al. 2021). Electron microscopy of cryofixed Arabidopsis cotyledons has revealed that CURT1A is required for pre-granal thylakoid assembly from PLBs, while CURT1C is involved in the growth of cubic crystals within PLBs (Liang et al. 2022).

In Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803, the CURT1 homolog, CurT, is essential for thylakoid architecture and PSII biogenesis. CurT deletion leads to loss of the CZ, a distinct biogenesis center at the plasma membrane where early PSII assembly occurs (Heinz et al. 2016a). Cryo-ET has revealed that CZs contain tubular thylakoid convergence membranes (TCMs), which interconnect with specific thylakoid sheets and form specialized contact sites (thylapses) with the plasma membrane, featuring a narrow 2–4 nm gap (Rast et al. 2019). These findings suggest that CurT is critical for organizing TCMs and maintaining thylakoid network integrity (Rast et al. 2019). The function of CURT1’s N-terminal amphipathic helix remains unclear. However, proteins involved in membrane remodeling, such as MinE (bacterial cell division) and Epsin (clathrin-mediated endocytosis), also contain similar helices, implying a role in membrane curvature induction (Armbruster et al. 2013). While Chlamydomonas possesses several CURT1 homologs, their physiological roles in thylakoid architecture remain to be characterized.

The protein termed “anchor of convergence membranes” (AncM) plays a distinct role in anchoring the CZ to the plasma membrane. Identified through suppressor screening in Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 CurT mutants, AncM is widely conserved in cyanobacteria but absent in algae and land plants (Ostermeier et al. 2022). Loss of AncM causes CZ detachment from the plasma membrane while preserving CZ morphology, suggesting its role in stabilizing thylakoid-plasma membrane interactions. Cryo-ET has identified a 2–4 nm structure connecting the CZ to the plasma membrane, potentially involving AncM (Rast et al. 2019). Like CurT mutants, AncM mutants exhibit PSII biogenesis defects, indicating its role in thylakoid biogenesis. However, the mechanism by which AncM mutation suppresses the photosynthetic defects of CurT mutants remains unresolved. These findings highlight the intricate relationship between TM domain formation and photosynthetic protein complex assembly.

FZL

FZL, a homolog of the dynamin-related protein fuzzy onion (FZO), mediates mitochondrial fusion in fungi and animals but is chloroplast-localized in plants (Gao et al. 2006, Low and Lowe 2006). Originally, FZL was shown to be involved in chloroplast division, whereas its localization to the TM implicated its role in TM remodeling. For example, in vivo studies indicate that FZL facilitates TM fusion in Chlamydomonas (Findinier et al. 2019). Its precise chloroplast localization varies across studies, likely due to differences in GFP fusion proteins or species examined. Immunostaining analysis in Arabidopsis confirms FZL localization at grana margins and curvature regions (Patil et al. 2018, Ogawa et al. 2023). Further research is needed to elucidate the remodeling function of FZL in the TM.

The Arabidopsis fzl knockout mutant exhibits normal growth with only mild chlorophyll reduction (Landoni et al. 2013, Tremblay et al. 2016, Liang et al. 2018), yet displays distinct thylakoid structural anomalies. Grana membrane alignment is disrupted, and connections between grana and stromal thylakoids are less distinct (Liang et al. 2018, Ogawa et al. 2023). These structural abnormalities suggest that FZL promotes grana-stromal thylakoid fusion, maintaining a tightly interconnected thylakoid network. Photosynthetically, the fzl mutant exhibits a slight decrease in the efficiency of photosynthetic electron transport compared to the wild type, both in PSII and PSI (Ogawa et al. 2023). Interestingly, fzl mutants display elevated non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), which cannot be explained by the decrease in electron transport, nor by the observed reduction in proton motive force (Ogawa et al. 2023). One possible explanation is that the impaired grana arrangement and membrane connection hinder efficient electron transfer via plastocyanin on the lumenal side of the TM, which potentially leads to a local imbalance in the proton gradient and NPQ induction. These findings suggest that proper TM formation mediated by FZL is essential for maintaining lumenal connectivity between grana and stroma thylakoids, thereby contributing to efficient electron transport. While these observations underscore the role of FZL in thylakostasis, future studies are needed to understand the functional and structural insights into the TM remodeling activity.

Perspectives

Recent advances have shed light on the various factors controlling thylakostasis. The current model of TM organization describes its distinct compartments. Structural analyses of the TM stacking, greatly enhanced by the rapid development of cryo-electron microscopy techniques such as tomography, have provided valuable insights. However, these studies have primarily focused on Chlamydomonas and a few other plants, with limited analysis in vascular plants. Cryo-ET has now achieved sufficient resolution to identify protein complexes on TMs. In addition, attempts to visualize membrane remodeling proteins using correlative light and electron microscopy have been reported, although these studies have relied on fixed sections. In the near future, advancements in TEM techniques will further aid in the elucidation of the architecture of grana and deepen our understanding of the structure and function of grana margins and curvature domains.

TMs do not maintain a fixed structure; instead, their morphology and stacking dynamically adjust to changes in the light environment and other physiological conditions to optimize photosynthetic efficiency. Achieving a comprehensive understanding of these processes will require interdisciplinary research that links membrane structure to function, ultimately clarifying the structural basis of thylakostasis. Progress in elucidating the lipid and protein complexes that constitute TMs is also essential for understanding thylakostasis. Further insights into lipid biosynthesis pathways and protein complex assembly steps will advance our knowledge of TM formation, particularly at the contact site between TMs and inner envelopes. In addition, VIPP1 and CURT1, key players in membrane remodeling, require further study in various plant species, particularly concerning their roles in environmental adaptation and stress responses. Future research will also explore interactions between membrane remodeling proteins, as the mechanisms underlying TM fission remain poorly understood. For instance, little is known about how TMs sense and respond to damage, execute repair, or undergo division.

Chloroplasts in vascular plants represent merely one form of plastid, and many aspects of membrane remodeling remain unexplored in other plastid types, such as amyloplasts, which contain few TMs. A comprehensive understanding of membrane remodeling across different plastids, including their structural dynamics and functional adaptations, is needed to fully elucidate the principles governing thylakostasis.
