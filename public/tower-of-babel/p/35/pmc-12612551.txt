Nerve Root Enhancement and Elevated Cerebrospinal Fluid Protein in Four Patients With SOD1 ‐Linked Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis
Authors: Veria Vacchiano, Cosmanna Ragucci, Giovanni Rizzo, Vitantonio Di Stasi, Francesca Pastorelli, Rita Rinaldi, Federica Provini, Emanuela Postiglione, Alessia Fiorentino, Valerio Carelli, Rocco Liguori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe superoxide dismutase type 1 (SOD1) gene has been implicated in both sporadic and familial forms of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). We report four ALS cases carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic SOD1 variants, characterized by albuminocytologic dissociation and nerve root enhancement.MethodsWe present the results of the diagnostic work‐up, including lumbosacral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium, electromyography (EMG), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis. We also assessed the relationship between the albumin quotient (Q‐Alb)—an index of blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction—and the disease progression rate (DPR) in 12 SOD1‐linked ALS patients (including the four described above) and in a cohort of 137 non‐genetic ALS (NgALS) cases.ResultsThe four patients presented with spinal onset (progressive lower limb weakness). The EMG ultimately showed diffuse subacute neurogenic changes, while CSF analysis revealed albuminocytologic dissociation. Lumbosacral MRI demonstrated contrast enhancement of the cauda equina roots. Immunomodulatory treatment was administered due to suspected immune‐mediated neuropathy, but all patients continued to deteriorate. Genetic testing revealed pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the SOD1 gene, confirming the diagnosis of ALS. CSF Q‐Alb and protein levels were similarly distributed between SOD1‐linked and NgALS patients. Q‐Alb and CSF protein levels showed a positive correlation with DPR in SOD1‐linked patients (Rho = 0.625, p = 0.03; Rho = 0.755, p = 0.005), but not in NgALS patients.ConclusionAlbuminocytologic dissociation and nerve root enhancement may occur in SOD1‐related ALS, expanding the spectrum of atypical ALS phenotypes.Keywords: albuminocytologic dissociation, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, nerve root enhancement, SOD1 gene

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons (UMNs and LMNs). Mutations in the Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene are responsible for approximately 20% of familial and 1%–2% of sporadic ALS cases [1]. Although the diagnosis of typical ALS is relatively straightforward, variants presenting with focal involvement or atypical features may be underdiagnosed.

We describe four ALS patients carrying SOD1 gene variants who were initially misdiagnosed and treated for immune‐mediated neuropathy due to some atypical features, including lumbar nerve root enhancement on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), as well as elevated protein and albumin quotient (Q‐Alb) values in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these atypical features remain unclear, with possible explanations including disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [2].

Methods

We described the disease course and diagnostic work‐up of four atypical ALS cases. We compared CSF protein concentrations and Q‐Alb values between a cohort of SOD1‐linked ALS patients and a cohort of non‐genetic ALS (NgALS) cases, defined as ALS patients testing negative for the major ALS‐related genes (SOD1, FUS, TARDBP, C9orf72). We explored the correlation between CSF protein and Q‐Alb values with the clinical features of both cohorts. The following clinical variables were collected at the time of diagnosis: age at onset; age at diagnosis; sex; disease duration (time elapsed between the disease onset and diagnosis); type of onset; ALSFRS‐R scale; phenotype categories [3]. The disease progression rate (DPR) was calculated as (48—ALSFRS‐R score at diagnosis)/disease duration.

From routine CSF analysis, we extracted protein and albumin concentrations to calculate the Q‐Alb index as follows: CSF albumin concentration (mg/dL)/serum albumin concentration (mg/dL). To facilitate statistical analysis, the obtained value was multiplied by 103.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

The Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables and the chi‐square test for categorical variables. Spearman's rank correlations were used to examine associations between CSF protein, Q‐Alb values and continuous clinical variables. Multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to confirm the associations between Q‐Alb and clinical variables, adjusting for covariates identified as significant in the Spearman correlation. p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Detailed clinical histories and diagnostic workups of the four ALS patients are summarized in Table 1. All patients presented with lower‐limb weakness as the initial symptom; in cases 2 and 4, this was preceded or accompanied by cramps and fasciculations. In two patients (cases 3 and 4), weakness rapidly progressed to involve the upper limbs, trunk, and bulbar muscles, resulting in severe disability within the first 18 months. Neurological examination consistently showed LMN signs, including diffuse muscle atrophy and weakness, while UMN signs subsequently emerged in three cases (1, 2, 4). Brain MRI was unremarkable in three patients, while case 2 showed a few white matter changes resembling demyelinating lesions. Lumbosacral MRI with gadolinium demonstrated mild‐to‐marked enhancement of the cauda equina roots in all patients (Figure 1). CSF analysis consistently revealed albuminocytologic dissociation, with elevated protein levels and increased Q‐Alb values, while white blood cell counts remained within normal limits.

Electrophysiological studies ultimately supported the diagnosis of a motor neuron disorder in all cases. In case 1, electromyography (EMG) revealed subacute neurogenic changes restricted to the tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius muscles, while nerve conduction studies (NCS) showed reduced CMAP amplitudes and absent late responses in the lower limbs, with preservation of sensory conduction. Case 2 demonstrated widespread subacute neurogenic changes involving bulbar, cervical, and lumbosacral regions, with normal NCS. Case 3 exhibited subacute neurogenic changes affecting bulbar, cervical, thoracic, and lumbosacral regions. In case 4, initial EMG showed only diffuse fasciculation potentials, whereas follow‐up studies revealed widespread subacute neurogenic changes across all body regions. None of the cases fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) established by the Peripheral Nerve Society (PNS) [4].

Given the suspicion of immune‐mediated neuropathy, all patients received immunomodulatory therapy; however, none showed clinical improvement, and disease progression continued. Genetic analysis confirmed pathogenic or likely pathogenic heterozygous SOD1 variants in all patients (Table 1).

CSF Q‐Alb and protein levels were similarly distributed between SOD1‐linked and NgALS patients (Table 2). In the NgALS group, Q‐Alb values did not correlate with age at onset (p = 0.068), age at diagnosis (p = 0.06), disease duration (p = 0.44), ALSFRS‐R score (p = 0.91), or DPR (p = 0.54), and did not differ by phenotype or type of onset (p = 0.472 and p = 0.835). Male patients exhibited higher Q‐Alb values than females (median 7.89 [IQR 5.95–11.49] vs. 6.06 [IQR 4.81–7.87]; p < 0.001). After adjusting for sex, linear regression analyses revealed no significant associations between Q‐Alb values and clinical variables.

CSF protein values resulted correlated with age at onset and diagnosis (Rho = −0.179, p = 0.037 and Rho = −0.19, p = 0.026, respectively), but not with disease duration (p = 0.296), ALSFRS‐R score (p = 0.953), DPR (p = 0.498), phenotype (p = 0.478), or type of onset (p = 0.951). Male patients showed higher CSF protein levels than females (median 53 [IQR 44.5–73.75] vs. 43 [IQR 34.5–53]; p < 0.001). After adjustment for sex and age at diagnosis, multivariate regression analyses confirmed no significant associations with clinical variables.

In the SOD1 cohort, Q‐Alb values were not associated with age at onset (p = 0.158), age at diagnosis (p = 0.072), sex (p = 0.282), ALSFRS‐R score (p = 0.217), or phenotypes (p = 0.876). Interestingly, Q‐Alb values were positively correlated with DPR (Rho = 0.755, p = 0.005) and inversely correlated with disease duration (Rho = −0.607, p = 0.036). After adjustment for disease duration, Q‐Alb values remained significantly associated with DPR (p = 0.031), but not with other clinical variables.

CSF protein levels were not associated with age at onset (p = 0.195), age at diagnosis (p = 0.096), disease duration (p = 0.083), ALSFRS‐R score (p = 0.224), phenotypes (p = 1), or sex (p = 0.373). However, CSF protein levels showed a positive correlation with DPR (RHO = 0.625, p = 0.03).

Discussion

In this study, we described four ALS patients carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the SOD1 gene who presented with atypical features, including contrast enhancement of the lumbar nerve roots and albuminocytologic dissociation. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these atypical features remain unclear.

Elevation of CSF protein and Q‐Alb levels has been reported in ALS patients, suggesting these could represent epiphenomena of neurodegeneration [5, 6, 7, 8]. In familial ALS, elevated CSF protein levels correlated with poorer survival, although genetic data were not available [5]; no such correlation was observed in sporadic cases [5].

Q‐Alb values have not been consistently associated with disease aggressiveness in sporadic ALS patients [6, 7], although one study demonstrated a relationship between BBB dysfunction and poor prognosis, specifically in the spinal phenotype [8]. These findings support blood–spinal cord barrier dysfunction as a potential contributor to ALS pathogenesis in spinal phenotypes. Indeed, early microvascular alterations in motor neuron–dense regions of the spinal cord might facilitate the extravasation of toxic blood components [9]; alternatively, neuroinflammatory pathways and astrocytic dysfunction may exacerbate barrier hyperpermeability [2, 10].

Conversely, nerve root enhancement is observed in a wide spectrum of disorders, including autoimmune polyneuropathies such as CIDP, as well as neoplastic and infectious processes. However, this finding does not exclude an ALS diagnosis. Indeed, root enhancement has been documented in a few ALS cases [11, 12, 13].

Notably, two isolated SOD1‐related ALS patients exhibited albuminocytologic dissociation and nerve root enhancement on spinal MRI [14, 15], while another SOD1‐ALS patient with subacute facial nerve palsy showed cranial nerve enhancement [13].

In this context, this radiological finding may reflect BBB disruption secondary to rapid neuronal degeneration [12] or neuroinflammatory processes [10].

Interestingly, in our SOD1‐ALS cohort, Q‐Alb values—reflecting BBB dysfunction—were associated with a faster disease progression rate. This association was not observed in non‐genetic ALS patients, corroborating previous findings [6, 7]. Notably, despite the atypical findings of increased CSF protein and lumbar root enhancement, none of these patients met the PNS criteria for the diagnosis of CIDP [4], underscoring that clinicians should not rely solely on protein levels or root enhancement when evaluating patients with suspected CIDP.

Our case series expands the phenotypic spectrum associated with SOD1‐linked ALS and demonstrates an association between BBB disruption indices and disease aggressiveness in this group.

Nevertheless, the limited size of the SOD1 cohort did not allow definitive conclusions as to whether SOD1 variants themselves significantly contributed to the presence of these atypical features or to BBB dysfunction. Additionally, we did not perform lumbosacral MRI with gadolinium in all SOD1 patients, as this test is not routinely performed for ALS diagnosis.

In conclusion, contrast enhancement and albuminocytologic dissociation are not specific for primarily immune‐mediated neuropathies. Patients without a sustained response to immunomodulatory treatments should undergo longitudinal evaluation to detect clinical/neurophysiological signs of motor neuron disease. Future studies are needed to determine whether root enhancement and BBB dysfunction are more closely associated with rapid ALS progression or with specific SOD1‐related ALS phenotypes.
