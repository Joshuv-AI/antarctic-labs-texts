N-acetylcysteine amide alleviates pathological neovascularization through mitochondrial protection and energy metabolism reprogramming of vascular endothelial cells
Authors: Jicheng Lin, Fengmei Yang, Andina Hu, Xiaoyu Tang, Likun Lin, Peiqi Wu, Haolin Kong, Changxuan Liu, Kaixuan Cui, Boyu Yang, Kun Xu, Shanshan Yu, Xi Lu, Yue Xu, Xiaoling Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hypoxia-induced pathological neovascularization is a major contributing factor to a variety of diseases across different age groups. Currently, the predominant therapeutic approach involves the repeated injection of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents, which are associated with several adverse effects that limit their long-term application. This study concentrates on the upstream regulators of VEGF within vascular endothelial cells (VECs) and uses the oxygen-induced retinopathy mouse model to demonstrate that N-acetylcysteine amide (NACA) exhibits significant antiangiogenic properties both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, NACA preserves mitochondrial homeostasis and mitigates mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) in VECs. Moreover, smart RNA sequencing and the Oxygen Consumption Rate experiment indicate that NACA reprograms the cellular energy metabolism by restoring oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) via targeting mitochondrial complexes I/V, a mechanism further confirmed by the partial blockade of NACA's effect with oligomycin. Additionally, NACA effectively rescues inner-retinal neurons within the avascular areas. We observed minimal toxicity after intravitreal administration of NACA, suggesting a favorable safety profile for its therapeutic use. Collectively, our findings confirmed that targeting mitochondrial homeostasis and cellular energy metabolism in VECs could serve as a promising therapeutic strategy, with NACA anticipated to emerge as a novel drug for diseases characterized by pathological neovascularization.

Introduction

Pathological neovascularization contributes significantly to the pathogenesis of various diseases impacting millions of people worldwide [[1], [2], [3]]. These conditions include retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) in neonates [4], age-related macular degeneration (AMD) in older adults [5], cancer [6] and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) [7] across all age groups, collectively constituting a substantial global public health challenge. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is the primary therapy used for these diseases, as VEGF is recognized as a key downstream effector within the signaling pathways associated with pathological neovascularization [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. However, these drugs exclusively target VEGF, with several limitations such as repeated injections and insensitivity in some patients [12]. These limitations are consistent with the complex mechanisms of pathological neovascularization. In recent years, many theories about the upstream regulators of VEGF have arisen, yet not been fully elucidated, making it difficult to develop clinical treatments other than anti-VEGF drugs.

Hypoxia is the central driving force behind neovascularization, influencing a series of genes and proteins that promote endothelial cell sprouting and proliferation [13]. As is well known, hypoxia leads to the activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1), which functions as a transcriptional activator to enhance neovascularization [14,15]. Furthermore, recent studies have revealed that mitochondrial dysfunction and energy metabolism reprogramming were implicated in the regulation of neovascularization in response to hypoxia [16,17]. As the sentinel cells responsible for detecting hypoxia, vascular endothelial cells (VECs) exhibit distinct energy metabolism patterns compared to other cells in the tissue. Katrien et al. highlighted that VECs predominantly use aerobic glycolysis for ATP production to facilitate rapid energy supply [18]. Although mitochondrial respiratory chain has a minimal contribution to the energy metabolism of VECs, mitochondria play a crucial role in the regulation of neovascularization [19]. Specifically, the mitochondria within VECs can influence blood vessel formation by modulating nitric oxide (NO) production, intracellular calcium ion (Ca2+) flow, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [20]. Moreover, mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) can regulate neovascularization by activating various downstream signaling pathways [21]. As for energy metabolism, researchers have demonstrated that VECs show abnormal enhancement of glycolysis and disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis in hypoxia-induced pathological neovascularization [17,22,23]. Enhanced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) has been shown to inhibit pathological neovascularization [24]. Collectively, targeting the mitochondrial homeostasis and energy metabolism of VECs may provide novel perspectives for clinical therapy of anti-neovascularization.

N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a thiol-containing antioxidant, acts as a precursor to glutathione (GSH) and modulates the intracellular redox state [25]. It has been widely used in clinics for various diseases characterized by oxidative stress [26]. However, NAC exhibits low bioavailability (6–10 %) and short half-life [27]. These limitations result in less effective outcomes in clinical trials than in experimental studies. To address these limitations, an Israeli research group designed and synthesized lipophilic thiol compounds to enhance bioavailability and membrane permeability [28]. Among these, N-acetylcysteine amide (NACA), a derivative of NAC with a neutralized carboxyl group, improves lipophilicity, membrane permeability, drug bioavailability, and GSH replenishment [27]. Isabelle et al. demonstrated NACA's strong blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability and protective effects using an in vitro model [29]. Prior research agrees that NACA protects against various diseases by increasing intracellular GSH and scavenging ROS to combat oxidative stress [[30], [31], [32]]. It also reduces renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis by modulating the p38-MAPK/iNOS pathway [33], provides neuroprotection through the Nrf2-antioxidant response element (ARE) pathway [34], and lessens airway inflammation by regulating NF-κB and HIF-1α activation [35]. Furthermore, recent studies have also noted that NACA offers neuroprotection by mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction and enhancing mitochondrial energetics [36,37]. However, whether NACA has a potential role in VECs and neovascular diseases has not yet been reported.

Retinal vasculature is an appropriate model for vascular biology research since it can be easily observed and topically treated, without concerns about first-pass metabolism or the blood-retinal barrier [38,39]. Moreover, the retina has the highest oxygen consumption rate per tissue volume among all organs [40,41]. In the current study, we determined the anti-angiogenic and mitochondrial protection effects of NACA, using a hypoxia-induced endothelial cell dysfunction model in vitro and oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) mouse model in vivo. We further clarified that NACA reprograms the cellular energy metabolism by enhancing OXPHOS. We also evaluated the neuroprotective effects and the safety for clinical applications of NACA. Our data highlighted NACA as a novel drug for protecting VECs and inhibiting pathological neovascularization.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Chemicals and antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Administration of NACA

NACA was purchased in powder form, and diluted in sterile PBS to prepare a stock solution of 25 mg/ml. For in vitro applications (except for the CCK-8 assay), the working solution was freshly diluted to 5 mM with culture medium. For in vivo experiments, the working solution was freshly made using sterile PBS solution at a final concentration of 50 mM and injected into the mice intravitreally (1 μl/eye) at postnatal day 12 (P12). The concentration of the NACA was based on previous studies [42,43] and the present pre-experimental results (data not shown). Mice in the other group received an equal volume injection of vehicle (PBS) as a control.

Cell culture and treatment of HUVECs and HRMECs

Primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (HRMECs) were purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd (#CP-H082, #CP-H130). HUVECs were cultured in HUVEC-specific culture medium (Pricella, Wuhan, China, #CM-H082), containing growth factors and 5 % fetal bovine serum. HUVECs were passaged with Accutase solution and used at low passage numbers, between 3 and 6. Similarly, HRMECs were cultured in HRMEC-specific culture medium (Pricella, Wuhan, China, #CM-H130), containing growth factors and 5 % fetal bovine serum. HRMECs were passaged with TryL-PE solution and used at low passage numbers, between 3 and 5.

As for the hypoxia treatment, cells were maintained under an atmosphere of 1 % O2 in the absence or presence of NACA and then used for further analysis. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5 % CO2 in a cell culture incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cell viability, proliferation, migration and tube formation assay

CCK-8 assay was used to determine cell viability. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 103 per well for 12 h. Different doses of NACA were added to the plates for a 12-h treatment. Following this, cells were washed by PBS and CCK-8 reagents were added to the culture medium for 2 h. Then the optical densities were measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm. The viability percentage was determined by comparing the experimental cells to the normal cells. EdU incorporation assay was used to assess cell proliferation. HUVECs were incubated with EdU for 2 h and stained according to the manufacturer's instructions. All fluorescence images were taken using confocal laser scanning microscopy (LSM980, Carl Zeiss, Germany). For scratch assays, HUVECs were seeded into six-well plates. The scratch line was made using a 200 μl pipette tip with 90 % confluent HUVECs, and the closing of the scratch line was photographed and compared with the 0-h images after 12-h-treatment. Transwell assays were performed as described previously [44]. Three fields of vision were randomly selected using a Leica light microscope (Leica, Germany). For the angiogenesis assay, the 24-well plate was pre-coated with 20 μl of Matrigel. When the Matrigel solidified, 2 × 104 cells were added on top of the Matrigel to each well. After 8 h, three fields of vision were randomly selected using the Leica light microscope.

Animals and the establishment of oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) model

C57BL/6J mice were obtained and raised from the Animal Laboratory of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (Guangzhou, China). The OIR mouse model was produced as previously documented [45]. In brief, P7 mice were exposed to a 75 % oxygen environment in a hyperoxic chamber for 5 days with their nursing mothers, then returned to room air for 5 days. Retinas were harvested at P14 for immunofluorescence and H&E staining of retina sections and P17 for other experiments. All animal experiments complied with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (approval number: O2023012). To account for possible litter-specific differences, each experimental group was obtained from at least three different litters and the number of pups per litter was controlled (5–10 mice per litter). Moreover, given the potential for sex as a biological factor, care was taken to ensure equal numbers of male and female pups in all experiments.

Immunofluorescent staining of whole-mount retinas and retina sections

Eyeballs were enucleated, fixed in 4 % PFA for 45 min, and dissected to remove the cornea, lens, sclera, and hyaloid vessels. Retinas were blocked with 5 % normal donkey serum and 2 % BSA (bovine serum albumin) in PBST for 12–18 h at 4 °C, then incubated with primary antibodies in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, they were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. Radial cuts were made to form a petal shape. For immunofluorescent staining, eyeballs were fixed in 4 % PFA overnight at 4 °C, dehydrated in 30 % sucrose, embedded in OCT, and sectioned at 10 μm. Sections were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies in DAPI solution. Images were captured with a Zeiss confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss Meditec, LSM 980, Jena, Germany).

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

After anesthetizing the mice with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), eyeballs were fixed in FAS eye solution for 24 h. They were then dehydrated in a series of alcohols and xylene, followed by paraffin embedding. The paraffin-embedded eyeballs were sliced into 3 μm sections using a microtome and stained by the Hematoxylin-eosin kit. Images were captured with Tissue fax (Tissue Gnostics, Tissue FAXS Q+, Vienna, Austria), and the thickness of the retina layers (GCL, INL, and ONL) was measured using Fiji software.

Cellular GSH content and GSH/GSSG ratio measurement

Cellular GSH content and GSH/GSSG ratio in HRMECs were measured using the GSH/GSSG Ratio Detection assay kit (fluorometric-green; ab205811, Abcam) following the manufacturer's instructions.

TOMM20 staining and super-resolution structured illumination microscopy (SIM) imaging

The morphology of mitochondria was indicated by TOMM20 staining. Images were collected using the Polar-SIM system (Airy Technologies Co., Ltd., China) with a 100 × /1.49 NA oil immersion objective (Nikon, Japan). The reconstruction process was conducted using the Airy-SIM software with the 2D-SIM algorithm. For data analysis, individual whole cells were outlined manually and auto-extracted from each resulting image. The extraction process was automated with a Fiji macro, which is available upon request. Then, the Fiji macro Mitochondrial Analyzer was used for the analysis of mitochondrial morphology. The number, area, and perimeter of mitochondria were calculated. The mitochondrial form factor (FF), a metric for mitochondrial length and branching, was derived from the area and perimeter using the formula: FF = (perimeter^2)/(4π × area). Furthermore, the number of branches and the number of branch end points were assessed.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM experiment was performed according to the protocols of previous studies [46]. Briefly, HRMECs were fixed in a glutaraldehyde and paraformaldehyde solution, post-fixed in osmium tetroxide, and stained with uranyl acetate. After rinsing, the samples were dehydrated, embedded in resin, and baked. Sections were cut, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged using a Morada CCD and Olympus software.

Mitochondrial ROS measurement

Each group of HRMECs was labeled with MitoSOX Red at a final concentration of 2.5 μM for 10 min at 37 °C. The cells were subsequently washed and stained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min.

CD31+ cell sorting and smart RNA sequencing (smart RNA-seq)

P17 mice from the PBS-treated (OIR + Veh) and NACA-treated (OIR + NACA) groups were sacrificed. The retinas were extracted and digested using a pre-prepared digestion solution made of RPMI 1640 medium, papain (40 U/ml), EDTA (0.1 mM), DNAase I (1.33 U/ml), and FBS (5 %). After complete digestion, the cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer and centrifuged (300g, 4 °C, for 5 min). Then the dissociated cells were resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium and counted. CD31+ retinal cells were enriched using magnetic bead sorting (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer's instructions. For smart RNA-seq, total RNA was extracted from CD31+ cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). The RNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform by Chi-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). Bioinformatics analysis was conducted in R Studio with R version 4.1.0 [47]. Significantly differentially expressed genes were analyzed by gene ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA).

Measurement of oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

The OCR and ECAR were measured in HRMECs using a Seahorse XF-24 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) following the procedure from the manufacturer [48]. During the experimental measurement of OCR, sequential injections of 1.5 μM oligomycin (an ATP synthase inhibitor), 2 μM FCCP (a proton uncoupling agent), and a combination of 0.5 μM rotenone and 0.5 μM antimycin A (inhibitors of the mitochondrial respiratory complex) were administered to inhibit the Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complex. From the OCR profiles, the basal respiration, ATP production and maximal respiration were calculated. The glycolytic activity was then measured by sequentially injecting glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1.5 μM), and 2-deoxy-glucose (2-DG) (50 mM). The parameters of cellular glycolysis (including basal glycolysis and glycolytic capacity) were calculated from ECAR profiles. All of the OCR and ECAR values were derived from 3-min measurement cycles and normalized to the cell number.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from HRMECs using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) with 1 % protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China). The PVDF membranes were blocked by protein-free rapid blocking buffer (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, #RM02956) at room temperature for 30 min. After blocking, primary antibodies were used in the incubation at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the membranes were washed six times for 5 min each with gentle shaking, then incubated with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, diluted in TBST, for 1.5 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. After washing in TBST, protein bands were visualized by an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Vazyme Biotech Co.,Ltd).

Statistical analysis

Based on results of the Shapiro-Wilk test consistent with normal distributions, all data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and a detailed p value was labeled in every figure. Unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used for normally distributed independent datasets with equal variances, while Mann-Whitney U test was used for independent datasets with non-normal distributions. More than 2 normally distributed datasets were compared by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison tests for dataset pairs of equal variance or by Dunnett's T3 multiple comparison tests for dataset pairs of unequal variance. Multiple non-normally distributed datasets were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The investigators were blind to the group information of the animal for data analysis. All calculations were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

NACA counteracts hypoxia-triggered proliferation, migration, and tube formation of HUVECs

Angiogenesis is dependent on cell proliferation, migration and tube formation of VECs. To investigate the pharmacological effects of NACA (its chemical formula is shown in Fig. 1A), the safe dose was first determined using the CCK-8 test. Results indicated that NACA concentrations lower than 20 mM had no significant effect on cell viability (Fig. 1B). According to previous studies [42,43,50], 5 mM NACA was used for the subsequent experiments. To determine the anti-angiogenic effect of NACA on HUVECs under hypoxic and normoxic conditions, HUVECs were kept in a hypoxia box for given incubation times (Fig. 1C) and various angiogenic properties were evaluated including proliferation, migration, and tube formation. Firstly, the quantification of EdU staining showed that NACA inhibited HUVEC proliferation under hypoxic but not normoxic condition. (Fig. 1D–H). As for cell migration, the scratch wound-healing assay showed that NACA led to decreased cell migration (Fig. 1E–I). Consistently, the transwell assay demonstrated that NACA significantly decreased the percentage of HUVECs migration to the lower compartment after 12 h of incubation time (Fig. 1F–J). Moreover, the tube formation experiment showed that the tube-forming ability of HUVECs was reduced by NACA treatment. Quantification of branch number showed that NACA treatment could inhibit excessive tube formation (Fig. 1G–K). NACA inhibits pathological retinal neovascularization (RNV) and prevents vascular leakiness in the OIR mouse model

The OIR mouse model (Fig. 2A) is widely used to investigate neovascular diseases. In this model, neonatal mice, when transitioned to room air following oxygen therapy, experience hypoxia in the avascular retina, which leads to the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS). This process upregulates angiogenic factors, resulting in pathological neovascularization, marked by the formation of neovascular tufts at the interface between avascular and vascular regions [51,52]. The vascular loss, vessel regrowth, neovascular tuft formation and vascular regression could be assessed quantitatively. The NACA treatment was performed by intravitreal injection, and the dose was 50 mM according to previous studies and the results in vitro. To investigate the changes in retinal vessels, whole-mounted retinas were immunofluorescence stained with CD31, examples of low- and high-magnification images of the experimental retina were shown in Fig. 2B. After intravitreal injection of NACA, the retinal neovascular area was reduced, while the avascular area was also decreased (Fig. 2C). To further clarify the antiangiogenic effect of NACA, frozen sections (Fig. 2D) and HE-stained paraffin sections (Fig. 2F) of eyeballs were performed to count neovascular cell nuclei anterior to the internal limiting membrane. Results showed that the number of neovascular cell nuclei anterior to the internal limiting membrane in the OIR + NACA group was less than that in the OIR + Veh group (Fig. 2E–G). Moreover, a Ki67 immunofluorescence staining assay was conducted to examine the proliferation of retinal endothelial cells. The result showed that the percentages of ki67/CD31 double-positive cells among CD31-positive cells were decreased after NACA treatment (Fig. S1A–B). Pathological RNV is always accompanied by leakage, the brightfield images were taken using an optical microscope (Fig. 2H) and Ter119 staining of red blood cells was performed to evaluate the leakage occurred in the retinas. The XY axis and XZ axis images of Ter119 staining were both displayed to give a multi-perspective image to show the Ter119+ areas (Fig. 2J). After NACA treatment, the hemorrhagic area in the brightfield decreased by 7.1 % (Fig. 2I) and relative Ter119+ cell numbers out of the vessel decreased by 79.3 % (Fig. 2K). Vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin) mediated endothelial cell-cell junctions are important for maintaining vascular barrier function. We further analyzed the morphology of cell junctions in confluent monolayers of HUVECs which were stained with VE-cadherin and F-actin (Fig. S2). Compared with the continuous junctions in the Ctrl (Control) and NACA groups, hypoxia-induced cell junctions exhibited as discontinuous and perpendicularly oriented VE-cadherin staining morphology, which were similar to the VEGF-induced remodeling cell junctions [53]. NACA repaired the continuity of VE-cadherin partially. These results demonstrated that NACA treatment significantly reduced retinal vascular permeability and prevented vascular leakiness.

Furthermore, it is well acknowledged that hypoxia and inflammation have a close and bi-directional relationship [54]. Retinal inflammation, such as activation of microglia and immune cells, can lead to pathological retinal neovascularization. The recruitment and activation of iba1+ microglia cells and CD45+ immune cells in the neovascular areas were examined by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. S1C and E). The results showed the number of inflammatory cells was reduced in the NACA treatment group (Fig. S1D and F).

NACA inhibits RNV via maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis of HRMECs

In order to clarify how NACA acts on the retinal VECs, we further detected the cell viability of HRMECs following NACA treatment. Result revealed that NACA concentrations lower than 20 mM were safe for HRMECs (Fig. 3A). Given that NACA has been reported to be a precursor of GSH, the intracellular GSH and GSSG contents were measured and the GSH/GSSG ratio was calculated. Quantitative analysis showed that GSH content in the hypoxia group was significantly lower than that in the normoxia group. NACA treatment mitigated the disruption of glutathione balance caused by hypoxia and restored both GSH levels and the GSH/GSSG ratio to normal (Fig. 3B–C). The structural and functional integrity of mitochondria is critically important for the mitochondrial homeostasis of retinal VECs. Immunofluorescence evaluation through TOMM20 staining indicated that NACA rescues the decrease in mitochondria numbers and abnormal mitochondrial morphology induced by hypoxia (Fig. 3D). Quantitative analysis processed by Fiji-Mitochondrial Analyzer plugin showed that the number, area, and perimeter of mitochondria all decreased after hypoxic treatment, while NACA rescued these phenotypes (Fig. 3E–G). Furthermore, compared to the hypoxia group, NACA lowered the form factor, number of branches, and number of branch end points significantly (Fig. 3H–J). Consistently, we assessed mitochondrial length and ultrastructure using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 3K). Discussion

Despite being one of the major options for the treatment of pathological neovascularization [59], anti-VEGF drugs show varying effectiveness in individuals [12,60]. It suggests that various molecules and mechanisms other than VEGF signaling may participate in the pathogenesis of neovascularization. Our research revealed that NACA, a small-molecule thiol antioxidant with high cell permeability, inhibits pathological RNV in both in vivo and in vitro experiments.

Mitochondria are key sensors and integrators of environmental stress [61]. Emerging evidence has emphasized that mitochondrial functions like quality control, energetics, and signaling are crucial for regulating permeability, tone, migration, and proliferation of VECs [19]. Given that NACA has been widely reported as an antioxidant in previous studies, we hypothesize that NACA may target mitochondria. Our data revealed that NACA opposed hypoxia-induced mitochondrial structural damage and mtROS. GO and KEGG analyses demonstrated enrichment of mitochondrial protein synthesis, ATP synthesis and mitochondrial respiratory chain activity. These results indicated NACA’ s positive role in preserving mitochondrial structure and repressing mtROS of VECs. As with all organelles, the structural integrity of mitochondria is crucially linked to their function. Studies have shown that regulating cellular energy metabolism, including glucose metabolism and fatty acid oxidation, can potentially influence physiological and pathological retinal angiogenesis. The loss of mitochondrial proteins in VECs, such as mitochondrial transcription factor A, respiratory complex IV component, or redox protein thioredoxin 2, slows down vessel growth and pathological arteriovenous malformation in the developing retina [46]. In OIR, genetic attenuation of endothelial cell glycolysis suppresses RNV [17,62]. Endothelial cell loss of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1a, a transporter of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria, impairs cell proliferation and compromises de novo nucleotide synthesis for DNA replication [63]. Interestingly, Magdalena et al. found that dysregulation of OXPHOS-related genes may impact neovascularization development [64]. In human retinal endothelial cells cultured in vitro, hypoxia caused a decrease in basal, ATP-linked, and maximal oxygen consumption rates [65]. In OIR retinas, global proteomics showed decreased retinal mitochondrial respiration. OCR and mtDNA/nDNA were also decreased at peak neovascularization [61]. These studies suggest that OXPHOS may be a potential target for anti-angiogenesis therapy. Consistently, transcriptomic analysis in the present study revealed that NACA significantly downregulated the hypoxia pathway and enhanced the activity of the mitochondrial OXPHOS, which was verified by the OCR and ECAR measurements. Furthermore, the dependence of NACA's anti-angiogenic effect on restored OXPHOS was clearly confirmed by pharmacological blockade of Complex V: co-administration of oligomycin eliminated NACA's ability to suppress pathological RNV in the OIR model. Thus, NACA alleviates hypoxia-driven RNV by repairing mitochondrial structure, restoring Complex I/V-dependent OXPHOS activity, and rebalancing cellular energy metabolism, with its therapeutic effect mechanistically dependent on functional ATP synthesis.

Beyond the effects on endothelial cells, NACA also prevents retinal neuron loss in the OIR model, suggesting a broader neuroprotective role. Similar findings have been reported for melatonin, which alleviates retinal neuron death through antioxidant and anti-apoptotic pathways [58]. Based on published literature and our data, we propose that NACA-mediated preservation of inner-retinal neurons involves multifaceted mechanisms. This protection is likely achieved through a combination of direct and indirect pathways. As a potent antioxidant, NACA directly mitigates hypoxia-induced mtROS, as demonstrated in HRMECs (Fig. 4A–B). It is plausible that NACA directly mitigates oxidative stress and subsequent apoptotic pathways in neurons, thereby promoting their survival under hypoxic insult [36,45]. Furthermore, NACA likely confers indirect neuroprotection by improving the retinal microenvironment. This concept is supported by the growing understanding of neurovascular coupling, where a healthy vasculature is fundamental for neuronal survival. For instance, properly guided blood vessels create a supportive microenvironment essential for nerve regeneration [66,67], and hypoxic preconditioning of endothelial cells enhances their secretion of protective factors, such as miR-126, which directly bolsters neuronal survival [68]. In line with these findings, by reducing pathological neovascularization, attenuating vascular leakage (Fig. 2H–K), and enhancing endothelial junction integrity (Fig. S2), NACA helps restore oxygen and nutrient delivery to the ischemic retina while facilitating the clearance of harmful metabolites. Collectively, these dual actions synergistically create a favorable environment for neuronal survival in the hypoxic retina. Therefore, the neuroprotection afforded by NACA may extend beyond merely preventing cell death, potentially enabling neurons to play an active role in retinal recovery, including the critical process of revascularization.

These findings suggest a positive feedback loop between neuronal preservation and the reduction of the avascular area. We propose that this loop operates through two interconnected mechanisms. First, the reduction of the avascular area itself is neuroprotective. By promoting physiological revascularization, NACA directly alleviates the severe hypoxia and ischemia that are the primary causes of neuronal death. Second, the survival of neurons and glial cells likely supports the revascularization process. For example, loss of the antioxidant regulator Nrf2 in Müller glial cells drives a pro-angiogenic response and worsens pathological neovascularization [69]. Similarly, deletion of SOCS3 in retinal neurons and glia disinhibits STAT3-VEGF signaling, leading to increased pathological neovascularization [70]. Furthermore, in a healthy context, perivascular neurons have been reported to influence the spatial positioning of penetrating vessels via Piezo2, thereby organizing the three-dimensional vascular structure in the developing mouse retina [71]. These studies suggest that neuron-vascular interactions may play a crucial role in capillary perfusion and the protection of retinal neurons. In OIR, neuronal loss disrupts this guidance, contributing to the formation of disordered pathological neovascularization instead of functional vessels. Therefore, by preserving neurons, NACA may help to maintain these essential guidance signals, enabling the growing vasculature to repopulate the avascular area in a more organized manner. In summary, NACA not only protects neurons by revascularizing the ischemic retina but also supports revascularization by protecting neurons. This two-way, synergistic interaction constitutes a key mechanism underlying the efficacy of NACA in the OIR model. Future studies using single-cell RNA sequencing and cell-specific models are warranted to further dissect the precise contributions of this neurovascular crosstalk.

The clinical application prospects for NACA are highly promising, particularly concerning its safety profile. Our research has shown that NACA exhibits no toxicity to HUVECs and HRMECs at concentrations below 20 mM. Previous studies involving neural cells have similarly confirmed its broad safety margin [36]. In terms of in vivo administration, our findings indicate that intravitreal injection is safe for retinal vasculature and neurons (Fig. 9A–I). Given NACA's excellent permeability across the BBB, intravitreal administration still permits systemic distribution of the drug via the bloodstream. Consequently, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the histological effects of NACA treatment on key systemic organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys (Fig. 9J). The findings corroborated its favorable safety profile. From a pharmacokinetic standpoint, NACA adheres to a two-compartment open model and exhibits a bioavailability substantially greater than that of NAC (67 % compared to 15 %) [72]. Furthermore, due to the anatomical intricacy of the retina's ten-layer structure and the presence of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB) [73], which is analogous to the BBB, NACA's superior cellular penetration and BBB permeability facilitate its effective delivery to retinal cells across all layers, thereby enhancing its therapeutic efficacy. NACA's high water solubility supports various administration routes, presenting potential for the development of non-invasive formulations such as eye drops or oral medications in the future to improve its clinical applicability. However, the bioavailability of such formulations may be considerably decreased, necessitating further investigation.

To build on these findings and move closer to clinical application, the following future directions are important. First, while intravitreal injection ensures precise delivery to the retina, its clinical convenience remains limited. Developing less invasive delivery methods, such as eye drops or hydrogel-based systems, would improve patient convenience and broaden the clinical use of NACA. Second, testing NACA in other models of pathological neovascularization will help confirm its broad therapeutic value. Besides, advanced techniques, such as single-cell sequencing and proteomics, could provide deeper insights into its multi-target effects and optimize its therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

In summary, the data from this study provide the first direct evidence that NACA can mitigate pathological neovascularization and preserve retinal neurons in an OIR mouse model. Consequently, NACA may represent a novel and promising therapeutic strategy for treating retinal diseases characterized by pathological neovascularization, with significant potential for future clinical translation.
