Factor XIII and cardiovascular disease: biomarker, culprit or victim in acute myocardial infarction outcomes?
Authors: Florian Bruns, Dobromir Dobrev, Anke C. Fender
Published: 2025

The International Journal of Cardiology Heart and Vasculature recently published an intriguing study that sought to connect the fibrin stabilizing factor FXIII with mortality after myocardial infarction (MI) [1].

During blood coagulation, thrombin activates FXIII to FXIIIa, a transglutaminase which cross-links fibrin chains in the growing clot for increased strength and resistance to fibrinolysis (Fig. 1). Failure of this essential hemostatic lever, for example through FXIII depletion during trauma injury will increase the risk both of rebleeding, fatal hemorrhage, and embolization. A looser clot with less fibrin cross-links will be more prone to rupture [2], possibly leading to distal thromboembolism. In their study, Traub and colleagues [1] took up the emerging association between FXIII and mortality specifically post-MI, and sought to identify confounders and contributors to this interaction. Modern invasive or surgical procedures have contributed to decrease mortality after acute MI [3], but reliable predictors are still underdeveloped. Traub and colleagues determined plasma FXIII activity in a cohort of 926 patients (median age 64 years, 23 % female) and for analysis of 30-day and 1-year all-cause mortality, grouped patients according to below or greater than the median level of 110 %. The low FXIII activity group showed more than 2-fold higher early and late all-cause mortality than those with higher FXIII activity, consistent with their generally higher risk profile encompassing older age, more frequent male gender, higher rates of prior MI, atrial fibrillation (AF) and diabetes mellitus, higher C-reactive protein (CRP) and low albumin. The major new finding of this study is that although low FXIII activity is associated with higher mortality, its predictive value disappears after adjustment for all confounders. By contrast, the authors identified low albumin as a strong and independent predictor of mortality post-MI, with low FXIII activity associating with mortality as an epiphenomenon.

What makes this study intriguing are the manifold possible conclusions to be drawn, depending on how the FXIII activity assay system used is interpreted. The authors applied a standard photometric kinetic assay in which FXIII is activated to FXIIIa ex vivo by addition of thrombin to plasma and quantification via ammonia release. Essentially, the assay measures the remaining activation capacity of the zymogen in the blood sample, rather than the concentration of activated FXIIIa present at the time of sampling. A less-than-median FXIII activity measurement could therefore mean that a substantial amount of zymogen was pre-activated in situ prior to sampling, perhaps during the thrombin burst at clotting initiation, leaving less to be converted during the assay procedure. In this hypercoagulant scenario, clots will conceivably be highly stabilized and resistant to lysis, but at the same time pose less risk for embolization. Alternatively, if the assay is interpreted to indicate FXIII activity, as is conventionally done, then lower FXIII activity will likely indicate formation of more fragile clots. In this hyperfibrinolytic scenario, lower clot cohesion and adhesion could increase risk of bleeding and at the same time permit embolisms by microthrombi tearing off the index clot. Unfortunately, the crude mortality data could not be dissected into specific causes of death. It would be helpful to know if causes of death were bundled into distal thromboembolic or bleeding complications.

The authors quite elegantly ship around this dilemma by focussing on the albumin connection and going with a scenario 3 that FXIII/FXIIIa is more of a bystander or minimally-relevant player in post-MI mortality. Their multivariate analysis supports that all-cause mortality in the group with below-median FXIII activity is by and large determined by the coexistent hypoalbuminemia.

Albumin regulates capillary membrane fluid exchange, molecular transport and colloidal osmotic pressure of blood. By virtue of its wide binding spectrum in human blood, albumin also plays a critical role in pharmaco/toxicokinetics of drugs with high plasma protein binding capacity, and has additionally been attributed anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticoagulant and anti-platelet activities [4]. The Copenhagen General Population Study reported that hazard ratios (HR) for each 10 g/L lowering of plasma albumin were 1.17 for ischemic heart disease, 1.25 for MI, and 1.46 for ischemic stroke [5], suggesting a strong biomarker role if not causal involvement in cardiovascular and thromboembolic disorders. In keeping with the findings by Traub et al. [1], lower albumin levels have been related to an incremental risk of in-hospital and to some extent late mortality in various settings [6,7] including acute MI [8,9]. In patients with acute MI, albumin correlated inversely with the level of D-dimers [9]. These are fibrin degradation products formed by plasmin cleavage of cross-linked fibrin strands and essentially reflect fibrinolysis (Fig. 1). High circulating D-dimers predict in-hospital mortality in a variety of disease settings including MI; a comparable predictive value is seen with the D-dimer-to-albumin ratio (DAR) [10]. Notably, higher DAR is independently associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and all-cause mortality after coronary intervention for ischemic heart failure in patients with diabetes.

Assuming then that a high D-dimer/low albumin state is hyperfibrinolytic and thereby drives mortality risk related to bleeding and thromboembolism, this would nicely fit with scenario 2 detailed above, where the lower FXIII activation determined by Traub et al. [1] is accompanied by also lower albumin and a heightened all-cause mortality after MI. A low serum albumin at admission has in fact been independently associated with a higher risk for bleeding events in patients with acute MI [11], further supporting this concept. Here it is worth pondering though that the low FXIII/low albumin/high mortality constellation identified Traub et al. [1] was more frequently complicated also by diabetes. Diabetes per se modifies serum albumin [12] and increases mortality after MI [13,14]. So how much of the mortality risk here can be attributed distinctly or jointly to the underlying diabetes, the hypoalbuminemia, or their combination also with low FXIII? As with all thought-provoking studies, the new insights provided open new questions to be pursued.

A clear limitation of study of Traub et al. [1] is the lack of data on medication history. Polypharmacy can increase MACE and mortality risk [15,16]. Especially the use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy and their combination would have been useful for more insightful interpretation of this in total intriguing report. All patients will undoubtedly have received the same guideline-directed antithrombotic medication after MI, but particularly the low FXIII/high mortality group, with its high proportion of prior MI and AF, will have received these drugs more frequently. Aspirin is a cornerstone of secondary prevention, but in a high proportion of patients, thrombotic events still occur. High on-treatment platelet activity has been linked with low serum albumin [17,18], yet at the same time, hypoalbuminemia independently associates with a high risk of bleeding on dual antiplatelet therapy [19]. The same has been reported for bleeding on direct oral anticoagulants [20]. These data suggest that in patients with low serum albumin, antithrombotic therapies may be prone to both failure and to exaggerated adverse events, which may conceivably have contributed to the increased mortality observed by Traub et al. [1].

Overall, low albumin does appear to indicate an increased risk of general mortality and adverse outcomes, quite likely related to perturbed hemostasis and/or fibrinolysis, and therefore also to bleeding and/or thromboembolism (Fig. 2). But key remaining questions are to what extent is serum albumin a biomarker, culprit or victim in acute MI outcomes, and how can admission levels of albumin be best implemented for risk stratification and disease management? A multimarker panel incorporating albumin together with inflammatory, coagulation and metabolic indices may be able to specifically identify the individual patient risk profile and threshold, and allow tailoring of monitoring and intervention. Robust MACE and mortality prediction has for example been reported for the fibrinogen-to-albumin [21,22], lactate-to-albumin [23,24], and CRP-to-albumin ratios [25,26] as well as the albumin-corrected anion gap [27] in various cardiovascular settings.
