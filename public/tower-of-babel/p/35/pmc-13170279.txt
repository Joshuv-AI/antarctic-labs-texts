Impact of the H-HOPE multisensory behavioural intervention plus Kangaroo Mother Care on early growth in preterm infants in Malawi
Authors: Esnath M Kapito, Ellen Chirwa, Elizabeth Chodzaza, Kathleen Norr, Li Liu, Rafael Angora Lazo, Crystal Patil, Rosemary White-Traut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo evaluate the efficacy of the Hospital-to-Home: Optimizing the Premature Infant’s Environment (H-HOPE) intervention for early infant growth, we compared mother-preterm infant dyads receiving both H-HOPE and Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) with those receiving KMC only, the standard of care for all preterm infants, at a single hospital in Malawi.MethodA prospective quasi-experimental study design was used at Zomba Central Hospital. Because the intervention took place in a single, large room without any auditory or visual privacy, the enrolment of the two study conditions (KMC only control and H-HOPE + KMC intervention conditions) was sequential to avoid overlap. There were 89 KMC only and 94 H-HOPE + KMC mother-preterm infant dyads in each study condition. Infant birth weight was between 950 and 2400 g. When infants reached clinical stability, the interventions commenced and continued through the 2-week post-discharge follow-up assessment.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in maternal and infant characteristics at baseline between the conditions. After adjusting for covariates, baseline birth weight was not significantly different. A multivariate mixed-effects regression model indicated a similar pattern in both study conditions from birth until 2 weeks post-discharge, characterized by an initial weight decline followed by a steady increase in a quadratic trend. At the 2-week post-discharge follow-up, infants in H-HOPE + KMC study condition showed greater early growth than infants in KMC only (mean weight = 2017.59 g vs. 1856.55 g, p = .03).ConclusionThese results suggest that the H-HOPE + KMC intervention promotes rapid early growth in preterm infants. H-HOPE + KMC is a low-cost, high-impact intervention that complements KMC. These promising pilot results provide initial evidence of the efficacy of H-HOPE + KMC in Malawi and justify the need for an adequately powered study to validate the findings and explore the long-term benefits of an integrated H-HOPE and KMC intervention.Keywords: Preterm birth, Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC), Hospital-to-Home: Optimizing the Preterm Infant Environment (H-HOPE), Early growth, Malawi

Introduction

While about 11% of newborns worldwide are born premature [1, 2], Malawi has the world’s highest prematurity rate at 18% [3]. Generally, the growth rate of preterm infants is lower than that of full-term infants in the first year of life [4]. This is influenced by physiological risks associated with preterm infants’ immature organs, which result in difficulties in extrauterine life adaptations with consequent prolonged hospitalisation, postnatal weight loss, and delayed enteral feeding [5–7]. One of the standards of care for stable preterm and low birth weight infants in Malawi is Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) [2]. Kangaroo Mother Care, established almost five decades ago, involves the placement of a preterm or low birth weight infant (wearing a diaper only) in skin-to-skin contact on the mother’s chest between the breasts [8–11]. KMC improves preterm infant survival and growth through promotion of thermoregulation, physiological stability, and breastfeeding [12–15] and substantially reduces all-cause mortality and infection in low-birthweight infants [16].

However, many surviving premature infants continue to be at high risk of poor growth and increased morbidity due to prematurity-related growth issues and developmental challenges. About 3100 preterm survivors in Malawi have developmental challenges every year [17]. These developmental challenges include cognitive, motor, and behavioural impairments [2, 18, 19]. Therefore, improving the early growth and development of surviving infants is paramount to prevent lifelong sequelae following preterm birth. Belfort and colleagues [20] reported that among infants born before 33 weeks gestational age, rapid early infant growth was associated with higher cognitive and motor development at 18 months corrected age (CA).

Although early infant interventions supporting rapid growth and neurodevelopment are important for preterm infants, Malawi has few options other than KMC. One promising intervention is H-HOPE (Hospital to-Home: Optimizing the Premature Infant Environment), which has been shown to promote growth and neurodevelopment in preterm infants [21]. The H-HOPE intervention is comprised of two components: the infant-focused component (Massage+) and the mother-focused education component, Parents+, consisting of social support through participatory guidance to provide Massage + to the infant with sensitivity to the infant’s cues [22].

In the United States (US), a randomized controlled trial (RCT) reported that the H-HOPE intervention was efficacious in promoting improved sucking, faster early weight gain, and more rapid growth in length in preterm infants over time compared to controls [23]. H-HOPE infants exhibited improved sucking organization during hospitalization, suggesting that H-HOPE improves maturation of oral feeding, a likely mechanism associated with more rapid growth and early weight gain [22–27]. No adverse events have ever occurred in any of the studies of Massage + or the earlier ATVV [28–35]. A different US study directly comparing infants receiving KMC only and those receiving Massage+ only found that infants receiving KMC only fed for shorter amounts of time than those receiving the Massage+ intervention [36]. However, to our knowledge, there is no evidence related to the efficacy of the H-HOPE intervention in promoting early infant growth in Africa, including Malawi.

This pilot study tested the H-HOPE intervention in Zomba, Malawi, from 2019 to 2020 by comparing infants receiving KMC only to those receiving H-HOPE + KMC. As a standard of care in Malawi, KMC is used on all clinically stable preterm infants to promote thermoregulation, physiological stability and breastfeeding [37]. There was a significant difference in the pilot’s primary outcome: mother-infant responsivity at 6-weeks CA, which increased substantially for infants receiving H-HOPE + KMC compared to those receiving KMC only [38]. The purpose of this report, using data from the same pilot study, was to examine whether infants receiving H-HOPE + KMC have more rapid early growth than those receiving KMC only.

Materials and methods

Study design

A prospective quasi-experimental study design with sequential recruitment of the two study conditions was used to compare early infant growth of preterm infants receivingKMC only (the control condition) and those receiving H-HOPE + KMC (the intervention condition). An experimental design with random assignment was not feasible because the KMC unit was a single room, offering no privacy. If both study conditions were present at the same time, the mothers receiving KMC only condition would be able to observe H-HOPE and possibly imitate it. Recruitment was designed using a wash-out interval long enough for all mothers in the prior study condition to be discharged. This avoided any overlap of the two study conditions [38]. The lack of privacy also meant that no blinding for staff was feasible because they could see whether mothers were providing H-HOPE + KMC.

Site

KMC is the national standard of care for all preterm infants in Malawi, so this study design and methods had to accommodate KMC unit policies. The study was conducted at the Zomba Central Hospital (ZCH) KMC unit, which admitted preterm infants who were delivered at ZCH or transferred because of their prematurity from other health centres and facilities in the Zomba district and nearby districts in the Eastern Region. Because there are very few incubators, and the KMC unit was small, stable preterm infants were moved into the KMC unit as soon as possible. Staffing in the KMC Unit included one patient attendant, one nurse-midwife for all hospitalized infants in the entire neonatal unit, and one clinical officer who conducted daily ward rounds for all hospitalized infants in the neonatal unit [38]. Physical assessments and follow-up appointments for discharged infants who received KMC were all conducted in the single KMC room.

All participants were admitted to the KMC unit, a 10-bed room with no partitions or privacy curtains. All mothers present can see and hear everything that happens, including any H-HOPE instructions and practice. No visitors are allowed in the KMC unit. Because infants on KMC generally are healthier at home than in the hospital, they are discharged once KMC care and breastfeeding are established, and the infant is stable. This often occurs within a few days after entering the KMC unit.

Sample and inclusion criteria

The sample consisted of 183 clinically stable preterm infants admitted to the KMC unit who received continuous skin-to-skin contact (KMC) with their mothers. Infant clinical stability was defined as stable temperature, not in respiratory distress, tolerating KMC position, and no signs of other major illness [38]. All preterm infants are eligible to start KMC if they weighed 1000 g or more at birth [37]. However, a few stable preterm infants weighing under 1000 g were also transferred to the KMC unit.

Infant inclusion criteria were having a birth weight between 950 and 2400 g, gestational age between 29 and 34 weeks at birth, clinically stable with no severe congenital neurological problems, and admitted to the KMC unit. Confirmation of gestational age for all infants was determined using the Modified Ballard Score [39]. All the infants in the H-HOPE + KMC intervention condition were eligible for the intervention when they reached the KMC unit, because they had to be stable and able to coordinate oral feeding by breast or spoon initially. Therefore, no infants received the intervention before it was developmentally appropriate. Mother inclusion criteria were being clinically stable, offering KMC to her infant, and being willing to participate in the study and return to the same facility for follow-up visits post-discharge.

Power considerations

The pilot study was powered for the primary hypothesis of mother-infant mutual responsivity at 6-weeks corrected age (CA). Specifically, we proposed a sample size of 89 per study condition for the detection of a group effect on mother-infant responsivity with Odds Ratio 2.10, when the proportion of high responsivity in the control condition was 0.63, assuming a compound symmetry (CS) structure, where the autocorrelation is 0.5 for the repeatedly measured responsivity. This power calculation was performed using the procedure for testing two proportions in a repeated measures design in the PASS (2023) software. For the secondary hypothesis on longitudinal infant growth, we did a post hoc power analysis using the software RMASS2 for a 2-arm longitudinal design with attrition [40]. Specifically, in a 4 timepoint 2-arm longitudinal design with 10% attrition between repeated measurements, or an overall retention rate of 72.9%, a sample size of 69 per study condition at the last time point will ensure 80% statistical power for the detection of a mean weight difference of 113 g (SD = 300 g), assuming a compound symmetry covariance structure for the repeatedly measured weight variable with between-repeated measure correlation of 0.5. Power calculations were performed controlling for a two-sided Type I error probability of 0.05.

Recruitment process

After screening for mother and infant eligibility, the mother was approached by a member of the research team who described the study and invited mothers to participate. In both the KMC-only and H-HOPE + KMC study conditions, some mothers delayed providing signed consent because they wished to consult with a relative first. Because no visitors were allowed, relatives often did not arrive until the day of discharge. All study participants provided signed informed consent before leaving the hospital.

Because the KMC unit was a single room, when mothers in the KMC unit were assigned to the H-HOPE + KMC condition, all mothers in the unit could observe the instruction and support about H-HOPE provided by one of the research team. To avoid confusion or distress, the KMC Unit leaders and the research team decided that when the mothers were assigned to H-HOPE + KMC all mothers could participate in the H-HOPE intervention, regardless of whether they had consented to be in the study. Therefore, mothers assigned to the H-HOPE + KMC study condition often began providing Massage + to their infants before they signed formal consent.

To avoid possible contamination of the control condition, our original plan was to recruit all the KMC only first and then the H-HOPE + KMC condition with a wash-out period. This period allowed all mothers in the KMC only study condition to be discharged before a new study condition began. However, the first KCM only condition, recruited from February to April 2019, had a higher-than-expected attrition rate of 37%. To ensure adequate participants in both study conditions, we modified our design. Because arrangements with the site had already been made to recruit the H-HOPE + KMC condition next, we recruited all that condition between May and September 2019. We increased enrolment to ensure an adequate number of intervention participants if the attrition rate remained high.

Finally, we recruited the remaining KMC only condition in October and November 2019. Occasionally, a mother-infant dyad in the H-HOPE + KMC study conditionreturned for a follow-up visit when the inpatient study condition was KMC only. When this happened, the staff on duty would make special arrangements for the follow-up visit to occur in a different room so that mothers assigned to the KMC only condition did not see the mother providing Massage + to her infant.

Interventions

KMC

In our study, all participants practiced continuous KMC. Mothers were encouraged to always put their infants in the KMC position except for brief occasions when they were going to the bathroom, shower, or meeting a visitor outside of the KMC unit. Mothers received hospital-provided meals within the unit. While on the unit, all infants were exclusively fed their own mother’s milk, either by breastfeeding or, for the few infants with immature sucking reflexes, by cup and spoon until breastfeeding was possible.

In the KMC unit, patient attendants supervise mothers who are typically discharged before reaching the recommended 2500 g if the infant is breathing normally, feeding well, has no major illness, and the mother is capable and understands KMC. Since most mothers are unable to weigh their infants at home, recommendations about discontinuation of KMC when the infant reaches 2500 g are made by a healthcare provider at follow-up visits.

H-HOPE + KMC

In addition to KMC, infants in the intervention study condition were given H-HOPE. H-HOPE is a behavioural intervention consisting of Massage+ twice daily and Parents+. Massage+ begins around 32 weeks post menstrual age, when infants can sustain an alert behavioural state, coordinate sucking, swallowing, and breathing, and show readiness for social interaction [22]. Massage+ begins with 30 s of auditory-only infant-focused stimuli, followed by a head-to-ankle massage for 9.5 min, and lastly, 5 min of vestibular stimulation (horizontal rocking). The auditory stimuli continue throughout the entire intervention. Visual (eye-to-eye) stimuli are offered when the infant becomes alert [41]. Massage + is offered with responsiveness to infant behavioural cues, e.g., pausing in response to infant disengagement cues [41]. No oil is used with Massage+. While in the KMC unit, mothers were given the Massage + Log and learned how to complete it every time they provided Massage + in the hospital. After hospital discharge, mothers continued to provide Massage+ twice daily and record it in the log until one-month CA. Mothers were asked to bring the Massage + Log to each post-discharge visit.

The four Parents+ sessions use a participatory guidance approach. The mother learns about her preterm infant’s behaviours and cues and receives social support [21]. Parents+ sessions one and two were adapted to be given to all mothers by the research assistants in the KMC unit, while sessions three and four occurred at the 2-week and 4-week post discharge visits respectively. Topics included providing Massage+, how to read, interpret, and respond to her infant’s cues, and signs of infant illness.

Performance of Massage + was assessed using a step-by-step Reliability Checklist previously validated in H-HOPE research [22, 23, 26]. The research team provided participatory guidance in the KMC unit until each mother achieved at least 95% reliability, which all achieved by their third attempt. Observation by the research team verified ongoing reliability at discharge and each follow-up visit. All mothers consistently maintained scores of 95% or higher, in line with previous findings [22, 23, 26]. Review of the Massage+ Logs and direct observation using the Reliability Checklist confirmed that all mothers who attended follow-up visits demonstrated continued high reliability (95%) and reported consistently providing the massage twice daily, as reflected in their logs (compliance).

Variables and operational measures

Infant characteristics included birthweight, sex, gestational age, and the presence of any health issues. Mother characteristics included age, parity, marital status, delivery mode (vaginal or Caesarean section) and education level. Infant and mother characteristics were retrieved from the medical record at baseline. Early infant growth was determined in this study using the single indicator of weight in grams, the only growth parameter routinely collected in the KMC unit. Weights were obtained at birth, the first and second day after birth, enrolment time (around day 6 after birth), discharge from the hospital (around day 7 after birth), and infant follow up, around 3 weeks after birth which was around 2-weeks post-discharge (Table 1). The research team weighed the infants in both arms of the study using an electronic infant scale (EBSH 20). The research team calibrated the electronic scale every time it was used by verifying that the scale registered 0 when nothing was being weighed. Weights were recorded in whole grams.

Procedures

Research team and training

The research team consisted of three part-time individuals, a patient attendant, nursing and midwifery officer (both working in the KMC unit), a nurse-midwife technician, and the investigator who is also the corresponding author. The investigator trained the team about research ethics and the consent process. Then the team learned how to administer the two components of H-HOPE. Finally, the team also learned how to conduct the post-discharge evaluations for all preterm infants and their mothers. Members of the research team were trained using a standardized guide for H-HOPE. Fidelity of the research team teaching Parents + was monitored by the investigator using return demonstration strategies during training and periodically during Parents+ sessions in the KMC unit.

Procedure for the study conditions

For both study conditions, all infants received KMC. The procedure was as follows: (1) after delivery, the preterm infant was weighed and assessed; and (2) once the infant was stable, both mother and infant were transferred to the KMC unit; and (3) KMC began. For the H-HOPE + KMC dyads, mothers in the H-HOPE + KMC condition participated in the Parents+ sessions and were taught how to perform Massage + by the research team. A member of the research team was available in the KMC unit daily to provide education and support regarding H-HOPE. For mothers in both study conditions, mothers who were interested in the research completed the informed consent process. These mothers returned for the 2-week post-discharge appointment. The 2-week appointment included a regular infant check-up that included weight and wellness checks.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were hypothesis-driven and conducted using SAS (Statistical Analysis Software), Version 9.4 [42]. Mother and infant characteristics were used as covariates in the regression. These included gestational age at birth, infant sex, mode of delivery, whether the infant had a non-severe health problem at birth, mother’s parity, marital status, education level, and mother’s age. Summary statistics of mother and infant characteristics were reported by study condition (H-HOPE + KMC vs. KMC) in Table 3. We used Chi-squares or t-tests to identify potential study condition differences in baseline characteristics. Mean infant weights over time were analyzed using t-tests comparing condition differences at each time point.

Multivariate analyses of infant weights to identify condition differences over time were conducted using mixed-effects regression models (MRM). Time was treated as a continuous variable, allowing infants to be measured on different days and the estimation of polynomial time effects. Because our primary focus is the intervention’s effect on early infant growth, interactions between time trend terms and condition were our focus of inference.

Model selection for the MRM analysis was conducted in three steps. First, to model the correlation structure for the repeatedly measured infant weight, we used likelihood ratio tests. This determined the number of random effects, such as random intercept, linear time trend, and quadratic time trend. In this step, all mother and infant characteristics, intervention condition, time effects up to quadratic form, and group-by-time interactions were included in the fixed effects model. Secondly, a backward selection method was employed to identify significant mother and infant baseline characteristics. Lastly, backward eliminations of insignificant higher-order polynomial terms and their group interaction terms were conducted for the identification of mean shape of change in infant weight over time and longitudinal study condition effect. In steps 2 and 3, the number of random effects was the same as it was determined in step 1. Residual diagnostics were conducted to assure model assumptions were being met.

Since MRMs use the full likelihood estimation method, the missing data mechanism was assumed to be Missing at Random (MAR) [43]. We compared all mother and infant characteristics between infants with and without missing data using t-tests and Chi-squared tests. These tests revealed comparability between infants with and without missing data (Table 2). All statistical tests were two-sided, controlling for Type I error probability of 0.05.

Results

The response rate at the 2-weeks post-discharge visit was 72% for KMC only and 73% for the H-HOPE + KMC condition.

Sample characteristics

Although this was a study without random assignment, there were no significant differences in infant or mother characteristics between the KMC only and the H-HOPE + KMC conditions (Table 3). Most of the mothers in the study had completed primary education. The average gestational age in the two study conditions was around 33 weeks. The study sample included slightly more female infants (57.45% vs. 49.44%) and more primiparas in the H-HOPE + KMC than in the KMC only condition. The KMC only condition experienced more non-severe health problems (8.99% vs. 4.26%) compared to H-HOPE + KMC condition (Table 3).

Mothers reported 15 deaths in this study,11 (12.36%) among those in the KMC only condition and 4 among those in the H-HOPE + KMC condition (4.26%). However, only four of these deaths occurred before discharge. Only these four deaths had an exact date and cause of death provided in the medical record, and only three more occurred before the first post-discharge visit. Although this difference is statistically significant, infant death was not a study aim, and our sequential recruitment in this small pilot did not occur during the same seasons for the two study conditions, making interpretation difficult.

Compliance to the H-HOPE intervention

Compliance to providing Massage+ twice daily until 6-weeks CA was determined by reviewing the Massage Log which mothers assigned to the H-HOPE + KMC study condition kept in the KMC unit and at home until the 6-week CA visit. The log is self-reported and there is no way to verify the accuracy of the mother’s self-report. However, the mother’s conversation about the massage was consistent with reported compliance. For the 69 mothers who returned for the 2-week post-discharge visit, we calculated the number of times mothers recorded giving Massage+. A total of 2,524 sessions of Massage+ were given to the infants in the H-HOPE + KMC intervention condition, with each infant given a minimum of 25 and maximum of 60 sessions at the 2-week post-discharge visit. Only five reported that they administered Massage+ only once instead of twice a day. These records indicated that most of the infants received Massage+ regularly at the recommended frequency of twice a day.

Growth

Baseline birthweight was not significantly different between the two conditions (Table 4). In this study, only two infants in the KMC only and three in the H-HOPE + KMC condition were under 1,000 g. To examine early infant growth over time, infant weights were evaluated whenever possible by the nurses from birth to 2 weeks post-discharge. On average, there were 4 repeatedly measured infant weights (range 2–6) over time (Table 4). All infants showed the expected pattern of early weight loss after birth, followed by weight gain. From two-sample t-tests at each time point, out of the six weight measures taken, only the 2-week post-discharge follow-up review was significantly higher for infants in the H-HOPE + KMC condition (2017.59 g) compared with infants in the KMC only condition (1856.55 g).

Table 4; Fig. 1 show the mean weights for both study conditions at each time point using all available weight data. At birth, the mean birthweight for infants in the H-HOPE + KMC condition was 82.9 g more than in the KMC only condition (p = .07). This difference was not statistically significant, yet might represent a clinical significance. However, this difference is only about a quarter of a standard deviation of birthweight. The two study conditions showed a similar trajectory of early weight loss between Day 1 and Day 5 (average day of signing consent to participate in the study). Infants receiving H-HOPE + KMC then had modest weight increases from Day 5 to Day 7 (average day of discharge). Infants receiving KMC only had a more rapid increase in weight between Day 5 and Day 7, allowing them to be discharged on Day 7. For infants assigned to both conditions, weight increased regularly between discharge and the first clinic visit at two weeks. Discharge meant a substantial reduction in exposure to the sound, light, and general stress in the KMC Unit for both infants and mothers.

Each additional gestational week increased an estimate of 71.35 g weight. Infants born via caesarean section were 193.74 g heavier than those from vaginal delivery (Table 5). All other covariates were not significant predictors of weight in the final model. This included infant sex; although male sex is associated with more morbidities [44] and there were a few more male infants in the KMC only condition. The difference was not significant at baseline and was dropped from the final model.

Discussion

This prospective quasi-experimental study compared early growth for 183 clinically stable preterm infants in Malawi who, although not randomly assigned, had similar baseline characteristics. Mother-preterm infant dyads in the control condition received KMC only, while those in the intervention condition also received H-HOPE, an early behavioural intervention which promotes preterm infant growth and neurodevelopment (H-HOPE + KMC). Both study conditions experienced a typical weight loss pattern in the immediate post-birth period, and their baseline characteristics were similar. At the 2-week post-discharge visit, the H-HOPE + KMC infants had significantly greater early growth, with a mean between-groups difference of 161 g. After the second clinic visit, by 6-weeks CA, the KMC only infants gradually caught up with the H-HOPE + KMC infants and differences in growth were not significant.

This more rapid early growth is consistent with two randomized controlled trials from the USA [23] and Colombia [45] which showed that infants receiving H-HOPE (earlier called the ATVV) versus controls, experienced more rapid growth both in weight and length compared to infants who did not receive the intervention [23, 45–47]. Similar outcomes are reported in studies using other infant massage techniques [48–51]. In a previous publication using the same data, infants in the H-HOPE + KMC study condition also had increased mother-infant responsivity at 6-weeks CA, the earliest time when the responsivity measure is validated [38]. This greater responsivity is a likely contributor to the increased infant early growth and is consistent with previous findings in US studies [23].

An important consideration is whether the observed difference in early infant growth for the intervention group is clinically meaningful. Between discharge and two weeks post-discharge, infants receiving H-HOPE + KMC gained an average of 161 g more than infants receiving KMC only. Although few studies provide estimates of average weight gain for preterm infants, a frequently used clinical standard for preterm infants weighing over 2 kg is a weight gain expectation of 20–30 g per day [52], suggesting that a 161 g difference in weight is clinically significant. Early weight gain in preterm infants is associated with better neurodevelopmental outcomes [20].

Previous studies suggest several mechanisms through which H-HOPE may promote infant growth [23, 53]. The maternally administered Massage+ component provides developmentally appropriate multisensory behavioural stimulation and enhances infants’ social interaction skills compared to KMC only [32]. The H-HOPE sessions help mothers recognize and respond to infant cues, leading to more effective interactions. Evidence from US-based clinical trial data suggest that improvements in sucking organization following Massage+ lead to greater alertness and feeding readiness and reduce infant fatigue, which may explain the observed early weight gain [22–25, 29]. Additionally, mothers receiving HOPE + KMC may experience a smoother transition from hospital to home, because H-HOPE may have increased their confidence and awareness while interacting with their infants.

Integrating H-HOPE with KMC significantly improved infant early growth among preterm infants in Malawi, a benefit likely linked to their complementary roles. KMC supports critical physiological needs of preterm infants [9, 11, 13, 54], while H-HOPE supports the social and interactive aspects, behavioural maturation, and feeding readiness [32, 34, 53, 55–57]. The combined effects of complementary advantages could have large impacts in settings like Malawi, South Africa and other LMICs, where KMC is the standard of care and continuously practiced. Since early infant growth is associated with improved neurodevelopment, an integrated intervention may offer a resource-efficient approach to improve outcomes of preterm infants [58, 59]. In settings where KMC is practiced intermittently, the combined impact of H-HOPE + KMC may not yield these synergistic effects.

Consistent with previous clinical trials with H-HOPE (previously named the ATVV) [23, 26, 32, 35, 57], our data support the conclusion that the H-HOPE intervention does not increase the risk of harm or adverse outcomes. The number of infant deaths was high, but the rate was comparable to a large Malawian study [60], and there was no evidence that H-HOPE contributed to adverse outcomes. Although infants receiving KMC only had a higher number of deaths compared with those receiving H-HOPE + KMC, this was not a primary focus of our study. Factors potentially impacting this finding, such as seasonality were not the same for the two study conditions, making interpretation of results difficult.

Strengths

A major strength of this study was its flexible, ethically sensitive approach to the unique conditions of the KMC unit. As described earlier, lack of privacy made random assignment impractical due to likely contamination between study conditions. To address this, we adopted sequential recruitment with a wash-out period long enough to ensure that the control condition was not exposed to the intervention. Also, when mothers in the KMC unit were all assigned to the H-HOPE + KMC study condition, the staff determined that these mothers, who all saw other mothers providing H-HOPE, should be allowed to participate in H-HOPE regardless of whether they had signed the consent. Requiring consent before participation in H-HOPE might have been perceived as coercive. These accommodations meant that most mothers assigned to the H-HOPE + KMC study condition began learning about and providing H-HOPE shortly after admission to the KMC unit, giving mothers more time to develop confidence about providing Massage+ before discharge. Importantly, this adaptation meant that the date of enrolment was not a reliable indicator of when the intervention began.

The strict no-visitors policy meant that few family members visited until just prior to discharge, making it difficult for mothers who wanted to discuss being in the research with family before signing the consent. To accommodate this policy and the ethical issue of not allowing discussion with family members before giving consent, mothers were allowed to enrol in the study as late as the day of discharge. This opportunity to consult with families may have increased mothers’ willingness to participate in the study. These accommodations to the KMC unit made the study unusual. However, our approach retained the advantages of prospective recruitment and ensured all participants received care in the same setting. We did not find evidence of any biases related to these adaptations to the KMC unit.

Other strengths included the highly trained research team, the high fidelity with which the adapted research protocol was carried out and high compliance by study participants to study requirements. In addition to the PI, the research team included three workers in the KMC unit hired to carry out the day-to-day research tasks in the KMC unit, a strength because they combined high familiarity with the research protocol and with the KMC unit and the mothers and infants it served. The observation of mothers when providing Massage+ documented high reliability and the Massage + log documented high compliance to providing it twice daily.

Limitations

Like most pilot studies, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power and generalizability of the results. Missing weight data relates to the fact that daily weighing is not routine in this setting, a problem that likely affected the accuracy and completeness of growth trajectories. Comparison of cases with and without missing data found no significant differences between the study conditions in their maternal or infant background characteristics. However, the overall impact of missingness is uncertain.

Due to the lack of random assignment, there may be potential imbalance and biases in the results. One imbalance in particular was birthweight which, although not statistically significant (p = .07), might have been clinically significant. However, the difference in mean birthweight of 82.9 g more in the H-HOPE + KMC condition compared with the KMC Only condition is only about a quarter of a standard deviation of birthweight, the birthweights for both study conditions fall between the 48th and 57th percentile [61] and diverged from the median by only 10%. Thus this difference is relatively small and not likely to be clinically significant. However, due to the lack of random assignment, the possibility of bias cannot be ruled out.

Although research staff were regularly present on the KMC unit throughout the study and did not observe major differences in KMC unit procedures, the lack of random assignment increased the risk of bias from unmeasured temporal or contextual factors. Allowing mothers to practice H-HOPE before formal consent could mean that mothers who found satisfaction in providing H-HOPE were more likely to enrol in the study than if they had been randomly assigned.

Overall, these limitations suggest that the results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies need to be adequately powered, include multiple sites, and ensure balanced designs to confirm these results and support the future scalability of H-HOPE in Malawi and other LMICs.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the combined effects of H-HOPE and KMC in low-resource settings. These pilot findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size, quasi-experimental design and high missing weight data. However, the results suggest that this combined intervention supports greater early weight gain in preterm infants in addition to previously identified improvements in mother-infant interactions and responsivity when compared with KMC only [38]., The effects of H-HOPE are complementary with KMC, which provides critical physiological stability, continuous breastfeeding, and maternal closeness [16].

For countries that support round-the-clock KMC, integrating H-HOPE would be a feasible, low cost, mother-driven and value-added approach to incorporating infant behavioural development into preterm infant care. Our adaptation, which also included group teaching within the KMC unit, shows that staff-led support for H-HOPE can be effectively implemented without requiring major changes to personnel or the addition of sizeable resources. However, because KMC is practiced with varying intensity across different contexts, this synergistic effect may not be present in countries that do not practice round-the-clock KMC. More research is needed to assess the impact of H-HOPE + KMC in diverse settings.

Our pilot suggests that integrating H-HOPE with KMC may help more preterm infants to not only survive but also thrive, by supporting optimal early growth and the development of stronger mother-infant relationships. The H-HOPE + KMC model represents a practical, mother-driven, low-cost strategy solution with the potential to improve both physical growth and developmental outcomes for preterm infants, particularly in resource-limited health systems. Adequately powered and multi-site trials are needed to confirm these promising results.

Definitions

Corrected Age (CA): chronological age of infant reduced by the number of weeks born before 40 weeks of gestation.

Motherese: the simplified and repetitive type of speech, with exaggerated intonation and rhythm, often used by adults when speaking to infants.

Postmenstrual age (PMA): the time elapsed between the first day of the last menstrual period and birth (gestational age) plus the time elapsed after birth (chronological age).

Nursing and midwifery officer: a professional trained in nursing and midwifery with a minimum of a degree.

Nurse-midwife technician: a worker with less training duration than nursing officers. This cadre was created in response to the severe shortage of professional nursing and midwifery staff.

Patient attendant: a worker belonging to a lay cadre who provides clinical support services and performs some of the same functions and tasks as professional health care workers, but who lack the same level of formal education and training as well as government registration.
