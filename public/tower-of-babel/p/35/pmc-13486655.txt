Machine learning- assisted remaining useful lifetime prediction of power electronic converters
Authors: Hussain Sayed, Harish S. Krishnamoorthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a methodology for the remaining useful life (RUL) prediction in power electronic converters based on the health monitoring of semiconductor devices and DC capacitor(s). The primary components considered are Gallium Nitride High Electron Mobility Transistors (GaN HEMTs) and aluminum electrolytic capacitors (AECs). The proposed methodology leverages long-term component characterization under accelerated aging testing in a laboratory setup. A statistical approach based on uniform probability density functions (PDFs) is utilized to estimate the system-level probability of survival under the desired operating conditions. Given that the PDFs and system-level probability calculations involve taking integrals and other complex operations, a machine learning (ML)-assisted model is utilized to reduce the computational burden on the controller units in power electronics converters. A neural network (NN) is used to process the experimentally derived degradation data and the extracted PDFs for arriving at a simple model presented by a few matrices that can even be deployed on modern microcontrollers or field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) for in-situ implementation. Experimental results obtained using a laboratory-scale prototype show that the proposed data-driven approach can achieve accuracy levels higher than 99% in predicting the time evolution between degradation checkpoints under accelerated thermal cycling conditions. This validation confirms the model’s consistency with established statistical approaches across the full reliability range (T99–T01). Hence, this approach can identify aged or potentially failing converters in-situ and avoid early decommissioning, thereby extending the operational life.

Introduction

Semiconductor devices and DC capacitors are critical components in power converters and typically, the most failure-prone elements at the system level. Power devices are routinely subjected to mechanical, electrical, and thermal stress, all of which contribute to increased risk of converter failure1. In mission-critical applications—such as data centers, aviation systems, and downhole tools— component failures can cause system-wide shutdowns, leading to substantial operational losses due to unplanned power supply interruptions. Recent surveys indicate that power semiconductor device failures account for over 35% of failures in industrial power converter applications1–3, significantly affecting both system reliability and operational costs4,5.

Understanding the intrinsic degradation mechanisms of these components is therefore essential for improving the reliability and availability of next-generation power electronic systems. Among advanced technologies, wide bandgap (WBG) semiconductor devices—including silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN) field-effect transistors—enable highly efficient, compact, and fast-switching power converters. SiC MOSFETs, in particular, have been widely studied for their ruggedness and high-temperature operation, with extensive research conducted on their aging behavior and failure precursors under accelerated stress conditions6,9.

As GaN technology adoption expands, particularly in lower-power applications of a few kilowatts or less such as data centers and consumer electronics, unique reliability concerns have emerged. The GaN high electron mobility transistor (HEMT) structure is susceptible to hot-electron injection during switching transitions and charge trapping in the off-state. These effects reduce free carrier density in the two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) channel, leading to increased dynamic and static on-state resistance (RDSon). As the RDSon rises, conduction losses grow, exacerbating thermal stress and potentially causing faster device failure10,11.

Accelerated thermal cycling (ATC) is a widely adopted reliability characterization method for power FETs12,13. During ATC testing, the junction of the device under test (DUT) is subjected to repeated temperature excursions above and below ambient over thousands of cycles, enabling the investigation of long-term degradation processes. In DC-powered thermal cycling, self-heating is induced by injecting current through the DUT during active periods, followed by passive cooling phases—resulting in a controlled temperature swing. A similar procedure is used with AC-powered thermal cycling, where both conduction and switching losses contribute to device heating.

For capacitors, numerous studies have proposed precise laboratory methods for measuring equivalent series resistance (ESR) and capacitance as indicators of aging14–20. However, these approaches are typically confined to developmental environments due to their complexity and measurement overhead. In-situ implementations face significant challenges, particularly in accurately resolving AC ripple voltages, currents, and harmonic phase angles in real time.

Recent advancements in machine learning and data modeling, physics-based and analytical approaches for remaining useful lifetime prediction of power electronics components at both component and system levels are gaining significant attention in academia and industry. In21, a physics-informed neural network was employed for RUL estimation of discrete power electronics devices. This research incorporated physics-based constraints, which led to significant improvements in the reliability and accuracy of neural network-based RUL predictions for power electronic devices. Intelligent long-term performance monitoring of power electronics systems is presented in22. A nonparametric surrogate model for predicting converter lifetime was proposed based on limited nonlinear data from theoretical reliability analysis. A Correlation-Driven Neural Network (CDNN) that captures mutual interactions between feature candidates affecting solder joint reliability was proposed for evaluating solder joint failures under thermo-mechanical processes in electronic devices23. Modern power electronics systems encounter challenges in terms of reliable planning and operation due to the proliferation of power converters24–26. Artificial intelligence (AI)-based power electronic systems with self-awareness and self-adaptability capabilities are overviewed in27, where opportunities for AI applications in power electronics are explored. Furthermore, the practical importance and industrial applicability of machine learning-based lifetime prediction in power electronics has not been thoroughly explored, particularly highlighting the need for efficient algorithms that can operate in real-world industrial environments28–30.

Despite extensive research on component-level degradation and accelerated testing, a critical gap remains in developing practical system-level reliability assessment methods that integrate real-world operational conditions with experimental qualification data. Existing reliability models often rely on complex electro-thermal simulations or detailed component data, which limit their applicability for in-situ monitoring and real-time prediction.

This paper proposes an end-to-end framework that bridges component-level degradation studies with system-level reliability assessment. A GaN-based H-bridge, operated as a DC–DC converter, is employed to replicate real-world operating conditions. The H-bridge hardware is used to estimate the survival index—representing the probability of system-level survival and remaining useful life (RUL)—based on experimental qualification data of GaN devices and aluminum electrolytic capacitors. This approach enables in-situ reliability monitoring and real-time lifetime prediction, thereby supporting predictive maintenance and enhancing the robustness of next-generation power electronic systems. An overview of the research methodology for GaN FET, capacitor and system-level reliability testing, modeling, and system-level RUL estimation and validation is presented in Fig. 1.

The framework begins with reliability characterization testing (using thermal cycling) of GaN HEMTs and aluminum electrolytic capacitors under accelerated aging conditions, monitoring key parameters including RDSon, losses, junction temperature, and capacitor impedance characteristics. The collected degradation data undergoes performance analysis and modeling to extract activation energy and thermal behavior through long-term testing processes. Mathematical equations are then utilized to estimate component-level RUL and probability density functions, enabling the calculation of system-level probability of survival based on experimental datasets. To overcome the computational complexity of these mathematical models for real-time implementation, a neural network model is developed and trained using the experimental data, resulting in a lightweight solution comprising matrix operations suitable for deployment on microcontroller and FPGA. The trained ANN model is subsequently implemented in a GaN-based H-bridge DC-DC converter for in-situ health monitoring, providing real-time RUL estimation ranging from T99 (0.99 probability of survival) to T01 (0.01 probability of survival), thereby enabling predictive maintenance and enhanced system reliability and availability.

Component-level characterization

More than 15 enhancement-mode (e-mode) GaN HEMTs with integrated gate drivers were subjected to long-term degradation testing under varied power and thermal cycling profiles, as detailed in previous studies31. To emulate practical deployment scenarios and further investigate component degradation patterns, a GaN-based H-bridge converter topology was developed. This configuration was selected due to its prevalence in real-world power conversion applications, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

A H-bridge converter testbed also enables impedance monitoring of an aluminum electrolytic capacitor operating at elevated ambient temperatures. Devices under test (DUTs) are operated using a pulse-width modulation (PWM) scheme with an active current control approach, as detailed in32. In this setup, the H-bridge performs DC-DC conversion, where the DC load current flows continuously to heat the GaN devices during defined thermal cycling intervals. An inductor connects the midpoint of both H-bridge legs, enabling current recirculation and minimizing energy losses in the long-term degradation testing process.

The junction temperature—which governs thermal stress and aging—is closely tied to conduction losses arising from the RMS load current. Notably, no heat sinks are used, and the switching frequency is limited to 2 kHz (as specified in Table 1) to minimize the cumulative contribution of switching-stress-induced trapping to the observed RDSon drift. It is acknowledged that hot-electron trapping and charge accumulation effects in GaN HEMTs are primarily governed by high peak drain voltages and high voltage slew rates (dV/dt) during switching transitions, rather than switching frequency per se6,11,33,34. Specifically, hot-electron injection into trap states in the AlGaN barrier layer and GaN buffer has been shown to reduce free carrier density in the 2DEG channel, contributing to dynamic RDSon increase — a phenomenon commonly referred to as current collapse6. Furthermore, charge trapping under high drain-source voltages during the off-state, particularly near the gate edge where electric field crowding occurs, can produce soft degradation signatures in RDSon even before permanent damage occurs11. By operating at a low switching frequency of 2 kHz with a DC-link voltage of 250 V, the number of high-field switching transitions per unit time is minimized, thereby reducing — though not entirely eliminating — the contribution of electrically-driven trapping mechanisms to the measured RDSon drift. The RDSon parameter monitored in this study therefore reflects the aggregate effect of both thermally-driven permanent degradation and any residual trapping-related contributions, without individual separation of these mechanisms. Future work incorporating dynamic RDSon measurements will aim to decouple these contributions for a more complete characterization of the degradation mechanisms at play. The junction temperature is estimated indirectly through online monitoring of on-state resistance (RDSon). It is noted that the measured RDSon integrates contributions from both thermally-driven permanent degradation and electrically-driven soft degradation — including charge trapping induced by voltage transients during switching6,11. Hot-electron trapping and surface/buffer charge accumulation near the gate edge, which are well-established degradation mechanisms in GaN HEMTs6, can contribute to soft degradation signatures in RDSon through high voltage peaks during switching transitions33, and this contribution cannot be entirely excluded from the observed degradation trends in this study. The RDSon health indicator therefore captures the aggregate degradation state of the device without individual separation of thermally-driven and electrically-driven mechanisms11,34.

It is important to clarify the junction temperature estimation methodology employed in this study. The primary junction temperature feedback signal used for thermal cycling control is derived indirectly from the measured RDSon value, which exhibits a well-characterized and monotonic dependence on junction temperature in GaN HEMTs. This electrically-derived temperature estimate is inherently representative of the active device junction region and does not suffer from the thermal resistance offset associated with surface-mounted temperature sensors. The thermocouples attached to the device package serve as a supplementary monitoring tool to verify thermal cycling window boundaries and detect anomalous thermal behavior, rather than as the primary junction temperature measurement. The RDSon -based junction temperature estimation therefore implicitly incorporates the combined effect of self-heating and ambient temperature variations, and may be influenced by changes in ambient conditions during long-term testing.

To accelerate capacitor degradation, the capacitor under test is wrapped with a heating pad to maintain elevated case temperature. In-situ monitoring circuitry, adapted from31, captures the on-state drain-source voltage and drain current for each DUT. These values are used to calculate RDSon and estimate junction temperature, enabling real-time adjustment of the thermal cycling window. Further analysis of temperature tracking is available in32.

The testbed comprises four 650 V / 7.5 A GaN HEMTs with integrated drivers. Each device is instrumented with thermocouples and voltage/current sensors for continuous online health monitoring. Control and data acquisition are performed via a MicroLabBox DS1102 (dSPACE), executing custom control algorithms and long-term data logging. The experimental platform is fully automated for months-long degradation studies, following the operational flow detailed in32. Key hardware specifications are provided in Table 1.

Characterization of GaN HEMTs’ degradation

The health monitoring strategy for GaN HEMTs is based on continuous in-situ tracking of the on-state drain-source resistance (RDSon) as the primary degradation indicator. The RDSon is selected as the health indicator because it directly reflects the condition of the two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) channel — the core active region of the GaN HEMT structure. As degradation progresses through mechanisms such as hot-electron trapping, charge accumulation, and thermomechanical stress, the free carrier density in the 2DEG channel diminishes, resulting in a measurable and monotonic increase in RDSon. This progressive drift in RDSon therefore serves as a reliable and non-invasive proxy for the overall degradation state of the device.

The RDSon is extracted in real time by measuring the on-state drain-source voltage (VDSon) and the corresponding drain current (IDS) during device operation, according to:

The junction temperature — which governs the rate of thermal stress and aging — is estimated indirectly from the measured RDSon value, exploiting the well-known temperature dependence of RDSon in GaN HEMTs. This indirect junction temperature estimation eliminates the need for invasive thermal sensors at the device junction, enabling a practical and non-intrusive monitoring approach suitable for in-situ implementation.

The online monitoring circuitry captures VDSon and IDS for each device under test (DUT) continuously throughout the thermal cycling process. These measurements are acquired via the dSPACE MicroLabBox DS1102 data acquisition system, which also executes the thermal cycling control algorithm in real time. The control scheme adjusts the load current to maintain the junction temperature within the defined thermal cycling window (25 °C to 180 °C), using the RDSon -derived temperature estimate as the feedback signal. This closed-loop monitoring and control architecture ensures consistent and repeatable thermal stress application across all devices under test, enabling reliable long-term degradation characterization.

The degradation behavior of GaN HEMTs was evaluated through in-situ monitoring of device parameters under repeated thermal cycling. During a typical thermal cycle, where the junction temperature is swept from 25 °C to 180 °C, the measured drain–source voltage (VDSon) and drain current (IDS) waveforms reveal distinct operational characteristics under pulse-width modulation (PWM) control. The thermal cycling process employs a controlled load current to reach a target current level, as detailed in22. The input current, which is approximately one-tenth of the load current, primarily compensates for minimal losses in the circulating current loop (load inductor losses and FETs’ losses). At a typical 3 A load current, the input current remains around 250 mA to 300 mA, substantially reducing total energy consumption for long-duration testing. The ON-state resistance, RDSon, calculated by dividing the VDSon by the corresponding load RMS current (i.e., IDS), demonstrates significant temperature dependence – which was online monitored to control the thermal cycling windows. A dSPACE unit was used to implement the control scheme as well as data acquisition unit. The thermal cycling testing operated for approximately 140 h of accumulated heating time, corresponding to ~ 10,000 thermal cycles with each cycle consisting of ~ 50 s of heating time and ~ 150 s of cooling time. Preliminary results were presented in22 and further evaluations are presented in the subsequent sections.

The RDSon measurement accuracy is governed by the resolution and noise floor of the voltage and current sensing circuits. The 16-bit ADC resolution of the dSPACE MicroLabBox DS1102 provides a theoretical voltage resolution sufficient to resolve millivolt-level VDSon variations at the operating current levels used in this study. The load current of 2.5 A (DC) produces a VDSon in the range of 450–575 mV across the degradation window (180–230 mΩ), providing a favorable signal-to-noise ratio for RDSon extraction. The 10 mΩ shunt resistor used for current sensing produces a voltage drop of approximately 25 mV at the nominal load current, which is within the measurable range of the data acquisition system. To minimize the influence of noise and measurement artifacts, RDSon values are computed from averaged waveform data over multiple switching cycles rather than from instantaneous single-point measurements. Both RDSon and VDSon measurements are periodically validated offline using a Keysight curve tracer and an oscilloscope with a low-voltage-range differential probe, performed every few hundred thermal cycles.

Characterization of aluminum electrolytic capacitor degradation

Capacitance and equivalent series resistance (ESR) values are crucial capacitor health indicators. For aluminum electrolytic capacitors (AECs), a 20% reduction in capacitance is conventionally considered the end of useful life (EOL), while a 50% to 100% increase in ESR also indicates EOL, making ESR a robust health indicator. In this study, the ripple content in the DC-link capacitor’s voltage and current waveforms was measured throughout the degradation process, as elaborated in the subsequent sections. The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) technique is employed to extract the dominant frequency spectrum in the current and voltage waveforms, enabling capacitor parameter calculation. For simplified in-situ health monitoring, this research proposes a straightforward method to monitor capacitor impedance based on voltage and current peaks, independent of frequency content. The results demonstrate a potentially valuable and practical solution for in-situ AEC aging monitoring.

The experimental setup utilizes the same configuration described previously for the degradation testing of an individual AEC (DC-link capacitor) connected to the GaN-based H-bridge converter. The H-bridge operation follows the aging process detailed in22. However, the thermal cycle time is extended to approximately 90 s of heating and 50 s of cooling for the GaN devices, ensuring operation within the thermal cycling window of 25 °C to 180 °C. During the cooling period of the GaN devices, the capacitor’s surface temperature is maintained at approximately 90 °C, rising to about 97 °C due to the capacitor’s self-heating during the GaN device heating phase.

The test capacitor (part number: UPH2W121MHD6) has a capacitance of 120 µF, a rated voltage of 450 V, and a ripple current rating of 800 mA(rms) at 105 °C and 120 Hz. According to the capacitor’s datasheet, the maximum dissipation factor (tan δ) is 0.15 at 400 V, 120 Hz, and 20 °C, resulting in a calculated ESR of 0.17 Ω. It is important to note that this ESR value is valid only at the specified frequency, temperature, and operating conditions.

During degradation testing, the capacitor’s voltage and current ripple contents are measured following the schematic shown in Fig. 3, with results depicted in Fig. 4. The capacitor impedance parameter extraction scheme includes bandpass filter to capture the ripple contents of the frequencies of interest. The waveforms are captured using a Tektronix oscilloscope (model: 5 Series B MSO) with 16-bit ADC and high-resolution data capture mode. The experimental data are processed using MATLAB tools. An inductor is placed between the main DC power supply and the DC-link capacitor to ensure that the converter ripple current is supplied exclusively by the capacitor. This configuration allows the main DC power supply to deliver nearly continuous DC current, as depicted in Fig. 4(a).

The capacitor’s voltage and current waveforms are measured using a resistor voltage divider circuit and a small shunt current resistor (10 mΩ), respectively, as shown in Fig. 4(b) and (c). The capacitor operates at 250 V DC (average) with a ripple current of 700 mA(rms) at an ambient temperature of 90 °C. To achieve this elevated ambient temperature for the capacitor, the device is wrapped with a heating pad (with heat controlled by adjusting the DC current supplied to the pad), and the temperature is monitored using a thermistor attached to the capacitor case.

In this research, two capacitor’s parameters (using ESR and C, or Z directly) extraction methods are explored to identify an applicable approach for in-situ components health monitoring for real-world applications, as presented below.

Capacitor parameters extraction method-1

The first method employs a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) applied to the voltage and current ripple content to extract frequency domain information and calculate the corresponding capacitor impedance at the dominant frequencies. Since the converter operates at a 2 kHz switching frequency, the dominant frequencies observed at the capacitor are 4 kHz and 8 kHz, corresponding to the second and fourth harmonics of the switching frequency. Bandpass filters (BPFs) are utilized to isolate these dominant frequency components, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The complete data processing scheme, which includes FFT, BPF, and subsequent computational operations, is implemented digitally. The voltage and current sample dataset used for extracting the corresponding frequency domain components comprises approximately 30 million data points captured over a 70-second measurement period. The measured voltage and current ripple content, along with their corresponding frequency spectrum, are presented in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively. Based on this analysis, the impedance is calculated at the dominant frequencies of 4 kHz and 8 kHz.

Measuring the voltage across and current through the critical capacitor using low-cost sense resistors provides a practical implementation approach. Capacitor parameters extraction method-2

The second method provides a simplified approach for impedance extraction suitable for in-situ health monitoring applications. Since capacitance (C) decreases and ESR increases with capacitor aging, these changes result in a consistent increase in impedance (Z) values, providing a direct indicator of capacitor health degradation. This method involves calculating the peak voltages and currents from the ripple content, from which the corresponding impedance values can be determined. The variation in impedance over time can then be characterized to assess the capacitor’s health condition. Compared to Method-1, this approach requires significantly less data processing, utilizing approximately 1 million data points versus the 30 million data sets required for the FFT-based method.

The peak values of the voltage and current ripple content are filtered with a digital low pass filter (where the cutoff frequency is 50 kHz) and then derived by calculating the mean value of the ripple envelope, as depicted in Fig. 6. The impedance is then calculated using these mean ripple values without requiring detailed frequency domain analysis or spectral decomposition. Both methods incorporate DC component removal from the voltage and current waveforms to isolate the AC ripple content. Experimental validation demonstrates that both methods reveal similar patterns in capacitor impedance variation with aging. However, Method-1 is recommended for applications requiring more precise impedance, capacitance (C), and ESR measurements due to its frequency-selective analysis capability. Remaining useful lifetime (RUL) extraction

Components-Level

Power electronic converters comprise various critical components, including power field-effect transistors (FETs), capacitors, and inductors. While system-level RUL represents the reliability is affected by all components, this study focuses on the reliability characterization of the e-mode GaN HEMTs and aluminum electrolytic capacitor (AEC) as the most failure-prone components in power converters. The failure criteria for these components are defined based on established degradation thresholds. GaN FETs are considered degraded when their drain-source on-resistance (RDSon) reaches 150% of the initial value, while the AEC is deemed degraded when its impedance (Z) increases to 200% of the initial value. These thresholds represent the end-of-life (EoL) conditions for prognostic analysis.

Experimental degradation data from31 demonstrates that ten GaN FETs subjected to ATC exhibit temperature-dependent degradation rates. Devices exposed to higher temperatures (200 °C and 180 °C) degrade faster than those subjected to lower temperatures (150 °C). The RDSon measurements are conducted at 25 °C and collected at various aging stages throughout the thermal cycling process. Raw data for RDSon degradation under 150 °C maximum thermal cycling conditions reveals a characteristic aging pattern: devices initially maintain relatively stable RDSon values during an incubation period before exhibiting progressive degradation with accumulated heating time under specific temperature stress conditions.

Based on the accumulated accelerated aging datasets presented in31, the degradation behavior is modeled using a first-order exponential function (i.e., RDSon = a*exp(bt)), where t represents the accumulated heating time over thousands of thermal cycles, as illustrated in Fig. 9. The device failure threshold is set at RDSon = 300 mΩ, corresponding to 150% of the initial value.

The RUL for each component at any given time can be calculated as the temporal difference between the current health indicator value and the failure threshold. Mathematically, the RUL is determined (i.e., TRUL−x = TEoL−x – tx), where, TRUL−x is the remaining useful life at time tx, TEoL−x is the time when the component reaches its failure threshold, and tx is the current operating time. This calculation involves finding the intersection points of the exponential degradation model: first with the current health indicator value at time tx, and second with the failure threshold at TEoL(i), as depicted in Fig. 9.

For capacitor samples, impedance measurements at 4 kHz (obtained using Method-1 presented in the previous section of the manuscript) are fitted with a first-order exponential degradation model, as shown in Fig. 10. The capacitor impedance failure threshold is established at 2.25 Ω, representing 200% of the initial impedance value.

The experimental results demonstrate variability in component degradation rates, necessitating probabilistic RUL representation. For the GaN FETs, RUL probability density functions (PDFs) exhibit characteristics consistent with either Gaussian or uniform distribution functions. Similarly, capacitor impedance RUL data best fits a uniform distribution, as shown in Fig. 11. For computational simplicity and based on the expectation that larger sample sizes will converge toward uniform distribution, this research adopts uniform distribution functions for both component types. However, other distributions such as generalized extreme value (GEV), beta, or normal distributions could be considered depending on the specific dataset characteristics and best-fit analysis.

The probabilistic nature of RUL predictions accounts for inherent uncertainties in lifetime estimation. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the RUL is represented by a probability density function (PDF) that describes the likelihood of predicted remaining life at different aging stages. This statistical approach provides a more realistic assessment of component reliability compared to deterministic predictions, acknowledging the stochastic nature of degradation processes in real-world applications. The specific distribution of the PDF depends on the respective components’ aging characteristic reflected by the health indicator. It can follow uniform distribution, normal distribution, etc., which needs to be first assessed using a ‘goodness-of-fit’ evaluation method.

The uniform PDF is applied to represent the statistical spread of remaining useful lifetime across the tested device population, capturing device-to-device variability in parametric end-of-life times observed experimentally. It does not represent the reliability distribution function of any individual device, which decreases monotonically as degradation accumulates over time. The choice of uniform distribution is a pragmatic, experimental data fitting motivated approximation reflecting the observed spread of RUL within the characterized degradation window.

System-level analysis and survival probability calculation

The system-level RUL prediction scheme captures the real-time reliability interactions among individual components by evaluating their collective health indicators (survival indices) under various operating conditions. Unlike component-level analysis, system-level prediction considers the interdependencies between components and their combined effect on overall converter reliability.

The overall system survival probability is calculated using a cumulative reliability model, where the system survives only if all critical components remain operational. The failure or full practical degradation of even one main component will constitute the system’s end of life, considering that there are no redundancies built in. This approach assumes that component failures are independent events, which is reasonable for the H-bridge converter configuration under study. Neural network model development and implementation

Fig. 13(a) A two-layer neural network model. (b) Probability of survival and system’s RUL at multiple time values in the future.

The calculation process of system-level RUL and PDF generation involves computationally intensive calculations requiring integration of Eqs. (6) and (7) along with subsequent processing steps. The PDF computation process demands substantial memory resources and processing power to determine RUL for multiple GaN FETs and capacitors within the converter system. To address these computational limitations and enable real-time implementation, a neural network model is developed and trained based on survival probability curves derived from various pre-evaluated RDSon and impedance (Z) value combinations obtained from experimental datasets within the ranges presented in31 and according to the degradation schemes shown in Figs. 10 and 11.

The developed neural network model employs a five-input architecture, accepting the measured values from four RDSon values (corresponding to devices S1-S4) and one impedance value (for capacitor C1) to predict system RUL at survival probability percentages ranging from 99% to 1%, as illustrated in Fig. 13. The neural network is formulated as a regression problem to identify underlying relationships between component health indicators and system survival characteristics. Rather than generating time-series outputs, the model produces discrete lifetime survival indicators (T-x) corresponding to each 10% decrease in survival probability, as shown in Fig. 13(b). This approach provides practical threshold-based predictions suitable for maintenance scheduling and operational decision-making. It may be noted that if measurements of health indicator values from additional components or elements are available, the neural network model will involve that many more inputs. For example, having an additional capacitor in the system would mean that the model will have 6 inputs. Moreover, other system factors such as ambient temperature, voltage, humidity, etc. can also be added as inputs to the neural network model if the probability of survival at different mission profiles are desired to be assessed. Hence, the proposed model is flexible in its implementation.

Here, a two-layer backpropagation network architecture is implemented, as illustrated in Fig. 14. The network features hidden layers with sigmoid activation functions and linear output neurons, optimized for modeling the nonlinear relationship between component degradation states and system survival probability. Validation of RUL and NN model

The on-resistance degradation of the GaN FETs is modeled using first-degree exponential curve fitting. This modeling approach is applied consistently across all devices, accounting for their respective thermal cycling conditions: both the top-side devices and bottom-side devices exhibit similar degradation patterns under their specific operating conditions. The impedance parameter (Z) of the DC-link capacitor follows the same exponential modeling approach. The electrolytic capacitor is assumed to operate at ambient temperature (25 °C) and experiences negligible degradation over the aging period, as shown in Fig. 19. This assumption is based on the significantly lower thermal stress experienced by the capacitor(s) compared to the FETs in most commercial applications.

Due to the varying thermal cycling temperatures experienced by the ten individual GaN FETs during testing, the Arrhenius equation is employed to normalize all degradation data to a unified operating temperature of 150 °C. This standardization enables consistent system-level RUL calculations and probability of survival analysis. The selection of 150 °C as the reference temperature reflects typical junction temperatures encountered in practical power electronic applications, where switching semiconductor devices operate significantly above ambient conditions due to power dissipation and thermal resistance. Figure 19 presents the experimental degradation curves for both RDSon and Z parameters across all H-bridge components (GaN FETs and capacitor), normalized to the unified operating temperature. These curves serve as the foundation for the RUL prediction model validation.

The PDF-based statistical framework and the neural network model are complementary approaches rather than independent: the statistical framework, implemented in MATLAB, serves as the primary physically grounded RUL estimation method, using experimentally derived uniform PDFs to compute system-level probability of survival through mathematical integration. The neural network model is subsequently trained to replicate this input-output mapping — taking degradation parameters (RDSon and impedance Z) as inputs and the MATLAB-computed RUL values (T99–T01) as training targets — providing a computationally efficient approximation suitable for real-time FPGA deployment. The PDFs are therefore embedded implicitly in the NN training process through the statistical framework outputs, rather than being directly input to the neural network.

This validation framework confirms that both the statistical and NN-based approaches provide reliable RUL predictions for GaN-based power electronic systems, with the NN model offering computational efficiency advantages for real-time applications.

The initial NN model development is conducted using thermal cycling data with a maximum temperature of 150 °C across ten individual GaN devices. To validate the model’s adaptability and extend its operational envelope, the GaN-based H-bridge system undergoes thermal cycling within an expanded temperature window ranging from 25 °C to 200 °C. This extended temperature range represents more aggressive operating conditions that may be encountered in high-power density applications or harsh environmental conditions. The validation under these conditions demonstrates the model’s robustness and practical applicability across diverse thermal stress scenarios. It is important to note that the 200 °C validation experiments were conducted on a new and independent set of GaN devices assembled in the H-bridge topology, separate from the individual device population used for NN model training under the 150 °C maximum thermal cycling condition. The degradation data — including RDSon and impedance (Z) values at each operating checkpoint — were fully and independently acquired through real-time experimental monitoring of these devices under the 25–200 °C thermal cycling window.

To accommodate the higher maximum thermal cycling temperature (200 °C), a time acceleration factor approach is employed to correlate the NN model predictions with the expanded operating conditions. The acceleration factor quantifies the relationship between degradation rates at different thermal stress levels. The time acceleration factor is determined using experimental degradation data from the H-bridge devices subjected to the 25 °C to 200 °C thermal cycling window. The RDSon and Z parameter values for each operating point (Beginning, Checkpoint-1, and Checkpoint-2) are aligned with their corresponding positions within the experimental datasets. It should be noted that operation at 200 °C may activate additional degradation mechanisms — including enhanced thermomechanical stress at interconnects and die-attach interfaces — that are not individually resolved in this study. The RDSon parameter, used as the primary health indicator, reflects the aggregate effect of all concurrent degradation processes. Therefore, the time acceleration factor employed here is a phenomenological quantity derived from observed parameter evolution, and caution should be exercised in extrapolating it to conditions where the dominant degradation mechanism may fundamentally differ from those at 150 °C.

Conclusions

This research presents long-term power converters remaining useful lifetime (RUL) characterization methodology and experimental validation, implemented through a GaN-based H-bridge configuration. A comprehensive statistical framework has been established for extracting probability density functions of individual system components, including both GaN FETs and electrolytic capacitors. This methodology enables accurate characterization of component-level degradation behavior and forms the foundation for system-level reliability assessment. The integration of individual component statistics into system-level probability of survival calculations and remaining useful lifetime (RUL) predictions has been successfully demonstrated through detailed case studies, providing a robust framework for power electronic system reliability engineering.

Component-specific degradation characterization are considered for aluminum electrolytic capacitors and e-mode GaN HEMTs. A dedicated degradation testing setup has been designed and implemented to characterize the aging performance of the capacitors and FETs under realistic operating conditions. Two distinct characterization methodologies have been developed for the capacitor’s health monitoring and compared for in-situ device condition monitoring, enabling real-time assessment of capacitor health without system interruption. Comprehensive degradation characterization of GaN devices has been conducted across multiple thermal cycling conditions, establishing a robust database of device aging behavior. The experimental results reveal that the AECs exhibit significantly shorter operational lifetimes compared to GaN FETs, establishing the capacitor as the primary life-limiting component in the H-bridge system. This insight has profound implications for system design and maintenance strategies, highlighting the need for capacitor-centric reliability planning. The experimental data forms the basis for both statistical and machine learning-based reliability prediction models.

An advanced neural network (NN) model has been developed using the extensive degradation datasets from individual GaN devices. The model architecture is specifically designed to capture the complex, non-linear relationships between operating conditions, degradation mechanisms, and remaining useful lifetime. The comparative analysis demonstrates robust alignment between NN model predictions and established statistical approaches for remaining useful lifetime estimation for power electronics converters. Cross-validation across multiple operating conditions confirms model reliability and generalization capability. The NN model is shown to achieve 99% or higher matching accuracy when compared to experimental results across diverse testing scenarios. It should be noted that the 99% accuracy figure reflects the model’s ability to predict time differences between specific operating checkpoints at the T50 reliability level under controlled accelerated thermal cycling conditions. The NN model demonstrates strong agreement with the statistical approach across the full T99–T01 reliability spectrum.

In addition, the developed NN model has been successfully deployed on Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) hardware through optimized weight and bias matrix implementations. This hardware deployment represents a significant advancement in practical reliability monitoring, enabling real-time RUL calculations within operational power electronic systems. This research showed successful FPGA implementation validates the feasibility of integrating advanced machine learning-based health monitoring directly into power converter control systems. This advancement opens new possibilities for intelligent power electronic systems capable of self-monitoring, predictive maintenance scheduling, and adaptive operational strategies, even in-situ (can also be referred to as ‘edge intelligence’).
