Relapse‐Associated and Relapse‐Independent Contribution to Overall Expanded Disability Status Scale Progression in Multiple Sclerosis Patients Diagnosed in Different Eras
Authors: Noemi Montobbio, Cinzia Cordioli, Alessio Signori, Francesca Bovis, Ruggero Capra, Maria Pia Sormani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe introduction of disease‐modifying therapies for multiple sclerosis (MS) has led to a deceleration of disease course over the years. Although decreased relapse rate constitutes a factor, the role of relapse‐associated worsening (RAW) and progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA) in MS course deceleration is still unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively examined long‐term Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) progression in patients referred to the MS Center of Montichiari (Italy) and diagnosed with relapsing–remitting MS from 1980 to 2022. To isolate PIRA, we deducted all EDSS changes associated with relapses from overall EDSS change. We compared the relative contribution of PIRA and RAW to EDSS progression in patients diagnosed in different periods using mixed‐effects models.ResultsA total of 1,405 patients were included in the study, of whom 231 were diagnosed in 1980–1996 (pre‐treatment era), 577 in 1997–2008 (injectable disease‐modifying therapy era), and 597 after 2008 (oral drugs, monoclonal antibodies, and anti‐CD20 era). Across ages, both PIRA and RAW were reduced in patients diagnosed in more recent periods as compared with earlier periods. The average contribution of PIRA to overall EDSS progression was already predominant in patients diagnosed in 1980–1996 (78%) and in 1997–2008 (76%), but it was significantly increased (p = 0.0009) in patients diagnosed in later years (87%).InterpretationThe deceleration of MS course observed throughout the years is determined not only by fewer RAW events, but also by a reduction in PIRA. However, the shift toward a mostly relapse‐independent progression highlights the importance of evaluating new therapies based on their effect on PIRA. ANN NEUROL 2025;97:95–103

Disability progression in patients with relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) has historically been associated with the residual disability accumulated after relapses. 1 , 2 However, this view has been challenged by increasing evidence showing that a substantial proportion of disability progression is independent from acute episodes already in the relapsing phase of the disease. 3 , 4

With the proliferation of disease‐modifying therapies (DMTs), the long‐term prognosis of RRMS improved over time, with patients diagnosed in more recent years reaching Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) milestones at an older age. 5 The proven efficacy of available DMTs in reducing the frequency of relapses constitutes a likely factor contributing to slower disability progression. However, it is unclear whether the deceleration of disease course is completely determined by a reduction in relapse‐associated progression events, or rather by a mixture of reduced relapse rate and slowed‐down relapse‐independent progression.

Herein, we assessed the relative proportion of relapse‐associated and relapse‐independent progression in long‐term EDSS trajectories, and the evolution of this proportion across epochs on a real‐life basis. We compared the different components of disability progression between patients who were diagnosed in different periods, ranging from the 1980s to the present time. Different epochs are associated with different availability, or patterns of use, of drugs. Once diagnosed with MS, patients gain access to approved DMTs. Thus, we used the year of diagnosis as a proxy for exposure to treatments available in different epochs. As such, although taking into account treatment switches over time, our modeling approach sets the focus on the impact of therapeutic strategies at the time of diagnosis.

Methods

The study adhered to the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies. The protocol for the study received prior approval by the Ethics Committee of Brescia with code NP 4052, and informed consent was obtained from each participant. We examined long‐term EDSS progression in outpatients evaluated at the MS Center of Montichiari (Brescia, Italy) and diagnosed with MS between 1980 and 2022 according to the current criteria, with a relapsing–remitting (RR) course at diagnosis, and with age at diagnosis of 18 to 60 years. This database updates the cohort, including patients diagnosed up to 2013, analyzed by Capra et al. 5 We maintained the same inclusion criteria, retaining all patients with at least 2 follow‐up visits in addition to the baseline visit, and complete baseline information (age, sex, date of first symptom, date of diagnosis, and baseline EDSS). We identified the baseline visit as the first available visit after the first MS symptom. The motivation behind this choice was to make baseline EDSS independent of the time to diagnosis, which differs across epochs due to the evolution of diagnostic criteria (see section “Baseline definition” of Supplementary Methods in Data S1 for further details). If the baseline visit occurred less than 1 year after a clinical relapse, the baseline was moved to the next available visit occurring at least 1 year from the last relapse. This was implemented to prevent an overestimation of the baseline value, as recovery from relapses has been shown to extend up to approximately 1 year after the event 6 (see section “Baseline definition” of Supplementary Methods in Data S1 for further details). As a consequence, we also excluded patients whose EDSS assessments were all within 1 year from clinical relapses.

Individual EDSS trajectories were obtained as the cumulative EDSS change from baseline for each patient at all available assessments. To evaluate the roles of relapse‐associated and relapse‐independent progression in the evolution of MS over time, we decomposed individual EDSS trajectories to isolate the different contributions. We defined sustained RAW events as all 6‐month‐confirmed relapse‐associated (ie, occurring within 90 days from a relapse) EDSS worsening events sustained for at least 1 year. The 1‐year requirement was imposed to rule out the temporary increase in EDSS after the inflammatory event, capturing only the residual disability accumulated after remission. 6 The EDSS change brought by each sustained RAW event was deducted from cumulative EDSS change at all subsequent time points, to extract relapse‐independent progression. To account for temporary increases in EDSS after an acute episode, we also detected every relapse‐associated transient worsening, and deducted the corresponding “bump” from the EDSS trajectory to isolate PIRA. We thus defined PIRA as the ensuing trajectory deprived of relapse‐associated contributions. As a result, the EDSS worsening of each patient was split into 3 components: sustained RAW, transient RAW, and PIRA (Fig 1). It should be noted that the same relapse can determine a combination of transient and sustained EDSS worsening. In such cases, the total EDSS jump from baseline was broken down into 2 parts, each assigned to the relevant component (as illustrated, eg, for the relapse at year 4, in Fig 1).

Schematic illustration of the decomposition of individual Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) trajectories into their relapse‐associated and relapse‐independent components. PIRA = progression independent of relapse activity; RAW = relapse‐associated worsening.

We compared the average course of each of the 3 components of EDSS progression in patients diagnosed in different years by aligning the individual trajectories according to age. Specifically, each point of the collective trajectory represents the average disability accumulation of patients in a specified age range. Such average gathers together EDSS values possibly recorded at different “absolute” times, but occurring at the same point in the lifetime of patients. As such, each patient contributes to the collective trajectory only in the age range represented in their follow‐up period. The choice to regress EDSS increase with age instead of disease duration was motivated by some observations. First, on the same lines as the previous publication, 5 we aimed at studying MS course on a “real‐life” basis by comparing the state of patients of different eras at the same age. Second, splitting the patients by year of diagnosis inevitably leads to different follow‐up lengths between groups. This would be a confounder for disease duration, whereas it does not interfere with age. Third, comparing disease duration across different epochs may give rise to a possible Will Rogers effect, 7 according to which a prognosis improvement can be observed over time due to different diagnostic and disease staging criteria.

The availability of different DMTs at the time of MS diagnosis clearly influences the therapeutic strategy adopted for each patient. The year of diagnosis may, therefore, be used as a real‐life proxy for exposure to treatments approved in different epochs. We used linear mixed‐effects models to assess whether the year of diagnosis modulated the extent of EDSS change. Specifically, with EDSS change as a dependent variable, we tested the fixed effects of diagnosis year in interaction with age, allowing for a subject‐specific breaking point in age between a relapsing and a non‐relapsing period; we also included sex, individual median interval between EDSS visits, and treatment efficacy score at each visit (0.0: no treatment, 0.5: moderate‐efficacy, 1.0: high‐efficacy, 8 , 9 as per Table S2) as covariates. The treatment efficacy score was included to take into account treatment switches over time. Continuous predictors (year of diagnosis, age, and median visit interval) were z‐score normalized. To account for the presence of patients with a slower or faster disease course, we included subject‐specific deviations from the common trajectory (random age slope, and random intercept, with an unstructured covariance matrix). The fitted model was used to evaluate the effect of the year of diagnosis on EDSS change. Identical models were fitted to study the effect of the year of diagnosis separately on the sustained RAW, transient RAW, and PIRA components of EDSS change.

To investigate the evolution of disease progression over the years more in depth, we split the patients by year of diagnosis, according to 3 treatment eras: 1980–1996 (pre‐treatment era), 1997–2008 (injectable DMT era), and 2009–2022 (oral drugs, monoclonal antibodies, anti‐CD20 era). As an update to the previous study, 5 we compared the age at which patients from the 3 groups reached EDSS = 6, using a Cox model adjusted for visit frequency and age at diagnosis. To assess the variation in relapse frequency across diagnosis periods, we computed the mean annualized relapse rate of patients diagnosed in each period over the first 10 years of their follow‐up period. The annualized relapse rate was computed on the 10‐year truncated follow‐up to correct for the different follow‐up lengths found in the 3 groups (Table 1). Next, we studied EDSS change from baseline as a function of age for the 3 diagnosis periods using linear mixed‐effects models. Specifically, we adopted the same model structure detailed above, but with the period of diagnosis as a categorical predictor, stratified into the 3 treatment eras. The fitted model was used to compare EDSS change from baseline between the different diagnosis periods, and to estimate the rate of change of EDSS as a function of age within each diagnosis period. The same modeling strategy was adopted to separately analyze the sustained RAW, transient RAW, and PIRA components of EDSS change.

To assess the relative contribution of PIRA to EDSS progression in different eras, we also computed the relapse‐independent fraction of EDSS progression as the ratio between the relapse‐independent cumulative change and the overall cumulative change in EDSS. We used linear mixed‐effects models as detailed above to statistically compare this ratio between the 3 diagnosis periods. All p values reported were Holm–Bonferroni corrected for the number of comparisons made.

Our choice of splitting the cohorts based on the year of diagnosis is justified in more detail in section “Cohort splitting” of Supplementary Methods in Data S1, in comparison with an alternative approach focusing on the effect of the ongoing treatment regime on the disease course.

To ensure that our choice of baseline visit did not distort the results, we carried out sensitivity analyses in the following cases: “Baseline after diagnosis” (only visits occurring after the MS diagnosis are considered as baseline); “No relapse‐free intervals for baseline” (a visit can be taken as baseline regardless of its distance from relapses; ie, removing the 1‐year distance constraint); “30‐day relapse‐free interval for baseline” (a visit is not considered as baseline if falling within 30 days from a relapse; ie, replacing the original 1‐year interval by a shorter interval of 30 days). See section “Baseline definition” of Supplementary Methods in Data S1 for more details.

As a further sensitivity analysis, we also computed the decomposition of EDSS trajectories based on all inflammatory activity (clinical relapses and new magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) lesions). Due to the impossibility of determining the exact time of appearance of a newly identified lesion, we arbitrarily set the date of MRI lesion formation as the mid‐time between the MRI scan reporting the appearance of new lesions and the preceding MRI scan. We then pooled together the data on clinical and MRI activity, and decomposed the EDSS trajectories into a sustained activity‐associated component, a transient activity‐associated component, and a progression independent of activity (PIA) 10 component. The analyses carried out on these components are detailed in the section “PIA trajectories” of Supplementary Methods in Data S1.

Results

Of the 2,530 patients diagnosed with MS at the Montichiari Center in 1980–2022, 1,405 were included in the analysis according to the criteria detailed above. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the cohort. Fig 2A plots the age‐aligned EDSS change from baseline averaged over the whole population, and its decomposition into the sustained RAW, transient RAW, and PIRA components. Trajectories were truncated between 30 and 60 years of age to exclude unreliable values due to an insufficient number of participants in the tails. The trend observed for each of the 3 components is consistent with what expected from average disease course. The sustained RAW component shows an initial growth corresponding to the relapsing phase of the disease, followed by a plateau; the transient RAW component is characterized by a weakly positive initial phase given by the average of EDSS bumps across the population, and fades to zero afterwards, as is does not carry any permanent disability accumulation; the PIRA component steadily grows over the entire follow‐up. However, the averaged trajectories conceal a progressive shift in EDSS values depending on the diagnosis period. EDSS course over age is modulated by the year of diagnosis, showing a decrease from earlier to later diagnosis periods within each age range (Fig 2B). Although the decreasing trend is evident for absolute EDSS values at all years, EDSS change from baseline appears to decrease starting from patients diagnosed after 1985. This is motivated by higher values at baseline in previous years, due to many late diagnoses. A similar pattern according to age and year of diagnosis is also observed separately for the (sustained) relapse‐associated and relapse‐independent components of EDSS change. Mixed‐effects model analyses confirmed a significant decrease in PIRA (p < 0.0001), sustained RAW (p < 0.0001), and overall progression (p < 0.0001) by year of diagnosis (Table S3), thus motivating the stratification by diagnosis period in subsequent analyses.

Of the patients included in the analysis, 231 were diagnosed in 1980–1996, 577 in 1997–2008, and 597 in 2008–2022. Fig 3A plots the cumulative probability to reach EDSS = 6 as a function of age for each diagnosis period, as estimated by a Cox model, updating and confirming the progressive slowdown of disease course over time observed previously. 5 Although just confirmatory of previous results from the same cohort, the updated analysis results were included in the current study, as the short follow‐up period of the most recent group in the original publication did not allow us to draw reliable conclusions. This deceleration of disease progression is coupled with a gradual decrease in annualized relapse rate (Fig 3B), thus predicting a contraction of the relapse‐associated component of EDSS progression over diagnosis periods. The sustained RAW component was indeed consecutively reduced in patients diagnosed in later periods as compared with patients diagnosed in earlier periods (1980–1996 vs. 1997–2008: p = 0.0077; 1997–2008 vs. 2009–2022: p < 0.0001). However, a significant reduction in the PIRA component was also observed (1980–1996 vs. 1997–2008: p = 0.0001; 1997–2008 vs. 2009–2022: p < 0.0001). The transient RAW only includes, by definition, EDSS changes that were not sustained, thus giving little contribution to overall progression. As noted above, its effect was confined to the initial, relapsing part of the disease course. Estimates and standard errors of the average curve slopes, quantifying the rate of growth of each trajectory, are also reported. The difference in the PIRA component between groups is mainly determined by different rates of growth (Fig 3C, bottom panel). On the other hand, the decrease in sustained RAW observed between the 1980–1996 and the 1997–2008 cohort is uniform across the whole curve (Fig 3C, top panel). Patients diagnosed in 1980–1996 appear to have already accumulated more residual disability from relapses at age 31–35 years, as compared with patients diagnosed in 1997–2008 at the same age. The comparable slopes indicate that, at older ages, sustained RAW accumulated at a similar rate for the 2 groups—thus always maintaining a higher cumulative value for the 1980–1996 group.

The above results suggest that the deceleration of the MS course observed throughout the years was determined not only by fewer RAW events, but also by a reduction in PIRA. However, the relative contribution of these 2 components to overall progression did not remain constant (Fig 4). The average contribution of PIRA to overall EDSS progression, already predominant in patients diagnosed in 1980–1996 (adjusted mean fraction 78%, CI 72–84%) and in 1997–2008 (76%, CI 72–80%), was indeed significantly increased (p = 0.0009) in patients diagnosed in later years (87%, CI 82–92%).

The results of all sensitivity analyses, summarized in Tables S5 and S6, were consistent with the main analysis. The sensitivity analysis based on all inflammatory activity (clinical relapses and MRI lesions) reduced the estimated activity‐independent fraction of EDSS progression to 71% for patients diagnosed in 1980–1996, 76% for patients diagnosed in 1997–2008, and 84% for patients diagnosed in 2009–2022. The relative pattern of evolution of the trajectory components remained substantially unchanged with respect to the main analysis.

Discussion

Disability progression in MS is caused by a combination of diverse mechanisms of injury and compensation that are in turn mediated by sociodemographic characteristics and behavior, with great variability across and within patients. The clinical expression of such a mixture of factors provided by disability scales only gives partial information to interpret the disease course. Nonetheless, long‐term EDSS trajectories can provide some insight on the temporal unfolding of the disease, and the relative timing of clinical relapses with respect to EDSS evolution can be leveraged to identify distinct components of disability progression.

The present analysis attempted to disentangle the relapse‐associated and relapse‐independent contributions to EDSS progression with a continuous, trajectory‐based (as opposed to binary, event‐based) approach. The analysis takes into account the magnitude of EDSS changes, allowing us to quantify the relative contribution of PIRA and RAW based not only on the number of events, but also on the actual disability accrual brought by each of them. This approach was combined with the age‐group rationale adopted in the previous publication 5 to compare disease progression at the same age in different eras, thus disentangling averaged EDSS trajectories from the patients' follow‐up periods.

In our decomposition of EDSS trajectories, the PIRA component includes all disability progression that is not relapse‐related, regardless of whether it occurs in the relapsing or secondary progressive phase of the disease. The present results showed a steady growth of the PIRA component across the whole disease course, suggesting relapse‐independent progression in RRMS may be an early manifestation of the same mechanism of gradual disability worsening taking place in the secondary progressive phase of the disease. 11

The results of the present analysis remained substantially unchanged when isolating progression independent of all inflammatory activity. However, this type of analysis is limited by the coarseness of MRI visits, preventing a precise determination of the dates of appearance of newly identified lesions. Further analyses on data with more frequent MRI visits will be needed to clarify the contribution of lesion activity.

A limitation of the present study was the evolution of EDSS assessment over time, as the Neurostatus certification was only acquired by the team at the Montichiari MS center after 2004. In contrast, as all EDSS assessments were carried out in the same center and by the same team throughout the whole data collection period, a high uniformity of approach is guaranteed.

Along with EDSS evaluation, diagnostic criteria also changed over time. As previously noted, 5 more sensitive diagnostic criteria may have enabled the diagnosis of older patients who were not previously diagnosed due to a milder disease course, determining the increase in mean age at diagnosis observed through the years in the present study. Such an increase was also observed on MSBase data. 12 , 13 The possible presence of patients with a slower (or faster) disease course was taken into account in the mixed‐effects analysis by including subject‐specific deviations from the group trajectories.

Another limitation was the inevitable “contamination” of EDSS trajectories by the introduction of new DMTs over time. Clearly, EDSS scores recorded over the years reflect not only the therapy received at the time of the MS diagnosis, but also later switches to newly available DMTs. This is taken into account in our mixed‐effects models through the inclusion of the treatment efficacy variable as a time‐dependent covariate. However, the focus of the analysis was to evaluate the evolution of real‐life disease course over epochs, based on the therapeutic strategy at the time of diagnosis—which in turn reflects the notion of “early treatment.” In fact, the present results indirectly highlight the heavy effect of early treatment on MS prognosis.

The present analyses revealed a deceleration of both relapse‐associated and relapse‐independent disability progression throughout the years. However, although clinical relapses are almost completely eliminated by modern high‐efficacy DMTs, the underlying disability progression independent of relapse activity is far from being halted or reversed. Our results showed a progressive shift toward a mostly relapse‐independent progression. This scenario calls for longitudinal studies of PIRA in terms of the underlying biological mechanisms, and for a solid and standardized framework for evaluating the effect of new therapies on PIRA.
