COVID‐19 Vaccine Mistrust, Racial Discrimination, and Conspiracy Beliefs Among Parents in Canada: Implications for Public Health
Authors: Rose Darly Dalexis, Yan Xu, Seyed Mohammad Mahdi Moshirian Farahi, Idrissa Beogo, Mwali Muray, Jude Mary Cénat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite studies documenting the evolution and factors related to COVID‐19 vaccination in Canadian children, gaps in research on COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust (VM) across ethnocultural groups hinder tailored public health responses. This study examines the sociodemographic characteristics and factors related to COVID‐19 VM among racially diverse Canadian parents with children aged 0–12 years from Arab, Asian, Black, Indigenous, and White communities. A cross‐sectional study was conducted among a representative sample of 2528 parents of children aged 0–12 years across Canada. They completed measures assessing COVID‐19 VM, conspiracy beliefs, health literacy, everyday racial discrimination and sociodemographic data. Descriptive statistics, ANOVA, and multiple regression analyses explored factors associated with VM, with subgroup analyses for racialized and Indigenous groups. Significant mean differences in VM were observed across racial groups (p < 0.001). Post hoc analyses showed that Black individuals scored higher on COVID VM (M = 12.4; 95% CI: 11.9–12.9) compared to White individuals (M = 11.1; 95% CI: 10.8–11.3; p < 0.001). Indigenous participants scored higher on COVID VM (M = 14.4; 95% CI: 13.7–15.1) compared to White (p < 0.001), Black (p < 0.001), Arab (M = 12.9; 95% CI: 12.4–13.5; p = 0.017), and Asian participants (M = 11.8; 95% CI: 11.3–12.3; p < 0.001). Regression analyses revealed that conspiracy beliefs were strongly associated with VM in both White (β = 0.61, p < 0.001, R² = 42.1%) and racialized individuals (β = 0.51, p < 0.001, R² = 34.0%). Everyday racial discrimination significantly predicted VM among racialized groups (β = 0.10, p = 0.001) but not White participants (β = −0.02, p = 0.313). Significant racial, gender, and age‐based disparities in COVID‐19 VM among Canadian parents underscore the urgent need for targeted, community‐driven, and antiracist public health strategies to enhance vaccine confidence, addressing unique barriers faced by racialized and Indigenous communities.Keywords: Arab, Asian, Black, Indigenous and White individuals; Canada; children; COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust; parents

Introduction

On July 14, 2022, the National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI) published an Advisory Committee Statement (ACS) opening COVID‐19 vaccination to children in Canada aged 6 months to 5 years [1]. This statement followed earlier recommendations by the NACI that allowed the vaccination of children aged 5 to 11 and adolescents [2]. Studies conducted with national samples or in various major cities showed that most parents intended to vaccinate their children against COVID‐19; however, the uptake was low, especially in children under the age of 5 [3, 4, 5, 6]. From the beginning of the COVID‐19 vaccination campaign, studies highlighted parents' hesitation and reluctance to vaccinate their children against COVID‐19 [4, 6]. Indeed, even parents who had themselves been vaccinated against COVID‐19 could be hesitant and reluctant to vaccinate their children [6]. Factors explaining this hesitation and reluctance include fear of side effects and the vaccine approval processes, low household income, being politically right‐leaning, parents being unvaccinated against COVID‐19, and belonging to racialized and Indigenous communities [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8].

However, despite efforts made in recent years to conduct inclusive research in Canada, studies carried out on COVID‐19 vaccine hesitancy, confidence, mistrust, and uptake in children have failed to recruit a large enough sample of racialized and Indigenous children or parents to disaggregate data by race. This has led some studies to consider broader categories such as “visible minorities versus non‐visible minorities (in reality, White people)”, “racialized versus non‐racialized (in reality, White people)”, or “Indigenous versus nonindigenous” [3, 6, 9]. Indeed, although children from racialized and Indigenous communities have often been found to be less vaccinated, not up to date with their vaccines, or their parents are more hesitant about vaccination [3], no study to date has compared different racial groups regarding mistrust in COVID‐19 vaccines. Furthermore, no study has examined differences in the explanatory factors of COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust based on children's racial background, particularly comparing Arab, Asian, Indigenous, Black, and White parents.

Many studies among parents have documented COVID‐19 vaccine hesitancy [7, 10, 11]. While vaccine hesitancy refers to a behavioral phenomenon characterized by the delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite its availability [12], vaccine mistrust reflects a deeper attitudinal dimension rooted in a lack of confidence in vaccines, healthcare systems, and institutions [13]. In racialized and Indigenous communities, vaccine mistrust is often the result of historical and ongoing experiences of colonization, discrimination, and medical racism and neglect, which shape collective perceptions of risk and safety [14, 15]. Clarifying this distinction is essential, as mistrust can be an underlying driver of vaccine uptake, particularly when structural and historical inequities erode confidence in public health authorities.

These gaps are observed in the scientific literature even though the COVID‐19 pandemic showed that racialized and Indigenous communities were particularly at risk, both in terms of infection rates and mortality [16, 17, 18]. The rapid spread of SARS‐CoV‐2 globally in 2020 also demonstrated that the vulnerability of one group could constitute a public health threat to the general population [19]. Understanding the factors explaining COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust across different racial groups should also enable the development and implementation of tailored measures to promote vaccination in these communities [20, 21, 22]. It is essential to move away from the colorblind approach still observed in public health instructions, guidelines, and programs and adopt a racially and culturally tailored framework that effectively addresses the specific needs of different ethnocultural communities. A colorblind approach in public health refers to developing policies, programs, and messages without considering how race, ethnicity, culture, or systemic inequities affect health outcomes [23, 24]. This can lead to ineffective or inequitable interventions because it overlooks the distinct experiences, barriers, and needs of racialized and ethnocultural communities. These issues are even more critical given the resurgence of previously eradicated infectious diseases among children in Canada, such as measles and rubella, observed during 2024 [25]. Considering these gaps in the scientific literature, the present article aims to examine the rates and factors associated with COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust in a representative sample of ethnically diverse parents of children aged 0 to 12 years from Arab, Asian, Black, Indigenous, and White communities in Canada.

Methods

Design and Data Collection

This study was conducted from October 2023 to November 2023 as a part of the “Perception of vaccine effectiveness among Canadian parents of children aged 0–12 and Canada's various ethnic communities” project. The study population comprised parents of children aged 0 to 12 years. Participants were recruited through an online survey using Computer‐Assisted Web Interviewing (CAWI), conducted by the Léger (LÉO) company. This panel includes over 420,000 active members across Canada. A random sample of 19,308 panel members was invited to participate via direct email. From this group, 4158 individuals accessed the survey. Out of those who clicked on the survey 1522 did not meet the eligibility criteria. Among the 2636 eligible respondents, 108 declined to participate, resulting in 2528 completed surveys and a response rate of 95.90%. To ensure racial and ethnic diversity in the sample, we also intentionally oversampled Arab, Asian, Indigenous, and Black participants using ethnic profiling data from the Leo panel. This approach allowed us to include groups that are frequently underrepresented in research or inaccurately grouped under the broad “visible minority” label and to analyze them appropriately. Each participant received a personalized, single‐use invitation link via email, ensuring they could only complete the survey once and preventing link sharing. The inclusion criteria were: (1) residency in Canada, (2) being a parent of a child aged 0–12, and (3) the ability to complete the survey in English or French. This study received approval from the University of Ottawa Research Ethics Board (#H‐04‐23‐9199), and all participants provided electronic informed consent. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross‐sectional research. To ensure the results were clear and inclusive, researchers and community leaders from diverse communities were actively engaged from the project's inception, in recruitment strategies, data analysis and interpretation, knowledge transfer materials and activities, through a very active advisory committee.

Measures

Sociodemographic. Participants completed a sociodemographic questionnaire collecting details on their gender, age, level of education, religious affiliation, province of residence, racial background, primary language, birthplace, marital status, employment status, and annual household income.

COVID‐19 Vaccine Mistrust

The COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust subscale from the COVID‐19 Vaccine Confidence and Mistrust Beliefs Scale was utilized [13]. This subscale comprises four items (e.g., I am concerned about serious side effects of a COVID‐19 vaccine), with participants rating each item on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The measure was first developed and validated among Black Americans with good psychometric properties. It was then used in different communities, including in Canada, with excellent internal consistency [26, 27]. The Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.89 in the current sample.

Conspiracy Beliefs

The COVID‐19 Conspiracy Belief scale was used to assess conspiracy beliefs about COVID‐19 [28]. This scale includes 10 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”). This scale measures information regarding the origin of COVID‐19, the number of fatalities linked to the virus, and vaccination. (i.e., “a vaccine against COVID‐19 already existed and was only made available once millions of people had been infected.”). Higher scores on this scale reflect stronger conspiracy beliefs. It was used in Canada with excellent internal consistency [29, 30]. The Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.93 in the current study.

Health Literacy

The health literacy scale (HLS) was utilized to assess health literacy [31]. This scale includes 8 items (e.g., “I know how to use the health information I find on the Internet to help me.”) rated on a 5‐point scale (from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”). The HLS measures individuals' perceived ability to locate and utilize health‐related information online, with higher scores indicating greater health literacy. It was used in Canada with excellent internal consistency [32, 33]. The Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.92 in the current study.

Everyday Racial Discrimination

The Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS) was used to assess everyday racial discrimination [34, 35]. The EDS includes 5 items (e.g., “You receive worse service than others at restaurants or stores.”) rated on a 6‐point scale from “never” to “almost every day”. Higher scores indicate higher rates of everyday discrimination. It was widely used in Canada with excellent internal consistency [36, 37, 38]. The Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.94 in the current study.

Statistical Analysis

Data was analyzed in SPSS 29 and Stata/SE 18. First, the frequency and percentage of the sample characteristics were calculated. Data were also weighted according to the 2021 general population census of Canada. The strata for the sampling weights included racial identity, age, sex, region, education level, and spoken language. Weighted sample characteristics were presented as a supplementary table (Table 1S). Second, the mean (M) of the outcome variable, COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust, was analyzed across sample characteristics using independent t‐tests and one‐way ANOVA. Post hoc analyses were performed on significant F‐test results using Tukey's tests. In addition, mean differences were examined both within each racialized group and between racialized groups. Third, two multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to test the associations of sample characteristics, everyday discrimination, health literacy, and conspiracy beliefs with COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust. The multicollinearity assumption was checked using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Outliers of continuous predictors were also checked using z‐score criteria. Observations that did not fall within −3 and +3 standard deviations were excluded from regression analyses. Adjusted R‐squared, along with F‐test results for models, were reported. Two post hoc power analyses were conducted to ensure the power of the results regarding the multiple linear regressions. The determinants in this analysis included the observed effect size (Cohen's F), an alpha of 0.05, sample size, and number of predictors. Adjusted R‐squares were converted to Cohen's F. This power analysis was performed in G*Power (version 3.1.9.4). As supplementary analyses, a series of hierarchical linear regression analyses were performed among racialized groups, including Black, Arab, Asian, and Indigenous participants. In Model 1, the association of health literacy and everyday racial discrimination with COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust was tested after controlling for sociodemographic factors. In Model 2, conspiracy belief was added to the model. In the process of conducting multiple linear regression analyses, certain variables exhibited missing observations or had fewer than 15 observations per category. Including such variables in the models would have substantially reduced the overall sample size due to listwise deletion. To deal with this issue and preserve statistical power, adjustments were made to the categorical variables, such as combining categories, minimizing the loss of sample size within each group. Standardized coefficients were reported in regression analyses to make a comparable effect across the predictors and to provide a clearer interpretation of their relative importance in predicting the outcome. A significance rate of ≤ 0.05 was used for all significance tests.

Before conducting multiple linear regressions, multicollinearity and outliers were checked. No multicollinearity was found for both models. Regarding outliers, with respect to White participants, 8 outliers were detected for conspiracy beliefs and health literacy, while 36 outliers were detected for everyday racial discrimination. Among racialized groups, only 9 outliers were detected for health literacy. These outliers were dropped from the analyses. Table 3 shows the multiple linear regression results. The multicollinearity assumption was met for both models. In the model for White participants, results showed that conspiracy belief was positively associated with COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust (β = 0.61, p < 0.001) after controlling for sociodemographic variables. No significant relation between COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust and everyday racial discrimination was found among White participants (β = −0.02, p = 0.313). Moreover, after adjusting for other variables, everyday racial discrimination was positively associated with COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust (β = 0.10, p = 0.001). The predictors in this model explained 34.0% of the variance. These findings indicate the important role of everyday racial discrimination among racialized groups. Discussion

In this large, cross‐sectional survey of over 2500 parents in Canada, we observed significant differences in COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust among racial groups, with mistrust being especially high among Black, Indigenous, and Arab parents. Women were more likely to have vaccine mistrust than men, especially among White, Asian and Indigenous parents. While sociodemographic factors such as age, education and income predicted vaccine hesitancy in the overall survey population, this was not a uniform finding among all racialized groups. Specifically, Black and Asian parents reported similar scores of vaccine mistrust across age, education, job status and household income strata. Finally, conspiracy belief was positively associated with COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust in both White and racialized parents, with everyday racial discrimination being a potent predictor of increased mistrust only among racialized respondents. However, known predictors accounted for a lower degree of heterogeneity in vaccine mistrust among racialized parents compared to White parents.

While children have lower rates of hospitalization and case fatality associated with COVID‐19 infections compared to adults, asymptomatic transmission is common and contributes to the risk of exposure within households and communities [39]. Pediatric COVID‐19 vaccines have been demonstrated to reduce symptomatic infections among recipients and contribute to lower pediatric COVID‐19‐related hospitalizations at a population level [40, 41, 42]. Ethnic and racial disparities in COVID‐19 exposure and complications have been observed among Canadian children: in a cohort study of 1632 children in school and day care in Montreal, Canada, a study observed a 1.9‐fold higher risk of COVID‐19 seropositivity among children of racial and ethnic minority groups compared to White children [43]. Examining COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust among racialized parents is therefore critical due to the indirect protective effect of household immunization on symptomatic infection among children, as well as the link between parental vaccine hesitancy and pediatric vaccination coverage [44, 45]. Our findings of higher vaccine mistrust reported by racialized parents are consistent with work by Shui and colleagues, who observed a more than twofold increased likelihood of high concern for vaccine safety among Black and Hispanic parents compared to White parents in a nationwide panel of U.S. adults [46].

Combined with our observation that the everyday experience of racial discrimination is a significant predictor of vaccine mistrust. Our hypothesis is that this relationship may stem from a cumulative erosion of trust caused by repeated experiences of unfair treatment, including racial discrimination in care, structural and systemic racism in health care and services, cultural insensitivity and implicit bias from physicians, nurses and other healthcare professionals, and inequitable access to healthcare, which reinforce the perception that healthcare systems and authorities do not act in the best interest of racialized and Indigenous communities [27, 47, 48]. Historical and contemporary manifestations of medical racism and the lack of integration of ethnocultural factors into public health strategies may further reactivate collective memories of harm and racism, leading individuals to approach vaccination campaigns with caution, skepticism, and mistrust [49, 50, 51, 52]. There is an urgent need for community‐led, bottom‐up approaches that leverage communal values that have been highlighted as facilitators to vaccine acceptance among racialized and Indigenous peoples in Canada [19]. In a systematic review of approaches to overcome COVID‐19 vaccine hesitancy among ethnic minority populations in the U.K., Hussain et al reported community engagement and tailored communication as crucial approaches to address vaccine mistrust. A community‐based participatory approach for which results are eagerly awaited is Project 2VIDA!, a multidisciplinary collaboration by academic researchers, community members and clinicians to address disparities in COVID‐19 uptake in Hispanic and Black communities across Southern California. Informed by the National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities (NIMHD) research framework, this multilevel intervention combines individual awareness and education, community outreach and health promotion, linkage with medical and supportive services, and community pop‐up clinics to rebuild trust and address vaccine hesitancy [53]. This aligns with U.S. survey findings from Shui et al., who observed that racialized parents with vaccine hesitancy were more likely to lack trust in their child's healthcare providers, and to disagree that their child's healthcare provider was easy to talk to [46]. Partnering with community members, leveraging community champions, and engaging with traditional media, social media and informal communication media (e.g., places of worship, family gatherings) to address vaccine‐related concerns are therefore key steps towards building a tailored approach to communication that addresses the complex and additive impacts of conspiracy theory, racial discrimination and structural inequities on vaccine mistrust among racialized parents.

Addressing vaccine hesitancy without acknowledging its underlying sociocultural and historical roots may limit the effectiveness of public health strategies. Among racialized and Indigenous communities, collective and intergenerational memories and complex racial trauma of medical racism and neglect can shape present‐day attitudes toward vaccination, sometimes overriding the perceived risks of COVID‐19 itself [49, 54]. Recognizing and validating these experiences within community settings, including in churches and local organizations, are therefore essential steps to rebuilding trust and promoting equitable vaccine uptake.

Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study that has evaluated COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust among parents in Canada with sampling to ensure racial diversity. However, our analysis is subject to several limitations. First, the cross‐sectional nature of our survey establishes association but not causality. Moreover, the possibility of reverse causality (e.g., that vaccine mistrust worsened conspiracy beliefs and/or experiences of racial discrimination) cannot be ruled out. Second, despite reaching a large sample of racialized parents, the sample size for specific groups (e.g., Latin American) is low and makes the determination of subgroup effects challenging. This is relevant, as unique challenges faced by racial groups preclude a one‐size‐fits‐all approach or one that assumes generalizability of study findings in a nonwhite respondent population to all its constituent communities. Third, the Leger survey was only available in English or in French. As such, this may have excluded participants who may have heightened challenges regarding health literacy, accessing health care services, and receiving the COVID‐19 vaccine. Fourth, our multivariable linear regression for both White and racialized parent respondents explained less than 50% of the variability in COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust. Derivation and validation of additional factors that inform vaccine mistrust in Canada are therefore needed in future research. Fifth, nonresponse bias cannot be ruled out given the response rate obtained in our study. Nonetheless, our findings of vaccine mistrust are consistent with data from other jurisdictions and settings, and correlate to racial disparities in COVID‐19 vaccine coverage observed from nationally representative surveys [55]. In addition, panel samples often overrepresent urban, well‐educated, and White individuals with stable internet access. Despite our efforts to oversample racialized and Indigenous participants, they remained highly educated. More diverse educational representation within racial groups, given the strong link between education and vaccine uptake, is necessary in future studies. Finally, one of the limitations of this study concerns the sampling strategy used. Although the sample was first designed to be representative of the Canadian population as a whole, this random sampling approach does not necessarily ensure a sufficient number of respondents within certain racial subgroups. We oversampled racialized and Indigenous groups to avoid limiting the statistical power and precision of estimates specific to these groups. However, a more purposeful sampling strategy, stratified by factors such as race, geography, or other sociodemographic variables, or separate independent representatives for each racial group, might have better captured the intrinsic variability within each group regarding mistrust of COVID‐19 vaccines.

Conclusions

In summary, we found significant racial, gender, and age‐based differences in COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust among parents in Canada. Racialized and Indigenous groups, women, and conspiracy beliefs were associated with a higher score of COVID‐19 vaccine mistrust. Furthermore, everyday racial discrimination emerged as a key predictor among racialized respondents, emphasizing the need for community‐driven, antiracist approaches to address vaccine mistrust and improve COVID‐19 vaccine coverage. This call to action is particularly urgent, as recent studies have shown that racial discrimination increases anxiety and depression among racialized and Indigenous Canadians, which may, in turn, have a substantial impact on engagement and trust in future vaccination campaigns [56, 57].
