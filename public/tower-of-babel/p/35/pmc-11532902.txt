Aberrant overexpression of myosin 1b in glioblastoma promotes angiogenesis via VEGF-myc-myosin 1b-Piezo1 axis
Authors: Weifeng Lv, Fan Yang, Zhengmao Ge, Lele Xin, Lingxue Zhang, Yaohong Zhai, Xian Liu, Qingdong Guo, Xinggang Mao, Peng Luo, Lei Zhang, Xiaofan Jiang, Yanyu Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive intracranial malignancy with poor prognosis. Enhanced angiogenesis is an essential hallmark of GBM, which demonstrates extensive microvascular proliferation and abnormal vasculature. Here, we uncovered the key role of myosin 1b in angiogenesis and vascular abnormality in GBM. Myosin 1b is upregulated in GBM endothelial cells (ECs) compared to the paired nonmalignant brain tissue. In our study, we found that myosin 1b promotes migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis of human/mouse brain ECs. We also found that myosin 1b expression in ECs can be regulated by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling through myc. Moreover, myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis via Piezo1 by enhancing Ca2+ influx, in which process VEGF can be the trigger. In conclusion, our results identified myosin 1b as a key mediator in promoting angiogenesis via mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (Piezo1) and suggested that VEGF/myc signaling pathway could be responsible for driving the changes of myosin 1b overexpression in GBM ECs.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is one of the most common intracranial malignancy with devastating prognosis. Even though a series of advancements have been achieved during the last 30 years, it is still lack of substantial improvements in the prognosis. The ability to invade diffusely into brain parenchyma makes it difficult for radical resection. Angiogenesis plays a significant role in GBM progression and aggression, which results in abnormal vasculature and extensive microvessel proliferation (1, 2). GBM vessels are usually twisted and highly permeable. GBM cells can migrate along the blood vessels to distant areas or invade through the highly permeable vessels to the surrounding normal brain tissues (3). The abnormal GBM vasculature usually exhibits a distinct gene signature compared to the normal brain vessels (4). For instance, the expression of tight junction molecules and transporters are decreased in GBM endothelial cell (ECs) compared to the normal brain ECs (5). Genes related to vessel permeability in ECs can be classified into five major subgroups: 1. tight junction molecules, such as Occludin, Claudin-1, Claudin-3, Claudin-5 Claudin-12, ZO1, and ZO2; 2. adherens junctions and related molecules, such as cadherins, platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule (PECAM), junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs), endothelial cell-selective adhesion molecule (ESAM), connexins, annexins, and dystrophin; 3. transcytosis-related molecules such as caveolae and clathrin; 4. transporters such as fatty acids transport protein-1 (FATP-1) and the major facilitator superfamily domain-containing protein 2a (MFSD2a) which transports fatty acids; the glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) and sodium-glucose cotransporters-1 (SGLT1) which transports glucose; the transferrin receptor (TfR) and insulin receptor which transports insulin (6); 5. the extracellular matrix (ECM) enzymes such as metalloproteinases (MMPs). The importance of anti-angiogenic therapy has been recognized during the last decades and has been applied in several types of cancers in clinical practice (7, 8). However, the anti-angiogenic drug such as bevacizumab yields modest improvement in GBM treatment in clinical trials (9). Therefore, it is fundamental to deepen our knowledge of the underlying mechanisms of GBM angiogenesis and look for new strategies to improve the clinical effects.

Myosin 1b belongs to the myosin superfamily which affects the motility and morphology of cells and was once called as myosin-1α or myr1 (10). Myosin 1b is associated with actin to regulate the formation of filopodia, membrane trafficking, and the formation of post-Golgi carriers (11). It has been reported that myosin 1b is tension-sensitive and is the most widely distributed myosin superfamily isoform compared to the other members of the myosin family (12). Myosin 1b resists to mechanical loads and forward motility via binding to actin strongly (13). The function of myosin 1b has not been understood clearly. Previous studies indicated that aberrant expression of myosin 1b is involved in cancer initiation and progression. Overexpression of myosin 1b derives cancer cells toward a more migratory phenotype in cervical cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and prostate cancer (14). In addition, it has been reported that myosin 1b is upregulated in tumors under hypoxia (15) and in ECs of tumors (16, 17). Moreover, we found that myosin 1b was upregulated in the ECs of GBM (18). Nevertheless, the function of myosin 1b in tumor vasculature is still poorly understood.

Piezo1 is the pore-forming subunits of mechanically activated channels located on the cell membrane, which is widely expressed in various tissues (19) and was discovered in 2010 (20). Piezo1 forms a homotrimer and adopts a triple-blade propeller-like structure (21). The function of Piezo1 is to sense environmental signals especially mechanical forces and to activate intracellular signaling pathways such as activation of calpains to induce Ca2+ influx and arouse a series of cellular responses (22); induction of ATP release, which subsequently activates purinergic P2X and P2Y receptors (23). Piezo1 has been reported to participate in cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis (24, 25). Myosin1 superfamily are actin-based motors participating in membrane tension sensing and transduction (26). It has been reported that myosin1c forms clusters to maintain enough tension for the opening of Ca2+-dependent transduction channels (27). Myosin 1b, as a contraction and tension-sensitive protein (28, 29), was found to promote EC proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis by our study, which inspired us to explore the relationship between myosin 1b and Piezo1.

In the present study, we explored the mechanisms behind the aberrant myosin 1b overexpression in the ECs of GBM compared to the normal brain tissue and examined the function of myosin 1b in angiogenesis and vascular integrity. We clarified that vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/myc could be the underlying signaling pathway which leads to the aberrant overexpression of myosin 1b in GBM ECs. Furthermore, we demonstrated that myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis via Piezo1 by inducing intracellular Ca2+ influx. Thus, we suggest that myosin 1b plays a pivotal role in GBM angiogenesis and vascular abnormality through VEGF-myc-myosin 1b-Piezo1 axis. Our study is the first step to explore the relationship between myosin 1b and GBM vasculature, which may shed lights on therapeutic interventions for GBM patients.

Results

Myosin 1b is upregulated in the ECs of GBM

Our recently published article indicates that myosin 1b is upregulated in GBM ECs (18). To investigate the expression of myosin 1b in GBM ECs, we reanalyzed the recently published dataset of single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of ECs from tumors and its paired nonmalignant brain tissue of four GBM patients (Student’s t test; p < 0.0001) (18) (Fig. 1A). The results showed that the expression of myosin 1b is upregulated in GBM ECs compared to the normal brain ECs. In addition, we collected clinical GBM samples and established the replication-competent avian sarcoma leukosis virus (RCAS)-induced mouse GBM models. The upregulation of myosin 1b in tumor vasculature was confirmed by performing immunofluorescent stainings on patient and mouse GBM samples and the normal control tissues (Fig. 1, B and C). The morphology of the samples were shown by H&E stainings (Fig. 1, D and E). Myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis and protects the integrity of endothelium

To investigate whether myosin 1b affects angiogenesis, we used two EC lines for further study. The mouse brain endothelial cell (bEnd.3) line and human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) were transfected with plasmids or siRNA to overexpress or silence myosin 1b. The ability of cell migration and proliferation were then examined. The overexpression (Fig. S1, A and B) or knockdown (Fig. S2, A and B) efficiency of myosin 1b was verified by real-time PCR and Western blot. The expression level of myosin 1b is positively correlated with the ability of cell migration, which was examined by wound-healing assay and transwell migration assay (Fig. 2, A–D). Myosin 1b was shown to promote cell proliferation by CCK-8 assay, in which results, the EC proliferation is suppressed when myosin 1b is lacking and vice versa (Fig. 2, E and F). In addition, sprouting angiogenesis assay (Fig. 2G) and tube formation assay (Fig. S2C) was performed, which showed that myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis. To mimic the situation of myosin 1b overexpression in GBM ECs and to explore the effect of myosin 1b on the integrity of GBM vasculature, bEnd.3 and HUVEC cells were transfected with plasmids for myosin 1b overexpression (ORF). Transwell permeability assay was then performed. The permeability readout (ratio of fluorescence) was normalized to the negative control which was coated with only ECM but no cells. The results showed less amount of transmigrated fluorescent-microspheres in the myosin 1b overexpressed group, which indicates that myosin 1b promotes endothelium integrity (Fig. S1C). To further explore the mechanisms, we selected and checked 36 genes which are related to the endothelium integrity. These molecules can be classified into five major subgroups: the tight junction molecules (occludin, claudins, and ZO), transcytosis (caveolae and clathrin), transporters (MFSD2a, FATP1, GLUT1, SGLT1, and TfR), the adherens junctions (cadherins, PECAM, JAMs, ESAM, connexins, annexins, and dystrophin), and the ECM enzymes (MMPs) which can degrade the basement membrane of vessels (30). According to our results of real-time PCR, when myosin 1b is overexpressed, the transcriptional level of claudin-1, claudin-12, Zo2, clathrin, Mmp1, Mmp9, Mmp14, Msfd2a, Fatp1, Sglt1, Glut1, TfR, VE-cadherin, E-cadherin, Dystrophin, Jam-A, Connexin 43, Annexin 5, and Annexin 12 were enhanced in bEnd.3; and the level of occludin, claudin-3, claudin-5, ZO1, clathrin, MMP9, MMP14, MSFD2a, FATP1, SGLT1, TfR, VE-cadherin, Dystrophin, JAM-A, JAM-B, PECAM-1, Connexin 43, ESAM, Annexin 5, and Annexin 12 were enhanced in HUVEC, both of which indicate that myosin 1b is involved in endothelium integrity in an overall protecting manner (Fig. S1, D–G).

We also checked some of the above genes at the protein level. They are E-cadherin, VE-cadherin, MMP1, MMP9, MMP14, Annexin 1, Annexin 3, Annexin 5, Annexin 6, Annexin 10, and connexin 43. By Western blot assay, it can be verified that myosin 1b promotes the expression of E-cadherin, VE-cadherin, MMP1, Annexin 3, Annexin 5, Annexin 10, and connexin 43 in both human and mouse ECs, whereas MMP9 is upregulated in only human ECs, while Annexin 6 is upregulated in only mouse brain ECs (Fig. S1B). These results indicate that myosin 1b participates actively in protecting endothelium integrity.

Above all, our findings indicate that myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis and shows an overall protection function in vascular integrity.

VEGF promotes myosin 1b expression in ECs through myc

We next explored which signaling pathway could be involved in mediating myosin 1b overexpression in GBM ECs. Hypoxia takes place frequently in tumors including GBM due to the conflicts between rapid tumor growth and insufficient blood vessels which triggers gene expression changes. VEGF is a key responding molecule to hypoxia (31), which plays critical roles in vasculogenesis during embryonic stages and angiogenesis in adults (32). We have shown that myosin 1b is upregulated in the ECs of GBM compared to the normal brain. We therefore speculated and investigated if VEGF signaling mediates myosin 1b expression in GBM ECs. We analyzed the expression of myosin 1b in both bEnd.3 and HUVEC cells upon stimulation of VEGFA. Cells were treated with VEGFA at different concentration for different time duration. The effect of VEGFA treatment on myosin 1b expression reaches the maximum at the concentration of 25 ng/ml for 48 h for HUVEC and for 24 h for bEnd.3 cells (Fig. 3A), which conditions were used for the following experiments. Myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis via Piezo1, in which process VEGFA can be the trigger

Myosin 1b is an actin depolymerase, which belongs to the myosin superfamily of actin-based motors which were found to participate in membrane tense sensing and transduction (26). Piezo1 is located on the cell membrane and transmits mechanical stimuli into biological cues, which induces calcium influx to promote cell proliferation and migration (19). In addition, Piezo1 has been reported to promote angiogenesis (35). Therefore, we speculated and investigated if Piezo1 is involved in myosin 1b-induced angiogenesis. Piezo1 channel agonist (Yoda1) and antagonist (Dooku1) were introduced. To determine the optimal concentration, ECs were treated with Yoda1 or Dooku1 at different concentrations for 24 h. The level of intracellular calcium content is used as the indicator since Piezo1 is known as a mechanosensitive Ca2+ ion channel. The results showed that the effect of Yoda1 reaches the maximum at the concentration of 2 μM for HUVEC and 4 μM for bEnd.3, whereas the effect of Dooku1 reaches the maximum at the concentration of 4 μM for HUVEC and 2 μM for bEnd.3 (Fig. 5, A and B), which concentrations were used for further experiments. To further verify the changes of calcium content resulted from the hyper- or hypo-activated Piezo1, we tracked the calcium changes by Fura 2/AM real-time signal measurement in ECs upon Yoda1 or Dooku1 treatment. According to the results, we could confirm that the intracellular calcium influx in ECs was regulated by Piezo1 agonist or antagonist (Fig. 5, C and D). To identify if myosin 1b-mediated angiogenesis is dependent on Piezo1, HUVEC or bEnd.3 cells with constitutive myosin 1b overexpression or knockdown were treated with Dooku1 or Yoda1 respectively, and spheroid-based 3D sprouting angiogenesis assay was performed. The results showed that overexpression of myosin 1b promotes EC sprouting, whose effect was abolished by Dooku1. In consistent with this, EC sprouting was inhibited by lacking of myosin 1b but was rescued by Yoda1 stimulation (Fig. 5, E and F). These results demonstrated that myosin 1b induces EC sprouting via Piezo1. Moreover, we have also checked if VEGFA induces EC sprouting through myosin 1b in the 3D sprouting angiogenesis assay, since we have seen myosin 1b upregulation in ECs by VEGFA stimulation. The results showed that VEGFA induces EC sprouting, whose effect was abolished by knocking down myosin 1b. Additionally, we also performed the 2D tube formation experiment in the ECs, whose results were consistent with the 3D sprouting angiogenesis assay (Fig. S5, A and B). Myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis via Piezo1 by inducing intracellular Ca2+ influx

Piezo1 is known as a mechanosensitive ion channel inducing intracellular Ca2+ influx. Therefore, we would like to check if Ca2+ influx is responsible for myosin 1b-induced angiogenesis via Piezo1 channel. To determine this, spheroid-based 3D sprouting angiogenesis assay was performed by culturing ECs in Ca2+-free or control medium. The results showed that myosin 1b promotes EC sprouting only in the presence of Ca2+, whose effect was diminished by Piezo1 blockade (Fig. 7, A and B). Moreover, we could also show that the intracellular Ca2+ influx in ECs were enhanced by myosin 1b overexpression, whose effect was abolished by Piezo1 antagonist (Dooku1) or Piezo1 knockdown (Fig. 7, C and D). Discussion

Increased angiogenesis is a hallmark of GBM (36). GBM vessels are abnormal which usually demonstrates disorganized, dilated, highly permeable characters, and thickened basement membrane (3). Vessels provide oxygen and nutrients for tumor growth and guide migration of tumor cells (37). The extent of GBM vascularization is positively corelated with the malignant degree and negatively associated with prognosis (38). Therefore, anti-angiogenic therapy is considered as an important strategy for GBM treatment and attracted attentions, even though it has been failed in the phase-Ⅲ clinical trials (39). Limited approach of the anti-angiogenic drugs appeared after a temporary remission of the cancer when applying bevacizumab (9). Moreover, it was reported that some of the patients treated with bevacizumab exhibited poorer prognosis which might be due to bevacizumab-induced malignant progression of GBM (40). To focus on neovessel normalization, blood flow restoring and drug delivery may obtain benefits (41). Deep insights in GBM vasculature and novel therapeutic strategies to improve GBM treatment is in urgent needs.

Abnormal gene expression and disturbed molecular signaling pathway are triggers of enhanced angiogenesis and aberrant vasculature in cancer (42). In this study, we have been focused on myosin 1b, a protein expressed aberrantly in the ECs of GBM compared to the normal brain tissue. Myosin 1b is widely involved in proliferation, migration, and filipodia formation of cells (13). Previous studies indicate that myosin 1b regulates initiation and progression of several tumors (14, 43). In addition, negative association between myosin 1b expression and the prognosis of GBM patients has been reported (44). In contrary to the other studies of myosin 1b in cancer, we have been focused on the ECs other than the tumor cells to explore its function on GBM vasculature. In this study, we showed that myosin 1b is upregulated in GBM ECs by analyzing both human patient and mouse GBM samples. We thereafter explored the function of myosin 1b in ECs by two different endothelial cell lines. According to our experimental results, we suggested that myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis via VEGF/myc/myosin 1 b/Piezo1 axis. Besides, we also suggest that myosin 1b protects blood vessel integrity.

VEGFA is a key molecule regulating angiogenesis and is usually highly expressed in GBM, of which environment is often hypoxic (45, 46). A previous study indicated that myosin 1b expression is upregulated under hypoxia (15). Thus, VEGFA is the first molecule coming to our sights to relate to the overexpressed myosin 1b in GBM ECs. Chen et al. found that myosin 1b can block the degradation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) (a marker of hypoxic condition) by inhibiting autophagosome-lysosomal fusion, to facilitate VEGF expression and thereafter promotes tumor angiogenesis in colorectal cancer (47). This is consistent with our findings that myosin 1b is associated with VEGF and that myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis. VEGF expression can be enhanced by the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway through HIF-1α which is a transcription factor. VEGFA can activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, which in turn enhances the expression of VEGFA (48). The PI3K/AKT/mTOR is an important pathway in regulating angiogenesis (49). Myosin 1b has also been reported to regulate PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway through binding to PI3K. Besides, Yu et al. silenced myosin 1b in mouse embryonic fibroblasts and showed that it impeded the activation of AKT in the nucleus (not in cytoplasm) (49).

To uncover the downstream mediator of VEGF in regulating myosin 1b, we proposed myc as a possible transcription factor based on the promoter structure of myosin 1b by one study (33). In addition, myc is a widely accepted oncogene in a plenty of tumors including GBM (50), which regulates cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (51). Moreover, several studies indicate that myc is involved in VEGF signaling. Ma et al. found that ANXA2 promotes the progression of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) through MYC-HIF1A-VEGF (52). Ethiraj found that myc and HIF-1α intersects to regulate angiogenesis in B-cell lymphoma. Knockdown of myc inhibited the transcription and expression of VEGF and suppressed the growth of B-cell lymphoma (53). Importantly, VEGF can promote the expression of myc. Zhao et al. demonstrated that VEGF promotes the expression of myc and Sox2 in breast and lung cancer stem cell through the VEGFR-2/STAT3 pathway (54). Those studies together suggested that VEGF and myc enhance each other in a positive feedback loop. In our research, VEGFA promoted the transcription and expression of myc, which is consistent with these findings. Furthermore, we have also demonstrated that myc is a transcription factor for myosin 1b and is critical in VEGFA-induced myosin 1b expression in ECs.

Myosin 1b is functioning in membrane tension sensing and transduction (25, 55, 56), which is located at the cell membrane ruffles to connect the actin cytoskeleton with the membrane (57, 58). Piezo1, as a mechanosensitive ion channel, is also located on the cell membrane. The function of Piezo family was firstly discovered in 2010 (20), which is to translate mechanical stimuli into biological cues and then induce Ca2+ influx. It was reported that myosin 1b can also sense and transmit the signals of mechanical stimuli, which can be inhibited by lacking of Ca2+ ions (59). As an actin depolymerase, myosin 1b involves in actin filament translocation, in which process the motility of myosin 1b is regulated by Ca2+ ion through binding to the IQ motifs of myosin 1b (13, 59, 60). Piezo1 is identified as a component of focal adhesions (FA) which are active sites for actin polymerization (4, 61). Above all, myosin 1b could be associated with Piezo1. Besides, Piezo1 has been reported to play critical roles in angiogenesis. According to the publication, global or endothelial-specific knockout of mouse Piezo1 profoundly disrupted the vascular development and was embryonic lethal. In adults, Piezo1 assembles in ECs to sense the blood pressure and control the release of nitric oxide to maintain proper perfusion (24). In our study, we found connections between myosin 1b and Piezo1 for the role in regulating angiogenesis. We have shown that myosin 1b regulates the expression of Piezo1 and the Ca2+ influx. We also demonstrated that Piezo1 is indispensable for myosin 1b-induced EC sprouting and tube formation. Moreover, Ca2+ influx via Piezo1 is critical for myosin 1b-induced EC sprouting. It is worth to note that myosin 1b-induced Ca2+ influx is diminished by either Piezo1 antagonist or knocking down Piezo1. Myosin 1b failed to induce EC sprouting in the absence of Ca2+. In addition, VEGF-induced intracellular Ca2+ influx is reversed by Piezo1 knockdown. We also found that Piezo1 is highly expressed and colocalized with the endothelium of GBM, where extensive angiogenesis is ongoing. In consistent with our findings, Li et al. found that Piezo1 can promote angiogenesis through the HIF-1α/VEGF pathway, and VEGF can in turn promote the expression of Piezo1 (62).

Myosin 2, as another important member of the myosin superfamily, is an actin-binding protein and is responsible for the generation of cytoskeletal tension (63), which has also been reported to be associated with Piezo1. According to the published article, Piezo1 activity is enriched at the force-producing adhesions region of the cell, where the mechanical force was generated by myosin 2 phosphorylation by myosin light chain kinase (MLCK). This study identified a critical role of myosin 2 in Piezo1 activation and the Ca2+ influx (64). Another study by Zhang et al. examined the role of Piezo1 in ultrasonic-mediated Ca2+ influx in HUVEC. The author found that knockdown of Piezo1 decreased the influx of Ca2+ induced by ultrasonic stimulation and confirmed that ultrasonic activates MLCK via Piezo1/Ca2+/CaM/MLCK pathway, which establishes a bridge between Piezo1 and MLCK, and furtherly with myosin 2 (65). A similar conclusion was obtained by Annalisa et al. who used Oenothera biennis cell culture hydro-soluble extract (ObHEx) to activate Piezo1. The ObHEx-stimulated Piezo1 can be blocked by MLCK suppression, which indicated the involvement of myosin 2 (66). Additionally, Yao et al. suggested that Piezo1 is a component of focal adhesions in nontransformed cells but not in tumor cells. Myosin 2 contractility is required for Piezo1 recruitment to adhesions to trigger integrin β3 adhesion turnover (67). These findings suggested that myosin 2 is closely associated with Piezo1 for its recruitment and activation. In our study, we identified that myosin 1b promotes angiogenesis via Piezo1-induced intracellular Ca2+ influx. However, whether myosin 2 also involves in Piezo1-mediated angiogenesis remains to be elucidated.

In addition, we also showed that myosin 1b promotes the expression of vascular integrity–related genes, such as tight junctions, adherens junctions, transporters, transcytosis-related molecules and ECM enzymes. Moreover, we showed that less fluorescent microspheres can pass through the EC layers when myosin 1b is overexpressed. Both results indicate that myosin 1b could promote vascular integrity of GBM, which however needs to be further confirmed by in vivo studies.

Experimental procedures

Cell lines and cell culture

HUVEC and bEnd.3 cells were provided by Shaanxi Normal University. HEK293T cells were provided by the Fourth Military Medical University. HUVEC and bEnd.3 cells were cultured in endothelial cell medium (1001, ScienCell) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (0025, ScienCell), 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (1052, ScienCell), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (0503, ScienCell). HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (11995073, Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10099141C, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (15240062, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Experiments were carried out when the density of cells were 80 to 90% of confluence.

Bioinformatics analysis of myosin 1b and Piezo1 expression in ECs

scRNA-seq datasets of ECs from GBM and paired nonmalignant control brain tissue were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE162631). Information for cells and samples were obtained from the previous study (18). Four hundred and sixteen ECs from peripheral EC type I (Pe1) cluster were considered as nonmalignant brain ECs. Six hundred and thirty four ECs from tumor core EC type I (Co1) and tumor core EC type II (Co2) clusters were considered as tumor ECs.

Animals, patient tissue samples, and the preparation

C57BL6 Gtva; Arf−/−mice were generated by Eric Holland and provided by Lene Uhrbom (Uppsala University). All experimental applications were carried out in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were housed in specific pathogen free barrier facilities. C57BL6 Gtva; Arf−/−female mice were randomly separated into two groups as control group and orthotopically transplanted group. RCAS-producing DF-1 cells (RCAS-PDGFB-HA) were orthotopically transplanted by stereotaxic injection of 105 cells into 6 to 8 weeks old mice to induce GBM as described in the previous publication (68). The control group were injected with equivalent PBS. Mice in all groups were decapitated under anesthesia via intraperitoneal administration of ketamine (75 mg/kg) plus xylazine (10 mg/kg). Mice were sacrificed upon symptoms of illness or at the endpoint of 15 weeks post-injection and were perfused intracardially with 4% cold paraformaldehyde phosphate buffer (PFA, pH 7.4). Mouse brain was fixed in 4% PFA (pH 7.4) for 4 h at room temperature, dehydrated by 20%–30% sucrose for 24 h at 4 °C, and then were frozen in OCT Cryomount (4583, Sakura Tissue-Tek) for ulterior immunohistochemical stainings. All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University (permit 20220897).

Human patient samples were collected from GBM patients after tumor resection surgery immediately at Xijing Hospital which contains the tumor tissue and adjacent normal tissue. The research protocol was performed following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided their informed consents, and the whole process was monitored by the medical ethics office of Xijing Hospital. Patient samples were fixed in 4% PFA (pH 7.4) for 24 h at room temperature, dehydrated by 20%–30% sucrose for 24 h at 4 °C, and then were frozen in OCT Cryomount (4583, Sakura Tissue-Tek) for ulterior immunohistochemical stainings. Ethical permission for using patient samples was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital (KY20193098).

Transient transfection

The cells were seeded into six-well plates and transient transfection was applied using lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Thermo Fisher Scientific) followed by the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA targeting human/mouse myosin 1b or Piezo1, the corresponding control siRNA (Table S1), shRNA plasmids targeting myc (MSH074042-LVRU6H for mouse, HSH102585-LVRU6H for human, FulenGen) or the corresponding empty plasmids (CSHCTR001-LVRU6H, FulenGen) were transfected into HUVEC or b.End3 cells.

Lentivirus packing, generation, and selection of myosin 1b or myc stable overexpressing/silencing cell lines

For lentivirus packaging, HEK293T cells were seeded into 10 cm plates, the Lenti-Pac HIV Expression Packaging Kit (LT001, Genecopoeia, Inc) was applied when the cell density reaches 80% of confluence. The recombinant expression plasmids or empty plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells together with the packing plasmid mix (LT001, Genecopoeia) using lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The supernatants which contain virus were harvested after 48 h. For formation of stable cell lines, the HUVEC and bEnd.3 were transfected with recombinant virus for 24h. Stable myosin 1b overexpressing or the corresponding control cells were selected by 600 mM G418 (10131035, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in HUVEC cells and 800 mM G418 in bEnd.3 cells; stable myc overexpressing or the corresponding control cells were selected by 0.8 mM puromycin (A1113803, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific.) in HUVEC cells and 0.6 mM puromycin in bEnd.3 cells; stable myc suppression or control cells were selected by 800 mM hygromycin B (10687010, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in HUVEC and bEnd.3 cells; stable myosin 1b suppression or control cells were selected by 0.8 mM puromycin (A1113803, Invivogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in HUVEC cells and 0.6 mM puromycin in bEnd.3 cells. The following plasmids were used: lentivirus plasmids containing full-length myc (EX-Mm30812-Lv105 for mouse, EX-15779-Lv105 for human), myosin 1b (EX-Mm20868-Lv151 for mouse, EX-E1335-Lv151 for human), or their corresponding empty plasmids (EX-NEG-Lv105 or EX-NEG-Lv151) were purchased from Guang-zhou Fulengen. ShRNA plasmids targeting myosin 1b (MSH106938-LVRU6P for mouse, HSH102685-LVRU6P for human) and the corresponding empty plasmids (CSHCTR001-LVRU6P) or shRNA plasmids targeting myc (MSH074042-LVRU6H for mouse, HSH102585-LVRU6H for human) and the corresponding empty plasmids (CSHCTR001-LVRU6H) were purchased from Guangzhou FulenGen.

Cell proliferation assay

HUVEC and bEnd.3 cells were seeded at the density of 1000 cells/well into 96-well plates in septuplets. Cell proliferation was estimated using the CCK8 kit (k1018, Apexbio). To each well, 10 μl CCK-8 solution were added, and the cells were then incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured by using a microplate reader (SH-9000; Hitachi).

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (50 nM Tris–HCl, 150 nM NaCl, 1% NP40, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% deoxycholate, and 1 mM PMSF) for 30 min on ice. A total of 30 μg protein was separated by gradient SDS-PAGE (4%–12% polyacrylamide). Proteins were then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked, immunoblotted with the indicated primary antibodies, and subsequently incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies. The signals were detected with the enhanced chemiluminescence system (Tanon 5500, Tanon Science and Technology). The following commercial antibodies were used: rabbit anti-VE-cadherin (ab205336, Abcam), rabbit anti-E-cadherin (3195T, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-MMP1 (ab134184, Abcam), rabbit anti-MMP9 (ab76003, Abcam), rabbit anti-MMP14 (ab51074, Abcam), rabbit anti-ANXA1 (32934T, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-ANXA3 (ab127924, Abcam), rabbit anti-ANXA5 (8555S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-ANXA6 (29015S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-ANXA10 (ab213656, Abcam), rabbit anti-connexin 43 (83469T, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-GAPDH (5174S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-myosin 1b (EPR16223, Abcam), rabbit anti-myc (9402S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-Piezo1 (15939-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit anti-actin (8457S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-tubulin (T3526, Sigma-Aldrich), and HRP-anti-rabbit IgG (7074, Cell Signaling Technology).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from bEnd.3 and HUVEC cells by using RNAiso Plus (9109 Takara Bio). The One-Step SYBR PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit (RR820A Takara Bio) was used for quantitative RT-PCR detection. The RT-PCR was conducted by using a Bio-Rad C1000 thermal cycler with the following conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The primers used in this study were synthesized by Beijing Genomics Institute and the sequences are listed in the Table S2. The relative expression level was calculated according to β-actin as the endogenous control.

Immunostainings on mouse and patient samples

Five micrometer tissue sections of mouse or human patient samples were cut with a Leica cryostat sectioning machine. Tissue sections were fixed in ice-cold methanol, blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by Alexa Fluor–labeled secondary antibodies incubation for 1 h at room temperature and mounted by DAPI Staining Solution (ab228549, Abcam). Images were acquired through 40 × or 20 × objectives by Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope. The following antibodies were applied: anti-CD31 (1:500, MA3105, Thermo Fisher Scientific), rabbit anti-myosin 1b (1:200, EPR16223, Abcam), rabbit anti-Piezo1(1:200, 15939-1-AP, Proteintech), goat anti-Armenian hamster Alexa 568 (1:1000, ab175716, Abcam), goat anti-Armenian hamster Alexa 488 (1:1000, ab173003, Abcam), goat anti-rabbit Alexa 594 (1:1000, ab150080, Abcam), and donkey anti-rabbit Alexa 488 (1:1000, ab150073, Abcam).

Wound-healing assay

For the wound-healing (scratch wound) assay, 2 × 105 cells/well were plated into a 6-well plate (three wells per group) and incubated to reach the confluence. The monolayer was scratched by using a fine pipette tip and washed with serum-free medium to remove detached cells. The cells were then cultured in complete endothelial cell medium supplemented with half of the serum supply concentration. Cells were photographed at 24 h post-wounding for myosin 1b knock down group and at 12 h for myosin 1b overexpressed group. The closure area of wound was calculated as follows: migration area (%) = (A0 –An)/A0 × 100, where A0 represents the area of initial wound area and An represents the remaining area of wound at the metering point.

Transwell permeability assay

Transwell inserts (0.8 μm, for 24-well plate) (3422, BD bioscience) were coated with ECM gel (3536-005-02, R&D Systems) and incubated for 3 h in incubator. Approximately 3000 cells of bEnd.3 or HUVEC with/without stable myosin 1b overexpression were seeded in each transwell. Sixteen hours post seeding, the medium of the upper chamber was changed to be medium containing 0.5% fluorescent-microsphere (234,340, Sigma Aldrich). Two hours later, take 100 μl medium per well to measure. The permeability was calculated as follows: permeability rate (%) = An/A0 × 100, where A0 represents the fluorescence absorbance value of the medium from the upper chamber and An represents the fluorescence absorbance value of the medium in the lower wells.

Transwell migration assay

Transwell migration assay was performed by using transwell insert (3422, BD bioscience) with a filter of 8 μm pore which were coated with ECM gel (3536-005-02, R&D Systems). 4 × 104 cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber of the insert and complete medium was added to the lower chamber. After 36 h incubation, the cells were fixed with methanol and stained with Giemsa. Cells on the top surface of the ECM gel were wiped off, and then the migrated cells in the gel were examined with Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope at 20 × magnification. Four random fields were photographed for counting purposes and the average number of migrated cells was used as a measure of migration capacity.

Intracellular calcium content detection

Cells with/without stable myosin 1b overexpression/knockdown were seeded in 6-well plates and grew until the cell density reaches 60% of the confluence; Yoda1, Dooku1, or VEGFA was added and incubated for 24 h, wash the cells with PBS, and then the intracellular calcium was determined according to the manufacturer’s procedure of the intracellular calcium content detection kit (s1603s, Beyotime). Piezo1 siRNA was transfected if applicable when the cells grow to 40% of confluence after seeding, medium with/without VEGFA was replaced 6 h post-transfection, and incubated for 24 h before checking. The agonist Yoda1 and the antagonist Dooku1 was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (SML1558-5 mg for Yoda1, SML2397 for Dooku1). Yoda1 or Dooku1 was dissolved and stored in DMSO (D8418, Sigma-Aldrich) at the concentration of 10 mM and was added to the cells at the final concentration as the following: 2 μM Yoda1 for HUVEC and 4 μM for bEnd.3; 4 μM Dooku1 for HUVEC and 2 μM for bEnd.3. VEGFA was purchased from MCE (HY-P7110 for human, HY-P70638 for mouse, MCE) and added to the cells at the final concentration of 25 ng/ml. The intracellular calcium concentration was calculated according to the equation of the standard curve: C = (OD value-0.028)/(1.2279 × N × V), where N represents times of dilution and V represents detection volume.

In vitro 3D spheroid cultivation and sprouting angiogenesis assay

Myosin 1b stable overexpression/knockdown cell lines were established for HUVEC and bEnd.3. Approximately 1 × 105 cells were added and suspended into the AggreWell400 Microwell culture plates (34,425, STEMCELL) per well to form spheroids. The cells were cultured in complete endothelial cell medium for two weeks in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Harvest and suspend the spheroids in a solution of 3 mg/ml rat tail collagen type I (354,236, Corning) with/without the addition of VEGFA (25 ng/ml), Yoda1(2 μΜ for HUVEC, 4 μΜ for bEnd.3), or Dooku1(4 μΜ for HUVEC, 2 μΜ for bEnd.3), respectively. The spheroid mixture was added onto 24-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. When the collagen gels were set, 100 μl complete endothelial cell medium with/without VEGFA (25 ng/ml), Yoda1 (2 μΜ for HUVEC, 4 μΜ for bEnd.3), or Dooku1 (4 μΜ for HUVEC, 2 μΜ for bEnd.3) was added respectively in each well and let the spheroids form sprouts for 24h. Sprouts were imaged by a Leica TCS SP8-X confocal microscope (Leica) at 100 × magnification. Average sprouts length and total numbers of sprouts were measured.

For the experiments exploring the role of Ca2+ ions in the myosin 1b–induced proangiogenic effect, the no calcium DMEM (21,068, Gibco) or DMEM (11960, Gibco) was used instead of the complete endothelial cell medium during the sprouts formation period. In this experiment, spheroids were cultured and formed in complete endothelial cell medium, harvested, and suspended as described above. When the collagen gels were set, 100 μl no calcium DMEM (21068, Gibco) or DMEM (11960, Gibco) without FBS supplementation (to exclude the effect of Ca2+ from FBS) was added per well and let the spheroids form sprouts for 16 h.

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells were seeded into six-well plates and grew until 60% of the confluence, transfect the cells with myc-encoding plasmids (EX-myc-pEZLv105) or the corresponding empty plasmids (EX-pEZ-Lv105), together with the control luciferase vector pGL3.0-Luc or the luciferase reporter pGL3.0-pmyosin 1b-Luc (Guangzhou Fulengen). The cells were harvested 48 h after transfection. The Luc activity was determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions (LF031, Guangzhou Fulengen).

Fura 2/AM Ca2+ real time measurement

Approximately 1 × 104 HUVEC or bEnd.3 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and grew for 24 hours. Wash the cells with HBSS (PB180123, Pricella) containing 1% bovine serum albumin for 3 times and incubate with HBSS supplemented with 2.5 μM Fura 2/AM for 45 min and then wash with HBSS buffer to remove the extracellular probes, add preheated HBSS buffer, place in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 30 min until measurement. Fluorescent signal was detected by Tecan Spark fluorescence microplate reader (Tecan) every 18 s with an alternating excitation at the wavelength of 340 and 380 nm, and the emission fluorescence was recorded at 492 nm. At the end of the experiment, a free-of-cells region was selected, and the averaged background frame was collected at each excitation wavelength for background correction. The amount of intracellular Ca2+ was calculated as fluorescence 340 readout/fluorescence 380 readout. In order to evaluate the function of Yoda1 or Dooku1, after 10 cycles of measurements, the cells were challenged with HBSS containing Yoda1 or Dooku1, and the Ca2+ was measured as described above.

H&E staining

Cryosections (5 μm thick) of mouse or patient GBM samples were stained with H&E Staining Kit (C0105S, Beyotime). Images were examined under bright-field using a slide scanner (Slideview VS200, Olympus).

ChIP assay

ChIP was performed using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (magnetic beads) (9003, Cell Signaling Technology), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected in a 15 ml Falcon tube and cross-linked by adding 1% (vol/vol) formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by the addition of glycine for 5 min at room temperature, and cells were washed twice with cold PBS. Cross-linked chromatin was digested with micrococcal nuclease for 20 min at 37 °C and then aliquoted and sonicated in 500 μl volume on ice. ChIP was performed using the rabbit anti-myc (9402S, Cell Signaling Technology) and mouse IgG (a negative control) (12–371B, Millipore) antibodies. After overnight antibody incubation at 4 °C, protein G magnetic beads were added and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. Protein G magnetic beads were washed extensively with low- and high-salt ChIP buffer. After reverse cross-linking by incubation at 65 °C for 2 h, chromatin DNA was eluted with DNA-elution buffer, and the precipitated DNA fragments were extracted and analyzed by quantitative PCR. The primer sequences used for ChIP–PCR were as follows: human myosin 1b promoter forward: TTCGCCCATTATCACACCAC; human myosin 1b promoter reverse: GCATAAGAACCACAGGCGCT; mouse myosin 1b promoter forward: ATCCTCACCTCACCCCATGA; mouse myosin 1b promoter reverse: TCTTCACCACGCTGGGATTC.

Tube formation assay

In vitro tube formation assay was performed by using the Angiogenesis Assay Kit (AB204726, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s procedure. Briefly, extracellular matrix solution was added to 96-well culture plate and was incubated for 1 h at 37 ºC to allow the solution to form a gel, then the cells were plated onto the gel and medium was added with VEGF, Yoda1, or Dooku1 at the concentration as follows: 25 ng/ml VEGFA for HUVEC and bEnd.3; 2 μM Yoda1 for HUVEC and 4 μM for bEnd.3; 4 μM Dooku1 for HUVEC and 2 μM for bEnd.3. Cells were incubated for 18 h to allow tube formation and were examined by using light microscopy. Images were acquired under 100 × bright-field. Three images were acquired and quantified for each group. The tubes were quantified by counting the numbers of the tubule structure in each image. A closed and uninterrupted loop structure was defined as “a completed tubule structure” and was counted as one tube. When the loop was interrupted, it was counted as 0.5 tube. Three independent experiments were performed.

Image analysis

Imaging of immunofluorescent staining was done by using the Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope and the ZEN 3.5 software. Images were analyzed by using the Image J 1.45s software (National Institute of Health).

Statistics

Statistics was performed by using the GraphPad Prism software and R software. The data were tested for normality by SPSS software, version 16.0 (SPSS, Inc). For comparison of two-group dataset, 2-sided, unpaired t test was applied. The nonparametric two-tailed Mann-Whitney analysis was used when data did not pass normality test. For comparison of datasets that were more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was applied. In this study, n represents individual samples. Each individual data point represents an independent biological replicate. Error bars in the graphs represent the SD. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, ∗; p < 0.01, ∗∗; p < 0.001, ∗∗∗; p < 0.0001, ∗∗∗∗.
