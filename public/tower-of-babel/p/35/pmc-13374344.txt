Diagnostic performance of systemic inflammatory and nutritional indices and their association with clinicopathological features in endometrial cancer: a retrospective study
Authors: Şule Gül Aydın, Şahin Yüksek, Mehmet Güzel, Mehmet Çopuroğlu, Hasan Can Toyganözü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic inflammatory and nutritional indices derived from routine blood tests have been widely investigated as prognostic markers; however, their diagnostic value in differentiating benign endometrial disease from endometrial cancer (EC) remains insufficiently defined. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of inflammatory and nutritional indices and to explore their associations with clinicopathological features in EC.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 270 patients who underwent surgical evaluation for endometrial pathology. The cohort comprised 135 patients with histopathologically confirmed endometrial cancer and 135 patients with benign endometrial conditions. Preoperative inflammatory indices, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI), were calculated. Clinicopathological characteristics and immunohistochemical findings were recorded. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of malignancy.ResultsAll inflammatory indices and PNI were significantly higher in the malignant group (all p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, age, body mass index, haemoglobin level, SII (OR = 1.004, 95% CI 1.001–1.007; p = 0.018), and PNI (OR = 1.380, 95% CI 1.228–1.550; p < 0.001) emerged as independent predictors of malignancy. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that PNI had limited discriminative ability (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.620, 95% CI 0.554–0.687), with a cut-off value of 52.0 (sensitivity 69.6%, specificity 50.4%).Within the malignant cohort, lower PNI values were significantly associated with adverse clinicopathological features, including deep myometrial invasion, cervical stromal invasion, lymph node metastasis, higher tumour grade, serous histology, and abnormal p53 expression.ConclusionsSystemic inflammatory and nutritional indices may provide complementary information in the evaluation of endometrial pathology. While SII appears to be associated with malignancy risk, PNI primarily reflects tumour aggressiveness rather than diagnostic discrimination. These readily available biomarkers may support preoperative risk stratification, particularly in patients with inconclusive imaging or biopsy results, where additional risk stratification may help guide the urgency or extent of surgical planning; however, further prospective studies are required to confirm their clinical utility.Keywords: Benign endometrial disease, Endometrial cancer, Prognostic nutritional index, Systemic immune-inflammation index

Introduction

Endometrial cancer ranks as the sixth most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women globally and represents the most common gynaecologic cancer in developed countries [1]. Although early stage disease is often associated with favourable outcomes following surgical treatment, the 5-year overall survival rate decreases dramatically to approximately 20–26% in patients with advanced or recurrent disease [2]. This substantial prognostic disparity highlights the need for reliable, non-invasive biomarkers that can improve risk stratification and support preoperative diagnostic and prognostic decision-making [3]. In this context, there is an increasing need for cost-effective and readily accessible preoperative tools, particularly in resource-limited or rapid clinical decision-making settings.

Systemic inflammation and nutritional status play critical roles in cancer development, progression, and metastasis [4]. Interactions between tumour cells and circulating inflammatory components, including neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and platelets, influence tumour behaviour and clinical outcomes in endometrial cancer [2]. In this context, inflammatory indices such as NLR, PLR, and MLR, as well as composite markers like SII and SIRI, have been investigated, with higher values associated with adverse oncologic outcomes [2, 5–7]. Similarly, the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), reflecting both nutritional and immune status, has been linked to unfavourable clinicopathological features, including deep myometrial invasion, high tumour grade, lymph node metastasis, and reduced survival [3, 4]. Recent experimental studies have also highlighted the role of molecular pathways and novel targets in endometrial cancer progression, further emphasizing the complex biological background underlying tumour development [8, 9].

Although these indices have predominantly been studied as prognostic markers, emerging evidence suggests potential utility in distinguishing benign from malignant endometrial conditions [7, 10, 11]. However, comparative data evaluating multiple inflammatory and nutritional indices simultaneously in this context remain limited. Furthermore, the ability of these markers to reflect tumour aggressiveness beyond conventional staging systems is not fully understood, particularly given the heterogeneity of endometrial cancer and variable outcomes among patients with similar stages [12]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, SIRI, and PNI in differentiating benign endometrial pathologies from malignant endometrial disease, and to investigate their associations with clinicopathological prognostic factors.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was performed at two tertiary gynaecologic oncology centres, Malatya Training and Research Hospital and Kayseri City Hospital, and included patients who underwent surgical treatment for endometrial pathology between January 2023 and September 2025. A total of 270 patients were included, comprising 135 patients with histopathological confirmed endometrial carcinoma and 135 patients with benign endometrial diseases. Patients were included consecutively during the study period based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

All malignant cases had histologically confirmed endometrial cancer and underwent complete surgical staging, including total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, with sentinel lymph node biopsy or systematic lymphadenectomy when indicated. Tumour staging was determined in accordance with the 2023 International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification, based on the final histopathologically assessment of surgically excised specimens. Following comprehensive surgical staging and pathological classification, postoperative management decisions, including observation or adjuvant treatment modalities, were determined in accordance with current clinical guidelines and individual risk factor assessments.

The benign control group consisted of patients with histopathologically confirmed non-malignant endometrial conditions, including proliferative and secretory endometrium, endometrial hyperplasia without atypia, endometrial polyps without evidence of malignancy, and patients with uterine leiomyoma who underwent hysterectomy for abnormal uterine bleeding and had concomitant benign endometrial pathology. The selection of the control group was intended to reflect common clinical scenarios encountered during the evaluation of suspected endometrial pathology. Although premalignant lesions such as endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia (EIN) may represent a biologically closer comparator, the limited number of such cases precluded separate subgroup analysis.

Patients with acute infection within two weeks before surgery, autoimmune or chronic inflammatory diseases, hematologic disorders, concurrent malignancies, or missing laboratory or pathological data were excluded. Patients who had undergone neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy were excluded from the study. Patients receiving any concomitant systemic treatment were excluded to minimize potential confounding effects on inflammatory and nutritional parameters.

Demographic characteristics, including age and body mass index (BMI), were recorded. Preoperative laboratory data were obtained from hospital records, using blood samples collected within two weeks prior to surgery. Laboratory parameters included white blood cell count, neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts, haemoglobin, albumin, red cell distribution width (RDW), mean platelet volume (MPV), plateletcrit (PCT), and platelet distribution width (PDW). For patients with endometrial cancer, preoperative endometrial thickness was measured by transvaginal ultrasonography. Tumour size was recorded from final pathology reports. Information regarding adjuvant therapy, surgical approach (laparotomy or laparoscopy), and sentinel lymph node biopsy was retrieved from medical records.

Clinicopathological variables collected from pathology archives included histological subtype, tumour grade, depth of myometrial invasion, lymph vascular space invasion (LVSI), cervical stromal invasion, lymph node metastasis, FIGO stage, oestrogen receptor (ER) status, Ki-67 proliferation index, p53 expression status, and microsatellite status. LVSI was recorded as a binary variable (present/absent) based on pathology reports; subclassification according to extent (none, focal, substantial) was not consistently available.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumour samples and evaluated by experienced gynaecologic pathologists. p53 expression was classified according to established staining patterns. Wild-type expression was defined as heterogeneous nuclear staining with variable intensity, whereas abnormal p53 expression (p53abn) was defined as either strong diffuse nuclear positivity in ≥ 80% of tumour cells (overexpression pattern) or complete absence of nuclear staining (null pattern), in accordance with WHO classification and prior studies [13, 14]. Ki-67 expression was classified as high (≥ 40%) or low (< 40%). Oestrogen receptor (ER) expression was defined as negative when ≤ 5% of tumour cells were stained and positive when > 5% showed nuclear positivity [15] .Microsatellite status was assessed by immunohistochemical evaluation of mismatch repair (MMR) proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6) and classified as microsatellite stable (MSS) or unstable based on loss of protein expression. Molecular classification based on POLE mutation status could not be performed due to technical and cost-related limitations; therefore, POLE status was not evaluated in this study.

Systemic inflammatory indices were calculated using previously validated formulas based on peripheral blood parameters. The NLR, PLR and MLR were defined as the ratios of absolute neutrophil, platelet, and monocyte counts to the absolute lymphocyte count, respectively [6, 7].The SII was calculated by multiplying neutrophil and platelet counts and dividing the result by the lymphocyte count, while the SIRI was calculated as the product of neutrophil and monocyte counts divided by the lymphocyte count [2, 5]. PNI was determined using the conventional formula based on serum albumin levels and total lymphocyte count. PNI was calculated based on serum albumin levels and total lymphocyte count using the conventional formula: albumin (g/L) + 5 × lymphocyte count (×10⁹/L), as previously described in the literature [4, 16].

The inflammatory indices (NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, and SIRI) and PNI were selected based on their established use in the literature and their ability to reflect complementary components of systemic inflammation and immune–nutritional status. While NLR, PLR, and MLR represent individual inflammatory pathways, SII and SIRI were included as composite indices integrating multiple parameters. PNI was incorporated as a marker of combined nutritional and immunological status.

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of the data was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent samples t-test. Variables with a non-normal distribution are expressed as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons involving more than two groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of PNI. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, and the optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index, with corresponding sensitivity and specificity values reported. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine independent predictors of endometrial cancer, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables included in the multivariable model were selected based on their statistical significance in univariate analysis and clinical relevance. Given that several inflammatory indices (e.g., NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, and SIRI) are derived from overlapping haematological parameters, these variables were not simultaneously included in the model to avoid multicollinearity. Instead, among correlated indices, representative variables demonstrating the strongest and most consistent associations in univariate analyses and clinical interpretability were selected a priori. This approach ensures that the final model (Table 1) identifies truly independent clinical predictors rather than associations driven by shared upstream haematological components. To further assess potential multicollinearity, variance inflation factor (VIF) values were calculated, and all variables demonstrated VIF values close to 1, indicating no significant collinearity.

To improve interpretability of effect sizes, SII was additionally standardized using z-score transformation, given its small unit scale, and logistic regression analysis was repeated using the standardized SII variable to estimate the odds ratio per standard deviation increase. Given the retrospective design of the study, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed, consistent with previous studies in this field [7]. The adequacy of the sample size was evaluated based on the events-per-variable (EPV) principle for logistic regression, and the number of outcome events was sufficient to ensure stable and reliable estimates. However, the sample size was considered adequate based on comparable studies in the literature and the number of events per variable in the multivariable analysis.

Associations between PNI and clinicopathological prognostic factors were examined using non-parametric tests based on data distribution. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Significant differences were also observed in haematological parameters. The malignant group exhibited higher neutrophil, monocyte, and platelet counts, while lymphocyte counts were lower (all p < 0.001). Haemoglobin, albumin, RDW, and MPV values were also significantly elevated in malignant cases, whereas PCT did not differ between groups.

All inflammatory indices, including NLR, MLR, PLR, SII, and SIRI, were significantly higher in the malignant group (all p < 0.001). Similarly, PNI levels were significantly higher in the malignant group [54.45 (50.95–58.05) vs. 52.00 (47.05–56.25), p < 0.001].

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that PNI had limited discriminative ability for distinguishing endometrial cancer from benign conditions (AUC = 0.620, 95% CI: 0.554–0.687, p = 0.001). The optimal cut-off value determined by the Youden index was 52.0, with a sensitivity of 69.6% and a specificity of 50.4% (Fig. 1). Despite this modest discriminative performance, the relatively higher sensitivity suggests potential utility in identifying patients at increased risk when interpreted alongside other clinical parameters.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine independent predictors of endometrial cancer (Table 1). Increasing age was independently associated with malignancy (OR = 1.145, 95% CI 1.083–1.211, p < 0.001). Higher BMI also remained a significant predictor (OR = 1.074, 95% CI 1.008–1.144, p = 0.028). Haemoglobin level was independently associated with malignancy (OR = 1.592, 95% CI 1.175–2.156, p = 0.003). Among inflammatory and nutritional indices, the SII showed a statistically significant association with malignancy (OR = 1.004, 95% CI 1.001–1.007, p = 0.018). The PNI was identified as an independent predictor (OR = 1.380, 95% CI 1.228–1.550, p < 0.001).

To improve interpretability, SII was additionally standardized (z-score transformation), and logistic regression analysis was repeated. When expressed per standard deviation increase, SII remained strongly associated with malignancy (OR = 3.512, 95% CI: 2.266–5.445, p < 0.001). Collinearity diagnostics showed that all variables had VIF values close to 1, confirming the stability of the multivariable model.

Among the 135 patients with endometrial cancer, the mean age was 61.4 ± 9.1 years and the mean body mass index was 32.3 ± 6.6 kg/m² (Table 3). The mean tumour size was 3.62 ± 2.06 cm. Myometrial invasion was < 50% in 83 patients (61.5%) and ≥ 50% in 52 patients (38.5%). Cervical stromal invasion was present in 16 patients (11.9%), while lymph vascular space invasion was identified in 33 patients (22.4%). Lymph node metastasis was detected in 20 patients (14.8%). Histologically, most tumours were endometrioid (85.2%), followed by serous (10.4%) and other subtypes (4.4%). Tumour grade distribution showed grade 1 and grade 2 tumours each in 53 patients (39.3%), and grade 3 tumours in 29 patients (21.5%). FIGO staging demonstrated a heterogeneous distribution across early and advanced stages. Oestrogen receptor positivity was observed in 118 patients (87.4%), and high Ki-67 expression was present in 48 patients (35.5%). Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that age, BMI, haemoglobin level, SII, and PNI are independently associated with the presence of endometrial cancer, while several other inflammatory markers showed significant associations in univariable analysis. In addition, lower PNI values were associated with adverse clinicopathological features among patients with malignancy. In our study, both age and BMI were significantly higher in the malignant group and remained independent predictors of endometrial cancer in multivariable analysis. This finding is consistent with the guidelines of the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO), the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO), and the European Society of Pathology (ESP), which highlight age as an independent prognostic factor associated with more aggressive tumour features and poorer oncologic outcomes, beyond its association with comorbidities [1]. In addition, obesity contributes to endometrial carcinogenesis through chronic inflammation and oestrogen excess [12]. These results reinforce the well-established role of age and obesity in malignant transformation of endometrial pathology.

Inflammatory markers, including the NLR, PLR, and MLR were significantly higher in patients with endometrial cancer than in those with benign uterine conditions. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting the diagnostic utility of systemic inflammatory indices in distinguishing malignant from benign endometrial disease. In particular, Qin et al. demonstrated that elevated NLR, PLR, and MLR were independently associated with endometrial cancer, while Marín et al. similarly reported significantly higher preoperative inflammatory indices in malignant cases, reflecting tumour-related systemic inflammation [7, 17].

In addition to their diagnostic implications, inflammatory markers have been extensively investigated for their prognostic significance in patients with endometrial cancer. A comprehensive meta-analysis by Jiale Lang et al. demonstrated that elevated preoperative NLR, PLR, and MLR were significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in endometrial cancer. Higher NLR and PLR values were particularly correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including advanced FIGO stage, deep myometrial invasion, lymph node metastasis, and higher tumour grade. The combined indicator with high NLR + high PLR + high MLR showed the poorest prognostic value [18]. These findings support the concept that systemic inflammatory response reflects tumor burden and immune suppression, thereby contributing to unfavourable oncologic outcomes in endometrial cancer [6]. Recent studies have further emphasized the role of systemic inflammatory response in endometrial cancer. Rossini et al. demonstrated that additional clinical risk factors are associated with measurable inflammatory changes even in early-stage disease [19]. Similarly, the SIR-EN index, a novel biomarker combining the SII and endometrial thickness, has been proposed for identifying patients at risk of endometrial carcinoma [20]. Muangto et al. also reported that preoperative NLR and PLR may serve as useful predictive markers. These findings are consistent with our results [21].

In the present study, RDW and MPV were also significantly higher in patients with endometrial cancer compared with the benign group in univariable analysis, supporting their potential role in malignancy prediction. RDW reflects heterogeneity in erythrocyte size and has been increasingly linked to cancer-related inflammation through cytokine-mediated mechanisms, particularly involving interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-α [22]. Previous studies have demonstrated associations between elevated RDW levels and advanced tumour stage, lymph node involvement, and poor prognosis in endometrial cancer. Notably, Eoh et al. reported that preoperative RDW values were significantly correlated with FIGO stage, body mass index, lymph node metastasis, and recurrence, while Kemal et al. reported that RDW levels were significantly elevated in patients with endometrial cancer compared with benign controls and increased progressively with higher tumour grade. Similarly, Zhong et al. identified RDW combined with CA-125 as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival [23–25].

MPV, a surrogate marker of platelet activation, has also been implicated in tumour progression and cancer-related inflammation. Although platelet consumption at inflammatory sites may theoretically reduce MPV, multiple studies have demonstrated increased MPV levels in various malignancies. In endometrial pathology, Yayla Abide et al. reported significantly higher preoperative MPV values in patients with endometrial carcinoma and endometrial hyperplasia compared with healthy controls [26]. Consistently, Karateke et al. observed the highest MPV values in patients with endometrial carcinoma, intermediate values in those with endometrial hyperplasia, and the lowest MPV values in the control group [27]. Additionally, Kurtoğlu et al. demonstrated that MPV had predictive value in distinguishing benign from malignant endometrial disease, supporting the role of platelet activation in endometrial carcinogenesis [28]. In line with previous studies, our findings further emphasize the role of MPV as an easily accessible inflammatory marker indicative of platelet activation in endometrial carcinogenesis.

SIRI has been less extensively investigated in endometrial cancer compared with other inflammatory markers. Previous studies evaluating inflammatory indices have emphasized their prognostic relevance rather than diagnostic utility; notably, elevated PIV and SIRI have been reported as independent predictors of both OS and PFS in endometrial carcinoma [5]. In line with these findings, a large multicentre study incorporating clinicopathological and inflammatory parameters into a nomogram identified SIRI as an independent predictor of recurrence-free survival, further highlighting its prognostic significance in endometrial cancer [12]. In contrast to the predominantly prognostic focus of existing literature, our study demonstrated that SIRI was not only associated with adverse outcomes but was also significantly higher in malignant cases compared with benign endometrial conditions in univariable analysis.

Although several inflammatory markers, including NLR, PLR, MLR, RDW, MPV, SIRI, and PNI, were significant in univariate analysis, only the SII and the PNI retained independent significance in multivariable analysis, suggesting that composite indices may provide more robust and integrative information than single inflammatory ratios. Although inflammatory markers may be influenced by factors such as age and BMI, the persistence of significant associations after multivariable adjustment suggests that these markers are independently related to tumour presence.

Among the evaluated indices, the SII emerged as an independent predictor of malignancy. SII reflects the combined effects of neutrophil- and platelet-mediated tumour-promoting inflammation and lymphocyte-mediated antitumor immune response. Although SII has been predominantly investigated as a prognostic marker in endometrial cancer, with higher levels associated with poorer overall and progression-free survival, recent studies have also highlighted its potential diagnostic utility [2, 5]. In this context, Kose et al. demonstrated significantly higher SII values in patients with endometrial cancer compared with benign and EIN groups, supporting the role of SII in distinguishing malignant from non-malignant endometrial pathology [29]. Consistent with these findings, our results suggest that SII may capture early tumour-related systemic inflammatory changes and could aid in preoperative risk stratification between benign and malignant endometrial conditions, in addition to its well-established prognostic significance.

An important and clinically relevant finding of this study concerns the behavior of the prognostic nutritional index. Data on the diagnostic value of PNI in differentiating benign from malignant endometrial pathology are limited. Aytekin et al. evaluated PNI and other inflammatory markers in patients with EIN and found no significant predictive value for concurrent endometrial carcinoma, likely due to the predominance of early-stage disease in their cohort [30].

In contrast, PNI was significantly higher in the malignant group and remained an independent predictor in multivariable analysis. This finding may be explained by the predominance of operable, early-stage patients with relatively preserved nutritional status, as well as the composition of the benign group, which included conditions associated with chronic abnormal uterine bleeding that may lower haemoglobin and albumin levels.

This apparent paradox may reflect stage-dependent biological effects: while preserved nutritional status contributes to higher PNI at diagnosis, progressive tumour burden and systemic inflammation in advanced disease are associated with declining PNI levels. Accordingly, PNI should be interpreted cautiously in diagnostic settings, as it may reflect a combination of baseline clinical factors and tumour-related biological processes rather than tumour presence alone.

This apparent dichotomy may reflect overlapping mechanisms. Baseline nutritional–immune status can be influenced by heterogeneous benign conditions, whereas significant catabolic decline and systemic inflammation become more evident with tumour progression and immune modulation. This interpretation aligns with findings in other gastrointestinal malignancies, where PNI has been shown to correlate more closely with disease burden and progression rather than initial disease presence [31, 32].

Consistent with this interpretation, lower PNI values within the malignant cohort were significantly associated with adverse clinicopathological features, including deep myometrial invasion, cervical stromal invasion, lymph node metastasis, higher tumour grade, serous histology, and abnormal p53 expression. These findings are in line with previous studies demonstrating that decreased PNI reflects cancer-related malnutrition, systemic inflammation, and impaired immune response, and is associated with poorer survival outcomes [3, 4, 33]. Similarly, specific histopathological patterns such as the microcystic, elongated, and fragmented (MELF) pattern have also been associated with adverse tumour characteristics, including deeper myometrial invasion and advanced stage in endometrial carcinoma [34], further supporting the link between tumour aggressiveness and unfavourable pathological features.

The relatively low AUC and modest sensitivity and specificity further support the limited diagnostic performance of PNI. Taken together, these findings suggest that PNI has limited diagnostic specificity but retains potential clinical relevance in reflecting tumour aggressiveness once malignancy is established. Therefore, PNI should be considered a complementary rather than a standalone biomarker [4, 33, 35]. From a clinical perspective, this level of sensitivity may still be relevant for initial risk stratification, particularly when combined with imaging or other clinical findings. Recent molecular studies, including analyses based on The Cancer Genome Atlas, have further highlighted the clinical relevance of novel biomarkers in endometrial cancer, supporting the integration of molecular and systemic markers for improved risk stratification [36].

This study has several strengths. The relatively large and well-balanced cohort enhances the reliability of comparisons. In addition, the simultaneous evaluation of multiple inflammatory and nutritional indices provides a comprehensive assessment. Furthermore, the inclusion of detailed clinicopathological and immunohistochemical data increases the clinical relevance of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias and limits causal inference. Second, the study was conducted at two tertiary centres, which may limit generalizability. Third, survival outcomes such as overall survival and progression-free survival were not evaluated. Fourth, the extent of LVSI could not be subclassified (none, focal, substantial) due to limitations in retrospective pathological data. Finally, although molecular characterization was partially performed, POLE mutation status could not be assessed due to technical limitations, and molecular subgroup analyses were therefore limited. Another important limitation is that the benign control group comprised a heterogeneous spectrum of conditions, including cases with abnormal uterine bleeding (e.g., leiomyoma), which may independently influence systemic inflammatory and nutritional parameters such as haemoglobin levels, platelet counts, and PNI. This potential confounding effect could partly contribute to the observed differences between benign and malignant groups. Therefore, the diagnostic performance of these indices should be interpreted with caution, and future studies using more homogeneous control groups are warranted. In addition, although adjuvant therapy data were reported, treatment decisions were based on postoperative pathological findings and may have been influenced by early prognostic factors, potentially introducing a degree of confounding. However, all analyses were conducted using pre-treatment blood parameters, which reduces the likelihood of reverse causality.

Future prospective, multicentre studies with larger populations and integrated molecular profiling are warranted to validate these findings. In addition, longitudinal evaluation of inflammatory and nutritional markers may provide further insight into their dynamic role in disease progression and clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that systemic inflammatory and nutritional indices offer complementary insights into endometrial pathology. While SII appears to reflect malignancy-related inflammatory changes, PNI is more closely linked to tumour aggressiveness and adverse clinicopathological features. Rather than serving as standalone diagnostic tools, these markers may function as supportive parameters in the clinical evaluation process. Incorporating such easily accessible biomarkers into preoperative assessment models may enhance individualized risk stratification. These markers may be particularly useful in patients with inconclusive imaging or biopsy findings, where additional risk stratification may help guide the urgency or extent of surgical planning. Nevertheless, prospective studies incorporating survival outcomes and molecular classification are needed to further clarify their clinical applicability.
