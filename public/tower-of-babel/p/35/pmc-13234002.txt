Effects of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated CEP55 gene knockout on immune evasion mechanisms of liver cancer cells
Authors: Chenwei Hu, Wei Zhang, Hailong Ge, Yu Wang, Chen Chao, Xingsong Shi, Xiaobing Zhou, Chen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the role of centrosomal protein CEP55 in immune evasion by liver cancer cells and evaluates the effects of its knockout using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. CEP55-knockout models were established in human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines Huh7 and HepG2, and alterations in immune-related molecules, tumor cell behavior, and antitumor immune responses were systematically assessed. CEP55 knockout significantly reduced PD-L1 expression while upregulating MHC class I levels, thereby enhancing tumor immunogenicity. Mechanistically, CEP55 deletion attenuated STAT1 activation, particularly under interferon-γ (IFN-γ) stimulation, suggesting involvement of the IFN-γ–STAT1 signaling axis in CEP55-mediated immune regulation. In parallel, CEP55 knockout markedly decreased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and suppressed the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β, indicating remodeling of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Functional assays demonstrated that CEP55 deficiency inhibited tumor cell migration and invasion and promoted apoptosis. Importantly, co-culture experiments revealed that CEP55 knockout enhanced T cell effector function, as evidenced by increased secretion of IFN-γ and Granzyme B and restored T cell–mediated cytotoxicity, even in the presence of IFN-γ stimulation. Collectively, these findings indicate that CEP55 promotes liver cancer immune escape and malignant progression through modulation of STAT1-dependent PD-L1/MHC-I expression, oxidative stress, and immunosuppressive signaling. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide, with approximately 800,000 new cases and more than 700,000 related deaths every year1. In East Asia, especially China, the incidence and mortality of liver cancer account for more than half of the global total, making it one of the major diseases that seriously threatens public health2. Despite advances in surgery, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, the prognosis of advanced liver cancer remains poor, with a low 5-year survival rate3. This poor prognosis is largely attributable to the high invasiveness of hepatocellular carcinoma and its complex immune evasion mechanisms4. In recent years, immunotherapy targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway has emerged as an important therapeutic strategy for liver cancer and has improved outcomes in a subset of patients5,6. However, liver cancer cells frequently evade immune surveillance by downregulating MHC-I or overexpressing PD-L1, thereby limiting the efficacy of immunotherapy7. In this context, it is particularly important to deeply explore the molecular mechanisms of immune evasion in liver cancer and find new therapeutic targets. Recent evidence has shown that aberrant expression of immune-associated oncogenes is closely linked to hepatocellular carcinoma progression and poor prognosis; for instance, elevated Vav1 expression has been significantly associated with advanced tumor stage, recurrence, and reduced overall survival in patients with HCC8. In addition, emerging transcriptomic studies have highlighted that immune evasion in hepatocellular carcinoma is not solely driven by immune checkpoint molecules, but is also tightly associated with tumor-intrinsic programs and immune microenvironment remodeling. Integrative analyses using bulk and single-cell transcriptomic approaches have revealed distinct immune infiltration patterns and gene signatures that strongly predict patient prognosis and immunotherapy response in HCC9.

CEP55 (centrosomal protein 55) is a protein that plays a key role in the process of cell mitosis10. It participates in the final process of cytokinesis by interacting with ESCRT (cytoplasmic membrane budding complex)-related proteins11,12. In recent years, studies have shown that CEP55 is abnormally highly expressed in a variety of tumors and is closely related to tumor aggressiveness and poor patient prognosis13. For example, in breast cancer, CEP55 promotes tumor cell proliferation and survival by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway14,15. High CEP55 expression is positively correlated with aberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway and promotes tumor progression by regulating downstream cell-cycle and anti-apoptotic molecules, such as Cyclin D1 and Bcl-210,16. In addition, CEP55 can also induce epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by regulating the TGF-β signaling pathway, thereby enhancing the migration and invasion capabilities of tumor cells. In lung cancer, high expression of CEP55 is closely related to changes in EMT marker molecules such as decreased E-cadherin and increased N-cadherin17,18. Beyond promoting tumor cell proliferation and migration, CEP55 has also been implicated in the maintenance of cancer stem cell–like properties. Through regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, CEP55 may enhance tumor cell self-renewal and drug resistance19. For example, in glioblastoma, the expression of CEP55 promotes the maintenance of stemness of tumor cells, thereby improving their resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy20. Although these studies have revealed the important role of CEP55 in tumorigenesis and development, currently little is known about its specific function in liver cancer, especially its role in the immune evasion mechanisms of liver cancer.

In recent years, CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology has become an important tool for studying gene function and developing cancer treatment strategies due to its efficiency, specificity and simplicity21. CRISPR-Cas9 enables targeted knockout of oncogenic drivers and can also enhance antitumor immunity through editing of immune checkpoint genes, such as PD-1 and PD-L122. For example, studies have used CRISPR-Cas9 to knock out PD-L1 in breast cancer cells, which significantly enhanced the sensitivity of tumor cells to the immune system23. In addition, CRISPR-Cas9 is also used to develop engineered T cells (such as CAR-T therapy) to improve their anti-tumor activity by editing specific genes24. However, there are currently no studies using CRISPR-Cas9 technology to explore the role of the CEP55 gene in immune evasion of liver cancer, which leaves an important gap in this research field.

This study aims to use CRISPR-Cas9 technology to knock out the CEP55 gene in liver cancer cells and explore its impact on immune escape-related molecules (such as PD-L1 and MHC-I) and immune regulatory functions. By elucidating the role of CEP55 in immune evasion, this study provides mechanistic insight into liver cancer immune regulation and supports the potential of CEP55 as a candidate target for precision immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Modeling and grouping

Tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues were collected from patients with liver cancer and quickly stored in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA and protein were then extracted for subsequent qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis. All samples were collected from patients, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzhou Jintan First People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2024011).

In this study, human liver cancer cell lines were used to construct in vitro models to investigate the effects of CEP55 gene knockout on immune-related functions of liver cancer cells. The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines HuH-7 and HepG2 were used in this study. HuH-7 cells were purchased from Solarbio Life Sciences (Beijing, China; SCC-110218), and HepG2 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA; HB-8065). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Guangzhou Yujia Biotechnology Co., Ltd., C0893-100 mL) and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Specific sgRNA sequences were designed for the CEP55 gene, and CRISPR-Cas9 sgRNA expression plasmids were constructed. After plasmids were transfected into Huh7 cells using liposome transfection, positive cell clones were screened. 48 h after transfection, Puromycin was added to screen cell lines that stably expressed the CRISPR-Cas9 system. To validate CEP55 genome editing, the sgRNA target region was amplified from Control, NC, and CEP55-knockout (KO) Huh7 cells and analyzed by Sanger sequencing. Sequencing chromatograms revealed indel-associated peak disorder around the predicted Cas9 cleavage site in KO cells, but not in Control or NC cells, confirming successful genome editing (Supplementary Figure S1). To investigate the effects of immune-related stimulation, exogenous interferon-γ (IFN-γ; Guangzhou Yujia Biotechnology Co., Ltd., P5664-100 µg) was applied to liver cancer cells. For condition optimization, cells were treated with IFN-γ at concentrations of 0, 10 and 20 ng/mL for 12 or 24 h, and the expression of immune-responsive genes was evaluated. Based on these preliminary experiments, IFN-γ at a final concentration of 10 ng/mL for 24 h was selected and used for subsequent functional assays.

Based on the constructed cell model, the cells were divided into the following five groups according to different treatment methods: (1) Control group: wild-type Huh7 cells without any gene editing or treatment; (2) NC group: Huh7 cells transfected with non-targeting (scramble) CRISPR-Cas9 sgRNA plasmids; (3) KO group: Huh7 cells transfected with CEP55-specific CRISPR-Cas9 sgRNA plasmids; (4) Control + IFN-γ group: wild-type Huh7 cells treated with 10 ng/mL IFN-γ for 24 h; (5) KO + IFN-γ group: CEP55-knockout Huh7 cells treated with 10 ng/mL IFN-γ for 24 h.

The NC group was included as a technical control to exclude potential nonspecific effects of the CRISPR-Cas9 system. Through this group design, the effects of CEP55 knockout and its interaction with exogenous IFN-γ were systematically investigated.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime Biotechnology, China; C0037). CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan™ FC microplate photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 51,119,800), and cell viability was expressed relative to the corresponding control group.

Cytotoxicity assay

T cell–mediated cytotoxicity against liver cancer cells was evaluated using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; C0016) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytotoxicity was expressed as the percentage of LDH release relative to maximum release. LDH activity in the culture supernatant was measured using a Multiskan™ FC microplate photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 51,119,800).

qRT-PCR

To detect the mRNA expression level of CEP55, TRIzol reagent (Guangzhou Yujia Biotechnology Co., Ltd, R0016) was used to extract total RNA from cancer tissues, normal tissues and Huh7 cells of liver cancer patients. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were measured using a UV spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 840–317,400), and the RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, SO131). The qRT-PCR reaction was performed using the SYBR Green kit (Guangzhou Yujia Biotechnology Co., Ltd, D7268S). The reaction conditions were 95℃ for 10 min of pre-denaturation, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ denaturation for 30 s, 60℃ annealing for 30 s, and 72℃ extension for 30 s. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene, and the relative expression was calculated by the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Three technical replicates were set for each sample (Table 1).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues and cells from different treatment groups using RIPA lysis buffer (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; PR20035), and protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. A total of 30 μg of protein per well was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA, IPVH00010). After blocking with 5% skim milk powder at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CEP55 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 81,693), PD-L1 (Abcam, UK; ab205921), MHC-I (Abcam, UK; ab240087), total STAT1 (Abcam, UK; ab92506), phospho-STAT1 (Abcam, UK; ab109461), and β-actin (Abcam, UK; ab8227). The next day, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 7074), and protein bands were detected using ECL colorimetric reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, 32,106) after incubation at room temperature for 1 h.

Flow cytometric analysis

After the indicated treatments, liver cancer cells were harvested and resuspended at 1 × 105 cells per sample. Cells were incubated with a PE-conjugated anti-human PD-L1 (CD274) antibody (BioLegend, USA, Cat#329,706) and a FITC-conjugated anti-human MHC class I (HLA-A, B, C) antibody (BioLegend, USA, Cat#311,404), together with the corresponding isotype controls, for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Samples were analyzed using a BD FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA; STEM-CAPO-0045-ZJF), and data were processed with FlowJo software. PD-L1 and MHC-I expression levels were quantified based on mean fluorescence intensity after exclusion of debris and doublets.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement

Intracellular ROS levels in Huh7 and HepG2 cells were measured using a ROS detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; S0033S). After the indicated treatments, cells were incubated with the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by three washes with PBS to remove excess probe. Representative fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan; IX73) under identical exposure settings. In parallel, fluorescence intensity was quantified using a microplate reader with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. The results were expressed as the average fluorescence intensity for statistical analysis.

ELISA

In this experiment, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to detect the concentrations of IL-10 and TGF-β in the cell culture supernatant. The culture supernatant from each experimental group was collected, centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris, and immediately stored at − 80 °C. The ELISA experiment used a 96-well ELISA plate, added a standard gradient and sample (100 μL per well), incubated for the corresponding time according to the instructions of the kit (Guangzhou Yujia Biotechnology Co., Ltd, P0206-100 mL), and incubated at 37 °C after adding the enzyme-labeled secondary antibody. Subsequently, after washing with washing solution 5 times, the color developing solution (TMB) was added, and the color was developed for 10–30 min in the dark, and the stop solution was added to stop the reaction. Finally, the OD value was read at a wavelength of 450 nm using an ELISA reader, and the concentrations of IL-10 and TGF-β in the sample were calculated according to the standard curve.

Transwell

The invasion ability of Huh7 cells was evaluated using a Transwell chamber with a polycarbonate membrane with a pore size of 8 μm. The upper chamber of the Transwell was coated with Matrigel (dilution ratio 1:8) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After 24 h of serum-free culture, the concentration of cells in each group was adjusted to 1 × 105 cells/mL, 200 μL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, and 600 μL of culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber as a chemical inducer. After 24 h of incubation, the cells that did not penetrate the membrane were wiped off, and the cells that penetrated the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, P0099) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, C0121). The number of cells that penetrated the membrane in 5 fields of view was randomly counted under a microscope. The experiment was repeated three times, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and statistical analysis was performed.

Scratch assay

The migration ability of Huh7 cells was evaluated by scratch assay. Each group of cells was inoculated in a 6-well plate. After the cells were spread to form a monolayer, a straight line was drawn in the center of the cell layer with a sterile pipette tip. The cells were gently rinsed with PBS to remove suspended cells, serum-free medium was added to inhibit cell proliferation, and the cells were placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for further culture. Images of the scratch area were taken at 0 h and 24 h, and the width of the scratch area was measured using ImageJ software and the migration distance was calculated. The experiment was repeated three times, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and statistically analyzed.

TUNEL

TUNEL staining was used to detect the apoptosis rate of tumor cells under co-culture conditions with T cells. PBMCs were isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy volunteers by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation, and then anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies (2 μg/mL, BioLegend, 422,603) and IL-2 (10 ng/mL, Guangzhou Yujia Biotechnology Co., Ltd, P5115-10 μg) were used to activate T cells for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Huh7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates, and after adherence, the control group, NC group, and KO group were cultured with untreated T cells. Tumor cells and T cells were co-cultured at a ratio of 1:5 for 24 h. After the culture, unbound T cells were washed away, and the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Then, the lytic enzyme was added according to the instructions of the TUNEL kit for digestion, and the DNA fragments were labeled at 37 °C for 1 h. TUNEL-positive cells and total cells were observed and counted under a fluorescence microscope, and the apoptosis rate was calculated (TUNEL-positive cells/total cells × 100%).

Data analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23 software, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or independent sample t-test, and P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Gene and protein expression levels were calculated and standardized according to the relative quantification method. ELISA, ROS level, invasion, migration, and apoptosis rate data were compared between groups by statistical tests, and the results were presented in charts.

Results

High expression of CEP55 in liver cancer tissues and its association with PD-L1, MHC-I, and STAT1 activation

qRT-PCR analysis showed that 10 ng/mL IFN-γ for 24 h was sufficient to induce significant IRF1 and PD-L1 expression in liver cancer cells, and this condition was therefore chosen for subsequent experiments (Supplementary Figure S2). qRT-PCR analysis showed that CEP55 mRNA expression was increased in liver cancer tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1A, p < 0.01). In liver cancer cell experiments, CEP55 mRNA expression was reduced following CEP55 knockout (Fig. 1B, p < 0.01). Western blot analysis showed that CEP55 protein expression was higher in liver cancer tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 1C, p < 0.01). In liver cancer cells, CEP55 protein expression was reduced following CEP55 knockout compared with the Control group, and this result was consistently observed in both Huh7 and HepG2 cells (Fig. 1D, E, p < 0.01). Under IFN-γ treatment, CEP55 protein expression was increased in the Control + IFN-γ group compared with the Control group, whereas CEP55 protein expression was lower in the KO + IFN-γ group than in the Control + IFN-γ group. At the same time, compared with the Control group, CEP55 knockout resulted in reduced PD-L1 expression, increased MHC-I expression, and a decreased p-STAT1/STAT1 ratio in both Huh7 and HepG2 cells (Fig. 1D, E, p < 0.01). Under IFN-γ treatment, PD-L1 expression was increased, MHC-I expression was reduced, and the p-STAT1/STAT1 ratio was increased in the Control + IFN-γ group compared with the Control group. In cells with combined CEP55 knockout and IFN-γ treatment, PD-L1 expression was lower, MHC-I expression was higher, and the p-STAT1/STAT1 ratio was lower compared with the Control + IFN-γ group (Fig. 1D, E, p < 0.01). Flow cytometric analysis showed that PD-L1 expression was decreased and MHC-I expression was increased in CEP55-knockout cells compared with the Control group (Fig. 1F, p < 0.01). IFN-γ treatment increased PD-L1 expression and decreased MHC-I expression in control cells, whereas under IFN-γ stimulation, CEP55-knockout cells exhibited lower PD-L1 expression and higher MHC-I expression than the Control + IFN-γ group (Fig. 1F, p < 0.01).

CEP55 gene knockout inhibits the invasion and migration ability of liver cancer cells

The Transwell invasion assay and wound healing assay were used to assess the effects of CEP55 knockout on the invasive and migratory abilities of liver cancer cells. The Transwell assay showed that, in Huh7 and HepG2 cells, CEP55 knockout (KO group) significantly reduced the number of invaded cells compared with the Control and NC groups (Fig. 2A, p < 0.01). IFN-γ treatment significantly increased the number of invaded cells in the Control + IFN-γ group (Fig. 2A, p < 0.01), whereas in the KO + IFN-γ group, the number of invaded cells remained significantly lower than that in the Control + IFN-γ group (Fig. 2A, p < 0.01).

Effects of CEP55 gene knockout on the invasion and migration ability of liver cancer cells. (A) Transwell invasion assay was performed to evaluate the invasive capacity of Huh7 and HepG2 cells. Scale bar = 50 μm. (B) Wound healing assay was used to assess the migratory ability of Huh7 and HepG2 cells. Scale bar = 100 μm. **p < 0.01 vs. Control; ##p < 0.01 vs. Control + IFN-γ.

Consistent results were observed in the wound healing assay. In Huh7 and HepG2 cells, CEP55 knockout significantly reduced the migration rate compared with the Control group (Fig. 2B, p < 0.01), while IFN-γ treatment significantly increased cell migration in the Control + IFN-γ group (Fig. 2B, p < 0.01). Notably, under IFN-γ stimulation, the migration rate in the KO + IFN-γ group was still significantly lower than that in the Control + IFN-γ group (Fig. 2B, p < 0.01).

CEP55 gene knockout reduces ROS levels and secretion of immunosuppressive factors and promotes apoptosis of liver cancer cells

ROS fluorescence staining showed that CEP55 knockout markedly decreased ROS levels in both Huh7 and HepG2 cells compared with Control and NC cells (Fig. 3A, p < 0.01). ELISA analysis further demonstrated that CEP55 knockout significantly reduced the secretion of the immunosuppressive cytokines TGF-β and IL-10 (Fig. 3B, C, p < 0.01). In contrast, exogenous IFN-γ treatment significantly increased ROS accumulation and the secretion of TGF-β and IL-10 (Fig. 3A–C, p < 0.01). Notably, under IFN-γ stimulation, cells lacking CEP55 maintained significantly lower ROS levels and reduced TGF-β and IL-10 secretion compared with IFN-γ–treated control cells (Fig. 3A–C, p < 0.01), indicating that CEP55 knockout effectively attenuated IFN-γ–induced oxidative stress and immunosuppressive factor production. TUNEL staining revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cells following CEP55 knockout compared with Control and NC cells (Fig. 3D, p < 0.01). In addition, ELISA results showed that co-culture with CEP55-deficient liver cancer cells significantly increased the levels of IFN-γ and Granzyme B in the culture supernatants (Fig. 3E, F, p < 0.01). Consistently, cell viability assays demonstrated that IFN-γ treatment increased tumor cell viability, whereas CEP55 knockout significantly reduced tumor cell viability under both basal and IFN-γ–stimulated conditions (Fig. 3G, p < 0.01). Moreover, cytotoxicity analysis showed that IFN-γ stimulation impaired T cell–mediated cytotoxicity, while CEP55 knockout markedly restored cytotoxic activity even in the presence of IFN-γ (Fig. 3H, p < 0.01). Collectively, these results indicate that CEP55 knockout remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, counteracts IFN-γ–induced immune evasion, enhances T cell effector function, and promotes immune-mediated elimination of liver cancer cells.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the role of CEP55 in the immune evasion of liver cancer and established CEP55-knockout models in two human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines, Huh7 and HepG2, using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. We demonstrated that CEP55 was highly expressed in liver cancer tissues and cells, and that its deletion markedly downregulated the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 while upregulating MHC-I expression, thereby enhancing tumor cell immunogenicity. Functionally, CEP55 knockout significantly suppressed tumor cell migration and invasion, indicating an important role of CEP55 in malignant progression. In addition, loss of CEP55 effectively reduced intracellular ROS levels and attenuated the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β, suggesting a remodeling of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Importantly, under both basal conditions and exogenous IFN-γ stimulation, CEP55 knockout consistently limited PD-L1 induction and ROS accumulation, highlighting its regulatory role in IFN-γ–associated immune adaptation. Furthermore, co-culture experiments revealed that CEP55 deficiency enhanced tumor cell apoptosis and promoted T-cell effector activity, supporting a functional link between CEP55 loss and improved antitumor immune responses. Collectively, these findings indicate that CEP55 acts as a tumor-intrinsic regulator that promotes immune evasion and tumor progression in liver cancer, providing experimental evidence for its potential relevance as an immunoregulatory target.

CEP55 is a protein that is abnormally highly expressed in a variety of tumors, but its mechanism of action in liver cancer has not yet been fully revealed. Studies have shown that PD-L1 inhibits the activation of T cells by binding to PD-1 on the surface of T cells, thereby allowing tumor cells to evade immune killing25. As a key molecule for antigen presentation, MHC-I expression downregulation is an important mechanism for tumor cells to evade immune surveillance26. This study found that CEP55 knockout significantly reduced the expression of PD-L1 and upregulated the level of MHC-I, which indicates that CEP55 may regulate the interaction between tumor cells and the immune system by affecting the expression of immune-related molecules. Combined with existing studies, it is speculated that CEP55 may promote PD-L1 expression and inhibit the upregulation of MHC-I by regulating signaling pathways (such as PI3K/AKT)27. In addition, the role of ROS in tumor immune evasion is dual. On the one hand, an appropriate amount of ROS can exert anti-tumor effects by promoting immune cell activity28; on the other hand, high levels of ROS can induce the secretion of immunosuppressive factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, inhibit T cell activity, and thereby promote tumor immunity escape29. This study shows that CEP55 knockout can significantly reduce ROS levels and the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β, which indicates that CEP55 may regulate the tumor immune microenvironment through ROS-mediated pathways30. In addition, although PD-L1 expression and ROS levels were significantly increased under IFN-γ treatment, CEP55 knockdown still effectively suppressed these indicators. This result is consistent with existing studies, indicating that IFN-γ induces PD-L1 expression through the STAT1 signaling pathway, and CEP55 knockout may maintain low-level PD-L1 expression by inhibiting the activation of this pathway31. This finding further validates the important role of CEP55 in tumor immune regulation. Notably, CEP55 knockout also significantly inhibited the migration and invasion abilities of tumor cells, which may be related to its regulation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)32. Studies have shown that CEP55 induces EMT by regulating the TGF-β signaling pathway, thereby promoting cell migration and invasion33. Combined with the findings of this study, CEP55 knockout may weaken the occurrence of EMT and the motility of tumor cells by reducing the secretion of TGF-β34. This mechanism deserves further exploration. Despite recent advances in immune checkpoint blockade, only a subset of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma derive durable clinical benefit. Primary and acquired resistance, tumor heterogeneity, impaired antigen presentation, and the presence of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment remain major clinical challenges that limit the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1–based therapies5. In line with these challenges, recent studies have emphasized the prognostic and therapeutic relevance of tumor-intrinsic regulators and immune microenvironment remodeling in hepatocellular carcinoma. Proteogenomic analyses have identified novel molecular signatures, such as KIF5B, that are strongly associated with poor clinical outcomes and oncogenic pathway activation in HCC, highlighting the value of targeting upstream regulators beyond classical immune checkpoints35. Moreover, high-resolution immune profiling using CyTOF and single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed that modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, including PD-L1-associated macrophage subsets and antigen-presenting phenotypes, can markedly influence tumor progression and therapeutic response in HCC36. In this context, our findings position CEP55 as a potential upstream modulator of PD-L1/MHC-I-mediated immune evasion and immune microenvironment regulation in liver cancer.

CEP55 has the potential to become an immunotherapy target for liver cancer, and its key role in tumor immune escape provides an important basis for subsequent research. From a therapeutic perspective, CEP55 may serve as an upstream regulator or combinatorial target to enhance existing immunotherapies. Pharmacological inhibition or RNA-based modulation of CEP55, in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, may represent a feasible strategy to improve antitumor immune responses and overcome adaptive immune resistance. CRISPR-Cas9 technology achieves precise targeting of CEP55 with its high efficiency and specificity, greatly enhancing the sensitivity of tumor cells to the immune system. In addition, combining CEP55 knockout with existing immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies) is expected to further improve the effect of immunotherapy. In the future, the combination of CEP55-targeted strategies with emerging immunotherapies (such as CAR-T cell therapy) may be explored to overcome immune evasion in liver cancer.

Although the findings reveal the key role of CEP55 in immune evasion of liver cancer, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, clinical tissue samples were included only to confirm CEP55 overexpression. Correlations between CEP55 expression and PD-L1/MHC-I levels or patient prognosis were not evaluated, which should be addressed in future studies with larger and well-characterized clinical cohorts. Second, CRISPR-Cas9 was used in this study as a mechanistic research tool, and the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of immediate clinical feasibility, given the current challenges related to in vivo delivery efficiency and safety. Third, this study is primarily based on in vitro cell models, and the lack of in vivo validation, together with the simplified IFN-γ stimulation conditions and the focus on PD-L1 and MHC-I, may not fully capture the complexity of the tumor immune microenvironment or the broader immunoregulatory network governed by CEP55. Future studies integrating in vivo animal models, expanded immune profiling, single-cell multi-omics approaches, and larger clinical datasets will be essential to comprehensively define the immunoregulatory role of CEP55 and to support its translational potential as a therapeutic target in liver cancer.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that CEP55 is a key regulator of immune evasion and malignant progression in liver cancer. CEP55 knockout downregulated PD-L1 expression, upregulated MHC-I levels, suppressed tumor cell migration and invasion, and reduced oxidative stress and immunosuppressive cytokine secretion. Moreover, loss of CEP55 enhanced tumor cell sensitivity to immune-mediated killing, even under IFN-γ stimulation. These findings identify CEP55 as a tumor-intrinsic modulator of antitumor immunity and provide a mechanistic basis for its further exploration as a potential immunoregulatory target in liver cancer.
