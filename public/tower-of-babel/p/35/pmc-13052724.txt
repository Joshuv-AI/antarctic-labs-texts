Effectiveness of mobile vaccine clinics on COVID-19 vaccination uptake in the USA: an observational study
Authors: Khanh N C Duong, Yue Zhang, Richard E Nelson, Andrew T Pavia, Barbara E Jones, Danielle T Nguyen, Cindy Wynette, Makoto M Jones, Matthew H Samore, Nathorn Chaiyakunapruk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLittle is known regarding the impact of mobile vaccine clinics (MVCs) on COVID-19 vaccination rates in the USA. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of MVCs in increasing COVID-19 vaccination uptake in the state of Utah.MethodsIn this longitudinal observational study, we collected and analysed data on MVCs and COVID-19 vaccinations in Utah from 1 April 2021 to 31 March 2022. The primary exposure was the weekly number of MVC days in host ZIP codes (where MVCs operated). The secondary exposure was the weekly number of MVC days in nearby ZIP codes. The outcome was the weekly first-dose vaccination rate. A mixed-effects zero-inflated beta regression model was used. Confounding variables adjusted in the model included the Health Accessibility Barriers Index, the Resource-Constrained Health System Index, the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), vaccine hesitancy and prior vaccination rates.ResultsMVCs were deployed for 2760 days (8.5 MVC days per 10 000 residents). MVC density was higher in areas with high proportions of Hispanic populations (11.7 days per 10 000 residents), urban areas (9.0 days per 100 000 residents), and areas with very high SVI (14.9 days per 10 000 residents). Each additional MVC day in a given week reduced the odds of no vaccination in that week by 79.5% (OR=0.205, 95% CI 0.064 to 0.659) in host ZIP codes and by 21.1% (OR=0.789, 95% CI 0.732 to 0.849) in nearby ZIP codes. For ZIP codes with vaccinations, each additional MVC day increased the odds of weekly vaccination rates by 1.6% (OR=1.016, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.032) and by 0.6% (OR=1.006, 95% CI 1.003 to 1.009) in nearby ZIP codes.ConclusionsMVCs increased COVID-19 vaccination uptake on both host and nearby ZIP codes. Policymakers can leverage these findings to use MVCs as a promising strategy to improve vaccine coverage for future pandemic responses or other vaccination programmes.

Introduction

Healthcare access is a critical issue in the USA, highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic.1 Limited access to healthcare resources contributed to worse COVID-19 outcomes, especially for people living in rural and underserved areas.1 2 Research found that individuals residing farther from healthcare facilities experienced 1.67 times higher COVID-19 mortality rates than those living nearby. This effect was even more pronounced in areas with larger Black and Hispanic populations.2

Healthcare access is also a key challenge to COVID-19 vaccine uptake and vaccine disparity in the USA.3 4 A study in 2022 found that counties with low vaccination rates (<50%) faced approximately 1.4 times greater healthcare access barriers and resource constraints than counties with high vaccination rates.5

Mobile vaccine clinics (MVCs) have been one of the strategies used to address access issues related to COVID-19 vaccination.4 6 These clinics bring vaccines to those in need and provide an easy vaccine registration process. This approach has been implemented worldwide, with evidence from literature supporting its effectiveness in various settings.710

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) in either low-income settings, such as Africa,7 or high-income settings, such as Switzerland,8 demonstrated that MVCs effectively increased COVID-19 vaccination uptake. Similar findings were found in real-world studies in North Macedonia,9 the United Kingdom (UK)10 and the Netherlands.11 However, the effectiveness of MVCs varied significantly across these studies.9 11 Notably, the UK study revealed that while MVCs increased overall vaccination uptake, it had a small or no effect on racial and ethnic minorities and the most socially vulnerable areas, whereas they were the primary targets of this intervention.10

In the USA, MVCs were deployed across the country during the COVID-19 vaccine rollout. Studies in Michigan,12 Massachusetts,13 Tennessee14 and North Carolina15 showed that racial and ethnic minority populations and individuals living in high social vulnerability areas were more likely to use these clinics. However, limited research has addressed the question of MVCs’ effectiveness on COVID-19 vaccination uptake. Additionally, how this effect varies across rural or urban areas, areas with high racial or ethnic minority populations or areas with different social vulnerability levels remain unknown in the USA as these factors were associated with low vaccine uptake and vaccine disparity in the US.1 3 4

In this study, we aimed to address these questions. Filling this existing literature gap is essential to have a comprehensive understanding of the role of MVCs in the COVID-19 vaccination programme in the USA. Policymakers can use these insights to enhance other vaccination initiatives and healthcare services as well as to prepare for future crises.

Materials and methods

The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline.16

Study context

This study was conducted in Utah, where 77% of the land area is classified as rural or frontier.17 The state’s population is also increasingly diverse with racial and ethnic minority increasing from 19.4% in 2010 to 24.6% in 2020.18 These geographic and demographic factors presented significant challenges for statewide COVID-19 vaccination efforts, particularly in reaching underserved populations. As of 31 March 2021, 28.2% of Utah’s population had received their first-dose COVID-19 vaccine, including 30.3% of the non-Hispanic population and 15.6% of Hispanic populations.19

To increase vaccination access among underserved populations, Utah implemented multiple strategies, including MVCs. This primary objective of the MVC strategy was to deliver vaccine directly to communities, especially in rural areas and areas with limited healthcare services. Operating as mobile vans or pop-up clinics, these MVCs were deployed in accessible community locations such as local markets, churches, workplaces, schools, faith-based organisations and senior centres. Registration processes were also simplified, which often required no prior appointment or identification.20 These MVCs were implemented through two mechanisms: (1) the state-level coordination by the Utah Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) via third-party contractors (eg, Nomi Van) to deploy MVCs on request of local health departments (LHDs) and (2) the LHDs directly run their own MVCs.

Study design and data sources

To assess the effects of COVID-19 MVCs on vaccination uptake in Utah, we conducted a longitudinal population-based observational study from 1 April 2021 to 31 March 2022.

Data on MVC events, including event name, location (ZIP code), dates and times, were obtained from the Utah DHHS and LHDs. We defined one MVC event occurring in a single day, regardless of its specific timing (weekday vs weekend) or operational hours, as a 1-day MVC. The total number of MVC days was aggregated at the ZIP code-week level.

COVID-19 vaccination data were obtained from the Utah Statewide Immunization Information System (USIIS), a centralised database of immunisation records for Utah population. USIIS is a provider-reported registry. Data are entered directly by authorised healthcare providers (eg, clinics, pharmacies and the public health departments operating the MVCs in our study) at or near the time of vaccine administration.19 The number of residents receiving their first COVID-19 vaccine dose was aggregated at the ZIP code-week level.

For analysis, we used ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs) instead of ZIP codes. ZCTAs, created by the US Census Bureau, approximate ZIP code areas while enabling access to demographic and socioeconomic data, which are unavailable for ZIP codes.21 To align ZIP codes with ZCTAs, we used the ZIP code to ZCTA Crosswalk database from the US Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA).22 We also excluded non-residential ZIP codes (ie, those with zero population) and ZIP codes with fewer than 100 residents to mitigate potential bias.23 24 For simplicity and consistency, we refer to ZCTAs as ZIP codes throughout this manuscript.

Exposure variables

The primary exposure variable was the deployment of MVCs within resident ZIP codes, measured by the weekly number of MVC days deployed within the ZIP codes (denoted as MVC within). Given potential vaccination mobility (ie, individuals travelling from their resident ZIP codes to nearby ZIP codes for vaccination) and spillover effects of MVC on nearby areas,7 11 we included a secondary exposure variable capturing the weekly number of MVC days in nearby ZIP codes (denoted as MVC nearby). ‘Nearby’ ZIP codes were defined based on the travel patterns of Utah residents for COVID-19 vaccination, as recorded in the USIIS database. Specifically, we calculated the distance between each resident’s ZIP code and the ZIP code where they received the vaccine, using the geographic centre point (centroid) of each ZIP code area from the US Census. To account for expected differences in travel behaviour, we separately estimated the median travel distance for individuals in urban and rural areas.20 We used these median values to define a ‘nearby’ ZIP code. Accordingly, a ZIP code was classified as ‘nearby’ if it was within 10 miles from an urban resident ZIP code or 25 miles from a rural one. ZIP codes were categorised as urban or rural areas based on the 2010 Rural-Urban Commuting Area codes (see online supplemental eMethods and online supplemental eMethods eFigures 1 and 2 for details).25

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was the first-dose COVID-19 vaccination uptake, measured as the weekly vaccination rate, calculated by dividing the number of individuals receiving their first-dose COVID-19 vaccine in a ZIP code by the total population of that ZIP code. ZIP code population estimates were obtained from the 5-year ACS, year 2021.26 We used total population estimates instead of the eligible population for each week (ie, those eligible for vaccination in a given week minus those already vaccinated the previous week) to simplify calculations and provide comparability with other studies.9 10

Confounding variables

Potential confounding variables influencing MVC allocation and vaccination uptake were identified based on a causal graph from a previous study.5 These included healthcare access barriers, resource-constrained healthcare systems, social vulnerability and vaccine hesitancy. We measured healthcare access barriers and constrained healthcare systems using the Health Accessibility Barriers Index and the Resource-Constrained Health System Index, respectively. Both indices are components of the US COVID-19 Vaccine Coverage Index developed by Surgo Ventures.27 Social vulnerability was measured using the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.28 Vaccine hesitancy was obtained from COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy data from the US Census Bureau’s Household Pulse Survey.29 Additionally, we adjusted for the baseline cumulative vaccination rate in each ZIP code one week before the study period, obtained from USIIS (details are provided in online supplemental eMethods eTable 1 and eFigure 3).7 8

Statistical analysis

We used a mix-effects zero-inflated beta regression model, adjusting for ZIP code-level random effects and fixed effects for time, to analyse the association between a 1-day MVC and weekly vaccination rate. This model consisted of two components: (1) a zero-inflated component, modelling the probability of no vaccination in a given week (ie, weekly vaccination rate=0) and (2) a conditional component, modelling the weekly vaccination rate in the range (0, 1). The model used a logit link function for both components.30 Effect sizes were reported as ORs with 95% CIs. The fixed effects of time were modelled as a cubic function. This specification was chosen after comparing models with linear (degree 1), quadratic (degree 2), cubic (degree 3) and locally estimated scatterplot smoothing time functions. The cubic model provided the best fit, as indicated by the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (see online supplemental eMethods eTable 2 and eFigure 4). To mitigate potential autocorrelation in the time-series data, we added the cumulative vaccination rate in the previous week (t-1) into the model, which we selected as it provided a better fit (ie, lower AIC and BIC) (see online supplemental eMethods eTable 3).

To examine MVC effects across different area characteristics, we incorporated interaction terms for rural versus urban areas, areas with high versus low proportions of Hispanic residents and varying SVI levels. Areas were classified as ‘low Hispanic’ if the proportion of Hispanic residents was less than 10%.31 ZIP code Hispanic population estimates were obtained from the 5-year ACS, year 2021.26

We obtained MVC data from Utah DHHS and 12 of 13 LHDs in Utah. Data from the San Juan County Public Health Department were unavailable. For the main analysis, we assumed zero MVC days for ZIP codes in San Juan County. This assumption was supported by Utah DHHS records, indicating that no requests for MVCs were made by this LHD. To test the robustness of our results, we excluded these ZIP codes in our sensitivity analysis.

Variable correlations were estimated using Pearson correlation for numerical variables and Cramér’s V for categorical variables, with results presented in correlation matrices.32 A two-sided α level of 5% (p<0.05) was considered statistically significant. All data analyses were performed using R (V.4.4.2).

Results

Study descriptive statistics

From the 2021 ACS data, we identified 298 ZIP codes in Utah. After excluding eight non-residential and 11 low-population ZIP codes (<100 residents), 279 ZIP codes remained for main analysis.

From 1 April 2021 to 31 March 2022, MVCs were deployed in Utah for 2760 MVC days, averaging 8.5 MVC days per 10 000 residents. Urban areas had a higher density of MVCs than rural areas (9.0 and 5.5 MVC days per 10 000 residents, respectively). MVCs were more concentrated in ZIP codes with high versus low proportion of Hispanic population (11.7 and 4.5 MVC days per 10 000 residents, respectively) and higher SVI (very high SVI: 14.9 MVC days per 10 000 residents; very low SVI: 2.2 MVC days per 10 000 residents). No clear patterns were observed in the MVCs distribution relative to levels of resource-constrained healthcare systems or the healthcare accessibility barriers.

Zero-inflated beta-regression model

The zero-inflated component demonstrated significant effects of MVCs within and nearby ZIP codes in reducing the odds of no vaccination. For the ZIP codes in Utah, each additional day of MVC in a given week, on average, decreased the odds of no vaccination in that week by 79.5% (OR=0.205, 95% CI 0.064 to 0.659). Similarly, each additional day of MVC within a ZIP code decreased the odds of no vaccination in the nearby ZIP codes by 21.1% (OR=0.789, 95% CI 0.732 to 0.849) (table 2). For other words, for every additional day in a week that an MVC was present in a given ZIP code, the likelihood that no one was vaccinated during that week decreased by 79.5% in that ZIP code and by 21.1% in nearby ZIP code.

The conditional component showed statistically significant associations of MVCs within and nearby ZIP codes on increasing weekly vaccination rates. For the ZIP codes with weekly vaccination uptake in a given week, each additional day of MVC increased the odds of weekly vaccination rate in that week by 1.6% (OR=1.016, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.032) in host ZIP codes, and by 0.6% in nearby ZIP codes (OR=1.006, 95% CI 1.003 to 1.009) (table 2). Alternatively, interpreting the results on the log-odds (logit) scale showed that each MVC day added 0.016 (coefficient; 95% CI 0.0003 to 0.032) to the logit of the weekly vaccination rate in the host ZIP code. Similarly, each MVC day in nearby ZIP codes adds 0.006 (coefficient; 95% CI 0.004 to 0.008) to the logit of the weekly vaccination rate.

Sensitivity analyses and multicorrelation

In sensitivity analyses, excluding nine ZIP codes in San Juan County yielded consistent effects of MVCs on weekly vaccination rates, with only minor changes in effect sizes (online supplemental eTable 4).

Correlation matrices for numerical and categorical variables showed 6 of 12 variable pairs had correlation coefficients greater than 0.3, with the highest correlation between rural–urban areas and HABI level (r=0.59) (online supplemental eTable 5 and 6).

Discussion

This study found that MVCs significantly reduced the probability of no vaccination in ZIP codes. Each additional MVC day decreased the odds of no vaccination in that week by 79.5% in host ZIP code and 21.1% in nearby ZIP codes. In ZIP codes with vaccination, MVCs provided a modest effect, each MVC day raised the odds of the weekly vaccination rate by 1.6% in host ZIP codes and 0.6% in nearby ZIP codes. These findings indicated that MVCs had positive effects on both host and nearby ZIP codes.

The reported effectiveness of MVCs varies considerably across previous studies.711 This variation can be attributed to multiple factors, including study design, setting, operational definition of MVCs, outcomes measures, study duration and baseline vaccination coverage at the time of intervention. For instance, an RCT in Africa, where baseline vaccination coverage was 6.2%–9.5%, reported a 26 percentage point increase in cumulative vaccination rates following a 2–3 day MVC intervention.7 Another RCT in Switzerland, which had a baseline vaccination rate of 55.5%, observed a 9 percentage points increase in cumulative vaccination rates measured 3 weeks after a single 4-hour MVC intervention.8 A real-world study in the Netherlands measured 1-to-6 MVC effect on daily vaccination rates over 4 months. In this study, where baseline vaccination coverage was already high (67%–89%), the authors found a modest increase of 0.22–0.33 percentage.11 Due to this significant heterogeneity, direct comparison of MVC effectiveness between studies must be made with caution. However, despite the varying effect sizes, these studies consistently affirm the effectiveness of MVCs in increasing COVID-19 vaccine uptake, which aligns with the findings of our study.

The mechanisms of MVCs’ effectiveness in increasing vaccination uptake are primarily expanding vaccination sites and providing easier registration, as noted in the literature.7 8 10 11 Additionally, MVCs are often accompanied by communication activities informing communities about MVC availability and locations, and vaccine information, which helps reduce vaccine hesitancy.7 Some researchers suggest that MVCs may indirectly increase vaccination rates at other sites (eg, hospitals and pharmacies) due to family or community influence based on the social impact theory.33 34 However, current existing evidence on the COVID-19 vaccine does not support this hypothesis. An RCT found no spillover effects of MVCs on vaccination uptake at hospitals or pharmacies.8 While further studies are needed to explore this point, it suggests that MVC serves a distinct population—individuals who prefer MVCs over traditional healthcare services.10 A US study revealed that approximately 25% of families who used MVC services would not have sought vaccination elsewhere.35 It highlights the role of MVCs as a valuable complement to healthcare delivery services to ensure broader population coverage.

We observed that MVCs were more effective in rural than urban areas, which is consistent with Lambooij et al’s findings. Their study showed a 1-to-6-day MVC event increased vaccination uptake by 0.22% in urban and 0.33% in rural areas.11 In our study, this greater effect appeared in the conditional component, not the zero-inflated component. This indicates that MVCs equally reduced no vaccinations, regardless of rural or urban settings, likely by addressing structural barriers in rural and some urban areas, such as limited healthcare facilities and transportation. The stronger effect of MVCs in rural areas in the conditional component implies additional benefits once vaccination begins. These findings address concerns from health officials about MVCs' effectiveness in rural settings.20 Given these results, we recommend that public health officials preferentially prioritise deployment of MVC in rural and frontier communities to maximise programme impact and reduce geographic health disparities.

We found no significant variations in MVC effectiveness between areas with high versus low Hispanic populations or varying social vulnerability levels. These non-significant interactions should be interpreted with caution. This finding could mean that MVCs were equally effective regardless of Hispanic population proportions and social vulnerability levels. However, it is also possible that our analysis lacked sufficient statistical power to detect smaller, yet potentially meaningful, differences in effect between these subgroups. An African RCT similarly found no differences in MVC effects by educational or socioeconomic factors (measured by land ownership and food security).7 A UK study, however, reported a smaller or no effect of MVCs in racial and ethnic minority groups, and in most socioeconomically disadvantaged areas compared with white populations and less disadvantaged areas.10 These findings may suggest additional factors associated with specific populations influence MVC impact. For instance, culturally tailored outreach may complement MVCs in Hispanic communities by addressing specific barriers. Future studies should explore whether such strategies enhance MVCs’ effectiveness in minority and disadvantaged populations.

Vaccination efforts involve a broad spectrum of public health strategies, such as outreach to communities,36 vaccine promotion through primary healthcare providers,37 financial incentives38 or public health messages.39 While our findings demonstrate that MVCs consistently increased vaccination across various settings, other strategies, such as public health messaging or financial incentives, showed minimal or no effects on vaccination uptake.7 38 39 This comparison provides insights for policymakers in selecting effective vaccination strategies.

However, the implementation of MVC in practice also presents several key challenges. First, MVC performance varied substantially across vaccination sites. For example, an Africa RCT found significant cross-team variation, with the most productive teams administering more than two times as many vaccines as less productive ones (daily range from 0 to 146 vaccines per team).7 This variability was also evident in the USA. A study in Massachusetts reported a wide range in weekly vaccinations at MVC sites, with a median of 152 doses (the IQR: 46–315).13 These discrepancies highlight the need for strategies to optimise and standardise MVC operational performance. Second, the costs associated with this delivery method are a key concern. For instance, the total cost to operate a single pop-up vaccination centre in New York was $25 880.40 A larger study of 34 vaccination sites run by LHDs in the USA reported highly variable expenses, with a median start-up cost per clinic of $15 348 (range, $1409 to $165 190), and a median daily operational cost per clinic of $10 314 (range, $637–$95 163). Staffing was the largest driver of these expenses, comprising over 80% of total costs.41 Previous economic assessments suggested that COVID-19 MVCs were cost effective in African and North Macedonia, based on simple cost-per-dose calculation (US$33 per dose in Africa and US$57.6 per dose in North Macedonia).7 11 However, this basic metric is insufficient to capture the opportunity costs of investing in MVCs over other public health strategies. While more formal cost-effectiveness analyses are needed, our findings point to a clear policy implication. Given these substantial operational costs, public health officials face a significant resource-allocation trade-off. Therefore, to ensure a cost-effective use of public funds, MVC deployment should be strategically targeted to populations or areas with the highest demonstrated benefit, such as the rural communities identified in our analysis, rather than being deployed broadly.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it evaluated MVC effects on overall vaccination uptake and assessed its variations based on area characteristics in the USA, filling gaps in the existing literature. Second, it estimated the spillover effects of MVCs on nearby ZIP codes, accounting for real-world geographic mobility in vaccine-seeking behaviour. Third, it analysed data over a 1-year period, providing insights into the longer term impact of MVCs while previous studies analysed data over shorter timeframes (2 weeks to 4 months).711

However, there are some limitations. First, as our study is observational, we could not establish causality of the effect as there might be other unmeasured and/or unobserved confounders beyond the confounders we controlled in our model. Second, we assumed a uniform performance across MVC sites and operational hours, despite evidence that performance is not uniform. For instance, vaccination numbers varied widely by team and weekend MVCs might have higher throughput than their weekday counterparts.7 13 41 Large differences in MVCs team performance were also observed even within a strict RCT design.7 Third, our study focuses on Utah residents, which may underestimate MVCs’ effectiveness due to out-of-state individuals receiving vaccinations in Utah. Fourth, the USIIS database has inherent limitations. Even though it is the most comprehensive immunisation database in the state,42 it may not capture all vaccinated individuals, such as persons experiencing homelessness or undocumented individuals who received vaccines at MVCs without identification. Furthermore, its accuracy is dependent on correct data entry by providers. Both of these factors could lead to an underestimation of the MVCs’ true impact. Fifth, we analysed only first-dose uptake, which may underestimate the overall effectiveness of MVC on all COVID-19 doses. Sixth, the definition of ‘nearby ZIP codes’ was determined by available data. Future research should refine the distance-based effectiveness of MVCs. Finally, our study was conducted in Utah, which limits the generalisability of our findings. The effectiveness of MVCs observed in this context is likely most applicable to other US states with large rural populations and similar geographic barriers to healthcare access. Conversely, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to high-density, urban contexts, where the primary barriers to vaccination may be different.

Conclusions

MVCs effectively increased COVID-19 vaccination uptake in ZIP codes where MVCs were not only operated but also had spillover effects in neighbouring areas, especially in low vaccination and rural areas. These findings support the MVC role in improving equitable vaccine access and informing public health strategies.
