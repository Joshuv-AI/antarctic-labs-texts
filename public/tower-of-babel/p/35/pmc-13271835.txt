Efficacy and Tolerability of Topotecan/Cyclophosphamide/Dinutuximab in Relapsed and Refractory High‐Risk Neuroblastoma: A Multi‐Institutional Retrospective Study
Authors: Benjamin J Lerman, Madeleine King, Rajen Mody, Sara‐Jane Onyeama, Araz Marachelian, Thomas Cash, Sara M Federico, Meaghan Granger, Ami V Desai, Suzanne Shusterman, Jennifer H Foster, Elizabeth Sokol, Chelsee Greer, Keri Streby, Holly J Meany, Kieuhoa T Vo, Margaret E Macy, Rochelle Bagatell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeChemoimmunotherapy with irinotecan, temozolomide, and dinutuximab (I/T/DIN) has emerged as first‐line therapy for relapsed/refractory (r/r) high‐risk neuroblastoma (HRNB) in North America. Topotecan and cyclophosphamide (T/C) are often used in combination with dinutuximab in the setting of lack of response, progression, or incomplete response following I/T/DIN, but efficacy and tolerability are unknown.MethodsEligible patients received one or more cycles of T/C/DIN for r/r HRNB. Response was assessed using the 2017 International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria.ResultsFifty‐four patients with r/r HRNB were included. Patients received a median of four cycles of T/C/DIN (range = 1–16); 48 (89%) had previously received I/T/DIN. Twenty‐three patients (42.6%) had an objective response (complete/partial/minor; OR) to T/C/DIN, 21 (38.9%) had stable disease, and 10 (18.5%) had progressive disease as best response. Among patients who previously received I/T/DIN, objective response rate (ORR) to T/C/DIN was 54% in those with prior OR to I/T/DIN and 23% in those without prior OR (adjusted odds ratio 4.40, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.09–17.75). Patients who had experienced on‐therapy progression with I/T/DIN had a similar ORR to T/C/DIN compared to those who never progressed (38% vs. 41%, p = 1.0). Twenty‐one patients (39%) required T/C/DIN dose modification—11 (52%) due to hematologic toxicity. Seven patients (13%) discontinued T/C/DIN due to toxicity.ConclusionT/C/DIN is an effective regimen for patients with r/r HRNB, producing ORRs similar to prior reports in chemoimmunotherapy‐naïve patients treated with I/T/DIN. Prior chemoimmunotherapy response may predict response to T/C/DIN, but even patients who progress while receiving I/T/DIN may benefit from the T/C/DIN combination.Keywords: chemoimmunotherapy, dinutuximab, immunotherapy, neuroblastoma

Introduction

Neuroblastoma is the most common extracranial solid tumor of childhood and accounts for greater than 15% of all childhood cancer deaths [1]. While subsets of patients with biologically favorable disease experience survival rates of ≥95% with minimal therapeutic intervention, outcomes for patients with high‐risk neuroblastoma (HRNB) remain poor [2, 3]. Despite intensive multimodal therapy, approximately 50% of patients with HRNB will develop recurrent or progressive disease [4, 5].

Over the last decade, however, therapy with the anti‐GD2 monoclonal antibody dinutuximab plus a chemotherapy backbone has revolutionized care for this patient population. Based on the results of Children's Oncology Group (COG) ANBL1221 as well as real‐world experience, combination chemoimmunotherapy with dinutuximab, irinotecan, and temozolomide with granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF) has emerged as the standard of care for patients with relapsed and refractory HRNB in North America [6, 7]. However, only 40%–50% of patients experience an objective response (OR) to I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF, and 72% of patients will experience relapse or progression within 2 years of receiving this therapy [8]. Thus, alternative therapies are needed.

Topotecan and cyclophosphamide are increasingly used in North America as an alternative chemotherapy backbone in combination with dinutuximab (T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF) for patients with r/r HRNB—particularly as second‐line chemoimmunotherapy. However, few studies have examined this combination's efficacy and tolerability, and none have investigated response in a population that has previously received chemoimmunotherapy [9]. The primary objectives of this study were to evaluate the objective response rate (ORR) and tolerability of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF in patients with relapsed and refractory HRNB, both with and without previous exposure to chemoimmunotherapy.

Participants and Methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the human participants committees at study sites, and a waiver of informed consent was obtained. Participants from 14 centers were screened, and relevant demographic, clinical, and outcomes data were recorded in a secure REDCap database. HRNB was defined per COG guidelines and confirmed by central review of records [10]. Eligibility criteria included a histologically confirmed diagnosis of HRNB at age less than 30 years and receipt of one or more cycles of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF for relapsed/refractory disease after initial frontline therapy. Participants were required to undergo one or more disease evaluations after receipt of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF to be considered evaluable for response, and all participants who received one or more cycles were considered evaluable for toxicity. Relapsed disease was defined as the development of new or additional disease after an initial response to treatment. Primary progressive disease was defined as progression in a patient who never achieved a response to treatment. Refractory disease was defined as a suboptimal response to frontline therapy without progression that led to a change from standard therapy on frontline protocols [11].

Treatment Regimen

Participants received one or more cycles of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF for relapsed/refractory disease between January 1, 2015, and September 1, 2024. Participants receiving topotecan and cyclophosphamide with both higher (1.2 and 400 mg/m2/dose, respectively) and lower (0.75 and 250 mg/m2/dose, respectively) dosing regimens were eligible. All participants received topotecan and cyclophosphamide intravenously daily on Days 1–5, dinutuximab (17.5 mg/m2/dose intravenously) daily over 10–20 h on Days 2–5, and GM‐CSF (250 µg/m2/dose subcutaneously) daily on Days 6–12 of a 21‐day cycle.

Classification of Diagnosis, Relapse, and Response to Therapy

Date of diagnosis was defined as the date of collection of a pathology specimen (bone, bone marrow, or soft tissue) consistent with HRNB. For patients with a prior diagnosis of non‐high‐risk neuroblastoma who were subsequently found to have high‐risk disease, the date of diagnosis of HRNB was used. Date of relapse, progression, or determination of refractory status was defined as the date that either imaging or tissue specimens identifying this event were obtained. Duration of treatment was measured by the number of cycles and the number of days from the first day of the first cycle of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF to the first day of the last cycle.

Responses to frontline therapy and T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF were classified by the International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria (INRC) as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), minor response (MR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD) based on institutional review [12]. When all INRG components were not available, response designations were included but appended Not Fully Evaluated (i.e., PR [NFE]), a practice that has been adopted in recent COG clinical trials for patients with relapsed neuroblastoma. NFE and fully evaluable participants are reported together for ease of interpretation of results. Patients were considered as having improved disease status from one cycle of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF to the next only if the subsequent disease evaluation resulted in a superior INRC overall response category. Patients with improvement on imaging or Curie score but the same INRC overall response category were not classified as having improved response.

Adverse Events and Dose/Timing Modifications

Dose modifications were defined as either reductions in the dose of topotecan, cyclophosphamide, and/or dinutuximab or reductions in the number of days per cycle of drug administration (i.e., 4 rather than 5 days of T/C per cycle). Timing modifications were defined as modifications to intervals between cycles (i.e., every 4 rather than every 3 weeks). Data regarding deviations from the standard treatment regimens were collected. Treating teams made decisions regarding dose and/or timing modifications.

Statistical Methods

Primary outcomes were the proportion of patients with an objective response (OR) after T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF and the proportion of patients who discontinued T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF due to toxicity. OR was defined as a CR, PR, or MR on a disease evaluation after initiation of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of patients requiring dose or timing modifications, and progression‐free survival (PFS) after initiation of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF. PFS was measured from the date of initiation of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF to the date of documented progression or death; patients were censored at the time of loss to follow‐up or initiation of alternative anticancer therapy.

Patient demographics, clinical features, tumor histology, and molecular features were summarized and compared between patients with and without an OR to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF. Wilcoxon and Fisher's exact tests were used for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. For the binary outcome OR, logistic regression was used to assess risk factors; odds ratios were estimated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For PFS, Cox proportional hazard models were used to assess risk factors, and Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed.

The following factors were considered for inclusion in multivariable analyses: age at diagnosis, race, ethnicity, sex assigned at birth, International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC), stage at diagnosis, history of relapsed/refractory disease, previous receipt of chemoimmunotherapy, previous response to or progression on chemoimmunotherapy, tumor MYCN amplification status, tumor ALK (anaplastic lymphoma kinase) aberration status, and presence of measurable versus evaluable disease at relapse, progression, or at designation of refractory disease [13]. Race and ethnicity were abstracted from the medical record. Tumors were designated MYCN amplified if there was a four‐fold or higher increase in MYCN signal compared with the reference probe or more than eight copies detected; tumors with MYCN gain (two‐ to four‐fold gain) were considered nonamplified [14]. ALK aberrations were defined as either amplifications or activating mutations in the tyrosine kinase domain. Candidate covariates were screened in univariable models first; those with univariable p < 0.2 were selected for inclusion in multivariable models. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Fifty‐five eligible participants were included in the retrospective review (median = 3, range = 1–14 participants per center), 54 of whom were evaluable for the primary endpoint of objective response rate (ORR; Table 1). One enrolled participant—a 14‐year‐old male with 95% marrow involvement—died of sepsis during Cycle 1 of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF prior to undergoing a disease evaluation, so was evaluable for toxicity but not response. All patients had metastatic disease at the time of treatment with T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF, and 29 (53.7%) had measurable disease. Of the 51 participants for whom tumor MYCN status was known, 14 (27.5%) tumors had MYCN amplification. Of the 44 participants with tumors for which ALK status was known, 10 (22.7%) had tumors that harbored an activating mutation or amplification of the ALK gene. Median age at diagnosis of HRNB was 45 months, and 28 participants were male (51.9%). Five participants had refractory disease at the time of treatment with T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (9.3%), 49 had relapsed disease (90.7%; median = 2 relapses, range = 1–10); none had primary progressive disease. Forty‐eight (88.9%) had previously received a median of six (range = 1–21) cycles of prior chemoimmunotherapy for relapsed or refractory disease—all with an irinotecan and temozolomide backbone. Of these participants, 40 (83.3%) experienced a relapse or progression event between therapy with I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF and T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF, and eight (16.7%) did not. Twenty‐six of the 48 patients who previously received chemoimmunotherapy (54.2%) had an objective response to that therapy, and 26 (54.2%) had progression while actively receiving that prior chemoimmunotherapy.

Dosing, Tolerability, and Toxicity of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF Therapy

Participants were treated with T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF at an initial cycle length of every 3 weeks (n = 37, 68.5%) or every 4 weeks (n = 17, 31.5%) (Table 2). Sixteen participants (29.6%) received higher dose cyclophosphamide and topotecan (400 and 1.2 mg/m2 ×5 days or similar), and 38 (70.4%) received lower dose (250 and 0.75 mg/m2 or similar). Participants received a median of four cycles of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (interquartile range = 2–7, range = 1–16) over a median of 87.5 days. In this real‐world cohort, 13 participants (24.1%) received additional treatment, including radiation (8, 14.9%) or another targeted agent (6, 11.1%) at some point during therapy with T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (Table S1). However, of these, only one (who started trametinib concurrently with T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF) experienced an OR potentially attributable to the concurrent therapy. Seven never achieved an OR following T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF, three continued previously initiated ALK‐directed therapy, and two started concurrent therapy after achieving their best response to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF.

Twenty‐one participants (38.9%) required a dose modification during treatment with T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF. Dosing was reduced pre‐emptively to prevent toxicity in seven patients, and due to on‐therapy toxicity in 14. Of these, 15 required dose modification of the cyclophosphamide/topotecan backbone alone, three of dinutuximab alone, and three of all agents. Dose modifications were most commonly made in response to hematologic (11 participants, 20.4%) and infectious (six participants, 11.1%) toxicities, and modifications due to on‐therapy toxicity were more common among participants receiving higher dose cyclophosphamide and topotecan (37.5% vs. 21.1%). Fifteen participants (27.8%) required a modification of the interval between cycles. One (1.9%) discontinued T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF due to acute toxicity, and six (11.1%) due to chronic toxicity (i.e., neutropenia, nausea). Participants who discontinued therapy due to toxicity did so after a median of two cycles (range = 1–9 cycles). Only one participant who received more than four cycles discontinued therapy due to toxicity. One participant died due to Clostridium septicum sepsis during therapy (1.9%).

Objective Response Rate and Factors Associated With Response to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF

Of the 54 participants, 23 (42.6%) experienced an OR to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (16.7% CR; 22.2% PR; 3.7% MR), 21 (38.9%) experienced stable disease (SD), and 10 (18.5%) experienced progression on the first disease evaluation (Table 3). ORR was similar between higher and lower dosing groups (43.8% vs. 42.1%, p = 1.0). Participants underwent their first disease evaluation a median of two cycles and a range of one to four cycles after starting therapy. Among participants with an OR, 81% achieved that response on their first disease evaluation. However, four participants improved to a CR during Cycles 5–8. Among participants who achieved SD, MR, or PR on first disease evaluation, nine of 39 (23.1%) experienced further improvement in INRC response (e.g., SD to MR or PR to CR) on a subsequent response assessment. Improvement was seen in six participants with initial SD (22.2%), two with initial MR (50%), and one with PR (12.5%; Table 3). Participants who received concurrent therapy with radiation and/or other targeted agents had a similar ORR to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (46.2%) as participants who did not (41.5%, p = 1.0).

In univariable analyses, demographic and clinical factors were similar among participants who did and did not have an OR to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (Table 1). However, those who responded to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF were significantly more likely to have previously achieved an OR during prior chemoimmunotherapy, even if they subsequently experienced progression while still on that regimen (73.7% vs. 41.4%, p = 0.03). While proportions of White and Asian participants did not significantly differ between responders and nonresponders, these factors did pass the univariable logistic regression screen and were included in the multivariable models. In a multivariable model including covariates passing the univariable screen, previous OR to chemoimmunotherapy was significantly associated with improved odds of response to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (odds ratio 4.40, 95% CI: 1.09–17.75; Table 4). This association remained present in a sensitivity analysis of only participants with relapsed disease (Table S2).

Notably, there was no association between history of on‐therapy progression on prior chemoimmunotherapy and objective response rate to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (38.4% vs. 40.1%). Of the 10/26 participants with a history of on‐chemoimmunotherapy progression who achieved an OR to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF, three achieved CR, six PR, and one MR (Figure S1). These participants had a similar distribution of age (45 vs. 52 months), MYCN status (25.9% vs. 30% amplified), marrow involvement (44.4% vs. 60%), and bony involvement (88.9% vs. 90%) compared to the overall study cohort. However, they were less likely to have measurable soft tissue disease (53.7% vs. 20%). Anatomical distribution of pre‐T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF on‐chemoimmunotherapy progression in these participants was: six bone, two marrow, three soft tissue, and one central nervous system (CNS).

Survival After T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF

Among all participants receiving T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF, the median PFS attributable to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF was 8.4 months, the 6‐month PFS was 56.1%, and the 1‐year PFS was 24.9% (Figure 1A). Four patients were censored for loss to follow‐up, and 19 were censored for initiation of alternative anticancer therapy. Median PFS was not reached in participants with refractory disease and was 8.4 months in participants with relapsed disease (Figure 1B). PFS did not differ between patients who did or did not receive concomitant therapy with T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (median PFS 8.1 vs. 8.7 months, log‐rank p = 0.69; Figure S2) or participants who had and had not previously received chemoimmunotherapy (median PFS 8.7 vs. 8.4 months, log‐rank p = 0.94; Figure 1C), although the number of chemoimmunotherapy‐naïve participants in this study was low. Among participants who previously received chemoimmunotherapy, those who had an OR to that prior therapy experienced superior PFS after T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF (median 10.9 vs. 4.8 months, p = 0.0035; Figure 1D), and those who experienced progression on that therapy experienced inferior unadjusted PFS (median 5.4 vs. 10.9 months, p = 0.04; Figure 1E). However, only the history of a previous OR to chemoimmunotherapy (among previous chemoimmunotherapy recipients) remained associated with PFS after multivariable adjustment (HR 0.32, 95% CI: 0.13–0.77; Table 5).

A total of 38 participants experienced relapse or progression between 3 weeks and 3 years after receiving T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF. Of these, 20 (52.6%) occurred while participants were actively receiving this therapy, 11 (28.9%) occurred after discontinuing T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF without intervening therapy, and seven (18.4%) occurred after the censoring for initiation of additional anticancer therapy (Table 2). The most common site of relapse was bone (89.4% of relapses), followed by metastatic soft tissue (85.3%), bone marrow (29.4%), and liver and lung (14.7% each). Of the 16 (29.6%) who did not relapse after T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF, the median duration of follow‐up prior to censoring was 5.5 months (range = 1.7–17.6 months). Twelve (75%) received additional anticancer therapy (alternate chemoimmunotherapy, MIBG, radiation, small molecule therapy), and four (25%) did not. Median duration of follow‐up for these four participants from the last cycle of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF was 7.7 months (range = 1.7–14.7 months).

Discussion

T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF has emerged as a second‐line chemoimmunotherapy regimen in patients with relapsed/refractory HRNB, but key questions regarding dosing, efficacy, and tolerability—particularly in patients previously treated with chemoimmunotherapy—were unanswered prior to this study. Here, we present the largest cohort of patients with relapsed/refractory HRNB treated with a chemoimmunotherapy regimen other than I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF. This is also the first study to quantify retreatment outcomes with second‐line chemoimmunotherapy in a population that has largely already received I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF. Remarkably, we observed response rates in this largely chemoimmunotherapy pretreated cohort that were similar to those seen in chemoimmunotherapy‐naïve patients receiving T/C/DIN (42%) and I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF (49%) [8, 9]. Despite this similarity in ORR, median (8.1 vs. 13.1 months) and 1‐year (24.9% vs. 50%) PFS in this cohort appeared lower than in patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy for the first time. This suggests that although response rates are similar, the durability of response with subsequent courses of chemoimmunotherapy may decrease. As expected, the response rate in our cohort was also higher than that in a COG study of topotecan/cyclophosphamide without dinutuximab (32%) despite the fact that the current cohort was more heavily pretreated [15]. Finally, we did not find evidence that higher dosing of cyclophosphamide and topotecan is associated with superior ORR.

Our data suggest that in a subset of patients, previous exposure to chemoimmunotherapy does not decrease the likelihood of objective response to retreatment with chemoimmunotherapy using an alternative chemotherapy backbone. Furthermore, the only factor associated with a lower likelihood of response to second‐line chemoimmunotherapy was a history of lack of response to prior chemoimmunotherapy. Even participants who had previously responded to chemoimmunotherapy but subsequently experienced progression while receiving chemoimmunotherapy had a similar ORR to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF compared to those who had never progressed on chemoimmunotherapy (40.9% vs. 38.4%). This subgroup did, however, experience a less durable response in unadjusted analyses—a difference that approached statistical significance.

As observed in the real‐world experience with I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF, most patients in this cohort experienced the best response to T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF on their first disease evaluation. However, we did observe development of CRs as late as Cycle 8 and durable responses of up to 18 months even in patients with PR or MR, suggesting that it may be reasonable to continue even this therapy in the setting of persistent (but not progressive) disease. As the population of patients who have received I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF for relapsed/refractory HRNB grows, so does the population of patients who have not responded, experienced response plateau, or progressed on this therapy. Our data will help oncologists and families make informed, data‐driven decisions regarding treatment with alternative chemoimmunotherapy regimens in a population for whom other therapies are lacking.

Despite a more myelosuppressive chemotherapy backbone and a more heavily pretreated population, T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF was generally well‐tolerated, with an identical incidence of regimen discontinuation due to toxicity (13%) as in the real‐world experience with I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF. However, 28/54 participants (51.9%) required a modification to drug dosing or timing. Notably, rates of dose modifications for on‐therapy toxicity were nearly double in the subset of participants receiving higher dose cyclophosphamide and topotecan. When this regimen was discontinued due to toxicity, it was most commonly discontinued within the first two to three cycles of therapy. While more than 25% required an increased time interval between cycles, patients who experienced a durable response and received more than four cycles of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF rarely discontinued therapy due to cumulative toxicity.

The biological mechanism behind tumor resensitization to chemoimmunotherapy after progression or response plateau remains unexplained. In vitro data suggest that under therapeutic pressure, neuroblastoma cells may convert to a more mesenchymal cell state that downregulates GD2 expression [16, 17, 18]. This adrenergic to mesenchymal conversion is bidirectional and reversible, suggesting that a “rest” period between chemoimmunotherapy blocks may allow for tumor reversion to a more adrenergic, GD2‐expressing state in which cells are more vulnerable to chemoimmunotherapy [19, 20, 21]. However, while this phenomenon has been shown in preclinical models, further study of tumor samples obtained after receipt of chemoimmunotherapy would be needed to demonstrate whether this is the basis for resensitization in patients. Furthermore, eight patients in this cohort received T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF immediately after I/T/DIN/GM‐CSF without an intervening period of progression and tumor regrowth. Further study of tumors from these patients, as well as those of others retreated with chemoimmunotherapy, is needed.

While this study's findings regarding efficacy and tolerability of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF will help oncologists and families make data‐driven decisions in the care of future patients with HRNB, this retrospective study has several limitations. First, patients were selected by their oncologists as appropriate candidates to receive T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF, introducing selection bias. Additionally, decisions regarding dose modifications and determination of therapy‐limiting toxicities represent the decisions of treating teams rather than adherence to a clinical trial protocol. Thus, factors beyond those measured may have impacted outcomes and treatment decisions. Finally, parameters for initiation and discontinuation of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF, supportive care strategies, and disease surveillance protocols were not standardized and may have been influenced by institutional practices and the availability of alternative therapies. However, this analysis is extremely valuable because it reflects real‐world practice. Moreover, this is the largest reported cohort of predominantly chemoimmunotherapy‐pretreated patients with HRNB who were re‐challenged with chemoimmunotherapy.

This study shows that T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF is a viable therapeutic option for patients with relapsed/refractory HRNB who have previously responded to chemoimmunotherapy, regardless of whether those patients later progressed on prior chemoimmunotherapy. Higher dosing of cyclophosphamide and topotecan is not associated with superior ORR, but may result in a higher incidence of toxicity. Data from the current study provide support for the upcoming randomized cooperative group trial of T/C/DIN/GM‐CSF with and without the addition of a novel agent that may augment immune responses in the setting of chemoimmunotherapy.
