A decade of aeromedical care for the older adult: a 10-year review of Irish HEMS cases in patients aged 65 and over
Authors: Daragh Mathews, David Hennelly, Sean Underwood, Cathal O’Donnell, Siobhan Masterson, Conor Deasy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince its inception in 2012, the Helicopter Emergency Medical Service (HEMS) has become a key component of Ireland’s emergency care framework, particularly in supporting access to specialised care centres for major trauma, stroke, and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). With an aging population - expected to exceed 1.1 million people aged 65 and older by 2031 - the demand for aeromedical transport of older adults is increasing. Older patients often present complex clinical scenarios due to frailty, polypharmacy, and comorbidities. However, the utilisation and effectiveness of HEMS in this population remain under-examined. This study aimed to provide a 10-year review of HEMS missions involving patients aged ≥ 65 years in Ireland.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on Irish HEMS missions between January 2013 and December 2022. Data on patient demographics, mission profiles, clinical interventions, and transport decisions were collected using multiple sources including initial emergency call and dispatch information, patient care reports and end of shift logs.ResultsOf 7,857 total HEMS missions over the 10-year period, age data was available for 5,815 patients and 1,875 (32.2%) involved patients aged ≥ 65 years. The median patient age was 74. Primary dispatches accounted for 48.7% of missions, with acute coronary syndromes (60.1%) and trauma from road traffic collisions (26.0%) being the leading medical and traumatic causes, respectively. The standdown rate was 16.8%. Most missions occurred in spring and summer (58%) and were more common in rural counties such as Mayo and Galway. It was shown that 24% of patients were not transported by helicopter, with 5.6% of those transported taken to primary percutaneous intervention centres and 6.2% of patients transported to Dublin based hospitals.ConclusionsOlder adults represent a significant proportion of HEMS activity in Ireland. While HEMS plays an important role in facilitating access to emergency care for this group, particularly in rural areas, patterns in dispatch, transport decisions, and destination hospitals highlight areas for system development. Enhanced age-specific triage protocols and further integration of geriatric considerations into aeromedical planning may improve equity and effectiveness as demand continues to grow.Keywords: Helicopter emergency medicine service (HEMS), Older adults, Prehospital care, Trauma, Triage

Background

The Helicopter Emergency Medical Service (HEMS) has played an integral role in the provision of emergency medical care in the Republic of Ireland since its establishment in 2012. Initially established as a pilot initiative through a collaboration between the National Ambulance Service (NAS) and the Irish Air Corps (IAC), the service has since expanded, with a second aircraft introduced in 2019 [1]. This expansion was initially supported by a charitable organisation, but since 2021, HEMS in Ireland has been fully state-funded. The service has evolved in response to national health policies that emphasise the centralisation of complex care, necessitating improved prehospital transportation solutions for critically ill and injured patients. As Ireland’s aeromedical service continues to mature, evaluating its effectiveness and identifying areas for enhancement will be crucial to optimising patient outcomes.

The restructuring of Ireland’s healthcare system has led to the development of specialised care centres for conditions such as major trauma, stroke, and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). As a result, the demand for aeromedical services has increased, ensuring that patients in need of time-sensitive interventions can reach these specialised facilities efficiently. HEMS has emerged as a crucial component of this strategy, bridging the gap between patients in remote or rural areas and advanced medical care.

With Ireland’s aging population - approximately 20% of the population projected to be over 65 by 2031 - the demand for specialised prehospital care for older adults is increasing. The Irish Longitudinal Study on Aging (TILDA) highlights that a significant portion of older adults live with multiple chronic diseases (e.g., cardiovascular issues, diabetes, dementia). This creates complex clinical scenarios that require coordinated, multi-specialty care. The increasing complexity of older patients’ needs calls for emergency medical teams, including HEMS, to have tailored protocols and training in geriatric care. This can include managing issues such as polypharmacy, cognitive impairment, and reduced physiological reserve.

The complexity of managing injured patients from this demographic is highlighted in the National Trauma Office’s recent report [2]. The report highlights that older adults with major trauma present unique challenges, including higher rates of frailty, comorbidities, and polypharmacy. These factors often result in atypical injury patterns, delayed diagnoses, and increased mortality. Early intervention, such as that provided by HEMS, is crucial for improving outcomes. However, there is a lack of comprehensive evaluation of HEMS utilisation and effectiveness in this population.

This study aims to provide a comprehensive 10-year review of cases involving patients aged 65 and older who were attended to by Irish HEMS. By analysing mission data and patient demographics, the study seeks to offer insights into how HEMS has contributed to the care of older adults and to identify potential areas for service improvement. Given the aging population and the increasing burden of complex medical conditions, understanding the role of aeromedical transport in elderly patient care is essential for shaping future service delivery in Ireland.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study designed to describe the mission profile and patient characteristics of the advanced paramedic (AP)-staffed HEMS in the Republic of Ireland over a 10-year period between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2022, focusing on patients aged 65 and older.

Setting & population

The Republic of Ireland covers an area of approximately 70,273 km² and has a population that exceeds 5.1 million people, with an annual growth rate of 1.3%. Population density at 73 per km² is lower than the European average, with the lowest density in the west and border regions (33 per km²).

In 2022, there were approximately 800,000 people aged 65 and over, making up about 16% of the population. By 2031, this number is expected to rise to 1.1 million, around 20% of the total population. By 2050, estimates suggest that over 1.5 million people (approximately 25–30%) of Ireland’s population will be aged 65 and older [3].

The NAS is the statutory pre-hospital emergency and intermediate care provider for the Irish State, whilst Dublin Fire Brigade provides Emergency Medical Services (EMS) for large parts of the population of Dublin. The NAS responds to over 350,000 ambulance calls each year and employs over 2,000 staff across 102 locations.

In the Republic of Ireland, HEMS respond to both medical and trauma emergencies and were initially established to serve patients in remote and rural locations in the west of the country. There are two HEMS bases, one in the midlands and one in the southwest, providing a national service. The services operate in partnership with the Irish Air Corps (IAC), which operates an Agusta Westland 139, and Gulf Med Aviation Services, which operates an Airbus EC135. Both operators provide a dual-pilot daytime-only service. Daylight hours in Ireland range from 17 h in spring and summer to eight hours or less during the winter months.

Both HEMS operate under a single governance and dispatch system. Aeromedical dispatch staff are non-clinical dispatchers guided by standardised tasking criteria incorporating specific clinical conditions, mechanisms of injury, and optimal locations where HEMS response may be most beneficial. HEMS clinical and flight crew members collaborate with aeromedical dispatchers to assess the clinical appropriateness of mission requests while considering aviation-limiting factors such as weather conditions and daylight restrictions. Unlike many international HEMS providers that operate at a prehospital critical care level, the Irish HEMS primarily delivers care at an Advanced Paramedic (AP) level – there is no physician on board. While the scope of practice includes limited extended interventions such as in-flight sedation and analgesia, it does not currently provide prehospital emergency anaesthesia (PHEA), prehospital blood transfusion, or emergency surgical interventions.

Data sources & participants

Aeromedical missions are dispatched by the NAS National Emergency Operations Centre (NEOC) Aeromedical Desk. The NAS routinely gathers health data on patients/incidents via multiple sources, including the Medical Priority Dispatch System™ (MPDS®), Computer-Aided Dispatch (CAD), and Siren Field User electronic Patient Care Reports (ePCR). Additionally, data is collected for ongoing governance, audit, and quality improvement of aeromedical services via shift incident reports (SIR).

HEMS-specific datasets were created on a standardised Excel sheet for quality improvement, clinical audit, and governance. Data was inputted by attending HEMS APs and included demographic, physiological, treatment, and mission profile variables.

Data validation

To ensure data accuracy and completeness, the HEMS datasets were cross-referenced with CAD exports, HEMS SIR, and ePCR data before being entered into the study database. This validation process aimed to maximise data integrity and reduce the risk of errors or missing information. Where data were missing for specific variables, these cases were retained in the denominator, and missing data were reported explicitly. Percentages were calculated based on the total number of missions in each age group, including cases with missing values.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient demographics, geographical distribution, receiving facilities, mechanisms/aetiologies of disease or injury, vital sign trends, transportation decisions, clinical interventions, and short-term clinical outcomes. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Software. (IBM, version 30). The chi-squared test was used to assess statistical significance between categorical proportional variables. Further post-hoc analysis was then carried out on statistically significant chi-squared tests.

Results

During the 10-year study period from 2013 to 2022, there were a total of 7,857 HEMS activations. Patient age data was available for 5,815 of these missions and 1,875 missions (23.9%) involved patients aged 65 years and older (Table 1). During the same time-period, HEMS attended to 751 (9.6%) paediatric patients (aged 16 or less) and 3,188 patients aged 17–64 (40.6%).

The majority of taskings were to patients with medical issues (1,327; 70.9%) and there were 544 (29.1%) taskings to trauma incidents (missing data 4 cases; 0.2%). In comparison, amongst adults aged 17–64, trauma accounted for 53.5% of taskings and medical cases for 46.3% (missing data 0.25%).

Of the 1,875 cases involving older adults, 1,382 (73.7%) were classified as completed missions, meaning patient contact was made, and treatment was initiated. A total of 315 (16.8%) cases were stood down after launch, meaning the mission was cancelled prior to patient contact. Among the completed cases, 334 (24.2%) patients were not transported by helicopter; alternative transport or on-scene management was used. For comparison, the standdown rate in adults aged 17–64 was 15.2%.

For the available data in terms of medical aetiology, acute coronary syndrome was the most encountered pathology at 60.1% of cases. This was followed by out of hospital cardiac arrest (19.2%), neurology including stroke (9.7%), and overdose, respiratory and sepsis-related calls. The most common traumatic incidents attended to were road traffic collisions (RTC; 26.0%), long falls (fall > 2 m height; 21.1%), farm accidents (19.7%) and low falls (fall < 2 m height; 18.1%).

Transport destination data were available for 1,356 missions. Among these, 17.8% of patients were not transported by helicopter. Of those who were, 5.6% were taken directly to Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PPCI) centres. Excluding PPCI transfers, the most common receiving hospital was University Hospital Galway (16.8%), followed by University Hospital Limerick (5.7%) and Cork University Hospital (5.5%). Dublin hospitals collectively received 6.2% of transported patients.

In comparison, for those aged 17–64, transportation data were available for 2,425 of 3,188 missions. Of these, 1,900 patients were transported by helicopter (59.6%) and 525 were not transported (16.5%). Data was missing for 763 missions (23.9%). Among those transported by helicopter, 428 patients (22.5%) were brought to Dublin hospitals.

Discussion

This study provides a decade-long overview of HEMS activity in Ireland involving patients aged 65 and older, a demographic that accounted for nearly one-quarter of missions with available age data. While much of the focus in prehospital literature has been on paediatric or younger trauma patients, our findings underscore the growing relevance of aeromedical support for older adults, particularly in the context of an ageing population and evolving trauma infrastructure.

Older adults were represented in HEMS activations at more than double the rate of paediatric patients, indicating a shift in service demand. This trend suggests that operational planning, clinical protocols, and workforce training should increasingly consider the unique needs of older adults. These patients often present with complex clinical profiles including frailty, polypharmacy, and atypical physiological responses, which may complicate prehospital assessment and triage. International evidence supports the role of HEMS in improving outcomes for older patients in time-sensitive medical and traumatic emergencies, such as acute coronary syndromes and major trauma. However, this benefit is contingent on timely and appropriate dispatch, which can be particularly challenging in this cohort due to atypical presentations and under-recognition [4–7].

Seasonal variation in HEMS utilisation was evident, with higher mission volumes in spring and summer. This likely reflects a combination of increased outdoor activity, improved weather, and extended daylight hours that facilitate air operations. The reduced winter mission volume may relate more to aviation constraints than clinical need, highlighting the importance of integrated land-based services during these periods.

The distribution of call types also differed by age. Among patients aged ≥ 65, over 70% of missions were for medical issues, contrasting with a trauma-dominant profile in the 17–64 age group (53.5% trauma). This reflects the higher burden of medical emergencies such as cardiac, neurological, and respiratory events in older adults. These findings suggest that HEMS systems traditionally designed around trauma responses may need to broaden their clinical focus and training to address medical complexity in this growing cohort.

During the study period, Ireland’s national trauma system was in development, and Major Trauma Centres (MTCs) were designated only in the latter years. As such, our data do not permit meaningful conclusions regarding MTC utilisation. However, a notable difference was observed in patient destinations: just 6.2% of patients aged ≥ 65 were transported to Dublin hospitals, compared with 22.5% of younger adults. Given the concentration of specialist services in Dublin, this raises potential questions around access equity. While geographic factors likely contribute, the disparity warrants further investigation, particularly to ensure that transport decisions are based on clinical need rather than systemic barriers or assumptions regarding benefit in older age.

The question of equitable access to HEMS for older adults remains complex. Although this group represented a significant proportion of cases, we lack national EMS utilisation data by age to determine whether HEMS access is proportional to need. The differences observed in dispatch classification - particularly the higher proportion of crew requests among older patients - suggest potential under-recognition at the point of initial triage. However, these findings alone cannot confirm inequity. Similar trends have been reported internationally, where older patients may be under-triaged due to subtle presentations or age-related bias in dispatch algorithms [8].

The standdown rate among older adults was 16.8%, lower than that reported in many international HEMS systems, where figures commonly range from 20 to 40% [9]. This may reflect more conservative tasking criteria, effective crew triage, or structural factors such as daylight-hour limitations. However, the absence of documented standdown reasons limits interpretation. As Ireland advances its trauma and retrieval infrastructure, incorporating age-specific considerations - such as frailty markers - into dispatch and cancellation protocols may improve both appropriateness and efficiency.

A striking gender imbalance was observed, with nearly 70% of older HEMS patients being male. While this may reflect differential risk exposure, referral patterns, or health-seeking behaviours, it is consistent with international studies and may have implications for service planning and outreach. Further exploration is needed to understand whether this represents epidemiological reality or a systemic bias in identification or dispatch.

The findings of this study support the need for enhanced geriatric-focused training for HEMS personnel, the development of age-sensitive triage tools, and consideration of older adults as a key demographic in service planning. As Ireland’s emergency care framework evolves, it will be critical to ensure that aeromedical services are responsive not only to trauma in younger patients but also to the diverse and growing needs of the older population.

Future work should evaluate outcomes following HEMS activation in older patients, assess the implementation of frailty or geriatric triage tools, and explore potential structural barriers to equitable access. These findings also hold international relevance for health systems undergoing demographic transition, especially in rural or resource-limited regions where HEMS is essential for timely access to advanced care.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from a national dataset spanning a full decade, providing a robust overview of HEMS utilisation among older adults in Ireland. The use of data from multiple linked sources- computer-aided dispatch, electronic patient care records, and shift reports - enhances the validity and completeness of operational and clinical information. Stratification by age subgroups (65–74, 75–84, ≥ 85) allows for more granular analysis of demographic trends and service usage across the older adult population. Additionally, the inclusion of comparative data for other age groups enables contextualisation of findings within the broader scope of national HEMS activity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged, however. The retrospective nature of the study limits causal inference. Incomplete data across some variables - such as mission status or destination - may introduce bias. Furthermore, the evolving nature of Ireland’s trauma system over the study period limits the interpretation of transport patterns to MTCs. No outcome data were available to assess the impact of HEMS on patient morbidity or mortality, and no comparison to overall EMS usage by age group could be made.
