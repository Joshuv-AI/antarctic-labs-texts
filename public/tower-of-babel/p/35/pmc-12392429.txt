Serotype 3 pneumococcal pleural empyema: a case report and review of the epidemiology
Authors: Maja Thoustrup Legardt Johansen, Frederikke Kristensen Lomholt, Jens-Ulrik Stæhr Jensen, Zitta Barrella Harboe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSerotype 3 pneumococcal infection can cause severe invasive disease, even in immunocompetent adults, and is potentially preventable by vaccination. Invasive pneumococcal disease caused by serotype 3 is currently the most frequent serotype found in adults in Denmark. In this report, we present a 62-year-old immunocompetent man diagnosed with a severe pleural empyema caused by serotype 3, requiring a long course of antibiotics. The patient experienced clinical improvement, and follow-up imaging showed near-complete resolution of lung infiltrates. We also review the epidemiology of serotype 3 invasive pneumococcal disease in adults in Denmark and discuss the challenges related to serotype 3 and effective vaccines.

Introduction

Despite widespread use of pneumococcal vaccines, Streptococcus pneumoniae, the pneumococcus, remains a cause of morbidity and mortality globally [1,2]. The pneumococcus causes non-invasive diseases, including otitis media, sinusitis, and pneumonia, as well as invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) [3]. Invasive pneumococcal disease occurs when normally sterile body fluids are invaded, leading to conditions like meningitis, endocarditis, and bacteremia. The pneumococcal polysaccharide capsule, an important virulence factor, is used to classify the bacterium into serotypes. Over 100 different pneumococcal serotypes have been identified with differing levels of invasiveness and pathogenic potential [4,5]. Certain serotypes, including serotype 3, have been associated with more severe disease compared to other serotypes and with a higher risk of death [5,6]. Furthermore, pneumococcal vaccines against serotype 3 appear to have a varying effect, making this serotype a persisting global challenge [7–9].

We present a case of an immunocompetent adult patient hospitalized with severe serotype 3 IPD. This case illustrates the severity of serotype 3 infection and reinforces the importance of clinical awareness even in patients who would not typically be considered at high risk. Furthermore, we discuss the clinical significance and epidemiology of serotype 3 IPD in Denmark, as well as the challenges related to vaccination against this specific serotype.

Case

A 62-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 3-day history of fever, coughing, shortness of breath, and right-sided chest pain that worsened with deep inspiration. He had no history of smoking or any underlying chronic disease, and he received no medication. One week before the presentation, he returned from a business trip to Spain but had no other risk exposures.

At presentation, his vital signs were as follows: respiratory rate 30 breaths per minute; percutaneous oxygen saturation 93% on atmospheric air; blood pressure 115/76 mmHg; heart rate 99 beats per minute; and a temperature of 38.0°C. Auscultation of the lungs revealed decreased respiratory sounds in the right lower lung field with rales. Laboratory findings were as follows: white blood cell count 12.0 × 109/L [normal range 3.5–8.8 ×109/L] with 10.7 × 109/L neutrophils [normal range 1.60–5.90 ×109/L]; and C-reactive protein 367 mg/L [normal range < 10 mg/L]. An arterial blood gas showed pH 7.46 [normal range 7.37–7.45], O2 7.8 kPa [normal range 9.6–14.4 kPa], CO2 4.5 kPa [normal range 4.7–6.0 kPa], lactate 1.4 mmol/L [normal range 0.7–2.1 mmol/L], and bicarbonate 25.3 mmol/L [normal range 22.0–27.0 mmol/L]. Point-of-care rapid influenza A/B and SARS-CoV-2 PCR tests were negative. A chest radiograph revealed an opacity at the basis of the right lung, interpreted by the radiologist as bronchopneumonia (Figure 1). The patient was started on empirical treatment with intravenous (i.v.) penicillin 2 million international units (IU) every 6 hours. Figure 1.Chest X-ray (PA) from the day of hospital admission.

Within 24 hours after admission, blood cultures were positive for S. pneumoniae — further identification revealed a pneumococcal serotype 3 isolate, sensitive to penicillin. The patient’s condition worsened clinically over the following days, with persisting fever, increased supplementary oxygen need, increased white blood cell count (15.7 ×109/L), and C-reactive protein (318 mg/L). On day 4, a chest computed tomography scan with contrast revealed right-sided pneumonic infiltrates and a pleural effusion consistent with empyema (Figure 2). Pleurocentesis was attempted multiple times, including under image guidance, but was unsuccessful – possibly due to the empyema being located behind bony structures. On day 7, focused lung ultrasound (FLUS) revealed a small, 2 cm-wide accumulation along the medial border of the left scapula that was not amenable to drainage. Thus, the conservative approach was continued, adding oral metronidazole 500 mg every 8 hours to the treatment empirically due to concern for anaerobic co-infection, despite cultures remaining negative for anaerobes. After the first week, a significant improvement could be seen in clinical imaging and blood biochemistry. On day 10, the patient was discharged from the hospital and continued the IV-antibiotic treatment at home for 3 weeks, followed by treatment with oral penicillin 1 million IU every 6 hours for a total of 6 weeks of antibiotic treatment. Oral metronidazole was also continued for 6 weeks in total. The patient continued clinical improvement throughout the weeks of antibiotic treatment. Four months after the hospital admission, a follow-up chest CT scan showed a remarkable regression of the lung opacities, and pleural empyema resolved without the need for surgical intervention (Figure 3). A 1-year follow-up chest CT scan showed near-complete resolution of the lung infiltrates. Figure 3.Chest computed tomography scan taken 4 months after hospital admission.

Since an episode of IPD is a great risk factor for a new occurrence of IPD, the patient was recommended vaccination with PPV23 (Pneumovax) according to guidelines from the Statens Serum Institut (SSI), which he received 4 months after recovery. SSI is a governmental public health and research institution under the Danish Ministry of Health, working with prevention and control of infectious diseases, biological threats, and congenital disorders [10].

Current epidemiology of invasive pneumococcal disease and pneumococcal serotype 3 in Denmark

Despite the inclusion of serotype 3 in both the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPV23) and the 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13), the continued persistence of serotype 3 IPD forms an important burden of disease. This trend of persistence has been observed in several countries, including Denmark [11].

In Denmark, the first pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) was introduced in 2007 [12] in the childhood vaccination program, and in 2010, this vaccine was replaced by a 13-valent PCV, which includes serotype 3 [13]. Pneumococcal conjugate vaccination had a rapid effect in reducing the incidence of IPD, both in vaccinated and non-vaccinated cohorts [13].

Surveillance of IPD has been mandatory since 2007 and is conducted by the Statens Serum Institut (SSI). The departments of clinical microbiology across the country send S. pneumoniae isolates from sample material from invasive infections to the laboratory for characterization and serotyping. To review the epidemiology of serotype 3 IPD, we retrieved data on laboratory-confirmed cases of IPD from 2010 to 2023. We reported the incidence as the number of IPD cases per 100,000 population stratified by serotype group: serotype 3, PCV13 serotypes excluding serotype 3, non-PCV13 serotypes, and unknown serotype. The data on IPD were obtained from the Pneumococcal Laboratory at the Department of Bacteria, Parasites, and Fungi, which collects data on S. pneumoniae cases [14]. Population numbers were retrieved from Statistics Denmark, the national central authority on Danish statistics [15].

Following the introduction of PCV13 in the childhood vaccination program, IPD incidence due to PCV13 serotypes, excluding serotype 3, declined dramatically, from 8.7 per 100,000 in 2010 to 0.6 per 100,000 in 2018, and has remained low. On the contrary, IPD incidence due to non-PCV13 serotypes tended to increase from 7.7 per 100,000 in 2010 to 11.4 per 100,000 in 2018. The incidence dropped first in 2019 and then during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021. Since 2021, the incidence has increased again, but the level in 2023 is still below the level in 2010, with 7.2 per 100,000 population. The incidence of serotype 3 IPD remained stable from 2010 to 2021, with 0.7–1.4 per 100,000, but has increased in both 2022 and 2023 (Figure 4). The rise in serotype 3 IPD was primarily driven in a rise in patients aged 50 years and older, whereas no apparent increase has been detected among children (Figure 5). In the age group 50–64 years (Figure 5(e)), the incidence of serotype 3 IPD increased from 1.8 to 2.8 per 100,000 from 2010 to 2023. For the age group 65+ years (Figure 5(f)), a rise in the incidence from 5.0 to 6.9 per 100,000 from 2010 to 2023 was seen. Discussion

Clinical significance of serotype 3

In Denmark, approximately 700 patients are treated for pleural infections, covering both parapneumonic effusion and empyema, each year [16]. With an estimated mortality rate of 15% [17], empyema is a serious complication of pneumonia, where pus accumulates in the pleural space. In our patient, a pneumococcal serotype 3 invasive infection resulted in bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia with empyema, leading to hospital admission and prolonged antibiotic treatment despite the younger age under 65 years, the absence of underlying medical conditions, including immunosuppression.

Pneumococcal serotype 3 is a serotype known to be associated with severe invasive infections. A retrospective evaluation of serotype-specific mortality found a relative risk (RR) of death of 2.54 (95% CI: 1.22; 5.27) associated with IPD caused by serotype 3 compared with a reference group consisting of all other serotypes except 1 and 3 [18]. Furthermore, in a large nationwide Danish cohort study with 18,000 patients over 30 years in a pre-PCV setting, serotype 3 infection was strongly associated with a higher 30-day mortality in IPD patients as compared to the reference serotype 1, the most prevalent serotype in the study population [19].

In a meta-analysis, a significantly higher risk of death was associated with bacteremic pneumonia caused by serotype 3 compared with serotype 14 [6]. Various other epidemiological studies have found a similar increased risk of death associated with serotype 3 IPD when comparing serotype mortality [20–27].

Specifically concerning pleural empyema, as seen in our patient, infection with serotype 3 has also been identified as a risk factor, as it is independently associated with the development of empyema [28,29]. Besides its association with a higher mortality and risk of empyema, serotype 3 is also related to a higher risk of septic shock, major cardiac adverse events, respiratory failure, and ICU-admission [20,30–33].

From an international perspective, various studies have found similar trends in the epidemiology as in Denmark. In England, Bertran et al. assessed IPD trends during the period of 2017–2023 using prospective surveillance data, where they found an increase in the proportion of PCV13-type IPD cases, mainly driven by a rise in serotype 3 cases, which became the most prevalent serotype by the end of the study period [34]. In Spain, serotype 3 also emerged as the most frequent serotype causing IPD among adults ≥65 years in a study characterizing > 10,000 IPD isolates [35]. Likewise, van der Linden et al. reported a rapid increase in serotype 3 IPD incidence in adults ≥60 years in Germany during 2003–2018, accounting for 21% of all cases in 2017/18 in this age group [36]. In France, a 17-year national surveillance study found that the serotype 3 IPD incidence has remained stable after introduction of the PCV13 vaccine, with no significant reduction in cases due to serotype 3 [37]. Furthermore, a Canadian nationwide study covering the period 2021–2022 also reported a rise in serotype 3 prevalence [38].

Studies from different regions published over the past 9 years, including Africa [39], Asia [40,41], and Europe [32,42–44] have shown similar trends. Overall, these studies consistently show a pattern of either persistence or even an increase in cases of serotype 3 IPD despite differences in methodology, including participant numbers, study duration, surveillance methods, age group classifications, and adjustments for risk factors.

Current and forthcoming vaccines and their effect on serotype 3 IPD

In Denmark, pneumococcal vaccination with either PPV23 or the 20-valent pneumococcal conjugated vaccine PCV20 (Prevnar) is recommended for adults aged ≥65 years if no other risk factors are present [45] and for younger individuals with certain comorbidities. Vaccination can prevent invasive pneumococcal disease, but with a serotype 3 infection, this might not be the case, as limited impact of both the PPV23 and the PCV13 vaccine has been seen in adults [7–9,46]. The limited efficacy of PPV23 and PCV13 against serotype 3 may be due to the unique structure of its polysaccharide capsule, consisting of two types of monosaccharides bound to the bacterial membrane via non-covalent interactions with phosphatidylglycerol [3]. It has been proposed that the non-covalent nature of the attachment of the polysaccharide chain to the phosphatidylglycerol allows serotype 3 to readily shed its abundant capsule material into the surrounding environment. Therefore, antibodies elicited by a pneumococcal vaccine targeting the capsule will instead bind to the released polysaccharides, reducing their ability to opsonize the bacteria [47]. The protective capacity of the antibodies will therefore be reduced, requiring a greater antibody response to be stimulated by the vaccine to combat strains of serotype 3.

In a Danish nationwide retrospective study, Nielsen et al. found a lack of vaccine effectiveness of PPV23 against serotype 3 IPD (−17% (95% CI: −83%; 25%)) in adults ≥65 years [7]. Protection against the other serotypes included in PPV23 was reported, with vaccine effectiveness of 58% (95% CI: 43%; 68%) for the remaining serotypes [7]. Furthermore, a retrospective evaluation from Germany with 928 patients ≥60 years did not find a protective effect against serotype 3 IPD either [46].

The introduction of PCV13 has remarkedly reduced vaccine-type IPD incidence in children via the direct protective effect [48]. In terms of indirect protection, PCV13 also caused a decline in the incidence of PCV13-type IPD in older adults due to its ability to reduce asymptomatic nasopharyngeal carriage and spread from the vaccinated children, also called the indirect effect [49–53]. The indirect effect of PCV13 against serotype 3 has not yet been reported [13,36]. More data on the direct protection against serotype 3 in adults granted by PCV13 are needed. Two studies from the U.S. evaluating vaccine effectiveness in adults aged ≥65 years both found estimates of protection with absence of statistical significance and suggesting no effectiveness; the results being 26% (95% CI: −58%; 65%) and 46.3% (95% CI: −2.4%; 71.9%, p-value: 0.059), respectively [8,9].

Children are commonly considered the main reservoir of pneumococcal strains, but recent studies suggest a potential adult reservoir, which could play a role in the persistence of serotype 3 IPD despite widespread use of PCVs in children [54,55]. Eradication of serotype 3 IPD seems difficult if adult-to-adult transmission maintains a pneumococcal reservoir in a population group not targeted by the current vaccination programs – even if the vaccines are effective against serotype 3.

A new strategy for prevention of IPD could include the newly FDA-approved 21-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (CAPVAXIVE, MSD) [56]. PCV21 targets the main serotypes infecting the American adult population, and it shows promising immunogenicity results against serotype 3 IPD in a phase 3 trial including adults aged 50 and older [57]. Compared with PCV20, the trial found a serotype-specific non-inferiority geometric mean titer (GMT) ratio against serotype 3 of 1.55 (95% CI: 1.40; 1.72%) at day 30 after vaccination. It remains to be seen whether this will be translated into clinical efficacy.

The effectiveness of the PCV21 vaccine, including serotype 3-specific effectiveness, remains to be evaluated as the vaccination rate increases in the population. As it is targeted at the serotypes causing IPD in the American population, the serotype coverage of the vaccine may vary in other adult populations. PCV21 is not yet approved in Europe. Currently, pneumococcal vaccination is recommended for adults aged 50 years and older in the U.S. [58].

A potential future prevention strategy could be pneumococcal protein vaccines, which may provide serotype-independent protection. A recent review by Ramos et al. provides an overview of the development of pneumococcal protein vaccines, including monovalent surface proteins such as pneumococcal surface protein A (PspA) and pneumococcal histidine triad protein D (PhtD), along with multivalent protein vaccines [59]. Multivalent protein vaccines, with various proteins combined, may better account for the amino acid sequence variations across different serotypes. Ongoing research evaluates antibody responses and vaccine tolerability to both types of protein vaccines as well as combinations of PCVs and protein vaccines. However, no protein vaccines have been approved to date [59]. Consequently, their effectiveness against serotype 3 remains unknown.

This study has limitations. First, only a single patient case is presented, limiting generalizability. Second, the reported incidences of serotype 3 IPD in our study may be lower than the actual burden of infection due to incomplete sampling.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our case highlights the severity of serotype 3 IPD, even in a previously healthy adult, emphasizing its pathogenic potential and association with complications such as empyema and bacteremia. Despite being included in pneumococcal vaccines, serotype 3 continues to persist among adults and even increases in incidence in some countries, including Denmark. Current evidence indicates that there are challenges in achieving optimal vaccine effectiveness against serotype 3 in adults. Continued surveillance and optimized vaccination strategies are essential to lower the burden of serotype 3 disease and reduce its associated morbidity and mortality in adults.
