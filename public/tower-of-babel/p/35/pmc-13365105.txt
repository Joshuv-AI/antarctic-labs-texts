A case report of carotid body tumor with rare thoracic vertebral metastasis and literature review
Authors: Fei Li, Jie Yang, Juan Zhang, Mengfan Zhao, Shuai Qie, Shasha Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalignant carotid body tumor (CBT), a type of carotid body paraganglioma (PGL), is defined by the presence of distant metastasis; histology alone cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant CBT.Case summaryWe present a 70-year-old female patient who had been suspected of having CBT for more than 10 years without specific treatment, and complained of abdominal pain for 6 months. Contrast-enhanced MRI and CT revealed a T11 vertebral lesion, followed by percutaneous biopsy of the lesion, which showed atypical cell nests. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for CD56, CgA, Syn and S-100, and negative for cytokeratin. A diagnosis of metastatic PGL consistent with malignant CBT was established. The patient received palliative radiotherapy to the T11 vertebra (20 Gy/5 fractions), which was well tolerated without obvious radiation-related adverse reactions. At the 12-month follow-up, the patient remained alive.ConclusionIn this case, a highly suggestive diagnosis of PGL (malignant CBT) with bone metastasis was made based on imaging features, T11 vertebral biopsy, and immunohistochemical results. It provides a reference for radiotherapy in similar clinical settings. For unresectable thoracic vertebral metastases, palliative radiotherapy (20 Gy/5 fractions) can be considered after clinical evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05208-5.Keywords: Carotid body tumor, Paraganglioma, Spinal metastasis, Radiotherapy, SDHB

Introduction

Carotid body tumor (CBT) is a paraganglioma (PGL) arising from chemoreceptor cells at the carotid bifurcation, with an incidence of approximately 1–2 cases per 100,000 people [1]. It accounts for 65% of all head and neck paragangliomas (HNPGL) [2]. The most common presentation is a painless neck mass, occurring in approximately 75% of cases, 85% of which are asymptomatic [3]. Although the overall prognosis of CBT is favorable, the 5-year survival rate for malignant cases is approximately 60%, and decreases to around 12% in patients with distant metastasis [4]. According to the latest World Health Organization (WHO) guideline, all paragangliomas should be regarded as tumors with malignant potential, with metastatic potential comparable to that of epithelial neuroendocrine tumors [5]. Since histologic examination alone cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant CBT, malignancy is defined mainly by the presence of lymph node or distant metastasis. Therefore, lifelong follow-up is essential for patients with carotid body tumor to promptly detect recurrence or distant metastasis and ensure timely intervention [6]. This case highlights the rare thoracic vertebral metastatic pattern of malignant CBT and its favorable response to palliative radiotherapy.

Case presentation

Clinical history

A 70-year-old female patient presented with intermittent right flank pain for 6 months, which had worsened over the past 4 days, on October 16, 2024. She had been suspected of having a right CBT for more than 10 years. The mass had slowly enlarged, and no treatment had been administered. She denied other chronic medical conditions and a family history of genetic disorders.

Thoracic computed tomography (CT) performed at an outside hospital on October 15, 2024, revealed flattening of the T11 vertebral body with discontinuous cortical bone and a soft-tissue density mass. Spinal canal stenosis was observed at the T11 level, suggestive of a space-occupying lesion with pathological fracture of the T11 vertebra. The patient was admitted to our hospital for further diagnosis and treatment.

Imaging evaluation

Thoracic spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed on October 18 (Fig. 1) demonstrated slight flattening of the T11 vertebral body with abnormal signals in the vertebral body and appendages, showing slightly long T1 and slightly short T2 signals and high signal on T2 fat-suppression sequences. The lesion showed expansive growth to the surrounding tissues with spinal canal stenosis at the T11 level, highly suggestive of metastatic tumor of the T11 vertebra. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) was recommended to identify the primary tumor and assess for distant metastasis, but this was refused by the patient and family.

Contrast-enhanced neck CT on October 23 (Fig. 2) demonstrated an oval soft-tissue mass in the right carotid sheath region, showing marked vascular enhancement with a central hypoenhanced component. The mass, measuring approximately 6.2 × 6.1 × 7.2 cm, is located at the carotid bifurcation, causing splaying of the internal and external carotid arteries with a cup-shaped appearance. No abnormal enlarged lymph nodes were identified in the surrounding regions. The lesion was significantly larger than on the prior images obtained in July 2019. Contrast-enhanced chest CT revealed multiple solid nodular opacities in both lungs. Some of the nodules exhibited mild heterogeneous enhancement with irregular margins. The largest lesion, measuring approximately 0.7 cm × 0.6 cm, was situated in the anterior segment of the right upper lobe.

Pathology

On October 25, CT-guided percutaneous core needle biopsy of the T11 vertebral occupying lesion was performed with a bone biopsy needle. Histopathological examination of the T11 vertebral lesion tissue revealed blood clots, fragmented bone tissue, and fibrous tissue, with scattered nests of atypical cells (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated the following: neural cell adhesion molecule 1 (CD56) positive (+), chromogranin A (CgA) positive (+), synaptophysin (Syn) positive (+), S-100 protein (S-100) positive (+), cytokeratin (CK) negative (-), vimentin (Vim) positive (+), and Ki-67 labeling index 5%.

Diagnosis

An oval soft-tissue mass is seen in the right carotid sheath region, demonstrating marked enhancement with central hypoenhancement, resulting in splaying of the internal and external carotid arteries in a “cup-spout” pattern, consistent with a CBT. Given the relatively low incidence of bone metastasis from malignant CBT, the isolated bone destruction at the T11 vertebral body requires further differentiation from primary malignant bone tumor, skeletal tuberculosis, and bone lymphoma; marked enhancement of the lesion favors a metastatic focus. Combined with the biopsy and immunohistochemical findings of the T11 vertebral body, the lesion is highly suggestive of metastatic paraganglioma, consistent with a malignant CBT.

Treatment

After multidisciplinary team discussion and in-depth communication with the patient and their family, palliative treatment was agreed upon and initiated. The patient received 30 mg of pamidronate disodium in glucose injection for anti-bone metastasis therapy, combined with external beam radiotherapy targeting the T11 vertebral lesion to alleviate pain and control local tumor progression. The target volume was defined as follows: the gross tumor volume (GTV) included the T11 vertebral body and the adjacent soft tissue mass, and the planning target volume (PTV) was defined as the GTV with a 0.5-cm circumferential expansion. The prescribed dose was 20 Gy to the PTV, delivered in 5 fractions of 4 Gy per fraction. The patient reported significant pain relief from metastatic lesions after radiotherapy, with no abnormal neurological function or obvious adverse reactions during 4 weeks of weekly follow-up.

Follow-up

The patient was followed up regularly in the outpatient clinic for 12-month after discharge, with reexamination every three months. The patient remained alive at the 12-month follow-up. Discussion

The incidence of CBT is relatively low, and its malignant transformation rate is merely 7.3%, as reported in the systematic review by Cesare Piazza et al. [7]. Among the 158 patients with malignant CBT included in that study, only 7 presented with vertebral body metastasis (Supplement). In this case, based on the patient’s clinical history and immunohistochemical findings, a final diagnosis of PGL with thoracic spinal metastasis was highly suspected, consistent with the clinical features of malignant CBT. The patient underwent fractionated palliative radiotherapy and completed 12 months of clinical follow-up. Given the substantial risks of massive hemorrhage, thrombosis and pseudoaneurysm formation, puncture biopsy of the primary cervical lesion was not performed [8]. Additionally, the patient declined further PET/CT examination and biopsy of the pulmonary lesion, leaving the nature of the pulmonary micronodules undetermined. Consequently, it was difficult to differentiate these micronodules as metastatic lesions, primary lung cancer or benign diseases, and accurate systemic staging could not be achieved. These represent the major limitations of the present case and also bring challenges to its diagnosis and management.

In recent years, with extensive research on the molecular basis of PGL, more than 20 germline and somatic mutations in PGL driver genes have been identified [9]. Of note, SDHB (succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit B) mutations represent one of the most clinically significant variants, strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes in CBT patients. Previous retrospective studies have shown that patients with SDHB variants have a median disease-free survival of only 97 months and a recurrence rate of 50%, while those without SDHB variants have a median disease-free survival of 293 months and a recurrence rate of 22.7%. For patients carrying SDHB variants, recurrence or metastasis of CBT may occur at any time within a follow-up period of up to 200 months [10]. The majority of metastatic tumours are associated with SDHB gene mutations [11, 12]. In this case report, the patient did not receive regular standardized follow-up, genetic counseling, or genetic testing for more than ten years after the initial suspected diagnosis, which precluded precise risk stratification and determination of the correlation between genotype and clinical phenotype. Although the patient exhibited an aggressive clinical course, the lack of genetic testing precluded clarification of its genetic background, which constitutes one limitation of the present case report. This case highlights that for patients with suspected PGL, genetic testing for SDHB and other high-risk pathogenic variants should be performed as early as possible, and a standardized long-term follow-up protocol should be established to achieve individualized risk stratification. Routine genetic screening is recommended for first-degree relatives of patients with familial PGL to enable early identification and personalized management of high-risk populations [13].

Many CBTs grow slowly and are not associated with obvious clinical symptoms [3, 14], which supports the use of an active surveillance strategy. During follow-up, PET-CT should be performed as early as possible if the tumor demonstrates significant volume increase, vascular invasion, or high suspicion of metastatic PGL. Studies have demonstrated that CBT may exhibit high fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) avidity, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) ranging from 2 to 44 [15], and their metabolic activity is closely associated with genotype. High FDG metabolism suggests the possibility of a succinate dehydrogenase mutation [16]. Furthermore, FDG uptake is significantly higher in metastatic than in non-metastatic CBTs, with a median SUVmax of 20.5 [15]. Enhanced chest CT identified a pulmonary lesion in this patient. Further PET/CT was recommended to evaluate its metabolic activity and screen for systemic metastasis. However, the patient declined this optimal diagnostic workup, along with invasive procedures for definitive pathological confirmation such as targeted biopsy and bronchoscopic sampling. As a result, the nature of the pulmonary nodule remains unclear, which compromises the accuracy of systemic tumor staging. This raises the risk of underestimating tumor burden and overlooking occult metastatic foci. Such diagnostic uncertainty further impairs staging precision, limits individualized treatment stratification and prognostic assessment, and negatively impacts clinical decision-making, palliative care formulation, and long-term follow-up strategies.

Notably, the tumor Ki-67 proliferation index in this patient was 5%, exceeding the 3% cutoff linked to aggressive biological behavior of tumors [17]. While Ki-67 is widely adopted as an objective biomarker in routine pathological diagnosis, its prognostic role in PGL remains to be fully validated. Recent studies have demonstrated that a Ki-67 index ≥ 3% is significantly correlated with an increased risk of disease progression, recurrence, and metastasis in patients with PGL [18]. Such evidence validates the prognostic utility of Ki-67 and supports its application as a reliable marker for risk stratification in PGL. Accordingly, the Ki-67 index of 5% in this case indicates a higher propensity for adverse clinical behavior and an unfavorable prognosis. Routine Ki-67 detection in clinicopathological evaluation facilitates more accurate prognostic assessment, helping clinicians develop individualized and rational treatment and follow-up regimens for patients with PGL.

Surgical intervention remains the definitive treatment for CBT. Nevertheless, owing to the unique anatomical location of CBT, the procedural risks and complications have long been a major concern for clinicians [19, 20]. Due to distant vertebral metastases, radical surgical resection is not feasible. Furthermore, the T11 vertebral metastatic lesion involves the vertebral body, epidural space and spinal canal, conferring an extremely high risk of surgical complications. In addition, malignant CBT is generally refractory to conventional chemotherapy, whereas high-level clinical evidence supporting targeted and immunotherapy remains limited. After multidisciplinary team discussion and full consideration of the patient’s preference, palliative radiotherapy was delivered. For the palliative pain control of bone metastases, single-fraction radiotherapy (8 Gy per fraction) provides pain relief comparable to that of conventional multifraction regimens [21–24]. Nevertheless, multifraction radiotherapy remains more widely used in clinical practice and is particularly recommended for patients with complex bone metastases [21]. The regimen of 20 Gy delivered in 5 fractions was finally selected. Given the patient’s clinical features of vertebral collapse, bone destruction and spinal stenosis, together with individual-specific risks including high pathological fracture risk and spinal canal involvement, this regimen enables effective local lesion control, arrests progressive bone destruction, and lowers the risk of pathological fracture and spinal cord compression. It also yields favorable treatment tolerability and achieves an optimal balance between oncological control and safety, overcoming the limitations of single-fraction radiotherapy, which is inadequate for local control and cannot halt progressive bone destruction.

Integrating imaging findings, T11 vertebral biopsy and immunohistochemical results, this case presents the clinical manifestations of a highly suggestive CBT complicated with thoracic vertebral metastasis, providing a reference for radiotherapy in similar clinical scenarios. Regular follow-up and standardized whole-course management are of great importance. Early genetic screening and genetic counseling should be performed as early as possible to guide subsequent surveillance and individualized treatment. After comprehensive clinical evaluation, palliative radiotherapy (20 Gy in 5 fractions) can be administered to improve the patient’s quality of life.
