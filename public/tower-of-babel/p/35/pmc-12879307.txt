Biological sex as a tailoring variable for exercise prescription in hospitalized older adults
Authors: Mikel L Sáez de Asteasu, Nicolás Martínez-Velilla, Robinson Ramírez-Vélez, Fabricio Zambom-Ferraresi, Arkaitz Galbete, Eduardo L Cadore, Mikel Izquierdo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSex-based differences in the clinical presentation and outcomes are well-established in patients hospitalized for geriatric syndromes. We aimed to investigate sex differences in response to in-hospital exercise on function, strength, cognition, and quality of life in acute care admissions.Methods570 patients (mean age 87 years, 298 females [52.3%]) admitted to acute care for elderly units were randomized to multicomponent exercise emphasizing progressive resistance training or usual care. Functional assessments included Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), grip strength, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and health-related quality of life (EQ-VAS).ResultsExercising females showed more significant SPPB improvements than males (between-group difference 1.48 points, p = 0.027), exceeding the minimal clinically significant difference. While female participants significantly increased handgrip strength and male patients improved cognition after in-hospital exercise compared to the control group (all p < 0.001), no sex differences occurred.ConclusionsFemales demonstrate more excellent physical function improvements compared to male older patients. Findings highlight the importance of tailored exercise incorporating patient factors like biological sex in geriatric medicine.Trial registrationNCT04600453.

Introduction

Sex-based differences in the clinical presentation and outcomes have been documented for decades in patients hospitalized for geriatric syndromes and confirmed in recent years [1,2]. Women generally have a more significant comorbidity burden and are more likely to have atypical symptoms than men [3]. In contrast, studies have reported women have longer life expectancy but often live longer with disabilities compared to men [4]. Canto et al., in an extensive survey of sex differences in older adults hospitalized for acute myocardial infarction, found admission functional impairment prevalence was higher in women versus men [5]. Compared to men, women have poorer physical functioning and a more rapid decline [[6], [7], [8]]. However, whether deterioration patterns differ by sex and physical/cognitive function remains unclear.

Discerning the specific pattern of functional decline, considering the demographic (e.g., sex) and clinical characteristics of hospitalized patients, may be able to provide effective interventions to prevent functional decline and establish an individualized approach. In this regard, in-hospital physical exercise interventions and early rehabilitation programs have been proposed to mitigate these deficits [9]. Although meta-analyses support in-hospital exercise effectiveness for functional enhancement in this patient population, a recent Cochrane review concluded uncertainty regarding acute hospitalization exercise effects on functional endpoints in older medical inpatients [10]. Moreover, exercise is rarely fully integrated into aged care and geriatric medicine practice, and high-quality evidence is still needed to confirm in-hospital exercise-induced health improvements [11,12].

Towards personalized geriatric medicine preventing hospital-acquired disability (HAD) [13], exercise appears to be an effective intervention with several clinical benefits, considering the wide variability in individual exercise response [14]. A recent dose-response meta-analysis of clinical trials has investigated the optimal exercise type and dose to improve function and minimize adverse events in acute hospitalizations, highlighting exercise as a core clinical practice [15]. Alongside exercise modality and dosage, biological factors like sex must be considered to understand the evidence base on optimal exercise prescription. However, no previous studies have investigated sex-based exercise training differences in acute hospitalizations.

Thus, this study aimed to determine the role of biological sex as a moderator of exercise efficacy in older acute care admissions. We hypothesized that biological sex differences would be observed in response to the in-hospital exercise program.

Methods

Study design

This study presents the effects on secondary outcomes from the multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT); the primary outcomes study and main impact can be found elsewhere [16,17]. The data collection took place from February 1, 2015, to April 1, 2021, in acute care for elderly (ACE) units at three tertiary hospitals in Spain (Hospital Universitario de Navarra of Pamplona, Hospital Central de la Cruz Roja of Madrid and Complejo Hospitalario Universitario of Albacete) (NCT04600453). This study adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guideline (S1 CONSORT Checklist). Admissions to the ACE units were mainly due to heart failure and pulmonary and infectious diseases from the emergency department.

The multicenter RCT complies with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [18] and was approved by the local ethics committee (Pyto 2018/7). All patients or their legal representatives gave written informed consent.

Participants

All patients admitted to the ACE units were examined by geriatricians to determine potential eligibility for the RCT. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥75 years, Barthel Index score ≥60 points (assessed two weeks before admission), ability to walk (with/without assistance), and ability to communicate and collaborate with the research team. Exclusion criteria included expected hospital stay of less than six days, very severe cognitive decline (i.e., Global Deterioration Scale score = 7), terminal illness, uncontrolled arrhythmias, acute pulmonary embolism, recent myocardial infarction, recent major surgery, or extremity bone fracture in the past three months.

Randomization and masking

After the baseline assessment, participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group using an online system generated by a statistician not involved in the study (http://www.randomizer.org). Participants were randomized 1:1 after the baseline evaluation, and the physical therapists running the exercise program were not involved in the endpoint assessments or randomization. It was not possible to blind the participants due to the nature of the intervention, but they were explicitly informed and reminded not to comment their randomization assignment with the assessment staff.

Procedures and interventions

Acutely hospitalized older patients meeting the inclusion criteria were randomly assigned to the intervention or control (usual care) group within the first 48 h of admission. The control group enrolled patients in the usual-care group, which received standard hospital care, which included physical rehabilitation focused on walking exercises to restore functionality when needed.

The exercise program (intervention group) lasted 3–7 consecutive days (including weekends) and was based on the Vivifrail multicomponent physical exercise program to prevent falls and weakness in older adults [19]. Patients participated in 2 daily sessions (morning and evening) of 20 min each. The morning sessions involved supervised progressive resistance/power training, balance, and walking exercises adapted to the individual's functional status using variable resistance training machines (Matrix; Johnson Health Tech and Exercycle S.L., BH group). Upper (i.e., seated bench [chest] press) and lower body strengthening exercises (squats rising from a chair, leg press, and bilateral knee extension) were performed at a load equivalent to 30%–60% of the one-repetition maximum, with 2–3 sets of 8–10 repetitions. Patients were encouraged to perform the concentric phase of exercises at high speed for optimized muscle power output, with correct execution guaranteed. Furthermore, balance and gait training exercises were prescribed, including semi-tandem foot standing, line walking, stepping practice, walking with small obstacles, proprioceptive exercises on unstable surfaces, alteration of the base of support, and weight transfer from one leg to the other. Adherence to the exercise intervention program was documented in a daily register. A session was considered completed when 90% or more of the programmed exercises were successfully performed.

In the evening, functional unsupervised exercises using light loads (0.5–1 kg anklets and hand-grip ball), such as knee extension/flexion, hip abduction, and daily walking in the corridor of the ACE unit, were completed. The duration of the exercises was based on the clinical physical exercise guide “Vivifrail” [19].

The intervention started when the clinician in charge of the patient deemed the patient's hemodynamic situation acceptable and when the patient could collaborate.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was a change in physical function during hospitalization (i.e., from admission to discharge) assessed by the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) test and the 6-meter gait velocity test (GVT). Secondary endpoints were muscle strength, cognitive function, and health-related quality of life (HRQL). The endpoints were evaluated at the start of the intervention and again on the day of discharge.

SPPB and 6-meter GVT

The SPPB includes the usual walking speed over 4 meters, a balance test (i.e., side-by-side stand, semi tandem, full-tandem), and the Five Times Sit to Stand Test (FTSST). The sum of the three individual categorical scores yields the final SPPB score (range points: 0 [worst]-12 [best]) [20]. For the 6-meter GVT, patients were instructed to walk at their self-selected usual pace on a smooth, horizontal walkway.

Muscle strength

Maximal isometric handgrip strength was measured in the dominant hand with a handheld dynamometer (T.K.K. 5401 Grip-D, Japan). Patients were placed in a sitting position in a chair, with an elbow fully extended, and asked to squeeze the handle as forcefully as possible for 3 seconds. After this, two valid trials followed, and the highest value was used for the analysis.

Cognitive function

Cognitive ability was evaluated by the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) test [21]. The MMSE assesses orientation, memory, attention, language, and visuospatial ability domains. It is scored from 0 to 30 points, with a score of 23 indicative of likely cognitive impairment.

HRQL

The visual analog scale (VAS) included in the EuroQol Five Dimensions (EQ-5D) questionnaire was used to examine changes in quality of life during acute hospitalization. Participants in the EQ-VAS were asked to indicate how good or bad their health state was on a scale from 0 (worst imaginable health state) to 100 (best imaginable health state) [22].

Meaningful clinical change for the endpoints

The inter-individual variability of the participants in their response to hospital usual care in the control group and exercise training in the intervention group was used to categorize them as responders and non-responders using the meaningful clinical change of each endpoint during hospitalization: 1 point for the SPPB test [23], 0.1 m/s for the GVT [23], 1 kg for the handgrip strength [24], 3 points for the MMSE test [25], and 3 points for the EQ-VAS [26].

Baseline clinical descriptors

Barthel Index was used to assess independence of activities of daily living (ADLs) and ranges from 0 (severe functional dependence) to 100 (functional independence). Cumulative Illness Rating Scale (CIRS) scale evaluates individual body systems, ranging from 0 (best) to 56 (worst). The Mini Nutritional Assessment examines nutritional status and ranges from normal nutritional status (24–30 points), risk of malnutrition (17–23.5 points), or malnourished (<17 points). One repetition maximum test (1RM) was performed to examine maximal dynamic strength for lower and upper limbs exercises (i.e., leg press, bench press and knee extension). The Yesavage Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) evaluates mental health status of the participants and ranges from 0 (best) to 15 (worst).

Statistical analysis

Statistical power considerations were reported previously [17]. We used the intention-to-treat approach. Baseline differences between the intervention and the control group were assessed by means and standard deviation for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical ones.

To evaluate the effect of the intervention in each sex, linear mixed models were fitted for each clinical outcome variable, stratified by sex. We included randomization group, time, and group-by-time interaction for each model as fixed effects and participants as a random effect, with a random intercept. Time was treated as a categorical variable. To compare the intervention effects between biological sex groups, a linear mixed model was fitted with the whole sample, including participants as a random effect and randomization group, time, sex, and their two and three-way interactions as fixed effects, using the latter to evaluate the differences. Between sex baseline differences were adjusted in all analyses. Data were analyzed using R version 4.1.0. A complete case analysis was performed. The significance level was set at α = 0.05, and all tests conducted were two-tailed.

Results

The study flow diagram and baseline participant characteristics are shown in Fig. 1 and Table 1, respectively. Of the 570 patients included, 298 were females (52.3%). The mean age was 87.4 (4.8) years (range 75–101), with 193 participants (33.9%) nonagenarians. The median hospital stay was 8 days in both groups and sex categories (interquartile range, 3 days for the control and 4 for the intervention groups, respectively). Adherence to the intervention was 96.5% for the morning sessions (i.e., 965 successfully completed sessions of 1000 total possible sessions). The drop-out rate was 19.65%. No exercise-related adverse events occurred.

Discussion

The primary finding is that biological sex influences in-hospital exercise efficacy on physical function in acutely hospitalized older adults, with females showing more significant benefits than males.

Prolonged inactivity during hospitalization often precipitates HAD in older adults [27]. Functional decline, impaired cognition, deteriorated muscle function, and reduced health-related quality of life occur with hospitalization [28]. Our results suggest even short hospital stays may maintain or improve these endpoints, particularly in females.

Previous meta-analyses proposed exercise as a core treatment to prevent functional declines in hospitalized older adults [29]. A recent analysis highlighted the non-linear relationship between physical activity and function to determine optimal dosing to improve function and minimize adverse events [15]. Our findings align with this evidence, showing significant functional improvements after individualized multicomponent intervention, with particular emphasis on progressive resistance training, compared to the hospital’s usual care. However, no prior experimental studies have reported differences in sex-based exercise effectiveness.

Multiple physiological sex differences may contribute to differential, adaptive exercise responses [30]. Our study shows substantially more significant functional benefits in females versus male participants, with more than 1-point SPPB score difference. In contrast, a recent meta-analysis found no sex difference in absolute or relative resistance training-induced physical function changes in older adults [30]. Sayer et al. [31] suggested older females may depend more on contraction velocity and males more on muscle strength to perform functional tasks. Further research should investigate neuromuscular adaptations underlying functional improvements in acutely hospitalized older patients.

Exercise appears effective for counteracting muscle function loss linked to hospitalization (i.e., muscle strength/power and muscle mass) [32]. Although female exercisers showed significant improvements in handgrip strength, we found no significant sex difference after individualized, multicomponent exercise. Meta-analyses have identified sex variations in upper/lower strength changes with various exercise programs (i.e., aerobic, resistance, multi-component interventions) in older adults [30,33]. Males typically exhibit greater muscle mass and strength than females, even after height/weight correction [34,35]. Sex differences exist in muscle regulation factors like muscle fiber composition [36,37]. However, the short-term exercise intervention makes it unclear whether these explain observed differences. Future studies should explore hospitalization exercise effects on muscle function physiology to elucidate the wide inter-individual variability [14].

The previous meta-analysis showed in-hospital exercise benefits cognition in older adults [38]. Exercise appears to positively impact hospitalization cognitive trajectory across domains, including executive function, fluency, and global cognition [39]. Our findings showed that significant cognitive enhancements were obtained in male individuals compared to the hospital’s usual care. Nevertheless, we found no significant cognitive differences between the sexes after exercise versus longer interventions [40]. A meta-analysis proposed aerobic, resistance, and concurrent training has more excellent executive function benefits in older women than men [41], possibly related to brain-derived neurotrophic factor regulation [42] or sex differences in domains like working memory [43]. In our study, greater cognitive enhancements were observed in female individuals (i.e., 2.06 points) versus male participants (i.e., 1.46 points) after short-term in-hospital exercise. The short hospitalization program may have made cognitive sex differences less likely than longer interventions.

We found no significant sex differences in health-related quality of life in response to exercise. However, female patients showed non-significant (p = 0.06) quality of life improvements after exercise versus controls. Although previous studies have investigated the effectiveness of in-hospital exercise trials [44,45], to our knowledge, this is the first to examine sex differences. Finally, acute medical condition and the number of comorbidities were similar between both study groups and sex, and these aspects could influence the lack of significant sex differences after the intervention.

Strengths and limitations

This secondary analysis has limitations. The primary analysis was previously reported [16,17]. Generalizability is restricted as we only included those with relatively good pre-hospital function, excluding those unable to walk independently at admission, which were excluded from the study. Sex distribution differed slightly between groups (intervention: 141 [50%] female, control: 157 [54%]) and biological sex factor was not considered in the randomization process. Blinding exercise prescribers, patients, and relatives was not possible, given the intervention. Moreover, the total number of exercise sessions (i.e., morning and evening sessions) and total exercise volume were not registered for each sex category. Finally, older patients with severe cognitive decline were excluded for the study.

Strengths include the multicenter randomized controlled trial design analyzing sex differences in an individualized, in-hospital exercise program for acutely hospitalized older adults, the first study in ACE units. It provides evidence of the importance of tailored exercise prescriptions for geriatric medicine, accounting for demographic and clinical factors like sex [11]. The RCT included older patients (mean age 87.4 years, 33.9% nonagenarians) with comorbidities and mild/moderate dementia often excluded from trials.

Conclusions

This analysis suggests that in-hospital multicomponent exercise, emphasizing resistance/power training, provides significant benefits for physical function compared to usual care in acute hospitalizations, highlighting sex differences favoring females. No sex variations occurred in strength, cognition or health-related quality of life after exercise among ACE unit admissions.
