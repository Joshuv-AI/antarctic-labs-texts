Comparative Analysis of Choroid Plexus Volume Between MOG Antibody Associated Disease and Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Jae‐Won Hyun, Shaun G Hong, Na Young Park, Su‐Hyun Kim, Ho Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChoroid plexus volume (CPV) has been proposed as a neuro‐immunological marker of multiple sclerosis (MS), but its relevance in myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease (MOGAD) remains uncertain. We analyzed CPV in 43 individuals with MOGAD, 48 with MS, and 44 healthy controls using a Bayesian Gaussian mixture modeling applied to 3D‐T1‐weighted‐MRI. Total intracranial volume‐normalized CPV did not differ between MOGAD and healthy controls, whereas the MS demonstrated significantly higher values than both groups (p < 0.001) and showed associations with clinical disability. These findings suggest relative preservation of CPV in MOGAD and support a closer association of CPV enlargement with disease‐related structural changes in MS than in MOGAD.Keywords: choroid plexus, MOG, MRI, multiple sclerosis

Introduction

The choroid plexus (CP) is a specialized structure within the ventricular system that contributes to cerebrospinal fluid production and central nervous system (CNS) homeostasis [1]. Beyond these functions, accumulating evidence indicates that the CP participates in neuroimmune interactions, suggesting a possible role in inflammatory demyelinating diseases of the CNS (CNS IDDs) [1, 2, 3].

In multiple sclerosis (MS), pathological studies have demonstrated inflammatory changes and enlargement of the CP, implicating it in disease‐related immune activity [1, 2, 3]. In line with these findings, recent MRI and molecular imaging studies have shown increased CP volume (CPV) and metabolic activity in individuals with MS, including those at early or preclinical stages, supporting CPV as a marker of MS‐associated inflammation [2, 3].

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease (MOGAD) is now recognized as a distinct inflammatory demyelinating disease with pathogenic mechanisms that differ from those of MS [4]. However, despite increasing understanding of MOGAD immune‐pathophysiology, the involvement of the CP has not been systematically examined. Accordingly, this study aimed to compare CPV in MS and MOGAD and to explore potential differences between the two diseases.

Methods

In this retrospective study, the National Cancer Center (NCC) registry was reviewed to identify individuals with MOGAD or MS who had comprehensive clinical data and a 3D brain MRI acquired during the inter‐attack period (≥ 3 months after the most recent clinical event). Forty‐three people with MOGAD and 48 people with MS were identified, with the two groups comparable in age, sex, disease duration, disability status, and presence of maintenance immunotherapy. In addition, 44 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls (HCs) were included. MOGAD was diagnosed according to the 2023 diagnostic criteria, and MS according to the 2017 McDonald criteria [4, 5].

Brain imaging was performed using a 3.0 T MRI (Philips Achieva MR, Netherlands) at NCC. T1 images were acquired using the following sequences: three‐dimensional high‐resolution T1‐weighted images (TR, shortest; TE, in‐phase; flip angle, 8°; field of view, 230 mm; matrix, 230 × 230; slice thickness, 0.5 mm; no gap; voxel size, 1 × 1 × 0.5 mm). Images were processed with FreeSurfer (v7.4.1) for anatomical segmentation. CPV was quantified using a fully automated pipeline combining Bayesian Gaussian mixture modeling with anatomy‐constrained refinement, adapted from prior probabilistic CP segmentation frameworks (Figure S1) [6, 7]. FreeSurfer parcellations served as anatomical priors and quality control anchors. Preliminary CP candidates were delineated within the ventricular compartment and subsequently refined through prespecified exclusion masks encompassing the septum pellucidum, midline slab, anterior horn, and periventricular white matter hypointensities, complemented by connected‐component filtering. Both the third and fourth ventricles were incorporated into the ventricular mask. Case‐specific parameter adjustments were applied where needed; resultant outputs underwent standardized visual quality adjudication. Adaptive parameters were required in 13 of 135 participants (HCs, 0/44; MOGAD, 2/43; MS, 11/48), and we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding all participants requiring adaptive parameters; the results remained unchanged. CPV was normalized to total intracranial volume (TIV). Whole brain, cortical gray matter, thalamic, and ventricular volumes were analyzed using FreeSurfer. The automated processing and quality control procedures were performed without reference to diagnostic group. Details of analytic methods are presented in Data S1: Method.

Group differences were assessed using ANOVA and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for age, sex, disease duration, expanded disability status scale (EDSS), and presence of maintenance immunotherapy at the time of MRI, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. To further assess the potential influence of brain lesion status in MOGAD, we performed additional sub‐analyses according to the presence of brain T2 lesions. Participants with MOGAD were stratified into those with and without T2 lesions. CPV/TIV was compared between the MOGAD group with T2 lesions and the MS and HC groups, and also between MOGAD participants with and without T2 lesions.

Spearman correlation analyses were performed separately within the MS and MOGAD groups to assess the associations between TIV‐normalized CPV (CPV/TIV) and clinical as well as radiological variables. Partial correlation analyses were subsequently performed. For clinical analyses, age, sex, EDSS, disease duration, and maintenance immunotherapy status were evaluated individually, with each analysis adjusted for the remaining covariates. For radiological analyses, whole brain, cortical gray matter, thalamic, and ventricular volumes, age, and sex were examined individually, with each analysis adjusted for the remaining covariates.

This study was approved by the IRB of NCC, and informed consent was exempted due to the use of de‐identified data.

Results

The clinical data of the participants are summarized in Table S1. There were no significant differences in age or sex among the three groups. Between the MOGAD and MS groups, disease duration, disability status, and use of maintenance immunotherapy at the time of MRI did not differ significantly.

CPV did not differ significantly between the MOGAD group (1.35 ± 0.34 mL) and HC (1.23 ± 0.31 mL). In contrast, CPV was significantly higher in the MS group (1.77 ± 0.43 mL) than in both the MOGAD group and HCs (p < 0.001 in both, Table 1 and Figure 1). This group difference remained significant after adjustment for clinical covariates. (partial η 2 = 0.19, p < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for the TIV‐normalized ratio (CPV/TIV multiplied by 1000). The CPV/TIV remained comparable between MOGAD (0.84 ± 0.20) and HCs (0.75 ± 0.17), whereas it was significantly elevated in MS (1.14 ± 0.25) relative to both groups (both p < 0.001). The group effect remained significant after adjustment for clinical covariates (partial η 2 = 0.23), with higher CPV/TIV in MS than in MOGAD (adjusted mean difference 0.264, 95% CI 0.159–0.369, p < 0.001) and HC (adjusted mean difference 0.266, 95% CI 0.076–0.457, p = 0.003).

In subgroup analyses, CPV/TIV in MOGAD participants with brain T2 lesions (n = 23; 0.87 ± 0.18) remained significantly lower than that of MS (p < 0.001) but did not differ from HC, before or after adjustment for clinical covariates (partial η 2 = 0.15, p < 0.001). CPV/TIV did not differ significantly between patients with MOGAD with (0.87 ± 0.18) and without T2 lesions (0.81 ± 0.22).

In the MS group, CPV/TIV was independently associated with EDSS after adjustment for age, sex, disease duration, and maintenance immunotherapy status (partial r = 0.46, p = 0.002). CPV/TIV was associated with TIV‐normalized thalamic volume (ρ = −0.386, p = 0.007) and TIV‐normalized ventricular volume (ρ = 0.572, p < 0.001). However, neither association remained significant after adjustment for the other variable. In the MOGAD group, no clinical or MRI variables except TIV‐normalized ventricular volume (ρ = 0.793, p < 0.001) were associated with CPV/TIV. Three participants with MOGAD had CPV/TIV values exceeding the mean value observed in the MS group (1.14); however, their EDSS scores at the time of MRI were low (0.0–2.0).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that TIV‐normalized CPV in MOGAD did not differ from HCs but was significantly lower than in MS. Additionally, CPV/TIV was significantly associated with EDSS in MS but not in MOGAD.

In MS, pathological and imaging studies support the CP as an immunologically active interface, and its enlargement has been reported in association with clinical disability progression [1, 2, 3, 8]. Consistent with these observations, CPV/TIV in our MS cohort was independently associated with clinical disability. CPV/TIV was also associated with ventricular volume in both MS and MOGAD, in line with previous observations that CP enlargement may partly reflect ex vacuo ventricular expansion [8, 9, 10]. However, an association between CPV/TIV and thalamic volume, a structure whose atrophy has been regarded as a marker of neurodegenerative processes, was observed only in MS [11]. Although this association was not independent of ventricular volume, it may support that the biological significance of CP enlargement differs between MS and MOGAD. In contrast, CPV/TIV remained comparable to HCs in MOGAD, including the subgroup with brain T2 lesions, and showed no significant association with clinical disability. Although a few individuals with MOGAD had CPV/TIV values exceeding the mean observed in MS, their low disability scores suggest that elevated CPV alone may not correspond to disability in MOGAD. Clinically, MS differs from MOGAD in that disability accrual may occur not only through relapse‐associated worsening but also via progression independent of relapse activity, whereas MOGAD primarily exhibits relapse‐driven disability accumulation [12]. In this context, the selective presence of CPV enlargement and its associations with disability and thalamic volume in MS, but not in MOGAD, may suggest that CPV is more closely associated with disease‐related processes in MS, with ventricular enlargement potentially representing a structural factor in this association.

Comprehensive MRI metrics, including 3D‐T2 lesion volume, were not consistently available for all individuals and therefore could not be incorporated as analytical variables. Nevertheless, the use of unified imaging and clinical protocols may have reduced differential measurement bias, and the incorporation of subject‐specific FreeSurfer parcellations with predefined multi‐step exclusion masking supports methodological reproducibility. In addition, the inclusion of CP structures from the third and fourth ventricles allowed a more comprehensive estimation. As MRI was obtained during the inter‐attack period and generally after disease onset, the relevance of these findings to the earliest stages of disease remains uncertain. Given the modest sample size and cross‐sectional design, future larger prospective longitudinal investigations are warranted to further clarify the potential role of CPV in CNS IDDs.
