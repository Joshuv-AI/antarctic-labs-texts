Multimodal fusion for equipment health status assessment based on dynamic attention mechanism
Authors: Yao Lei, Jianyin Zhao, Weimin Lv, Song Gao, Jinhe Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurately capturing the evolving temporal correlations between unstructured textual features and multi-modal parameter data is pivotal for robust equipment health assessment. Conventional multimodal fusion methods typically fail to capture temporal variations across modalities: text features exhibit stage-specific changes, such as slight abnormal noise in the early fault stage and severe vibration in the late degradation stage, while parameter data contains latent temporal patterns like wavelet energy accumulation in specific frequency bands during the fault precursor period. The attention mechanism is a highly promising architecture to address this issue. This study proposes a dynamic attention-driven multimodal feature fusion method for equipment health status assessment. This method integrates a hybrid time-frequency encoding framework, combining wavelet packet decomposition (WPD), fast Fourier transform (FFT), and discrete Fourier transform (DFT) with textual feature extraction based on bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT). On the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) bearing fault dataset, the proposed method improves classification accuracy by 7.2% compared with conventional symmetric attention models and achieves an AUC-ROC of 0.951. The proposed method captures evolving multimodal signals, allowing for more accurate and interpretable health assessment, thereby providing valuable technical support for real-time monitoring and preventive maintenance of equipment.

Introduction

Industrial systems increasingly generate high-dimensional, heterogeneous monitoring data. In these environments, capturing the evolving temporal correlations between unstructured textual logs and multi-modal parameter data is critical. Conventional single-modal methods often struggle with these complex fault patterns, particularly when addressing concept drift and the non-linear coupling of features. Recent progress in multimodal data fusion has demonstrated that integrating heterogeneous information such as vibration signals, acoustic data, and textual logs provide a multi-dimensional representation of equipment status, which is essential for enhancing diagnostic accuracy and fault mechanism interpretation.

Effectively modeling the temporal dynamic correlation between textual features and parameter data remains a core challenge in equipment health status assessment. In practical industrial scenarios, modal features exhibit stage-specific evolutionary characteristics: equipment logs transition from slight abnormal noise in early stages to severe vibration during late-stage degradation, while parameter data contains latent temporal patterns, such as wavelet energy accumulation in specific frequency bands during fault precursor periods. However, conventional multimodal fusion methods typically rely on static weight allocations, failing to capture these time-varying focus points, which limits early fault warning capabilities and compromises the assessment of fault severity.

Despite the potential of attention mechanisms to improve feature fusion, four critical limitations hinder their practical industrial application: Current frameworks face four main limitations: limited adaptability to time-varying states, weak semantic alignment between text and signals, isolated use of time-frequency tools, and the persistent trade-off between interpretability and real-time performance.

To address these challenges, this study proposes a multimodal fusion method based on a dynamic attention mechanism. We frame the synthetic textual modality as a semantic anchor that contextualizes parameter data, bridging the gap between symbolic information and signal features. This hierarchical alignment ensures the model remains robust against noise-induced perturbations, satisfying the core criteria of inter-modal information complementarity in multimodal learning. The core innovations of this work are threefold. First, we propose a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) -powered dynamic attention mechanism that adaptively re-weights modal importance based on historical states, effectively resolving the limitations of static weighting in capturing instantaneous anomalies. To support this mechanism, we introduce a non-redundant hierarchical encoding framework: this synergizes BERT-based semantic extraction which serves as a ‘semantic anchor’ for cross-modal alignment with a hybrid time-frequency signal processing pipeline (WPD-FFT-DFT) designed to sense incipient faults. Finally, the architecture is optimized for industrial deployment, utilizing DFT for dimensionality reduction to achieve a processing capacity of 121 samples per second, thereby breaking the traditional trade-off between model interpretability and real-time response.

Research background and history

The widespread adoption of IoT devices and the continuous advancement of intelligent environments have driven industrial systems to become increasingly complex, leading to a substantial growth in both the volume and complexity of generated data. This paradigm shift is extensively documented in literature1. Conventional detection methods, such as statistical techniques2, clustering algorithms3, and classification models4, have been widely applied for data analysis. However, in dynamic data flow scenarios where distribution and evolution patterns continuously shift, the drawbacks of these conventional anomaly detection methods have become increasingly prominent2. For example, traditional hierarchical clustering methods often struggle to determine the optimal number of clusters for high-dimensional data and are highly dependent on core parameter selection5. Furthermore, technical challenges such as concept drift6, high-dimensional data7, computational efficiency8, and feature engineering9 significantly limit their applicability in dynamic industrial scenarios.

While advanced deep learning methods demonstrate enhanced adaptability and scalability, they face significant hurdles in handling heterogeneous data types10 and ensuring model interpretability11. Beyond temporal modeling, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a significant trend for processing complex and heterogeneous equipment data12. By modeling system components as nodes and their functional logical associations as edges, multi-receptive field Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) effectively capture machine fault patterns13. Such multimodal graph fusion networks have been successfully applied to the health management of industrial robots14. Furthermore, deep spatial-temporal graph attention networks have compensated for the inability of traditional flat features to represent complex interactions15. This spatial correlation modeling exhibits strong complementarity with temporal dynamic characteristics, although the dynamic alignment of these spatial features with unstructured textual semantics remains a critical research gap.

The integration of multimodal data including numerical, categorical, textual, and time-series data7,16 represents a significant trend in fields ranging from industrial monitoring to healthcare. Cross-modal learning aims to establish associations and mapping relationships among these diverse data types17, enhancing the computer’s ability to understand complex environments. This approach supports a wide range of applications, including image retrieval18, medical impact analysis19, and multimodal sentiment analysis20. Fusion methods are primarily divided into joint, collaborative, encoder, and split fusion21,22. However, effectively integrating these heterogeneous modalities remains a key challenge, as conventional methods often fail to fully explore potential connections. While the emergence of attention mechanisms23,24 and gradient-based visualizations25–27 has provided new ideas for improving decision-making transparency, their application in equipment health assessment continues to be relatively limited.

The attention mechanism, initially proposed in the context of natural language processing28, emulates the way the human brain allocates resources to focus on key information. With the rapid development of deep learning, recurrent neural networks (RNNs)29 and their variants, such as LSTM30 and gated recurrent units (GRU)31, have been extensively applied in sequence processing. Attention mechanisms have further diversified to include self-attention32, cross-attention33, simple attention34, and dynamic attention35. Dynamic attention, in particular, enables models to adjust weights based on input, which is essential in industrial scenarios where anomaly types and frequencies vary over time. This adaptability is particularly advantageous for monitoring the operating status of equipment throughout its lifecycle.

Currently, specialized attention mechanisms play a central role in multimodal fusion. The Adaptive Cross Modal Attention (ACMA) mechanism36 automatically adjusts weights based on different modal contents, while the Transformational Attention Network (MCTA)37 utilizes Transformer architectures to compensate for missing modal information. To address transparency, the Interpretable Multimodal Fusion Attention Network (IMFAN)38 provides clear visual and textual explanations of modal contributions. Furthermore, applying attention at different network levels captures both global and local features in tasks like image recognition39 and natural language processing40. In multi-scale feature fusion41, attention mechanisms effectively combine features across different scales to improve recognition and detection accuracy.

Despite these advancements, existing equipment health assessment frameworks still face four critical limitations: (1) a lack of dynamic adaptability to time-varying states; (2) weak cross-modal semantic alignment between text and parameters; (3) non-synergistic extraction of time-frequency features; and (4) the trade-offs between interpretability, accuracy, and real-time performance. Beyond these gaps, emerging methods for class-imbalance, such as conditional GAN (CGAN)42, and physics-guided soft sensing frameworks43, have become research focuses. However, these approaches are often equipment-specific and lack the flexibility for broad cross-modal integration, gaps that the proposed dynamic attention-based multimodal fusion framework aims to address.

Method design

Model structure

To address the challenges of cross-modal alignment and real-time responsiveness, we designed a four-stage collaborative framework as shown in Fig. 1. The pipeline begins with a Data Preprocessing Module that normalizes heterogeneous inputs, serving as the foundation for the subsequent Dynamic Attention Module. Here, an LSTM-based generator dynamically re-weights features based on historical states, feeding optimized vectors into the Feature Fusion and Classification Module for final health status assessment.

Fig. 1Dynamic attention-based multimodal fusion architecture flowchart.

Dynamic attention-based multimodal fusion architecture flowchart.

The architecture addresses two key challenges: cross-modal semantic alignment, where the preprocessing layer aligns text and parameter dimensions to resolve semantic discrepancies; and accuracy-real-time balancing, wherein the dynamic attention-DFT-MLP pipeline captures stage-specific features via LSTM-powered weights while optimizing processing delay through DFT-based dimensionality reduction.

Data Preprocessing Module is the foundation of the model, responsible for the systematic normalization of heterogeneous data. For textual data, the BERT-base model is employed to transform discrete logs into high-dimensional semantic vectors, preserving complex contextual features. For parameter data, the module applies standardization to eliminate dimensional scale differences, followed by WPD and FFT to extract rich time-frequency details and localize characteristic fault frequencies.

Dynamic Attention Module, as the core component of the framework, this module integrates a time-varying weight generator and hybrid attention computation. The weight generator, constructed using LSTM networks, leverages historical hidden states and cross-modal queries to predict real-time importance distributions. The hybrid computation mechanism innovatively fuses text-driven static weights, providing stable prior knowledge, with parameter-sensitive dynamic weights that adapt to the equipment’s instantaneous operating status.

In the Feature Fusion and Classification Module, DFT frequency domain converter performs a deep transformation of the fused features to capture global correlations and extract patterns at a macro level. Subsequently, a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) utilized for its strong nonlinear mapping capabilities, progressively abstracts these features to output the probability distribution of various health states, thereby achieving a precise evaluation of the equipment’s condition.

Dynamic attention module

As the core component of the proposed framework, the dynamic attention module illustrated in Fig. 2 is designed to adaptively optimize the allocation of attention across heterogeneous modalities, thereby enabling the accurate extraction and fusion of critical information during equipment operation. This module primarily comprises a time-varying weight generator and hybrid attention computation, which together address the limitations of static attention by tracking long-term temporal dependencies.

To adapt to heterogeneous input modalities, the time-varying weight generator is extended to accept multi-dimensional parameter feature vectors denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{X}}_{{\mathrm{hetero}}}}=\left[ {{{\mathrm{X}}_{{\mathrm{vib}}}},{{\mathrm{X}}_{{\mathrm{sound}}}},{{\mathrm{X}}_{{\mathrm{curr}}}}} \right]$$\end{document}, where each sub-vector corresponds to a heterogeneous signal type. This extension ensures that the model automatically emphasizes critical heterogeneous signals while suppressing redundant information across modalities. By integrating the time-varying weight generator with the hybrid attention mechanism, the module enhances the model’s overall analytical capability and understanding of the equipment’s health status.

Feature fusion and classification module

DFT transforms the fused features into frequency-domain representations, revealing global correlations and suppressing load-induced fluctuations that time-domain analysis often misses. For a time-domain signal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x\left( n \right)$$\end{document} of length N, the formula for its discrete Fourier transform is as follows:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X\left( k \right)=\sum\nolimits_{{n=0}}^{{N - 1}} {x\left( n \right){e^{ - j\frac{{2\pi }}{N}kn}}} ,k=0,1, \cdots ,N - 1$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X\left( k \right)$$\end{document} is a frequency domain signal, j is an imaginary unit, and k represents the frequency index. Through DFT conversion, the features processed by dynamic attention can be mapped from the time domain to the frequency domain, thereby extracting global correlation features in the frequency dimension.

As a classic feedforward neural network, MLP has strong nonlinear mapping capabilities and is used to achieve accurate classification of device health status. This module uses MLP to efficiently classify complex feature data processed via DFT. The internal multi-layer neural structure of MLP abstracts and extracts features from data layer by layer, eventually outputting the probability of the device being in different health states. The calculation process of MLP includes an input layer, multiple hidden layers, and an output layer. Assuming the input feature vector is x, the activation function of the hidden layer is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma \left( \cdot \right)$$\end{document}, and the weight matrix and bias vector of each layer are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W^{\left( l \right)}}$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b^{\left( l \right)}}$$\end{document} (where l represents the number of layers), then the forward propagation process of MLP can be represented as:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h^{\left( l \right)}}=\sigma \left( {{W^{\left( l \right)}}{h^{\left( {l - 1} \right)}}+{b^{\left( l \right)}}} \right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h^{\left( l \right)}}$$\end{document}is the hidden state of the layer and the initial input is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h^{\left( 0 \right)}}=x$$\end{document}.17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y={\mathrm{softmax}}\left( {{W^{\left( L \right)}}{h^{\left( {L - 1} \right)}}+{b^{\left( L \right)}}} \right)$$\end{document}

where L is the total number of hidden layers, and y is the output vector, representing the probability distribution of devices in various health states. In the hidden layer, Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function is typically employed to enhance the nonlinear expression ability of the model, and its formula is given as follows:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma \left( x \right)=\hbox{max} \left( {0,x} \right)$$\end{document}

The output layer uses the softmax function to convert the values into a probability distribution, expressed as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{s}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}\left( {{z_i}} \right)=\frac{{{e^{{z_j}}}}}{{\sum\nolimits_{{j=1}}^{K} {{e^{{z_j}}}} }}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${z_i}$$\end{document} is the input of the ith neuron in the output layer and K is the number of categories of health status.

Ultimately, the collaborative mechanism between the DFT frequency- domain converter and MLP classifier is categorized into frequency domain feature enhancement and nonlinear classification decision. Frequency domain feature enhancement is the process of transforming dynamic attention processed features into frequency domain representations via DFT, highlighting the frequency components of the features and their global correlations. Nonlinear classification decision-making is the process of abstracting frequency domain features through multiple layers of nonlinear transformations in MLP, and ultimately outputting the probability distribution of device health status. By combining this deep transformation with classification decision-making, this module can effectively capture the complex patterns and potential connections of device operation status, achieving accurate evaluation of device health status.

Experiment and result analysis

Dataset

To validate the generalization ability, robustness, and adaptability of the proposed multimodal equipment health status assessment model, three public datasets covering different equipment types and operating conditions are adopted for experimental verification: the CWRU bearing dataset, the Intelligent Maintenance Systems (IMS) bearing dataset, and the Xi’an Jiaotong University-Shenyang University of Technology (XJTU-SY) gearbox dataset. All datasets are split into training (70%), validation (20%), and test (10%) sets following a consistent ratio, with samples of all health states evenly distributed to avoid category bias. Synthetic textual data is generated for each dataset based on unified principles, mapping equipment physical states inferred from labeled fault types and severity to human-interpretable natural language descriptions, while ensuring temporal coherence by linking consecutive text entries to form semantic sequences rather than isolated labels.

The CWRU bearing dataset, developed by the CWRU Mechanical Engineering Laboratory, serves as the benchmark dataset for baseline verification. It contains vibration signals of rolling bearings under normal operation and 9 distinct fault conditions, with sampling frequencies of 12 kHz and 48 kHz, covering 0–3 HP load conditions. Fault types include inner ring (IR) damage, outer ring (OR) damage, and rolling element (BA) damage, with damage sizes of 0.007 inches, 0.014 inches, and 0.021 inches. The dataset is divided into 9800 training samples, 2800 validation samples, and 1400 test samples. Synthetic textual descriptions for this dataset include stage-specific fault characteristics.

The IMS bearing dataset, provided by the University of Cincinnati, is used to verify adaptability to variable operating conditions. It includes vibration signals of 4 bearing test rigs (Bearing 1–4) under variable speed and load conditions, with a sampling frequency of 20 kHz, covering three rotational speeds (1797 rpm, 1890 rpm, 1980 rpm) and two load levels (2 HP, 3 HP). Fault types consist of inner ring damage, outer ring damage, and combined inner-outer ring damage (IR + OR), with damage sizes ranging from 0.005 inches to 0.020 inches. The dataset is partitioned into 8400 training samples, 2400 validation samples, and 1200 test samples. Synthetic textual data for the IMS dataset reflects variable condition characteristics.

The XJTU-SY gearbox dataset, jointly developed by Xi’an Jiaotong University and Shenyang University of Technology, is employed to validate adaptability to different equipment types. Focusing on gearbox fault diagnosis, it covers 5 health states: normal operation, gear tooth wear, gear tooth pitting, bearing inner ring fault, and combined gear-bearing fault. The test rig comprises a 3-phase asynchronous motor, a two-stage parallel gearbox, and a magnetic powder brake, with a sampling frequency of 12.8 kHz and rotational speeds of 1200 rpm, 1500 rpm, and 1800 rpm, and load conditions varying from 0 N·m to 15 N·m. The dataset is split into 9100 training samples, 2600 validation samples, and 1300 test samples. Synthetic textual descriptions for the XJTU-SY dataset are tailored to gearbox-specific fault features.

Notably, the division of all datasets is performed on the original continuous signals … prior to any signal segmentation operations. This procedure ensures that there is no signal overlap between training and testing subsets, despite the high window overlap rate used during sample generation. This design prevents potential data leakage caused by high overlap rates of sliding windows in subsequent sample generation. Additionally, the synthetic text in all datasets differs fundamentally from structured sensor data, aligning with the core definition of a language modality in multimodal learning.

Experimental setup and steps

Step 1: Data cleaning and normalization. We adopted the 3σ rule for outlier detection on the raw vibration signals of the Drive End (DE) and Fan End (FE), which is justified by the statistical characteristics of the CWRU bearing vibration signals: useful fault-related impulsive peaks typically fall within 3σ of the signal mean, are continuous in the time-frequency domain concentrated in 10–15 kHz bands, consistent with Sect. 4.5 feature importance analysis, and correlate with mechanical fault mechanisms, while spurious noise outliers often deviate beyond 3σ with no temporal coherence or correlation with fault characteristic frequencies; additionally, the CWRU dataset’s vibration signals (12 kHz/48 kHz sampling rate) exhibit a Gaussian-like distribution after preprocessing verified by Shapiro-Wilk test,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p>0.05$$\end{document}, satisfying the 3σ rule’s statistical premise. To confirm no diagnostically useful signals were removed, we conducted verification experiments: the removed outliers accounting for 0.8%–1.2% of total samples are temporally isolated and randomly distributed outside the 0–24 kHz fault characteristic frequency range, and when identical models were trained on 3σ-processed data and raw data without outlier removal, the 3σ-processed model achieved an incipient fault recognition rate of 91.3% for 0.007-inch damage, with both models maintaining 98.5% accuracy for late-stage 0.021-inch damage, confirming 3σ removal only eliminates noise. After outlier removal, we normalized the signals according to Eq. (2) mapping values to [0,1] to eliminate dimensional differences between DE and FE signals and avoid model training bias caused by inconsistent data scales.

Step 2: Sample generation and feature extraction of vibration signals. Since the dataset splitting (Sect. 4.1) has been completed on the original continuous signals, the following sample generation process is executed independently for the training, validation, and test sets to ensure no cross-subset signal overlap. Using a 400-point window (≈ 33.3 ms at 12 kHz sampling frequency, aligned with the rotational period of CWRU bearings: 33.4–34.7 ms for 1730–1797 rpm, ensuring one complete mechanical cycle per window) and a 100-point sliding step (≈ 8.33 ms, resulting in 75% window overlap to cover multi-cycle information via consecutive samples), generate independent samples. Use db4 wavelet basis to decompose into three layers and extract the energy proportion of each sub-band. Perform FFT on the 400-point signal with a frequency resolution of\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_{res}}=12000/400=30{\mathrm{Hz}}$$\end{document}, sufficient to distinguish CWRU bearing fault frequencies, calculating the energy distribution in the 0–24 kHz frequency domain, and extract the top 50 frequency components as frequency domain features. Calculate 32 dimensional features, including time-domain mean, standard deviation, kurtosis, and frequency-domain peak frequency to create a vibration feature vector\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X_p}$$\end{document}. Text data is matched with the corresponding vibration signal window based on the timestamp of the log entry. If the text does not have an accurate timestamp, a sliding window is used to match the nearest neighbor entry. Use Hugging Face’s BERT-base truncated model to segment and fill text, generating 768-dimensional embedding vector\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X_t}$$\end{document}. Then, the 768-dimensional BERT embeddings are normalized and projected onto a 32-dimensional semantic space through a linear layer, aligning them with the vibration feature dimension.

Step 3: Multimodal data fusion first initializes the static weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha _{static}} \in {{\mathbb{R}}^{1 \times 768}}$$\end{document} through the last layer of pre-trained BERT model weights, representing the prior importance of text features. Subsequently, based on LSTM, a time-varying weight generator takes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X_t}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X_p}$$\end{document} as inputs, and outputs a dynamic weight matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W_t} \in {{\mathbb{R}}^{1 \times 32}}$$\end{document}that represents the real-time importance of vibration features. The LSTM parameters are set to\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{dropout}}=0.2$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{batch\_size}}=64$$\end{document}, and trained for 50 epochs. Perform mixed calculation according to Eq. (13), where the text embedding is projected to 32 dimensions through a linear layer, and the vibration features are concatenated with the text features into a 64-dimensional vector.

Step 4: DFT frequency domain conversion and feature enhancement take the mixed features output by the dynamic attention module as input, utilize the first 50 frequency components covering 0–24 kHz, and compared with the characteristic frequency range of bearing fault features in the CWRU dataset, as described in Eq. (14). Subsequently, extract characteristic frequencies related to bearing faults from CWRU dataset, suppress time-domain signal fluctuations resulting from load changes, eliminate the influence of speed differences under different loads (0–3 HP), and map the signals uniformly to frequency domain space.

Step 5: MLP classifier design and training takes 50 dimensional DFT features corresponding to the 0–24 kHz frequency domain as input. The first layer of the hidden layer captures the general frequency domain patterns of bearing faults, while the second layer focuses on the specific fault types in the CWRU data. The output layer is a 10 neuron Softmax classifier, corresponding to the 10 health states of CWRU.

Ablation experiment and results

We conducted ablation experiments on the CWRU dataset to quantify the contributions of specific components, including the weight mechanisms and time-frequency feature extraction modules.

Ablation experiments were designed to validate the contributions of key components (dynamic-static weight fusion, WPD, FFT, DFT) across all three datasets, ensuring the method’s design rationality and component combined effect are not limited to the benchmark CWRU dataset. Two sets of ablation groups were tested: one focusing on weight mechanisms (static weight, dynamic weight only, proposed dynamic-static fusion) and the other on time-frequency techniques (Ablate WPD, Ablate FFT, Ablate DFT, Ablate WPD + FFT).

Weight mechanism validation

As shown in Table 5 for the CWRU dataset and consistent trends across IMS and XJTU-SY datasets, the proposed dynamic-static weight fusion model achieves the highest performance. On CWRU, it reaches 94.7% accuracy and 0.951 AUC-ROC, outperforming the static weight model with 87.5% accuracy and 0.910 AUC-ROC and the dynamic weight only model with 89.7% accuracy and 0.924 AUC-ROC. This confirms that integrating text-driven static prior knowledge with parameter-sensitive dynamic weights mitigates the rigidity of fixed weights and the noise sensitivity of dynamic weights alone that persists on IMS where the proposed model achieves 91.8% accuracy compared to 83.2% for static attention and on XJTU-SY with 90.3% accuracy versus 81.5% for static attention.

To intuitively demonstrate dynamic weight adaptation, Fig. 5 compares static and dynamic attention distributions on the CWRU dataset, highlighting stage-specific focus on 10–15 kHz components. This adaptive allocation is consistent across IMS and XJTU-SY, where dynamic weights emphasize gear meshing frequency components for XJTU-SY and speed-sensitive frequency bands for IMS, further validating the mechanism’s generalization.

Time-frequency technique validation

Table 6 for the CWRU dataset, Table 7 for the IMS dataset, and Table 8 for the XJTU-SY dataset collectively confirm the non-redundant combined effect of WPD-FFT-DFT. WPD is critical for early fault detection. Removing WPD reduces incipient fault recognition rate by 24.1% on CWRU from 91.3% to 67.2%, 25.4% on IMS from 88.6% to 63.2%, and 25.9% on XJTU-SY from 86.4% to 60.5%, as its three-layer db4 decomposition captures subtle high-frequency energy accumulations unavailable from time-domain statistics. FFT enables fault type localization. Omitting FFT decreases accuracy by 5.6% on CWRU, 6.5% on IMS, and 6.8% on XJTU-SY, as it maps signals to mechanical fault characteristic frequencies such as bearing inner ring frequency and gear meshing frequency. DFT enhances anti-interference capability. Removing DFT lowers AUC-ROC by 2.3% on CWRU, 2.6% on IMS, and 2.6% on XJTU-SY, as it transforms 64D fused features into 50D frequency-domain representations, suppressing load and noise interference.

The Ablate WPD + FFT group achieved the poorest performance, with an accuracy of 82.4% on CWRU, 79.2% on IMS and 77.4% on XJTU-SY, which underscores that WPD and FFT form the foundation of high-quality parameter feature extraction, while DFT integrates cross-modal context. Their complementary nature is indispensable across equipment types and operating conditions.

Dynamic weight visualization analysis

From the perspective of weight mechanisms, the static weight mode with α fixed at 0.5 exhibited limited adaptability to the dynamic operating states of equipment. Its uniform weight allocation failed to prioritize critical features such as high-frequency abnormal signals in parameters or fault-related semantics in text, leading to suboptimal performance in both early fault detection and late-stage fault severity assessment. The dynamic weight only model, while capable of adjusting attention based on real-time parameter variations via the LSTM-based time-varying weight generator detailed in Sect. 3.3, lacked stable semantic prior guidance from text. This resulted in occasional over-sensitivity to noise, such as misinterpreting transient load-induced fluctuations as fault signals. In contrast, the proposed model’s fusion of static and dynamic weights leverages the macro contextual support of text to provide a stable fault type discrimination basis, while dynamic weights enable real-time adaptation to parameter changes, effectively enhancing the model’s robustness and accuracy as further evidenced by the adaptive weight distribution in Fig. 5.

Regarding the time-frequency analysis techniques, the ablation results explicitly validate their hierarchical synergy and non-redundancy. WPD is critical for incipient fault detection, as it enables precise extraction of subtle energy accumulations in high-frequency sub-bands through three-layer db4 wavelet decomposition detailed in Sect. 3.2. This capability is essential for identifying 0.007-inch inner ring and outer ring faults. Its replacement by time-domain statistical features causes a 24.1% drop in Incipient Fault Recognition Rate on CWRU. Meanwhile, FFT plays a pivotal role in parameter preprocessing by efficiently localizing deterministic fault characteristic frequencies linked to mechanical mechanisms; its omission increases outer ring and rolling element misclassification by 12.3% and reduces overall Accuracy by 5.6% on CWRU. Additionally, DFT acts as a global correlation bridge in feature fusion as detailed in Sect. 3.4, transforming 64-dimensional fused features into 50-dimensional frequency-domain representations to integrate text semantics with local time-frequency features, suppressing load interference from 0 to 3 HP and noise. Without DFT, AUC-ROC decreases by 2.3% on CWRU, weakening cross-condition consistency.

Notably, the poor performance of the Ablate WPD + FFT group underscores that WPD and FFT are foundational for high-quality parameter feature extraction. Raw time-domain signals lack both the time-frequency detail provided by WPD and the characteristic frequency anchors provided by FFT, leaving DFT with low-quality inputs that cannot support effective multimodal fusion. These findings collectively demonstrate that WPD, FFT, and DFT address distinct technical bottlenecks in equipment health assessment, corresponding to early fault detection, fault type classification, and interference resistance. Their complementarity proves indispensable to the proposed model’s performance.

In practical equipment health monitoring scenarios, the combination of these three time-frequency techniques and the dynamic-static weight mechanism ensures the model not only detects subtle faults in a timely manner but also classifies them accurately and robustly. This provides reliable technical support for equipment preventive maintenance, effectively reducing losses caused by unexpected failures.

Real-time and accuracy analysis

To verify the stability of the model in extreme scenarios, real-time Gaussian white noise was added to the CWRU dataset, Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)=20dB, test delay and accuracy changes, and the test results are shown in the Fig. 7. Figure 7 verifies the balanced design of accuracy, interpretability, and real-time performance via the accuracy-latency trade-off under Gaussian white noise. Static attention and cross attention are distributed along the gray dashed line, showing a conventional trade-off between lower latency and higher accuracy. Due to noise causing the model to iterate repeatedly, the static attention accuracy of the weights decreased to 82.1%, and the delay increased to 14.3 s. However, the fast filtering of noise interference by dynamic weights maintained the accuracy of our method at 94.7%, and the delay remained stable at 8.2 s. The effectiveness of the time-varying weight adaptive method has been verified, indicating that dynamic attention can capture temporal dependencies through LSTM, suppress weight fluctuations caused by high-frequency noise, and achieve a balance between accuracy and real-time performance in noisy environments. The proposed method’s position above the ideal trade-off line confirms that dynamic attention suppresses noise interference, while DFT and LSTM optimization maintain low delay. This breaks the traditional accuracy-real-time trade-off, fully achieving the research goal of adapting to industrial noisy environments.

Conclusion and Prospect

This study presented a dynamic attention-driven multimodal fusion framework designed to resolve the conflict between interpretability and real-time performance in equipment health assessment. Extensive experiments on CWRU, IMS, and XJTU-SY datasets confirmed that our approach effectively captures evolving temporal correlations, achieving over 90% accuracy across diverse operating conditions. By synergizing BERT-based semantic anchors with a WPD-FFT-DFT signal processing pipeline, the model not only identifies incipient faults earlier than conventional methods but also ensures robust operation in noise-heavy industrial environments. The experimental results provide clear analytical conclusions, which are summarized as follows:

Experimental results primarily demonstrate that the proposed dynamic attention mechanism significantly outperforms static approaches by capturing long-term temporal dependencies via its LSTM-powered weight generator. This performance is underpinned by the non-redundant synergy of the WPD-FFT-DFT framework, where ablation studies confirm that omitting any component leads to a measurable drop in fault localization or interference capabilities. Furthermore, the integration of BERT-based semantic features acts as a stabilizing anchor, ensuring robustness even under low signal-to-noise ratios where conventional models typically falter. Critically, these gains in accuracy do not compromise efficiency; the model maintains a low latency of 8.2 s, satisfying the strict real-time requirements of industrial edge deployment.

The proposed framework exhibits strong generalization to heterogeneous multimodal fusion scenarios, as validated by experiments on vibration-sound-text and vibration-current-text combinations. The modular preprocessing pipeline standardizes heterogeneous signals while preserving modal-specific characteristics, and the LSTM-powered dynamic attention mechanism adaptively allocates weights to emphasize complementary fault information across modalities. Results show the method maintains ≥ 91% accuracy and ≥ 87% incipient fault recognition rate on heterogeneous combinations, outperforming conventional attention models by 5–8%. This confirms the method’s practical value in industrial settings where diverse sensors are deployed, addressing the core challenge of heterogeneous signal alignment and fusion.

In summary, the experimental results verify that the proposed multimodal fusion method effectively addresses the core challenges of temporal dynamic correlation capture, cross-modal alignment, and real-time robustness in equipment health assessment. Future research will focus on two directions: Exploring model compression techniques to further reduce computational complexity while maintaining performance, adapting to resource-constrained edge devices in industrial sites; Integrating dynamic attention with advanced temporal modeling and GNNs to build a spatio-temporal dynamic assessment framework, thereby supporting maintenance decisions for complex large-scale industrial systems.
