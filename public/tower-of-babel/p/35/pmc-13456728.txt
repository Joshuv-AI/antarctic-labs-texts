Successful resection of a giant pericardial myolipoma in a resource-limited setting: a case report
Authors: Mekonnen Feyissa, Desalegn Fikadu, Abenizer Berhanu, Shitaye Zewuede, Eyerusalem Fekade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Primary pericardial tumors are exceedingly rare. To date, there have been no reports of myolipoma occurring in the pericardial cavity. A dumbbell-shaped myolipoma with intrapericardial extension and a narrow pedicle traversing the pericardial sac is exceptionally rare. Surgical excision of such a large mass is particularly uncommon in resource-limited settings. Here, we report a case of the successful resection of a giant pericardial myolipoma in a 50-year-old female patient managed in a low-resource environment.Case summary:A 50-year-old female with a history of hypertension presented with progressive shortness of breath. Laboratory investigations were unremarkable, and cross-sectional imaging revealed a large fat-containing anterior mediastinal mass. The patient underwent surgery through a median sternotomy, which revealed two large lipomatous masses connected by a narrow pedicle traversing the pericardial sac, producing a dumbbell-shaped configuration. Both masses were successfully excised. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of myolipoma. The patient had an uneventful recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 14.Discussion:Primary pericardial myolipomas are rare, and most patients are asymptomatic. When symptomatic, compressive features predominate. Echocardiography serves as an initial screening tool, but contrast-enhanced cross-sectional imaging remains the cornerstone for diagnosis, surgical planning, and evaluation of tumor extent. Excision of large intrapericardial tumors requires a multidisciplinary approach to achieve optimal outcomes.Conclusion:This case highlights the rarity of giant pericardial myolipomas and demonstrates that successful surgical excision is feasible, even in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a myolipoma in the pericardial cavity. Pericardial masses are rare clinical findings and may represent a wide spectrum of etiologies, ranging from benign cysts to malignant neoplasms. Among these, large pericardial tumors with “dumbbell” morphology are particularly uncommon and may pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to their size, location, and potential for compression of adjacent mediastinal and cardiac structures. We present a case of a huge dumbbell-shaped pericardial mass, highlighting its imaging characteristics, differential diagnoses, and the clinical implications of such an unusual presentation. Similar rare cases have been described in the literature, underscoring the importance of cross-sectional imaging in diagnosis and management planning. This case report is presented in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[1].

Case presentation

We present a 50-year-old female who came with an exacerbation of shortness of breath of 6 months’ duration. For this complaint, she visited several local health centers and was treated for community-acquired pneumonia with oral antibiotics.

She is a known hypertensive patient, taking amlodipine 10 mg PO daily. Apart from this, she has no other chronic medical conditions or history of previous surgical procedures. She has no significant family history or history of tobacco use.

Upon presentation to our hospital, her vital signs were within the normal range. Pertinent physical examination findings were noted on cardiovascular examination: S1 and S2 heart sounds were audible but distant, with no murmurs or additional sounds. She also had bilateral grade I pitting edema, without distended neck veins or significant respiratory findings.

Laboratory investigations were unremarkable. A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) was performed, but it did not clearly visualize the mass, prompting a contrast CT scan. A contrast-enhanced chest CT revealed a large anterior mediastinal mass with no clear plane between the mass and the pericardium (Fig. 1). The content of the mass was predominantly fat, suggesting a lipomatous tumor. The smaller extra-pericardial mass was excised first after its pedicle was identified and divided. Subsequently, the larger intra-pericardial mass was approached. On opening the pericardium, approximately 150 ml of hemorrhagic fluid was evacuated. The tumor was then dissected free after its anatomy and relations to the underlying structures were clearly defined, allowing safe excision.

Hemostasis was secured, and tissue samples were sent for histopathological examination. Bilateral chest tubes were inserted. The sternum and soft tissues were approximated and closed in layers. The patient was extubated safely and transferred to the ICU for postoperative monitoring. Histopathological assessment of the tissue shows lobules composed of mature fat cells interdigitating with fascicles of bland smooth muscle cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and blunt-ended nuclei. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. The left chest tube was removed on postoperative day 3, and the right on day 5. She was discharged on postoperative day 14 with significant improvement. At 6-week, 3-month, and 6-month follow-ups, she remained asymptomatic. She was advised to follow up in the cardiothoracic clinic; at last contact, she was symptom free.

Discussion

Primary cardiac neoplasms are rare, with a prevalence of approximately 0.02%–0.056%[2]. Neoplastic involvement of the pericardium by direct invasion or metastatic disease, by contrast, is 100–1000 times more common[3]. The most common benign pericardial mass is a pericardial cyst, followed by lipoma. Primary malignant neoplasms of the pericardium include a variety of sarcomas as well as lymphomas[4].

Cardiac lipomas can occur anywhere within the heart. Of these, 53.1% are located within the cardiac chambers, 32.5% in the pericardium, 10.7% within the myocardium, and 3.7% involve multiple structures[5].

Intrapericardial lipomas are often asymptomatic, particularly in otherwise healthy individuals, and only occasionally cause symptoms such as dyspnea, fatigue, palpitations, arrhythmias, syncope, or precordial pain[6]. In contrast, when considering cardiac lipomas as a whole, more than half (around 66%) are clinically symptomatic, with manifestations ranging from chest discomfort to syncope depending on their size, location, and degree of myocardial involvement[5]. Thus, while intrapericardial lipomas are frequently silent, the majority of cardiac lipomas in general present with clinically significant symptoms.

The key to timely diagnosis of primary cardiac tumors lies not only in detecting their presence but also in accurately defining their location, extent, and relation to surrounding structures, as well as differentiating benign from malignant lesions. TTE is the procedure of choice for initial screening and evaluation of cardiac neoplasms, particularly for intracardiac and valve-attached masses; however, it has limitations in assessing pericardial lesions, the right heart, mediastinum, and para-cardiac structures[7]. In such cases, cardiac MRI provides superior visualization and tissue characterization, while electron beam CT offers additional morphological detail. Importantly, the use of contrast enhancement on MRI or CT can help distinguish thrombi, which typically do not enhance, from true neoplastic masses[8].

A definitive diagnosis is often not possible based on imaging alone, particularly when the tumor arises in unusual locations. In such cases, complete surgical excision, without violating the tumor capsule, may serve both a diagnostic and curative role[9].

Histologically, myolipoma of soft tissue consists of a variable admixture of mature adipocytes and well-differentiated smooth muscle cells, with a notable predilection for retroperitoneal and abdominal sites. The main histologic differentials include other spindle cell–fat cell tumors such as spindle cell lipoma, mammary-type myofibroblastoma, lipoleiomyosarcoma, and fat-forming solitary fibrous tumor[10].

Mehta et al reported a 51-year-old man with a dumbbell-shaped intrapericardial lipoma initially mistaken for a pericardial cyst, which was excised en bloc via midline sternotomy with partial cardiopulmonary bypass[11]. In contrast, our patient had a similarly configured intrapericardial mass extending through the pericardium, but the tumor was safely removed by dividing it at the narrow segment and excising it as two separate pieces through median sternotomy, without the need for bypass. This highlights how different surgical strategies can achieve complete resection depending on intraoperative findings and tumor configuration.

Conclusion

Intrapericardial masses are rare, and to date, myolipoma has not been reported in this location. The diagnosis and management of such cases can be particularly challenging in resource-limited settings, where advanced imaging and surgical facilities may not always be readily available. Despite these limitations, our patient underwent successful surgical excision of the mass, highlighting the importance of careful clinical evaluation, timely intervention, and multidisciplinary collaboration. This case underscores that even in resource-constrained environments, a successful outcome is achievable with proper planning – and it adds a previously unreported entity (pericardial myolipoma) to the surgical literature.
