Two-hour nicotine withdrawal does not alter interoceptive dysfunction in male smokers with nicotine dependence: evidence from heartbeat-evoked potentials
Authors: Jing Zhang, Lu Hou, Wenjie Shi, Gang Chen, Wenzhong Huang, Xiaohong Liu, Xuezheng Gao, Zhenhe Zhou, Hongliang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNicotine dependence (ND) is an addictive disorder involving physical and psychological dependence on nicotine. Nicotine withdrawal encompasses a spectrum of physiological and psychological changes, often manifesting as significant emotional dysregulation. Understanding whether smokers have interoceptive dysfunction and the effects of 2-hour nicotine withdrawal on this function may provide valuable insights to enhance smoking cessation programs. In this study, we used heartbeat-evoked potentials (HEPs) to investigate male smokers’ interoceptive function and the changes in this function after 2-hour nicotine withdrawal.MethodsParticipants included 32 ND patients and 32 healthy controls (HCs), and HEP measurements were performed during the heartbeat attention task. The HEP amplitudes were compared by using a mixed model Repeated Measure Analysis of Variance.ResultsHEP amplitudes in the ND group were lower than those in the HC group, especially evident in the central lobe and parietal lobe regions. However, there were no differences in electrocardiogram (ECG) amplitudes between the two groups. There were no differences in the HEP amplitudes between baseline (Time 1) and withdrawal state (Time 2) in all brain regions for the ND group, and 2-hour nicotine withdrawal did not alter the HEP amplitudes. HEP amplitudes in central lobe and parietal lobe regions were lower than those in frontal lobe region in the ND group, and parietal lobe region had the lowest HEP amplitudes. The somatic awareness scores and autonomic nervous system scores of Body Perception Questionnaire in the ND group were lower than those in the HC group.ConclusionsEvidence from the abnormal HEP characteristic suggests that ND patients have interoceptive dysfunction. However, we found no evidence that 2-hour nicotine withdrawal improved interoceptive dysfunction in ND patients. Targeting interoceptive function in smoking cessation programs offers a novel approach to addressing the physiological and psychological aspects of nicotine dependence, providing a comprehensive strategy for improving outcomes in individuals striving to quit smoking.Keywords: Nicotine dependence, Interoception, Nicotine withdrawal, Heartbeat-evoked potentials, Heartbeat attention task

Background

Nicotine dependence (ND) is defined as an addictive disorder in which an individual develops a physical and psychological dependence on nicotine [1]. The process of nicotine withdrawal, which frequently occurs concurrently with smoking cessation, gives rise to substantial neurochemical alterations within the brain, which exert a pivotal influence on mood [2, 3]. Nicotine withdrawal can cause complex changes in physiology and psychology, particularly a range of mood disorders such as anxiety and depression [4].

Interoception refers to the perception and processing of internal bodily signals, which includes sensations from the cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, and somatosensory systems [5]. Interoception plays a pivotal role in cognitive processes, given that it encompasses the perception of one’s own bodily sensations. These internal states, in turn, influence one’s emotional and cognitive experiences [6–9]. These regions are involved in the processing and regulation of internal signals, which may influence an individual’s cravings and responses to addictive substances. Previous studies have discussed the role of the insula in interoception, as well as interactions with internal signals and consciousness [7]. Thus, interoception plays an important role in regulating emotions, managing physiological states, guiding behavioral decisions, and maintaining mental health.

Heartbeat-evoked potentials (HEPs) are elicited by heartbeats, detectable at the scalp’s surface, reflecting the brain’s cortical processing of cardiac activity [10–12]. HEPs are neurophysiological markers critical for understanding the cognitive processing of cardiac signals, which are an essential component and a significant neural marker of interoception [13, 14]. When an individual perceives a heartbeat, it leads to changes in the amplitude and morphology of the HEP, indicating activation of interoception. HEPs serve as a measure of the neural processing of heartbeat signals, which are fundamental to interoceptive awareness [15]. Research suggests that variations in HEP amplitude correlate with individual differences in interoceptive accuracy; that is, individuals with more pronounced HEPs tend to have a heightened ability to perceive internal bodily sensations, such as heartbeats [16].

The primary regions implicated in the HEP encompass the insula and the anterior cingulate gyrus, which integrate interoceptive signals from the body to facilitate a comprehensive perception of the body’s state [13]. HEPs can reflect how individuals process interoceptive signals in different emotional states, e.g., emotional states such as anxiety and depression can alter the characteristics of HEP [7, 14]. Patients with anxiety disorders and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) have been observed to exhibit impairments in the processing of interoceptive signals, which has been linked to a reduction in the intensity of their HEP responses [17], [18]. In addition, HEP amplitudes are reduced in patients with schizophrenia, reflecting reduced processing of interoceptive signals [19].

Nicotine pharmacokinetics dictates a rapid central nervous system uptake (within seconds post-inhalation) and a distribution half-life of approximately two hours [20, 21]. A two-hour period of nicotine abstinence is not associated with the manifestation of significant withdrawal symptoms such as anxiety or depression [22–24]. This specific temporal window therefore offers a unique opportunity to isolate and examine the pure neurophysiological effects of declining nicotine concentration on interoceptive processing, independent of confounding mood disturbances that emerge later in withdrawal.

To address this research gap, the purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of nicotine withdrawal on interoception. We recruited male ND patients and employed a heartbeat attention task to induce heartbeat-evoked potentials, which were used as the neural marker of interoception [25]. HEP measurements were conducted at two key time points: immediately after smoking (baseline) and following a 2-hour period of nicotine withdrawal. Exploring the effects of pure nicotine withdrawal on interoception is of great significance, and clarifying this relationship can provide an empirical basis for the development of more comprehensive smoking cessation strategies. Given that interoceptive function is currently known to be modifiable through psychological interventions such as mindfulness training, the identification of reliable neuroelectrophysiological markers for interoceptive dysfunction in nicotine dependence may provide a reference for future clinical translation [26, 27]. We hypothesized that ND patients would exhibit interoceptive functional deficits, and that these deficits would persist following short-term withdrawal. Accordingly, we propose the following hypotheses: (1) ND patients show interoceptive functional deficits compared to HCs, as reflected by reduced HEP amplitudes and lower scores on body perception scales; (2) These deficits will not be significantly altered after a 2-hour nicotine withdrawal period.

Materials and methods

Time and setting

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the affiliated mental health center of Jiangnan university (WXMHCIRB2023LLky066). All participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted from May 1, 2023 to October 31, 2024 at the Department of Psychiatry, the affiliated Mental Health Centre of Jiangnan University.

Diagnostic approaches and participants

The diagnosis of nicotine dependence was confirmed by experienced psychiatrists in accordance with the diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria through structured clinical interviews. During the interviews, psychiatrists assessed all relevant criteria to ensure that each participant in the ND group met the diagnostic requirements. The inclusion criteria for the ND group were as follows: (a) the subject met the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for nicotine dependence; (b) the subject did not meet any diagnostic criteria for mental disorders, any DSM-5 criteria for axial disorders, or personality disorders; (c) the subject ranged in age from 18 to 65 years; (d) the subject smoked a minimum of 10 cigarettes per day in the past 12 months; (e) a clinical evaluation and medical records did not reveal neurological or mental illness, or alcohol or other drug addiction.

The inclusion criteria for the HC group were as follows: (a) absence of ND criteria as defined in the DSM-5; (b) age between 18 and 65 years; (c) absence of history of mental disorders or physical illness.

A priori power analysis was performed with G*Power software. For the repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) involving within-between interactions, with α set to 0.05 and power (1-β) set to 0.95, the analysis revealed that a minimum of 14 participants per group was necessary to identify a medium-to-large effect size. The study population comprised 32 ND patients who were exclusively cigarette smokers, without using e-cigarettes or any other tobacco products. The HC group consisted of 32 healthy individuals whose age, education, and handedness were aligned with those of the ND group. Both groups of participants were recruited through local advertisements in Wuxi City, Jiangsu Province, China, and received US $84.0 as compensation for their participation.

Experimental procedures and the heartbeat attention task

Before the study, demographic information was collected, and all participants were assessed using the following instruments: the 24-item Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-24) [28], the 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA-14) [29], the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) [30], and the Annett Handedness Inventory [31]. The Body Perception Questionnaire (BPQ) is an instrument used to assess body perception, including body awareness and reactivity [32]. High scores on the BPQ indicate exceptional interoceptive abilities and visceral nervous system perception. Responses are given on a 5-point scale for all subscales. This study focuses on somatic awareness, autonomic nervous system and stress response. Carbon monoxide (CO) levels in breath were detected using the QT-200PLUS portable carbon monoxide detector (Shenzhen Wellcome Technology Co., Ltd., China). The ND group was measured under two different conditions: baseline (Time 1), after finishing their last cigarette; and nicotine withdrawal (Time 2), two hours after finishing their last cigarette. For the non-smoking HC group, the same measurements conducted at time 2 were used to assess the test-retest reliability of HEP, thereby determining the natural fluctuation range over a 2-hour interval without any intervention. This design helps distinguish whether changes in the ND group exceed the natural fluctuations observed in the HC group, thus isolating the specific effects of nicotine withdrawal.

We used E-Prime 3.0 (Psychology Software Tools Incorporated, Pittsburgh, United States) to write the heartbeat attention task. Participants were measured at a distance of 50 centimeters (cm) from the computer screen. As shown in Fig. 1, the participants were permitted four trials of practice prior to the formal experiment, with the objective of ensuring that they had a comprehensive understanding of the rules. At the outset of the experiment, participants were asked to look at the center of the computer screen where the word ‘+’ appeared. First, a picture of the heart is shown in the center of the screen for 20 s, during which the participants were asked to focus on their own heartbeat and to feel it with their heart instead of measuring the pulse with their hands. Afterwards, a question will appear: “How much attention do you devote to your heart?” followed by a scale of 0–9, with 0 being “very poor” and 9 being “excellent.” The participants were required to make a selection within a nine-second timeframe via the use of a computer keyboard. In order to prevent participants from becoming fatigued and thereby maintaining optimal levels of attention, the trial incorporated a sound attention block. The participants heard white noise through external headphones and focused on the sound, which was also presented for 20 s. This was followed by the question, “How much attention do you devote to the sound?” The participants were required to select a rating from a computer keyboard within a nine-second timeframe. The two stimuli were presented in this manner for a total of 20 trials, with intertrial interval (ITI) varying randomly between 5 and 15 s. The duration of two-round tests for ND patients in normal and withdrawal states was a total of 30 min. The HC group remained consistent.

Electroencephalogram recording and analysis

The BioSemi Active Two system (BioSemi Inc., Amsterdam, Netherlands) was employed to record the EEG at a frequency of 500 Hz (Hz). An EEG was recorded in accordance with the International 10/20 system and utilising a bespoke BrainCap (EasyCap, Herrsching, Germany) comprising 64 Ag/AgCl ring electrodes. The electrodes utilized for the recording of vertical and horizontal electrooculograms (EOG) were positioned beneath the left eye and on the lateral canthi of both eyes. The electrodes used to record the ECG are placed between the fifth and sixth intercostal space, 1 to 2 cm to the left of the spine. The reference electrodes were positioned on the right and left mastoid bones, while a grounding electrode was placed beneath the left collarbone. The band-pass filtered EEG and EOG data ranged from 0.05 to 100 Hz, with electrode impedance remaining below 5 kiloohms (kΩ). After recording, data were processed offline using MATLAB 2020b (MathWorks, Inc.) with the EEGLAB toolbox. EEG was filtered offline using a band-pass-filtered with a cutoff frequency of 0.3–30 Hz. The heartbeat attention EEG data was segmented from 0 to 20 s, followed by the segmentation of the R peak event from − 100 to 650 milliseconds. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was used to remove eye movement artefacts, muscle electrical activity and cardiac field artifact (CFA) [33]. All amplitude segments below − 70 microvolt (µV) or above 70 µV were removed. Following artifact rejection, the average number of valid trials retained per condition were: ND group at Time 1: 184.09 ± 43.95 trials; ND group at Time 2: 165.69 ± 39.64 trials; HC group at Time 1: 173.53 ± 57.58 trials; HC group at Time 2: 165.41 ± 44.21 trials. There were no significant differences in the number of retained trials between groups (all p > 0.05).

In conjunction with previous studies, we chose brain regions of interest to study, namely the frontal lobe (F1, F2, Fz, F3, F4), central lobe (C1, C2, Cz, C3, C4) and parietal lobe (P1, P2, Pz, P3, P4) [34]. For each region of interest (ROI), HEP amplitudes were computed by averaging across all electrodes within that region. These brain regions are respectively involved in distinct aspects of interoceptive processing: the prefrontal cortex is responsible for the cognitive and emotional evaluation of bodily states; the central cortex is primarily involved in the integration of visceral signals; the parietal cortex is associated with the spatial representation of the body and the allocation of attentional resources toward internal sensations. These fronto-centro-parietal regions have been identified as reliable generators of the HEP, and alterations in HEP amplitudes within these regions are closely linked to interoceptive dysfunction across a broad range of clinical populations [13, 14, 35].

Previous studies have found that HEP and ECG signals partially overlap for up to 450 milliseconds (ms) after the R wave [36, 37]. Therefore, we chose to study the 455 to 595 ms time window after the R wave, in which ECG signal interference was minimal. [38, 39] In addition, to make the experiment more rigorous, we also compared ECG amplitudes in the time window from 455 ms to 595 ms after the R wave to avoid confounding cardiac field artifact [40].

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States) was used to analyze data. The mean age, education level, handiness, BPQ, HAMD and HAMA scores were compared using t-tests or Chi-squared tests. Comparison of CO levels in the ND group were conducted with a paired sample t-test. The mean amplitudes of the HEP were compared by using a mixed model Repeated Measure Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with a 2 group (ND group vs. HC group) × 2 time point (time 1 vs. time 2) × 3 region of interest (ROI: frontal lobe vs. central lobe vs. parietal lobe) design. To assess the group differences in ECG amplitudes, we conducted a 2 group (ND group vs. HC group) × 2 time point (time 1 vs. time 2) mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA). The degrees of freedom were corrected using the Greenhouse-Geisser method if the assumption of sphericity was violated. Effect sizes were further estimated using partial eta squared (η2). Post hoc analyses were performed when there was a significant interaction, and Bonferroni correction was employed to manage the possible type I errors caused by multiple comparisons. Alpha values of 0.05 were regarded as significant.

Comparisons of CO levels in the ND group

Using paired t-tests to compare CO levels at two time points, there was a significant difference between the withdrawal state (mean 9.07 parts per million (ppm); SD = 3.01) and the normal state (mean 12.89 ppm; SD = 3.50) in the ND group (t = 10.821, p < 0.001), with CO levels in the withdrawal state being lower than those in the normal state.

Heartbeat-evoked potentials

As shown in Fig. 2, using HEP mean amplitudes as dependent variables, a 2 group (ND group vs. HC group) × 2 time point (time 1 vs. time 2) × 3 ROI (frontal lobe vs. central lobe vs. parietal lobe) repeated measures ANOVA with group (ND group vs. HC group) as a between-subjects factor and time point (time 1 vs. time 2) and ROI (frontal lobe vs. central lobe vs. parietal lobe) as a within-subjects factor revealed the interaction effect for group × time point × ROI was not significant (F1.34, 83.31 = 0.931, p = 0.364, η2 = 0.015). There were also no significant interaction effects for group × time point (F1, 62 = 1.726, p = 0.194, η2 = 0.027) and time point × ROI (F1.34, 83.31 = 0.497, p = 0.536, η2 = 0.008). The interaction effects for group × ROI was significant (F1.31, 80.93 = 7.947, p = 0.003, η2 = 0.114, 95% CI [0.015, 0.251]). The main effects of group and ROI were significant (for group: F1, 62 = 9.736, p = 0.003, η2 = 0.136, 95% CI [0.018, 0.299]; for ROI: F1.31, 80.93 = 26.161, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.297, 95% CI [0.141, 0.440]). The main effect of time point was not observed (F1, 62 = 0.007, p = 0.934, η2 < 0.001). To interpret the significant group × ROI interaction, and given the absence of significant effects involving the time factor, post-hoc comparisons were performed on the data averaged across the two time points. These analyses confirmed that the ND group exhibited significantly lower HEP amplitudes than the HC group specifically in the central lobe (p = 0.001) and parietal lobe (p = 0.002), but not in the frontal lobe (p = 0.115). Within the ND group, HEP amplitudes in the central and parietal lobes were also significantly lower than those in the frontal lobe (all p < 0.05), a pattern not observed in the HC group.

The ECG mean amplitudes were compared by using a 2 group (ND group vs. HC group) × 2 time point (time 1 vs. time 2) repeated measures ANOVA. As shown in Fig. 3, the results revealed that neither the interaction nor the main effect between the two groups reached statistical significance (p > 0.05), indicating that the notable discrepancy in HEP mean amplitudes between the two groups was not attributable to cardiac field artefact.

Post-hoc characterization of the null time effect

To further characterize the null effect of time, follow-up paired comparisons (with Bonferroni correction) were conducted within each group and brain region. For the ND group, the mean change in HEP amplitude from Time 1 to Time 2 was not significant in any region: frontal lobe (mean difference = 0.028 µV, 95% CI [− 0.086, 0.141], p = 0.626), central lobe (mean difference = − 0.018 µV, 95% CI [− 0.105, 0.070], p = 0.689), and parietal lobe (mean difference = − 0.110 µV, 95% CI [− 0.273, 0.053], p = 0.182). A similar pattern of non-significant changes was observed in the HC group (all p > 0.05). All confidence intervals were centered on and included zero, providing precise estimates that support the conclusion that the 2-hour withdrawal period did not exert a meaningful effect on HEP amplitudes.

BPQ scores

As shown in Fig. 4, independent samples t-tests were conducted on the scores of somatic awareness, autonomic nervous system and stress response of the two groups. The results indicated that the ND group exhibited significantly lower scores than the HC group in somatic awareness and autonomic nervous system scores (p < 0.05). Conversely, no significant differences were observed in stress response scores. The findings suggest that ND patients demonstrate a reduction in the perception of internal bodily signals and a diminished autonomic nervous system reactivity in comparison to healthy individuals.

Analysis of correlations

Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to analyze the somatic awareness and autonomic nervous system scores in the ND group. As shown in Fig. 5, the analyses revealed a significant positive correlation, i.e., the lower the autonomic nervous system scores of the smokers, the lower the somatic awareness scores (r = 0.613, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the effects of 2-hour nicotine withdrawal on interoception in ND patients. The main findings can be summarized as follows: (1) Compared to HC group, ND patients exhibited significantly reduced HEP amplitudes, particularly over the central and parietal cortical regions; (2) A 2-hour period of nicotine withdrawal did not alter HEP amplitudes in the ND group; (3) On the Body Perception Questionnaire, ND patients reported lower scores in somatic awareness and autonomic nervous system reactivity compared to HC group; (4) Within the ND group, these two BPQ dimensions were positively correlated. In this study, we used a heartbeat attention task to evoke HEPs, which reflect interoceptive function. Our results showed that the HEP amplitudes of the ND patients were lower, especially evident in the central lobe and parietal lobe regions. According to the comparisons of the ECG amplitudes from 455 ms to 595 ms after the ECG R-wave between the two groups, we confirmed that the differences in HEP amplitudes were not due to cardiac field artifact. The lack of a significant Time main effect or Group × Time interaction indicates that the 2-hour nicotine withdrawal period did not alter HEP amplitudes in either group. Importantly, a post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study had adequate statistical power (approximately 78%) to detect a medium within-subject effect (Cohen’s d = 0.5) for the time factor. The observed effect size for the time factor was negligible (η² < 0.001), far below the threshold for a small effect. These considerations strengthen the interpretation that the lack of significant change is more likely due to the absence of a meaningful effect of short-term withdrawal, rather than to limitations in statistical power. In our sample, this interoceptive dysfunction may be relatively stable and was not measurably modulated by short-term fluctuations in nicotine concentration. Additionally, ND patients showed lower somatic awareness and autonomic nervous system scores on the BPQ.

HEPs are crucial for understanding the neural underpinnings of interoception, providing insights into how bodily signals are integrated into higher cognitive functions. Previous studies have indicated significant interoceptive dysfunction in addicted patients, and reduced HEPs in addicted patients may be due to interoceptive deficits [41, 42]. Our results demonstrated that ND patients exhibit markedly decreased HEP amplitudes, which suggests that ND patients may exhibit impaired interoceptive function. This is in accordance with previous research on drug and alcohol addiction [43, 44]. These findings lead us to hypothesize that ND patients may have an interoceptive dysfunction. This could manifest as a reduced ability to perceive heartbeats, or a slower neural processing speed of cardiac signals, which might ultimately be reflected in lower HEP amplitudes. Importantly, it was found that HEP amplitudes did not change after a 2-hour nicotine withdrawal period, suggesting that the reduction in nicotine concentration during short-term withdrawal has no effect on interoceptive dysfunction. Although nicotine exerts its effects primarily through the dopamine system and acetylcholine receptors in the brain, resulting in alterations in neural activity [1, 45], a 2-hour period of nicotine withdrawal may not be enough to change these functions in the brain, and the function of the autonomic nervous system between the heart and the brain has not yet been fully affected, so a short period of decreasing nicotine concentration results in no improvement in the interoceptive impairments.

Previous research has shown that nicotine affects cortical areas associated with perceptual and motor processing by modulating acetylcholine receptors in the central nervous system, so chronic nicotine use can lead to adaptive changes in these receptors, reducing the brain’s ability to process internal signals such as heartbeats [46, 47]. The central lobe region is the area of the brain near the primary sensory cortex and the motor cortex. This brain region is responsible for processing sensory information and motor control of the body, and activity in the central lobe region is critical for accurately perceiving internal changes in the body (such as heartbeat). The parietal lobe region is the key area responsible for processing sensory information and spatial perception, which not only processes external sensory inputs such as touch, temperature, and pain, but is also related to the perception of signals within the body, helping us to perceive signals that are subjected to the body internally, such as the heartbeat and the rhythm of breathing [48]. Our results show that there were differences in HEP amplitudes in different brain regions in ND patients, with the HEP amplitudes in the central and parietal regions being lower than those in the frontal lobe region, and the parietal lobe region having the lowest HEP amplitudes. One potential mechanism underlying these regional differences could involve nicotine’s modulation of neuronal activity. It is plausible that chronic nicotine exposure might inhibit the processing of interoceptive signals within these circuits, potentially contributing to the observed decrease in HEP amplitudes and a blunted awareness of internal states like heartbeat in ND patients.

The results of the correlation analysis indicated that the lower the autonomic nervous system scores of ND patients, the lower their somatic awareness scores. We speculate that there may be physiological and psychological adaptive mechanisms for the diminished awareness of internal signals in ND patients. Chronic smoking can lead to a blunting of the autonomic nervous system, and ND patients may no longer be sensitive to the body’s normal responses (e.g., physiological changes during stress and exercise), which may contribute to an overall lower autonomic nervous system response. Additionally, ND patients may ignore bodily discomfort in order to continue consuming nicotine. This psychological adaptation mechanism can help them temporarily alleviate the negative sensations caused by smoking, which in turn leads to a decrease in their ability to perceive bodily signals.

The identification of interoceptive dysfunction as a stable trait in ND—even during early withdrawal—suggests that it may serve as a novel target for smoking cessation interventions. Therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing interoceptive awareness, such as mindfulness training that directs attention to bodily sensations, or interoception-focused components integrated into cognitive-behavioral therapy (e.g., body scanning exercises), could be explored as adjuncts to existing treatment programs. By refining the perception and cognitive interpretation of internal states (e.g., craving, withdrawal discomfort), such approaches may facilitate the development of alternative, non-smoking coping strategies, thereby addressing both physiological and psychological aspects of nicotine dependence.

This study has some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design and modest sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the all-male sample precludes generalization to females. Third, HEPs index neural processing of cardiac interoception during focused attention; findings may not extend to other interoceptive domains (e.g., respiratory, gastrointestinal) or to behavioral accuracy measures. Finally, the 2-hour abstinence window captures only early withdrawal, and exhaled CO is an indirect surrogate for plasma nicotine/cotinine. Future work requires larger, sex-balanced cohorts, multimodal interoceptive assessments, longer withdrawal periods, and direct biochemical measures to confirm and extend these results.

Conclusion

Evidence from HEPs indicates that ND patients exhibit interoceptive dysfunction. Importantly, 2-hour nicotine withdrawal did not improve interoceptive dysfunction in ND patients. Targeting interoceptive function in smoking cessation programs offers a novel approach to addressing the physiological and psychological aspects of nicotine dependence, providing a comprehensive strategy for improving outcomes in individuals striving to quit smoking.
