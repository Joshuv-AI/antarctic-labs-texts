Peripheral Blood Inflammatory Markers and Immune Cell Profiles in Smokers With and Without COPD
Authors: Wenying Lu, Maddison Waters, Josie Larby, Sapha Shibeeb, Steven Bozinovski, Stavros Selemidis, Jonathan McQualter, Ross Vlahos, Md Imtaiyaz Hassan, Heinrich Weber, Sukhwinder Singh Sohal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCigarette smoking is a major driver of airway and systemic inflammation and contributes to the development and progression of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). This study compared peripheral blood inflammatory markers and immune cell profiles in smokers with and without COPD and in non-smoking controls.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, blood samples were collected from 80 participants (n = 20/group): non-smoker controls (NS), smokers with normal lung function (NLFS), current smokers with COPD (COPD-CS), and ex-smokers with COPD (COPD-ES). Total white cell count (WCC) and leukocyte differentials (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils) were measured using an automated haematology analyser (Sysmex XN2000). Systemic inflammatory markers were measured using C-reactive protein (CRP, Roche Cobas Pro) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR, Diesse VES-MATIC Easy). Lung function parameters (including FEV1, FVC, DLco, FEF25–75%, and SpO2) were assessed and correlated with blood markers.ResultsCompared with NS, WCC, neutrophils, and monocytes were higher in both COPD groups and in NLFS (all p < 0.05). Lymphocytes were higher in NLFS and COPD-CS (p < 0.05). Eosinophils were higher in COPD-ES and NLFS relative to NS (p < 0.05). ESR and CRP were elevated in NLFS and both COPD groups compared with NS (p < 0.05). In COPD, higher blood inflammatory cell counts were associated with poorer lung function (negative correlations; p < 0.05).ConclusionPeripheral blood inflammatory markers are increased in smokers, including those with preserved lung function, and in COPD. Differences in eosinophil patterns between COPD current smokers and ex-smokers suggest inflammatory heterogeneity and should be confirmed in larger, well-characterized cohorts. These findings support the move towards careful personalized medicine interventions, based on individual inflammatory profiles.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the third leading cause of death worldwide and remains a major contributor to morbidity, mortality, and substantial health burden across populations [1]. A defining pathological hallmark of COPD is chronic airway inflammation, which supports both the onset and progression of the disease. Among the most significant risk factors are cigarette smoking, as well as other harmful particles such as particulate matter in air pollution, aerosols from electronic cigarettes, heated-tobacco products, occupational dust, and chemicals, which also play a crucial role [2–5]. These particles trigger multiple inflammatory pathways, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This process drives the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells, ultimately disturbing the fine protease–anti-protease balance in the lungs and promoting tissue injury and remodelling [2, 6]. Such inflammatory processes, particularly those induced by cigarette smoking, are closely related to airway structural changes and recurrent exacerbations of COPD [3].

Chronic inflammation is a central component of both the pathogenesis and clinical course of COPD. It predominantly affects the lung parenchyma and small airways, resulting in progressive structural damage and airflow restriction [7, 8]. Characteristically, the inflammatory pattern in COPD is distinguished by an increase in the number of neutrophils in the airway lumen, together with elevated numbers of macrophages, CD8+ T lymphocytes, and B lymphocytes [9]. This cellular profile reflects the chronic immune activation that drives both the structural and functional decline observed in patients with COPD.

Although airway inflammation is widely recognized as a key driver of COPD progression and exacerbations, current first-line treatment of COPD remains inadequate. This is mainly due to difficulty in addressing the underlying inflammatory process, which is often resistant to corticosteroid treatment [10]. Furthermore, COPD is clinically and biologically heterogeneous, and the absence of clearly defined inflammatory endotypes complicates the development of effective targeted therapies [10].

Given the central role of chronic inflammation and the marked heterogeneity of COPD, improved characterization of inflammatory patterns using clinical accessible measures is needed. Blood-based inflammatory markers offer a practical means of assessing systemic inflammation and may provide indirect insights into the airway inflammatory processes. This study primarily aimed to characterize peripheral blood inflammatory cell profiles in cigarette smokers, with and without COPD, compared to non-smoking individuals with preserved lung function. We further examined associations between blood inflammatory markers, smoking exposure, and lung function, to distinguish inflammatory changes related to smoking from those associated with established COPD. By comparing smokers with and without COPD, this study also aimed to identify candidate blood biomarkers with potential clinical relevance and to explore inflammatory patterns that may contribute to COPD heterogeneity, and also may support a phenotyping approach to COPD and inform future personalized therapeutic approaches for COPD management.

Methods

Participants

Patient consent was obtained and blood samples were collected from smokers who were grouped into subgroups of current smokers with COPD (COPD-CS, n = 20), ex-smokers with COPD (COPD-ES, 20), and smokers with normal lung function (NLFS, 20) and compared to non-smoker controls without COPD (NS, 20). Patient demographics are shown in Table 1. The selection criterion for ex-smokers was having quit smoking over 1 month earlier. COPD stage is classified based on the Global Strategy for Prevention, Diagnosis and Management of COPD (GOLD) 2023 report [1]. All patients were in a stable COPD condition, and no exacerbations were reported at the time of enrolment. Blood samples were collected before the bronchoscopy procedure (for clinical indications), during the fasting period, and prior to administration of any medications. At the time of recruitment, no vaping or other substances were used. Other contributor factors such as occupational exposure are not included in this study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (Tasmania) (Ethics: H0013051). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study. Analysis

Inflammatory cells, including total white cell count (WCC), neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), were tested and analysed using the Sysmex XN2000 Haematology System (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) and Roche Cobas Pro Analyzer and Diesse VES-MATIC Easy instrument, respectively. The level of each inflammatory cell type was analysed and correlated with demographics including age and pack-years, along with respiratory parameters, tested using a BodyBox preVent flow sensor (PFS) plethysmograph (MGC Diagnostics, Melbourne, Australia), including forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLco), forced expiratory flow at 25–75% of forced vital capacity (FEF25–75%), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2).

Statistical Analysis

A non-parametric one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using the unpaired Kruskal–Wallis test, and a two-tailed Mann–Whitney t test was performed between groups. A two-tailed Spearman test was selected to analyse the correlation between smoking exposure, inflammatory cell counts, and lung physiological parameters. The statistical analysis was completed using GraphPad Prism V10.2.3 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA), with a p-value ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Total WCC and Monocyte Levels

The total WCC (cells/mL) in COPD groups was significantly higher (median, range: -CS 7.3, 4.2–13.9, p < 0.0001; ES 7.15, 3.9–13.6, p < 0.0001) than NS (median, range: 4.7, 2.6–6.4) (Fig. 1a). Similarly, total WCC and monocytes were significantly higher in the NLFS (total WCC, median, range: 6.9, 4.0–15.8, p < 0.0005; monocytes, median, range: 0.55, 0.3–1.7, p < 0.005) than in NS (Fig. 1a, b).Fig. 1Comparison of white blood cell count levels and monocyte count levels between groups. a The total white blood cell count (WCC) levels were statistically significantly increased in normal lung function smokers (NLFS), current smokers with COPD (COPD-CS), and ex-smokers with COPD (COPD-ES) relative to non-smokers (NS). b The monocyte levels were statistically significantly higher in NLFS, COPD-CS, and COPD-ES than in NS. Neutrophil, Eosinophil, and Lymphocyte Levels

In COPD groups, the neutrophil count (cells/mL) was significantly elevated (median, range: CS 5.1, 1.8–10.6, p < 0.0001; ES 4.65, 1.8–11.8, p < 0.0001) compared to NS (median, range: 2.5, 0.6–4.1). Similarly, the neutrophil count was significantly higher in the NLFS (median, range: 3.85, 0.9–13.2, p < 0.005) than in NS (Fig. 2a). The level of eosinophils (cells/mL) was significantly higher in COPD-ES (median, range: 0.2, 0.0–0.6, p = 0.0002) and in NLFS (median, range: 0.1, 0.0–0.4, p = 0.02), but with no statistical significance in COPD-CS (Fig. 2b). Lymphocyte levels (cells/mL) were increased in COPD-CS (median, range: 1.6, 0.6–3.1, p < 0.05) and in NLFS (median, range: 1.95, 0.2–3.3, p < 0.01) compared to NS (median, range: 1.2, 0.3–1.9), but with no statistical significance in COPD-ES (Fig. 2c). The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (cells/mL) was significantly higher in COPD groups (median, range: CS, 3.31, 1.3–9.4, p < 0.01; ES, 3.1, 1.0–13.1, p < 0.005) and in NLFS (median, range: 2.5, 1.1–12.0, p < 0.05) than in NS (median, range: 2.0, 0.8–5.0) (Fig. 2d). The lymphocyte cell levels were significantly higher in the GOLD 1 group than the GOLD 2 group (p < 0.05), but no difference with GOLD 3 and GOLD 4 groups. Inflammatory Marker Levels: ESR and CRP

The ESR level (mm/h) was significantly higher in COPD groups (CS mean, range: 22.0, 1.0–86.0, p < 0.05; ES mean, range: 14.5, 1.0–49.0, p < 0.05) and NLFS (mean, range: 16.5, 1.0–82.0, p < 0.005) compared to NS (median, range: 7.5, 0.5–20.0) (Fig. 3a). The CRP level (mg/L) was also higher in COPD groups (CS mean, range: 8.5, 0.5–110.0, p < 0.0001; ES mean, range: 4.0, 0.5–87.0, p = 0.0003) and in NLFS (mean, range: 5.5, 0.5–127, p = 0.0002) than NS (mean, range: 0.5, 0.0–5.0) (Fig. 3b). Correlation Between Smoking History and Inflammatory Cell Level

We found a significant positive correlation between smoking pack-years and total WCC, neutrophil, monocyte, eosinophil counts, CRP, and ESR levels. There was no correlation for lymphocyte count in those who had a smoking history (classified as ever smokers), including NLFS, COPD-CS, and COPD-ES (Table 2). In particular, the levels of neutrophils, ESR, and CRP were strongly positively correlated with smoking pack-years in COPD-CS (p < 0.05), along with a trend for positive correlation between WCC and smoking pack-years in COPD-CS. COPD-ES showed a significant positive correlation between smoking history and ESR and CRP levels (p < 0.05) (Table 2). Interestingly, we found that the level of lymphocytes was negatively correlated with smoking pack-years in COPD-CS (r′ = −0.4587, p < 0.05), whereas there was a positive correlation between lymphocyte level and smoking pack-years in COPD-ES (r′ = 0.453, p = 0.059) (Table 2), and in NLFS (r′ = 0.4975, p < 0.05) (data not shown). Correlation Between Smoking History and Lung Function Parameters

We observed a significant decline in lung function parameters, including FEV1 (p < 0.005), FVC (p < 0.05), DLco (p < 0.05) and FEF25–75% (p < 0.0005), along with blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) (p < 0.05), with increased smoking pack-years in all those who had smoked (Table 4). Particularly, in the COPD-CS group, smoking pack-years had a significant negative correlation between FEV1 (p < 0.05) and FEF25–75% (p < 0.01), while in the COPD-ES group, we found a trend for negative correlation in the same lung function parameters (FEV1 and SpO2), close to statistical significance (Table 4). Discussion

In COPD, the blood inflammatory profile is considered to reflect systemic and airway inflammation, which may provide useful insights into disease severity, progression, and response to therapy. In this study, we observed significantly elevated levels of inflammatory cells in the blood of COPD groups and normal lung function smokers (NLFS) compared to the non-smokers, suggesting that cigarette smoking exposure is associated with detectable systemic inflammatory changes, even in the absence of airflow limitation.

Both blood and lung tissue in patients with COPD demonstrate increased inflammatory cell populations, primarily neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes [11]. Neutrophils are the earliest innate immune cells to respond during infection or tissue injury. They rapidly migrate from resident pools within the lung to the affected site, and further recruit from the circulation [12]. However, evidence indicates that the inflammatory profile may vary with disease stage. In mild to moderate COPD, inflammatory patterns show a different cellular profile compared to normal controls, moving away from the traditional COPD inflammatory response [13, 14]. Monocytes also contribute to chronic inflammation in COPD by increasing recruitment from the bloodstream, which subsequently differentiates into macrophages within lung tissue [13]. Lymphocytes represent another critical inflammatory cell type, playing an important role in the pathology of COPD, particularly during progression to severe disease. Previous studies have shown increased levels of neutrophils and monocytes in the sputum of patients with COPD, a characteristic of chronic lung inflammation [15]. In our study, we observed significantly elevated blood neutrophil and monocyte counts in both COPD-CS and COPD-ES groups. In addition, blood lymphocyte levels were significantly higher in the COPD-CS group, with a trend towards higher levels in the COPD-ES group. The increased number of lymphocytes in COPD lungs may perpetuate neutrophilic inflammation, contributing to the persistence of inflammation. Smoking is a key factor leading to neutrophilic inflammation [10], and a positive correlation was identified between smoking exposure and blood levels of neutrophils and monocytes in the COPD groups, particularly in COPD-CS. Interestingly, we also found significantly increased blood neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts in the NLFS group, with no statistical difference compared with the COPD-CS and COPD-ES groups. These findings further indicate that cigarette smoking alone, independently of airflow limitation, may contribute to systemic inflammatory changes and could be relevant to future disease risk; however, longitudinal data are required to clarify this relationship.

Inflammatory cell distribution within airway wall tissue appears more variable and dependent on airway location and disease severity. Several studies have reported a significant reduction in neutrophil numbers in the large airways of patients with COPD compared to non-smokers, while no substantial changes are observed in the small airways [14, 16]. Neutrophil infiltration, however, is more pronounced in patients with advanced disease stages (GOLD stages III and IV), whereas lower levels are typically seen in mild COPD [17]. Similar patterns have been described for macrophages. Increased macrophage numbers were observed in the large airways of COPD patients with severe chronic bronchitis, but no significant change was detected in the small airways of smokers and COPD patients [12, 18].

Lymphocyte levels are also implicated in COPD pathogenesis, with elevated levels associated with progressive tissue destruction and airway obstruction and increased severity of airflow limitation [17]. Chronic lymphocytic infiltration may amplify neutrophilic inflammation, reflecting a sustained phase of immune activation [19, 20]. Lymphocyte profiling has therefore been proposed as a potential tool for distinguishing COPD phenotypes and predicting therapeutic responses, although this remains exploratory and requires further validation [9]. Conversely, a decrease in lymphocyte counts has also been reported in some patients with COPD. Peripheral blood lymphocyte count has been identified as a marker of chronic stress and a poor prognostic factor, associated with worse outcomes and increased mortality risk [21].

Activated neutrophils release proteolytic enzymes, such as neutrophil elastase and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS) in COPD [12]. These are closely related to the process of activation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in COPD. Studies have previously provided significant evidence that EMT markers, such as S100A4 and MMP9, are increased in COPD patients, and that EMT is correlated with airway remodelling, angiogenesis, and pulmonary physiological changes in these patients [22–24]. EMT activity is also closely linked to the development of lung cancer in COPD patients [25–27].

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio is an indicator of systemic inflammation, and higher ratios are associated with poorer outcomes [28]. In this study, we found a significantly higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in COPD-CS and COPD-ES than NS. Although the higher ratio is associated with more frequent exacerbations, worse lung function, and increased mortality [29], the cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inference, and clinical implications should be interpreted conservatively. These patients need an optimal treatment strategy that is closely monitored. Neutrophilic inflammation in COPD is poorly responsive to corticosteroid treatment, even at high doses [10], underscoring the need for new therapeutic strategies for neutrophil-dominant phenotypes.

Eosinophilic inflammation has been suggested as an emerging feature of COPD, with 20–30% of patients exhibiting an eosinophilic phenotype [30, 31]. This phenotype has been linked with exacerbation risk and a favourable response to inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) [32, 33]. Eosinophilic inflammation is typically assessed through cytological analysis of sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) specimens [34]. While sputum and BAL analyses often demonstrate increased eosinophils in COPD [35–38], only a minority of patients show persistently elevated levels. Smoking status appears to exert a differential effect, with higher BAL eosinophil counts observed in current smokers relative to former smokers with COPD [39]. In our study, blood eosinophil counts did not differ between NLFS and current smokers with COPD, whereas higher eosinophil levels were observed in ex-smokers with COPD. The clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain and may reflect differences in smoking status, treatments, or disease phenotype.

CRP is a well-known marker of systemic inflammation, particularly in a high proportion of COPD patients with elevated levels of CRP. CRP correlates directly with disease severity and mortality, which can help identify these patients and enable subsequent management [40]. Higher CRP levels are associated with worsened FEV1 [41], suggesting that subclinical systemic inflammation may accelerate respiratory function decline [42]. Additionally, increasing CRP levels during acute exacerbations of COPD are linked to up-regulation of vascular endothelial growth factor, potentially heightening the risk of cardiovascular complications [43]. Similar to CRP, ESR, a commonly used non-specific index of disease activity, is useful for monitoring chronic conditions [44]. ESR levels are frequently increased in patients with COPD, with higher levels correlating with increased disease severity. Also, the increased ESR levels reflect the systemic inflammation characteristic of COPD [44]. ESR rises in response to increased serum levels of acute phase proteins, such as fibrinogen and immunoglobulins, as well as in response to anaemia [45], and may be linked to increased exacerbations and cardiovascular risk. The increase in CRP and ESR levels observed in our study supports the presence of ongoing systemic inflammation in COPD; however, these markers are influenced by multiple factors and should be interpreted within a broader clinical context.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the relatively small sample size limited statistical power and precluded more detailed subgroup analyses, particularly in relation to COPD phenotypes and disease severity. Secondly, we did not include patient comorbidity data at the time of recruitment into the analysis. Comorbidities, such as lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and infections, may independently influence levels of inflammatory biomarkers and blood cell profiles, potentially confounding the observed associations. Thirdly, information on inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use was not included in the analysis. ICS therapy can influence blood eosinophil counts and other inflammatory markers, and its absence represents an important limitation. Fourthly, the cross-sectional study design prevents assessment of temporal relationships or causality between inflammatory changes, smoking exposure, and disease progression. Lastly, blood biomarkers were used as indirect indicators of inflammation and may not fully reflect local airway inflammatory processes. Our future work will investigate the inflammatory cell distribution within the airway wall tissue and the correlation between ICS use in these COPD patient cohorts.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that blood inflammatory cell counts and systemic inflammatory markers are elevated in smokers with COPD and in smokers with normal lung function compared with non-smokers, supporting the presence of smoking-associated systemic inflammation. Blood inflammatory profiling may provide complementary information regarding inflammatory status in COPD; however, its role in disease phenotyping, risk stratification, and treatment guidance should be interpreted cautiously. Blood eosinophil counts may be useful for informing treatment decisions in selected patients, particularly those who do not respond to long-acting inhaled bronchodilators, although this approach is limited by inter-individual variability and the absence of elevated eosinophils in many patients with COPD. Additionally, elevated CRP and ESR levels can be used as general indicators of systemic inflammation and potentially facilitate early intervention and reduce the risk of complications. The strong correlation between smoking history and increased inflammatory cells underscores the critical role of smoking in the development and progression of COPD, reinforcing the need for smoking cessation programs as a key preventive and management strategy for COPD. While these findings support the move towards personalized medicine, where treatment decisions take into account variations in inflammatory profiles to enhance patient outcomes and quality of life [31, 46, 47], further longitudinal studies with larger cohorts, detailed comorbidity data, treatment stratification, and direct assessment of airway inflammation are required before firm clinical conclusions can be drawn. Overall, addressing inflammation through targeted therapies, smoking cessation, and personalized treatment plans remains an important aspect of COPD management and minimizing associated risks.
