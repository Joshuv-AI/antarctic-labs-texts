Changes in the Prevalence of Antimalarial Partner Drug Resistance Markers and Policy in 6 Sub-Saharan African Countries From 2000 to 2021: A Systematic Review
Authors: Alexandra Walker, Amanda Ross, Christian Nsanzabana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrompt malaria case management is a cornerstone for malaria control and elimination. However, this strategy is threatened by the development of antimalarial drug resistance. Resistance is mediated through spontaneous genetic changes such as mutations, but drug pressure is the main driver of resistance spread. Molecular markers of resistance may provide insight into spatiotemporal dynamics of drug resistance and how drug policy changes may affect the spread of resistance.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review to assess the dynamics of Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, Pfdhfr, and Pfdhps mutations from 2000 to 2021. Six countries from sub-Saharan Africa were selected by availability of molecular data and varying antimalarial drug policies: Kenya, Malawi, and Uganda in East Africa and Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, and Nigeria in West Africa. Medline, Embase, Cochrane, and Elsevier databases were searched for relevant literature, and identified records were screened for prevalence data and extracted.ResultsOverall, 138 studies were included. The estimated prevalence of Pfcrt 76T declined following cessation of chloroquine, though at variable levels among countries. All countries saw an increase in Pfmdr1 N86/D1246 prevalence, with faster increases in East Africa, while Pfmdr1 184F prevalence increased, except in Burkina Faso. The prevalence of Pfdhfr (51I/59R/108N) and Pfdhps (436A/437G/540E) mutations reached fixation levels in most countries; however, the 164L and 581G mutations increased during the period only in Kenya and Uganda.ConclusionsOur study provides compelling evidence on the impact of antimalarial drug policy change on molecular markers of resistance and their potential use to monitor drug resistance spread.Keywords: antimalarial drug resistances, antimalarial drug policy, molecular markers, prevalence, systematic review

Over the last 20 years, a major change in antimalarial drug policy has occurred in endemic settings, with countries shifting toward the use of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) [1]. A pivotal factor in shaping global antimalarial drug policy was the emergence of drug resistance to the previously used drugs, chloroquine (CQ) and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) [2]. Although the efficacy of ACTs for the treatment of uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum infection remains high in sub-Saharan Africa, drug resistance is a threat to the artemisinin compound and the partner drug [3].

Partial resistance to artemisinin was first identified in Cambodia in 2008 [4] and subsequently in other areas of the Greater Mekong Subregion (GMS) [5, 6]. The ensuing failure of ACTs to clear the infection and a lack of novel therapeutics have made treatment in the GMS difficult [7]. Independent emergence of artemisinin resistance has recently been reported in several East African countries [8–10]. As the majority of malaria cases and deaths occur in sub-Saharan Africa, where transmission remains high in many settings [11], a situation similar to the GMS could have disastrous consequences [12]. It is therefore vital that the spread of antimalarial drug resistance to artemisinin and partner drugs be monitored and contained as much as possible.

As the frequency of exposure to a drug increases within a community, so does the drug pressure, driving selection and leading to the further spread of resistant parasite clones [13]. However, the impact of policy changes on the spread of antimalarial drug resistance is not well established, including changes in first-line treatment or preventative chemotherapies, such as intermittent preventative treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) and seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC).

The use of surveillance tools to observe spatial and temporal trends can give policy makers an idea of past, present, and future trends of drug resistance. One strategy is to track molecular markers associated with antimalarial drug resistance [14]. Mutations involving the genes P. falciparum chloroquine resistance transporter (Pfcrt) and P. falciparum multidrug resistance protein 1 (Pfmdr1) are commonly assessed for ACT partner drug resistance, as the presence of either the wild type or mutant clone is associated with reduced susceptibility to lumefantrine or amodiaquine, respectively [15]. As SP is still typically used in preventative chemotherapies, mutations in P. falciparum dihydropteroate synthetase (Pfdhps) and P. falciparum dihydrofolate reductase (Pfdhfr) are associated with sulfadoxine and pyrimethamine resistance, respectively, and are commonly assessed in conjunction [16]. The prevalence levels and changing dynamics of these markers over time may be influenced by antimalarial drug policy changes. By understanding how these policies influence molecular markers of drug resistance, there is the potential to inform future policies.

East Africa and West Africa have historically contrasting antimalarial drug use, differences in the timing and nature of policies, and variation in the emergence and spread of resistance. While several previous reviews have explored trends in individual countries, no review to date has undertaken a comparison between East and West Africa.

In this systematic review, we aimed to describe the changes in the prevalence of molecular markers associated with ACT partner drugs and preventative chemotherapies occurring after the introduction or cessation of key drug-based interventions or drug policy changes in countries in East and West Africa. To demonstrate the feasibility of this approach while capturing regional diversity, 3 countries from each region were selected.

METHODS

The PRISMA statement (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) was used as a framework to guide the conduct and reporting of this systematic review [17]. The selected outcome measure was the prevalence of Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, Pfdhfr, and Pfdhps gene polymorphisms in each country between 2000 and 2021. These molecular markers were chosen as they represent several partner drugs in ACTs or the drugs used in preventative chemotherapies, and they have been validated according to the World Health Organization [18] (Table 1).

Countries were selected to represent the range of policy decisions and antimalarial drug use. This selection allowed for comparison between the contrasting country use profiles of the prevalence of different drug resistance markers and the potential impact from policy and antimalarial drug changes. In total, 6 countries were selected: Kenya, Malawi, and Uganda in East Africa and Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, and Nigeria for West Africa.

Search Strategy

Four electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane. An initial search was performed in October 2022, encompassing literature from 1 January 2000 to 18 October 2022. The search was updated in September 2023 to include any additional studies that collected data in the period. Controlled vocabularies, synonyms, and indexed terms were included when possible. Full search terms are available in Supplementary Information 1, Supplementary Table 1. The Worldwide Antimalarial Resistance Network Molecular (WAARN) Surveyor Database was also searched for additional records or unpublished data [19].

Study Selection and Screening

Citations for identified records were imported into Rayyan [20] directly or by using EndNote software [20]. Following identification, confirmation, and removal of duplicates, one individual performed title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening according to the eligibility criteria (Supplementary Information 1, Supplementary Table 2). The criteria included original research within a selected country during the selected period and the prevalence of 1 or more of the selected markers of antimalarial drug resistance.

Data Extraction

Data from eligible publications were extracted, including country of origin, year of collection, the number of genotyped samples, and the number of samples carrying each molecular marker of interest. Additional data included study design, age group, genotyping method, and district of collection.

Data Analysis

Study characteristics were summarized as frequencies and percentages per country. The prevalence of associated mutations for each study was plotted over time (2000–2021) for each country and gene separately. The timing of changes in antimalarial drug policy for the recommended first-line treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum and preventative chemotherapies was marked on the plot. This allows the patterns in prevalence to be visualized relative to the timing of policy changes. Point size was proportional to the number of samples that were genotyped for each study. If a study reported single and mixed genotypes of a mutation, these were combined to give the total number of positive samples. For prevalence data collected over multiple years (≤3 years), the data were combined into a single time point, represented by the midpoint of the collection period.

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2) [21].

RESULTS

Study Characteristics

A search of the literature returned 1409 publications (Figure 1). Of these, 629 duplicate records were removed before screening, and 551 were excluded per the selection criteria. A total of 229 records were sought for retrieval, with an additional 22 identified during extraction. Overall, of the 249 full reports retrieved, 111 failed to meet the selection criteria, leaving 138 studies eligible for inclusion. Sixty-nine studies were from the selected East African countries, 58 studies were from the West African countries, and 11 were multicountry studies (Supplementary Information 2, Supplementary Table 1). No formal risk of bias was performed during extraction, although risk of bias was assessed for the pattern over time rather than for individual studies.

PRISMA flowchart [17] for the identification and selection of studies.

The years of sample collection for the studies were spread between 2000 and 2021 (Figure 2), with the highest frequencies between 2010 and 2018. Collection of samples occurred throughout the period for most countries, except for Malawi, which had no studies included after 2012, and Côte d’Ivoire, which had a low overall number of studies and intermittent collection years. Collection for multicountry studies, of which imported cases were included, primarily occurred after 2010.

Study data collection frequency per year. Studies with multiple collection years appear once for each year in the dataset.

Overall, Pfcrt was the gene genotyped in the highest number of studies (88/138, 64%) and in each country, except for Kenya and Nigeria, where Pfmdr1 was the most genotyped gene (Table 2). Few studies genotyped all genes (24/138, 17%).

Approximately, half of the studies used a cross-sectional study design (76/138, 55%), and restriction fragment length polymorphism and polymerase chain reaction/sequencing were the dominant methods for genotyping (Supplementary Information 2, Supplementary Table 2). Children <18 years old were the most frequently sampled age group, with more than one-third of these studies sampling children between the ages of 3 and 59 months (25/65, 38%). Collection varied among districts in each country, with sample collection often heavily concentrated in specific areas (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2).

Estimated Prevalence of Pfcrt 76T Mutation

Between 2000 and 2021, all countries in this study experienced a decline in Pfcrt 76T mutation prevalence, following the first-line treatment change away from CQ (Figure 3). In West Africa, in the years after the cessation of CQ, 76T mutations continued to rise before a declining trend was observed, even though from 2011 the prevalence appears to remain sizable in all 3 countries, demonstrating incomplete elimination of the 76T mutation. In East Africa, the prevalence of 76T remained at high levels in Kenya and Uganda up to 10 years after formal cessation of CQ, before declining to negligible levels. In Malawi, 76T mutation levels were very low (<10%) from 2000 with respect to the other countries. Overall, despite CQ cessation and a decline in 76T mutation prevalence, the mutation remained present in Burkina Faso and Nigeria in West Africa and Uganda in East Africa between 2017 and 2021. Regional differences in each country are seen in Supplementary Information 2, Supplementary Figure 3.

Estimated Prevalence of Pfmdr1 Mutations

We excluded Côte d’Ivoire, Malawi, and the Pfmdr1 1034C and 1042D mutations since limited data were available. After the introduction of artemether-lumefantrine (AL) and artesunate-amodiaquine (AS + AQ) in Burkina Faso in 2005, a downward trend was observed in all mutations of the Pfmdr1 gene (Figure 4). However, the same trends were not observed for Nigeria, even though the country also introduced AL and AS + AQ in 2004, with the prevalence of 86Y and 184F varying greatly within a year and over time (86Y, 0%–86% in 2014 and 0%–96% in 2016; 184F, 6%–87% in 2014). Although a number of studies were conducted post–SMC rollout (2013), there is no clear trend, although there is within-region heterogeneity present as well as between-region variation (Supplementary Information 2, Supplementary Figure 4).

In contrast, Kenya and Uganda in East Africa introduced AL as the only recommended first-line antimalarial treatment from 2004, and in both countries a substantial decrease was observed for 86Y and 1246Y after this time. Before 2004 in Kenya, the prevalence of all Pfmdr1 mutations was >50%, with the subsequent years showing a slight increase before decreasing throughout the period. The estimated prevalence of the 184F mutation fluctuates over time but follows a general upward trend, although prevalence varies among regions (Supplementary Information 2, Supplementary Figure 4). Even though no studies were performed before the introduction of AL, similar trends were seen in the studies from Uganda in all regions. Both 86Y and 1246Y are shown to decline sharply from 2010, demonstrating minimal prevalence in more recent years (86Y, approximately 1% from 2018 to 2021; 1246Y, 0%–10% from 2018 to 2021). Conversely, the prevalence of 184F increases throughout the period, from approximately 12% in 2004 to >70% in 2021.

Estimated Prevalence of Pfdhfr Mutations

The use of SP for IPTp was introduced in the early 2000s in all countries, with the exception of Malawi, which formally introduced IPTp in 1993. The trends of the estimated prevalence of Pfdhfr mutations (51I, 59R, 108N, and 164L) over time in the different countries vary by mutation (Figure 5). In Burkina Faso, 51I, 59R, and 108N were at low levels in 2000, before increasing steadily from 2004 to >90% in 2016. Pfdhfr mutations were also present in Nigeria before the introduction of IPTp (2004) and increased to high levels in the following years, even after SMC was introduced. One study demonstrated the presence of 164L in Nigeria, imported to China, while no studies observed the mutation in Burkina Faso. There were no data available after 2007 for Côte d’Ivoire.

In East Africa, the prevalence of the triple mutation (51I, 59R, 108N) was already at fixation from 2000 in each country, with the exception of 1 Kenyan study showing the prevalence of 108N at <5% between 2000 and 2006 [22]. However, another study demonstrated a prevalence of 51I, 59R, and 108N mutations of >80% in 2006 in the same region (Coast) and site [23]. The 164L mutation was found intermittently at low levels throughout East Africa from 2003. In Kenya, it was found in only the Nyanza region, while in Uganda, it was present in all included regions and appeared to be increasing (Supplementary Information 2, Supplementary Figure 5). There were no data available before the introduction of IPTp or SMC for the 164L mutation.

Estimated Prevalence of Pfdhps Mutations

The estimated prevalence of Pfdhps mutations varies between East and West Africa (Figure 6). In Burkina Faso, 436A and 437G were frequently found at >50% and increased slightly from 2000 to 2016, while the 540E mutation was found only twice, in 2003 and 2014, at a prevalence <11% [24, 25]. No data were available for Côte d’Ivoire after 2005. Although the majority of the sample collection occurred after 2010 in Nigeria, the 437G mutation increased from 2003, as did 436A from 2005. The K540E mutation generally remained below 25% prevalence, with the exception of 1 study, which demonstrated a prevalence of 94% in 2016 [26]. The 581G mutation was also found in Nigeria. Overall, fluctuations in mutation prevalence may be attributed to regional heterogeneity (Supplementary Information 2, Supplementary Figure 6).

By comparison, in East Africa, 437G and 540E were the dominant mutations, primarily found at ≥75% before or just after the initiation of IPTp, and remained so throughout the period. Although the 436A mutation was seldom genotyped and the 581G mutation was reported at <10%, a study from Kenya conducted between 2003 and 2005 reported a prevalence of 68% for the 436A mutation and 85% for the 581G mutation [27]. Subsequent studies reported a maximum 7% prevalence of 436A and 5% prevalence of 581G within the same region and throughout the country (Supplementary Information 2, Supplementary Figure 6). In Uganda, the trends appear similar to Kenya; however, the 581G mutation has varied throughout the years, gradually increasing since 2004 in most regions, with a prevalence ≥25% in the central and western regions. The 436A mutation was genotyped once [28].

DISCUSSION

The increasing frequency of molecular studies since 2000 enables an updated profile on the temporal trends of antimalarial drug resistance markers. In this review, 6 countries were chosen from sub-Saharan Africa, with the results allowing observations of how policy changes may have affected the prevalence of antimalarial drug resistance markers in the past 20 years.

Cessation of CQ use as a first-line treatment varied among countries [1]. Consistent with previous publications, the prevalence of the Pfcrt 76T mutation declined in all countries, following the cessation of CQ use [22, 29–32]. However, return to the K76 wild type occurred at different rates in West and East Africa and coincided with the timing of the policy change and the region. Additional factors that may have contributed to the rate of wild type return include inherent differences among the countries, such as initial prevalence, transmission intensity, access to new first-line treatments, coverage levels, and the availability of CQ and its continued use [32].

Following the recommendation from the WHO for the use of ACTs to treat malaria, countries within sub-Saharan Africa varied in their selection for first-line treatments. The use of AS + AQ was primarily confined to West Africa, where it was used as a first-line treatment with AL, while countries in East Africa tended to use only AL. No data were collected for Central Africa in this review; however, the presence of mutations has been shown to be geographically linked to both East and West [33, 34], with similarities reflected not only in the policy changes but in the preference for antimalarial drugs [1, 35].

Although AS + AQ and AL have opposing selection pressures [15, 36, 37], the associated mutations in the Pfcrt and Pfmdr1 genes showed increasing prevalence of wild type clones (K76 and N86/D1246) following the initiation of ACTs in all countries. Return to wild type Pfmdr1 genotypes occurred faster in East Africa when compared with West Africa. This is in line with a systematic review by Okell et al, who found that the prevalence of 86Y and 1246Y mutations declined faster in countries that already had high rates of Pfmdr1 mutations and used AL as a first-line treatment [38]. The use of ACTs for treatment in West Africa and SMC may account for the slower decrease of 86Y and 1246Y mutations [39]. These policies may also contribute to the observable trend of decreasing 184F, while in East Africa, 184F is shown to be increasing. Due to increasing ACT use and decreasing CQ availability and use, it is difficult to assess the impact that ACTs may have on 76T mutations.

As levels of CQ treatment failure increased, it was used in combination with or replaced by SP in many East African countries, including Malawi, Kenya, and Uganda [40]. Indigenous mutations in the Pfdhfr and Pfdhps genes conferring low to moderate levels of resistance emerged at multiple locations throughout Africa [41] and spread from South East Asia, like the Pfcrt mutations before it [2 , 42]. High levels of Pfdhfr triple (51I/59R/108N) and Pfdhps double (437G/540E) mutations were observed in East Africa from 2000 to 2021, despite the change in policy to ACTs between 2004 and 2007 in the included countries. In West Africa, policy changes to ACTs as a first-line treatment occurred after 2004, due to later failure rates of CQ [1]. The prevalence of Pfdhfr (51I/59R/108N) and Pfdhps (436A/437G) gradually increased throughout the period, although SP was recommended only for IPTp and SMC, except as a treatment for uncomplicated disease in Côte d’Ivoire between 2003 and 2005 [35 ]. A worrying trend is the increasing prevalence of Pfdhfr 164L and Pfdhps 581G in Kenya and Uganda, as increasing mutations in both genes confer higher levels of SP resistance. However, a number of studies have continued to show the protective efficacy of SP in pregnancy, despite high levels of resistance [43–45]. Continued surveillance and monitoring of the protective efficacy and prevalence of antimalarial markers are required.

This study had several limitations. Only 3 countries were selected per region, with each of those countries identified as potentially having sufficient temporal data for each selected molecular marker. Additionally, data are presented for each selected country on a national level, assuming no heterogeneity within countries. As the countries have different levels of transmission and access and rates of treatment, the results are unlikely to be representative of all districts within a country. Furthermore, collected data were primarily from the same sentinel sites within a country, and the same molecular markers of resistance were typically assessed, creating a potential selection bias. Migration patterns within and between countries, particularly border areas, were also not accounted for. Using the timing of a policy change as an indicator for a change in patterns of antimalarial drug use may not have been accurate, as policy initiation can take many years to reach all areas of the country and therefore may not be representative of a change in drug use. AL and AS + AQ are first-line treatments in Burkina Faso and Nigeria; however, no data were available to inform the proportions of use for each drug. This is particularly important with the policy change to ACTs, due to the opposing selection pressure of the drugs. Additionally, in all countries, antimalarial drugs other than the recommended first-line treatment are available in pharmacies or private facilities, although the proportion of use is not known.

Despite a number of limitations, this study provides a systematic review of the timing of policy changes and the temporal patterns of the prevalence of mutations in 6 countries. There is the potential that it can be expanded and performed for all countries in sub-Saharan Africa, with periodic updates to fill knowledge gaps in the region. This knowledge may be able to inform future policy by providing key indicators, such as time scales and prevalence limits for drug resistance markers of certain antimalarial drugs. The research may also be able to inform parameterization of mathematical models of malaria for predicting the impact of future policy changes. Further data collection is required to accurately assess the impact of historical and current drug use on the prevalence and temporal trends of molecular markers associated with antimalarial drug resistance.
