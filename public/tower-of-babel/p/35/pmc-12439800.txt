Factors influencing primary care physicians recommending patients to use digital health technologies for self-management: A cross-sectional study across 20 countries
Authors: Wuraola Oluwayomi Odunlami, Edmond Li, Geva Greenfield, Gabriele Kerr, Austen El-Osta, Rosy Tsopra, Heidrun Lingner, Ensieh Memarian, Robert Hoffman, Katarzyna Nessler, Geronimo Jimenez, Claire Collins, Davorina Petek, Ana Clavería, Maria José Fernández, Gustavo Gusso, Mehmet Ungan, Greg Irving, Liliana Laranjo, Saira Ghafur, Gianluca Fontana, Josip Car, Benedict Hayhoe, Azeem Majeed, Ana Luisa Neves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExpanding access to self-management via Digital Health Technologies may supplement traditional care, mitigating pressures on primary care through self-management. Primary Care Physicians (PCP) can play a critical role in the integration of digital health technologies into patient care, but it is unclear what factors influence PCPs’ recommendation of such technologies.AimsTo identify the factors associated with PCPs recommending digital health technologies to patients for self-management before and during the pandemic.MethodsPCPs across 20 countries completed an online questionnaire between June and September 2020. The outcome was a self-report of recommending patients to at least one of six listed forms of digital health technologies. Univariable logistic regression models were performed to explore factors associated with recommending digital health technologies to patients before and during the pandemic.Results1,592 PCPs were included. Before the pandemic, the odds of recommending digital health technologies for self-management were lower for PCPs not involved in teaching, or practising in Turkey, Australia, Chile, Colombia, France, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia, and Spain. During the pandemic, PCPs practising in rural settings had higher odds of starting to recommend digital health technologies, as well as those from Brazil, Colombia, and Italy. There was no significant difference in recommending digital health technologies before and during the pandemic.ConclusionsInvolvement in teaching (pre-pandemic) and practising in a rural setting (during the pandemic) positively influenced the recommendation of digital health technologies. Significant variation in recommending digital health technologies was present across countries.

Introduction

Primary care systems provide highly efficient and cost-effective preventative healthcare and manage long-term conditions (LTCs) and non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as cardiovascular disease and Type 2 diabetes [1,2]. However, growing workload and demand driven by an ageing population with increasing multimorbidity contribute to significant challenges in workforce recruitment and retention in primary care [3,4]. As substantial growth in the workforce is unlikely to be achievable, healthcare systems globally are embracing digital health technology (DHT) innovations to manage demand and workload pressures more effectively [5].

The adoption of DHT for self-management of LTCs and NCDs could ease pressures on primary care services while supporting greater patient autonomy, well-being and self-actualisation [7]. Examples range from online patient information and health education resources to symptom checkers and self-assessment tools. The use of such DHT by patients remotely thus frees primary care physicians (PCPs) to attend to other patients whose clinical needs cannot be addressed remotely.

While important variations between countries are still observed, there is growing policy support internationally for the use of health apps in routine clinical practice [6,7]. However, these solutions are rarely recommended in practice [8], and may be subject to the extent to which health systems fund their usage [9]. Patient uptake of DHT may be also influenced by reimbursement and digital prescribing schemes implemented at a health system level. Healthcare systems are still to find effective strategies to encourage healthcare providers to become active advocates of DHT for patients who might benefit from them; these might include demonstrating the evidence base for these solutions, or the formal endorsement by government bodies or regulation agencies [8,10]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many of the requirements for DHT implementation were removed, particularly for telemedicine and remote care. A recent scoping review [11] provided an overview of technologies implemented during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic and reported that synchronous teleconsultations were the most common technologies implemented; however, the impact on the adoption of other DHT, particularly those supporting self-management, has been little explored.

As care coordinators, PCPs are important stakeholders in the integration of DHT into patient care. At the frontline of care, PCPs represent both a source of authority and trust that can positively influence patients’ use and adoption of DHT. Despite their potentially critical role in the diffusion of digital innovation, and the growing body of evidence suggesting their desire to embrace DHT [12,13], the role of PCPs in this respect remains largely understudied.

Few studies conducted before the pandemic in 2020 explored doctors’ intentions and attitudes towards recommending DHT [14,15]. These studies reported overall positive attitudes towards DHT but seldom explored factors underlying these intentions [16].

This study aimed to identify the PCP factors associated with recommending DHT for self-management before and during the pandemic. A secondary objective was to characterise possible changes in the proportion of PCPs recommending patients DHT for self-management during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study used an online survey completed by PCPs, reported here according to the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies [17]. The research was conducted by a primary care consortium (inSIGHT Research Group) including health professionals from 20 countries (Australia, Brazil, Canada, Chile, Colombia, Croatia, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the USA). A favourable opinion was obtained from [Institution name removed for blinding purposes] Research Ethics Committee: #[Names removed for blinding purposes]. Additional local ethics approval was requested as needed.

Participants

Participants were deemed eligible if they were PCPs working in primary care in one of the 20 participating countries between 1 March and 30 September 2020. Recruitment was conducted by a combination of convenience sampling from personal and professional networks and social media platforms (i.e. Facebook and LinkedIn), and snowball sampling.

Sample size

A minimum sample size of ≥ 901 responses with a minimum cluster size of 50 responses per country was required to achieve a confidence level of 95%, according to the published protocol [18]. This was calculated using a total population of 527,970 PCPs estimated to work within the 20 countries from publicly available data sources, and an anticipated response rate of 30% was anticipated, based on previous reports of PCP response rates [19]. A full methodology of the survey is detailed elsewhere [18].

Data sources

The original survey was developed in English by the research team, and subsequently translated to French, German, Italian, Portuguese and Spanish by local leads to maximise participation. National leads piloted the survey and generated edits in various languages for national, cultural or organisational adaptation. The survey consisted of 30 questions divided into four sections covering [1] participants’ information [2], use of digital technologies [3], PCP experience and perceived impact and [4] perspective on the future of digital-first models in primary care. The full questionnaire is available as a published paper [18].

Variables

In this study, we used, extracted and analysed variables regarding participant information, and the use of digital technologies (i.e. recommending DHT for self-management to patients).

The predictor variables analysed were PCPs’ demographics and practice setting. The variables included gender, age, years worked as a PCP, involvement in teaching activities (i.e. teaching trainees or medical students, or being affiliated with a university), country of practice, average hours worked per week, and practice setting (i.e. rural, urban, or mixed).

The outcome variable was self-reporting of recommendations regarding the use of DHT for self-management to patients, before (as baseline) and during the COVID-19 pandemic (defined as from 11 March 2020). Options of DHT for self-management included [1] Symptom checker/self-assessment tool [2], Online information resources [3], Health trackers [4], Mindfulness apps/websites [5], Online counselling services, and [6] online crisis resolution services. PCPs were considered to recommend DHT for self-management if answering ‘yes’ to at least one of the options of DHT previously described.

Statistical analysis

The data of all participants were used in the analysis, even if missing parameters for specific answers. No data imputation techniques were used, as the proportion of missing data was minimal (n = 13, 0.81%); minimal statistical power would be lost due to the large sample size. Descriptive statistics were performed using total and relative frequencies for categorical variables.

Univariable logistic regression analyses were used to determine the characteristics associated with PCPs recommending DHT for self-management to patients, before the pandemic. For the period during the pandemic, these analyses were only done for those who were not prescribers before the pandemic. Univariable logistic regression was used for exploratory purposes to model the likelihood of recommending DHT according to each predictor variable. Reference categories were selected based on theoretical relevance and interpretability. For instance, ‘Ireland’ was chosen as the reference country due to its large sample size, while the 30–39 age group was selected as a baseline for comparison because it represents mid-career PCPs who are likely to be actively engaged in digital health adoption.

The univariable logistic regression analyses are presented as crude odds ratios (OR), with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) and p-values (P). Data analysis was performed using R Studio (v. 4.2.1).

McNemar’s Chi-squared (χ2) test was used to analyse the variation in recommending DHT for self-management at baseline and during the pandemic. p <.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

Participants

A total of 1,592 participants from the 20 countries completed the survey. The majority were female (60.9%, n = 969), over 40 years old (60.4%, n = 962), and were involved in teaching activities (63.9%, n = 1017). Most (62.4%, n = 994) practised in an urban setting and more than half (56.9%, n = 905) had over 10 years of experience working as a PCP. A full description of the sample is provided in Table 1.

Factors associated with recommending DHT for self-management before the pandemic

Univariable logistic regression analysis was performed for exploratory purposes (a detailed overview is provided in Table 2). Relative to those with over 20 years of practice, PCPs with fewer than 5 years of practice had lower odds of recommending DHT (OR: 0.61; 95% CI: 0.46 to 0.81, p < 0.001). Not being involved in teaching activities and working more hours per week were both associated with lower odds of recommending DHT (OR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.51 to 0.78, p < 0.001 and OR: 0.99; 95% CI: 0.98 to 1.0, p = 0.043, respectively). There were statistically significant differences by country, with PCPs from Australia, Chile, Colombia, France, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia, Spain, and Turkey having lower odds of recommending DHT than PCPs from Ireland (please see Figure 1). There was no evidence of differences in odds of recommending DHT by gender, age, or practice setting.

Variations in primary care physicians recommending DHT for self-management during the COVID-19 pandemic

More than half of the respondents (53.2%) recommended DHT before the pandemic (n = 848). Almost a quarter of those not recommending DHT at baseline started recommending them during the pandemic (22.7%, n = 169). These changes were not statistically significant (p=.215, Supplementary Table 1) and resulted in an overall small increase in the proportion of PCPs recommending DHT (54.7% vs 53.2%, +1.5%) during the pandemic.

Factors associated with starting to recommend DHT for self-management during the COVID-19 pandemic

Of the factors explored with univariable logistic regression models (Table 2), only country of practice was found to be associated with starting to recommend DHT during the COVID-19 pandemic. PCPs employed in Colombia and Italy had 5.7- and 3.6-times higher odds, respectively, of starting to recommend DHT than PCPs from Ireland.

Discussion

Principal findings

Our analyses showed that at baseline, the odds of recommending DHT for self-management were higher for PCPs involved in teaching, and lower for those with fewer than 5 years of experience as a PCP, who worked more hours per week, and for those employed in Turkey, Australia, Chile, Colombia, France, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia, and Spain, relative to those employed in Ireland. A small increase in the proportion of PCPs recommending DHT for self-management was observed during the pandemic (+1.5%), but this was not statistically significant. Higher odds of starting to recommend DHT during the pandemic were found for PCPs practising in Colombia or Italy, relative to those practising in Ireland, but no statistically significant differences were found for the other factors.

Comparison with existing literature

Factors associated with primary care physicians recommending DHT for self-management before the pandemic

We found that PCPs not involved in teaching activities had lower odds of recommending DHT for self-management. This is consistent with previous reports suggesting that having a role as an educator might have a positive role in innovation adoption [20,21]. This effect may be linked to greater exposure to new technological innovations through educational programs and contact with potentially tech-savvy trainees [22].

PCPs in Australia, Chile, Colombia, France, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia, Spain and Turkey were less likely to recommend DHT at baseline, with significantly lower odds (42–82%) than their counterparts in Ireland. This may reflect variations in country-level policies on the utilisation of DHT in primary care before and during the pandemic [23], as well as potential variations in health systems structure and digital maturity. Patient factors, as well as PCP factors not covered in this study, may also contribute to these differences [5].

No significant association was found between the age of the physician and the use of DHT at baseline. Previously published evidence has found inconsistent results; a mixed-methods study [15] suggested that professional characteristics, including age, have no impact on digital recommendation intentions, whereas a qualitative study has suggested that younger PCPs find DHT more favourable [24]. In our study, no significant association was found between gender and recommending DHT at baseline; previous studies reported conflicting results on the influences of gender on digital affinity and digital recommendation intentions [15,16,25]. We did find that PCPs with fewer than 5 years of work as a PCP had lower odds of recommending DHT at baseline than those with more than 20 years of experience. Previous evidence exploring this association is limited to small qualitative surveys including one interview-based study [26] that reported that PCPs with >20 years of experience are less willing to utilise DHT in clinical practice, contrasting with the findings of our study. Practice setting was not significantly associated with recommending DHT for self-management at baseline, contradicting a previously published report [24] which showed that PCPs in urban settings had significantly greater recommendations of health apps than PCPs in rural settings.

Factors associated with primary care physicians starting to recommend DHT for self-management during the pandemic

PCPs employed in Colombia or Italy were positively associated with starting to recommend DHT during the pandemic. This may reflect country-specific policies relating to the implementation of DHT in response to the pandemic. For example, Italy, which experienced one of the highest COVID-19 cases in early 2020, had a rapid engagement in digital transformation through the development of digital solidarity packages and policies to engage the diverse adoption of digital practices [27,28].

In contrast to the baseline analysis, teaching activity was not a significant predictor of starting to recommend DHT during the pandemic. A possible explanation may be the disruption in traditional teaching structures inflicted by the pandemic, as well as a potential dilution of its positive impact in the context of the significant system pressures experienced during the pandemic.

Variations in primary care physicians recommending DHT for self-management during the COVID-19 pandemic

Despite the well-recognised impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the use of technology supporting remote consultation in primary care, we only found a modest (+1.5%), non-significant increase in the proportion of PCPs recommending DHT during the pandemic. Most existing literature exploring variations in DHT use is dominated by DHT in the context of virtual consulting and reported a drastic rise in uptake [29]. Our results are therefore consistent with the findings of a scoping review [11], which showed DHT implemented during the first wave of the pandemic were dominated by telemedicine at 84.9%, while other technologies such as symptom trackers, and educational platforms used for self-management had a more modest adoption (4.8% and 1.6%, respectively).

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the factors influencing PCPs’ recommendation of DHT for self-management using data from multiple countries. Previous studies were limited to individual technologies and small sample sizes from one region. We provide an international perspective by including a relatively large sample size of 20 countries, thus increasing the potential for generalisability. A comprehensive set of characteristics, both at individual and setting levels, was collected and analysed, allowing us to go beyond the few predictors analysed in the past [15,16,24].

However, some limitations must also be considered when interpreting our findings. We used an online self-reporting survey reliant upon convenience sampling; this choice may have introduced both selection and reporting bias. Although the snowball sampling method helped with recruiting hard-to-reach groups, this may have resulted in participants with similar characteristics. This may have been mitigated somewhat by the large sample size and geographical variations. Although we were able to include PCPs from 20 countries, the actual number of observations per country was limited. The disparities in the number of responses received from the various countries (e.g. Ireland at 16% vs. all other countries ranging from 3 to 6%), are also likely to have a distorting factor on our findings. The survey being conducted during the early stage of the pandemic may have impacted the findings and their relevance going forward as more technologies have been implemented since. Furthermore, the survey design increased the risk of recall bias that could result in PCPs under- or over-estimating DHT recommending, and social desirability bias risk raised from over-reporting positive behaviours.

Finally, while this study benefits from exploring a multitude of DHT for self-management, ecological fallacy may arise from the inferences made from an aggregated level that may not be representative of all six DHT for self-management analysed in this study.

Future research

Future research should consider a differentiated analysis of specific DHT categories using multivariate modelling approaches, such as generalised estimating equations, to provide deeper insights into factors influencing the recommendation of distinct DHT, as well as expanding the range of technologies, evaluated to other clinically relevant solutions (i.e. apps for general health behaviour change, chronic disease management, and medication adherence support). Future research could capitalise on routinely collected data in countries that are currently reimbursing digital prescriptions (e.g. the progressive ‘Digitale Gesundheitsanwendungen’ (DiGA) approach recently adopted in Germany) [9,30]. Furthermore, additional efforts could examine how other technological advancements such as generative artificial intelligence, have influenced current attitudes towards digital health adoption since the time of data collection.

Implications for practice

It has become increasingly evident that modern healthcare providers require DHT training so as to best leverage the novel digital tools available effectively [31,32]. The World Health Organisation identified digital health training as a priority to accelerate the adoption of DHT by health professionals and highlighted the urgent need to develop new approaches for embedding digital health teaching digital health in undergraduate curricula [32]. Equally, there is a pressing opportunity for General Practice/Family Medicine training programmes to consider how to strategically incorporate digital health into their training curricula. Doing so would help prepare and support PCPs by familiarising them with DHT and thus enable PCPs to better recommend them to their patients. Likewise, government regulatory bodies or medical associations could endorse – and potentially reimburse – technologies that have proven their value, so that PCPs have evidence and institutional backing when recommending patients to DHT [5,10].

Conclusion

Our research highlights the importance of teaching involvement by PCPs as a key factor influencing the recommendation of DHT and the significant role that PCPs can play as educators in driving innovation and promoting the adoption of DHT among patients. While the findings shed light on key factors influencing DHT recommendation, it also emphasises the need for further investigations into other potential drivers at both the clinician-patient level and the national health system level. Moreover, exploring variations in DHT adoption across different countries can facilitate cross-national learning and enhance the implementation strategies employed to address health inequalities and alleviate the burden on overburdened primary care systems. By gaining a deeper understanding of these factors, we can improve implementation approaches to the adoption of DHT and ultimately use DHT to mitigate health disparities and alleviate the strain on primary care systems.
