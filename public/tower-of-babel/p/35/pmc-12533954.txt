“It really is HPV that’s pulling us down”: Findings from a health plan quality improvement learning collaborative targeting the HEDIS IMA measure
Authors: Shaylen Foley, Ashleigh Flowers, Jennifer Isher-Witt, Katherine Crawford, Jennifer Nkonga, Michelle Burcin, Anne H. Gaglioti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine could prevent 37,000 HPV-related cancers annually in the U.S. yet uptake is suboptimal. Health plans cover 95% of children, implement quality improvement (QI) interventions, and report the Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS®) Immunizations for Adolescents (IMA) measure. The American Cancer Society (ACS) convened payors in the ACS HPV Vaccination Learning Collaborative from 2022 to 2024 in three 12-month cohorts. Participants set QI targets, implemented interventions, and joined best-practice sharing calls. ACS provided a data reporting tool, resources, and one-on-one support. Plans submitted aggregated vaccination data. We used Wilcoxon Signed Rank test to assess average rates pre- and post-intervention. Ten participants consented to 60-minute semi-structured interviews that we transcribed and analyzed thematically. In 2022, we engaged 27 plans (35 service lines) from 19 states with 264,089 enrolled 13-year-olds. In 2023, participants represented 14 plans (16 service lines) from 11 states with 127,554 enrolled 13-year-olds. The 2024 collaborative included 26 plans (39 service lines) from 17 states with 249,927 enrolled 13-year-olds. We saw significant percentage point (pp) increases in 2023 for HPV completion (+3.0pp, p < .01), meningococcal (+3.3pp, p < .05), tetanus, diphtheria, & pertussis (Tdap) (+4.8pp, p < .01), and IMA (+3.0pp, p < .01) and in 2024 for HPV completion (+3.0pp, p < .001), Tdap (+1.7pp, p < .05), and IMA (+2.0pp, p < .001). Qualitative successes included intervention adaptations, age 9 initiation, data use, and collaboration. Barriers included project duration, turnover, and provider buy-in. We found the QI learning collaborative model acceptable to health plans and supported implementation of QI interventions to increase HPV vaccination.

Introduction

The human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine could prevent over 37,000 cancers each year in the United States, but 19 years after its introduction in 2006, uptake remains suboptimal and lags other routine adolescent vaccinations.1 Following the COVID-19 pandemic, ordered doses of the HPV vaccine dropped disproportionately compared to other adolescent or child vaccines, creating a 3.9 million dose deficit; the largest deficits were for publicly funded doses and rural geographies.2 These gaps will impact future cancer diagnoses and treatment. In 2024, 37 million children were enrolled with Medicaid or CHIP insurance.3 Health plans can reach both adolescents and vaccinating providers to provide education, incentives, and other targeted public health interventions to increase HPV vaccination uptake.4 Health plans can also reach adolescents who lack connection to a medical home and may be missed through traditional outreach by health systems.5

Health plans may prioritize HPV vaccination to save costs. There are six types of cancer preventable by the HPV vaccine, including cervical, anal, penile, oropharyngeal, vaginal, and vulvar cancer.6 Recent estimates of the cost of HPV-related cancer care found $93,272 for the 2-year incremental direct costs of cervical cancer, $134,045 for oropharyngeal cancer and $141,095 for vaginal cancer.7 Other studies have found the costs related to HPV-related cancers are high.8–12 Supplemental analysis found the annual burden of HPV-related cancer care exceeded 2.9 billion dollars (USD 2020).7 None of these studies assessed the full economic cost of HPV-related cancers, including indirect costs like lost wages or productivity, out-of-pocket costs for transportation or caregiving, or opportunity costs due to the time patients and families spend on treatment and caregiving.13 These assessments also fail to quantify the long term financial burden and financial distress caused by a cancer diagnosis, referred to as financial toxicity.14 Routine screening is not available for five cancers attributable to HPV (anal, penile, oropharyngeal, vaginal, and vulvar cancers), which are often detected later when treatment costs are higher. The HPV vaccine is the best way to prevent HPV-related cancers. Individuals vaccinated for HPV also have reduced costs associated with cervical cancer screening, cervical precancer treatment, treatment of anogenital warts, recurrent respiratory papillomatosis,15–19 and are at lower risk of pregnancy related outcomes like low birth weight and preterm birth.20–22

Health plans implement interventions directed at in-network providers and enrolled patients to increase adolescent vaccination.23,24 Effective HPV vaccination-specific interventions include provider- or system-focused efforts like education on strong recommendations, assessment and feedback in combination with education, reminder and recall systems, standing orders, and multicomponent interventions.23,25–28 Effective patient-focused interventions, aimed at increasing demand, include patient reminder and recall, group and individual education, small media, and financial incentives combined with reminders.26 Multicomponent interventions that target both patients and providers have been shown to have the most positive impact.26 Despite unclear evidence on provider incentives for vaccination, many plans already tie payments to performance measures that include HPV vaccination in value-based payment models.24 While health plans can implement most of these strategies, there is a need to better understand how to customize interventions to organizations like plans that do not deliver patient services, to explore how plans can target HPV vaccination specifically within the adolescent immunization platform, and to assess impact on vaccine uptake when plans undertake this work.24,29

Health plans are also well placed to promote the HPV vaccine series starting at age 9. Research has shown that earlier administration results in higher immune response,30,31 and improves the durability of this immune response over time.32,33 On-time vaccination prior to natural exposure to HPV is critical to vaccine effectiveness.34 Adolescents who start the series at younger ages are more likely to complete the series on time by age 13,35–37 a factor that may motivate health plans, who report adolescent vaccination coverage by age 13 as a part of the National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA)’s Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS®).

To prevent HPV-related cancers, lower the cost of HPV-related healthcare expenditure, and spur action among health plans with a focus on starting the HPV series at 9, the American Cancer Society (ACS) convened health plans in the ACS HPV Vaccination Learning Collaborative from 2022–2024. The purpose of this work is to assess the effectiveness of the learning collaborative on increasing HPV vaccination rates and to explore health plan successes and challenges when implementing evidence-informed interventions.

Materials and methods

The following section details the implementation components of the national learning collaborative and the mixed-methods evaluation approach used to assess its outcomes.

Learning collaborative objectives

ACS developed the learning collaborative to educate and activate health plans on HPV vaccination, to create a space for shared learning, to provide plan staff with tools and resources, and to catalyze health plans to implement quality improvement (QI) evidence-informed interventions to increase HPV vaccination.

Learning collaborative recruitment and structure

This project recruited health plan QI staff to participate in a 12-month learning collaborative. Participants served state-specific geographies. There were no limitations by geography, plan membership/company size, or insurance type. Most plans joined by invitation from an ACS staff partner based on perceived interest. Some plans recruited brand-affiliated plans from other states. Sample size was determined pragmatically by enrollment, rather than by statistical power considerations. Participants agreed to complete a Data and Reporting Tool (DART) for project planning and data collection. Participants set quality improvement targets for their 12-month project, implemented multicomponent interventions aimed at increasing HPV vaccination, and joined ACS-facilitated quarterly best-practice sharing calls. ACS provided plans with resources, one-on-one programmatic support, and the opportunity to learn from other health plans. Learning collaborative evaluation framework

We conducted a mixed-methods evaluation using the CDC Framework for Evaluation in Public Health.38 We developed an evaluation planning matrix with questions, measures, data sources, and domains (Appendix A, Table A1). This manuscript will detail our findings to evaluate the program objectives.

Quantitative data collection

Participants completed the DART pre-intervention, mid-intervention, and post-intervention (Appendix A, Table A2). Pre-intervention we collected plan demographics, service line vaccination rates, a self-assessment of current adolescent vaccination practices, and project goal setting. Post-intervention instruments collected implemented interventions, vaccination rates, and feedback. The DART was designed following formative interviews and expert input to align with similar QI tools at ACS.39,40 We piloted it in 2021. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Vanderbilt University.41,42 Plans reported vaccination data for up to four targeted service lines. Service lines, or “products,” are distinct categories of insurance for specific patient groups. Plans self-categorized targeted service lines into commercial, exchange, Medicaid, or other. Aggregated vaccination rates followed the HEDIS Immunizations for Adolescents (IMA) measure: the percentage of the plan’s active patients who turned 13 during the measurement period who were up-to-date with HPV, meningococcal, and Tdap vaccines on or by their 13th birthday.43 We measured the following outcomes: HPV ≥1 (HPV initiation): Eligible adolescents with one or more doses of the HPV vaccineHPV ≥2 (HPV completion): Eligible adolescents with two or more doses of the HPV vaccineMeningococcal: Eligible adolescents with one dose of meningococcal vaccineTdap: Eligible adolescents with one dose of tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis (Tdap) vaccineIMA: Eligible adolescents with two or more HPV doses, one meningococcal dose, and one Tdap dose

Quantitative data quality review and analysis

We reviewed vaccination data at each timepoint using standardized data quality checks to make service line exclusions (Appendix A, Figure A1). We notified plans with issues for corrections. We excluded data for entire service lines or specific outcomes. All included service lines had to have a high-quality value reported for HPV completion. After exclusions, we analyzed data from 35 service lines in 2022, 16 in 2023, and 26 in 2024. We excluded all midpoint data for the purposes of this manuscript.

We calculated descriptive statistics for demographic and intervention characteristics across participating plans. We calculated change in vaccination rates using service line as the unit of observation. To test the hypothesis that vaccination rates changed significantly across participating service lines from pre-intervention to post-intervention, we used Wilcoxon Signed Rank test and a critical α = .05. We used non-parametric statistical tests after a visual scan of the small sample suggested a non-normal distribution. We did not correct for multiple comparisons since we considered each vaccination rate a separate hypothesis test. Cohen’s d for repeated measures was used to compute effect size for vaccination rate change. Since there were continuing and unique plans participating each year, we conducted analysis for each year as separate cohorts. Due to the sample size and because each plan could select a customized package of evidence-informed interventions, we did not conduct analysis to assess the impact of individual interventions. Our overarching goal was instead to assess whether implementing a combination of interventions resulted in increased HPV vaccination rates.

We ran two additional sensitivity analyses. First, we descriptively compared vaccination rates by service line type: Medicaid, commercial, exchange, and other (we combined the last two categories due to sample size). Second, we assessed whether outcomes differed among returning plans in 2023 and 2024 compared to first time participants. The first analysis used a Kruskal – Wallis equality-of-populations rank test analyses and a critical α = .05. The second used a two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann – Whitney) test and a critical α = .05. We considered each vaccine a separate hypothesis test.

Due to the limited power of our service line sample size, we also conducted separate mixed-effects logistic regression models for each vaccination outcome and year in the hope of better accounting for the large patient volume of participating service lines. Findings from this analysis were largely congruent to what is presented and are therefore not detailed in this manuscript. Stata version 15.1 was used to export, clean, and analyze data presented.44

Qualitative data collection & analysis

We conducted ten in-depth interviews with participating health plans in fall 2022. Interviews included plan representatives who participated in the 2022 collaborative. Interviews were conducted in English, included one or two participants, and approximately 60 minutes. Two team members joined in each call, one to lead the conversation and another to take notes. All interviews were audio and video recorded using Microsoft Teams.45 We obtained verbal consent prior to recording. Health plans were chosen using convenience sampling, though we attempted to vary plan structure, size, and geography. We used a semi-structured interview guide to explore (1) how participation in the learning collaborative changed the way plans implement quality improvement interventions to improve HPV vaccination rates, (2) the processes, interventions, and efforts health plans used to impact HPV vaccination among their enrolled patients during their 12-month project, including successes and challenges, and (3) participant reactions and feedback on the learning collaborative structure, resources, and programming. ACS team members also reviewed qualitative write-in data from the DART where plans described individual interventions and summarized key themes and quotes. DART write-in data included participants from all submissions for each cohort.

We hired FTI Consulting to analyze qualitative interview data. ACS shared notes and transcripts through a secure file-sharing link. FTI used a rapid qualitative analysis approach, closely reviewing all summary notes and transcripts and looking for repetitive themes. They summarized key themes and extracted illustrative quotes from transcripts. ACS extracted themes and content from the FTI analysis and merged it with the review of DART write-in responses. The final themes reported in the manuscript reflect an additional level of thematic analysis that incorporated write-in response data, quantitative findings, and the program evaluation questions.

Institutional review board

The Institutional Review Board of Morehouse School of Medicine waived ethical review and approval for both years of learning collaborative evaluation data collection because the proposed activities are program evaluation (2229482-1, 2124431-2). Morehouse approved a separate protocol institution for the 2022 qualitative interviews after expedited review (1972235-1).

Results

Between 2022 and 2024, ACS engaged health plans in a learning collaborative focused on activating and resourcing plans to implement 12-month quality improvement (QI) projects aimed at increasing HPV vaccination uptake, creating a QI team, implementing evidence-informed interventions, and participating in learning collaborative calls.

These plans reported a total of 19.5 million in-network patients, with 264,089 enrolled 13-year-olds (median of 2,880). The 2023 collaborative was smaller, comprising nineteen plans representing eleven states and approximately 9.7 million individuals of all ages and 136,373 13-year-olds (median of 4,263). In 2024, 26 plans from 17 states reported 1.2 million 9 to 13-year-old in-network patients (median of 30,516). Most participating plans reported an affiliation with a large national plan, such as Elevance (formerly Anthem), UnitedHealth Group, Aetna, Cigna, or Humana. Far fewer reported an affiliation with an integrated delivery network (IDN). Most plans reported using multiple data sources when reporting vaccination data, including claims, state immunization registries, and electronic health records. Nearly all plans included ages 9–10 in their project focus, as recommended by ACS. In 2022, 3 (11.1%) plans continued participation from the 2021 pilot project. In 2023 and 2024, 11 (78.6%) and 9 (34.6%) plans, respectively, returned to participate.

Participating plans submitted data for 39 service lines in 2022, 19 service lines in 2023, and 39 lines in 2024. Across all years, Medicaid service lines were the most common (43.6% in 2022, 63.2% in 2023, 69.2% in 2024). Service line volume ranged from in size from nine 13-year-olds enrolled in a small exchange plan to 61,957 enrolled in a large Medicaid plan. Medicaid lines were the largest, representing around 74.4% to 86.6% of active 13-year-olds across cohorts. Other participating service lines that represented fewer adolescents overall included commercial, exchange, or other (often the Children’s Health Insurance Program, or CHIP).

Interview participants represented Medicaid Managed Care Organizations (n = 7) and Commercial/Exchange plans (n = 3). Three represented more than one service line type. The majority of 2022 interview participants held a quality improvement position with job titles like Quality Improvement Specialist or Clinical Quality Program Director. Half had a specific sub-focus such as provider outreach, member outreach, or data and reporting.

In 2022, HPV series initiation (HPV ≥1 dose) for 27 service lines increased +1.5pp (SD 6.1) from an average of 59.1% to 60.6% (p = .41). HPV series completion at 35 service lines increased from 30.5% to 31.2% (+0.7pp, SD 5.6, p = .80). Meningococcal and Tdap also had average increases of +1.8pp (SD 7.3, SD 7.7; p = .26, p = .23). None of the increases were significant. Cohen’s d showed a small positive effect for HPV initiation (+0.25), meningococcal (+0.25), and Tdap (+0.23).

In 2023, average HPV completion rates at 16 service lines increased significantly by +3.0 pp (SD 4.1), from 31.3% to 34.2% (p = .004). Implementation had positive spillover effects on meningococcal (+3.3 pp, SD 5.7, p = .044), Tdap (+4.8pp, SD 7.4, p = .002), and IMA (+3.0 pp, SD 3.9, p = .005). We found moderate effect sizes for meningococcal and Tdap (+0.58 and +0.65) and borderline large effect for HPV completion and IMA (+0.76 and +0.77). The effect sizes for HPV completion and IMA indicate a 70.5% chance that a service line’s vaccination rate is higher post-intervention compared to pre-intervention.46

In 2024, average HPV initiation for 22 service lines increased by +1.9pp (SD 6.9, p = .08) and HPV completion for 26 lines increased by +2.0pp (SD 1.6, p < .001). Tdap and IMA also had significant increases, +1.7 pp (SD 3.6, p = .046) and +2.0pp (SD 1.9, p < .001). Cohen’s d showed a large effect for HPV completion and IMA (+1.25 and +1.06), indicating an 81.2% chance that a service line’s HPV completion rate is higher post-intervention.

Sensitivity analysis to determine variation in vaccination rate change across different service line types did not detect significant differences (Appendix B, Table B1). Additional sensitivity analysis to see whether participation in consecutive cohorts impacted changes in vaccination rate changes found borderline significance for HPV initiation in 2023, indicating a trend that plans participating in for a second year in 2023 had higher magnitude rate change than first-time participants (Appendix B, Table B2).

Qualitative themes and quotes from the ACS HPV vaccination learning collaborative with health plans.

Across these interventions, health plans described efforts to focus on HPV vaccination at age 9 with both providers and patients. Most efforts expanded ongoing interventions to the full recommended age range approved by CDC. Participants highlighted provider education and outreach as key strategies to improving HPV vaccination uptake at age 9, especially leveraging materials that concisely present relevant data with actionable takeaways. Plans broadened their patient outreach interventions to include this age group in communication efforts like birthday cards, texting campaigns and patient newsletters.

Plans also reported that examining their adolescent vaccination data at project setup helped drive their interest, priorities, and interventions during the collaborative. Plans discussed previous prioritization of the IMA measure and the adolescent vaccination platform more generally. However, most plans had not previously assessed or focused on HPV initiation (which is not part of HEDIS reporting), nor had they looked at their HPV completion rates very critically. Until prompted, some of our participating plans did not realize the degree to which HPV completion was linked to their HEDIS overall IMA performance.

We also asked health plans to describe how non-HPV QI work is accomplished within their teams and how they decide on objectives for their work, providing deeper insight into a plan’s vaccination results and intervention decisions. The majority described an internally collaborative approach involving multiple departments within the health plan. QI teams were small but often worked with other teams like marketing, program evaluation, care management, provider outreach, and compliance. Some plans reported using this type of collaboration in their HPV-vaccination project during the learning collaborative. Two plans described how being a subsidiary of a larger plan provided additional resources, including access to nationally coordinated intervention components like branded mailers or QI workshops.

Health plan quality improvement implementation challenges

Health plans encountered several implementation challenges during the learning collaborative. Plans described challenges when implementing patient-focused interventions, including limited patient engagement in plan patient websites/portals and incorrect or missing contact information. When targeting providers or health systems, plans reported challenges with staff turnover and the decline in well-child visits after the COVID-19 pandemic. When implementing interventions to increase initiation of the HPV vaccine at age 9, some plans reported experiencing provider pushback on this recommendation.

Health plans described needing more time than 12-months to implement targeted interventions. Medicaid plans described delays due to approval processes for new materials. Many plans in the 2022 collaborative continued their efforts in 2023 to complete implementation efforts initiated in 2022. We saw an influx of new plans in 2024. We did sensitivity analysis to see whether participation in consecutive cohorts impacted changes in vaccination rate changes. While we were not able to detect significance for most outcomes, there was borderline significance for HPV initiation in 2023 (Appendix B, Table B2).

Other implementation challenges included changes in plan leadership. Four of the plans interviewed were unable to implement any HPV vaccination interventions during the 2022 learning collaborative due to challenges with organizational buy-in, limited resources, and capacity. A few individuals reported having limited or no support from other departments or a broader team at their health plan and instead attempted to move their HPV project and efforts forward individually.

Response and feedback to the learning collaborative structure

Health plans shared feedback about participating in the learning collaborative during interviews. They found the learning collaborative to be useful, noting that the resources, varied perspectives, and presentations around best practices and peer-learning were valuable. They also appreciated the collaboration and partnership with their ACS representative. Interviewees requested additional health plan-oriented HPV vaccination resources, such as a best practice guide and a data visualization template to use with providers.

Discussion

From 2022 to 2024, the ACS HPV Vaccination Learning Collaborative demonstrated that health plans can benefit from sharing promising practices and leveraging disaggregated HPV vaccination data to improve overall IMA performance and increase HPV vaccination coverage. Participation supported plans to look more closely at HPV vaccination data and implement both new and modified QI interventions specific to the HPV vaccine series. Health plans engaged readily in the learning collaborative and reported satisfaction, demonstrating acceptability of this approach. These efforts contributed to positive vaccination rate changes among the 13-year-old patients in targeted service lines in 2023 and 2024. Interviews with participating plans and review of intervention descriptions also demonstrated key implementation successes and challenges that can inform the design and implementation of future health plan learning collaboratives.

Vaccination outcomes

Average adolescent vaccination rates improved among the 13-year-old patients covered by targeted service lines, particularly in years two and three of the collaborative. National HPV completion rates among Medicaid plans reported for the HEDIS IMA measure have dropped year over year, from 37.8% in 2020, to 36.1% in 2021, 35.6% in 2022, and 35.8% in the most recently available data for 2023.43 This trend is supported by other evidence.2,47 Our findings suggest that participating plans may have mitigated the overall negative impact of the pandemic on HPV vaccination completion coverage among enrolled adolescent patients. The exception to this was the HPV initiation rate measure, which saw non-significant increases and even a decline among participating plans all years of implementation. This finding may be due to the impact of the pandemic, particularly among newly enrolled plans in 2023. It is also possible that plan efforts to improve HPV initiation at age 9 were not reflected in the age 13 data reported and are a lagging indicator as children who were age 9 in the years 2022–2024 did not reach age 13 during the project period. We also observed increased uptake of meningococcal and Tdap vaccinations in our sample, a trend observed previously in quality improvement projects aimed at improving HPV vaccination.39,48

Key lessons of the learning collaborative implementation

The learning collaborative model was acceptable and adopted by health plan partners.

Health plans compete for Medicaid contracts and beneficiary enrollment. In this program, ACS served as a third-party convener providing access to subject matter experts. Participating plans demonstrated high levels of engagement throughout the collaboratives, as evidenced by consistent attendance and data sharing. We believe the positive feedback in interviews and successful enrollment and ongoing engagement of health plan partners in the learning collaborative demonstrate the implementation outcomes of acceptability and adoption.49 A prior ACS quality improvement collaborative has used similar data to assess implementation science outcomes.50 We recommend that future health plan collaboratives focused on improving HPV vaccination rates pilot data reporting on ages 9–12. This model enabled ACS to support implementation of ACS guidelines in practice and could serve as a template for other cancer risk modification initiatives with health plan partners.

Disaggregation of the HEDIS IMA measure helped drive quality improvement work

Health plans report the industry HEDIS IMA measure annually to assess adolescent vaccination coverage by the 13th birthday. The learning collaborative provided plans with both the motivation and technical assistance to disaggregate HPV series doses from the overall measure. In interviews, plans reported this disaggregation process useful to their quality improvement project. The collaborative also highlighted a key limitation of the IMA measure more generally: it does not assess vaccination rates below age 13. Increasing capacity to measure uptake for ages 9, 10, 11, and 12 will allow plans to better gauge progress on the IMA measure. ACS QI efforts with health systems have stratified data on ages 9–10, 11–12, and 13 since 2020 to drive targeted interventions.39

Health plans need time to drive positive outcomes

We found that health plans require more than a year of implementation to see improvements in adolescent vaccination coverage. Health plans are large, which necessitates time to set up and implement meaningful interventions across departments and measure impact. We recommend future health plan quality improvement projects use a minimum of a two-year timeline.

Plans must adapt interventions to target HPV vaccination uptake specifically

Health plans often target the entire adolescent vaccination platform or focus on improving well-child visits. Our interviews suggest that the learning collaborative helped plans customize interventions to HPV. Quality improvement teams collaborated internally to move interventions forward, while the learning collaborative fostered external collaboration with other plans and ACS. We found cross-cutting collaboration improved the feasibility of implementing multi-component interventions that were HPV vaccination specific.

Implementing interventions to promote HPV series initiation at 9 was possible

The learning collaborative promoted HPV series initiation at age 9. We were not certain health plans would be interested in or able to support this approach. Though some reported provider pushback, most plans expressed buy-in to promote age 9 initiation, and many reported successfully implementing interventions to promote, remind, and educate providers and parents on starting the HPV series at age 9. The most commonly reported efforts included provider education and outreach about starting vaccination at age 9 and expansion of patient communication efforts to include age 9.

Targeting Medicaid plans may help reach vulnerable or missed adolescents

Current threats to Medicaid insurance could impact an estimated 8.7 million people, including 600,000 children, and prove dire for cancer prevention of future generations.51 These vulnerable populations had the largest HPV vaccination deficits due to the COVID-19 pandemic.2 This learning collaborative model may be particularly relevant to Medicaid plans, whose enrolled patients are often reassigned from other Medicaid plans in the same state. This collaborative approach allows Medicaid plans in a single state or across states to synergize to improve adolescent and HPV vaccination coverage.

Health plans prioritized HPV vaccination implementation resources

Plans requested additional implementation guidance in their feedback. As a result, in 2023–2024, ACS’s Implementation Science team and the National HPV Vaccination Roundtable developed “Cancer Prevention Through HPV Vaccination: An Action Guide for Health Plans” to provide a framework for health plans interested in implementing quality improvement projects to increase HPV vaccination.52 In 2025, ACS released the “HPV Vaccination Communication Toolkit: A Resource for Health Plans” to provide outreach templates for plans.53

Future recommendations, strengths, and limitations

Future research is needed to assess differences in vaccination outcomes between service line type and patient volume to better inform programming. Structured studies with comparison groups and randomized designs may be useful to investigate the role of individual interventions on HPV vaccination change at plans. Lastly, future efforts should explore how this model may be adapted to other cancer content areas and to assess the cost and/or cost-effectiveness of implementing HPV vaccination interventions through plans.

This study had the following strengths: (1) Prior research has called for additional literature for “how to translate evidence into practice” and “efforts to study how intervention models can be implemented on a wide scale and adapted to different settings.”23 This programming aimed to adapt and disseminate existing implementation evidence on adolescent vaccination at health insurance plans, a novel partner with broad reach, using a learning collaborative model. We found that the learning collaborative model was acceptable and adopted by health plans and resulted in positive outcomes. (2) Health plans are largely left out of the literature even though most implement quality improvement initiatives. This publication adds real-world evidence to demonstrate the potential impact of these quality improvement initiatives on population health goals and metrics. (3) Including three consecutive cohorts in our analysis allowed for greater insights and learning about the complexities and opportunities for health plan partnership conveners.

This research has several limitations. Data was aggregated at the service line level, limiting our sample size and power to detect significance. Submitted data also had issues with completeness and quality and had wide variation in patient volume. Health plans implemented combinations of interventions, preventing us from determining the individual interventions most effective at increasing uptake. Without a comparison group, it is difficult to contextualize or generalize the vaccination increases we observed, meaning that our findings may be subject to internal validity threats such as maturation. Lastly, we do not know the cost of implementing these quality improvement projects and therefore cannot determine the return on investment for health plan decision makers.

Conclusions

Our findings support our conclusion that a QI learning collaborative model was an acceptable intervention for health plans and supported implementation of QI interventions to increase adolescent HPV vaccination. These efforts contributed to positive vaccination rate changes in HPV series completion and the overall adolescent vaccination IMA measure at targeted service lines in the 2023 and 2024 cohorts. This approach enables plans to work within their existing QI structure using provider- and patient-focused interventions with population-level cancer prevention reach and with opportunities for adaptation to other cancer risk modification or early detection initiatives.
