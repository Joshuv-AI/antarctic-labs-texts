Rotating visual spaces induce behavioral biases consistent with recalibrated heading integration in humans
Authors: David G. Behery, Diana M. Green, Viktor H. Klimczyk, Michael Pigin, Alexandra T. Keinath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryOrganisms from flies to mice to humans maintain an internal representation of heading by integrating their movements across time. To avoid compounding errors, this integration must be precisely calibrated. Recent evidence from rodents indicates that this calibration is inherently plastic, as immersion in rotating visual spaces induces a bias in neural heading integration that outlasts that experience. Here, we show that humans also exhibit behavioral biases consistent with recalibrated heading integration under similar circumstances. Leveraging immersive virtual reality, we find that rotating visual spaces biases human heading judgements for up to minutes following the experience. The direction of the bias matches the direction of the rotation, while the magnitude scales with its speed. Trials with more head movement accrue more bias. Visual motion alone is sufficient to induce a bias. These results are consistent with the plastic recalibration of human heading integration by rotating visual spaces, mirroring neural findings in rodents.

Introduction

The ability to navigate is a fundamental necessity of all mobile organisms. To do so, many species, including flies,1 rodents,2 bats,3 and humans4,5,6,7 rely on an internal representation of heading instantiated by dedicated neural circuitry, often referred to as a neural compass. With their neural compass, these navigators maintain an estimate of their current heading by integrating their head movements across time, a process known as angular integration or path integration.8,9,10,11,12,13,14 To function as intended, this process must be carefully calibrated such that each head movement yields the proper adjustment of the internal representation. Otherwise, the error will grow and grow with each new movement, and the navigator will quickly become misoriented.

While extensive experimental and computational evidence demonstrates that stable visual cues can correct misorientation in the neural compass,15,16,17,18,19,20,21 recent evidence indicates that angular integration itself can be recalibrated by consistently rotating visual spaces.22 In these experiments, activity was recorded from large populations of neurons to characterize the neural compass as mice freely explored a circular platform. A circular screen surrounded this platform, displaying a single visual landmark (a white stripe). When this landmark was slowly rotated (e.g., 1.5° s−1 or 3° s−1), the orientation of the neural compass slowly rotated to match. Crucially, when the landmark was removed, the orientation of the neural compass continued to rotate for tens of seconds, with the direction and speed of this rotation reflecting the previous experience. This persistent rotation demonstrates that the integration of head movements in the mouse neural compass is not static, as often assumed, but can instead be recalibrated by experience with a rotating visual space.

Given the extensive evidence of similar representations of heading across species, we hypothesized that human heading judgements should also exhibit behavioral signatures of recalibration following experience with rotating visual spaces. We tested this possibility in three experiments leveraging untethered immersive virtual reality (VR). We found that the slow rotation of a square walled environment induced a bias in heading judgments, which can persist for minutes when the walls are removed. The direction of the bias matched that of the wall rotation, and the magnitude of the bias scaled with the speed of the rotation. Trials with more head movement accrued more bias following rotation. Replacing the walls with an optic flow stimulus composed of many briefly visible moving dots induced a smaller but significant bias in the direction of rotation. Together, these results demonstrate that human heading judgements exhibit biases following experience with rotating visual spaces, which are consistent with the recalibration of heading integration, mirroring neural findings in rodents.

Results

A sound-guided task to assay human heading judgments in immersive virtual reality

We developed a task to assay human heading judgements following experience with rotating and stable visual spaces where auditory cues guided the participant to turn, remember, and retrieve headings. In these first experiments, the visual space consisted of square gray walls, a tan ground plane, and a rotationally symmetric blue skybox with the participant standing in the center of this space (Figure 1A). On hum trials, the participant heard the steady hum of a singing bowl, i.e., an instrument typically used in meditative practice, which produces a consistent, calm, loop-able tone. The volume of this hum was determined by the difference between the participant’s current heading and a secret target heading. The goal of the participant was to turn to face the secret target heading, where the volume of the hum was reduced to total silence. Maintaining this heading for half a second triggered the next trial to begin. On test trials (Figure 1B), the participant first heard a bell, indicating that they should remember their current heading. Next, the walls disappeared, and the participant completed a hum trial which forced them to turn away from their remembered heading. At the completion of the hum trial, a chime indicated that they were to retrieve their remembered heading by returning to this heading and pressing a button on the handheld remote. Following retrieval, the walls returned, and participants completed one or two hum trials before the next test trial.

Each block began with 30 hum trials to provide the participant with a few minutes of experience with the environment prior to any test trials. This was followed by 25 test trials. In Block 1, the walls were always stable. In Block 2, the walls slowly rotated around the participant clockwise at either 1° s−1 or 2° s−1, randomized across participants (n = 30 participants per speed group). Note that during test trials, all heading judgements were made in the absence of the walls; as all other aspects of the environment were rotationally symmetric, any influence of wall rotation must reflect an influence of past experience rather than the current sensory experience. All participants experienced the stable environment first because we suspected that experience with the rotating environment might carry over to future blocks, a hypothesis that we test in a later experiment. In debriefing, no participants reported noticing the manipulation, though we cannot rule out this possibility entirely.

Slowly rotating walls induce a persistent bias in human heading judgments

In the stable block, participants made quick and accurate heading judgements, with mean absolute error well below 90° (random guessing) and typical median test trial durations of 10–15 s, including both the outbound and return trajectories during which the walls were absent (Figures 1C and 1D). Next, we compared heading judgments on test trials during stable and rotation blocks to determine whether recent experience with the rotating visual space biased these judgments. To do so, we computed the signed difference between the target and retrieved headings. Then, for each block, we computed the circular mean of these differences as our measure of bias, excluding trials with absolute bias >90° (random guessing; 2.3% of all trials, 2.53% of stable block trials, 2.07% of rotation block trials).

We first conducted an ANOVA of bias with block (stable or rotation) and speed group (1° s−1 or 2° s−1) as factors, and with participant identity included as an additional random factor. This analysis revealed that biases differed significantly between blocks (F(1,58) = 22.61, p = 0.0000135) with a marginally significant interaction between blocks and rotation speed group (F(1,58) = 3.70, p = 0.059; Figure 1E). No main effect of rotation speed group was observed (F(1,58) = 1.96, p = 0.167). Post-hoc comparisons showed that biases differed significantly between blocks in both rotation speed groups (paired t-test: 1° s−1, t(29) = 2.40, p = 0.0231; 2° s−1, t(29) = 4.14, p = 0.000276). Heading judgments during stable blocks were not significantly biased (t-test vs. 0: 1° s−1, t(29) = 0.27, p = 0.793; 2° s−1, t(29) = 0.43, p = 0.672). Heading judgments during rotation blocks were significantly biased in the direction of the wall rotation (t-test vs. 0: 1° s−1, t(29) = 2.17, p = 0.0384; 2° s−1, t(29) = 6.87, p < 0.0001). The marginally significant interaction between rotation speed groups was driven by a greater bias during 2° s−1 rotation blocks than 1° s−1 rotation blocks (2-sample t-tests: t(58) = 2.265, p = 0.0273), while stable blocks did not significantly differ (t(58) = 0.129, p = 0.8975). This demonstrates a scaling of bias with the speed of the wall rotation. Notably, biases were roughly 50% of what would be expected from full recalibration given trial durations (i.e., Figure 1D) and rotation speeds. Our results are thus consistent with partial recalibration following removal of the recalibrating cue as observed in rodent neural assays.14,22,23,24 Similar results were observed when normalizing biases by the variability in judgments for each participant (Figure S1).

In rotationally symmetric environments such as those confronting participants during test trials, the navigator must rely on path integration alone to maintain their heading estimate. Under these conditions, if rotating spaces recalibrate human heading integration specifically then we would expect test trials with more total head movement to accrue larger biases after experiencing this rotation.25,26,27 Moreover, because our test trials included self-guided head scanning, total head movement and trial duration were partially decorrelated (within-participant correlation: median r = 0.760, interquartile range [0.667, 0.832]). Thus, insofar as total head movement and trial duration are dissociable, we would expect to find a greater dependence of bias on total head movement than trial duration.27 We next tested these possibilities.

Even under stable conditions, humans vary in their heading integration biases.26 Because of this, we first focused on how the relationship between head movement and bias changed between blocks within a participant. To this end, we fit a general linear model between total head movement and bias across trials within each block separately for each participant (Figure 1F). We then compared the slopes and intercepts of these fits between blocks. If rotating visual spaces recalibrates heading integration specifically, then we should observe a positive change in slope during rotation blocks relative to stable blocks, with no change to fit intercepts. This is, in fact, what we observed (Figure 1G). Moreover, greater positive change in slope correlated with greater bias during the rotation block (Figure 1H), as well as with greater increase in bias between stable and rotation blocks (Figure 1I). These findings are thus consistent with recalibrated heading integration as the source of bias induced following experience with rotating visual spaces.

To directly contrast the potential influence of total head movement and trial duration on bias, we next took an ANOVA-based approach. Here, bias on each trial was explained on the basis of total head movement, trial duration, block (stable or rotation), and speed group (1° s−1 or 2° s−1), with participant identity included as a random factor. Bias, total head movement, and trial duration values were z-scored within participants prior to this analysis to account for variability between participants in these measures. This ANOVA revealed (Table S1): (1) a significant main effect of block reflecting the increase in bias during rotation blocks, (2) a significant interaction between block and speed group reflecting the larger increase in bias during rotation blocks for the 2° s−1 speed group, and (3) a significant three-way interaction between total movement, block, and speed group reflecting the stronger relationship between head movement and bias during rotation blocks, especially for the 2° s−1 speed group. All other effects, including those that included trial duration as a factor, did not reach significance. These outcomes echo the results of our previous analysis and are consistent with the recalibration of human heading integration in rotating visual spaces, persisting at least on a timescale of seconds.

Induced biases can persist for minutes in rotationally symmetric visual spaces

In the prior experiment, biases in heading judgements were assessed only seconds after the most recent experience with the rotating space. In the next experiment, we tested whether these biases could persist over longer timescales. To do so, we repeated our initial experiment with the following changes. Each participant (n = 34) experienced a 2° s−1 rotation block followed immediately by 25 test trials during which the walls never reappeared and the visual world remained rotationally symmetric (Figures 2A and 2B). We refer to these latter trials as the extinction block. The rotation block lasted on average 17.08 ± 4.48 min (mean ± standard deviation), with an average of 11.60 ± 3.10 min (mean ± standard deviation) of exposure to the rotating walls during this time. If biases induced by the rotation of the visual space persist on longer timescales, then we might expect significant positive biases in both the rotation block and the extinction block that followed. To test this prediction, we computed bias as in our first experiment, excluding trials with absolute bias >90° (2.6% of all trials, 2.12% of rotation block trials, 3.06% of extinction block trials). Consistent with this prediction, we observed positive biases in both blocks (t-test vs. 0: rotation block, t(33) = 4.425, p = 9.9e-5; extinction block, t(33) = 2.234, p = 0.0324), with a significant decrease between the blocks indicating a weaker but lingering bias on average during extinction trials (paired t-test: t(33) = 2.315, p = 0.0270; Figure 2D). Similar results were observed when normalizing biases by the variability in judgments for each participant (Figure S1).

We suspected that extinction block trials shortly after the rotation block might exhibit stronger biases that decay across the block, echoing similar dynamics in mice.22 To test this possibility, we first divided our data into groups of five trials for each block and computed bias separately for each group of trials (Figure 2D). During the rotation block, significant positive biases were observed for each trial group (t-test vs. 0; group 1: t(33) = 3.410, p = 0.0017; group 2: t(33) = 2.986, p = 0.0053; group 3: t(33) = 3.472, p = 0.0015; group 4: t(33) = 2.951, p = 0.0058; group 5: t(33) = 4.835, p = 0.00003; uncorrected). During the extinction block, we observed significant positive biases for the first, second, and fourth groups of trials (group 1: t(33) = 2.782, p = 0.009; group 2: t(33) = 2.244, p = 0.032; group 4: t(33) = 2.165, p = 0.0377; uncorrected), with numerically positive biases which did not reach statistical significance for the remaining groups of trials (group 3: t(33) = 0.368, p = 0.7152; group 5: t(33) = 0.584, p = 0.5631; uncorrected). Given that the first 10 trials typically took participants 3 to 5 min to complete following their last experience with the rotating walls (Figure 2C), these findings demonstrate that prior experience can continue to bias heading judgements for at least minutes. Moreover, this pattern suggests that biases weakened over the course of the extinction block. To characterize this directly, we next estimated the time course of bias decay by fitting an exponential to bias as a function of the number of trials and the time since the participant last experienced the rotating walls (see STAR Methods). In both cases, biases were estimated to decay on a timescale of minutes (Figures 2F and 2G). This decay was specific to the extinction block (Figures 2F and 2G). Together, these findings demonstrate that tens of minutes of experience with a rotating visual space induces a bias in human heading judgements that can persist for minutes.

Consistent visual motion alone is sufficient to induce a heading judgment bias

Previous work has demonstrated that visual cues can orient the neural compass and behavioral heading judgements,1,22,28,29 with evidence suggesting that landmarks experienced as stable and the layout of boundaries play particularly important roles.20,30,31,32,33,34,35,36,37,38 It is therefore possible that perceptually stable cues or environmental boundaries also play a privileged role in the calibration of heading integration. Conversely, optic flow information alone can be sufficient for path integration and heading estimation.24,39,40,41,42 Thus, it may be possible to induce the recalibration of heading integration with consistent visual motion alone, even in the absence of stable environmental features. We tested these possibilities in a follow-up experiment.

In this experiment, we repeated our initial design except that we replaced the square walls with a cylindrical optic flow stimulus. That is, the participant (n = 30) was surrounded by small black dots in random locations forming a cylinder around the participant. Each dot disappeared after 2 s and was replaced by a new dot in a random location on the surface of the cylinder so that the space always contained 250 dots. During the first block, these dots remained in the same location for their entire lifespan. During the second block, all dots moved coherently at 2° s−1 in a counterclockwise direction. Similar to the walls in the original design, all dots disappeared during test trials and reappeared following retrieval. Despite the lack of persistent visual cues throughout the experiment, participants remained accurate, with a median stable block accuracy of 18.45° and an interquartile range [15.52°, 30.21°] (t-test vs. 90°: t(29) = 36.6877, p = 7.4264e-26).

If consistent visual motion is sufficient to recalibrate heading integration, then we would expect to observe significant heading judgment biases in the rotation block but not the stable block with this stimulus. On the other hand, if stable visual cues or boundaries are necessary to recalibrate heading integration, then we would not expect to observe significant biases in either block. To test these possibilities, we computed bias as in our first experiment, excluding trials with absolute bias >90° (3.07% of all trials, 3.20% of stable block trials, 2.93% of rotation block trials). We observed no significant bias in the stable condition (t-test vs. 0: t(29) = 0.150, p = 0.881508) but a significant bias in the rotation condition (t(29) = 2.504, p = 0.018151), with a significant difference between the two blocks (paired t-test: t(29) = 3.635, p = 0.001068). Of note, however, the bias we observed in the rotation block with the optic flow stimulus was marginally weaker than the bias we observed when rotating walls at the same speed (2-sample t-test: t(58) = 1.745, p = 0.0863; Figure 3C). Normalizing biases by the variability in participants' judgments tightened these results (Figure S1). Discussion

To keep track of their heading, many navigators integrate their head movements across time. To remain accurate, this integration must be carefully calibrated. Although often assumed to be static, recent neural evidence from rodents indicates that heading integration can be plasticly recalibrated in rotating visual spaces. Here, we tested whether human heading judgments also exhibit behavioral signatures of plastic recalibration following experience with rotating visual spaces. Across three experiments, we find that rotating visual spaces induces a bias in heading judgements which outlasts the experience with these spaces. The direction of the bias matches that of the rotation, while the magnitude of the bias scales with the speed of the rotation. Trials with more head movement accrued more bias following the rotation experience, consistent with a specific impact on the integration of head movements across time. Biases can persist for minutes following the experience in rotationally symmetric spaces. Finally, visual motion alone is sufficient to induce recalibration, albeit less effectively than the rotation of stable environmental boundaries. Altogether, these results are consistent with the dynamic recalibration of human heading integration by recent visual experience, mirroring neural findings in rodents.

The experiments reported here were motivated by recent work characterizing the neural compass of mice in rotating visual spaces.22 In those experiments, a rotating visual cue induced drift in the orientation of the neural compass in the anterodorsal thalamic nucleus, matching the rotating cue. After this cue was removed, the orientation of the neural compass continued to drift for tens of seconds, with the direction and magnitude determined by the recent experience. Although our results are entirely based on behavioral judgements of heading, the striking similarity across species and assays suggests that our results may be the product of similar neural computations in the human head direction network. Nevertheless, how these neural and behavioral biases come to be remains to be determined. One hypothesis suggested by prior computational modeling involves plastic changes to vestibular integration within the head direction circuit,22 possibly within or upstream of the anterodorsal thalamic nucleus. Alternatively, these biases might reflect motor adaptation due to the consistent mismatch (i.e., overestimation and underestimation) between turns in the direction of versus against the rotated visual space.43 Such motor adaptation could inform heading integration in the neural compass or produce behavioral biases independent of this circuit. Ultimately, disentangling these various possibilities in freely moving humans currently presents a technical challenge. Given our findings, however, we would predict that rotating visual spaces should induce similar biases in behavioral assays of heading in rodents. Future work in rodents might thus combine neural circuit manipulations with neural recordings and/or behavioral assays to tease apart these competing alternatives.

Precise calibration is not a need unique to heading integration. For example, many navigators, including rodents and humans, rely at least in part on the path integration of movements through space to maintain a representation of their current location.8,9,10,12,44 Recent work has shown that similar visual cue manipulations can recalibrate the neural integration of movements through space in rats.23 Moreover, optic flow alone is sufficient to induce this recalibration.24 Based on these findings, we would expect human judgements of distance and location to be similarly malleable. Together, these results suggest that visual experience plays an important role in calibrating the integration of navigationally relevant variables more generally. Given its ubiquity, it is likely that dynamic calibration confers advantages over a static mapping of movement to representational change. For example, visual recalibration might allow the navigator to better adapt to changing environments or to recover following an insult that impacts their ability to move or the neural circuits mapping these movements.

Finally, our findings and previous related findings demonstrate that visual experience can recalibrate the integration of heading and distance, but whether this role is unique to vision is unclear. Previous work has shown that visual input can play a strong and even unique role in determining behavioral and neural assays of orientation,13,20,32,34,35 suggesting that vision may play a privileged role in calibration as well. However, how other external (e.g., olfactory and auditory) and internal (e.g., vestibular and motor efference) cues contribute to integration calibration is yet to be determined. The gradual extinction of bias we observed in a rotationally symmetric visual space indicates that some other input does play a role in calibration, likely vestibular or motor efference cues, given the lack of other symmetry-breaking external cues.12,45 Moreover, the weaker orientational bias we observed following the rotation of a visual motion stimulus relative to the rotation of walls at the same speed demonstrates that not all visual experience is equivalent. This may reflect inherent perceptual differences between walls and the object-like dots that made up the visual motion stimulus. This may also reflect differences in the perceived stability of the stimuli, as the dots popped into and out of existence while the walls were relatively more stable. Future work might contrast the contributions of visual and nonvisual cues to determine whether and how these various inputs combine to calibrate heading integration.

Limitations of the study

Our findings were motivated by, and are parsimoniously consistent with, the dynamic recalibration of heading integration by rotating visual spaces. Nevertheless, our ability to draw firm conclusions about the mechanistic bases of our results is inherently limited by our behavioral assay. As noted in the discussion, the recalibration of heading integration could be accomplished in different ways, and our findings might also be consistent with other mechanisms operating in tandem or independent of recalibration. Our results are also limited to the induction of bias by visual stimuli. Future work might address the contributions of other modalities.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Experiment 1 (walls) included data from 60 participants, with a gender distribution of 33 female and 27 male, and an age distribution of 19.71 ± 2.33 years (mean ± standard deviation). Experiment 2 (extinction) included data from 34 participants, with a gender distribution of 21 female and 13 male, and an age distribution of 20.53 ± 3.22 years (mean ± standard deviation). Experiment 3 (optic flow) included data from 30 participants, with a gender distribution of 17 female and 13 male, and an age distribution of 19.93 ± 3.34 years (mean ± standard deviation) ranging 18 to 36 years. Sample sizes were determined prior to the experiments based on power estimates derived from other related VR studies of angular integration in humans.26,27 In all experiments, approximately 5 to 10 participants ended the experiment early due to nausea or dizziness; data from these participants were not analyzed. All participants provided informed consent in accordance with the Institutional Review Board of the University of Illinois Chicago (STUDY2023-0394). Participants were paid or received course credit for their participation in these experiments. Participants were recruited through the psychology subject pool and via fliers advertising the study around the psychology department.

Method details

Data acquisition

All experiments were carried out on an untethered HTC VIVE XR Elite head-mounted display with a resolution of 1920 x 1920 pixels per eye yielding a field of view of approximately 110° and a refresh rate of 90 Hz. Prior to the experiment, interpupillary distance and focus for both eyes were adjusted for each participant. All experiments were run via local execution on the head-mounted display. Experiment 1 (walls) took 43.26 ± 12.49 min (mean ± standard deviation) to complete, Experiment 2 (extinction) took 36.22 ± 9.23 min (mean ± standard deviation) to complete, and Experiment 3 (optic flow) took 48.19 ± 16.10 min (mean ± standard deviation) to complete. Experiments were typically completed on a single battery charge, but sometimes batteries were replaced between blocks. Throughout the experiment, the position and heading in all three dimensions relative to the virtual environment were timestamped and recorded at a rate of 90 Hz in a comma separated file written to the local storage of the headset. Participant responses were registered by pressing the primary A button on the VIVE remote. All analyses were derived from these data.

Virtual environments

All experiments were developed in the Unity game engine (2022.3.8f1) by the experimenters. In all experiments, the ground plane consisted of a uniform tan plane. The skybox consisted of a rotationally symmetric clear blue sky. A single light source illuminated the environment from above the center of the environment. In walled environments, the walls consisted of a uniform grey texture bounded the environment at 15 m x 15 m and were 1.875 m tall. In the optic flow experiments, the stimulus was composed of 250 randomly distributed black dots. Each dot was a sphere with isotropic diameter 0.3 m. Dot locations were randomly initialized along a 10 m x 10 m cylinder surrounding the participant, with height randomly sampled on the range of 0.1 m to 3 m from the ground. All dots had a lifespan of 2 seconds and were replaced by a new random dot after expiring.

Experimental design

All experiments followed a similar blocked design as described in the main text. Each block consisted of hum trials and test trials. On hum trials, a constant hum (a singing bowl) played over headphones with its volume determined by the difference between the participant’s current heading and a secret target heading. The goal of the participant was to turn to face the secret target heading, where the volume of this hum was reduced to total silence. Maintaining this heading (within ±7.5°) for half a second triggered the next trial to begin. On test trials, the participant first heard a bell, indicating that they should remember their current heading. Next, any polarizing visual cues disappeared and the participant completed a hum trial which forced them to turn away from their remembered heading. At the completion of the hum trial, a chime indicated that they were to retrieve their remembered heading by returning to this heading and pressing a button on the handheld remote. Following retrieval, any polarizing visual cues returned in a position consistent with the ongoing rotation (except during extinction), and participants completed one or two hum trials before the next test trial.

Each block began with 30 hum trials to acclimate participants to the visual environment (except during extinction), followed by 25 test trials. All secret target headings were sampled randomly from a uniform distribution with the constraint that each heading be at least 45° away from the previous target heading. Participants were instructed to rotate their whole bodies when turning to mitigate the use of non-path integration strategies and were monitored by the experimenter for compliance. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes open throughout the experiments, and all participants confirmed that they remained visually alert during debriefing. Participants were offered a break between blocks in most experiments except in the extinction experiment, where extinction test trials followed immediately after rotation block test trials. All experiments began with five practice hum trials and five practice test trials with visible text instructions in the presence of stable cues to ensure the participant understood the task.

Quantification and statistical analyses

Data analysis

For all analyses, the position and heading data recorded at a rate of 90 Hz were loaded into MATLAB (R2024b) and analyzed using custom written scripts. The primary outcome for the majority of analyses was bias in heading judgements. Bias was computed as follows. First, we computed the signed difference between the target and retrieved headings, with positive values chosen to match the direction of bias expected from the manipulation (Clockwise during wall experiments; counterclockwise during the optic flow experiment). Then for each block we computed the circular mean of these differences, divided by the circular standard deviation of these differences, yielding a z-scored measure of bias. We chose to z-score our bias values because participants varied in their precision, and z-scoring reduced the overestimation of bias in subjects with more variable judgements. Any heading judgement more than 90° away from the target (chance for random guessing) was excluded from analysis.

To fit the decay of bias during the extinction block as a function of trial number and minutes since experience with the walls, we defined our decay function as:(Equation 1)b(x)=se−τxWhere b(x) is bias expected at the x th timepoint, τ is the constant which determines the rate at which bias decays, and s is a scalar. We fit both τ and s to minimize the mean squared error between b(x) and our actual data using MATLAB’s fmincon. To estimate the variability of our fits, we repeated this process while bootstrapping our sample by randomly resampling our participants with replacement 1000 times. The fits reported in Figure 2 reflect the variability across these 1000 bootstrapped samples.

Statistics

All statistical tests are noted as they appear in the text and figure captions, and reported outcomes are uncorrected and two-tailed unless otherwise noted. The unit of analysis was either participant or trial, as noted in the main text. Exact n’s are reported for each analysis as they occur in the main text. Box-and-whisker plots denote the minimum and maximum (whisker), the interquartile range (box), and median (central line). In ANOVAs, the outcomes of comparisons with random factors included as control variables are not described. All statistical analyses were carried out in MATLAB (R2024b).
