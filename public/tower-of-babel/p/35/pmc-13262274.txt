Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) following spinal surgery pose a substantial morbidity burden, prolonging hospitalization, increasing healthcare costs, and necessitating unplanned re-operations (Peng et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2023). While overall SSI rates in spinal procedures vary significantly depending on procedural complexity, patient comorbidities, perioperative management, and environmental factors, lumbar microdiscectomy carries a comparatively lower risk than instrumented spinal fusions (Tunthanathip et al., 2019; Hopkins et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020; Pull ter et al., 2010; Olsen et al., 2003; Fei et al., 2016; Anderson et al., 2017; Pull ter et al., 2009). However, even within this lower-risk cohort, postoperative SSI remains a clinically significant concern due to its potential to necessitate early re-operation. Although re-operation following SSI is a rare event, its consequences are profound, underscoring the need for precise risk stratification to enable early intervention. Given the inherent challenges of predicting rare events, developing models that accurately differentiate patients at elevated versus lower risk remains a critical step toward advancing precision medicine in spinal surgery.

Both, traditional statistical approaches, as well as machine learning models have identified key predictors of SSI, such as diabetes, obesity, prolonged operative time, smoking, and revision surgery. However, these models have shown variable predictive performance, with reported areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) values ranging from 0.76 to 0.99, raising concerns about their generalizability across different patient populations. Compared to traditional statistical methodologies, algorithms such as random forests and gradient boosting can leverage complex interactions between patient-specific and procedural factors, capturing nonlinear relationships that conventional statistical models often overlook (Turlip et al., 2025; Dagli et al., 2023). Despite these advances, few studies have specifically targeted the prediction of re-operation following SSI in lumbar microdiscectomy, highlighting a critical gap in the literature.

Thus, the objective of this study was to develop and validate a machine learning (ML)-based predictive model for 30-day re-operation following SSI in lumbar microdiscectomy patients. By leveraging the American College of Surgeons (ACS) National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) database and implementing advanced feature selection and model optimization techniques, we aimed to refine risk stratification in neurosurgery and contribute to the broader discourse on rare event prediction. Integration of comparative development and validation of traditional statistical models is ongoing.

Materials and methods

Guidelines

The design and reporting of this study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD + AI) guidelines (Collins et al., 2024; von Elm et al., 2007).

Data source and patient population

This observational study utilized de-identified patient-level data from the ACS NSQIP Participant Use Data File (PUF). The ACS NSQIP PUF is a standardized, publicly accessible dataset containing aggregated, de-identified patient records from participating hospitals across the United States. As per the ACS NSQIP Data Use Agreement, no institutional review board approval was required, and no patient consent was necessary. Patients were included if they met the study's target population criteria, defined by the presence of Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 63030 as the primary procedure and the availability of all required study variables for their respective years of data collection. Exclusion criteria included cases with missing data for any variable of interest, specifically if the variable was not collected at all in that year. This ensured that all included cases had consistent and complete temporal availability of variables, preventing bias due to year-to-year variations in data collection.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the development and validation of a machine learning algorithm to predict early post-operative re-operation following a post-operative SSI. Secondary outcomes included an interpretability analysis using SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP).

Variables

The following variables were extracted for each eligible patient: age (years), sex, race, Hispanic ethnicity, smoking status, American Society of Anesthesiologists' (ASA) score, Revised Risk Analysis Index (RAI-rev) score, functional status, history of congestive heart failure (CHF), history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), history of medication-requiring hypertension, history of steroid use, history of bleeding disorder, history of insulin-dependent diabetes, history of non-insulin-dependent diabetes, history of dialysis, history of disseminated cancer, history of ascites, pre-operative laboratory values (albumin [grams per deciliter {g/dL}], alkaline phosphatase [units per liter {U/L}], bilirubin [milligrams per deciliter {mg/dL}], blood urea nitrogen (BUN) [milligrams per deciliter {mg/dL}], creatinine [milligrams per deciliter {mg/dL}], hematocrit (HCT) [percent {%}], international normalized ratio (INR), platelet count [1000 cells per microliter {1000/μL}], partial thromboplastin time (PTT) [seconds {s}], serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT) [units per liter {U/L}], sodium [millimoles per liter {mmol/L}], and white blood cell count (WBC) [1000 cells per microliter {1000/μL}]), operative time (minutes), post-operative ventilator dependence, post-operative outcomes (any post-operative SSI, superficial SSI, deep-incisional SSI, and organ/space SSI), overall 30-day re-admission rate, re-admission associated with post-operative SSI occurrence, any re-operation within 30 days, and re-operation following SSI.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis followed a rigorous, multi-stage analytical framework incorporating robust statistical methodologies and machine learning validation techniques. Initial baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics, with continuous variables presented as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. For categorical data, univariate analyses were performed using the Pearson χ2 test, with Fisher's exact test applied when any cell in the contingency table had an expected count less than 5. For continuous data, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normal distributions, while the unpaired t-test was used for normally distributed data. Analysis revealed minimal missingness (<5% across all variables combined), but to ensure methodological rigor, multiple imputation was performed using chained equations with 5 imputations.

To prevent data leakage, imputation was conducted within each cross-validation fold. The machine learning pipeline incorporated nested cross-validation with an inner loop for hyperparameter optimization and feature selection, and an outer loop for unbiased performance estimation. Feature selection was performed through Bayesian optimization to identify the most predictive variables while controlling for multicollinearity. Model performance was evaluated using a comprehensive set of metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity (recall), specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1-score, Brier score, AUROC, area under the precision-recall curve (AU-PRC), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) - the latter being particularly valuable for imbalanced datasets as it provides a balanced measure of binary classification performance even when classes are of very different sizes. Following an initial evaluation of multiple models, XGBoost with Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was selected for further optimization, demonstrating the highest predictive performance and effectively mitigating class imbalance challenges. Various classification thresholds were examined, including the default 0.5, Youden's index-optimized threshold, F1-score optimized threshold, and the MCC optimized threshold. To ensure robust estimates of model performance, 95% confidence intervals were calculated using bootstrapping with 10,000 resamples. SHAP analysis was conducted to ensure model interpretability, quantify the relative contribution of individual features, and identify the most influential variables driving predictions, including SHAP summary plots and mean absolute SHAP value plots. All statistical analysis and the figures were conducted and generated using Python, version 3.9.6 (Python Foundation, Wilmington, DE).

Results

Study population

After screening the ACS-NSQIP database (N = 11,634,075), 79,870 patients met the inclusion criteria, with 462 (0.6%) undergoing re-operation following post-operative SSI (Fig. 1). Univariate frequentist statistical analysis revealed statistically significant differences in multiple demographic, clinical, and perioperative factors. The re-operation following SSI group had a statistically significant increase in the proportion of female patients, American Indian or Alaska Native race, smokers, ASA class 3 and 4, total functional dependence, COPD, medication-requiring hypertension, steroid use, bleeding disorders, insulin-dependent and non-insulin-dependent diabetes, dialysis, ascites, and longer operative time, compared to the control group (Table 1). Additionally, they had significantly lower albumin and hematocrit levels, higher alkaline phosphatase and PTT, and increased WBC count. Postoperatively, they exhibited significantly higher rates of superficial, deep incisional, and organ/space SSI, overall 30-day readmission, SSI-related readmission, and re-operation within 30 days (Table 2).

Primary outcomes

Validation revealed robust model performance across all classification thresholds. Accuracy remained consistent at 0.993 (95% CI: 0.992–0.993) across all thresholds (Table 3). Sensitivity ranged from 0.924 (95% CI: 0.815–0.989) at the default threshold to 0.985 (95% CI: 0.959–1.000) at the MCC-optimized threshold, while specificity remained stable at 0.993 (95% CI: 0.992–0.994). The PPV varied slightly, ranging from 0.436 (95% CI: 0.428–0.443) to 0.437 (95% CI: 0.429–0.446), while the NPV remained at 1.000 across all thresholds. The F1-score ranged from 0.593 (95% CI: 0.564–0.619) at the default threshold to 0.605 (95% CI: 0.596–0.615) at the MCC-optimized threshold. The MCC increased from 0.632 (95% CI: 0.590–0.661) to 0.654 (95% CI: 0.643–0.664), and the Brier score was consistently low at 0.006 (95% CI: 0.006–0.007). The AUROC remained at 0.996 (95% CI: 0.996–0.996) across all thresholds, and the AU-PRC ranged from 0.426 (95% CI: 0.385–0.471) to 0.426 (95% CI: 0.402–0.452) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study determined whether an ensemble machine learning model, optimized through advanced feature selection and enhanced with synthetic data augmentation, could accurately predict early post-operative re-operation following SSI in patients undergoing lumbar microdiscectomy. The rarity of this complication may have had previously impeded prediction efforts, yet our findings indicated that robust machine learning approaches can indeed reliably predict such rare events.

Recent literature on ML models for predicting SSI in surgery has not demonstrated consistently superior performance compared to traditional models, despite their theoretical advantages in capturing complex interactions and nonlinear relationships (Piebpien et al., 2024). Advances in model architecture and pipeline optimization strategies have enhanced predictive accuracy, particularly in rare event modeling, showing promising results (Fernández et al., 2018; Chawla et al., 2002; Srinivas and Katarya, 2022; Akiba et al., 2019). While early re-operation following SSI in lumbar microdiscectomy is rare, our study demonstrates that robust ML methodologies can still achieve strong discrimination. Existing models have primarily focused on higher-risk spinal procedures, making this study one of the first to apply optimized ML techniques for successful rare event prediction in spinal surgery (Scheer et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2018; Joshi et al., 2019; Jacobs et al., 2019). These developments reinforce the growing role of ML in surgical risk stratification and highlight the shift toward data-driven, procedure-specific predictive modeling in spine surgery.

Firstly, we observed that the XGBoost model, coupled with SMOTE, provided high discriminatory power (AUROC 0.996; Brier score 0.006). This performance suggested that generating synthetic minority-class samples effectively countered the scarcity of SSI-related re-operations, a key hurdle in rare-event modeling. Additionally, balancing the dataset in this manner preserved predictive accuracy, indicating that robust oversampling techniques can be applied to other similarly low-incidence contexts. Consequently, this strategy provides a viable framework for advancing predictive modeling in rare-event surgical outcomes. Third, the SHAP analysis offered insight into essential predictors—such as existing SSI, race, smoking status, diabetic status, and RAI-rev scores—cementing their pivotal role in risk stratification. Although these factors had individually been studied, their combined influence in an optimized model broadened our understanding of why certain patients were more vulnerable to re-operation. Importantly, such transparency can enable more personalized perioperative counseling, with surgeons and patients collaborating on tailored interventions or follow-up plans.

Secondly, Performance remained stable across multiple thresholds, including those optimized by Youden's index, F1-score, and MCC, indicating that threshold selection did not markedly compromise sensitivity or specificity. This consistency suggested that clinicians could fine-tune decision points to accommodate diverse clinical settings without substantially altering predictive performance. Hence, threshold optimization offers practical flexibility, aligning model outputs with institutional or resource-based priorities.

Unsurprisingly, the SHAP analysis highlighted a handful of dominant features—particularly existing SSI—as key drivers of re-operation risk. Despite the class imbalance, these variables held significant explanatory power, illuminating why certain patients were more prone to negative outcomes. Incorporating richer clinical variables, detailed operative data, and nuanced imaging markers could further refine predictive accuracy, ensuring that individuals at highest risk are identified promptly and managed with targeted perioperative strategies.

Limitations

Even though this study presents multiple strengths, it must be interpreted within the context of its limitations. First, the retrospective design introduced the possibility of residual confounding, as unmeasured variables could have influenced both predictors and outcomes. Second, although the ACS-NSQIP database provides robust data, it may lack the spine-specific granularity—such as nuanced operative details or radiographic parameters—needed for accurate rare-event modeling. Third, while data imputation and synthetic sample generation were carefully performed, these methods may introduce biases if the original data distribution was not fully captured. Fourth, external validation across diverse healthcare settings, each with distinct patient demographics and clinical protocols, is necessary to confirm generalizability. Finally, despite strong internal validity, a prospective assessment—potentially incorporating a silent phase after external validation and using a granular institutional registry, with or without re-training—would offer a more definitive measure of real-world impact on patient outcomes and surgical decision-making.

Conclusions

This study showed that an optimized machine learning approach, combining advanced feature selection with synthetic data augmentation, accurately predicted the rare event of early post-operative re-operation following SSI in lumbar microdiscectomy. Although external validation and prospective implementation remain essential to confirm real-world benefit, these findings underscore the feasibility of data-driven risk stratification to improve outcomes and guide surgical strategies in spine care.
