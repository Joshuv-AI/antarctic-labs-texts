Proof-of-concept study on Bruch’s membrane opening minimum rim width (BMO-MRW) and optic nerve head morphology in people living with HIV
Authors: Lungile M. Buthelezi, Alvin J. Munsamy, Khathutshelo P. Mashige
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHIV and antiretroviral therapy (ART) are known to affect ocular health, yet the impact on optic nerve head parameters remains underexplored. This study investigates ONH structural parameters in HIV-positive adults using a novel Bruch’s Membrane Opening-Minimum Rim Width (BMO-MRW) method, which offers a precise assessment of ONH integrity.MethodsThere were 62 eyes of HIV on ART (HIV-ART), 20 eyes of HIV not on ART (HIV-N-ART), and 82 HIV negative healthy controls (HIV controls). Participants underwent Optical Coherence Tomography to measure optic nerve head morphology using Bruch’s Membrane Opening Minimum Rim Width protocol. Generalized estimating equations and effect size calculations compared thickness differences between groups. Multivariable regression and odds ratio models examined associations between ONH markers and clinical variables (CD4 count, CD4%, ART duration, viral load).ResultsCompared to controls, the HIV-N-ART group demonstrated significantly thicker ONH parameters, particularly APS-ppRNFL thickness in the inferior sector (Cohen’s d = 1.02). No significant differences were observed between HIV-ART and controls. In the HIV-ART group, higher CD4 counts were negatively associated with global BMO-MRW and APS-ppRNFL thickness (β = -0.10, p = 0.03), whereas in the HIV-N-ART group, higher CD4 counts positively correlated with inferior temporal BMO-MRW and APS-ppRNFL thickness (β = 1.08, p = 0.01). ART duration showed mixed associations with ONH measurements in treated individuals.ConclusionThis proof-of-concept study demonstrates that all HIV factors (especially CD4 count) may influence ONH morphology that can be used as potential structural biomarkers. These findings highlight the potential of optic nerve head measurements as biomarkers for assessing the structural impact of HIV and ART. ART-treated individuals exhibited ONH thinning, while ART-naïve participants showed thicker nerve fibre layers, possibly reflecting early inflammatory or compensatory mechanisms before treatment initiation. these preliminary findings support further investigation to validate these associations and explore their clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04349-5.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a significant global health challenge, with 39.0 million people living with the virus in 2022, including 37.5 million adults [1]. Antiretroviral therapy (ART), the standard treatment for HIV, combines multiple drug classes targeting different stages of the viral lifecycle to achieve viral suppression. By suppressing viral replication, ART restores immune function and significantly extends the lifespan of people living with HIV (PLHIV). Currently, 77% of the global adult population has access to this ART [2]. However, as HIV has transitioned from an acute, life-threatening infection to a chronic condition, PLHIV are increasingly affected by long-term complications such as neurocognitive disorders and ocular abnormalities [3]. The causes of these complications remain unclear, but both HIV and prolonged ART use appear to contribute to these abnormalities [4]. With the extended life expectancy of PLHIV, understanding the long-term impact of chronic HIV infection and ART exposure on various physiological systems, including the optic nerve head, has become critical.

HIV has been associated with structural changes in the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), with studies reporting peripapillary RNFL (ppRNFL) thinning in immunocompromised individuals with CD4 counts below 100 cells/mm3, as well as in those with CD4 counts above this threshold [5, 6]. These findings suggest that RNFL thinning may be an early marker of ocular HIV pathology. However, more recent studies present conflicting evidence, showing that immunocompetent individuals (CD4 counts ≥ 350 cells/mm3) exhibit thicker RNFL layers compared to age- and gender-matched controls [4, 7]. These discrepancies highlight the potential of optic nerve head morphology as a non-invasive retinal imaging biomarker for detecting early HIV-associated changes, particularly in cases where retinal disease may be subclinical [7–9]. While most existing studies have focused on ART-treated individuals, there is a significant gap in research on structural alterations in immunocompetent PLHIV who have not yet initiated ART. Investigating such changes could provide important insights into the direct impact of HIV on the optic nerve head before any treatment-related effects.

The Bruch’s membrane opening minimum rim width (BMO-MRW) has emerged as a valuable biomarker for assessing and monitoring the optic nerve [10–12]. This is due to its ability to provide a precise evaluation of the neuroretinal rim and the axonal structure of retinal ganglion cells [13]. The BMO-MRW measures the shortest distance from the inner opening of Bruch’s membrane to the internal limiting membrane. Studies suggest that BMO-MRW has superior diagnostic utility, correlating more strongly with visual field examination results than conventional metrics [14–16]. Given the limited evidence on optic nerve head changes in ART-naïve, immunocompetent PLHIV, this proof-of-concept study aims to provide preliminary evidence for the potential use of optic nerve imaging as an early marker of ocular involvement in HIV.

Methods

This was a proof-of-concept study designed to explore potential optic nerve head morphological alterations in PLHIV. Three groups of adult individuals were recruited: The HIV group was stratified into 31 HIV-infected individuals on ART for > 6 months (n = 62 eyes) (HIV-ART) and 10 HIV-infected individuals not on ART (n = 20 eyes) (HIV-N-ART). The third Group had 41 HIV-negative controls (n = 82 eyes) (HIV control). Recruitment took place in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. All participants were tested at Grey’s Hospital. Participant health records were used to confirm HIV status and clinical history. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Selection criteria

Participants had to be adults (≥ 18 years) with no history or presence of ocular disease, ocular opportunistic infection, chronic or acute systemic comorbidities, with a best corrected visual acuity of 6/7.5 or better (measured by Snellen chart). To minimize optical or biometric confounders affecting OCT measurements, participants were excluded if they had significant refractive errors, defined as myopia greater than − 6.00 dioptres (D), hyperopia greater than + 5.00 D, astigmatism greater than − 3.00 D, or anisometropia greater than ± 1.50 D. Keratometry was performed on all participants using the Tomey OA-2000 biometer. For inclusion in the HIV-ART and HIV-N-ART groups, CD4 counts had to be ≥ 350 cells/mm³, and viral loads ≤ 1000 copies/mL. Participants in the HIV-N-ART group (newly diagnosed) were evaluated within 24 to 48 h of diagnosis and before initiating ART, to minimize the confounding influence of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) during the first week of treatment [17].

Data collection

All ophthalmic examinations, including OCT and slit-lamp assessments, were performed by a trained optometrist. All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, which included visual acuity testing, ocular biometry, and anterior segment evaluation using slit-lamp biomicroscopy. Fundus photography was performed to assess retinal and vascular health and rule out infectious retinopathy. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging was conducted using the Spectralis system (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Only high-quality scans (quality score ≥ 20) were included in the analysis.

Morphometric analyses of the optic nerve head

Standard peripapillary retinal nerve fibre layer (S-ppRNFL)

To assess conventional ppRNFL thickness, a 3.5 mm-diameter circular scan centered on the optic disc was acquired. The Heidelberg Spectralis software generated thickness measurements across four quadrants, six sectors, and a global average using the Garway Heath map.

Glaucoma module premium edition (GMPE) anatomic positioning system (APS): bruch’s membrane opening-minimum rim width (BMO-MRW)

Bruch’s membrane opening – Minimum rim width (BMO-MRW) and ppRNFL are surrogate parameters measuring retinal ganglion cell loss. Bruch’s Membrane Opening–minimum rim width provides the true anatomic optic nerve boundary and the most geometrically accurate measurement of the neuroretinal rim. The BMO-MRW is the minimum distance between Bruch’s membrane opening and the internal limiting membrane around the optic nerve. A smaller or decreasing BMO-MRW can be indicative of progressive optic nerve damage. To assess the thickness of the neuroretinal rim, 24 radial scans centred on the BMO are captured. The green arrow in Fig. 1A indicates the BMO-MRW, which is the shortest distance between BMO and the inner limiting membrane. The BMO area was computed automatically by the built-in Spectralis software. The BMO area refers to the cross-sectional area of the BMO at the optic nerve. The GMPE APS-based peripapillary retinal nerve fibre layer (APS-ppRNFL)

Utilizing the BMO-MRW as a reference, the GMPE APS module enabled anatomically aligned ppRNFL measurements [18]. The measurement of the ppRNFL using the BMO APS (APS-ppRNFL) measures three high-resolution ppRNFL scans of increasing sizes of 3.5 mm (3.5 mm APS-ppRNFL), 4.1 mm (4.1 mm APS-ppRNFL), and 4.7 mm (4.7 mm APS-ppRNFL) (Fig. 1B, C, D, respectively). The 4.1 mm and 4.7 mm scans provide reliable diagnostic measurements when the traditional 3.5 mm S-ppRNFL scan is confounded by pathology.

Data analysis

Data was organized in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS version 27. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and was found to be normally distributed. Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to evaluate differences in optic nerve head morphology across the groups. Given that measurements were collected from both eyes of many participants, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were employed to account for intra-subject correlation between eyes. GEE models were constructed for each optic nerve head parameter, with group (HIV-N-ART, HIV-ART, HIV-negative control) as the main predictor. HIV-negative controls served as the reference group. An exchangeable correlation structure was used, and models were adjusted for age and sex. Beta coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were reported. As this was an exploratory proof-of-concept study, effect sizes were emphasized alongside p-values to guide interpretation. Odds ratios were estimated to evaluate the likelihood of ONH parameter alterations in relation to HIV and ART status. Multivariable regression analyses were performed to assess associations between ONH parameters (including BMO-MRW and APS-ppRNFL thickness) and clinical variables such as CD4 count, CD4%, ART duration, and viral load. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 164 eyes from 82 participants were included: 62 eyes from HIV-ART (n = 31), 20 from HIV-N-ART (n = 10), and 82 eyes from HIV-negative controls (n = 41). All HIV groups showed immunocompetence. The mean CD4 count and ART duration for the HIV-ART group were 624.53 ± 209.86 cells/mm³ and 8.57 ± 4.59 years, respectively. The mean CD4 count of HIV-N-ART was 405 ± 60.00 cells/mm3. Other means for demographics and HIV factors are detailed in Table 1. All HIV patients were on Dolutegravir treatment at the time of the study. Axial length was examined to account for potential ocular magnification effects on optic nerve head measurements. No significant differences were found between groups, minimizing its impact as a confounder.

Optic nerve head thickness comparisons across groups

Mean ± standard deviation values of global and sectoral optic nerve head parameters (BMO-MRW, S-ppRNFL, APS-ppRNFL) by group are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Overall, the HIV-N-ART group showed the highest average thicknesses across parameters, while the HIV-ART group exhibited comparatively lower values.

The Bonferroni corrected ANOVA comparisons demonstrated significant differences in optic nerve head thickness among the study groups, with HIV-N-ART showing the greatest thickness compared to HIV controls. In S-ppRNFL, significant group differences were observed in the global (p < 0.001), superior (p = 0.035), inferior (p < 0.001), inferior nasal (p = 0.005), inferior temporal (p = 0.001), and temporal (p = 0.014) regions. For BMO-MRW, HIV-N-ART exhibited the thickest segments, with significant differences in the global (p = 0.043), superior nasal (p = 0.030), superior temporal (p = 0.040), and inferior nasal (p = 0.030) regions.

Further analysis of APS-ppRNFL at 4.1 mm and 4.7 mm diameters revealed that HIV-N-ART had the thickest global segment among all groups. At the 4.1 mm circle, the global thickness was 160.26 ± 15.51 μm (p = 0.005), while at the 4.7 mm circle, it was 87.90 μm (p = 0.001). The inferior nasal and inferior temporal sectors were consistently thicker in HIV-N-ART compared to both HIV-ART and controls across standard and APS-ppRNFL measures.

To assess the clinical relevance of these findings, Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated using controls as the reference group (Fig. 2). Effect sizes between HIV-ART and controls were generally small, suggesting minimal clinical relevance to the observed thickness differences. HIV-N-ART exhibited significantly greater effect sizes compared to HIV controls, particularly in the inferior regions. At the 4.1 mm circle, the global thickness demonstrated a large effect size (d = 0.86, CI: 0.36–1.36), with the largest thickness differences in the inferior (d = 1.23, CI: 0.71–1.75) and inferior nasal (d = 1.02, CI: 0.51–1.52) regions. S-ppRNFL

Global S-ppRNFL thickness was significantly greater in the HIV-N-ART group compared to HIV-negative controls (β = 10.16 μm, 95% CI: 3.18 to 17.14, p < 0.001). Sectoral differences included significantly increased thickness in the inferior (INF: β = 20.11, 95% CI: 7.70 to 32.52, p < 0.001), inferonasal (IN: β = 20.07, 95% CI: 2.60 to 37.54, p = 0.02), and inferotemporal (IT: β = 21.13, 95% CI: 5.34 to 36.92, p = 0.01) regions. The temporal sector also showed a modest but statistically significant increase (T: β = 8.87, 95% CI: 0.84 to 16.89, p = 0.03). No significant differences were observed in the HIV-ART group relative to controls in any region.

APS-ppRNFL (3.5 mm)

Thickness was significantly greater in the HIV-N-ART group in the inferior (INF: β = 17.82, 95% CI: 4.59 to 31.06, p = 0.01), inferonasal (IN: β = 20.60, 95% CI: 3.52 to 37.67, p = 0.02), and inferotemporal (IT: β = 14.45, 95% CI: 1.89 to 27.00, p = 0.02) sectors compared to HIV-negative controls. No significant changes were observed in the HIV-ART group.

APS-ppRNFL at (4.1 mm)

Both HIV-N-ART and HIV-ART groups showed significantly increased global ppRNFL thickness compared to HIV-negative controls (HIV-N-ART: β = 18.36, 95% CI: 8.41 to 28.31, p < 0.001; HIV-ART: β = 23.94, 95% CI: 20.70 to 27.19, p < 0.001). In the HIV-N-ART group, increased thickness was also observed in the inferior (INF: β = 16.45, 95% CI: 6.25 to 26.65, p = 0.002), inferonasal (IN: β = 20.82, 95% CI: 6.60 to 35.04, p = 0.004), and inferotemporal (IT: β = 14.45, 95% CI: 1.90 to 27.00, p = 0.024) sectors. No significant sectoral differences were seen in the HIV-ART group despite the global increase.

APS-ppRNFL at (4.7 mm)

The HIV-N-ART group exhibited significantly increased global ppRNFL thickness (β = 6.90, 95% CI: 1.75 to 12.05, p = 0.009), with regional increases in the inferior (INF: β = 13.71, 95% CI: 4.42 to 23.00, p = 0.004) and inferonasal (IN: β = 15.38, 95% CI: 4.01 to 26.75, p = 0.008) sectors. No significant differences were found in the HIV-ART group.

BMO-MRW

There were no significant differences in global or sectoral BMO-MRW between either HIV-positive group and controls.

Associations of optic nerve head parameters with HIV factors

Multivariable regression analysis was performed to examine the associations between optic nerve head parameters and four key HIV-related factors: CD4 count, CD4%, viral load, and ART duration. Findings revealed distinct patterns in HIV-ART and HIV-N-ART groups, with CD4 count and CD4% showing positive associations with optic nerve thickness in HIV-N-ART but negative associations in HIV-ART. Longer ART duration was associated with both increases and decreases in optic nerve head measurements in HIV-ART.

CD4 count and optic nerve head parameters

For both groups, the same optic nerve head parameters were analyzed (BMO-MRW and APS-ppRNFL across all sectors). Only statistically significant associations are shown in the figure.

Although a negative association was observed for both global (β = −0.10, p = 0.03) and superior temporal (β = −0.11, p = 0.04) BMO-MRW thickness in the HIV-ART group, the superior-temporal sector is only one of 24 radial segments contributing to the global mean. Therefore, while the effects are directionally similar, the global findings reflect a more widespread pattern across the optic nerve head rather than being driven by a single sector. Discussion

This proof-of-concept study aimed to explore whether HIV-related factors are associated with morphological alterations in the optic nerve head, and whether optic nerve imaging could serve as an early indicator of ocular involvement in PLHIV. The study revealed differences in ONH parameters between the HIV-ART, HIV-N-ART, and HIV control groups. These differences provide preliminary evidence of structural variation in optic nerve morphology across groups, potentially related to HIV infection and treatment status.

A key observation was the significantly thicker inferior ppRNFL in the HIV-N-ART group compared to both HIV-ART and HIV-negative controls. A key observation was the significantly thicker global and sectoral ppRNFL in the HIV-N-ART group compared to controls. GEE analysis showed that individuals not on ART had significantly thicker S-ppRNFL globally (β = 10.16 μm, 95% CI: 3.18 to 17.14, p < 0.001), especially in the inferior (β = 20.11 μm, p = 0.001), inferior nasal (β = 20.07 μm, p = 0.02), and inferior temporal (β = 21.13 μm, p = 0.01) sectors. These findings were mirrored in the APS-ppRNFL at 4.1 mm and 4.7 mm scan diameters, where the HIV-N-ART group consistently showed greater thickness compared to both HIV-ART and HIV-negative controls, particularly in the inferior and inferior nasal sectors. The APS_ppRNFL at 4.1 mm, and 4.7 mm circles revealed large effect sizes in the inferior and inferior nasal sectors, indicating a substantial thickening of the ppRNFL in the absence of ART. This is especially notable in the inferior sector, where the highest effect size was observed. These findings point toward the potential for early structural changes in the ppRNFL of HIV-positive individuals who are not receiving ART, with significant thickening occurring in these regions. These observations are further supported by the logistic regression (odds ratios) derived from the BMO-based measurements. These elevated odds ratios highlight the heightened risk of ppRNFL thickening in individuals not on ART, suggesting that ART plays a critical role in inflammation reducing oedema and altering the structural integrity of the ppRNFL. The large effect sizes and high odds ratios in key quadrants underscore the clinical relevance of these findings. Furthermore, the utility of BMO-based measurements appears to surpass that of standard S-ppRNFL in detecting morphological changes, making them novel valuable tools for early detection and monitoring of optic nerve health in PLHIV.

This study also observed increased odds ratio in the global sector and the inferior temporal quadrant for APS_ppRNFL in the HIV-ART group which may also indicate localised thickening, reflecting a compensatory mechanism or subclinical inflammation, although to a lesser degree compared to the HIV-N-ART group. This may also reflect evidence of the “Legacy Effect”, where even after ART, there remains persistent inflammation [19]. Lab-based research is necessary to confirm the prevalence of inflammatory markers across these groups before and after ART initiation. While the observed associations are compelling, it is important to emphasize that this study does not establish causality. The elevated odds ratios highlight potential associations between lack of ART and structural thickening of the ppRNFL, however, further longitudinal work is needed to determine whether these changes represent reversible oedema or persistent inflammation.

Notably, we observed a divergence in the association patterns between BMO-MRW and APS-ppRNFL parameters in the HIV-N-ART group, particularly with CD4% levels. While BMO-MRW showed a positive correlation with CD4%, APS-ppRNFL (especially at the 4.7 mm diameter) exhibited a negative correlation. This apparent inconsistency in associations between BMO-MRW and APS-ppRNFL may reflect the spatially heterogeneous impact of early HIV-related neuroinflammation on the optic nerve [20]. While both parameters reflect ganglion cell axons, they are derived from distinct anatomical zones. Similar discordances have been reported in glaucomatous and neurodegenerative optic neuropathies, where peripapillary and ONH rim measurements may not change in tandem due to differences in axonal vulnerability or measurement sensitivity [21].

One possible mechanism for the obseved ppRNFL thickening in the HIV-N-ART group could be related to alterations in axonal transport mechanisms. HIV infection is known to impact neuronal function and axonal transport in various parts of the nervous system [22]. As the “Back of the Eye Is the Front of the Brain” [23], it is plausible that similar mechanisms could be contributing to the observed ppRNFL changes. These disruptions may occur due to direct viral effects, immune activation, or associated neuroinflammation [24]. Emerging evidence suggests that systemic immune activation and neuroinflammation in HIV may also manifest in the optic nerve head, possibly via shared pathways of microglial activation and blood–retina barrier dysfunction [24, 25] It is hypothesized that, in HIV-N-ART individuals, subclinical neuroinflammatory processes may interfere with normal axonal transport mechanisms, potentially contributing to early structural changes observed in the optic nerve. However, this remains speculative and warrants further functional investigation. This may affect the retrograde and anterograde transport processes, leading to an accumulation of proteins or organelles in the optic nerve fibres [22, 26]. This buildup could manifest as ppRNFL thickening in the early stages of HIV-related neurodegeneration, even before the onset of clinically detectable optic neuropathy or retinitis.

Chronic low-grade inflammation could trigger local immune responses in the retina and ONH, leading to reactive gliosis, axonal swelling, or neurofilament accumulation, which could further explain the thickening of the ppRNFL observed in this group [5]. Inflammatory cytokines and other immune markers released during HIV infection could impair the efficiency of axonal transport by affecting molecular motors and microtubule stability, which are crucial for the efficient functioning of the axonal transport system [22, 27–29]. To confirm these observed effects, visual evoked potentials (VEP) to measure the electrical activity of the brain in response to visual stimuli, providing a functional assessment of the optic nerve’s ability to transmit signals [30]. Visual evoked potentials (VEPs) have been used as non-invasive tools to evaluate visual pathway function, particularly in demyelinating and inflammatory conditions. Delayed VEP latencies are commonly interpreted as evidence of impaired neural conduction, most often attributed to demyelination rather than axoplasmic transport dysfunction per se [30]. While HIV-related neuroinflammation may plausibly affect axonal function, including axoplasmic flow, there is currently no direct evidence linking VEP latency changes to disrupted axoplasmic transport in this population. Alternatively, pattern electroretinography (pERG) could be performed to measure the electrical responses of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) to patterned visual stimuli, which assesses the functional integrity of these cells. Reduced pERG amplitude can indicate RGC dysfunction, which may result from defective axonal transport, especially if there is an accumulation of cellular debris in the axons or a loss of trophic support due to impaired retrograde transport [31]. If this concept of altered axonal transport as a mechanism for ppRNFL thickening is true, it must be questioned if early therapeutic interventions targeting axonal transport may help prevent neurodegeneration in HIV patients (especially at early diagnosis) and if this would mitigate changes over time?

The CNS HIV Antiretroviral Therapy Effects Research (CHARTER) study that 52% of their cohort had cognitive impairment and suggests that these findings will likely grow as the population of PLHIV ages [32]. Even though the relationship between CNS and ONH were not explored in this paper, other authors have alluded to the CNS changes that relate to retina and visual function (VEP) changes in HIV people and that OCT may be a valuable proxy tool to track CNS integrity [21, 22]. Therefore, although ART appears to mitigate inflammation-related thickening in the short term, it remains important to track individuals on long-term ART to ensure there are no delayed degenerative changes in the ONH that could signal broader neurodegenerative processes.

As a proof-of-concept study, this investigation was designed to provide initial insights into ONH morphology in the context of HIV and ART. While the cross-sectional design allows for the identification of potential associations, it does not permit conclusions about causality. Future longitudinal studies will be valuable in establishing temporal relationships and understanding the long-term effects of HIV and ART on optic nerve head morphology. Additionally, the modest sample size, while appropriate for an exploratory study, may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader HIV population. Nevertheless, the trends observed here lay important groundwork for larger, confirmatory studies. Additionally, we acknowledge that ocular parameters such as refractive error and corneal curvature (keratometry), which may influence OCT measurements, were not accounted for in this analysis and should be considered in future studies.

Investigating the effects of different ART regimens on retinal health, especially their neuroprotective or neurotoxic potential, will be crucial for guiding treatment decisions. Additionally, studies should explore the role of systemic inflammation, immune activation, and oxidative stress in retinal damage, and the potential of CD4% as a more reliable marker for ocular changes compared to CD4 count alone. Emphasising regional analysis of the ppRNFL, particularly in vulnerable sectors like the superior temporal and inferior nasal, may provide valuable insights into differential patterns of retinal damage.

Conclusion

This study identifies key retinal quantitative imaging markers and clinical variables, such as CD4 count, ART duration, and viral load, as potential correlates of optic nerve head structural alterations in immunocompetent PLHIV. The findings highlight the potential of APS_ppRNFL as a sensitive biomarker for the early detection of optic nerve changes before clinical signs of retinitis or neuropathy emerge. Timeous treatment of PLHIV is vital to protect the optic nerve structures from potential neuronal damage caused by the disease. Those on long-term ART must be tracked over time to ensure that treatment may not be causing alterations of the ppRNFL that may affect visual function.
