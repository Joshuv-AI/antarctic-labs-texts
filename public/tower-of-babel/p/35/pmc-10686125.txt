Effect of electron donors on CO2 fixation from a model cement industry flue gas by non‐photosynthetic microbial communities in batch and continuous reactors
Authors: Cecilia Lizeth Alvarez‐Guzmán, Karla María Muñoz‐Páez, Idania Valdez‐Vazquez
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this work was to evaluate the effect of different inorganic compounds as electron donors for the capture of CO2 from a model cement flue gas CO2/O2/N2 (4.2:13.5:82.3% v/v) using a non‐photosynthetic microbial community. The inoculum obtained from a H2‐producing reactor was acclimated to CO2 consumption achieving 100% of CO2 removal after 45 days. Na2S, MnCl2, NaNO2, NH4Cl, Na2S2O3, and FeCl2 were used as energy source for CO2 fixation by the acclimated microbial community showing different efficiencies, being Na2S the best electron donor evaluated (100% of CO2 consumption) and FeCl2 the less effective (28% of CO2 consumption). In all treatments, acetate and propionate were the main endpoint metabolites. Moreover, scaling the process to a continuous laboratory biotrickling filter using Na2S as energy source showed a CO2 consumption of up to 77%. Analysis of the microbial community showed that Na2S and FeCl2 exerted a strong selection on the microbial members in the community showing significant differences (PERMANOVA, p = 0.0001) compared to the control and the other treatments. Results suggest that the CO2 fixing pathways used by the microbial community in all treatments were the 3‐hydroxypropionate‐4‐hydroxybutyrate cycle and the Wood‐Ljungdahl pathway.

INTRODUCTION

As atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) concentrations continued to increase at accelerated rates, the challenge for the fossil fuel industry is to find cost‐effective solutions to reduce the release of CO2 and other pollutants into the atmosphere (David et al., 2007). Anthropogenic emissions of CO2 into the atmosphere come from three main sources: oxidation of fossil fuels, deforestation and other land‐use changes, and carbonate decomposition. Cement production is the largest source of CO2 emissions from decomposition of carbonates (Worrell et al., 2001). According to Cuellar‐Bermudez et al., 2015, the cement industry is responsible for 8% of total global CO2 emissions with 1.4 Gt of CO2 emissions per year (Schakel et al., 2018). The flue gases produced from cement industry contain CO2 formed during clinker production, which is the main component of cement, and CO2 formed from fuel combustion (average total 17.8%). These flue gases also contain oxygen (O2, 7.5%), water (H2O, 18.2%), nitrogen (N2, 56.5%) and in a lesser extent (<1.47 g/Nm3) dust, carbon monoxide (CO), oxides of nitrogen (NOx), sulfur oxide (SO2) and hydrogen chloride (HCl) (Schakel et al., 2018). Different Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) approaches have been considered to mitigate these CO2 emissions. Some techniques are based on physical and/or chemical processes such as adsorption, absorption, membrane separation, cryogenic distillation and chemical looping combustion, with the subsequent CO2 storage in the oceans or in geological formations, which represents a long‐term risk due to the possibility of gas leakage (Stephens, 2006).

A more attractive solution involves the biological mitigation of CO2, which is considered a promising and eco‐sustainable method compared to current chemical or physical approaches, as they can operate at ambient conditions and CO2 can be transformed into value‐added products (Lee et al., 2020). Currently, in the literature the biological CO2 capture from these off‐gases has been addressed mainly through the use of photosynthetic processes using microalgae, since low CO2 concentration in flue gas (4%–17.5%) is suitable for the cultivation of these microorganisms (Oliveira et al., 2020; Schakel et al., 2018). The application of microalgae for the CO2 capture faces several limitations. For instance, it is necessary to consider their dependence on light as energy source and the requirement of expensive reactor configurations, and also, the presence of inhibitory compounds in flue gases can be deleterious for these microorganisms; therefore, a prior flue gas pretreatment is required. Moreover, the flue gas streams have high temperatures, which most microalgae do not tolerate; it has been reported that temperatures higher than 35°C can be lethal for the majority of microalgae species (Vale et al., 2020).

Non‐photosynthetic microorganisms are gaining recognition as a promising approach for carbon capture and conversion (Salehizadeh et al., 2020). Unlike microalgae, non‐photosynthetic microbes are versatile in terms of energy utilization as they can obtain energy from inorganic compounds instead of light and use it to fix carbon in metabolic pathways like the Calvin cycle, the Wood‐Ljungdahl pathway (WLP), the 3‐hydroxypropionate‐4‐hydroxybutyrate (3HP‐4HB) cycle, the di‐carboxylate‐4‐hydroxybutyrate (DC‐4HB) cycle, and the reverse tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycle (Fast & Papoutsakis, 2012; Zhang et al., 2022). In the Calvin cycle, inorganic carbon is reduced to 3‐phosphoglycerate, while in the other mentioned pathways inorganic carbon is reduced to acetyl‐CoA, which can be further converted into different value‐added products.

Multiple electron donors can support chemoautotrophic growth (Claassens et al., 2018), and several authors have assessed the effect of these compounds as energy sources for CO2 fixation. However, these studies often evaluate unrealistic gas mixtures, with compositions not typically found in waste streams emitted by the cement industry. For instance, Hu et al. (2010a) and Hu, Wang, Zhang, Le, and Fu (2014) evaluated the effect of Na2S, Na2S2O3 and NaNO2 on the CO2 fixation under aerobic conditions (air/CO2, 80:20% v/v; H2/O2/CO2, 80:10:10% v/v) using marine non‐photosynthetic microbial communities. Similarly, Wang et al. (2017) assessed the influence of Na2S2O3 on carbon fixation by Halothiobacillus neapolitanus from a gas containing CO2/O2 (10:21%). Other studies have reported anaerobic conditions; for example, Ye et al. (2020) evaluated the performance of CO2 fixation by Paracoccus denitrificans PJ‐1 using different Na2S2O3 concentrations applying a mixture of CO2/N2 (20:80% v/v), whereas Hu et al. (2010b) and Hu et al. (2011) reported the carbon fixation from CO2/N2 (20:80% v/v) using a non‐photosynthetic microbial community and Na2S, Na2S2O3 and NaNO2. In these studies, the authors found that effectiveness of the electron donor was influenced by their concentrations and interactions between them (Hu et al., 2011; Ye et al., 2020).

To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies applying flue gases in non‐photosynthetic systems. Even less, there are no studies assessing the effect of different inorganic electron donors using this particular gas composition with high O2 content (CO2/O2/N2, 4.2:13.5:82.3% v/v). Therefore, the aim of this study was to acclimate a H2‐producing microbial community to use CO2 from a model flue gas as carbon source and to determine the best electron donor for an efficient CO2 capture. Moreover, the effect of the electron donors on the structure of the microbial community was also evaluated.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Inoculum and culture medium

The inoculum was obtained from a H2‐producing sequencing batch reactor, inoculated with a heat‐treated anaerobic granular sludge from a flour industry. The H2‐producing reactor was fed with 20 g VS/Ld of organic fraction of municipal waste (OFMW) at 37°C and a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of 16 h. The inoculum (mixture of cells, unfermented substrate, and soluble products) was lyophilized (Labconco FreeZone 6 freeze‐dryer, Labconco Corp) and was preserved at −4°C until its use. The inoculum, after lyophilization, was composed of Prevotella (53%), Bifidobacterium (13%), Bacteroides (5%), Tannerellaceae (2.8%), Lactobacillus (2.6%), Erysipelotrichaceae UCG‐006 (2.7%), Clostridium sensu stricto 12 (1.8%), Sutterella (1.7%), Crassaminicella (1.6%), Pseudoramibacter (1.5%), Dialister (1.3%), Propionibacterium (1.1%), Pseudomonas (1.1%), [Ruminococcus] gauvreauii group (0.9%). The mineral culture medium used for gas fermentations contained (in mg/L, Rachbauer et al., 2017): KH2PO4 408.0, Na2HPO4 426.0, CaCl2·2H2O 110.0, MgCl2·H2O 100.0, NH4Cl 300.0, NaCl 300.0, Na2S·9H2O 144. 1 mL/L of a trace elements 1000X solution containing (in mg/L): H3BO3 0.05, ZnCl2 0.07, CuCl2·2H2O 0.05, MnCl2·4H2O 2.0, (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O 0.1, AlCl3·6H2O 0.09, CoCl2·6H2O 1.0, NiCl2·6H2O 0.3, EDTA (disodium salt) 1.0, FeCl2·4H2O 2.0, Na2SeO3 0.126 was also used (Rachbauer et al., 2017).

Acclimation of the inoculum to the CO2 capture

The acclimation of the inoculum to the CO2 capture from CO2/O2/N2 (4.2:13.5:82.3% v/v) (CRIOGAS, Querétaro, Mexico) was performed in 250 mL anaerobic bottles (Bellco Glass Inc.) with a working volume of 100 mL. The glass bottles were inoculated with 2 g/L of lyophilized inoculum in the liquid medium. The pH was adjusted and maintained at 7.0 ± 0.001 using 100 mM HEPES buffer. After inoculation, the headspace was flushed for 3 min with CO2/O2/N2 and incubated at 37°C and 150 rpm in an orbital shaker. Since the gas mixture CO2/O2/N2 does not contain any electron donor for the CO2 fixation, 30 mM of Na2S⋅9H2O was added to the culture medium. Seven culture flasks were sacrificed at different intervals over a 45‐day period to analyse the gas composition, production of soluble metabolites and the microbial community.

Effects of electron donors on CO2 fixation

After the acclimation, the effect of Na2S, MnCl2, NaNO2, NH4Cl, Na2S2O3, and FeCl2 on carbon fixation from CO2/O2/N2 was evaluated. The concentration of each electron donor was chosen based on the electrons required to reduce 4 moL CO2 into 1 moL butanol, considering the degree of reduction of each electron donor and the stoichiometric reaction of equation 1. The chosen concentrations were: Na2S 80 mM, MnCl2 28 mM, NaNO2 35 mM, NH4Cl 26 mM, Na2S2O3 20 mM, and FeCl2 27 mM.

Each electron donor was evaluated independently in batch gas fermentations in 250‐mL serum flasks with a working volume of 100 mL. Initial pH was adjusted and maintained at 7.0 ± 0.001 using 100 mM HEPES buffer. The cultures with mineral medium were inoculated with 15% v/v of the previously acclimated inoculum (section 2.2). The headspace of the serum flasks was flushed with CO2/O2/N2 for 3 min and incubated for a 14‐day period at 37°C and 150 rpm in an orbital shaker. Each electron donor was evaluated in quadruplicate. A control experiment consisting only of mineral medium and 15% v/v of the acclimated inoculum was also performed. Gas and liquid samples were taken at the end of the incubation period to analyse the gas composition, production of soluble metabolites, and the microbial diversity.

Biotrickling filter set‐up

A biotrickling filter (BTF) (Figure 1) was made of methacrylate with a packed volume of 0.7 L (Muñoz‐Páez & Buitrón, 2022). The BTF was filled with a previously colonized material made of polyethylene terephthalate, about 10 mm in diameter, and a surface area >~700 m2/L (Matrix™, Seachem Laboratories, Inc.), which supports aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. The nutrient solution was the same used in batch experiments and was recirculated (1.0 L) with a peristaltic pump at a fixed rate of 160 mL/min (Figure 1). The pH was adjusted automatically at a value of 7.0 ± 0.001 by adding NaOH or HCl solutions. The temperature of 37°C in the BTF was maintained by circulating hot water through a water jacket. The composition of the fed gas was a synthetic mixture of CO2/O2/N2 (4.2:13.5:82.3% v/v) similar to that of flue gases in a 10 L gas bag (Supelco Supel‐Inert Multi‐Layer Foil, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). Analytical methods

The content of CO2 and O2 in the headspace of the bottles was measured with a gas chromatograph (8610C, SRI Instruments, Torrance, CA, USA) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and two packed columns (6′ × 1/8″ silica gel packed column and a 6′ × 18″ molecular sieve 13 × packed column). The injector and detector temperatures were fixed at 90°C and 150°C, respectively. The initial column temperature was 40°C, held for 4 min, then gradually increased to 110°C at 20 mL/min. The final column temperature was held for 3 min. N2 was used as a carrier gas at a 20 mL/min flow rate.

Liquid samples were collected on days 3, 10, 14, 39 and 45 during the acclimation of the microbial community to the CO2 capture and at the endpoint of the evaluation of the electron donors on CO2 consumption. The liquid samples were centrifuged at 25,245 g for 5 min. The supernatants were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane (Whatman) to quantify soluble metabolites such as organic acids and alcohols. Short and medium fatty acids were analysed using a 1260 Infinity HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a diode array detector (DAD) with a detection wavelength of 210 nm, a refractive index detector (RID), and an AMINEX HPX‐87 H column. A 0.005 M H2SO4 solution served as the mobile phase at a 0.6 mL/min flow rate with a column temperature of 50°C.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Samples containing microbial cells were centrifuged at 25,245 g for 10 min, and the cell pellets (~1.0 g) were stored at −20°C for the subsequent molecular analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from replicates of final samples of acclimation and from the evaluation of electron donors using PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (Mo Bio Laboratories Inc) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA concentration was quantified by spectrophotometry using a NanoDrop 2000c (Thermo Scientific). DNA samples with a concentration of 30–50 ng/μL were submitted for Illumina MiSeq paired‐end sequencing analysis. The presence of bacteria and archaea was evaluated by the amplification of the 16S rRNA gene using the primers 515FB GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA (forward) and 806RB GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT (reverse). The length of the nucleotide sequences was 250 bp, which were submitted to GenBank, and the accession numbers are SRR23875030-SRR23875049. Moreover, the nucleotide sequences were analysed using the software package DADA2 v1.22 implemented in R v4.2.0 to remove low‐quality data and identify Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) and chimeras. SILVA v 138 was used for taxonomy assignation.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the treatments with the different electron donors was performed by a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and a Tukey test using Microsoft Excel v 14.0. Treatments with p < 0.05 were statistically significant.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Acclimation of the inoculum to the CO2 fixation

First, the potential of a fermentative microbial community to use CO2 as carbon source from CO2/O2N2 was evaluated. The parental microbial community consumed 1.7 mmol CO2/L (equivalent to 65% CO2 consumption) during the first 3 days of incubation (Figure 2A). Then, fresh CO2/O2/N2 was injected to the reactors by Day 7 to ensure the carbon availability for the microbial community. However, CO2 consumption did not increase probably due to the depletion of the electron donor; therefore, 30 mM Na2S was supplemented to the culture medium by Day 10. In spite of the availability of CO2 and electron donor, the CO2 consumption still did not increase, probably due to an inhibitory effect of H2S released from Na2S oxidation. In the early stage, the parental microbial community was not acclimated to H2S; therefore, it could have negatively affected the CO2 fixation. H2S is toxic for acetogenic bacteria through the interference with electron transport within the cell; however, this inhibition is reversible as stated by Ntagia et al. (2021). Based on this assumption, the cultures continued in incubation without sampling nor disturbance over a period of 25 days, to allow the microbial community to oxidize the residual Na2S in the medium and to acclimate to the accumulated H2S. By Day 39, the CO2 consumption was restored and reached 3.5 mmol CO2/L (50% of the total available CO2). By Day 40, the headspace of the reactors was replaced with fresh CO2/O2/N2 and Na2S was added to the medium. By Day 45, the microbial community succeeded in consuming 100% of the available CO2, a total of 6.1 mmol CO2/L. Regarding O2, the microbial community fully consumed it during the fermentation process (Figure 2A). The carbon fixed by the microbial community was converted into acetate and propionate, being acetate the most abundant (Figure 2B). The production of these metabolites gradually increased since the beginning of the acclimation; however, from day 14 to 39, their concentration remained constant probably due to the period of acclimation to H2S; later, after the addition of CO2/O2/N2 and Na2S, the concentration of both acetate and propionate slightly increased reaching 1.35 and 1.0 mmol/L of acetate and propionate, respectively (equivalent to 2.36 mmol/L of carbon fixed).

Effect of different inorganic electron donors on CO2 fixation

Since non‐photosynthetic microorganisms rely on the use of chemical energy instead of light to carry out the CO2 reduction, the use of an adequate energy and reducing power source is key for the CO2 fixation and conversion (Erşan & Park, 2020). Molecular H2, reduced nitrogen (NH4 + and NO2 −), sulfur (H2S and S2O3 −2) and metal (i.e., Fe2+ and Mn2+) compounds serve as electron donors for these bacteria (Labrenz et al., 2005). Although H2 has been recognized as an efficient electron donor (Claassens et al., 2018), currently it is valorized as an important feedstock in industry and as a clean biofuel (Mah et al., 2019); therefore, in this study it was not considered for its evaluation. In contrast, reduced nitrogen, sulfur and metal compounds can be easily obtained from readily available and low‐cost sources such as waste waters and mining residues (Claassens et al., 2018). Herein, Na2S, MnCl2, NaNO2, NH4Cl, Na2S2O3 and FeCl2 were evaluated as electron donors for carbon fixation from CO2/O2/N2 (4.2:13.5:82.3% v/v). The results showed that the control experiment consisting of only mineral medium and inoculum, achieved a CO2 consumption of 1.12 mmol/L (43% of total available CO2) (Figure 3A), which was attributed to the electrons derived from NH4Cl used as the nitrogen source and possibly to trace amounts of Na2S from the acclimation stage. The effectiveness of the electron donors for CO2 consumption in this study was Na2S > MnCl2 = NaNO2 = NH4Cl = Na2S2O3 > FeCl2. The use of Na2S achieved the highest CO2 consumption of 2.6 mmol/L, which represents 100% of the CO2 available in the gas mixture, while MnCl2 was the second‐best electron donor with a CO2 consumption of 1.9 mmol/L (74% of the total available CO2). Treatment with NaNO2 led to the CO2 consumption of 1.6 mmol/L (60%), which was statistically similar (p = 0.12) to the treatment with MnCl2. The use of NH4Cl, Na2S2O3 and FeCl2 led to the CO2 consumption of 1.5, 1.3 and 0.7 mmol/L, respectively, which showed no significant difference compared to the control (p > 0.11). According to Hu, Wang, Zhang, Wang, et al. (2014), H2 is the most efficient electron donor for chemolithoautotrophic microorganisms, since it can be used directly by hydrogenase enzymes, followed by reduced sulfur, reduced nitrogen and metals compounds; however, the utilization of the electrons derived from these compounds depends on the type of autotrophic microorganisms (Di Capua et al., 2019).

Non‐photosynthetic CO2 fixation occurs under aerobic (Calvin cycle, 3HP‐4HB cycle) and anaerobic (Calvin cycle, WLP, DC‐4HB cycle, rTCA cycle) conditions (Fast & Papoutsakis, 2012; Zhang et al., 2022); therefore, the presence of O2 in the gas mixture played a crucial role in the CO2 fixation pathways used by the microbial communities.

Treatments with Na2S and MnCl2, which showed high CO2 consumption, also reached a high O2 consumption of 8.4 mmol/L (100%) and 6.5 mmol/L (77%), respectively (Figure 3A). It is possible that microbial communities might have carried out the 3HP‐4HB cycle at the beginning of the fermentation when the headspace of the cultures contained about 13% of O2; then, after O2 was depleted, the anaerobic conditions for the WLP were established. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that acetate and propionate were the main metabolites produced during these fermentations (Figure 3B). Acetate is produced in the WLP and propionate in the 3HP‐4HB cycle. The production of these organic acids is fundamental in each of these CO2 fixation pathways since their production is linked to the synthesis of ATP, which is essential in the energy‐limited conditions of non‐photosynthetic carbon fixation. The use of Na2S2O3 also led to a high O2 consumption of 96% yet with a low CO2 uptake (41% of the available CO2) with no significant difference compared to the control (p = 0.78). Probably, most of the Na2S2O3 could have acted as O2 scavenger (Gu & Sensors, 2008) instead of electron donor, resulting in an incomplete CO2 consumption. The NaNO2, NH4Cl and FeCl2 treatments achieved the O2 consumption of 2.1, 3.0 and 3.5 mmol/L, respectively, which was lower or equal to that of the control (3.5 mmol/L) (Figure 3A). Similar to treatments with Na2S and MnCl2, propionate and acetate were the main metabolites produced during NaNO2, NH4Cl and FeCl2 treatments, suggesting that the 3HP‐4HB cycle and the WLP might have also been carried out in these treatments. This hypothesis is in agreement with the study by Wang et al. (2014), which showed that multiple carbon fixation pathways were used by a microbial community, with a mixture of electron donors and CO2 and HCO3 − as substrate.

In literature, little is known about the effect of electron donors on the non‐photosynthetic CO2 fixation from flue gases containing O2 (CO2/O2/N2). However, some studies have assessed the CO2 fixation involving aerobic conditions. For instance, Hu et al. (2010a) reported the use of a gas mixture containing air/CO2 (80:20% v/v) as substrate for a marine microbial community. The authors evaluated the effect of different electron donors and showed that the use of Na2S, Na2S2O3 and NaNO2 increased the CO2 fixation 100%, 200% and 200%, respectively. Other work by Xu et al. (2023) confirmed that Na2S alone and also in mixtures with Na2S2O3 and NaNO2 was an efficient electron donor for a marine microbial community using air/CO2 (80:20% v/v). In this study, Na2S was the most efficient electron donor for CO2 reduction using CO2/O2/N2, whereas Na2S2O3 and NaNO2 were not suitable for this process, which may be attributed to the different operational conditions as well to the different structure of the microbial community used in this study.

For instance, Xu et al. (2023) used a microbial community obtained from sediments collected from a deep‐sea hydrothermal vent, which after domestication using air/CO2 (80:20% v/v) and a mixture of Na2S, Na2S2O3 and NaNO2 was enriched with unclassified_f_Sphingomonaceae, unclassified_f_Rhodobactaceae, unclassified_f_Rhizobiaceae and Erythrobacter. On the other hand, in our study a completely different inoculum was used, which originally derived from an anaerobic granular sludge from a flour industry. After the exposure of the microbial community to CO2/O2/N2 and Na2S, a totally different group of bacteria to that reported by Xu et al. (2023) proliferated (Figure 4D) dominated by Lysinibacillus, Bacillus, Olsenella, Proteinivoravales, Actinomycetaceae among others.

Effect of different electron donors on the structure of the microbial community

Different inorganic compounds can be used as electron donors for CO2 fixation; however, their effectiveness greatly depends on the way microorganisms harness the energy derived from these compounds. This is because, different chemolithoautotrophic species exhibit preference over certain electron donors, which is attributed to their different enzymatic machineries. In this study, a non‐metric multidimensional (nMDS) analysis identified the influence of different electron donors on the structure of the microbial community. Results showed that the microbial community of the control treatment was not statistically different from those microbial communities that used Na2S2O3, MnCl2, NaNO2 and NH4Cl as electron donors (p > 0.09); therefore, these samples overlapped (Figure 4A). On the other hand, the microbial communities that used Na2S and FeCl2 completely diverged from the control (p < 0.03), which is remarkable. Na2S and FeCl2 treatments had the best and the poorest performance of carbon fixation, respectively, indicating that these electron donors imposed a strong selection pressure on microbial community composition, while treatments that used Na2S2O3, MnCl2, NaNO2 and NH4Cl shared members in the microbial community. Moreover, the diversity of the microbial communities in each treatment was assessed using the Chao‐1 and Shannon estimators. These analyses showed that the richness and diversity of the Na2S microbial community increased 3‐fold compared to the control and the other treatments (Figure 4B,C). On the other hand, these parameters did not change when using Na2S2O3 (p > 0.95), MnCl2 (p > 0.60), NaNO2 (p > 0.16) and NH4Cl (p > 0.50). Although the richness and diversity of FeCl2 microbial community showed no statistical difference compared to the control, this treatment showed a tendency to decrease, suggesting that either the use of FeCl2 itself, or the concentration used in this study, affected negatively the initial microbial community and, therefore, the carbon fixation. As stated by Baek et al., 2019, microbial communities can be affected by heavy metals above certain concentration; the toxic effect depends on the sensitive of microorganisms to metal toxicity. According to Auffan et al. (2008), Fe (II) or Fe0 is cytotoxic to bacteria because they induce oxidative stress triggered from the generation of reactive O2 species when Fe and O2 react, causing disturbs in the redox processes involved in microbial energy metabolism. The lowest values of richness and diversity suggest that perhaps, reactive O2 species might have affected part of the microbial community using FeCl2.

To identify the most contributing taxa enriched in the different treatments with the electron donors, a similarity percentage (SIMPER) analysis was performed (Figure 4D). As showed in the nMDS analysis (Figure 4A), the control, Na2S2O3, MnCl2, NaNO2 and NH4Cl treatments formed a cluster which according to the SIMPER analysis were mainly composed by Pseudomonas and Thermicanus, which are known as heterotrophic, facultatively anaerobic bacteria. Moreover, autotrophic microorganisms including Bacillus, Achromobacter, Acinetobacter and Methylobacterium‐Methylorubrum were also enriched in these treatments. Although the structure of the microbial communities of these treatments showed no statistical difference among them, it was possible to distinguish the proliferation of specific taxa depending on the electron donor used (Figure 4D); for instance, the highest abundance of Pseudomonas (92%) was observed in treatments with Na2S2O3. According to Lalucat et al. (2006), Pseudomonas stutzery uses Na2S2O3 as inorganic energy source either in presence or absence of O2; however, this bacterium cannot generate enough energy to support autotrophic growth from Na2S2O3 oxidation (Sorokin et al., 1999). Yet, this specie may play an important role in carbon fixation due to its ability of extracellular transference of electrons through cytochrome c and the secretion of redox mediators (Li et al., 2022). In Na2S2O3 treatment, the carbon fixation was probably performed by the autotrophic Achromobacter which showed its highest abundance (7%) under this condition. This genus performs CO2 fixation using Na2S2O3 as energy source through the WLP (Jadhav & Jadhav, 2017). Moreover, the highest abundance of Bacillus (95%) was observed in treatments with NaNO2. While a wide variety of Bacillus species have been described as heterotrophs, there are some species exhibiting autotrophic metabolism. Chen et al. (2019) reported the isolation of Bacillus sp. Wy065 from soil samples showing carbon fixation activity via the Calvin cycle. The authors reported that among the electron donors evaluated, the use of NaNO2 enhanced the biomass production using NaHCO3 as sole carbon source, which is consistent with the results of this study. Moreover, the use of MnCl2 favoured the proliferation of Acinetobacter (96%) and Methylobacterium‐Methylorubrum (17%). Species of Acinetobacter have been described that perform anaerobic autotrophic denitrification processes using Mn (II) as electron donor (Su et al., 2015). Additionally, Methylobacterium‐Methylorubrum possesses the ability to metabolize C1 compounds using energy from Mn (II) oxidation as reported by Camargo‐Neves and Welington (2019) Interestingly, Thermicanus genus was enriched in MnCI2, Na2S2O3, NH4Cl and NaNO2 treatments indicating that this heterotrophic genus might have had symbiotic interactions with other heterotrophic and/or autotrophic members of the microbial communities. Gößner et al. (1999) described that the microaerophilic Thermicanus aegyptius grew commensally with the autotrophic Moorella thermoacetica via interspecies electron transfer and by providing the anoxic conditions required by M. thermoacetica; perhaps, a similar behaviour may have occurred in these treatments.

FeCl2 microbial community was composed by a reduced number of taxa as indicated by its low richness and diversity indices (Figure 4B,C), comprised only by Alicyclobacillus (83%), Lysinibacillus (14%), Bacillus (0.8%) and Pseudomonas (1%). Alicyclobacillus is a strictly aerobic bacteria with heterotrophic metabolism (Ortiz‐Cortés et al., 2021). Karavaiko et al. (2005) described that this genus performs the dissimilatory oxidation of iron and sulfur, which can explain its proliferation in this treatment. On the other hand, Lysinibacillus, an autotrophic genus with iron and sulfur redox/metabolic genes (Rathod et al., 2019) could have participated in the CO2 fixation together with autotrophic species of Bacillus. Notably, the fact that Bacillus and Lysinibacillus were the only autotrophic taxa in this treatment, suggests that the proliferation of other autotrophic microorganisms was affected by the FeCl2 concentration used, and/or by the ROS produced from the reaction of FeCl2 with O2 in the gas mixture. The proliferation of Bacillus and Lysinibacillus under these conditions can be attributed to the fact that members of these genera have mechanisms to cope with iron toxicity, including the secretion of iron carriers, organic acids, biosurfactants, biomethylation and redox processes (Peng et al., 2021). Furthermore, they possess mechanisms to thrive under the oxidative stress caused by ROS such as upregulation of catalases and thioredoxins (Mols & Abee, 2011).

Conversely, the Na2S microbial community with the highest richness and diversity indices among all treatments was composed by 12 taxa, with Lysinibacillus (an iron‐ and sulfur‐oxidizing genus) as the most abundant (17%). Other heterotrophic members such as Olsenella (7%), Bifidobacterium (7%), Lactobacillus (7%), Pediococcus (5%) and Proteinivoracales (5%) were also enriched using Na2S. Moreover, autotrophic taxa were also present: Bacillus (9%), Actinomycetaceae (4%), Hungateiclostridiaceae (3%), DNF00809 (3%) and Clostridium sensu stricto (2%).

A canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) was adopted to assess the relationship of the different members of the microbial communities, the gas consumption, as well as the production of metabolites (Figure 5). The distribution of the taxa was divided in three clusters. Cluster 1, gathered microorganisms with a positive correlation with CO2 consumption and propionate production, including autotrophic members like Hungateiclostridiaceae, Clostridium sensu stricto, DNF00809, Bacillus and Actinomycetaceae and heterotrophic taxa such as Proteinivoracales, Bifidobacterium, Olsenella, Pediococcus, Thermicanus and Pseudomonas. These taxa belong to the microbial communities of treatments using Na2S and MnCl2 (with the highest CO2 consumption), NaNO2, NH4Cl and Na2S2O3 (with intermediate CO2 consumption), and the control. Since acetate and propionate are two metabolic products related to the WLP and the 3HP‐4HB cycle, respectively, it was expected that both products showed a positive correlation with CO2 consumption; nevertheless, the CCA only indicated a positive correlation with propionate, which was less abundant compared to acetate (Figure 3B). This can be explained by the fact that acetate was produced in a similar range between treatments, therefore, the CCA could not establish a positive relationship with CO2 consumption. The fact that acetate was the main metabolic product in almost all treatments (except for Na2S2O3 treatment) suggests that the dominant CO2 fixing pathway in these treatments was the WLP. Taxa present in cluster 1 such as Hungateiclostridiaceae, Proteinivoracales, and Clostridium sensu stricto belong to the class of Clostridia, which are recognized as anaerobic acetogenic bacteria that fix carbon through the WLP (Schuchmann & Müller, 2016). Regarding the 3HP‐4HB cycle, according to Gonzalez‐Garcia et al. (2017), this cycle has been identified only in phototrophic Chloroflexacae and archaea, which were not observed in this study. This CO2 fixation pathway is relatively new, described in 2007 by Berg et al. (2007); therefore, there is currently little information available about all the microorganisms that can perform it. Ruiz‐Fernández et al. (2020) recently found DNA sequences associated with the 3HP‐4HB cycle in other bacteria belonging to the Actinobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria classes, suggesting that this cycle could be more diversified than currently thought. Here, several taxa belonging to the Actinobacteria class, including Olsenella, DNF00809, Actinomycetaceae and Bifidobacterium, positively correlated with CO2 consumption which suggests that they might have performed the CO2 fixation through the 3HP‐4HB cycle under aerobic conditions. Moreover, cluster 2 containing Achromobacter, and Lysinibacillus presented a positive correlation with O2 consumption. These bacteria are iron‐ and sulfur‐oxidizer microorganisms present in the experiment using Na2S2O3, one of the most O2 consuming treatments with a low CO2 consumption. Finally, cluster 3 was composed exclusively by Alicyclobacillus, and correlated positively with acetate, lactate and formate production. This genus was enriched only in the FeCl2 treatment with the lowest CO2 consumption. These results indicate that the addition of different electron donors changed the structure of the initial microbial community (control treatment) which was mainly composed by Pseudomonas and Bacillus (Figure 4D), confirming that different taxa were selected according to their capacities to metabolize these inorganic compounds and to consume the substrate containing CO2/O2/N2.

Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) showing the ordination of taxa, gas consumption, and metabolites produced during carbon fixation.

Operation of a biotrickling‐filter reactor for CO2 fixation using CO2/O2/N2 as substrate

After identifying Na2S as the best electron donor for CO2 fixation using a gas mixture containing CO2/O2/N2, the process was scaled to a laboratory biotrickling filter (BTF) reactor. These reactors are equipped with large surface area available to form biofilm in an inert packing material (Figure 1), which generally offers more valuable gas‐to‐liquid mass transfer (Kusnere et al., 2021) during gas fermentation. Herein, the operation of the BTF started with 30 mM Na2S, showing an initial CO2 and O2 consumption of 43% and 35%, respectively by Day 1 (Figure 6). After this, O2 consumption increased to 78%, but CO2 consumption decreased to 34%, indicating that the electron donor was being depleted. Thus, 30 mM of Na2S was added to the aqueous phase which restored the CO2 consumption to 46%, which rapidly decreased to 4% after 1 day of operation. It is notable that at this point of the BTF operation, O2 consumption maintained stable with a consumption of 100%. By Day 5, 30 mM Na2S was supplemented again to the culture medium. In this stage, CO2 consumption showed the same decreasing tendency but at a lower rate. Afterwards, since 30 mM of Na2S was being rapidly consumed, the concentration was increased to 70 mM Na2S and was added to the medium by Day 9 before CO2 consumption continued decreasing. After this, CO2 consumption stopped but was reactivated by Day 11 and maintained between 25%–32% until Day 14. Again, after this period, 70 Na2S was supplemented to the medium achieving a high CO2 consumption of 56%, however, after this, the consumption of both CO2 and O2 was disturbed, which can be possibly attributed to the accumulation of abiotic precipitates produced during sulfide oxidation. This phenomenon has also been reported by other authors; for instance, Hu, Wang, Zhang, Le, and Fu (2014) observed the accumulation of precipitates when using Na2S as electron donor, the authors reported that these precipitates affected negatively the CO2 fixation by a marine microbial community, making difficult the cell recirculation and decreasing the CO2 fixation efficiency. Interestingly, in this study, after the disturbance of gas consumption, CO2 consumption was restored by Day 19, showing an increasing CO2 consumption reaching its highest value of 77%. These results indicate that the microbial community used in this study was resilient to the adverse effects of these compounds.

Kinetics of CO2 and O2 consumption during the operation of the biotrickling filter reactor. Grey arrows represent the addition of 30 mM Na2S, and black arrows represent the addition of 70 mM Na2S.

It is noteworthy that the addition of the electron donor was a crucial factor influencing the CO2 consumption by the microbial community, as the electron donor serves as the sole energy source in this system. Therefore, the automated electron donor supplementation could potentially enhance CO2 fixation performance. To achieve this, it is necessary to determine several parameters, such as the electron donor consumption rate and the optimum retention time of the liquid medium. Nevertheless, while Na2S proved to be an effective electron donor, the formation of abiotic precipitates represents a disadvantage, creating challenges in obtaining the information required. Thus, more research is needed to develop strategies that help to overcome this constraint.

Currently, there are no other studies that evaluate the CO2 fixation from flue gases in BTF reactors; only a few studies have applied this configuration for CO2 fixation through biogas upgrading; for instance, Muñoz‐Páez and Buitrón (2022) assessed the biomethane production in a BTF using a synthetic gas mixture containing CO2/H2 (40:60% v/v) evaluating the effect of EBRT, pH, and temperature, achieving a CO2 removal of 24%. Other study by Thapa et al. (2022) showed the conversion of two stoichiometric CO2/H2 ratios into biomethane in a BTF reactor, showing that in the first stage the use of a ratio of 1:4 allowed a CO2 conversion efficiency of 23.92%, whereas in a second stage a ratio of 1:6 led to a CO2 conversion efficiency of 92.58%. Recently, other studies have addressed the conversion of CO2 from syngas using these reactors. For instance, Asimakopoulos et al. (2020) implemented two trickle‐bed reactors setups operated under mesophilic and thermophilic conditions for the biomethanation of syngas composed by H2/CO2/CO/CH4 (45:25:20:10% v/v). Comparing our results, the 77% of CO2 consumption from CO2/O2/N2 (4.2:13.5:82.3% v/v) achieved in this work is comparable with other BTF‐CO2 fixing reactors, with the advantage of not depending on the use of H2 as electron donor.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, the CO2 fixation and the structure of a fermentative microbial community were affected by the electron donors evaluated. The use of Na2S and MnCl2 promoted a high CO2 consumption, whereas FeCl2 affected negatively the CO2 fixation. Other electron donors NH4Cl, NaNO2 and Na2S2O3 showed a similar CO2 to the control with the model flue gas and the microbial community used in this study. These results highlight the importance of selecting the right electron donor for non‐photosynthetic CO2 fixation processes. Also, the structure of the microbial community changed completely using Na2S and FeCl2, supporting the proliferation of different autotrophic and heterotrophic taxa. Results suggest that the microbial communities employed the 3HP‐4HB cycle (aerobic) and the WLP (anaerobic) for carbon fixation, being the latter the dominant CO2‐fixiation mechanism in most of the treatments; however, more studies are needed to validate this hypothesis. Additionally, the CO2 fixation of up to 77% in a continuous BTF shows the potential of this system to be implemented on industrial scale. Nevertheless, the accumulation of abiotic precipitates produced during Na2S oxidation represents an important challenge for its further application; thus, more research is required to overcome this issue.
