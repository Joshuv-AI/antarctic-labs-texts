Clinically altered brain activity may not look like aged brain activity: Implications for brain‐age modeling and biomarker strategies
Authors: Lukas A W Gemein, Sinead Gaubert, Claire Paquet, Joseph Paillard, Sebastian C Holst, Thomas Tveitstøl, Ira R J H Haraldsen, Tony Kam‐Thong, David J Hawellek, Jörg F Hipp, Denis A Engemann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBrain‐age gap (BAG), the difference between predicted age and chronological age, is studied as a biomarker for the natural progression of neurodegeneration. The BAG captures brain atrophy as measured with structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Electroencephalography (EEG) has also been explored for estimating the BAG (EEG‐BAG). However, studies showed mixed results for the EEG‐BAG including counterintuitive findings of younger predicted age in clinical populations, raising doubts about its utility as a clinical tool for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer's disease (AD).METHODSThis study critically examined brain‐age estimation from spectral EEG power as a common measure of brain activity in two of the largest public EEG datasets containing heterogeneous clinical cases alongside controls including MCI and AD. EEG recordings were analyzed from individuals with heterogeneous neurological conditions (n = 898, Temple University Hospital Abnormal EEG corpus [TUAB] data; n = 417 MCI & n = 311 dementia, Chung‐Ang University Hospital EEG [CAU] data) and controls (n = 1245, TUAB data; n = 459, CAU data).RESULTSWe found that age‐prediction models trained on the reference population systematically underpredicted age in clinical conditions showing strong and systematic, age‐related differences in EEG power compared to controls. Data exploration and simulations revealed how diverging age‐related trends in specific EEG frequencies can account for a negative EEG‐BAG.DISCUSSIONThe utility of brain age as an interpretable biomarker relies on the observation from structural MRI that progressive neurodegeneration often broadly resembles aging. This assumption can be violated for functional assessments such as EEG spectral power related to different neurological and psychiatric conditions or medications. The sign of the BAG may therefore not be meaningfully interpreted as an individual aging metric, hence hampering its utility as an endpoint or biomarker in MCI and AD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, biomarker, brain‐age gap, dementia, electroencephalography, machine learning, magnetic resonance imaging, mild cognitive impairment

INTRODUCTION

Machine learning (ML) holds promise to innovate biomarker development with heterogeneous biomedical data. 1 , 2 In neuroscience, the utility of ML is hampered by small sample sizes. 3 , 4 , 5 The field has been exploring the potential of training models on large datasets from the general population to enhance the study of smaller clinical datasets. 6 Applied ML has explored self‐supervised learning techniques in which larger datasets are used to learn features that can then be used for smaller datasets. 7 , 8 , 9 Neuroscientists have explored a number of approaches focusing on analysis of reference data which hold promise to provide biomedically interpretable outputs. This includes normative modeling, 10 brain growth charts, 11 , 12 and ML‐derived measures of brain aging. 13 , 14

Brain‐age research has mostly focused on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). 13 , 15 This approach estimates the relationship between chronological age and anatomical MRI features, for example, cortical and subcortical tissue statistics including volume, surface area, or thickness, as these MRI features decline in elderly populations. Neurological insults can prematurely cause brain atrophy or accelerate brain volume loss, hence increasing the apparent age of the brain. 16 As brain‐age models trained on the general population are applied to neurodegenerative populations including mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer's disease (AD), systematic overestimation occurs as the brain of a patient appears “older” compared to the population reference. 6 , 17 This is captured by the brain‐age gap (BAG) computed by subtracting the chronological age from the MRI‐predicted age, 18 so that positive values represent a higher biological age of the brain. In patients suffering from different neurological conditions a positive BAG has been consistently reported 17 , 19 , 20 , 21 and a higher BAG predicted mortality, 22 highlighting the potential of the BAG as a prognostic biomarker. The BAG could therefore be useful for stratification or as an endpoint in interventional neuroscience studies. As brain‐age models leverage large datasets, the BAG may show higher sensitivity for gauging brain health at baseline or track disease‐related progression of cerebral atrophy.

A recent line of research has explored brain‐activity measures for brain‐age modeling. 23 Studies focusing on functional MRI (fMRI) have pointed out that functional connectivity measures contribute complementary information over anatomical features, which can lead to improved clinical characterization. 24 Electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) have also been studied for brain‐age modeling with ML. M/EEG measures large‐scale synchrony of cortical neurons at scales from milliseconds to hours, 25 , 26 hence providing a unique view on brain function. Multimodal studies have found that MEG contributes unique information on age compared to fMRI and MRI 27 , 28 and several publications have demonstrated successful age prediction from EEG signals 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 highlighting complementary physiological information relative to MRI.

These results suggest that EEG is attractive for applying brain‐age models in real‐world settings, as the ease of access to EEG enables its use in a wide range of situations. Besides laboratory‐based resting‐state EEG, brain‐age modeling has been explored during sleep, 29 , 33 general anesthesia, 34 and at‐home EEG recordings during sleep or meditation. 33 However, the implied clinical significance has rarely been systematically explored. Most studies do not involve separate training on healthy reference populations and testing on clinical populations, 31 , 35 hindering clinical interpretation of brain age estimated from EEG (EEG‐BAG).

Previous work has demonstrated the feasibility of computing EEG‐BAG scores in laboratory‐based studies using sleep EEG, which reported a mean absolute error of approximately 5 to 8 years in large baseline datasets. 29 , 36 , 37 , 38 These results were comparable to EEG‐BAG scores derrived from wakeful resting EEG and MEG. 30 Applications of these models to cohorts of dementia patients showed a positive EEG‐BAG—in line with an interpretation as a measure of brain aging—and reported that brain age was elevated in dementia patients. 36 , 38 Another related study found elevated EEG‐BAG obtained from sleep as a risk factor of mortality. 37

On the other hand, paradoxical effects were reported by studies in which patients appeared to unexpectedly show “younger brain age,” suggesting that observed brain activity changes may oppose those changes seen with healthy aging. 34 , 39 Before examining the implications for MCI and AD, and, to develop an understanding of this phenomenon, we first review incidental reports of paradoxical EEG‐BAG across different clinical contexts such as general anesthesia and heterogenous neurological anomalies including epilepsy and strokes. Using a deep convolutional neural network applied to raw EEG signals, Gemein et al. 39 incidentally found a negative EEG‐BAG, suggesting “younger” brain age, in patients with EEG anomalies in the Temple University Hospital Abnormal EEG corpus (TUAB). 40 , 41 Another study investigated a cohort of 324 patients including elderly people at risk of postoperative anesthesia‐induced cognitive decline. Analyzing EEG power observed during general anesthesia with Riemannian models, Sabbagh et al. 34 found that among older patients undergoing propofol anesthesia, those who showed anomalies (iso‐electric burst suppression) 42 , 43 had lower brain age, implying an induced negative, “younger,” EEG‐BAG. These results suggest that, compared to the training cohort, profound shifts of EEG power were present in the testing set. Such shifts can be transient, as induced by anesthetics and their individual dosage requirements, 34 but they can also reflect chronic neurodegenerative alterations that are well documented for AD. Numerous studies have characterized a cortical slowing phenotype in patients with AD with simultaneous increase of power in low frequencies and decrease of power in high frequencies that was then related to amyloid and tau pathology. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 This exemplifies that synaptic pathology can lead to reorganization of brain activity that shows substantial differences between elderly patients and elderly healthy controls. And this raises the question whether the normative brain‐age concept readily generalizes to measures of brain function such as the EEG: Brain volume shrinks with age and does so faster in dementias. On the other hand, EEG activity can increase unexpectedly as a result of pathological changes in neuronal excitability and compensatory neurocognitive mechanisms. 50 , 51

This study was motivated by the incidental observation of negative EEG‐BAG in prior work. 34 , 39 In this work, we aim to determine whether this “paradoxical” result is a common feature of standard ML models applied to EEG from diverse cohorts and to explore potential mechanisms related to alterations of EEG power spectra that can drive such counterintuitive brain‐age predictions.

RESULTS

EEG‐based brain‐age models trained on healthy controls predicted substantially younger brain age for clinical cohorts

We constructed age‐prediction models on EEG power spectra using two large public datasets containing clinical EEG data alongside controls, the TUAB (n = 2143) 40 , 41 and the Chung‐Ang University Hospital EEG (CAU, n = 1187) 52 dataset. The TUAB dataset contained heterogenous archival data from patients seeking neurological counseling with precise diagnoses unknown for privacy reasons. However, medical reports mentioned epilepsy for around 87% and stroke for 12% of the EEG (referred to as “pathological” in the TUAB literature). 40 The CAU dataset contains patients diagnosed with MCI and various forms of dementia. Here we focused on AD dementia cases (the dominant subcategory). As the age range in CAU was limited, we pooled the control populations across both datasets to obtain a wider age distribution and increase the size of the training dataset. Applying a ridge regression model, we first established that age predictions showed accurate centering and scaling (R2 = 0.61; Figure 1A), in line with reported errors for MEG and EEG across different datasets (R 2 = 0.5–0.75). 30 , 39 , 53 We then investigated the ensuing EEG‐based brain‐age gap (EEG‐BAG) for clinical recordings (Figure 1B). We observed that the mean EEG‐BAG was negative across all clinical subgroups, ranging from ≈ –10 years (TUAB) to ≈ –6 years (CAU MCI) whereas controls showed predictions centered around zero (Figure 1C, left panel). The point clouds (Figures 1A, B) showed a wider spread for clinical groups, suggesting that less accurate predictions may reflect increased biomedical heterogeneity.

To rule out technical errors, we scored predictions using, both, the R 2 metric sensitive to translation and scaling errors as well as Spearman's rank correlation which is robust to these errors and captures non‐linear correlations (Figure 1C). In line with negative brain age (Figure 1A), the R 2 scores below zero suggest absence of model fit (Figure 1C, mid panel). However, Spearman rank correlations reveal positive association between brain‐age predictions and the actual age of the patients (Figure 1C, right panel). This suggests that the models learned systematic patterns from the data that generalize to the clinical population despite a global negative shift in EEG‐BAG scores. Hence, negative EEG‐BAG scores did not imply global prediction failure, that is, random guessing.

A limitation of our analysis arises from the substantially higher age of clinical groups in the TUAB and CAU datasets (Figures 1A–B, marginal histograms), rendering age a strong differentiator between the groups. Unfortunately, no neurological scores or other clinical variables were available for the two datasets that could enable further contextualizing the EEG‐BAG scores. We hence explored the utility of EEG‐BAG for discriminating between cases and controls conditional on age (Figure 2). For TUAB, EEG‐BAG contained statistical signals while, at the same time, revealing that EEG‐BAG showed negative correlations with the predicted probability of being a case, conditional on chronological age (Figure 2A). The picture was more nuanced for the CAU dataset: The same analysis did not yield a significant discriminatory effect of EEG‐BAG conditional on chronological age and its non‐linear square function. However, subsequently, a bivariate logistic regression model revealed that EEG‐BAG was significant and showed negative directionality, once the age term was removed, confirming its discriminatory value and complementarity relative to the non‐linear squared function of chronological age. This suggests that EEG‐BAG was redundant with age but not age squared (see Table S1 in supporting information).

EEG‐BAG captured information complementary to age but with unexpected directionality. To gauge the clinical utility of the EEG‐BAG scores for distinguishing between groups while controlling for age, we constructed statistical logistic regression models, additively combining age and brain age for separating cases and controls. Taken together, these results systematically add to the recent literature that had incidentally observed unexpected directionality in brain‐age predictions using EEG. 34 , 39 We observed a negative EEG‐BAG in populations for which one assumes lower brain health and advanced biological age. 6 , 27

Clinical EEG shows complex increases and decreases in brain activity

To better understand the negative (younger) EEG‐BAG in elderly clinical populations, we inspected age‐related trends in the data used as input to the age‐prediction models. Here, it is worthwhile to note that M/EEG studies investigating which brain signals drive age prediction pointed out that brain‐age models used information that was widespread across the entire power spectrum and not concentrated on specific frequency ranges. 27 , 33 , 34 , 54 , 55 If age‐related trends for key features are modified or even inverted in specific frequencies, models trained on healthy references, potentially listening to all frequencies, will be affected and may fail to provide an accurate estimate of the relationship between EEG and age.

We therefore investigated differences in log power spectral densities (PSD) between the case and the control groups (Figure 3). In both datasets (TUAB and CAU) and across all conditions (EEG anomalies, MCI, dementia), we observed simultaneous decrease and increase in EEG power depending on the frequency comparing cases to controls (Figure 3A). For clinical groups we found higher power in low frequencies (up to ≈ 8 Hz) and lower power in higher frequencies (≈ 8–32 Hz) compared to controls (Figure 3A, right panel). Moreover, topographic analyses revealed that this statistically significant pattern of power differences in low versus high frequencies (P<0.05) was expressed over large coherent sets of electrodes across a wide range of frequencies (Figure 3B).

The visualization of EEG power by quintiles suggests that the strength of this difference pattern may depend on age in potentially complex ways (Figure 3A). To overcome the challenge of imbalanced sample sizes between groups and age quintiles for the choice of meaningful binning (Tables S2–S4 in supporting information), we built a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) estimating average log EEG power as a function of group, age, and frequency (octaves 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 from the wavelet‐frequency grid) as well as all interactions. Next to main effects of group, frequency, and age, we found multiple significant higher order interactions for both datasets (Tables S4 & S5 in supporting information). Marginal means analyses at the centroids of the age quantiles and the octave frequencies revealed that power differences between cases and controls depended on age (Figure 4) and flipped their sign between low and high frequencies in line with the main effects (Figure 3). However, importantly, in TUAB the difference magnitude increased in older groups (Figure 4A), whereas in CAU, the frequency extent was narrower in younger people and broadened with age (Figure 4B).

These analyses demonstrate that clinical anomalies were associated with diverse and complex deviations from the EEG signals of age‐matched controls along the frequency spectrum and across age groups. The observed signature of higher power in low frequencies and lower power in high frequencies resembles the commonly studied cortical slowing phenotype characteristic of neurological disorders including AD dementia 56 , 57 , 58 and epilepsy. 59 Taken together, this suggests that EEG power spectra from patients do not simply look like prematurely aged—“old”—EEG power spectra from healthy reference populations.

Explaining the paradoxical EEG‐BAG with differences in cross‐sectional EEG trends for cases versus controls

We observed unexpected directions of association between EEG‐based brain‐age predictions in clinical cohorts. And we observed complex differences in EEG power spectra between clinical cases and healthy controls that depended themselves on chronological age. Synthesizing these findings provides us with possible explanations of paradoxically “younger” brain age in clinical populations. For example, neurodegeneration leads to aging trends in structural MRI that parallel aging trends seen during aging in controls, although earlier and faster. 6 , 13 , 14 , 16 , 17 For MRI, changes due to disease and aging are similar and differ mostly in their timing (illustrated in Figure 5A). On the other hand, given the rich brain signals captured by EEG, neither the characteristics of the changes nor their timing must be necessarily similar between disease and normal aging (illustrated in Figure 5B).

Accordingly, diseased brain activity and aged brain activity, thus, could diverge in multiple directions with time. As the brain‐age predictions are based on models trained with healthy controls, we can see them as nearest‐neighbor mappings to the reference population. Unexpected directionality of the EEG‐BAG occurs if patients’ EEG data do not follow typical aging trends and, for example, exhibit levels of brain activity typical of younger healthy controls. In fact, comparative visualizations of the EEG power spectra revealed a cortical slowing phenotype 49 , 57 , 58 , 60 , 61 , 62 for cases but not controls (Figure 3A, Figure 4). To establish a mechanistic link between model predictions and changes in EEG data, we conducted computational simulations in which we induced a cortical slowing phenotype on the testing data of controls. This was sufficient for producing a negative EEG‐BAG (Figures 5C–D). Inspection of the ML model revealed a dominant prediction rule relating beta‐band power around the central Pz electrode to higher age in the control cohorts (Figures 5E–G), whereas power trends over the central electrodes in that frequency range were reversed for case cohorts (Figure 3B). Similar deviations of power may occur via medication affecting spectral power, such as allosteric gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) modulators 63 (e.g., benzodiazepines) or differences in baseline physical activity. 64 It is therefore important to appreciate that clinical EEG signals, even if involving neurodegenerative disorders, do not necessarily present phenotypes associated with healthy aging. Conditions such as AD or epilepsy are caused by a complex interplay of genetics and life history, leading to physiological processes that do not occur equally in unaffected people. 65 , 66

DISCUSSION

We explored potential pitfalls of EEG‐based brain age, and confirmed previous incidental findings of clinical conditions were associated with lower rather than higher brain‐predicted age. 33 , 39 We argued that in clinical conditions such as neurodegenerative disorders, progressive changes in EEG can be different from those associated with normal aging in complex ways. This systematically impedes direct generalization of the MRI‐based brain‐age framework to EEG. Structural brain changes due to neurodegeneration are taken to be similar in their pattern but earlier and faster versions of the brain changes with aging. 6 , 13 , 14 , 16 , 17 Across large and ethnically diverse cohorts including > 3000 EEGs from the United States and South Korea, we demonstrated that age‐related changes in EEG power showed complex alterations in different clinical conditions with simultaneous frequency‐relative increases and decreases compared to age‐matched controls (Figures 3, 4). Unlike what the structural MRI literature found, that is, neurodegenerative atrophy with a pattern resembling an older brain, a clinical EEG does not necessarily look like an EEG of an older brain from a control cohort. We found that brain‐age models trained on the control groups showed systematically negative associations between brain age and clinical cohorts (“younger” brain age). The consistency of these patterns across datasets suggests that potential demographic confounds such as differences in head shape were mitigated using the 10 to 20 electrode placement system, data harmonization using the global scale factor, as well as joint model training with cross‐validation on control samples from both datasets. Crucially, our data simulations (Figure 5) mechanistically explain this paradox by showing that preferential sensitivity of the model to positive high‐frequency power led to systematic underprediction of age as the cortical‐slowing phenotype was induced computationally. Taken together, our results illustrate the hidden complexity of brain‐age modeling with functional data and raise the question how to best support the endeavor of exploring neurological biomarkers with rich brain‐activity measures using ML.

One motivation for the brain‐age methodology has been enhancing the analysis of smaller datasets in, for example, a clinical trial by applying ML models trained on population‐level data. Anatomical brain age has been explored for this end 6 , 17 as it has the advantage of providing an intuitive summary of structural changes and it may be effective at augmenting datasets in which normal aging and pathology share common effects. Emerging data from the pharmacological context suggests that the effects of study drugs may introduce similarly paradoxical effects for structural MRI as well: Monoclonal antibody therapies for AD were shown to induce an unexpected additional rate of brain atrophy. 67 , 68 Antisense‐oligonucleotide therapies can be associated with an expansion of the size of the ventricular system. 69 In the case of the antibody treatments, these changes occur even in the face of an established clinical benefit. Accelerated brain atrophy associated with a clinical benefit would imply a complex difference between brain‐age predictions and changes in the objective brain health for structural MRI due to treatment. Moreover, a recent study investigating brain age using structural MRI images in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)—a cohort with substantial co‐pathology and biomedical heterogeneity—has observed high variability and bias in brain‐age predictions coupled with low correlations with cognitive assessments, highlighting the mismatch between cross‐sectional and longitudinal modeling as an angle of explanation. 70

As the number of larger datasets for model training keeps growing also for EEG, it becomes practical to train models directly on targets of interest (e.g., diagnosis, progression) rather than constructing proxy measures. Our statistical analyses of cases versus controls in TUAB or AD dementia versus MCI in CAU add to the growing body of clinical neuroscience literature highlighting the capability of EEG for directly capturing brain signals in diverse populations of interest. 51 , 54 , 71 , 72 , 73 Exploring the wealth of existing ML models and methods for domain adaptation and transfer learning 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 might unlock reasonable brain‐age applications. But given the methodological issues exposed in this work, the indirect brain‐age modeling approach—applied to EEG—may not deliver on the promise of a simple and interpretable clinical tool that tells how “old the brain looks.” 13 , 17 , 79 The EEG‐BAG might still be useful in conjunction with other data as a proxy measure by adding statistical information to smaller clinical datasets even if falling short of providing an actionable biomarker. 6 Yet, even that path remains uncertain as a recent study investigated the advantage of MRI‐derived brain‐age models versus direct models of AD dementia and did not report any advantages of the brain‐age approach over direct prediction of dementia‐specific outcomes. 80

It is noteworthy that some applications of the EEG‐BAG showed expected results with higher brain age in dementia patients. 29 , 36 , 37 , 38 In that case, expected directionality would suggest that the prediction functions are of a similar shape and only shifted in time, for example showing similar aging trends in key features (Figure 5A). Expected directionality of EEG‐BAGs observed in studies involving sleep data 29 , 36 , 37 might imply that some of the sleep characteristics, for example, amount of deep sleep, slow‐wave activity, spindles, and sleep fragmentation, may lie on parallel trajectories in healthy and pathological aging and that those features were picked up by the prediction models. This would be consistent with positive associations between sleep impairments and markers of neurodegeneration, including MCI, 81 alongside tau and amyloid beta (Aβ) levels in the cerebrospinal fluid. 82 , 83 Even a single night without sleep can enhance cerebrospinal fluid markers of Aβ and tau by > 35% and translates into an MRI‐based brain age increase of 1 to 2 years. 84 , 85 On the other hand, it is possible that EEG‐derived brain‐age modeling based on anatomy‐informed source imaging extracts a trajectory that is constrained by the MRI. In a recent study reporting EEG‐BAG with expected directionality, mutual information features estimating synergy versus redundancy between brain regions conditional on all other neural interactions were used as model input. 38 This characteristic feature was by design independent from overall brain‐activity differences and instead, potentially, isolated changes in the spatial structure of the data related to the integrity of long‐range neuronal connections. The focus on spectral power and simple regularized linear prediction models in our study can be seen as a limitation. Yet, unexpected EEG‐BAG directionality has even been observed with complex deep learning architectures that can learn sophisticated features from raw EEG traces. 39 And none of this lessens the point developed in this work that, given potential confounds of EEG power, the behavior of the EEG‐BAG is not self‐evident and requires an explicit strategy or strong empirical sensitivity analysis. Even then, not all models with expected directionality and accurate predictions are guaranteed to be useful. 80

We argue that the intrinsic richness and versatility of EEG at detecting functional anomalies longs for intensified modeling both in case cohorts and control cohorts beyond ML‐based classification. For example, normative modeling 10 , 11 , 86 aims to create statistical descriptors of normal variations and can therefore be used to identify individuals that deviate therefrom. Brain growth charts describe development of the brain throughout the human lifespan. 12 Event‐based modeling aims to identify the sequence and timing of progression to improve understanding of the disease and to identify possible points for intervention. 62 , 87 The identification and definition of informative EEG biomarkers would be greatly facilitated by carefully assessing disease trajectories, their divergence to normative baselines, 86 modeling disease stages and progression of severity. 62 , 87 , 88 , 89 , 90

Despite the growing public availability of large amounts of training data, the field continues to face challenges of limited data availability. This has even affected our current work as a limitation as no detailed diagnoses were made available for TUAB and diagnoses for AD were purely clinical for the CAU data. The fundamental implication is that large samples from healthy reference populations are not sufficient. To perform trajectory modeling or direct classification, large datasets describing the clinical population of interest are required. The significance of ML for EEG comes down to the availability of trusted data—the right data and not only the big data. For one, there might be hidden potential in the process of examination, that is, increasing data quality by limiting the influence of adverse behavior such as lack of physical exercise, 91 and repeated assessment to cover intra‐subject variability. 33 For another, targeted studies of specific diseases and disorders with longitudinal assessments 92 are key to advancing the acceptance of EEG in clinical practice and as a trusted source for deriving robust biomarkers. We argue that, to make a difference, the community must come together and focus on overcoming the impediments for collecting large‐scale neurological data with high‐quality EEG.

METHODS

Data

TUAB

We analyzed recordings of the TUAB (control group [CG]: n = 124,554, cases: n = 899). 54 , 55 We focused on this dataset as it is widely used by the ML community for model development and we incidentally observed unexpected negative age predictions in prior work for this dataset. The age range of CG in TUAB is 12 to 95 years and 16 to 96 years for the case cohort. We excluded all EEGs from participants younger than 18 years (reflected in the sample sizes reported above).

As precise diagnoses are not available for this dataset and the recordings from that latter group contained heterogeneous anomalies stated by trained medical observers, the designation “pathological” is commonly used in the literature using the TUAB dataset. However, the reference publication reported that the medical reports mentioned epilepsy and stroke for 87% and 12% percent of the recordings, respectively, and we refer to the clinical group as cases or clinical cohort here to use more descriptive vocabulary. We refer to the “normal” group as controls or control cohort. That EEG was used for deriving the group definitions poses additional imitations.

CAU

We therefore focused on a second large‐scale clinical dataset, the CAU (CG: n = 459, MCI: n = 417, and dementia: n = 311) 52 corpora. This dataset was released to facilitate development of ML models and has the advantage that clinical groups of interest are defined based on standardized clinical criteria and not the EEG. Importantly, “the subjects were considered with MCI by the following criteria: i) intact function in activities of daily living, ii) the presence of memory complaints, iii) objective cognitive impairment (SD below education‐ and age‐adjusted norms) in more than one cognitive domain including memory on a comprehensive neuropsychological battery (…), iv) a Clinical Dementia Rating of 0.5, and v) non‐demented case according to the Diagnostic and DSM‐IV criteria.” 52 “Inclusion criteria for the normal subjects were as follows: i) intact activities of daily living and ii) no abnormality (within 1.0 SD of education‐ and age‐adjusted norms) on a comprehensive neuropsychological battery.” 52

The age range of the CG in CAU is 23 to 88 years, 50 to 93 years in MCI, and 54 to 96 years in AD dementia. We refer to individuals with normally/healthy/non‐pathologically labeled EEG recordings as controls or control cohort (CTL) and to individuals with a pathologically/MCI/dementia labeled recording as a clinical group or cases or clinical cohorts. It must be noted, though, that all recordings took place in a clinical setting, that is, were recorded from individuals that came to a hospital and for which staff decided to conduct an EEG examination. Furthermore, we used the terms “recordings” and “individuals” interchangeably, as we included one recording per participant.

ML pipeline

Preprocessing

The datasets were harmonized as closely as possible in terms of the individual's state (resting), referencing (common average), channel number (19), and order (alphabetically). To avoid potential anti‐aliasing artifacts and to use the wealth of data for estimation of power spectra, we kept individual recordings at their native sampling frequencies (200 Hz for CAU, 250 Hz or 256 Hz for TUAB). However, we ensured identical settings for high‐pass (1 Hz), low‐pass filtering (90 Hz), and notch‐filtering (60 Hz) using finite impulse response filters. We otherwise applied preprocessing as documented in our previous work 54 , 55 including bad segment rejection and epoch repair through autoreject, removal of artifact sources via independent components analysis, accounting of interindividual variability of global amplitude levels with the global scale factor. For additional details and citations, see the supporting information (section: EEG processing).

Feature extraction and model construction

We computed covariance matrices from EEG signals filtered with log‐linearly spaced Morlet wavelets 30 , 31 , 55 which we transformed to log power features (by extractring the log diagonal). We used ridge regression with hyperparameter tuning via generalized cross‐validation, which has proven highly effective and simple in concert with power‐spectral features. 55 We applied the same model configuration as in previous work. 55 For additional details, see the supporting information (section: EEG processing).

Model evaluation

We conducted 10‐fold cross‐validation on the joint control groups of TUAB and CAU. Data were shuffled and stratified with respect to the dataset of origin. For the joint model, to account for distribution differences between the datasets and groups, we included a dataset indicator to fit a dataset‐specific intercept term. During every fold, data (log power values and site indicator) as well as targets (age) were scaled to zero mean and unit variance with respect to the training set. In addition to the control groups, we evaluated the linear model (RidgeCV) on all clinical groups of both datasets.

EEG‐BAG

We computed EEG‐BAG as predicted minus chronological age to obtain intuitive measures of underestimation (negative sign) or overestimation (positive sign) of chronological age. We computed R2 scores as a metric sensitive to offset and scaling errors. Additionally, we computed Spearman rank correlation coefficients to assess the strength and direction of the monotonic relationship between the predictor and target variables.
