Diagnosis and emergency surgical management of stercoral colitis-induced colonic ischemia: A case report and literature review
Authors: Sirote Wongwaisayawan, Chonlada Krutsri, Arichaya Koosaksathaporn, Pattawia Choikrua
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceStercoral colitis is an urgent complication of fecal impaction that requires aggressive management. The rare complicated with bowel ischemia requires a high index of suspicion for early diagnosis. This case report describes the detection and management of this rare and fatal complication of stercoral colitis.Case presentationAn 80-year-old man presented after 3 days of obstipation. Abdominal plain radiography revealed several air-fluid levels in the colon with centralized small bowel gas. Computed tomography revealed fecal impaction and stercoral colitis without evidence of bowel ischemia.Clinical discussionFecal impaction and stercoral colitis without evidence of bowel ischemia was suspected. Owing to the development of refractory septic shock, we performed damage control surgery. Definitive surgery with end ileostomy was follow by 48 h later. The patient was discharged home safely.ConclusionStercoral colitis-induced ischemia is rare but potentially fatal; ischemia should be highly suspected. CT can help diagnosed of stercoral colitis but no single parameters for diagnosed of bowel ischemia. Prompt resuscitation and surgical exploration with damage control surgery are recommended.

Introduction

Chronic constipation leading to fecal impaction from the sigmoid colon to the rectum is mostly seen in elderlies with risk factors, such as bed-ridden status, chronic opioid or NSAIDs used [1]. Stercoral colitis is an unusual complication. Pathophysiology involves increasing intra-luminal pressure leading to pressure necrosis, together with inflammation lead to colonic wall ischemia, ulcer, sepsis, and septic shock, respectively [2]. The common sites locate in the least blood supply area (Recto-sigmoid or anterior rectum). Therefore, stercoral colitis usually progress proximally to cecum, secondary to increase intra-luminal pressure upstreaming [[1], [2], [3]]. However, early diagnosis of Stercoral ischemic colitis is challenging. A high index of suspicion in patients who develop sepsis with constipation is needed. An abdominal CT scan plays an important role but negative finding cannot exclude ischemia [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Morbidity and mortality rates range from 32 % to 57 % and higher with perforation or ischemia [3,4]. Therefore, a timely diagnosis and management can decrease morbidity and mortality.

We report a rare case of stercoral colitis complicated by entire colonic ischemia with refractory septic shock, with a literature review. Our aim was to demonstrate how to achieve early diagnosis and management of the condition.

This case report was written in accordance with the SCARE 2020 [5].

Literature search

The systematic literature review was performed in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines (Fig. 1) [6]. The PICO was adult patients over 15 year-old who had stercoral colitis complicated by colonic ischemia. We searched the PUBMED and SCOPUS databases for articles published between 2012 and 2022 in the English language, including case reports and original articles. The search terms were “stercoral colitis” OR “fecal impaction”. We recruited 354 studies from PUBMED and 184 studies from SCOPUS. After screened the titles and abstracts of the identified articles for potentially relevant studies, 561 studies were excluded. Full-text assessment was then performed to assess eligibility for inclusion in the current study. Finally, 5 studies were included, 2 studies from references were included, and including our case. If the first author was uncertain whether a study should be included, the corresponding author was consulted. The data were extracted, and patient characteristics, namely signs and symptoms at presentation, imaging findings, management, complications, follow-up, and outcome, were collected. Case presentation

History of present illness

An 80-year-old man with a 3-day history of constipation and progressive abdominal pain and a 1-day of low-grade fever and definitely oliguria.

On arrival, the patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. He had markedly abdominal distension, mild tenderness in the left lower abdominal quadrant without peritonitis. Per-rectal examination revealed impaction of hard stool. Shortly after arrival, the patient developed respiratory failure of rate 32 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation was 92 %, with shock which blood pressure was 59/31 mmHg and pulse rate were 102 beats per min.

Endotracheal intubation, volume resuscitation, and two inotropic drugs were administered. Blood culture was collected before empirical antibiotics. A foley catheter was inserted for monitoring urine as goal directed therapy. Abdominal radiographs revealed fluid-filled colon, dilated and centralized small bowel gas, and feces along left side colon (Fig. 2). Intra-abdomen, or either colonic obstruction was the suspected source of infection, which wasn't immediately mandated for surgical treatment. Abdominal CT scan with triple phase contrast was performed to find out whether there was any other indication that mandated the surgery. However, it illustrated fecal impaction at rectosigmoid colon with stercoral colitis which showed thickening, pericolic fat stranding together with mildly decreased enhancement of colonic wall, but without obstruction, pneumoperitoneum, portal vein gas, or obvious bowel ischemia (Fig. 3).Fig. 2Initial supine and upright abdominal radiographs. The patient was admitted to critical care unit with diagnosis of stercoral colitis with septic shock and respiratory failure. So, we decided to perform non operative treatment as fecal disimpaction for improving the colitis condition, which was kept in mind that the clinical of colitis could probably either improved or worsen. Unfortunately, after short close observation and adequate resuscitation, the clinical condition worsened as previous state in the manuscript. Finally, we immediately decided to do the operation.

History of past illness

The patient had underlying hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and triple-vessel disease and was taking daily aspirin and clopidogrel. He had chronic constipation without treatment or screening colonoscopy.

Physical examination upon admission

At the critical care ward, the blood pressure was maintained with two inotropic drugs. Mild left lower abdomen tenderness without peritonitis was found.

Laboratory examination

Leukocytosis with white blood cell count of 20.06 × 103/cumm and neutrophils predominating att 94 %, without bands, and platelet count of 219 × 103/cumm were found. Serum lactate was 5.1 mmol/l. There wasn't any coagulopathy. Acute kidney injury was found which serum creatinine rising from 0.98 to 1.54 mg/dl, which GFR was dropped to 42 ml/min/1.73 m2.

Final diagnosis

Stercoral colitis-induced bowel ischemia with septic shock was diagnosed. A timeline is show on Fig. 4.Fig. 4A timeliness of this case report. ER; emergency room, ICU; intensive care unit, OR; operation room, CT; computed tomography.Fig. 4

Treatment

Firstly, the patient was admitted to the critical care unit for non-operative management with an initial diagnosis of stercoral colitis with septic shock. Cardiovascular resuscitation with two inotropic drugs and respiratory support were performed. Manual fecal evacuation and polyethylene glycol were administered to relieve constipation. Unfortunately, the treatments failed. Serum lactate was worsening from 5.1 to 7.89 mmol/l over 2 h even adequate fluid resuscitation. Additionally, persistent metabolic acidosis with an arterial pH of 7.241 and bicarbonate of 14 mmol/l; coagulopathy, seen as an increase in the international normalized ratio from 1.08 to 1.5; and acute renal failure, seen as an increase in serum creatinine from 1.54 to 2.22 mg/dl occurred. Colonic ischemia was suspected as a complication of stercoral colitis with evidence of progressive septic shock and multi-organ failure, and immediately emergency source control with damage control surgery was mandated.

Intra-operatively, we found transmural colorectal ischemia progressing from the rectum to the cecum. Damage control surgery was done by resection of the mid-rectum to the terminal ileum without restoration and temporary abdominal closure, with 60 mins period and 20 ml of blood loss.

Postoperative resuscitation was performed until the hemodynamic state stabilized. Definitive surgery was performed 48 h later. A total resection of the lower rectum and anal canal owing to progression of rectal ischemia, end ileostomy was performed. The gross specimen revealed pressure necrosis of the lower rectum with normal mucosa in the anal canal, which confirmed the primary source of ischemia (Fig. 5).Fig. 5The gross specimen(A) and residual specimen(B). Show gross specimen of damage control resection of stercoral colitis induce ischemia and definite surgery gross specimen.Fig. 5

After the surgery, the patient's clinical status improved gradually. The acute kidney failure improved without long-term hemodialysis. The inotropic drugs were gradually tapered. The pathological report of colon indicated transmural necrosis with chronic colonic ulcer, but without vascular thrombosis or any malignancy, which confirmed the ischemia as progression of stercoral colitis. Together with findings in the lower rectum and anal canal indicated diffuse mucosal necrosis of bowel wall, which also confirmed the cause of the ischemia as fecal impaction causing local pressure necrosis (Fig. 6). The patient was discharged home safely.Fig. 6The histology specimen. Show mucosal necrosis in 40×(A) and 100×(B).Fig. 6

Outcome and follow-up

Sutures were removed in the out-patient unit. There were no complications from the ileostomy.

Discussion

Stercoral colitis develops from fecal impaction in the colon and rectum, resulting in rising of intraluminal pressure, colorectal wall ulceration, pressure necrosis of the colorectal wall, and inflammatory or infectious colitis, respectively [1,2]. Risk factors are bed-ridden, elderly, and medications that inducing constipation. Stercoral colitis occurs mostly in the sigmoid colon due to the narrowest part with less blood supply, and the hardest of feces [3,4]. The second most common location is the rectum or rectosigmoid junction, especially anteriorly, which is the anti-mesenteric section with a relatively low blood supply described as Sudeck's point [2,4] [[7], [8], [9]]. This condition is rare and need high index of suspicious [2,3] due to high morbidity and mortality when complicated by septic shock [[7], [8], [9]]. Early diagnosis of bowel ischemia, especially from stercoral colitis, is challenging. As with other causes of bowel ischemia, a high index of suspicion is required, and nothing confirmed unless explore to see it. The incidence of stercoral colitis is extremely low [2]. Mortality from stercoral colitis-induced ischemia is high, and this rate depends on how early the diagnosis is made, by combined sign of intra-abdominal infection and laboratory tests such as CBC or serum lactate, and proper managements of sepsis resuscitation [1,2] [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Our literature search identified seven English language case reports of stercoral colitis-induce ischemia [3,4] [[7], [8], [9], [10]] and show in Table.1. The average age was 67.29-year-old (range: 26–87 year- old). The incidence was similar in men and women. The common signs and symptoms suggestive of stercoral colitis are constipation with fecal impaction, abdominal pain and distension, and/or fever, which are the keys of stercoral colitis with or without ischemia. [[1], [2], [3], [4]] [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Laboratory findings provide indirect evidence of ischemia as leukocytosis and blood lactate >4 mmol/l, which is an indicator of septic shock. Severe refractory septic shock mostly occurs in stercoral colitis with ischemia due to progression of infection and bacteremia. The radiograph may show colonic distension and fecaloma along left side of colon. However, these findings are not specific. The diagnosis of stercoral colitis is confirmed by contrast enhanced abdominal CT scan [3], which is the most appropriate modality. Moreover, it also helps excluding alternative diagnosis, distinguishing simple fecal impaction from stercoral colitis and demonstrating related complications such as ischemia [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Uncomplicated fecal impaction or fecaloma appears on CT as colonic distension containing fecal material of a diameter equal to or greater than the colon. The colonic wall should remain thin [16]. If left untreated, the progressive intraluminal pressure will eventually compromise the blood supply, leading to ischemia, ulceration and perforation. In this stage, focal wall thickening of the affected colon and pericolonic fat stranding may be present. These likely symbolize colonic edema or ischemia [17,18]. Later, a perfusion defect or mucosal discontinuity is seen and indicates a change from colonic ischemia to infarction. This will appear as discontinuity of colonic mucosal enhancement or decreased enhancement compared to adjacent normal bowel loops [3], as seen in the presented case. Dense mucosa sign and mucosal sloughing may also be seen. Dense mucosa is defined as the increased mucosal lining density on pre-contrast CT and believed to represent mucosal hemorrhage [3]. The presence of extraluminal air bubbles or pericolonic abscess suggests that perforation has occurred [18]. A few articles denote that dense mucosa, perfusion defect, ascites and increased length of the affected colon >40 cm are significantly correlated with mortality [3]. Although, abdominal CT is the high accuracy key investigation modality for stercoral colitis [3] [[10], [11], [12]]. There is no single clinical, laboratory, or imaging modality for diagnosis of bowel ischemia; surgical exploration is the only option. On the other hand, the NOMI is a type of acute mesenteric ischemia which is vasospasm resulting in the ischemic change of the wall without anatomical obstruction of the vessel. It usually occurs in the distribution of the SMA arcade, as in the anatomy of the small bowel or right-side colon. Furthermore, it usually shows narrowing of the origin of the SMA, or even multiple branches of SMA, string of sausages sign, spasm of mesenteric arcades, and also impaired filling of intraluminal vessels on the CT scan. However, this case report didn't show any NOMI characteristics. Moreover, the pathological report of colon indicated transmural necrosis with chronic colonic ulcer, but without vascular thrombosis or any malignancy, which confirmed the ischemia as progression of stercoral colitis.

For emergency surgery of the reported patient, the damage control surgery is mandate. The damage control surgery is now widely performed in non-trauma abdominal pathology patients who need decontamination or infection source control while unstable hemodynamics, generalized peritonitis, acute mesenteric ischemia, and complicated pancreatic surgery. The damage control surgery is a procedure that resection of pathologic segment, deferred anastomosis by intestinal ends temporary left without reconstruction, and negative pressure dressing of open abdomen. The patients continue postoperative hemodynamics support, correct acidosis, hypothermia, and coagulopathy. After hemodynamics was stabilized 24–48 h (maximum 72 h), definite surgery with bowel reconstruction and/or closure of abdomen was performed. This procedure and timing of procedure is safe without increasing morbidity and mortality [19].

In summary, there is no single clinical, laboratory, or imaging finding to confirm the diagnosis of bowel ischemia in stercoral colitis. Close observation of the findings in multiple modalities with prompt surgical exploration with damage control surgery is recommended.

Conclusion

Stercoral colitis-induced ischemia is a rare but potentially fatal condition. Once a diagnosis of stercoral colitis is made, a high index of suspicion of ensuring ischemia is necessary, even if CT findings are inconclusive for ischemia. Close observation for the clinical signs of septic shock and providing hemodynamic support in stercoral colitis with prompt resuscitation and prompt surgical exploration with damage control surgery is recommended.
