Trends in tuberculosis-related mortality among adults 35–85 years old in the United States, 1999 to 2022: a nationwide analysis
Authors: Olivia Foley, Natalie Perme, Taylor Billion, Abubakar Tauseef, Renuga Vivekenandan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) is a significant cause of mortality in the United States (US), impairing individual health and causing national financial burden. Although TB-related mortality has declined in recent years, a variety of factors still make TB difficult to prevent, necessitating further analysis of which demographic groups are most impacted by TB.MethodsTrends in TB-related mortality in individuals aged 35 to 85 + years in the US from 1999 to 2022 were analyzed utilizing the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database. Following data collection, age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) and average annual percent change (AAPC) in TB-related mortality were examined. Data was further stratified by sex, race, age, region, and locality.ResultsBetween 1999 and 2022, there were 26,600 deaths related to TB in the US. Overall TB-related mortality significantly declined between 1999 and 2022 (AAPC, -3.95%). Males had consistently higher AAMR than females, with 16,741 deaths among males and 9,859 deaths among females. All racial groups, including Asian or Pacific Islander, Black or African American, White, and Hispanic or Latino individuals experienced significant declines in mortality (AAPC, -3.24%, -5.75%, -3.83%, -4.70%, respectively). Asian or Pacific Islander, Black or African American, and Hispanic or Latino patients had consistently higher AAMR than White patients between 1999 and 2022, however. Individuals older than 65 experienced significantly higher AAMR than younger individuals. South and West regions had higher AAMR than Northeast and Midwest regions, with the West and South experiencing the smallest and largest declines in mortality that were statistically significant, respectively (AAPC, -3.04%, -4.66%). TB-related mortality was higher in urban areas, with 20,680 deaths compared to 3,707 deaths in rural areas.ConclusionAlthough TB-related mortality has declined in the US overall, this improvement has not been experienced equally by all demographic groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03931-3.Keywords: Tuberculosis (TB), Mortality, Stratification, Trends, Disparities

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a contagious infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis that requires complex, long-term treatment and can result in numerous negative impacts on an individual’s health, including higher all-cause mortality rates for the remainder of life [1, 2]. Looking specifically at the United States (US), it was found that TB-related mortality decreased nationwide by 83.31% between 1980 and 2014 [3]. The incidence of TB in the US has also significantly decreased, from 9.7 cases per 100,000 people in 1993 to 2.2 cases per 100,000 people in 2020 [4]. Despite scientific and medical advances that have improved treatment and disease outcomes, drug-resistant forms of the bacteria, treatment compliance and comorbid conditions create difficulty with successful treatment and eradication.

Researchers estimate that effective global control and treatment of TB could prevent approximately 40,000 TB cases in the US between 2020 and 2035 [5]. This is expected to save nearly $800 million in healthcare costs and generate $2.5 billion in productivity gains during that period [5]. Although the US has made significant progress in reducing TB incidence and related mortality over the past few decades, the disease has not been completely eradicated. To enhance TB treatment and control, benefiting both the US financially and improving public health, it is crucial to understand current trends in TB-related mortality nationwide. This study will offer up-to-date data on the current TB-related mortality rate among adults aged 35–85 + years in the US and provide an analysis of population groups most affected by TB. This age group was selected based on the available data to focus on older adults and eliminate possible confounding variables when children and young adults are included in mortality trends. This updated data will help facilitate the development of targeted treatment and support strategies for different demographic groups, with the ultimate goal of improving TB outcomes and the overall health of the adult population in the US.

Methods

Study design and population

This study analyzed mortality data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) database for TB-related deaths in the US between 1999 and 2022. The Multiple Cause of Death database on CDC WONDER contains county-level national mortality and population data based on death certificates, which identify underlying causes of death. The causes of death, as pulled from the death certificate, correspond to searchable ICD-10 codes. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 10th Revision, Clinical Modification codes were used to analyze data regarding TB-related mortality and included ICD-10 codes A16-19 (A16, A17, A18, and A19). Diagnoses captured in these ICD codes include respiratory tuberculosis, tuberculosis of nervous system, tuberculosis of other organs, and miliary tuberculosis. This public-use database was utilized to extract data on TB-related mortality across different sexes, racial groups, localities, and regions in the US. This study was exempt from institutional review board (IRB) approval, as CDC WONDER database data is anonymized and publicly available.

Researchers extracted data from the combined time periods of 1999 to 2020 and 2020 to 2022. This data included number of deaths, crude mortality rates, and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). AAMRs are weighted averages of the age-specific death rates, where the weights represent a fixed population by age, allowing for comparison of relative mortality risk among groups over time. The year "2000 U.S. standard" was the default population selection for the calculation of age-adjusted rates in the CDC WONDER data and was used in this study [6].

The study population included the ten-year age groups of 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85 +. Data for adult patients under age 35 was excluded due to unreliability of the data in this age group and pediatric patients were excluded entirely. Sexes included males or females. Race and ethnicity groups included non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black or African American, NH Asian or Pacific Islander, and Hispanic or Latino. The NH American Indian or Alaska Native group was excluded due to unreliability of the data in CDC WONDER for this group within the study parameters. For urban–rural classifications (localities), the National Center for Health Statistics Urban–Rural Classification Scheme was used to divide the population into urban (large metropolitan area [population ≥ 1 million], medium/small metropolitan area [population 50,000 to 999,999]) and rural (population < 50,000) counties per the 2013 US census classification [7]. This data stratified by locality was not obtained for the time period of 2020 to 2022 due to unreliability of the data during those years. Regions were classified into Northeast, Midwest, South, and West according to Census Bureau definitions utilized by the CDC WONDER database.

Statistical analysis

After initial data collection, AAMR and average annual percent change (AAPC) throughout the study period were examined for each group. Initial data interpretation and graphical representation were conducted using Microsoft Excel, allowing for a comprehensive overview of mortality during the study period. The Joinpoint Regression Program (Joinpoint version 4.9.0.0 available from the National Cancer Institute in Bethesda, Maryland) was then used to determine trends in mortality [8]. This program identifies significant changes in annual mortality trends over time through Joinpoint regression, which fits models of linear segments where significant temporal variation occurred. Annual percentage change (APC) with 95% CIs for the AAMRs were calculated for the line segments linking a Joinpoint using the Monte Carlo permutation test [8]. The weighted averages of the APCs were calculated and reported as AAPCs and corresponding 95% CIs, summarizing the reported mortality trend for the entire study period. These were considered increasing or decreasing if the slope describing the change in mortality over the specified time interval was significantly different from zero using a 2-tailed t-test. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 (with significant data represented by asterisk ‘*’ in results and figures) [8].

Results

Overall data

Between 1999 and 2022, there were 26,600 deaths related to tuberculosis (TB) in the United States overall. Throughout the study period, there was a decrease in TB-related mortality (AAPC, −3.95%*, 95% CI, −4.44% to −3.39%) (Supplemental Fig. 1). Between 1999 and 2001 overall AAMR decreased significantly from 1.24 to 0.95 (APC, −13.28%*, 95% CI, −16.37% to −6.31%), decreasing further to 0.45 in 2014 (APC, −5.59%*, 95% CI, −6.62% to −1.68%), and finally remaining constant at 0.45 in 2022 (APC, 1.35%, 95% CI, −0.76% to 7.11%) (Supplemental Table 1, Fig. 1).

Demographicinformation

Stratification by Sex

Of the overall TB-related deaths from 1999 to 2022, 16,741 deaths (62.9%) were among male patients and 9,859 deaths (37.1%) were among female patients. Males had consistently higher AAMRs compared with females from 1999 to 2022 yet saw a greater decrease in mortality (AAPC in males −3.79%*, 95% CI, −4.36% to −3.26%, vs. −3.23%*, 95% CI, −3.95% to −2.67% in females) throughout the study period (Supplemental Table 1, Supplemental Fig. 1). Male AAMR decreased from 1.94 in 1999 to 0.71 (APC, −7.17%*, 95% CI, −8.83% to −6.08%) and further decreased to 0.70 in 2022 (APC, 0.03%, 95% CI, −1.49% to 2.77%) (Supplemental Table 1, Fig. 1). In females, the AAMR decreased significantly from 0.77 in 1999 to 0.40 in 2007 (APC, −7.08%*, 95% CI, −12.19% to −1.03%) (Supplemental Table 1, Fig. 1). Female AAMR continued to decrease from 0.40 in 2007 to 0.39 in 2012 (APC, −2.47%, 95% CI, −9.39% to 2.52%), further decreased to 0.28 in 2015 (APC, −9.42%, 95% CI, −13.74% to 6.36%), and finally increased to 0.34 in 2022 (APC, 3.69%, 95% CI, −5.34% to 12.63%) (Supplemental Table 1, Fig. 1).

Stratification by Race

Of the overall TB-related deaths from 1999 to 2022, 5,529 deaths were among NH Black patients, 12,720 deaths were among NH White patients, 3,621 deaths were among Hispanic patients, 3,991 deaths were among Asian or Pacific Islander patients, and the remaining deaths reported were among other racial groups not examined in this study. All races experienced an overall decrease in TB-related mortality from 1999 to 2022.

Asian or Pacific Islander patients experienced a decrease in overall TB-related mortality from 1999 to 2022 (AAPC, −3.24%*, 95% CI, −4.08% to −2.38%), yet maintained the highest AAMRs throughout the study period (Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Fig. 2). TB-related AAMR decreased for Asian or Pacific Islander patients from 5.35 in 1999 to 1.97 in 2014 (APC, −5.47%*, 95% CI, −8.32% to −4.15%), but then increased to 2.06 in 2022 (APC, 1.09%, 95% CI, −1.78% to 10.34%) (Supplemental Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3).

Trends in TB-related age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) stratified by race in the US, 1999–2022 (Asian or Pacific Islander 1999 to 2014 APC: −5.47*, 2014 to 2022 APC: 1.09; Black or African American 1999 to 2015 APC: −9.11*, 2015 to 2022 APC: 2.41; White 1999 to 2007 APC: −8.01*, 2007 to 2015 APC: −4.89, 2015 to 2022 APC: 2.48; Hispanic or Latino 1999 to 2014 APC: −6.79*, 2014 to 2022 APC: −0.65)

Trends in TB-related age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) stratified by age, 1999–2022 (Age 35–44 1999 to 2012 APC: −8.84*, 2012 to 2022 APC: −4.00; Age 45–54 1999 to 2017 APC: –5.30*, 2017 to 2022 APC: 5.86; Age 55–64 1999 to 2007 APC: −7.94*, 2007 to 2019 APC: −0.93, 2019 to 2022 APC: 9.59*; Age 65–74 1999 to 2014 APC: −6.58*, 2014 to 2022 APC: 4.18*; Age 75–84 1999 to 2014 APC: −6.35*, 2014 to 2022 APC: 0.17)

NH Black or African American patients experienced the greatest overall decrease in TB-related mortality of any racial group throughout the study (AAPC, −5.75%*, 95% CI, −6.62% to −5.04%) (Supplemental Fig. 2). TB-related AAMR among NH Black or African American patients decreased from 3.62 in 1999 to 0.68 in 2015 (APC, −9.11%*, 95% CI, −10.50% to −8.21%) and slightly increased to 0.89 in 2022 (APC, 2.41%, 95% CI, −1.74% to 14.82%) (Supplemental Table 2, Fig. 2). Despite this increase between 2015 and 2022, overall TB-related mortality rates declined among NH Black or African American patients throughout the study period.

NH White patients experienced a decrease in overall TB-related mortality from 1999 to 2022 (AAPC, −3.83%*, 95% CI, −4.40% to −3.32%) (Supplemental Fig. 2). TB-related AAMR for NH White patients decreased significantly from 0.78 in 1999 to 0.36 in 2007 (APC, −8.01%*, 95% CI, −13.30% to −6.21%), decreased again to 0.25 in 2015 (APC, −4.89%, 95% CI, −6.83% to 5.70%), and finally increased insignificantly to 0.28 in 2022 (APC, 2.48%, 95% CI, −1.05% to 11.54%) (Supplemental Table 2, Fig. 2).

Hispanic or Latino patients also experienced a significant decrease in TB-related mortality from 1999 to 2022 overall (AAPC, −4.70%*, 95% CI, −5.78% to −3.79%) (Supplemental Fig. 2). TB-related AAMR for this patient population decreased from 2.71 in 1999 to 0.72 in 2014 (APC, −6.79%*, 95% CI, −11.87% to −5.40%), and continued to decrease to 0.65 in 2022 (APC, −0.65%, 95% CI, −4.16% to 12.73%) (Supplemental Table 2, Fig. 2).

Stratification by Age

Of the overall TB-related deaths from 1999 to 2022, 1,389 deaths were among patients in the 35–44 year age group, 2,993 were among patients in the 45–54 year age group, 4,657 deaths were among patients in the 55–64 year age group, 5,682 deaths were among patients in the 65–74 age group, 7,060 deaths were among patients in the 75–84 year age group, and 4,819 were among patients in the 85 years or older age group. All age groups included in the study experienced an overall decrease in TB-related mortality during the study period. AAMR decreased in conjunction with decreasing age by group (Supplemental Table 3, Supplemental Fig. 3). Patients under age 35 were excluded from the study due to unreliable data within the study parameters in the database, which is discussed in the limitations section.

Patients aged 35 to 44 experienced an overall decrease in mortality from 1999 to 2022 (AAPC, −3.46%*, 95% CI, −5.11% to −1.98%) and had the overall lowest AAMR of the six age groups studied (Supplemental Table 3, Supplemental Fig. 3). TB-related AAMR significantly decreased for this age group from 0.29 in 1999 to 0.07 in 2012 (APC, −8.84%*, 95% CI, −13.87% to −6.33%), then increased to 0.12 by 2022 (APC, 4.00%, 95% CI, −0.95% to 18.73%).

Patients aged 85 or older experienced the largest overall decrease in mortality during the study period (AAPC, −4.72%*, 95% CI, −5.29% to –3.95%) and maintained the highest overall AAMR of the six age groups at both the start and end of the study period (Supplemental Table 3, Supplemental Fig. 3). TB-related AAMR significantly decreased for this age group from 8.11 in 1999 to 5.66 in 2001 (APC, −15.97%*, 95% CI, −20.83% to −6.48%), then further decreased to 2.34 by 2022 (APC, −2.04%, 95% CI, −8.97% to 5.12%).

Stratification by Region

Of the overall TB-related deaths from 1999 to 2022, 4,195 deaths were among patients in the Northeast, 4,424 deaths were among patients in the Midwest, 10,434 deaths were among patients in the South, and 7,547 deaths were among patients in the West. All four regions across the United States experienced an overall decrease mortality from 1999 to 2022.

The Southern and Western regions both had consistently higher AAMRs compared to the Northeast and Midwest regions, with the West having the highest overall AAMR among all four regions and the smallest decline in mortality (AAPC in West −3.04%*, 95% CI, −3.91% to −2.29%; vs. in South −4.66%*, 95% CI, −5.23% to −4.19%; vs. in Northeast −3.56%*, 95% CI, −4.23% to −2.93%; vs. in Midwest −3.30%*, 95% CI, −4.35% to −2.35%) throughout the study period. All regions, except for the Northeast, saw increases in TB-related AAMR during 2021 and decreases in 2022, with Northeast experiencing the opposite trend during those years (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4).

Trends in TB-related age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) stratified by region in the US, 1999–2022 (Northeast 1999 to 2005 APC: −10.19*, 2005 to 2014 APC: −4.00; 2014 to 2022 APC: 2.24; Midwest 1999 to 2012 APC: −7.31*, 2012 to 2022 APC: 2.17; South 1999 to 2009 APC: −8.10*, 2009 to 2017 APC: −4.09*, 2017 to 2020 APC: 9.14, 2020 to 2022 APC: −8.62; West 1999 to 2015 APC: −4.60*, 2015 to 2022 APC: 0.65)

Patients in the West experienced a decrease in overall mortality from 1999–2022 (AAPC −3.04%*, 95% CI, −4.23% to −2.93%), yet maintained the highest AAMRs throughout the study period (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4). TB-related AAMR significantly decreased for patients in the West from 1.56 in 1999 to 0.65 in 2015 (APC −4.61%*, 95% CI, −7.65% to −3.71%), but then plateaued and slightly increased from 2015 to 2022 (APC 0.65%, 95% CI, −2.73% to 12.86%) (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4).

Patients in the South experienced the greatest overall decrease in mortality of the four regions throughout the study period (AAPC, −4.66%*, 95% CI, −5.23% to −4.19%), but maintained the second highest AAMRs behind the West (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4). TB-related AAMR significantly decreased for patients in the South from 1.47 in 1999 to 0.72 in 2009 (APC −8.11%*, 95% CI, −11.14% to −6.72%) and further decreased to 0.62 in 2017 (APC −4.09%*, 95% CI, −9.00% to −0.43%). TB-related AAMR increased to 0.68 in 2020 (APC 9.14%, 95% CI, −4.49% to 13.28%), then after a jump to 0.73 in 2021, decreased again to 0.65 in 2022 (APC −8.62%, 95% CI, −15.09% to 2.39%) (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4).

Patients in the Northeast experienced a decrease in mortality throughout the study period (AAPC, −3.56%*, 95% CI, −4.23% to −2.93%) (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4). TB-related AAMR significantly decreased for patients in the Northeast from 1.06 in 1999 to 0.53 in 2005 (APC −10.19%*, −17.24% to −6.92%) and further decreased to 0.36 in 2014 (APC −4.00%, 95% CI, −7.79% to 0.58%). By 2022, there was a slight increase in TB-related AAMR to 0.46 (APC 2.24%, 95% CI, −0.67% to 11.68%) after having decreased to 0.39 in 2021 (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4).

Patients in the Midwest started with the lowest overall mortality of the four regions and experienced the second smallest, but significant, decrease (AAPC −3.30%*, 95% CI, −4.35% to −2.35%) during the study period (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4). TB-related AAMR significantly decreased for patients in the Midwest from 0.96 in 1999 to 0.34 in 2012 (APC −7.31%*, 95% CI −10.73% to −5.62%). For the next decade, mortality rates fluctuated around 0.3 to 0.5, with an overall increase from 2012 to 2022 (0.34 to 0.37, APC 2.17%, 95% CI, −0.86% to 11.09%) (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 4).

Stratification by Locality

Of the overall TB-related deaths from 1999 to 2020, 20,680 deaths were among patients in urban areas and 3,707 deaths were among patients in rural areas. Urban areas had higher TB-related AAMRs compared with rural areas from 1999 to 2020 and saw a smaller decrease in mortality (AAPC, urban −4.37%*, 95% CI, −4.92% to −3.78%; vs. rural −4.55%*, 95% CI, −5.65% to −3.53%) throughout the study period (Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Fig. 5). Urban area AAMR significantly decreased from 1.31 in 1999 to 1.02 in 2001 (APC, −12.65%*, 95% CI, −15.75% to −6.20%), further significantly decreased to 0.45 in 2015 (APC, −5.39%*, 95% CI, −6.33% to −3.44%), then increased to 0.51 in 2020 (APC, 2.16%, 95% CI, −1.44% to 12.1%) (Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Fig. 5). Rural area AAMR significantly decreased from 1.09 in 1999 to 0.43 in 2009 (APC, −7.79%*, 95% CI, −15.78% to −5.74%), then further decreased and later plateaued to 0.47 in 2020 (APC, −1.51%, 95% CI, −3.92% to 8.45%) (Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Fig. 5) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study highlights key findings on TB-related mortality in the US, demonstrating an overall decrease from 1999 and 2022, likely due to improvements in TB treatments. As of 2018, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends treatment of drug-susceptible tuberculosis involving a 2-month intensive phase with RIPE therapy (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol daily), followed by 4 months of dual therapy with isoniazid and rifampicin. Though cost-effective and successful, this 6-month regimen can be lengthy with many side effects, and compliance can be a further issue [9]. Recent research has also introduced four new drug regimens for multidrug-resistant TB, reducing treatment time for these strains by 60% while maintaining efficacy and safety [10]. These advancements are crucial for improving overall TB treatment and supporting public health efforts to eradicate the disease.

A retrospective study analyzing the mortality rates among TB survivors and a control population between 1993 and 2002 found that TB survivors experience more all-cause deaths post-treatment than those who had never been infected with TB [2]. As such, it is important to determine which populations are most impacted by TB disease burden to make advanced treatments available for these groups and improve population health overall. Initial prevention of TB is an important public health endeavor, and the BCG vaccine remains the only licensed vaccination for tuberculosis prevention but is not widely administered in the US. Furthermore, this vaccine tends to be most effective in young children and thus cannot address the prevalence of disease and disparities in the adult population seen in this study [11].

This study highlighted that despite experiencing a greater overall decrease in mortality, males experienced consistently higher AAMRs regarding TB-related deaths than females between 1999 and 2022. Numerous studies have identified male sex as a risk factor for increased TB incidence and mortality, and these study results indicate that this trend is continuing [12, 13]. Alcohol consumption and smoking are both risk factors for TB contraction, and men experience higher usage rates than women in both categories [14]. This indicates that decreased alcohol use and smoking among men could potentially improve TB-related mortality in this sex group overall.

A well-known risk factor for TB is living in congregate environments, like homeless shelters and prisons. Men are more likely to be homeless compared to women, though men tend to use less shelter services. Women are more likely to have children with them and need the layer of security provided by a shelter setting, however they are also underrepresented in homelessness statistics [15, 16]. According to the most recent data released from the Prison Policy Initiative, men also make up over 91% of the state prison populations in the US, thus putting them at greater risk of exposure to infectious illnesses, such as TB [17]. Race also compounds this issue, with racial disparities in the criminal justice system being evident. As of 2016, despite making up only 12.3% of the US population, NH Black persons made up 33.3% of incarcerated individuals in state prisons [17]. This study displays certain demographic groups who are at higher risk of mortality via TB once infected, including men and non-white individuals; this disproportionately affects persons in congregate living environments who have higher risk of contracting the disease in the first place. Looking at the availability of treatment and compliance for individuals in these congregate environments who contract TB would be a useful area of further study on this topic to better understand how the higher risk groups in these settings could receive more equitable, effective treatment, thus reducing mortality. Important data to collect could include rates of RIPE regimen, or other first-line treatment, by providers for patients in these settings, amount of TB treatments covered by insurance, and insurance rates among these patients.

When stratified by race, data showed that all races experienced a decline in TB-related mortality. NH Black or African American, Asian or Pacific Islander, and Hispanic or Latino populations experienced consistently higher AAMRs than their NH White counterparts. TB impacts a wide variety of diverse populations within the US, with a larger disease burden among certain racial and ethnic groups, which is reflected in the data. Disparities have been shown between different population cohorts, and one study published in 2014 found that from 1999 to 2009, American Indians/Alaska Native individuals had a significantly higher death rate due to TB than did NH White persons [18]. Additional research studies have found that NH Black and Hispanic individuals face higher TB-related mortality and complication rates when compared to NH White individuals [19]. These racial disparities can potentially be explained by the historical impact that structural racism and racial discrimination have on diverse racial and ethnic groups, as well as disparities in incarceration rates as previously mentioned. Race and ethnicity are social, not biological, concepts, yet these categorizations negatively correlate with many discriminatory processes that have led to undue exposure to TB risk factors, especially in non-White patients [19, 20]. Risk factors that stem from systemic racism and discrimination disproportionately affect minority groups and must be addressed to improve TB-related outcomes in these groups moving forward. These risk factors include comorbid illness, poverty, unemployment, and lack of insurance, among others [19, 21, 22].

HIV has been shown to be an independent risk factor for TB contraction and mortality [12, 13, 19, 23]. One recent study found that “TB accounted for 1 out of every 3 deaths among persons with HIV in 2017” [24]. Furthermore, among persons diagnosed with concurrent TB and HIV, both NH Black and Hispanic patients were found to be at higher risk of contracting TB due to recent HIV transmission [24]. Additionally, immunosuppressed individuals, such as transplant patients and those with chronic diseases including diabetes, cancer, and viral hepatitis, are also at higher risk and this risk is only compounded by additional racial and ethnic disparities as found in this study [25].

This study demonstrated that people aged 65 years and older bear a much larger health burden due to TB than those younger than 65. Along with age independently being identified as a risk factor for TB contraction and mortality, there are a variety of other factors that contribute to this trend [12]. Residing in a long-term care facility, a type of congregate living environment, has been identified as a risk factor for mortality, similar to residing in a prison or a homeless shelter. This majorly impacts older populations who tend to live in these environments more than any other age group [13, 23]. Along with the environmental risk, comorbid illnesses, such as cancer and diabetes, increase risk of death from TB as mentioned previously. Older populations accumulate a larger amount of chronic disease burden due to time and as such are undoubtedly at higher risk of disease mortality [26]. Advanced age upon contraction of TB is also associated with increased risk of treatment failure and mortality, which can explain the findings presented from this study [4]. The increasing weakness of a patient’s immune system as they age decreases their ability to fight off infections such as TB successfully, increasing mortality risk regardless of other risk factors.

TB-related mortality varies by region, with the West and South regions exhibiting the highest AAMR values in the study. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in socioeconomic status (SES), a recognized risk factor for higher TB burden [27]. A study in California, located in the West region, highlighted a significant disparity in TB rates between low and high SES areas [28]. Notably, eight out of the top ten poorest states in the country are in the South, with one also in the West [29]. Therefore, focusing on these regions and areas with low SES will be crucial for effective TB treatment nationwide.

Despite a common trend that health outcomes are worse in rural areas due to a lack of access to adequate healthcare, this study found that TB-related mortality is worse in urban areas of the United States. TB is a contagious disease that can be spread via droplets in the air, with proximity and larger populations increasing risk and inherently allowing for easier spread [27]. Urban areas are generally more crowded than rural areas, potentially explaining the difference in TB-related mortality. As such, urban areas are crucial to target with strict isolation protocol and treatment implementation to reduce spread of TB overall.

Limitations & conclusion

Between 1999 and 2022, the US population experienced a significant decline in TB-related mortality overall. Upon further analysis, this study revealed notable disparities in TB-related disease burden between sexes, races, age-groups, regions, and localities.

Data collection from the CDC WONDER database poses some limitations, as other variables and social determinants of health could be impacting these trends that are not listed in the public database. One notable limitation with this database for TB specifically is that the death certificates may not always accurately capture whether TB was a contributing factor in a person’s death. This could be more accurately done with TB verified cases from the CDC, which look at death by tuberculosis incidence. This could paint a clearer picture of mortality rates that are impacted by differences in TB incidence and would be an excellent area of future analysis on this topic building off our study [30].

This study included data on only adults aged 35 or older and did not measure the pediatric population. With the ten-year age group of 35–44 being the youngest group with reliable data for the chosen variables, conclusions drawn from this study cannot be reliably generalized to persons of all ages, as all those under age 35 were excluded. Along with this, researchers were only able to analyze TB-related mortality stratified by locality (rural vs. urban) from 1999–2020 due to insufficient data beyond this date. Finally, the American Indian or Alaskan Native racial group was excluded from the study due to a lack of reliable data within the study parameters. Whether this is due to very low disease prevalence in this community leading to unreliable data or lack of appropriate data gathering for these communities cannot be determined, but it is important to consider, nonetheless. Despite these limitations, however, the disparities in TB burden identified in this study warrant further attention and investigation to eliminate TB-related mortality in the US.
