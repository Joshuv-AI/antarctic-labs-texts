Novel Amino Acid Changes Increase Phosphoenolpyruvate Carboxylase Output in planta
Authors: Jinrui Sheng, Kevin Frausto, Sarah Haesaerts, Taylor J. Meyer, Maruša Prolič‐Kalinšek, Seán E. O'Leary, Remy Loris, Paul B. Larsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCharacterization of previously reported Arabidopsis mutants with increased aluminum (Al) resistance (alr) resulting from greater release of Al‐chelating malate identified three unique amino acid substitutions that each impacts PHOSPHOENOLPYRUVATE CARBOXYLASE 1, a key anaplerotic carbon fixation enzyme. Expression of mutant versions of AtPPC1 in an Arabidopsis ppc1 loss‐of‐function mutant increased Al resistance in association with upwards of threefold higher release of Al‐chelating malate from roots. Analyses of enzyme kinetics and protein structure of the variants revealed that each amino acid position is critical to proper interplay between the allosteric regulator malate and the active site. Since these affect amino acid positions generally conserved amongst plant PPCs, they were individually introduced into maize PPCs including ZmPPC1, the isozyme responsible for the first step of C4 photosynthesis, and ZmPEP7, a key PPC in resupply of the TCA cycle via anaplerosis. This resulted in similar or greater improvements in enzyme activity and reduced regulation in vitro for both ZmPPC1 and ZmPEP7 along with increased PPC‐dependent output in planta for a ZmPEP7 transgenic. Therefore, engineering these amino acid changes into plants in general could be useful for increasing carbon fixation by PPCs to turn excess CO2 into a resource for our growing population.

Introduction

Aluminum (Al) toxic soils represent one of the largest agricultural challenges since they comprise approximately 50% of the world's arable land (von Uexküll and Mutert 1995). Under acidic conditions Al speciates to the Al3+ form, which is highly inhibitory to root growth through binding to a wide range of both apoplastic and symplastic biochemical targets largely because of its strong affinity for anionic sites (Kochian 1995; Kochian et al. 2015). While plants have developed various strategies to deal with Al toxicity including true tolerance to internalized Al, due to the complexity of targets the best adaptation for plants to grow in an Al toxic environment is to reduce internalization through release of Al‐chelating organic acids including malate and citrate into the rhizosphere (Delhaize et al. 1993; Miyasaka et al. 1991; Pellet et al. 1995; Ryan et al. 1995a, 1995b). Enhanced organic‐acid release can be achieved either through higher rates of exudation as a result of greater expression of malate and/or citrate transporters (Delhaize et al. 2004; Hoekenga et al. 2006; Magalhaes et al. 2007; Sasaki et al. 2004), or by transgenic manipulation of expression levels of key metabolic enzymes involved in production of these Al‐chelators (De la Fuente et al. 1997; Begum et al. 2009). Yet currently there are no reports of engineering native metabolic enzymes to improve their output to promote Al resistance, which not only could improve agricultural output in Al‐toxic soils but also yield the dual advantage of increasing conversion of excess CO2 into these beneficial organic acids.

Results and Discussion

PPC1 Mutations Confer Al Resistance

Arabidopsis mutants (alr) with increased Al resistance due to greater exudation of Al‐chelating malate were previously reported (Larsen et al. 1998), but the specific nucleotide sequence changes of these semi‐dominant mutations were not identified. We leveraged next‐generation whole‐genome sequencing to identify these changes, starting with a genomic DNA library extracted from seeds of an original mutant, alr−128. A homozygous mutation was found in the alr−128 genome at nucleotide 19885293 of chromosome 1 proximal to its previously determined location, which is near the microsatellite marker nga280 (Larsen et al. 1998) (Figure 1A and Supporting Information S1: Table 1). This C to T transition in gene At1g53310, encoding PHOSPHOENOLPYRUVATE CARBOXYLASE 1 (PPC1), was consistent with the EMS‐mutagenesis approach used to generate the alr mutants.

Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase is a key carbon fixation enzyme and part of a four‐member family in Arabidopsis that has closely related homologs in plants and bacteria in general (Chollet et al. 1996; Sanchez et al. 2005; Ting and Osmond 1973). The single‐base substitution in alr−128 results in an AtPPC1 missense mutation, G678S. Sequencing of At1g53310 in two additional previously reported alr mutants, alr−108 and alr−139, also revealed EMS‐dependent mutations (C to T in each CDS), resulting in an A651V AtPPC1 variant for alr−108 and T778I for alr−139. Sequence comparison of AtPPC1 to other plant PPCs (Figure 1B), including members of the Flaveria sp. family that have been used for key PPC studies along with agricultural plants including Zea mays, Sorghum bicolor, Glycine max and Oryza sativa, shows that the amino acid positions affected in the primary structure of AtPPC1 are widely and highly conserved (Besnard et al. 2002; Bläsing et al. 2002; Engelmann et al. 2003; Jacobs et al. 2008; Lin et al. 2004; O'Leary et al. 2011; Paulus et al. 2013a, 2013b; Wang et al. 2016) including in those PPCs that employ C4 attributes such as ZmPPC1 that participates in C4 photosynthesis. This strongly suggests that the apparent beneficial effects of these amino acid changes on AtPPC1 output may be translatable to key agricultural plants in general to increase PPC‐dependent carbon fixation.

PPCs are foundational anabolic enzymes that exist in bacteria and plants primarily as part of anaplerosis to replenish intermediates of the TCA cycle through production of oxaloacetate (OAA) from phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and CO2‐derived bicarbonate (HCO3 −) (Chollet et al. 1996). Arguably, increased PPC output in plants would be translated into increases in essential OAA‐dependent processes such as malate production in C4 photosynthesis; malate‐dependent resistance to soil stressors such as Al, low phosphate, excess ammonium, and salinity; malate‐fed nitrogen fixation; and production of OAA‐derived amino acids including aspartate, lysine and methionine (Chollet et al. 1996; Wang et al. 2016; Perez‐Lopez et al. 2023; Arias‐Baldrich et al. 2017). Because C3 PPCs such as AtPPC1 are required for anaplerosis, they are considered to comprise a key node in central metabolism that is highly regulated. This is in contrast to the aforementioned highly specialized C4 PPCs that participate in C4 photosynthesis as a means of increasing the supply of CO2 to the metabolic phase of photosynthesis through the formation of OAA and subsequently malate.

In order to demonstrate that the missense mutations attributed to alr−108, alr−128 and alr−139 are indeed the basis for the Al resistance phenotype in each, a transgenic approach was utilized in which gene constructs consisting of the native promoter, UTR's, exons and introns of At1g53310 were independently introduced into an Arabidopsis mutant that has a T‐DNA insertion at this locus (ppc1, Salk_088836). In conjunction with this, two amino acid changes that are key differences between C3 and C4 PPCs were included in the study to determine if either could also unlock C3 PPC activity to confer Al resistance. C4 versions such as maize PPC1 (ZmPPC1) have two key alterations (A780S and R890G) that make these variants highly optimized for activity and output (Besnard et al. 2002). A780S is an active site mutation that affects the affinity for HCO3 − (DiMario and Cousins 2019) whereas R890G alters the binding pocket for malate, with introduction of Gly for Arg reducing the affinity for and inhibitory effects of this allosteric inhibitor (Engelmann et al. 2003; Jacobs et al. 2008; Paulus et al. 2013a, 2013b; Endo et al. 2008). This then allows the enzyme to stay active at higher internal malate concentrations, including those in mesophyll cells as part of C4 photosynthesis (Weiner and Heldt 1992).

Elevated PPC Output Confers Al Resistance

Comparison of growth of Col‐0 wt and the ppc1 loss‐of‐function mutant revealed that ppc1 roots are slightly more Al sensitive, which is consistent with the loss of expression resulting from the T‐DNA insertion in the fifth intron of At1g53310 (Figure 1C). Because competitive PCR showed the ppc1 loss‐of‐function mutant had no detectable expression of At1g53310 (Sanchez et al. 2005), it made for an excellent model for substituting modified versions of the AtPPC1 gene into this background for subsequent analysis of the impact of each mutation on Al resistance.

We next leveraged the ppc1 loss‐of‐function mutant background to generate multiple transgenic lines for the following: AtPPC1 WT , AtPPC1 A651V , AtPPC1 G678S , and AtPPC1 T778I using modified forms of the previously discussed PPC1 transgene. We also generated a set of AtPPC1 R886G lines reflecting natural variation between C3 and C4 versions. Generation of transgenic lines for AtPPC1 A776S was also attempted yet expression of this variant could not be achieved in planta. Competitive PCR analysis revealed successfully restored expression of all other transgenes in the ppc1 background resulting in multiple transgenic lines for each variant (Supporting Information S3: Figure 1).

Seedlings of each transgenic line along with Col‐0 wt and ppc1 were grown for 6d in the absence or presence of highly inhibitory levels of AlCl3 in a soaked gel environment (pH 4.2) after which root lengths were measured (Figure 2A). Consistent with the requirement of AtPPC1 for normal Al resistance, ppc1 roots were slightly more inhibited by Al than either Col‐0 wt or AtPPC1 WT transgenic lines. In contrast, transgenic lines expressing either AtPPC1 R886G or any of the alr AtPPC1 variants showed greatly increased Al resistance with the transgenics for AtPPC1 A651V and AtPPC1 G678S being especially superior even in comparison to AtPPC1 R886G . It is not clear why some transgenic lines were not as robust as Col‐0 wt in the absence of Al and it is possible that lines such as AtPPC1 A651V line 1 and AtPPC1 T778I line 30 have reached the limit of what can be tolerated in planta with increased PPC output.

To deepen our understanding of the physiological basis for the effects of the AtPPC1 variants, the best performing line for each transgene variant was selected for further analysis. This included growth across a range of Al concentrations (pH 4.2) where the alr AtPPC1 variants were found to maintain strong root growth beyond that of even AtPPC1 R886G at levels of Al that are highly inhibitory to AtPPC1 WT roots (Figure 2B). While some transgenic lines were shorter than controls in the absence of Al in Figure 2A, this seemingly was experimental variability or line to line differences from transgenesis rather than a consequence of the various mutations since this was not observed in Figure 2B. In support of our findings in a soaked gel environment, use of a hydroponic system comparing growth with and without 25 μM AlCl3 (pH 4.2), the latter of which is an environmentally relevant concentration (15), shows the same pattern of response with all of our PPC variants having greater growth with Al than the controls. Consistent with these findings, Al‐treated roots of the alr AtPPC1 variants and to a lesser extent AtPPC1 R886G had reduced accumulation of Al‐induced callose, a polysaccharide indicator of Al‐dependent damage (Weiner and Heldt 1992; Llugany et al. 1994; Horst et al. 1997), as well as reduced Al uptake (Vitorello and Haug 1997) (Figure 2D). Measurement of organic acid exudation by roots of the AtPPC1 variants showed that in comparison to Col‐0 wt, ppc1 and AtPPC1 WT , the alr AtPPC1 transgenics along with AtPPC1 R886G all had increased malate and citrate exudation with the line for AtPPC1 A651V having almost 300% more malate release than Col‐0 wt. Unlike what was previously reported (Larsen et al. 1998), no pyruvate was detected in our root exudates. Analysis of internal levels of organic acids in roots revealed a similar outcome for malate with transgenic lines expressing modified forms of AtPPC1 having upwards of twofold higher malate compared to controls. The observed increases in malate both internally and externally were greatest for AtPPC1 A651V and AtPPC1 G678S , which is consistent with these being the most Al‐resistant lines both in our current analyses and in a previous study (Larsen et al. 1998). In contrast to internal malate, levels of internal citrate were largely comparable for all lines tested suggesting that while enhancing flow of PEP through PPCs, the alr mutations do not cause an associated drag on catabolism through pyruvate kinase. As indicated by the comparable lengths of control roots and adult plant size (Figure 2B, Supporting Information S3: Figure 2), there is no obvious growth penalty for overproduction of malate at these levels or obvious negative effects on adult plant size or morphology. Of course, there is almost certainly a limit to what can be tolerated by plants with regard to increased flow through central metabolism especially without a concomitant increase in relevant sink strength. Yet based on our results that clearly show Al resistance is conferred by these novel changes to AtPPC1 that were identified in an unbiased mutant screen, there is substantial room for improvement of PPC output in planta by introduction of these single amino acid changes.

alr Mutations Desensitize AtPPC1 to Malate and Improve Other Kinetic Parameters

To understand the effects of these missense mutations on enzyme function, each of the alr mutations along with the mutations for AtPPC1 A776S and AtPPC1 R886G were individually introduced into an AtPPC1 cDNA construct in pET22b to be used for bacterial expression. Following production by Escherichia coli, AtPPC1WT and variants were purified to homogeneity and analyzed for their enzymatic and structural properties. For enzymatics, PPC1 steady‐state turnover at varying PEP concentrations was measured in the presence of KHCO3. Formation of the OAA product was quantified through its conversion to malate by malate dehydrogenase (MDH), in an NADH‐dependent reaction (Supporting Information S3: Figure 3) (Janc et al. 1992). Steady‐state initial reaction rates were fitted to the Michaelis‐Menten model to determine K m PEP , V max , and K i malate for AtPPC1WT and variants. PPC susceptibility to malate was quantified by the addition of increasing concentrations of this allosteric inhibitor to the assay. All enzyme variants analyzed showed a linear dependence of their steady‐state turnover on enzyme concentration in the range used for our assays (Supporting Information S3: Figure 4). For crystallography, the structures of AtPPCWT and variants in the absence or presence of phosphate (R state) or malate (T state) were determined, with the former chosen as a proxy for the substrate due to the rapid enzymatic conversion of PEP.

Enzymatic analysis of AtPPC1WT revealed kinetic parameters to be expected of an anaplerotic C3 type PPC including being highly sensitive to concentrations of malate that are comparable to predicted cellular concentrations in other plant species (Figure 3A, Supporting Information S3: Figure 5, and Table 1) (Weiner and Heldt 1992). At the structural level, malate‐dependent inhibition and the transition between the R‐ and T‐states are manifested at the C‐terminal segment G962‐G967 and the internal segment H635‐P656, both of which are required to form a functional malate binding site in the T‐state (Figure 3B and Supporting Information S1: Tables 2 and 3).

A651, G678, T778, and R886 all reside in the same region of AtPPC1 in the neighborhood of the malate binding site, however with the exception of R886 they are not directly involved in malate binding. Besides AtPPC1G678S, these mutations (AtPPC1A651V, AtPPC1T778I, AtPPC1R886G) do not influence directly the R state, and no significant structural adjustments are observed in this state suggesting that when the enzyme is active these amino acid changes are well accommodated. In a remarkable contrast with the enzyme kinetics of the AtPPC1WT enzyme, the A651V substitution effectively abolished PPC inhibition by malate (Figure 3C,D and Table 1), even at concentrations approaching 50 mM. Beyond malate inhibition, AtPPC1A651V also showed substantially enhanced steady‐state turnover. K m PEP was reduced by ~80% for AtPPC1A651V, accompanied by an ~15% reduction in V max compared to AtPPC1WT in the absence of malate that likely would have little drag on enzymatic activity at cellular concentrations of malate. AtPPC1A651V has an obvious structural effect with the more bulky Val side chain pushing against loop R637‐G644 to prevent a steric clash (Figure 3D). This loop contains R643, the side chain of which makes two hydrogen bonds to the bound malate. Yet this Arg side chain in AtPPC1A651V does not move significantly (0.4 Å, within the errors of the coordinates) and the corresponding hydrogen bonds are retained. The AtPPC1A651V mutation thus likely weakens the T conformation, shifting the structural equilibrium towards the R state. In addition, this mutation weakens malate binding as the electron density for malate in its canonical binding site is far poorer than what is observed for AtPPC1WT. Unexpectedly, a malate molecule is bound to AtPPC1A651V at what was formerly identified as the citrate binding site in AtPPC3 (PDB entry 5FDN) (Connell et al. 2018). This site involves R116, G645 and R886 (R116, G647 and R887 in AtPPC3) and is located 9.5 Å from the canonical malate binding site (Supporting Information S3: Figure 6), suggesting that in conjunction with observed increases in enzyme velocity at high concentrations, high malate could instead serve as an activator of AtPPC1A651V.

Comparison of AtPPC1G678S to AtPPC1WT showed that while more subtle it also had a significantly higher K i malate in conjunction with an an increase in V max in the absence of malate (Figure 3E and Table 1). The overall increase in robustness, which is consistent with this amino acid change also affecting the R state, is in spite of this amino acid position not being directly associated with either the active site or the malate binding pocket. Structural analysis shows for the R‐state, the Ser side chain AtPPC1G678S makes novel hydrogen bonds to the side chains R190 and E674 that may provide additional stabilization of the R‐state. For the T‐state, AtPPC1G678S likely increases K i malate by indirectly weakening malate binding through Q675 as the amino acid change induces a small (largest Cα movement 1.0 Å) local shift of the segment I673‐H681 (Figure 3F). Additionally, a new hydrogen bond is potentially present between the Ser side chain in AtPPC1G678S and the main chain carbonyl of E674.

AtPPC1WT was also enzymatically compared with AtPPC1A776S, which affects an amino acid position that is a determinant for K m HCO3‐ and K m PEP found in C4 PPCs, and our novel active site change AtPPC1T778I. Even though the equivalent to AtPPC1A776S in C4 PPCs is associated with decreased Km HCO3‐ (DiMario and Cousins 2019), in the context of AtPPC1 this amino acid substitution made it impossible to achieve saturation kinetics at concentrations of PEP and HCO3 − successfully used in our other PPC assays possibly because as previously reported this change also increases K m PEP similar to what we also observed (Supporting Information S3: Figure 5). This implies that this amino acid substitution in isolation does not benefit activity for a C3 anaplerotic enzyme such as AtPPC1 and may account for failure to recover transgenic Arabidopsis ppc1 lines that express AtPPC1 A776S possibly resulting from a negative effect on metabolism conferred by this compromised variant. Similar to our in planta analyses, AtPPC1T778I confers an obvious benefit to AtPPC1 activity with this amino acid change sharply reducing K m PEP while increasing K i malate (Figure 3G and Table 1). AtPPC1T778I is the most conservative structural change of our alr mutations without any detectable overall adaptation in either the T or R state (Figure 3H). Close examination of the structure shows that accommodation of the additional methyl group of Ile in the T state leads to local molecular crowding via van der Waals contacts with the side chain of V966. In contrast, in the R state, the C‐terminal segment L963‐G967 adopts an orientation that allows accommodation of the larger side chain of AtPPC1T778I without steric hindrance. Thus, again the T‐state becomes less stable, resulting in weakened binding of malate and likely the higher K i malate .

Finally, comparison of AtPPC1R886G with AtPPC1WT shows a substantial increase in K i malate yet no obvious beneficial effects for other kinetic parameters (Figure 3I and Table 1). This argues that the effect of AtPPC1R886G is strictly related to malate binding, which is similar to what has been demonstrated when comparing C3 and C4 PPCs for this amino acid change (Paulus et al. 2013a, 2013b). R886 is located in a disordered region in the R‐state that lacks electron density in both the structures of AtPPC1WT and AtPPC1R886G. AtPPC1R886G likely does not affect the R‐T equilibrium but instead directly impacts malate binding by removing a stabilizing positive charge (Figure 3J). R886 does not directly contact malate, but may contribute to binding via an electrostatic interaction that is lost in AtPPC1R886G similar to the situation in C4 versions of PPC such as has been described for Flaveria trinervia (Jacobs et al. 2008; Paulus et al. 2013b). The side chain of R886 in the T state also makes a hydrogen bond to Q675, which itself interacts with malate. The loss of this hydrogen bond in AtPPC1R886G may therefore indirectly reduce the affinity for malate.

Enzyme Enhancement Is Translatable to Agricultural PPCs

Since the amino acid positions affected by the alr mutations are strictly conserved amongst a wide range of plant PPCs, it was of interest to determine whether the observed effects of these on AtPPC1 function could be translated to PPCs from agricultural plants in order to test their utility for increasing carbon fixation in general. Zea mays PPC1 (ZmPPC1) was chosen as an exemplar PPC from an agriculturally important monocot that has been evolutionarily highly optimized due to its key role in C4 photosynthesis (Huber and Edwards 1975; Sage 2004; Svensson et al. 2003). As with AtPPC1, each of the alr amino acid changes was engineered individually into ZmPPC1. The ZmPPC1 equivalents of AtPPC1A776S and AtPPC1R886G were not necessary since C4 PPCs already host these substitutions, the latter of which accounts for ZmPPC1 being partially desensitized to malate. Following bacterial expression, ZmPPC1WT and variants were purified to homogeneity and their enzymatic parameters were determined (Figure 4A, Supporting Information S3: Figures 7–9). As with ZmPPC1WT, all variants showed a dependence of their turnover rate on enzyme concentration. From this analysis, K m PEP , V max , and K i malate were determined for ZmPPC1WT, with the latter indicating that even though relatively high, ZmPPC1WT still is negatively impacted by concentrations of malate that can occur in planta (Table 1), which are estimated at ~1.5 mM in maize bundle sheath cells (Weiner and Heldt 1992). For ZmPPC1A655V, enzyme kinetics were greatly improved compared to ZmPPC1WT. This was most notable for K i malate , which was found to be upwards of 10‐fold higher than ZmPPC1WT with this variant still being largely active even at 50 mM malate. Additionally, there was a large improvement for K m PEP with a reduction in V max that would be inconsequential at cellular malate concentrations. For ZmPPC1G682S, both K m PEP and K i malate were substantially improved in comparison to ZmPPC1WT with little negative impact on V max . As with AtPPC1G678S, the most notable benefits were seen at higher concentrations of PEP and malate. Finally, ZmPPC1T782I was also almost completely desensitized to malate as shown by a substantial increase in K i malate . K m PEP for this variant was also greatly reduced in comparison to ZmPPC1WT with a negative impact on V max that would have little effect at cellular concentrations of malate. Taken together, the data strongly argue that these amino acid changes identified in Arabidopsis may be translatable to ZmPPC1 in planta and could have similar beneficial effects on C4 photosynthesis as seen for PPC‐dependent malate exudation by Arabidopsis roots.

Because we have found that our alr mutations confer increased PPC output in Arabidopsis roots, it was also of interest to determine if these changes could also boost output of an anaplerotic C3‐type PPC found in maize roots. This was particularly intriguing since increased PPC output by maize roots could not only improve maize root growth in marginal soils but might also represent a novel approach for increasing carbon deposition as a means to mitigate excess CO2 levels. For this approach, ZmPEP7, which is a maize PPC isozyme that employs C3 attributes and is expressed primarily in roots (Kawamura et al. 1990), was chosen to determine whether these mutations could have similar impacts on its enzyme kinetics as compared to AtPPC1 and ZmPPC1. Mutations tested included our alr changes (ZmPEP7G672S and ZmPEP7T772I) and a representative of natural variation (ZmPEP7R880G) not found in C3 PPCs including ZmPEP7. It was not possible to assess the impacts of ZmPEP7A645V due to inability to produce this version of the protein to sufficiently high enough levels likely due to unintended effects of expression of this unregulated PPC on bacterial metabolism. As with our other analyses, ZmPEP7WT and variants were produced to homogeneity using a bacterial expression system after which they were tested for activity using the aforementioned malate dehydrogenase coupled assay. Similar to our other PPCs tested, ZmPEP7WT and all variants showed dependence of their turnover rate on enzyme concentration (Supporting Information S3: Figures 10–12).

Analysis of enzyme kinetics for ZmPEP7WT revealed that consistent with its presumed housekeeping role, this isozyme had relatively low K m PEP and K i malate , the combination of which is expected to maintain an appropriate balance between anabolism and catabolism (Figure 4B and Table 1). Analysis of the variants showed that similar to AtPPC1G678S and ZmPPC1G682S, ZmPEP7G672S had an overall increase in robustness compared to ZmPEP7WT including increased V max and a K i malate that was nearly threefold higher. Enzymatic assays with ZmPEP7T772I produced similar outcomes to AtPPC1T778I and ZmPPC1T780I including a reduction in K m PEP and a tenfold increase in K i malate , both of which almost certainly would offset in planta the reduction in V max observed in the absence of malate. Finally, as with AtPPC1R880G, the effects associated with ZmPEP7R880G were limited to K i malate , which was substantially increased even while V max and K m PEP were largely unaffected. Taken together, these results indicate that the identified alr‐related amino acid changes and their consequent effects are likely translatable to ZmPEP7 and other root‐localized anaplerotic PPCs.

In support of this argument, we successfully introduced a transgene for the ZmPEP7 G672S variant into maize B104 using a transgenic approach in which a mutant version of ZmPEP7 under the control of the native promoter along with the 5′‐ and 3′‐UTRs, and all exons and introns was added by Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation. This transgene, which carried a two nucleotide change to the codon for amino acid position 672 that results in the G to S substitution, was found to be expressed in ZmPEP7 G672S Line 8‐13 (Figure 4C inset). Unfortunately, homozygotes for the ZmPEP7 G672S transgene in Line 8‐13 could not be found likely due to there being a limit to how much central metabolism can be improved without concomitant improvements to related sinks, yet this was not expected to be problematic due to the semi‐dominant nature of the alr mutations as seen in Arabidopsis (Larsen et al. 1998). It should be noted that unlike what was done for our analyses of Arabidopsis with transgenic AtPPC1 genes in a ppc1 background, a pep7 maize knockout line could not be employed and the transgenic line carried both the native ZmPEP7 and the ZmPEP7 G672S transgene. Future work is needed to determine if editing of the native ZmPEP7 would allow for isolation of homozygous lines for the various alr mutations.

Analysis of phenotypes of plants following growth for 4 weeks showed that there was no penalty for size of the transgenics expressing ZmPEP7 G672S compared to the B104 controls (Figure 4C). Metabolic profiling of each showed a marked increase in oxaloacetate (OAA) related compounds in roots of transgenics expressing ZmPEP7 G672S compared to B104, including aspartate and malate that are both directly derived from OAA (Figure 4D). Several amino acids and TCA cycle intermediates along with other aspartate‐related molecules were also significantly higher in the transgenic compared to B104 (Table 2), which would be expected for greater PPC‐dependent output in planta resulting from deregulation of this anaplerotic enzyme. It was not possible to test roots of the transgenic line for improved Al resistance or organic acid exudation due to an inability to grow these plants aseptically because of inherent fungal contamination commonly found in maize seed. Metabolomic analysis of V3 leaves from each showed a much lower impact of the transgene compared to roots, which would be expected since ZmPEP7 is not the predominant PPC expressed in maize leaves (Supporting Information S2: Table 4). Notably though, several amino acids as well as citrate/isocitrate were also found at greater levels in the transgenic compared to B104 consistent with greater supply of OAA to the TCA cycle.

From our work, it is clear that both anaplerotic C3 and photosynthetic C4 PPCs can be improved by our unexpected novel amino acid changes to increase greatly their output in vitro and in planta, with there being obvious agricultural benefits that could arise from these. This is particularly surprising for ZmPPC1, which is a maize C4 PPC long considered to be highly optimized due to its key role in providing CO2 for photosynthesis. The changes associated with the alr mutations are the only identified approaches to improve output from C4 PPCs because they already employ beneficial natural variation that sets them apart from housekeeping C3 PPCs. Since plants are natural agents of carbon fixation in large part due to PPC function, these novel amino acid changes provide a simple strategy for modifying plants in general by gene editing to improve markedly their capability for carbon fixation all while boosting production of oxaloacetate. Oxaloacetate is foundational to many anabolic processes including replenishment of the TCA cycle as well as serving as a precursor to a myriad of biomolecules, many of which are valuable agricultural outputs.

It remains to be seen if these changes represent viable approaches for improving these agricultural outputs since while we have found ways to improve the source (i.e., PPC output) that in some contexts such as Al resistance are tolerated, it is almost certain that in other scenarios an accompanying increase in sink strength will be necessary (for a discussion of metabolic economy in plants see Hofmeyr and Cornish‐Bowden 2000). For example, C4 photosynthesis might only realize a benefit if increases in PPC output are coupled with greater capacity to accommodate higher levels of metabolic outcomes including Calvin‐Benson cycle intermediates and glucose. Prior reports argue that upsetting the metabolic balance can have negative consequences (e.g., Zhu and Galili 2003), which even we have seen as evidenced by an inability to generate a homozygous ZmPEPG672S transgenic maize line, so careful consideration of source sink relationships will be necessary for studies testing whether these changes have utility. With this in mind, it is likely that we were able to isolate these missense mutations originally because increased malate production can be accommodated by release into the rhizosphere, which represents an infinite sink by which excess levels of metabolites can be harmlessly sent into the soil environment. In our opinion, these changes represent a critically needed molecular toolbox with the potential for improving carbon fixation by any plant PPC while, if paired with improved sink strength, enhancing PPC‐dependent processes such as C4 photosynthesis, nitrogen fixation, generation of essential nutrients including oxaloacetate‐derived amino acids, and growth on marginal lands such as those with Al toxic soils.

Materials and Methods

Identification of the Arabidopsis alr Mutations

For production of a DNAseq library, genomic DNA was extracted from seeds of Arabidopsis mutant alr−128 after which it was sheared to 300–400 bp fragments with a Covaris Ultrasonicator. A DNAseq library was prepared using the NEBNext Ultra DNA library prep kit for Illumina in combination with NEXTFlex DNA Barcodes adapters (BIOO Scientific). The DNAseq library was sequenced at the University of California‐Irvine Genomics Core using an Illumina HiSeq sequencing system and analyzed using systemPipeR. For confirmation of the alr−128 mutation, genomic DNA was used for PCR analysis in which At1g53310 was amplified and sequenced. Subsequently, genomic DNA was isolated from seeds of alr−108 and alr−139, with At1g53310 PCR‐amplified using PfuTurbo (Agilent) from each and analyzed by sequencing.

Generation of Transgenic Plants

For creation of transgenic Arabidopsis plants, a transgene construct consisting of 1700 bp of the native promoter of At1g53310 along with the 5′‐ and 3′‐UTR's and all exons and introns was generated by PCR and cloned using EPI400 CopyCutter E. coli cells (LGC Biosearch Technologies) using listed primers (Supporting Information S1: Table 5). Following cloning in pBLUESCRIPT SKII the AtPPC1 WT construct was sequenced and subsequently mutagenized using the Stratagene Quikchange II Site‐directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent) to introduce mutations corresponding to A651V, G678S, A776S, T778I, and R886G. AtPPC1 WT and mutant versions were cloned into the binary vector pCB302, resequenced and then transferred into an Arabidopsis ppc1 mutant using Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation. Transgenic seedlings were identified by growth on MS media supplemented with 10 μg/mL glufosinate and 100 μg/mL vancomycin, with resistant seedlings rescued for seed production. Homozygous progeny were subsequently identified by germination on MS media with glufosinate after which confirmed lines were tested for restored AtPPC1 expression by competitive PCR using published primers followed by XhoI digestion and electrophoretic separation (17).

For maize, a genomic construct for ZmPEP7 WT , consisting of 2Kb of promoter, both 5′‐ and 3′‐UTRs and all exons and introns, was generated via PCR and mutagenized to introduce the desired codon change (G672S). Following transfer to the T‐DNA vector pDW3894, the transgene was introduced into maize B104 using Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation for tissue culture after which glufosinate‐resistant seedlings were rescued. Subsequently, T2 seeds were generated via self‐pollination and subsequent selection for progeny carrying the gene for gluofosinate resistance as assessed by PCR. Expression of the transgene was determined by PCR of cDNA generated from 1μg total RNA of a limited region of the ZmPEP7 gene containing the codon change followed by Hpy188I digestion, with the introduced change creating a new restriction site as visualized on a 3% agarose gel.

Plant Growth Conditions

Arabidopsis plants were grown in a Percival 136LLVL growth chamber set at 20°C with a 24 h light cycle. In order to test for Al resistance, seedlings were grown using a previously described Al‐soaked gel system at pH 4.2 (Larsen et al. 1996). Seedlings were grown for 6–7 d depending on experiment after which roots were measured. For Al‐responsive growth of Arabidopsis in hydroponics, seedlings were germinated in Corning Costar 6‐well plates containing 10 mL per well of growth media (pH 4.2) without agar in the absence of Al for 4 d after which roots of 10 seedlings for each line were measured. An additional 10 seedlings each were then transferred to either 0 or 25 μM AlCl3 (pH 4.2) and grown for an additional 3 d after which root length was determined. Aniline blue (callose) and morin (Al internalization) staining were done as previously described (Larsen et al. 1996; Larsen et al. 1998) with seedlings for callose staining grown for 6 d either in the absence or presence of 1.25 mM AlCl3 (pH 4.2) and seedlings for Al internalization grown either in the absence or presence of 1.00 mM AlCl3 (pH 4.2). Both staining experiments were visualized using a Keyence BZ‐X800E Fluorescence Microscope. Maize plants were grown for 4 weeks at 25°C under a 16/8 h light/dark cycle in standard potting mix. Maize plants were grown for 4 weeks at 22°C in standard potting mix.

Organic Acid Measurements

For measurement of organic acids in root exudates, Arabidopsis seedlings were grown for 5 d on soaked gel plates (pH 4.2) without added AlCl3, after which exactly 50 seedlings were transferred to sterile Corning Costar 6‐well plates containing 10 mL per well of 100μM CaCl2 (pH 4.2). After incubation for 24 h, the solution was collected and dried for measurement of organic acids using either the l‐Malic Acid Assay Kit or Citric Acid Assay Kit (Megazyme). For measurement of internal organic acids in Arabidopsis roots, seedlings were grown for 6 d on vertical plates (pH 4.2) with 2% plant agar after which roots were harvested. Following weighing, roots were immediately ground in 70% ethanol and then re‐extracted with 95% ethanol after which ethanol samples were dried and used for organic acid quantification as described above.

For metabolomic analyses of maize roots, plants were grown for 4 weeks in soil after which roots were photographed, harvested, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground and lyophilized (SP VirTis Benchtop Pro BTP‐8ZL00W). Following this, 10 mg of dried tissue from each sample was extracted with monophasic extraction solvent (30:30:20:20 acetonitrile, methanol, isopropanol and water), sonicated, and vortexed all at 4°C. Metabolites were quantified using a TQ‐XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer coupled to an I‐class UPLC system (Waters). Separations were conducted on a ZIC‐pHILIC column (2.1 × 150 mm, 5 µM) (EMD Millipore). The mobile phases were (A) water with 15 mM ammonium bicarbonate adjusted to pH 9.6 with ammonium hydroxide and (B) acetonitrile. The flow rate was 200 µL/min and the column was held at 50°C. The injection volume was 2 µL and the gradient consisted of 0 min, 90% B; 1.5 min, 90% B; 16 min, 20% B; 18 min, 20% B; 20 min, 90% B; 28 min, 90% B. The MS was operated in multiple reaction monitoring mode. Source and desolvation temperatures were 150°C and 600°C, respectively. Desolvation gas was set to 1100 L/hr and cone gas to 150 L/hr. Collision gas was set to 0.15 mL/min. All gases were nitrogen except the collision gas, which was argon. Capillary voltage was 1 kV in positive ion mode and 2 kV in negative ion mode. Targeted data processing was performed using open‐source Skyline software 3.

Bacterial Expression and Purification of AtPPC1, ZmPPC1, and ZmPEP7 Variants

For generation of PPC protein for determination of enzyme kinetics, AtPPC1, ZmPPC1, and ZmPEP7 were cloned by PCR as cDNAs into pBLUESCRIPT SKII after which relevant mutations were introduced via site‐directed mutagenesis as described above (Supporting Information S1: Table 5). Following sequencing, cDNAs, which included an engineered 6x‐HIS tag on the 5′ end, were transferred to pET22B. For expression of AtPPC1 and ZmPEP7 variants in E. coli BL21‐CodonPlus‐RIL, cells were grown overnight at 37°C until OD600 reached ~0.6 and then transferred to 18°C for 18 h for induction. A modified expression protocol was used that relied on auto‐induction following depletion of glucose in the medium in order to prevent complications from expression of a hyperactive metabolic enzyme (0.6% glycerol, 0.05% glucose, 0.2% lactose). Expression of ZmPPC1 variants was identical with the exception of inclusion of 0.1 M IPTG at the time of induction.

Cells were harvested and lysed by sonication in the presence of extraction buffer (50 mM Tris Base pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5%(v/v) glycerol, 40 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT) supplemented with 1X Xpert protease inhibitor cocktail (GenDEPOT). Following centrifugation to remove cellular debris, samples were loaded onto a GE Healthcare AKTA FPLC equipped with a HisTrap HP His tag protein purification column (Cytiva), washed, and then treated with elution buffer (50 mM Tris Base buffer pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5%(v/v) glycerol, 400 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT and 1X Xpert protease inhibitor cocktail) to recover the protein of interest. Subsequently, eluates were loaded back onto the AKTA FPLC to run through a size exclusion column (Hi Load 16/60 Superdex 200, GE‐Healthcare) with elution buffer (50 mM Tris Base 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, and 2 mM DTT). PPC protein was collected and concentrated to at least 6 mg/mL in 50% glycerol for storage at −20°C and use in activity assays. Protein concentration was determined using the Hartree‐Lowry Folin assay followed by measurement on a NanoDrop 2000 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher).

Analysis of Enzyme Kinetics of AtPPC1, ZmPPC1 and ZmPEP7 Variants

For PPC assays, approximately 0.165 μg of AtPPC1 or 0.15 μg of ZmPPC1 and ZmPEP7 proteins were tested for activity in a standard assay mixture consisting of 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 mM KHCO3, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.15 mM NADH, and 10 units/mL MDH (Sigma Aldrich). Assays varied in concentrations of PEP (0‐7.5 mM, 10 mM HCl) and malate (0–50 mM, 10 mM KOH) depending on experimental setup. Reactions were measured at 25°C in UV transparent 96 well microplates (Corning) using a Perkin Elmer Wallac 1420 Victor 2 Multilabel Counter with an excitation filter at 355 nm (±20) and emissions filter at 460 nm (±12.5). Rates of OAA formation by PPCs were determined as a function of NADH depletion resulting from MDH‐dependent conversion of OAA to malate. Reactions were initiated by addition of PEP. All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism for Mac, Version 9.4.1.

Structural Analysis of AtPPC1 Variants

Crystallization of the AtPPC1 variants (13 mg/mL in 50 mM Tris pH7.5; 100 mM (NH4)2SO4; 10 mM MgCl2; 5%(v/v) glycerol; 1 mM TCEP) was screened with the Mosquito (SPT Labtech, Cambridge, UK) using the sitting‐drop method. To produce larger crystals required for data collection, the successful conditions were repeated manually by hanging with drops of 1 μL protein solution mixed with 1 μL precipitant solution and equilibrated against 200 μL precipitant solution in 48‐well crystallization plates and incubated at 19°C.

All data were collected at the Soleil synchrotron (Saint‐Aubin, France) on beamlines Proxima‐1 or Proxima‐2A at a wavelength close to 1.0 Å and at 100 K using an EIGER‐X 16 M (Dectris Ltd.) detector. A rotation range of 0.1°/image was used in all cases. Data were integrated and merged using XDS (Kabsch 2010). In the case of the free structure of AtPPC1T778I and of the malate complex of AtPPC1G678S, the unmerged data from XDS were corrected for anisotropy using Staraniso (Tickle et al. 2018).

The structures of wild‐type AtPPC1WT with and without malate were determined by molecular replacement with Phaser (McCoy et al. 2007) using the coordinates of A. thaliana PEP3 (pdb entry 5fdn) (Connell et al. 2018) and Z. mays PPC1 (pdb entry 1jqo) (Matsumura et al. 2002) respectively as search models. These two structures were later used to determine the other structures via molecular replacement or molecular substitution. Refinement was carried out with phenix.refine using a maximum likelihood target (Afonine et al. 2012) combined with manual rebuilding in Coot (Emsley et al. 2010). Secondary structure and ncs restraints were used throughout. Crystallization, data collection and refinement data are given in Supporting Information S1: Tables 2 and 3.
