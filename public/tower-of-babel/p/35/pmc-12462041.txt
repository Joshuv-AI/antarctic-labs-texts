Biomarker genes for model-based prediction of drought-stress perception levels in rice
Authors: Kanami Yoshino, Ryokei Tanaka, Saki Yoshida, Yuko Numajiri, Shota Teramoto, Yuka Kitomi, Yusaku Uga, Taiji Kawakatsu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought is a global challenge that severely restricts crop yields and threatens food security. Plants respond to drought stress by modulating gene expression before visible phenotypic changes occur. However, most studies of drought resistance have examined phenotypes after drought treatment, with little emphasis on how severely the plants were perceiving drought-stress conditions before the appearance of stress symptoms. We therefore developed drought-stress biomarkers (DSBMs) to detect drought-stress perception levels based on gene expression profiles by performing time-series transcriptome analysis and phenotypic analysis of rice (Oryza sativa) under drought conditions in the growth chamber.ResultsTime-series RNA-seq of the drought-susceptible rice cultivar IR64 revealed drastic changes in the transcriptome after 4–6 days of drought treatment in plants grown in pot culture mimicking drought conditions in the field, particularly for genes related to photosynthesis. Among the differentially expressed genes, we selected 23 DSBM genes that consistently responded to drought stress. Rehydration immediately reset the changes in expression of these DSBM genes, indicating that their expression changes reflect current drought-stress perception levels, but not stress memories. Responses of DSBM genes tended to be conserved among rice accessions, irrespective of the rice subpopulation (such as indica, aus, and japonica). We developed a machine learning model using the expression levels of DSBM genes trained by the time-series RNA-seq data for IR64. This model successfully predicted the drought-stress perception levels of various rice accessions, representing the probability of exposure to drought treatment, with an accuracy of 75%. Extreme root architecture traits, such as the largest root surface area, narrowest crown root diameter, and largest ratio of deep rooting, influenced the predicted drought-stress perception levels.ConclusionWe identified DSBM genes and developed a machine learning model as a robust tool for assessing drought-stress perception levels in rice. Monitoring and predicting drought-stress perception levels should contribute to more efficient crop management and breeding schemes. Furthermore, our dataset would serve as a resource for further understanding the mechanisms of drought resistance in rice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07175-5.Keywords: Drought stress, Rice, Machine learning, Biomarker, Transcriptome

Background

Droughts are frequent in many parts of the world and are predicted to increase further in the future [1–3]. This poses a major risk to rice (Oryza sativa) production in most farming ecosystems, such as irrigated, rainfed, and upland conditions. Irrigation systems may be unsustainable because they require copious water. Likewise, rice production under rainfed or upland field conditions might become unstable due to the increased frequency of drought stress. It is therefore essential to develop drought-resistant or water-saving rice cultivars. Many genes and loci for drought resistance in rice have been identified [4, 5]. However, only a limited number of rice varieties exhibit high drought resistance [6], and few effective screening methods at the molecular level are available for identifying drought-resistant varieties [7].

Drought resistance is typically assessed based on phenotyping of agronomic traits. For example, aboveground phenotyping includes examining grain yield and morphological/physiological changes, such as a decrease in tiller number, reduction in plant height, leaf rolling, and leaf senescence under drought-stress conditions [8, 9]. Drought resistance is closely associated with root system architecture. DEEPER ROOTING 1 (DRO1) regulates root growth angle and causes deeper rooting, which allows plants to access water deep underground; thus, rice plants with functional DRO1 alleles show reduced yield loss under drought conditions [9]. Similarly, thicker crown root diameter is associated with drought resistance, particularly under upland cultivation, as it enables roots to penetrate hard soils [10, 11]. However, these phenotypes appear only when gene expression is regulated in response to drought stress.

Transcriptome analysis enables detailed investigations of the complex molecular mechanisms underlying drought-stress responses, which are often challenging to fully comprehend solely through morphological phenotyping. For example, temporary drought stress delays flowering due to the prolonged expression of the flowering repressor Ghd7 (GRAIN NUMBER, PLANT HEIGHT AND HEADING DATE 7), resulting in the delayed expression of the florigen genes HEADING DATE 3a (Hd3a) and RICE FLOWERING LOCUS T 1 (RFT1) [12]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are often identified by comparing the transcript abundances of samples grown under control vs. drought conditions or those of drought-sensitive vs. drought-tolerant cultivars [13–15]. In addition to simple comparative transcriptomics between two groups, some studies have utilized DEG analysis at multiple timepoints, in multiple tissues, or in multiple cultivars [16–20]. Such multi-scale transcriptome studies increase the chance to both identify and characterize DEGs. For example, some genes remain upregulated even after drought stress is relieved (i.e., low specificity), while others are upregulated exclusively when the plant is perceiving drought stress (i.e., high specificity). These levels of specificity of DEGs can only be assessed through time-series transcriptome analysis.

Considering their application for molecular breeding and the diagnosis of drought stress, it is important to identify DEGs that are consistent (i.e., upregulated or downregulated during drought), specific (i.e., reflecting current drought stress, not memory), and general (i.e., similar, although not always the same, changes in expression in many cultivars) using comprehensive multi-scale transcriptome data. These characteristics may collectively be described as “biomarkers” for abiotic stress. Although the concept of biomarkers is mainly used in medical science, several studies have identified metabolites or transcripts as biomarkers of abiotic stress in rice [21–23]. For example, 17 metabolites (including some sugars and gibberellins) are listed as drought biomarkers in rice based on a bio-simulation using publicly available transcriptome datasets [21]. Also, a large-scale metabolomic study of 292 indica rice accessions identified some metabolites with strong correlations with yield under drought [23]. While many transcriptome studies of drought-stress responses in rice have been conducted, it is crucial to select biomarker genes by evaluating their stability, specificity, and generalizability. To obtain high-quality, consistent, multi-scale transcriptome data for screening drought-stress biomarker (DSBM) genes, it is important to control and monitor drought-stress conditions within and across experiments. However, reproducing field-like drought conditions in controlled environments remains challenging.

In this study, we performed time-series transcriptome analysis of the drought-sensitive rice cultivar IR64 under controlled drought-stress conditions using a pot cultivation system with automatic bottom irrigation (iPOTs) [24]. The iPOTs mimics the soil conditions under drought stress in the field with high reproducibility [12, 25]. We identified DSBM genes that responded specifically and consistently to drought stress. Subsequently, we trained a machine learning model to assess drought-stress perception levels based on the expression profiles of these DSBM genes and evaluated the accuracy of the model using an independent dataset consisting of transcriptomes from various rice accessions. Our findings should facilitate rice production and breeding under drought, and our dataset would be a useful resource for exploring the drought-resistance mechanisms in rice.

Methods

Plant materials, growth conditions, and stress treatments

The rice (Oryza sativa L.) varieties used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Seeds were surface sterilized in 375 ppm of pefurazoate at 15°C for 24 h, soaked in water, and incubated in the dark at 30°C for two days for germination. The plants were cultured in calcined clay (Profile Greens Grade, Profile Products LLC, http://www.profileevs.com) instead of natural soil. The calcined clay was washed with water before use, dried, placed in pots (24.5 cm upper outer diameter, 20 cm lower outer diameter, 30 cm height; SP2530P, Tecs Inc., http://www.tecs.ne.jp) to a depth of 25 cm, and irrigated with modified Kimura B [26] culture solution (273.2 µM K2SO4, 546.9 µM MgSO4.7H2O, 182.0 µM NaH2PO4.2H2O, 182.3 µM CaCl2.2H2O, 182.4 µM CaSO4.2H2O, 1.2 mM KNO3, 411.2 µM NH4Cl, and 4 mg L−1 FeC6H5O7.nH2O [pH 5.5]) before sowing. The germinated seeds were individually planted in separate pots and cultivated in a growth chamber (Nippon Medical & Chemical Instruments Co., http://www.nihonika.co.jp) under a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod, with relative humidity of 50% during the day and 60% at night. The temperature in the growth chamber was maintained at 25°C at night and varied from 25°C to 30°C during the day. The iPOTs [24], which incorporate an auto-irrigation system to mimic soil conditions similar to drought stress in the field, was used in this study. This system features three soil moisture sensors positioned at the bottom, middle, and top of the pots, allowing soil volumetric water content (SWC) to be monitored in these layers of the soil. The pots were automatically irrigated with reverse osmosis water to a height of 10 cm from the bottom of the pot to ensure that the top layers of the soil were moistened during the irrigation period. The plants were subjected to two drought-stress conditions: moderate and severe drought stress (Fig. 1). For moderate-drought treatment, automatic irrigation was stopped at 14 days after sowing, allowing water to drain from the bottom of the pot. For severe-drought treatment, automatic irrigation was stopped at 14 days after sowing, and the pots were elevated above the drainage hose to promote faster drainage. For control treatment, irrigation was maintained throughout cultivation.

Experimental design. a Experiment 1: To develop drought-stress biomarkers and for statistical modeling to assess drought-stress perception levels. b Experiment 2: To evaluate the specificity of the biomarkers in response to drought stress. c Experiment 3: To validate the accuracy of the drought-stress assessment based on the selected biomarkers

Experimental design

Three different cultivation experiments were conducted (Fig. 1). First, to develop drought-stress biomarkers (DSBMs) and for statistical modeling to assess drought-stress perception levels based on the expression profiles of the biomarkers, rice cultivar IR64 plants at 14 days after sowing were subjected to two drought conditions (moderate and severe) and well-watered conditions (control) (Fig. 1a). Due to space limitations, the experiments were performed separately (i.e., control/moderate-drought stress and control/severe-drought stress). The newest fully expanded leaves (4–5 cm long, Supplementary Fig. 1) from each plant were collected every day from 14 to 26 days after sowing (0–12 days after starting drought treatment) for RNA-seq (three biological replicates per timepoint).

Second, to confirm the specificity of the DSBM genes in response to drought stress, IR64 plants at 14 days after sowing were subjected to severe-drought treatment for nine days, followed by irrigation for six days (Fig. 1b). During this period, the newest and second-newest fully expanded leaves (Supplementary Fig. 1) from each plant were collected for RNA-seq analysis at six timepoints: two hours, six days, and nine days after drought treatment and two hours, three days, and six days after rewatering treatment (three biological replicates per timepoint).

The third experiment was designed to validate the accuracy of the drought-stress assessment based on the selected biomarkers. Forty rice accessions were grown: IR64; Dro1-NIL, a near-isogenic line with DRO1 alleles in the IR64 genetic background with deep root system architecture [9]; Kinandang Patong (KP); Koshihikari; and 36 accessions from NARO Worldwide Rice Collection (WRC) [27, 28] (Supplementary Table 1). All accessions were grown under well-watered (control) and moderate-drought conditions (Fig. 1c). The newest fully expanded leaves were collected from the plants at 19 days after sowing (five days after drought treatment) for RNA-seq (three biological replicates per line and treatment).

Phenotypic evaluation

In the first and second experiments, plant height (PH, cm) was measured prior to sampling for RNA-seq, and the remaining shoot was used to measure shoot dry weight (SDW, g). In the third experiment, SDW, PH, and tiller number (TN) were measured at 28 days after sowing with 14 days of irrigation followed by 14 days of moderate-drought treatment.

RNA extraction, mRNA-seq library preparation, sequencing, read processing, and analysis

The leaf samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after sampling and ground to a fine powder with Multi-beads Shocker (Yasui Kikai). For the first and second experiments, total RNA was extracted from the samples using guanidine isothiocyanate-based buffer and magnetic beads as described previously [29]. For the third experiment, total RNA was extracted from the samples using RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (E7760L, New England Biolabs, http://international.neb.com) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequence reads were mapped to the reference genome of rice cultivar Nipponbare (IRGSP-1.0) using STAR aligner (ver. 2.7.3a) with the options ‘–outFilterMultimapNmax 1 –outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.4 –outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.4’ [30] The read count matrix was obtained using featureCounts (v2.0.0) with the option ‘-p’ [31].

DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with the relative log expression (RLE) normalization method [32] (threshold: absolute value of the log2[fold change] ≥ 2 and adjusted P value < 0.05) by comparing the control samples to samples treated under moderate and severe-drought conditions at each timepoint. Genes with fewer than ten reads in all samples were removed prior to statistical analysis using DESeq2. To focus on highly expressed genes, DEGs with average transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) values ≤ 2 in all samples were also removed after DEG analysis.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using log2(RLE normalized counts + 1) using the R function prcomp with default settings or the option ‘scale = T’ to reveal clusters of samples in the first two principal components. Since the initial quality check by PCA using all samples identified two extreme outliers, which were controls at 4 days after stress treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2), these samples were omitted from subsequent analysis. Heatmaps were plotted using the ComplexHeatmap package [33] in R. Clustering analysis was performed using hclust (default settings in the ComplexHeatmap package) or the k-means method. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using agriGO [34] and visualized using corrplot [35]. The normalized counts of DEGs on each day were plotted using R function, plot and matplot. From these plots, genes consistently up- or down-regulated during drought were selected as biomarker genes (see Results and Discussion for details).

Drought stress assessment based on biomarkers

A classification model was trained to predict drought-stress perception levels based on the expression profiles of the selected biomarker genes. For statistical modeling, counts per million reads (CPM) were calculated using the fpm function in the DESeq2 package with the robust = TRUE option to apply RLE normalization. Using the expression profiles (CPM values) of the biomarkers from the 144 IR64 samples (two drought levels [moderate and severe] × two treatments [control and drought] for each drought level × three biological replicates of pots × 12 timepoints after one day from the start of drought stress) from the first experiment, a random forest model [36] was trained using the expression profiles of the biomarkers as explanatory variables and the treatment (drought or control) as the response variable without considering the difference of the drought stress level and timing. In other words, both of the samples under the moderate-drought treatment and the samples under the severe-drought treatment were labeled as samples belonging to “drought” class, and all other samples were labeled as “control” class to train the random forest model as a two-class classifier. This analysis was performed using the caret package in R [37] with the method = ‘rf’ option. The ‘mtry’ hyperparameter of the random forest model was tuned by the ‘trControl’ option of the train function of the caret package by setting the arguments as follows: method = ‘repeatedcv’, number = 10, repeats = 50, selectionFunction = ‘best’. The accuracy of the drought-stress assessment based on the trained model was tested on samples of the 40 accessions from the third experiment. Using the predict function with the type = ‘prob’ option, the random forest model calculated the probability of the sample belonging to the drought treatment group, which was interpreted as the predicted drought-stress perception level. To interpret the prediction result, the predicted drought-stress perception levels calculated by the random forest model were compared with the aboveground phenotypes (such as SDW, PH, and TN) measured in the third experiment as well as with the root phenotypes measured in our previous study [11].

Results and discussion

Drought-stress treatment

We applied moderate and severe drought treatments for 14 to 26 days after sowing (see Methods). Under non-stressed conditions, SWC (soil volumetric water content, mm3/mm3) was maintained at 0.35–0.5 in the top layer and 0.5–0.7 in the middle and bottom layers of the soil (Supplementary Fig. 3). Upon application of drought treatment, the SWC in the upper layer promptly dropped to approximately 0.3 and remained consistent (Supplementary Fig. 3). In the middle layer, SWC decreased to 0.4–0.45 and gradually decreased to 0.35–0.4 with prolonged treatment, with a more significant rate of decrease under severe-drought conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3). For the bottom layers, SWC dropped to 0.5–0.6 under moderate-drought conditions and either remained stable or slightly decreased over time (Supplementary Fig. 3). By contrast, under severe-drought conditions, SWC in the bottom layers fell to 0.45–0.55 and gradually decreased to 0.4–0.45 with prolonged treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3). Therefore, the most prominent difference between moderate and severe drought treatments was the SWC in the bottom layers of the soil.

Dynamic transcriptomic changes in response to drought stress

IR64 is classified as a drought-susceptible rice cultivar due to its shallow roots and low yields under drought stress [9]. To investigate how IR64 responds to drought treatments, we measured shoot dry weight (SDW, Supplementary Fig. 4a) and plant height (PH, Supplementary Fig. 4b) and performed time-series RNA-seq of IR64 using the same plants at 13 timepoints under moderate and severe drought stress vs. control conditions (Fig. 1a). IR64 started to show decreases in both PH and SDW at 4 DAD (days after drought treatment), especially under severe-drought treatment. The differences in these parameters between moderate and severe drought treatment were greater at 7 DAD and later. Principal component analysis (PCA) roughly separated the transcriptomes of IR64 plants under control conditions and drought treatments after around 4 DAD (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 4). Further drought stress had a greater impact on the transcriptome, depending on the intensity and duration of drought treatment (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 5).

We identified 588 DEGs between IR64 plants grown under control and drought conditions on at least one day. k-means clustering based on expression levels classified the DEGs into ten clusters, and dynamic changes occurred in the early (0–2 DAD), middle (3–5 DAD), and late (6–12 DAD) drought phases (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table 2). Although the ranges of expression changes reflect different intensities of drought treatment, each cluster showed a similar global trend between moderate and severe drought conditions: upregulated in the early (cluster 1), early-to-mid (cluster 2), early-to-late (cluster 3), and mid-to-late drought phases (cluster 4); downregulated in the early-to-mid but upregulated in the mid-to-late drought phase (cluster 5); or downregulated in the early-to-mid (cluster 6), early-to-late (clusters 7–9), and mid-to-late drought phase (cluster 10) (Fig. 2b). However, severe-drought treatment specifically downregulated genes in the mid-to-late drought phase (clusters 1 and 2) and upregulated genes in the mid-to-late (cluster 6) and early drought phases (clusters 8–10) (Fig. 2b). As observed in the PCA (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 5), the mid-drought phase is the transition phase during which the expression levels of DEGs drastically changed.

Gene ontology analysis revealed that genes related to photosynthesis, including those encoding ribulose bisphosphate carboxylases, apoproteins, and photosynthetic reaction center proteins, were enriched in clusters 1 and 6 (Fig. 2c). The upregulation of photosynthesis-related genes suggests that photosynthesis is activated in response to acute water loss. However, this activation was transient, as it immediately stopped when the water content stabilized under moderate-drought conditions or persisted for only two days under severe-drought conditions. Furthermore, the expression of these genes was suppressed in the late phase under severe-drought, suggesting that prolonged severe-drought stress represses photosynthesis.

Besides photosynthesis-related genes, cluster 1 also included OsCHLOROPLAST VESICULATION (OsCV), a gene involved in abiotic stress-induced chloroplast degradation [38]. The knockdown of OsCV enhances photosynthetic activity [38], suggesting that photosynthetic activity might be modulated by a negative feedback loop between the upregulation of photosynthesis-related genes and the degradation of chloroplasts under drought-stress conditions. Although cluster 2 was enriched with genes related to oxidation reactions and cluster 3 was not significantly enriched with genes related to specific biological processes, both of these clusters included genes related to leaf senescence and chlorophyll metabolism, such as NON-YELLOW COLORING 3 (NYC3; cluster 2), OsNAC-LIKE, ACTIVATED BY APETALA3/PISTILLATA (OsNAP; cluster 3), OsCYTOKININ OXIDASE 11 (OsCKX11; cluster 3), and STAY GREEN RICE (SGR; cluster 3). These results suggest that drought stress induces leaf senescence. Genes in both clusters were more strongly upregulated during the early-to-mid drought phase, but their expression levels were lower in the late phase under severe vs moderate drought conditions. Genes in clusters 5 and 6 were downregulated in the early-to-mid drought phase but upregulated in the late phase. Cluster 5 was enriched with genes related to translation, including 12 genes encoding chloroplast ribosomal proteins, whereas cluster 6 was enriched with genes related to carbon and nitrogen metabolism. These results suggest that drought stress inhibits primary metabolite biosynthesis and chloroplast development and functions in the early-to-mid drought phase but activates these process in the late phase. Since growth retardation began at 4 DAD and became more prominent at 7 DAD, the phases of transcriptome changes may reflect the degree of growth retardation.

Our data suggest that plants employ the following sequential responses to cope with drought stress. Initially, plants enhance photosynthetic activity to temporarily overcome drought stress without compromising growth. Subsequently, plants begin to balance growth and stress tolerance by reducing photosynthetic activity. Finally, under prolonged severe drought, plants suppress growth and recover damaged chloroplasts. These multi-step responses underlie the trade-off between growth and stress tolerance by fine-tuning the allocation of limited resources and energy.

Identification of drought stress biomarker genes

As the drought-susceptible variety IR64 showed dynamic transcriptome changes under drought conditions, transcriptome changes could be used as an indicator of the stress levels that plants are sensing under drought stress. This suggests that a set of genes could be used as DSBMs that distinguish whether plants are suffering from drought stress based on their expression levels. To identify DSBM genes, we screened genes whose expression levels were relatively stable under control conditions and continuously up- or downregulated under drought-stress conditions in IR64. Using these criteria, we selected 23 genes as DSBM genes (Table 1, Fig. 3). Nine of these genes were upregulated (uDSBM) and 14 were downregulated (dDSBM) by drought stress. Most DSBM genes do not exhibit the clear diurnal rhythms during early growth (Supplementary Fig. 6).

To confirm the specificity of the DSBMs in response to drought stress, we examined the expression profiles of the DSBM genes and the plant phenotypes before and after rehydration (Fig. 1b). Both PH and SDW decreased in response to drought stress, but these effects were alleviated after rehydration (Supplementary Fig. 7). DSBM genes showed similar responses to drought-stress in the newest and second-newest leaves: uDSBMs were upregulated and dDSBMs were downregulated under drought-stress conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8). Rehydration reconfigured the expression profiles of the DSBMs. The expression levels of more than half of uDSBM genes and approximately one-third of dDSBM genes returned to normal 2 h after rehydration in both the newest and second-newest leaves (Supplementary Fig. 8). The expression levels of most DSBM genes returned to normal or shifted in the opposite direction (i.e., uDSBM genes became downregulated, and dDSBM genes became upregulated) three days after rehydration in both the newest and second-newest leaves (Supplementary Fig. 8). Four uDSBM genes were upregulated in the second-newest leaves, and two dDSBM genes were downregulated in the newest or second-newest leaves six days after rehydration, but the expression levels of the other DSBM genes either returned to normal or shifted to the opposite direction (Supplementary Fig. 8); this shift might help plants physiologically recover from drought stress. Overall, DSBM genes responded specifically to drought stress. Therefore, the expression changes of DSBM genes likely indicate the plant’s perception of the current drought-stress condition, rather than the effects of past drought-stress conditions.

The DSBMs include two genes encoding transcription factors (TFs), three genes encoding Bowman-Birk type bran trypsin inhibitor (BBTI) precursors, and two genes encoding ribonuclease T2 (RNS) family domain-containing proteins. The remaining DSBM genes encode proteins with diverse predicted molecular functions. While RNS4 has been identified as a positive regulator of abscisic acid (ABA) signaling and is likely involved in drought tolerance, the roles of other DSBM genes in drought-stress responses remain unclear [39, 40]. Given that MYB TFs such as OsMYB48-1, OsMYB1R1, and OsFLP are drought responsive and involved in drought tolerance, the MYB TF gene LOC_Os01g47370, which we identified as an uDSBM, might function as a novel drought-responsive TF gene [41–43]. The dDSBM OsIRO2 (LOC_Os01g72370) encodes a bHLH TF involved in Fe uptake under Fe-deficient conditions [44]. OsIRO2 is upregulated by Fe deficiency, suggesting that drought might inhibit Fe uptake from the soil. BBTI genes are involved in plant responses to wounding and fungi. The heterologous expression of maize (Zea mays) BBTI genes increased drought tolerance in Arabidopsis thaliana by maintaining higher relative water contents and reducing drought-induced oxidative stress in leaves [45–47]. The suppression of three BBTI genes (LOC_Os01g03310, LOC_Os01g03340, and LOC_Os01g04050), which were identified as dDSBMs, might be associated with lower relative water contents and increased oxidative stress in leaves.

The uDSBM OsSSI (LOC_Os06g06560) encodes soluble starch synthase I [48], a key enzyme in starch biosynthesis, which plays a crucial role in grain filling in rice. While numerous studies have examined starch dynamics during the grain-filling stage, its role under drought stress at earlier developmental stages remains less explored. Moderate-drought stress during seedling growth has been shown to significantly increase starch accumulation, ultimately enhancing grain yield [49]. Starch accumulates in guard cells, helping regulate stomatal movement and minimize water loss [50]. Additionally, under controlled osmotic stress conditions, starch-derived metabolites contribute to maintaining carbon balance, supporting continued plant growth in challenging environments [51]. These findings suggest that the upregulation of starch synthesis genes may represent an adaptive response to drought stress. The uDSBM OsNRT2.4 (LOC_Os01g36720) encodes a nitrate transporter and is upregulated under high-nitrate conditions [52]. Since low SWC decreases nitrate uptake [53], it seemed unlikely that the upregulation of OsNRT2.4 under drought stress was due to higher nitrate levels. OsNRT2.4 is also upregulated by treatment with the phytohormones IAA, jasmonic acid, and salicylic acid, but not ABA [52]. Another uDSBM, OsMAX1d (LOC_Os02g12890), is involved in strigolactone biosynthesis, although the enzymatic activity of OsMAX1d is weaker than that of other OsMAX1 homologs [54]. Crosstalk between strigolactone and ABA plays an important role in drought tolerance [55]. Therefore, the changes in expression of some DSBM genes might reflect the crosstalk among plant hormones. Further experiments using mutants of DSBMs would help clarify the roles of these genes in the drought-stress response.

Conserved responses of DSBMs among various rice accessions

To examine whether DSBMs can be utilized to monitor drought-stress sensing in diverse rice accessions, we analyzed the expression patterns of DSBM genes at five days after moderate-drought treatment (Fig. 1c) in 40 accessions (12 aus, 14 indica, 5 japonica, and 9 admixed subpopulations). Most DSBM genes responded to drought-stress treatment as expected in all accessions tested, regardless of subpopulation (Fig. 4). However, some DSBM genes differentially responded to drought-stress treatment in certain accessions (Fig. 4), particularly for dDSBMs. For example, LOC_Os01g72370 and LOC_Os03g51530 were upregulated in WRC13 and WRC16 in response to drought treatment. Furthermore, more than half of dDSBM genes were upregulated in WRC32 and WRC37 in response to drought treatment.

Heatmap showing the relative expression levels of drought-stress biomarker genes under drought-stress conditions. Forty accessions were classified into four subpopulations. Gene expression levels are shown as log2(fold change) relative to the control for each accession. SDW, shoot dry weight; PH, plant height; TN, tiller number

We measured SDW, PH, and TN in the 40 accessions at 14 days after drought-stress treatment (Fig. 4). Drought treatment inhibited plant growth in most accessions, indicating that this treatment was stressful to these accessions. However, drought treatment increased SDW in WRC16, WRC22, and WRC32, suggesting that this treatment increased biomass in these accessions as an adaptive response. Alternatively, perhaps mild-drought treatment was favorable for growth in these accessions. PH was unaffected in these accessions, but TN increased in WRC22, perhaps leading to an increase in SDW in this accession. Both uDSBM and dDSBM genes tended to respond to drought treatment in WRC22 like they did in IR64. DSBM genes also tended to respond to drought treatment in WRC16 like in IR64, but the changes in expression were relatively minor in WRC16. Furthermore, some dDSBM genes were upregulated by drought treatment in WRC16. Although uDSBM genes were slightly upregulated in WRC32, eight out of 14 dDSBM genes were upregulated by drought treatment in this accession. The differential responses of DSBM genes to drought treatment in WRC16, WRC22, and WRC32 suggest that rice accessions may vary in how they sense and respond to drought stress.

To compare the responsiveness of DSBM genes to that of known drought-responsive genes, we examined the expression levels of 17 drought-responsive TF genes (Supplementary Fig. 9). Only AP59 and SNAC1 clearly responded to moderate-drought conditions. However, AP59 did not respond to drought conditions in more than half of aus and indica accessions. SNAC1 was upregulated in most accessions, but the extent of changes in expression of these known TF genes tended to be lower than that of uDSBM genes. Our drought treatments mimicked drought conditions in the field, in which plants gradually suffer effects of drought stress on their viability. By contrast, under the extremely severe-drought conditions employed in most previous experiments, plants were suddenly exposed to lethal levels of drought stress [56]. Therefore, this may highlight the higher sensitivity of DSBM genes to drought stress.

In summary, drought treatment tended to retard plant growth and affect the expression profiles of DSBM genes, regardless of subpopulation. Therefore, although the DSBMs were selected in IR64 using time-series RNA-seq, they could be applied to a wide range of accessions across subpopulations.

Model-based assessment of drought-stress perception levels

We trained a random forest classification model to assess the drought-stress perception levels of plants based on the expression profiles of the 23 DSBM genes in IR64 analyzed by time-series RNA-seq. The trained model predicted the treatment (drought or control) of 177/235 samples (accuracy = 75.3%) in the third experiment (Supplementary Table 3), showing that whether plants were suffered from drought stress was predictable based on the expression profiles of the 23 DSBM genes.

To further explore the results of model prediction, we compared the predicted drought-stress perception level, representing the probability of being classified as a drought-treated sample in the trained random forest model, with the SDW of each accession (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 3). When we set the cutoff of the predicted drought-stress perception level at 0.5, the random forest model correctly predicted the treatment (control or drought) of all samples for only six accessions (IR64, KP, WRC02, WRC15, WRC24, and WRC50). In addition, all drought-treated samples showed higher predicted drought-stress perception levels than any of the control samples for 13 accessions (Dro1-NIL, Koshihikari, WRC01, WRC05, WRC13, WRC18, WRC19, WRC22, WRC27, WRC31, WRC33, WRC34, and WRC42). Thus, our model correctly assessed the relative stress levels of 19 accessions of the 40 investigated. Furthermore, the predicted drought-stress perception level appeared to be negatively correlated with SDW. We assessed this relationship by calculating the correlation among samples and among accessions under each treatment (Supplementary Fig. 10). Plants/accessions with low-stress levels assessed based on the 23 DSBMs at five days after drought treatment tended to show higher SDW at 14 days of drought treatment than the other plants/accessions grown under drought treatment. We also performed correlation analyses for the other phenotypes, such as TN (Supplementary Fig. 11) and PH (Supplementary Fig. 12), but the observed relationships were unclear.

Predicted drought-stress perception levels and shoot dry weights of the 40 accessions. Solid vertical lines indicate that all control and drought-treated samples were separated at the predicted drought-stress perception level of 0.5; dotted vertical lines, drawn at a border of the control and drought-treated samples, indicate that all drought-treated samples showed higher stress levels than the control samples

Next, we compared the predicted stress levels and root traits to examine whether specific root architectures affect model-based predictions. First, we focused on eight accessions (WRC13, WRC16, WRC19, WRC34, WRC35, WRC37, WRC44, WRC100) for which the model incorrectly classified more than two samples (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Fig. 13a). Most of these accessions showed predicted drought-stress perception levels of > 0.5 for control samples. Although the differences in predicted stress levels between control and drought-treated samples were smaller, the predicted stress levels tended to be higher in drought-treated samples, except for WRC37 and WRC44. This suggests that DSBM genes responded to drought treatment in these accessions and that the relative stress levels could be assessed in these accessions. Six out of eight accessions showed extreme root characteristics among those of the 40 accessions analyzed in our previous study, in which rice plants were grown under upland conditions [11]: WRC16, WRC19, and WRC34 had the largest lateral root surface areas, and WRC37 had the largest crown root surface area (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Fig. 13b and c). Larger root surface areas are expected to lead to more efficient water uptake. Therefore, perhaps these four accessions naturally demand more water than the others, resulting in higher drought-stress perception levels, even under control conditions. WRC35 exhibited the narrowest crown root diameter (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Fig. 13 d). Crown root diameter is associated with drought tolerance [11], suggesting that WRC35 is drought-susceptible, and control conditions might be perceived as drought treatment for this accession. WRC100 had the highest ratio of deep rooting, indicating that its roots tended to grow straight down into the soil (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Fig. 13e). This suggests that WRC100 could access water from deeper soil layers, resulting in lower drought-stress perception levels under drought treatment. In addition to the above insights focusing on the accessions showing unique root architecture traits, we calculated the correlation between the predicted drought-stress perception level (averaged over three plant replicates within control and drought treatment) and the root traits (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. 14). The lateral root surface area and the predicted drought-stress perception level, particularly under the control treatment, were positively correlated (r = 0.44; Fig. 6). This supports that the accessions with large root surface area might perceive drought stress even under the control treatment in our experiment. However, the relationship between the predicted drought-stress perception level and root phenotypes were not clear in most cases (P > 0.05 in four out of the eight panels in Supplementary Fig. 14), which is not surprising given the complex biological mechanism to sense and react to drought.

Relationship between the lateral root surface area and the predicted drought-stress perception level under the control treatment. Accessions with larger lateral root surface area (measured in a previous study [11]) tended to show higher drought-stress perception level even under the control treatment in our experiment

Collectively, our findings suggest that root architecture—especially drought tolerance-related traits such as root surface area, root diameter, and the ratio of deep rooting—directly or indirectly affects drought-stress perception levels. Therefore, our model might be more suitable for assessing the degree of drought stress rather than simply classifying whether plants are exposed to drought stress. Besides, this implies that the random forest (or any other machine learning) model-based assessment of drought-stress perception level might be improved by considering the root architecture traits.

Conclusions

In this study, we revealed gene expression trajectories in response to drought stress and identified DSBM genes that respond stably and specifically to drought stress. We developed a random forest model to predict the drought-stress perception levels in plants based on the expression of DSBM genes. This approach enabled us to evaluate DSBM genes in various rice accessions and confirm their versatility. Since drought stress affects gene expression levels well before plant growth retardation becomes detectable, this model-based prediction allows the monitoring of drought-stress perception levels and the efficient screening of drought-resistant lines in large-scale experiments. The RNA-seq and phenotypic datasets obtained in this study could serve as resources for further studies on the mechanisms of drought-stress tolerance in rice.
