Complete mitochondrial genome of Baylisascaris sp. from a striped skunk (Mephitis mephitis) in Kansas
Authors: Theresa A Quintana, Lauren E Camp, Steven A Nadler, Jeba RJ Jesudoss Chelladurai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBaylisascaris species parasitizing skunks have received limited attention compared to the well-characterized raccoon roundworm (B. procyonis). We present the first complete mitochondrial genome of a Baylisascaris sp. collected in Kansas, United States, from a striped skunk (Mephitis mephitis). We morphologically identified the nematode as Baylisascaris sp., extracted DNA, sequenced it on the Illumina platform, and de novo assembled the complete mitochondrial genome. The complete mitochondrial genome was 14,752 bp, consisting of 12 protein-coding genes, 2 rRNAs, and 22 tRNAs. The genome had a significant AT-skew (70.5%). The mitochondrial genome obtained in this study was 98% identical to that of B. procyonis and grouped most closely to B. procyonis with 100% bootstrap support in a phylogenetic analysis of ascarid nematodes. The mitochondrial genome generated in this study (GenBank accession no. PZ025724) will benefit future studies in diagnostic epidemiology and systematics and has implications for public health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00436-026-08732-z.Keywords: Mitochondrial genome, Ascaridoidea, Classification, Public health, Animals

Introduction

Baylisascaris (Ascarididae, Ascaridinae) is a genus of parasitic nematodes that infects a diverse range of carnivorous and omnivorous mammals, including raccoons, skunks, bears, red pandas, badgers, and mustelids (Sprent 1968; Sapp et al. 2017). The most significant species from a public health perspective is B. procyonis, the raccoon roundworm, which can cause neural larva migrans in humans, especially in young children who may inadvertently ingest embryonated eggs from contaminated soil (Kazacos 2001; Graeff-Teixeira et al. 2016).

Historically, Baylisascaris nematodes from raccoons were referred to as B. procyonis (Stefanski and Zarnowski, 1951) and those from skunks as B. columnaris (Leidy, 1856) (Sprent 1968). However, the designation of these nematodes from raccoons and skunks as separate species has been increasingly questioned (Franssen et al. 2013; Choi et al. 2017; Camp et al. 2018; Gu et al. 2024). Morphologically, the two species are almost indistinguishable except for the shape of the denticles (Sprent 1968). Multiple molecular studies have revealed remarkably low nucleotide divergence between raccoon- and skunk-derived worms across mitochondrial markers including cytochrome c oxidase 1 (cox1) and 2 (cox2) (Franssen et al. 2013; Choi et al. 2017; Camp et al. 2018; Gu et al. 2024), NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2 (nad2) (Choi et al. 2017), several transfer RNA (tRNA) genes (Choi et al. 2017), and 12 S ribosomal DNA (Camp et al. 2018). Nuclear DNA genes show the same pattern of low nucleotide divergence, including internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS-1) and 2 (ITS-2) (Franssen et al. 2013; Camp et al. 2018; Gu et al. 2024), 28S ribosomal DNA, major sperm protein (msp), and two exon-primed, intron-crossing loci genes, alcohol/ribitol dehydrogenase (ard-1) and histidyl tRNA synthetase (hars-1) (Camp et al. 2018). Phylogenetic analyses using single genes, multiple genes, and population genetic models have yielded inconsistent results regarding species boundaries (Franssen et al. 2013; Camp et al. 2018; Gu et al. 2024). Consequently, the current classification of Baylisascaris species needs additional investigation in light of available molecular evidence.

Mitochondrial genome studies of Baylisascaris species derived from skunks are of particular significance due to the limited data available, with no mitochondrial genome currently reported. Complete mitochondrial genomes have the potential for providing greater resolving power than single-gene analyses (Havird and Santos 2014; Zhang et al. 2021). In this study, we sequenced the complete mitochondrial genome of a skunk-derived Baylisascaris sp. on the Illumina platform (GenBank accession no. PZ025724) and examined its phylogenetic relationship with selected species from the family Ascarididae.

Materials and methods

An immature worm was collected during necropsy from the duodenum of a male striped skunk (Mephitis mephitis) from Kansas, that was presented to the Kansas State Veterinary Diagnostic Lab (KSVDL) in 2024 and was transferred to the Parasitology laboratory for identification. It was preserved in 70% ethanol and stored at -20°C until further processing. Total DNA was extracted using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and eluted in nuclease-free water and stored at − 20°C. Total DNA was quantified using a Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Eugene, OR). DNA was submitted to CD Genomics (Shirley, NY) for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing data were obtained in FASTQ format and are available through the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) sequence read archive (SRA) database (accession number SAMN61218381; BioProject: PRJNA1484093). Reads were trimmed using Trim Galore! version 0.6.10 (de Sena Brandine and Smith 2021) on Galaxy servers (Krueger et al. 2023) to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. Both raw and trimmed reads underwent quality control using Falco version 1.2.4 (Dierckxsens et al. 2016). The trimmed reads were then assembled into a circularized mitochondrial genome using NOVOPlasty version 4.3.5 (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009) on the High-Performance Computing (HPC) system at the Alabama Supercomputer Authority. The assembled genome was annotated with MITOS2 version 2.1.9 to identify protein-coding genes, rRNAs, and tRNAs. tRNA gene annotations were verified using ARAGORN version 1.2.41 (Laslett and Canback 2004). A circularized genome map was created using Proksee version 2026.02.09 (Grant et al. 2023). Genes were concatenated in R version 4.4.2 using the phylotools library version 0.2.5 (Zhang 2024).

To perform a phylogenetic analysis, we obtained 11 complete mitochondrial genomes from GenBank for species belonging to the family Ascarididae, with Ancylostoma caninum (accession no. NC_012309) used as an outgroup. The final dataset consisted of twelve protein-coding and two rRNA gene sequences for each mitochondrial genome. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT version 7.487 (Katoh et al. 2002), trimmed with trimAI version 1.2 (Nguyen et al. 2014), and concatenated in their genomic order beginning with nad4L with custom R scripts run in R version 4.4.2. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed from the concatenated dataset in IQTREE version 1.6.12 (Trifinopoulos et al. 2016) using partition-specific models (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) selected by ModelFinder based on Bayesian information criteria (BIC) (Letunic and Bork 2021). The results were visualized with iTOL version 7.3 (Letunic and Bork 2021).

Results and discussion

We present the first complete mitochondrial genome of Baylisascaris derived from a skunk. We used microscopy to examine the immature worm recovered from a striped skunk and confirmed its identification as Baylisascaris sp. (Supplementary Fig. 1). The complete mitochondrial genome is 14,752 bp and includes 12 protein-coding genes, 22 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and 2 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs). The atp8 gene is missing, consistent with other members of the family Ascarididae (Jex et al. 2008; Xie et al. 2011). The genome from this study was 98.07% identical to the reference B. procyonis complete mitochondrial genome (NC_016200). Figure 1; Table 1 show the gene organization and annotation of the complete mitochondrial genome of the skunk-derived Baylisascaris. This skunk Baylisascaris uses the invertebrate mitochondrial code (translation code 5). Of the 12 protein-coding genes, 2 start with the codon ATT (nad4l and nad5), 1 with ATA (atp6), 3 with GTG (nad2, cytB, and cox3), and 6 with TTG (nad1, nad4, cox1, cox2, nad3, and nad6). The stop codons include 4 genes ending in TAA (nad4l, nad2, nad3, and nad5) and 8 in TAG (nad1, atp6, cytB, cox3, nad4, cox1, cox2, and nad6). The lengths of the small and large rRNA are 699 and 963 bp, respectively. The nucleotide composition of the genome is approximately 21.9% adenine (A), 8% cytosine (C), 48.6% thymine (T), and 21.5% guanine (G), with a total AT content of approximately 70.5%. Start and stop codon usage, AT content, and genome size are consistent with other mitochondrial genomes of species in the family Ascarididae (Okimoto et al. 1992; Jex et al. 2008; Xie et al. 2011).

Gene annotation of the mitochondrial genome of Baylisascaris sp. from a skunk

Phylogenetic analysis of the 14 concatenated mitochondrial genes (12 protein-coding genes and 2 rRNA genes) is presented in Fig. 2. The skunk-derived Baylisascaris from this study is in Clade 1, grouping with B. procyonis, forming a strongly supported monophyletic clade (100% bootstrap support). Clade 2 is comprised of B. ailuri, B. schroederi, and B. transfuga. Other Baylisascaris spp. listed in Sprent (1968) and those analyzed in previous molecular studies (Tokiwa et al. 2014; Pérez Mata et al. 2016; Camp et al. 2018; Barrera et al. 2022), including B. devosi from a pine marten, B. potosis from kinkajous, B. tasmaniensis from a Tasmanian devil, B. laevis from a marmot, and B. venezuelensis from spectacled bears, were not analyzed here due to lack of complete mitochondrial genome data for these species. Our findings further support the growing evidence that skunk-derived and raccoon-derived Baylisascaris are closely related with low levels of genetic differentiation, reinforcing the need to obtain additional data for a taxonomic assessment of Baylisascaris from these hosts (Camp et al. 2018; Gu et al. 2024).

Clarifying the taxonomic relationship between skunk- and raccoon-derived Baylisascaris species has direct implications for public health. Raccoons and skunks exhibit overlapping geographic ranges throughout much of the United States. If skunks are infected with B. procyonis, or if skunk-associated Baylisascaris and B. procyonis represent the same species, skunks, in addition to raccoons, would contribute to environmental contamination, and the zoonotic risk would be equivalent. However, even if they are distinct species, skunk-derived Baylisascaris species remain a public health concern given the capacity for larval tissue migration in humans (Kazacos 2001). The epidemiological significance of Baylisascaris spp. from skunks requires further investigation.
