From analytical similarity to clinical confidence: how regulatory evolution ensures comparable immunogenicity in streamlined biosimilar development
Authors: Sandra Ribes, Marc Rosenbaum, Laura Hartmann, Lisa Allmannsberger, Johann Poetzl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiosimilars are biological medicines developed once exclusivity for the reference product has lapsed and are regulated through a stringent approval pathway, demonstrating the same efficacy, similar safety, and immunogenicity as their reference products. After two decades of clinical experience and scientific progress, regulatory agencies worldwide are transitioning toward a streamlined (also named tailored) biosimilar development that primarily relies on analytical and pharmacokinetic (PK) similarity, without routinely requiring comparative efficacy studies (CES). This narrative review discusses how comparable immunogenicity is demonstrated within streamlined development. Comparable analytical data provide the pivotal evidence for concluding comparable immunogenicity of a biosimilar candidate. Scientific evidence shows that PK similarity studies provide immunogenicity outcomes highly concordant with those observed in CES. Well-designed PK studies, particularly in healthy volunteers, provide sensitive and clinically informative immunogenicity assessments while minimizing confounding factors associated with disease and concomitant treatments. These studies can adequately characterize the onset, magnitude, and evolution of humoral immune responses without underestimating comparative immunogenicity. In the future, in silico and in vitro tools may enable additional risk-based assessment of immunogenicity differences between biosimilar and reference product. In conclusion, extensive scientific and regulatory evidence supports that streamlined biosimilar development provides a scientifically rigorous framework for demonstrating comparable immunogenicity.

Introduction

Biosimilars are biological medicines that are highly similar to an already authorized reference product and may enter the market once the patent protection and market exclusivity rights of the reference product have expired. Since their first launch in the European Union in 2006, biosimilar medicines have continued to provide high-quality therapeutic alternatives at more affordable prices, thereby supporting the sustainability of healthcare systems and enabling earlier, longer, and broader patient access to essential treatments, including immunology medicines and life-saving oncology medicines [1–3].

As therapeutic proteins, biosimilars may elicit an immune response that results in the development of anti-drug antibodies (ADA). While most biotherapeutics are immunogenic to some extent, the clinical consequences of an ADA response can vary widely. These effects may range from negligible to clinically relevant, for example, by altering the effectiveness and safety profile of the product [4,5]. ADA formation can reduce drug exposure, with or without an impact on clinical efficacy, or result in mild and generally manageable hypersensitivity reactions [6]. Consequently, the evaluation of immunogenicity remains an important component of the development and regulatory assessment of both reference products and biosimilars [7].

With substantial clinical experience accumulated over two decades of biosimilar use, the data requirements for biosimilar approval have evolved. Multiple regulatory agencies now recognize that establishing analytical similarity between a proposed biosimilar and the reference product, supported by a clinical pharmacokinetic (PK) study, is generally sufficient to infer a conclusion of biosimilarity, without routinely requiring comparative efficacy studies (CES). Major regulatory agencies have reflected this view, framing the approach as “streamlined biosimilar development” or a “tailored clinical approach,” and have updated their guidelines to recognize the omission of CES as a scientifically justified and viable option in biosimilar development and approval [8–11]. As of today, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA), in the respective draft guidance (October 2025) and final reflection paper (March 2026), state that CES may still be warranted in specific circumstances, such as when (i) the molecule is not adequately characterized analytically, (ii) its mechanism of action and structure-function relationships are insufficiently understood, or (iii) meaningful and clinically relevant PK characterization is not feasible, for example, for locally acting products such as intravitreally administered products with minimal systemic absorption [9,10].

As biosimilar development continues to evolve, products approved through streamlined development are expected to enter the market in increasing numbers. This narrative review examines how comparable immunogenicity is demonstrated within streamlined biosimilar development. In addition, we explore future directions for immunogenicity assessment, considering advances in in silico and in vitro methodologies while maintaining alignment with evolving regulatory expectations.

Methodology

This article is a narrative review conducted using a non-systematic literature search approach according to established methodological principles for narrative reviews [12]. The objective was to evaluate the scientific and current regulatory foundations of streamlined biosimilar development, with a particular focus on immunogenicity assessment and the respective contributions of comparative analytical assessment, PK similarity studies and CES.

Relevant regulatory documents were identified through targeted searches of publicly available websites of major health authorities involved in biosimilar evaluation, including the EMA, the FDA, the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), Health Canada, and the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH).

The scientific literature was identified using a targeted search strategy informed by references cited in regulatory guidance documents and supplemented by searches of PubMed and Google Scholar. Search terms included combinations of “biosimilar,” “streamlined development,” “tailored clinical approach,” “immunogenicity,” “anti-drug antibodies,” “PK study,” “PK similarity study,” “comparative clinical study,” and “comparative efficacy study.”

Eligible publications included peer-reviewed articles published in English between 1 April 2016 and 1 April 2026. All article types were considered, provided that they addressed biosimilar development, immunogenicity, regulatory science, or comparative clinical evidence relevant to streamlined development.

Given its narrative nature, this review has inherent limitations. The selection and interpretation of evidence may be subject to selection, retrieval, and publication bias. As streamlined biosimilar development is an evolving area of regulatory science, differences among regulatory jurisdictions and future policy updates may affect the applicability of some observations presented in this review.

Understanding the science behind the adoption of streamlined/tailored biosimilar development

The main pillar for demonstrating biosimilarity relies on robust and comprehensive comparative analytical and functional studies that establish the physicochemical, structural, and functional similarity of the proposed biosimilar candidate to the reference product and evaluate the impact of any differences, if they exist. Clinical development programs for biosimilars have traditionally included both a PK similarity study and a CES. In practice, biosimilar candidates supported by extensive and robust analytical and functional similarity data were routinely advanced into clinical studies in patients, in which safety and immunogenicity were assessed descriptively as secondary endpoints [13]. In their review, Goto and collaborators suggest two reasons why sponsors may have traditionally conducted CES: (i) to support approvals in regions where regulatory authorities still mandate such studies, or (ii) to mitigate the risk of development delays, anticipating that comparative quality and PK data alone might not be sufficient to justify a waiver of these studies [13].

Biosimilar CES studies generally require a larger number of participants and are often more extensive and costly than those required for new molecular entities [14,15]. Importantly, CES are not statistically powered to detect differences in immunogenicity outcomes. This raises an important scientific question regarding the extent to which CES provide additional comparative immunogenicity and safety data beyond those obtained from PK similarity studies.

Years of clinical experience with biosimilar products have generated cumulative scientific evidence that question the role of CES in biosimilar development. Furthermore, advances in analytical technologies enable precise and comprehensive comparative analytical assessments [16]. Multiple studies conducted by regulators and industry have shown that CES lack the sensitivity to detect anything other than large analytical differences, which can be identified much more sensitively through analytical studies [13,15,17–24]. Accordingly, the FDA has reported refusing approval of six biosimilar applications due to deficiencies identified in analytical data [21]. Notably, in only one of these six cases the difference was also flagged in a CES. To date, no clinical study submitted in biosimilar applications to the FDA has revealed a concern that had not already been detected during the comparative analytical assessment (CAA). These observations reinforce the fundamental role of the CAA, which provides a more sensitive evaluation of potential differences than the comparatively “blunt instrument” of CES [21].

As a result, the routine requirement to conduct CES has increasingly been recognized as a barrier to biosimilar development. In response, regulatory agencies acknowledge the new reality with more complex biologics approaching loss of exclusivity and have revised their frameworks to enable a more streamlined, science-driven approach, reducing reliance on extensive CES [8–11]. This shift has been formalized across major regions, with agencies acknowledging that, in most cases, analytical similarity supported by PK comparability and confirmatory immunogenicity data is sufficient to conclude biosimilarity. Notably, regulatory agencies are increasingly aligned in recognizing that immunogenicity can generally be evaluated within comparative PK studies. The MHRA, the health agency of the United Kingdom, was the first agency to pioneer streamlined development in its 2021 guidance, stating that a CES “may not be necessary in most cases” [8]. 2025 was a breakthrough year for agencies worldwide to foster streamlined development. The FDA issued draft guidance encouraging biosimilar developers to consider a streamlined development [9]. The EMA started guideline development for tailored biosimilar development with the publication of a reflection paper, which was finalized in March 2026 [10]. The ICH initiated the drafting of ICH M18 guideline, which will foster global harmonization of the regulatory requirements for streamlining among the participating regulatory agencies [25]. Health Canada finalized guidance that reverses the burden of proof from justifying why a CES study is not needed to why such a study should be needed for regulatory decision making [11]. With regulatory authorities and the scientific community increasingly aligned on what streamlined development entails, it remains important to understand and examine the scientific critical components to determine similarity. Within this framework, immunogenicity remains a critical component of biosimilarity assessment. The following sections explore how comparable immunogenicity is demonstrated in streamlined biosimilar development, with particular attention to the scientific elements that support a scientifically robust, clinically meaningful evaluation which is proportionate to the molecule’s actual immunogenicity risk.

Elements assuring comparable immunogenicity of biosimilars in the context of streamlined development

Analytical characterization: the scientific bedrock of immunogenicity comparability

The adoption of streamlined development does not change the fundamental foundation of similarity. On the contrary, it reinforces the central importance of comprehensive analytical characterization as the primary basis for assessing not only efficacy and safety, but also immunogenicity comparability.

Understanding the mechanism of action of the molecule, together with the ability to extensively characterize its structure and function using state-of-the-art physicochemical methods and functional assays, is crucial for demonstrating that the biosimilar is highly similar to the reference product at the physicochemical and in vitro functional levels [10]. Biosimilars of more complex yet still characterizable molecules, such as antibody-drug conjugates and other multispecifics, can be developed through streamlined development.

Quality Attributes (QAs) are physical, chemical, biological, or microbiological properties. QAs can impact biological function (efficacy), PK, safety and/or immunogenicity. The attributes are carefully evaluated in the CAA.

Several QAs directly influence the likelihood of an ADA response, highlighting their importance in the assessment of immunogenicity. Because biosimilars share an identical amino acid sequence with their reference products, they also present the same T cell epitopes, which are key determinants of immune recognition. This sequence identity provides the strongest evidence that the biosimilar is not expected to be more immunogenic than the reference product. Further relevant QAs are aggregates, non-human glycans (e.g., alpha-Gal motif) [26–28] and process-related impurities such as host cell proteins [29]. Extensive reviews of process- and product-related factors contributing to immunogenicity are provided by Singh, Kuriakose and Krishna [30–32]. Maintaining these QAs within acceptable limits is essential to minimizing immunogenic risk and ensuring patient safety. Current manufacturing quality standards require that, in particular, process-related impurities are minimized to the lowest achievable levels. A comprehensive CAA demonstrating no meaningful differences between the biosimilar and the reference product in attributes known to potentially impact immunogenicity provides the basis for a conclusion of comparable immunogenicity.

The concept of comparability allows for quality differences as long as they do not translate into relevant clinical differences [11,33]. Although manufacturing process changes and biosimilar development are distinct scenarios, both rely on the same scientific principle that analytical and functional characterization provides the most sensitive assessment of potential product-related differences and their potential clinical impact [34,35]. Importantly, the comparability principle has been routinely applied to support manufacturing process changes for biological medicines since the 1990s. These modifications are inevitable throughout the life cycle of biological medicines and have been well established for reference products well before biosimilar competition existed [35,36]. Even in the case of major process changes that represent large and potentially impactful changes to the manufacturing process such as the replacement of the master cell bank, these modifications have typically been implemented based on comparability exercises that rely mainly on analytical data, and in rare cases, are complemented with supportive PK trials [37]. This analytical confirmation supports the expectation that clinical performance of biologic medicines as well as safety and immunogenicity remain unaffected and prevents the need to conduct unnecessary CES. Recent examples of master cell bank changes of Entyvio, Opdivo, and Dupixent [38–40] illustrate the importance of analytical comparability, while PK studies provided supportive descriptive information. Notably, a numerically two-fold higher incidence in ADA rates of the new versus the old originator Opdivo process was concluded as comparable in the Public Assessment Report [39]. Overall, the scientific and regulatory experience supports that analytical characterization represents the most sensitive, reliable, and clinically relevant tool for ensuring immunogenicity comparability between biosimilars and their reference products.

PK similarity studies reflect comparative immunogenicity outcomes from CES

An important consideration in streamlined biosimilar development is whether the omission of CES may result in the loss of clinically meaningful immunogenicity information. Current regulatory and clinical evidence suggests that this is generally not the case. Immunogenicity outcomes obtained in PK studies are generally consistent with those reported in larger and longer CES, irrespective of the underlying immunogenicity risk of the molecule.

In 2021, EMA regulators published the results of an analysis of 29 biosimilar monoclonal antibodies and fusion proteins and found no cases in which immunogenicity outcomes differed between PK studies and CES [41]. Similarly, a review of the analytical and clinical data supporting 12 biosimilar approvals concluded that appropriately powered PK similarity studies yielded robust comparative safety and immunogenicity information, particularly when strong analytical and functional similarity, as well as comparable PK and impurity profiles, had been demonstrated [19].

Additional evidence comes from a study evaluating seven biosimilar ustekinumab products. This comparative analysis showed consistency between PK and CES immunogenicity findings in biosimilar ustekinumabs, i.e., immunogenicity incidence was similar between single-dose healthy volunteer PK studies and multiple-dose patient studies [24]. Lower ADA incidences were observed in all biosimilar products compared with the originator product in single-dose PK as well as in multiple-dose CES studies. Therefore, in those seven cases, CES did not provide additional meaningful clinical immunogenicity data compared to single-dose studies. The observed lower ADA incidence across all biosimilars compared to their reference products also correlated with results from thorough analytical characterization. In particular, differences in product quality attributes known to potentially trigger immunogenicity, such as non-human glycans α-1,3 galactose and N-glycolylneuraminic acid were detected. The levels of these non-human glycans were generally lower for all biosimilars compared to the originator product, which is in line with lower ADA incidence observed on clinical studies [24]. Overall, these case studies show that comparative analytical assessment in combination with comparative clinical single-dose studies are sufficient to demonstrate similar immunogenicity and to establish biosimilarity.

A broader assessment is provided by a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by FDA authors that evaluated safety and immunogenicity data from 49 FDA-approved biosimilars [23]. This analysis included products with low inherent immunogenicity risk, such as bevacizumab, denosumab, and etanercept, as well as products with higher immunogenicity rates, including multiple adalimumab biosimilars. Irrespective of the inherent immunogenicity risk of the molecule or the potential clinical relevance of the immune response, ADA and neutralizing antibody (NAb) incidences observed in PK similarity studies were generally consistent with those reported in subsequent CES, indicating that PK studies did not underestimate comparative immunogenicity [23].

To maximize sensitivity for detecting potential differences, PK similarity studies should be conducted in homogeneous populations that minimize sources of biological variability. Healthy volunteers represent the most sensitive population for the comparative assessment of immunogenicity [42,43]. Unlike patient populations, healthy volunteers have an intact immune system capable of mounting an ADA response to the biotherapeutic and are not affected by disease-related factors or concomitant medications that may confound immunogenicity findings. In some cases, when the safety profile of a product precludes administration to healthy individuals, as may occur for certain oncology products, PK similarity studies must be conducted in patients.

Differences in dose and dosing regimens between study settings should still be acknowledged. PK similarity studies in healthy volunteers typically involve a single administration of a subtherapeutic dose, whereas patients in CES often receive higher and repeated therapeutic dosing over extended periods. From an immunological perspective, sustained exposure to high concentrations of antigen following repeated dosing may influence the magnitude and detectability of measurable ADA responses. It may also lower immunogenicity due to immune tolerance effects [44]. Collection of immunogenicity data in a PK study following a single administration is also appropriate as the half-life of the biosimilar/reference product is generally sufficiently long to induce an ADA response. Consequently, the lower drug dose typically used in single-dose PK studies may be an advantage for comparative immunogenicity assessment. These concepts are supported by recent clinical evidence. In a study evaluating three adalimumab dosing regimens, including a single administration in healthy volunteers and two repeated-dosing regimens in patients, ADA responses were detected as early as four weeks after treatment initiation [44]. ADA incidence was higher in the single-dose cohort (91.7%) than in the multiple-dose cohorts (54.8% and 64.4%) at Week 12. The authors proposed that prolonged exposure to high antigen concentrations during repeated dosing may attenuate observable ADA responses despite continued immune stimulation. The study also demonstrates that the relative comparison between the biosimilar and the reference product is already apparent early in treatment and remains consistent during longer follow-up. Similar findings have been reported for natalizumab, where biosimilar and reference products demonstrated comparable immunogenicity across study designs, while overall ADA incidence was numerically higher in healthy volunteers receiving a single dose (87% − 92.0%) than in patients receiving repeated treatment (73.7% − 79.4%) [45]. Furthermore, observed titers and NAb incidence were comparable across study designs [45]. Overall, these studies indicate that extended observation periods with longer-term treatment did not provide additional information relevant to comparative immunogenicity assessment.

Additional evidence indicates that key features of the ADA response can be characterized following the administration of a single dose in healthy participants. Single-dose PK studies are sensitive for detecting the onset and evolution of humoral immune responses, particularly when the study design incorporates more frequent sampling in the early period following drug administration [44,46]. In an adalimumab PK study, ADA magnitude was measured using signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) instead of titer, enabling a detailed characterization of the dynamics of the ADA responses through ADA area under the curve S/N–time analyses. The results after administration of a single dose showed characteristic temporal patterns consistent with primary and secondary immune responses, indicating class switching from early IgM responses to IgG responses. A second study reported IgG4 class switching within 4 weeks following administration of a single dose of adalimumab [44]. Taken together, these findings suggest that a full humoral antibody response can be detected after a single administration, without requiring multiple dosing.

Overall, the accumulated evidence indicates that well-designed PK similarity studies can provide meaningful and reliable insights into comparative immunogenicity. These studies are capable of characterizing the onset and evolution of ADA responses and, across multiple biosimilar studies, have yielded immunogenicity results that are consistent with those observed in CES.

In vitro and in silico immunogenicity assessment

Building on the analytical and clinical evidence for regulatory decision-making, there is growing interest in leveraging non-clinical methods to assess immunogenicity risk during biosimilar development. These approaches may provide additional insights into the evaluation of immunogenicity.

In vitro immunogenicity assays model key steps involved in the development of an immune response to therapeutic proteins [47]. The primary objective of these in vitro assays in biosimilar development is to identify and evaluate product- and process-related differences between the biosimilar and its reference product, including key immunogenicity risk drivers such as aggregates [48,49], post-translational modifications (PTMs) [50], immunologically relevant process-related impurities (e.g., host cell proteins (HCPs)) [51], and formulation-related factors.

It is important to emphasize that in vitro immunogenicity assessments do not evaluate the potential clinical consequences for patients, nor do they directly predict the likelihood or severity of ADA development.

Although these assays offer significant promise, interpretation of in vitro immunogenicity data remains challenging due to the biological complexity of these assays. Therefore, harmonized best practices for experimental design, assay conditions, controls, and data analysis are critical to ensure greater robustness and comparability across industry [52,53]. These elements are also key for data interpretation by health authorities and the future deployment of these methods as a risk assessment tool in streamlined biosimilar development.

Notably, in vitro and in silico assessment tools are already accepted in other regulatory contexts involving potentially immunogenic products. For example, both the FDA and the EMA recognize the use of in vitro and in silico approaches to evaluate the immunogenicity risk of peptide-related impurities in generic synthetic peptides [54,55]. This supports the use of these approaches as part of a risk-based immunogenicity assessment strategy. For comparative biosimilar assessments, in vitro assays must be qualified as fit-for-purpose and include adequate controls to define assay performance and dynamic range. Future studies should evaluate the ability of these assays to reliably detect differences between biosimilars and reference products in terms of immunogenicity-relevant QAs. This will determine whether these assays can be employed as additional risk assessment tools for specific use cases within biosimilar development. To this end, it will also be critical to assess whether a correlation exists between in vitro immunogenicity data, differences in immunogenicity-relevant QAs, and the clinical immunogenicity outcomes, as well as how strong this correlation is.

In vitro assays can be complemented by in silico, i.e., computational, tools, which predict the binding of processed peptides to major histocompatibility complex class II molecules based on the primary sequence of a therapeutic protein.

Since a biosimilar and its reference product have identical amino acid sequences, in silico tools can be used during biosimilar development to evaluate novel sequence-variant impurities that are not present in the reference product. They may also be applied to assess the immunogenicity risk associated with HCPs, however additional evaluation of cross-reactivity to human homologs is needed [56,57].

Other impurities, such as aggregates or PTMs, cannot be evaluated using current in silico tools, as these algorithms can only process natural amino acids. For generic synthetic peptides, however, approaches have been proposed to evaluate unnatural amino acids, which are frequently observed in peptide-related impurities, by using existing in silico tools [58].

Conclusion

There is extensive scientific evidence supporting streamlined biosimilar development, whereby detailed analytical and functional comparisons, supported by PK similarity and a supportive clinical evaluation of immunogenicity, can provide sufficient evidence for regulatory decision-making. Accumulated evidence from biosimilars used worldwide shows that CES have generally not provided additional meaningful data when robust analytical and PK similarity have been established, nor have they identified clinically relevant quality differences that were not detectable through analytical methods. These observations confirm the high sensitivity and clinical relevance of analytical comparability, which is also routinely applied to evaluate manufacturing changes to biologic medicines. Within a risk-based development framework, similarity in safety profiles and ADA responses can be reliably assessed in PK studies conducted in healthy volunteers, making the omission of CES scientifically justified in most circumstances while recognizing that specific situations may still warrant additional clinical data.

Future perspective

Immunogenicity will remain a critical consideration in biosimilar development, and its assessment will continue to be guided by a risk-based approach. As regulatory frameworks continue to evolve, biosimilar development is poised to become more efficient while safeguarding the rigorous standards for patient safety and therapeutic efficacy.

Comprehensive analytical similarity data, together with a clinical PK study, are increasingly recognized as providing more sensitive and highly informative data than CES for regulatory decision-making. Ongoing advances in in vitro and in silico methodologies, combined with optimized risk-based immunogenicity testing strategies, may further support biosimilarity assessments. For in vitro immunogenicity assays, existing gaps in standardization, data interpretation, and cross-industry alignment need to be addressed, and assays must be qualified as fit-for-purpose. This is essential to generate robust, interpretable data for submission to health authorities, thereby enhancing both the acceptance and the regulatory impact of these methods.

Taken together, streamlined biosimilar development ensures that immunogenicity evaluation remains scientifically rigorous, clinically relevant, and proportionate to risk.
