Valbenazine Sprinkle: An Alternative Formulation of Valbenazine for Oral Administration in Patients With Dysphagia
Authors: Nagdeep Giri, Roland Jimenez, Linda Rees, Alexander Mar, Ali Bristow, Mello Hebert, Jessica Maynard‐Scott, Hui Zhang, Satjit Brar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTValbenazine is a vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitor approved for the treatment of tardive dyskinesia and Huntington's chorea. Dysphagia, pill aversion, and fear of swallowing are common in patients with these conditions. A new valbenazine sprinkle capsule, to be swallowed whole or the granule contents sprinkled onto soft food, was developed and approved based on the studies presented here. Study 1 evaluated the stability of valbenazine granules in soft foods. Recovery of valbenazine granules within 2 h after addition to applesauce, pudding, and yogurt was 95.6%–100.2%, indicating acceptable stability. Study 2 was a randomized, open‐label, 2‐cohort, phase 1 study in healthy volunteers. Plasma concentrations of valbenazine and its active metabolite, [+]‐α‐dihydrotetrabenazine ([+]‐α‐HTBZ) were measured serially after a single dose under various conditions. Cohort 1 evaluated bioequivalence between the sprinkle capsule and the original capsule with compacted powder (reference). Pharmacokinetics of valbenazine and [+]‐α‐HTBZ were similar between the whole sprinkle capsule and reference. When granules were sprinkled on applesauce, a lower valbenazine Cmax and AUC were observed relative to reference; all other parameters were similar. Cohort 2 evaluated food effects on valbenazine granules and found that the pharmacokinetics of valbenazine and [+]‐α‐HTBZ were generally similar between fed and fasted states. Overall results from both cohorts for the 90% CIs of the geometric mean ratios indicated bioequivalence between the whole sprinkle capsule or granules sprinkled on applesauce and reference, with minor exceptions. These findings support that the VMAT2 inhibitor valbenazine can be used with multiple modes of oral administration.

Introduction

Valbenazine, a highly selective vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitor, is approved for the treatment of tardive dyskinesia (TD) and chorea associated with Huntington's disease (HD) [1]. Both TD and HD chorea are characterized by hyperkinetic movements, with TD being a typically persistent drug‐induced movement disorder associated with exposure to antipsychotics and other dopamine receptor blocking agents and HD being an inherited neurodegenerative disorder that progresses over time [2, 3, 4].

While the etiology of TD and HD chorea differ, the abnormal movements of both disorders can interfere with swallowing. In TD, persistent tongue protrusions and disturbed gag reflexes are common symptoms of TD, and may be associated with dysphagia in these patients [5]. In HD, choreic movements in the oropharyngeal muscles can lead to dysphagia, while neck or trunk hyperextension can make swallowing or eating difficult [6]. Up to 90% of individuals with HD may experience some degree of dysphagia during the progression of their disease, with 35% experiencing dysphagia in early stages [7, 8]. Dysphagia and other swallowing difficulties may have a range of negative consequences, from problems with malnutrition to decreased adherence to medications, and aspiration pneumonia, a leading cause of death in HD [9, 10].

Dysphagia and pill aversion have also been associated with mental health disorders, which are common in both TD and HD. Patients who take antipsychotics are at risk of developing TD [11], and HD is characterized by a triad of psychiatric, cognitive, and motor symptoms that progress over time [12]. In a systematic review of 6 studies that included patients with schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, major depression with and without psychotic features, bipolar disorder, borderline personality, anxiety disorders, and alcohol dependence, the reported frequency of dysphagia or choking ranged from 9% to 42%, higher than the estimated 6% prevalence in the general population [13]. A relationship between antipsychotic use and dysphagia has also been documented in case reports and smaller studies among psychiatric patients, with reported prevalences of swallowing problems among people receiving antipsychotics ranging from 5% to 30.5% [14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

Patients with mental health conditions, including schizophrenia, depression and anxiety [19, 20], can also experience gagging and nausea when swallowing, as well as a heavy feeling in the stomach [19]. More generally, non‐physiological dysphagia (e.g., pill aversion) can arise from psychological or emotional factors, such as fear of choking in patients with HD chorea [7], or sensitivities to pill size, shape, or texture, or higher pill burden [19, 21, 22]. Pill aversion has been reported among 11%–54% of adults in various clinic settings (e.g., community pharmacies and hospital clinics) [20].

Dysphagia may be particularly problematic in older adults, as aging can negatively affect head and neck anatomy, as well as the physiologic and neural mechanisms involved in swallowing [23]. Older adults may also have medical and psychiatric conditions that can impede their ability to swallow, including strokes, head and neck cancer, dementia, and Parkinson's disease [24]. Prevalence estimates of swallowing difficulties among the older community‐dwelling adults range from 5% to 72% (mean, 15%) [25], with one study reporting that 50.2% of patients in nursing homes experience dysphagia [26]. Comorbid conditions and polypharmacy with associated medication side effects (e.g., dry mouth) may additionally contribute to swallowing difficulties and pill aversion in elderly adults.

As might be expected, dysphagia and pill aversion can lead to poor medication adherence [20]. Community‐based surveys have reported that problems with swallowing pills led to skipped medication doses in 23%–69% of people, including 27% of nursing home residents [21, 27, 28]. To facilitate oral dosing and maintain adherence, cutting or crushing medication to mix with drinks or foods is a strategy used in patients with dysphagia or pill aversion. However, many oral medications, including tablet or capsule formulations of deutetrabenazine (immediate‐ or extended‐release deuterated form of tetrabenazine), pantoprazole, duloxetine, bupropion, quetiapine (extended‐release), and metformin (extended‐release), should not be cut or crushed before administration as this will result in a change in pharmacological activity in the body and potentially have ramifications related to the safety and/or efficacy of the medication [29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. Even for medications that can be crushed, knowledge and application of appropriate crushing practices can vary among practitioners who administer these medications [35].

The pharmacology and pharmacokinetics of valbenazine have been studied, though its mechanism of action in the treatment of TD or HD chorea remains unclear [1, 36, 37]. In the body, valbenazine is converted via hydrolysis of a valine ester to its primary metabolite NBI‐98782, also known as [+]‐α‐dihydrotetrabenazine ([+]‐α‐HTBZ). [+]‐α‐HTBZ exhibits potent VMAT2 inhibition with negligible affinity for dopamine, serotonin, and adrenergic off‐target receptors [37]. Pharmacokinetic analyses of valbenazine in healthy subjects indicate rapid absorption (time to maximum concentration [t max] of 0.5–1 h) and an elimination half‐life of 15–22 h. The active metabolite, [+]‐α‐HTBZ, is formed gradually (t max 4–8 h) and has a half‐life similar to valbenazine (16–23 h), suggesting formation rate‐limited clearance. This pharmacokinetic profile supports once‐daily dosing of valbenazine [1].

Recognizing the need for an effective medication that offers alternative modes of oral delivery for patients with TD or HD who have dysphagia or pill aversion, in vitro studies were conducted that demonstrated the acceptability of crushing the compacted powder content of the original valbenazine capsule for mixing with soft foods and liquids or dispersing in water for delivery via a gastrostomy tube (G‐tube) [38].

A new valbenazine sprinkle capsule is now available for both TD and HD chorea [1]. These capsules can be swallowed whole or opened to allow the granule contents to be sprinkled onto various soft foods (but not mixed with water or milk nor delivered via a G‐tube). Development and approval of the sprinkle capsule were based on the studies presented in this report.

Materials and Methods

Study 1: Stability in Soft Foods and Liquids

Study 1 evaluated the stability of valbenazine granules in soft foods and liquids of varying acidity. Valbenazine sprinkle capsules (80 mg) were manually opened and the granules were sprinkled onto 1 tbsp of applesauce, chocolate pudding, or yogurt. The granules were also added to liquid buffer solutions (pH 1.2, pH 4.5, and pH 6.8) to assess stability across a wide pH range, as well as to fed‐state simulated gastric fluid (FeSSGF) to mimic the digestive environment of the stomach [39].

All soft food and liquid samples were analyzed in duplicate for valbenazine and its known degradants ([+]‐α‐HTBZ and NBI‐139326) using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) at the following time points: immediately before the addition of granules, immediately after addition (0 h), and at 0.5, 1, and 2 h after addition. Acceptability (i.e., stability in the tested food or liquid) was defined as valbenazine recovery of 95%–105% and recovery of ≤ 2.0% degradants, including known degradants within normal manufacturing specifications ([+]‐α‐HTBZ ≤ 1.2%, NBI‐139326 ≤ 0.4%) and unspecified degradants (≤ 0.2%). The acceptability range for valbenazine recovery (95%–105%) is more conservative than the industry standard of 90%–110%, a range that is generally considered acceptable for ensuring a medicine's stability throughout its shelf‐life [40, 41].

Study 2: Bioequivalence and Food Effect

Study Design and Participants

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki for biomedical research involving humans and the International Conference on Harmonization Harmonized Tripartite Guideline regarding Good Clinical Practice and with Good Laboratory Practice. The study was sponsored by Neurocrine Biosciences (San Diego, California). The study was approved by the institutional review board of the study center. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their enrollment in the study.

Study 2 was a randomized, open‐label, 2‐cohort, phase 1 study (Figure 1). Eligible participants included healthy men and women, aged 19–55 years with a body mass index between 18 and 32 kg/m2. Key exclusion criteria included a history of long QT syndrome or cardiac arrhythmia and prior exposure to VMAT2 inhibitors; CYP2D6 poor or ultrarapid metabolizers were also excluded, based on genotyping during screening.

Phase 1 study design.

For Cohort 1 (bioequivalence cohort), a 3‐seqfuence, 3‐period crossover design was used to evaluate bioequivalence between the valbenazine sprinkle capsule and the valbenazine capsule with compacted powder content. Participants were randomized according to a predetermined scheme to 1 of 3 treatment sequences, each consisting of 3 treatment periods (Figure 1A). Sample size for both cohorts was based on practical considerations. In each treatment period, participants received a single dose of 80 mg of valbenazine as one of the following preparations: as a whole sprinkle capsule (test formulation), as granules sprinkled on applesauce (test formulation), or as a whole valbenazine capsule with compacted powder (reference formulation). Participants fasted overnight (≥ 10 h) and then took valbenazine as described above, along with 240 mL of water; a standard meal was provided 4 h after treatment administration. Participants underwent a 7‐day washout between treatment periods to mitigate any carry over effect from the prior period.

For Cohort 2 (food effect cohort), a 2‐sequence, 2‐period crossover design was used to evaluate the effect of food on the pharmacokinetics of valbenazine when taken as granules sprinkled on applesauce. Participants were randomized according to a predetermined scheme to 1 of 2 treatment sequences, each consisting of 2 treatment periods (Figure 1B). During each treatment period, participants fasted overnight (≥ 10 h) and then took valbenazine granules (80 mg) sprinkled on applesauce with 240 mL of water, either with a standard high‐fat, high‐calorie meal as defined by the US Food and Drug Administration [42] (fed state, test condition) or with no meal (fasted state, reference condition). Participants who took valbenazine granules sprinkled on applesauce in the fasted state continued to fast for 4 h following valbenazine administration before receiving a standard meal. Participants underwent a 7‐day washout between treatment periods.

Pharmacokinetic Analyses

During each treatment period, blood samples were collected 1 h predose and throughout a 96 h sampling period postdose (Figure S1). Plasma concentrations of valbenazine and its active metabolite, [+]‐α‐HTBZ, were analyzed according to a validated method using HPLC with tandem mass spectrometry [37].

In each cohort, the pharmacokinetic analysis population included all randomized participants who received at least 1 treatment dose, had 1 postdose blood sample, and had 1 available pharmacokinetic parameter. Data were excluded if the participant's predose concentration of valbenazine or [+]‐α‐HTBZ was > 5% of the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) for that participant or if the participant vomited following ingestion of valbenazine. Plasma concentrations that were below the limit of quantification (< 1.00 ng/mL for valbenazine or < 0.10 ng/mL for [+]‐α‐HTBZ) were set equal to zero for linearly scaled data analyses or “missing” for log scale data analyses.

Pharmacokinetic parameters were derived from plasma concentrations by noncompartmental methods using Phoenix WinNonlin v8.2 (Certara USA Inc.). Descriptive statistics were performed for the following pharmacokinetic parameters for both valbenazine and [+]‐α‐HTBZ: Cmax; area under the curve (AUC) from 0 h to the last measurable concentration (AUC0–tlast); AUC from 0 h extrapolated to infinity (AUC0–∞); AUC extrapolated from time t to infinity as a percentage of the total AUC (%AUCextrap), time to Cmax (t max); and terminal half‐life (t 1/2). In addition, the apparent systemic clearance after oral administration (CL/F) and apparent volume of distribution during the terminal phase after oral administration (Vd/F) were derived for valbenazine and summarized descriptively.

Determination of Bioequivalence

Geometric mean ratios (GMRs) between test and reference conditions were determined for AUC0–tlast, AUC0–∞, and Cmax using an analysis of variance model with log‐transformed values and with sequence, period, and treatment as fixed effects. Bioequivalence between test and reference conditions was defined as the 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for GMRs falling between 80% and 125%.

Safety Assessments and Analyses

The safety populations for each cohort included all randomized participants who received ≥ 1 treatment dose. Safety assessments included adverse event (AE) monitoring, physical examination, 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG), clinical laboratory assessments (hematology, clinical chemistry, and urinalysis), and vital sign measurements. Safety data were summarized using descriptive statistics.

Results

Study 1: Stability in Soft Foods and Liquids

The recovery of valbenazine granules within 2 h after addition to applesauce, pudding, and yogurt, ranged from 96.9% to 100.1%, indicating acceptable recovery and stability of valbenazine when mixed with these soft foods (Figure S2). Furthermore, valbenazine recovery within 2 h ranged from 96.5% to 102.1% after dissolution of granules in liquid buffers and FeSSGF (Table S1). Recovery of degradation products was within acceptable limits for all tested soft foods, liquid buffer solutions, and FeSSGF, except when valbenazine granules were added to the pH 6.8 buffer solution. When added to the pH 6.8 buffer solution, recovery of a known degradant (NBI‐139326) and unspecified degradants both exceeded the acceptability range in ≥ 1 sample at ≥ 1 timepoint. The maximum recovery for NBI‐139326 was 0.65% (acceptability limit 0.4%), and the maximum recovery for unspecified degradants was 0.27% (acceptability limit 0.2%).

Study 2: Bioequivalence (Cohort 1)

Study Participants

In Cohort 1, 24 participants were randomized across the 3 treatment sequences (Table S2). Of these, 12 (50.0%) were male and 16 (66.7%) were white, with a mean age (±SD) of 34.1 (±10.7) years. All Cohort 1 participants completed the 3 treatment sequences; data from 1 treatment period (valbenazine granules sprinkled on applesauce) of 1 participant were excluded from the pharmacokinetic analysis due to vomiting following ingestion of valbenazine. All participants included in the analysis for bioequivalence had quantifiable concentrations at the last sampled timepoint (96 h postdose).

Pharmacokinetics and Bioequivalence

The pharmacokinetics of valbenazine and [+]‐α‐HTBZ were similar between the whole sprinkle capsule, granules sprinkled on applesauce, and the whole capsule with compacted powder (reference), with the exception of an observed lower Cmax for valbenazine when taken as granules sprinkled on applesauce (Figure 2A,B, Table 1).

Safety

Valbenazine was well tolerated, regardless of formulation, with no serious treatment‐emergent AEs (TEAEs), severe TEAEs, or discontinuations due to TEAEs (Table S3). Among the 24 participants in Cohort 1, 13 (54.2%) experienced ≥ 1 TEAE during the 3 treatment periods. The most common TEAEs were headache and nausea, which occurred in 7 (29.2%) and 6 (25.0%) participants, respectively. No abnormal, clinically significant findings were observed for ECGs, laboratory values, or vital signs.

Study 2: Food Effect (Cohort 2)

Study Participants

In Cohort 2, 14 participants were randomized across the two treatment sequences (Table S2). Of these, 8 (57.1%) were male and 10 (71.4%) were white, with a mean age of 36.1 (±7.9) years. Two participants (1 from each treatment sequence) withdrew consent after beginning the second treatment period (1 in the fasted‐state treatment, 1 in the fed‐state treatment) due to potential exposure to the COVID‐19 virus; neither withdrawal was related to tolerability issues or adverse events. Per the study protocol, both participants were replaced. Pharmacokinetic analyses included 12 participants who completed the study, all of whom had quantifiable concentrations at the last sampled timepoint (96 h postdose). Safety data were collected in 14 participants, which included the 2 participants who replaced those who withdrew.

Pharmacokinetics and Bioequivalence

When granules were administered on applesauce, the pharmacokinetics of both valbenazine and [+]‐α‐HTBZ were similar between fed (with high‐fat meal) and fasted (without high‐fat meal) states with the exception of an observed lower Cmax for valbenazine. Valbenazine Cmax was reduced by ~15% in the fed state, relative to the fasted state (Figure 2C,D, Table 3). The 90% CIs of the GMRs of AUCs for both valbenazine and [+]‐α‐HTBZ in the fed state were within the criteria for bioequivalence to the fasted state. The 90% CI of the GMR of Cmax for [+]‐α‐HTBZ in the fed state was within the criteria for bioequivalence to the fasted state but not for valbenazine (90% CI: 76.0% to 96.3%) (Table 4).

Safety

Valbenazine granules sprinkled on applesauce were well tolerated, regardless of fed or fasted state (Table S3). No serious TEAEs, severe TEAEs, or discontinuations due to TEAEs occurred. Among the 14 participants in Cohort 2, 5 (35.7%) experienced ≥ 1 TEAE during the 2 treatment periods. Headache occurred in 2 (14.3%) participants; all other TEAEs occurred in 1 participant. No abnormal, clinically significant safety findings were observed in ECG, clinical laboratory results, and vital signs.

Discussion

Given the prevalence of dysphagia, fear of choking, and swallowing problems in patients with TD or HD chorea, there is an unmet clinical need for effective medications that can be safely administered by alternative modes of oral delivery. With 2 approved oral formulations, valbenazine is the only VMAT2 inhibitor that provides options for treating patients with TD or HD chorea who may have difficulty swallowing whole pills. Alternative modes of oral administration can also benefit care partners of patients who require assistance taking medications and for healthcare providers in institutional settings, including hospitals and long‐term care facilities.

Results presented here demonstrate that the valbenazine sprinkle granules, whether taken as a whole capsule or sprinkled onto soft foods, were stable and comparable to the valbenazine capsule with compacted powder (reference formulation).

Based on AUC0–∞, AUC0–tlast, and Cmax values for valbenazine and its active metabolite [+]‐α‐HTBZ, valbenazine sprinkle capsule was considered bioequivalent to the capsule with compacted powder. Similarly, pharmacokinetic analyses demonstrated bioequivalence between granules on applesauce and compacted powder capsule, with the exception of valbenazine Cmax. Importantly, the AUC0–∞, AUC0–tlast, and Cmax of active metabolite [+]‐α‐HTBZ, which is highly selective for VMAT2 and largely responsible for the pharmacologic activity of valbenazine [36, 37, 43], were all within the criteria for bioequivalence.

In addition, evaluation of valbenazine and [+]‐α‐HTBZ pharmacokinetics following administration of valbenazine granules with or without food (high‐fat meal vs. fasted states) supports administration of valbenazine granules without regard to food intake. AUC values of valbenazine and [+]‐α‐HTBZ fell within bioequivalence criteria. Cmax fell within bioequivalence criteria for [+]‐α‐HTBZ but not valbenazine. Finally, valbenazine granules were well tolerated when taken as the sprinkle capsule or as granules sprinkled on soft foods, with no serious TEAEs, no severe TEAEs, and no TEAEs leading to study discontinuation.

When administered onto 3 common soft foods (applesauce, yogurt, pudding), recovery of valbenazine was within the predefined acceptable range for all conditions tested for up to 2 h, the time limit for storage at room temperature following preparation according to the valbenazine prescribing information [1]. Recovery of known and unspecified degradants was within normal manufacturing specifications, with the exception of the known degradant NBI‐139326 at 1 and 2 h following addition of valbenazine granules to a pH 6.8 buffer solution. The recovery and stability of valbenazine during in vitro analysis align with the observed findings of bioequivalence and support an expectation of maintained safety and efficacy with these alternative modes of oral administration when following the dosing instructions in the prescribing information.

The finding of valbenazine stability when granules were sprinkled on a wide range of soft foods or mixed with liquids (pH 1.2–6.8) builds on previously published in vitro studies that established the suitability of crushing the compacted powder content of valbenazine capsules for addition to soft foods, mixing with drinks, or dispersing in water for G‐tube administration [38]. Given that Study 1 showed stability in conditions below pH 6.8, various acidic or slightly acidic soft foods can be tried, such as cottage cheese, hummus, jellies/jams, or mashed fruits. However, if in doubt, all 3 soft foods used in Study 1 (applesauce, yogurt, pudding) demonstrated acceptability and are listed in the approved dosing recommendations for valbenazine sprinkle [1]. Moreover, mixing granules into water or milk is prohibited in the dosing recommendations because those liquids have a pH > 6.8, which causes faster degradation of the medication.

The formulation of valbenazine sprinkle capsules may be of particular relevance to long‐term care facilities or nursing homes, where a significant proportion of patients may experience dysphagia or pill aversion [26]. In a survey of 158 patients with HD in Dutch nursing homes, 91% had at least one symptom of dysphagia and 52% expressed a fear of choking [7]. With the fear that these individuals might choke, as expressed by both informal and formal caregivers (68% and 34%, respectively), managing the consistency of food and drinks was cited as a common adaptive strategy [7]. The alternative modes for oral delivery of valbenazine provide options in this setting. Moreover, valbenazine has been shown to be effective and well tolerated in patients with TD who were 65 years of age and older [44], with no dose adjustment necessary in elderly patients, making it a uniquely appropriate option for this patient population [1].

Pill crushing or mixing with food is a common strategy used to address swallowing difficulties and their associated negative consequences on medical therapy including suboptimal dosing, potentially reduced therapeutic effect, and challenges in medication adherence over the short and long term [20]. In HD chorea and TD, these difficulties are often due to negative impacts of the disease on the swallowing process; however, it is possible that patients requiring treatment with a VMAT2 inhibitor may also experience pill aversion leading to swallowing difficulties. Valbenazine sprinkle capsules with granule contents provide alternatives for medication administration, which can address individual patient barriers and improve adherence to treatment.

Findings from the in vitro and bioequivalence studies reported here were reviewed by regulatory authorities during the successful application for the new sprinkle capsule formulation, which has received marketing approval in the United States.

In conclusion, a new valbenazine sprinkle capsule has been approved and is now available in addition to the original capsule with compacted powder. The sprinkle capsule can be swallowed whole or opened to sprinkle granule contents on soft food. With these two formulations, valbenazine is the only VMAT2 inhibitor that offers once‐daily dosing with multiple modes of oral administration that are suitable for patients with TD or HD who experience difficulties swallowing, whether due to physiological factors (e.g., abnormal movements, aging, comorbid conditions) or non‐physiological factors (e.g., pill aversion, fear of choking).
