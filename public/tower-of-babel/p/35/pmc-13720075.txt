Statistical insights into the role of HCN–mineral interactions in aqueous aerosols during chemical evolution
Authors: Antonio López-García, Elena González-Toril, Fuencisla Cañadas, Marta Ruiz-Bermejo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe polymerization of hydrogen cyanide (HCN) is considered a central route to molecular complexity in prebiotic chemistry, following the seminal synthesis of adenine reported by Oró. Here, we investigate how mineral identity and mineral loading modulate cyanide reactivity in aqueous aerosols using selected geologically relevant silicate, oxide, carbonate, sulfide, and mixed mineral substrates as simplified analogues of early planetary mineral–water interfaces. Using a target-agnostic, systems-level strategy, we combine complementary spectroscopic and chromatographic techniques, such as FTIR spectroscopy, HPLC–FLD and GC–MS, with multivariate statistical analyses (CATPCA and PCA) to interrogate highly complex reaction mixtures from cyanide polymerizations in the presence of aqueous aerosols. This approach reveals statistically robust, mineral-dependent trends in spectroscopic and chromatographic profiles that are strongly dependent on both mineral abundance and analytical methodologies. Our results demonstrate that minerals could act either as inert matrices or as active modulators of cyanide chemistry, while highlighting the power of untargeted statistical frameworks to extract chemically meaningful information from analytically intractable prebiotic systems, as is the case of the organic complex mixtures from cyanide polymerization reactions.Subject terms: Origin of life, Analytical chemistry

Introduction

The pioneering and successful Miller–Urey experiment1, the Butlerov reaction2, and Oró’s synthesis3,4 constitute three foundational pillars of experimental prebiotic chemistry. The Miller-Urey experiment demonstrated the abiotic formation of amino acids through the activation of a gaseous mixture simulating the atmosphere of early Earth1,5. The second one, commonly known as the formose reaction, produces ribose through the heating of formaldehyde under alkaline conditions2,6. Finally, Oró reported the production of adenine through the thermal treatment of cyanide solutions in the presence of ammonium salts3,7. These three syntheses have been extensively studied; however, they share a fundamental challenge: analytical intractability arising from the inherent chemical complexity of the raw reaction mixtures, which comprise a huge number of analytes that are extremely difficult, if not practically impossible, to identify individually and unambiguously. While these synthetic pathways provide elegant and insightful models of plausible abiotic environments for understanding the emergence of key bioorganic molecules in modern biochemistry, the crude reaction mixtures they produce are highly complex, containing both discrete compounds and polymeric materials. All of them are clear examples of uncontrolled increasing of molecular complexity under likely prebiotic conditions. As a result, they present a huge challenge for analytical chemists. Traditionally, studies of such complex mixtures have focused on identifying specific, well-defined analytes using targeted protocols, often based on chromatographic techniques8. However, this targeted analytical approach inevitably leads to a substantial loss of chemical information. By the contrary, statistical approaches are central to systems-level science, enabling the extraction of meaningful insights, as for example, from complex chemical datasets. The multivariate statistical methods are particularly powerful for providing holistic overviews of such datasets9. Moreover, studies by the Cronin group show that complex untargeted networks (typically prone to combinatorial chaos, i.e., the uncontrolled proliferation of a vast number of possible molecular products) can be environmentally steered by plausible geological constraints, such as pH or mineral surfaces, toward distinct, less diverse, and more functionally coherent product ensembles10,11. Reaction cycling and the presence of salts or mineral surfaces reduce product multiplicity and enhance the yields of specific compounds, demonstrating environmental “selection” in non-biological mixtures11. These results experimentally support the idea that minerals can program organic chemistry, offering a target-agnostic framework for probing how early abiotic Earth conditions could have shaped emergent chemical complexity.

From this perspective, the aim of the present work is to investigate the effect of a representative set of minerals on the polymerization of cyanide under plausible prebiotic conditions using aqueous aerosols, as a simplified model to understand how the increasing of the molecular complexity can be affected by the geochemical conditions. Thus, a range of terrestrial depositional environments and extraterrestrial analogues were simulated (Table 1 and Fig. 1. Texts S1 and S2), considering both the likely ubiquity of HCN (e.g., refs. 12–18) and the widespread formation of aqueous aerosols under early Earth and planetary conditions19–22. The selected mineral substrates were chosen to cover broad geochemical diversity and to represent mineral–water interfaces potentially relevant to early planetary environments. The set includes single-phase silicate, oxide, carbonate, phosphate, sulfide and sulfate minerals, together with mixed assemblages such as volcanic ash, aeolian dust, hydrothermal mud and a Martian regolith simulant. Thus, single-mineral experiments allow the influence of specific mineral phases to be evaluated, whereas mixed substrates provide simplified analogues of more complex sedimentary, hydrothermal, volcanic, arid and planetary surface systems. On the other hand, although the cyanide concentration employed here (1 M) may appear high, recent estimates suggest that HCN concentrations of up to ~ 0.01 M could have been reached in the primitive ocean16, with the early atmosphere acting as the major source of HCN13,14,18. These concentrations are sufficient to sustain cyanide polymerization and could have been further enhanced by evaporation in alkaline environments, such as soda lakes23.

These environments may serve as sites of HCN production or accumulation (e.g., from atmospheric input13,14) and provide conditions for the generation of aqueous aerosols through physical processes at the water–air interface. Together, these factors create scenarios in which HCN-bearing aqueous phases and mineral substrates could coexist and interact under geochemically and prebiotically plausible conditions.

HCN polymerization has been extensively examined due to its proposed central role as a prebiotic precursor (e.g., refs. 12,24–28), its behavior in mineral-rich settings remains largely unresolved. To our knowledge, only two studies have directly evaluated mineral influences on the generation of (bio)organic products from HCN polymerization: (i) gamma-irradiated HCN–Na-montmorillonite mixtures showed a substantial decrease in carboxylic acid formation29; (ii) and experiments simulating a serpentinite-hosted alkaline hydrothermal system similarly reported inhibited production of bioorganic compounds, relative to mineral-free controls30. On the other hand, aqueous aerosols are believed to have formed readily on early Earth19 and are known to enhance both the formation and diversity of polar compounds, including amino and carboxylic acids20,21,31, as well as N-heterocycles32. In fact, interest in aqueous aerosols-mediated prebiotic chemistry has grown considerably in recent years due to the particular characteristic of these water systems (e.g., refs. 33–37).

Accordingly, the experimental framework adopted here encompasses a chemically plausible range of prebiotic scenarios in which mineral-dependent effects can be systematically assessed. They were produced from cyanide polymerization reactions using an active aerosol cycle over four days, as an initial experimental approach, in the presence of different inorganic substrates. This study focuses exclusively on the sol fraction (i.e., the soluble phase obtained as the supernatant after centrifugation), since the generation of gel phases (insoluble and dark precipitated solids), which are characteristic of alkaline cyanide polymerizations12, is inhibited by aqueous aerosols and particularly negligible for short reaction times (around three days) and in the lack of air31. Specifically in this work, a non-targeted statistical approach was applied to these sol fractions, including: (i) an initial exploration of FTIR (Fourier Transform Infrared) spectral data; (ii) analysis of primary amine distributions using HPLC-FLD (High performance Liquid Chromatography-Fluorescence Detector); (iii) detection of chromatographic peaks related to polar organic compounds by GC–MS (Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry) following acid hydrolysis; and (iv) assessment of the potential formation of nitrogen-containing heterocycles (mainly triazines and pyrimidines) by GC–MS after basic hydrolysis. Note that although no specific analytes are explicitly targeted for the statistical study, the methodology involves sample processing steps designed to detect particular classes of compounds. Therefore, the “target-agnostic” nature of the present work applies primarily to its overall statistical framework, whereas certain analytical steps necessarily entail selective sample preparation and analysis.

As discussed below, certain minerals exert statistically significant effects on the spectroscopic and chromatographic profiles of the resulting cyanide-derived mixtures, with additional modulation due to the mineral loading (weight of mineral used in each experimental series). However, these trends cannot be fully generalized across minerals, as the observed outcomes also depend on the post-reaction treatment of the samples and the analytical method applied. These aspects are examined in detail, along of the following pages, with respect to both the analytical behavior of the mixtures and the environmentally conditioned growth of molecular complexity.

Results and discussion

CATPCA of spectral and chromatographic signatures from sol fractions in mineral-driven aqueous aerosol cyanide reactions (30 mg series)

Categorical Principal Components Analysis (CATPCA) is a dimensionality reduction technique designed for categorical and ordinal variables that transforms the original variables into a reduced set of uncorrelated components, thereby revealing latent patterns and simplifying the interpretation of complex datasets38. Although originally developed as a general-purpose statistical method, CATPCA has been successfully applied in chemical research, particularly for the interpretation of spectroscopic and chromatographic data, where it facilitates classification of the samples by their spectroscopic and chromatographic profiles and enhances interpretability of the raw data39,40. In this study, CATPCA was applied to FTIR spectra, as well as HPLC and GC–MS chromatograms, obtained from the samples summarized in Table 2 to evaluate the overall effects of inorganic compounds and minerals on cyanide polymerization reactions. This approach provides an integrated view of how minerals influence increases in molecular complexity. FTIR spectra and all chromatograms, recorded in triplicate (Figs. S2–S6, representing three independent experiments), for the 30 mg sample series (Table 2) were included in these first CATPCA analyses.

For the statistical analysis of the FTIR spectra data, only the spectral region between 2300 and 2000 cm⁻¹ (the nitrile region) was considered, as it does not overlap with absorption bands of the inorganic substrates investigated (Fig. S1) and corresponds to a diagnostic region for cyanide-derived polymers41,42. Chromatographic profiles of primary amines, also for this 30 mg samples series, were obtained using a modified version of a commercial HPLC–FLD method for amino acid identification43. In addition, GC–MS chromatograms acquired after acid hydrolysis allowed a broader characterization of polar compounds, including species bearing amine, hydroxyl, and carboxyl functional groups. This approach has previously been used by us to obtain an initial, comprehensive overview of polar organic compounds present in complex mixtures resulting from cyanide polymerization reactions31,44, using BSTFA (see experimental section) as derivatization reagent to obtain the corresponding TMS-derivatives. Finally, CATPCA was applied to GC–MS profiles, of these same samples (30 mg series), obtained after basic hydrolysis, using an analytical protocol specifically developed to detect selected N-heterocycles such as pyrimidines and triazines45.

The resulting Correlation Matrices S1–S4, and the corresponding CATPCA biplots derived from the different spectroscopic and chromatographic datasets, related with the samples of the 30 mg series, are shown in Fig. 2. On the other hand, in a general way, in CATPCA, the correlation coefficient (r) is interpreted as follows: loadings close to 1 or −1 indicate strong positive or negative relationships, whereas values close to 0 indicate little or no relationship. Accordingly, throughout this study, correlations are classified using the following criteria: strong (r ≥ 0.85), high (0.85 > r ≥ 0.75), moderate (0.75 > r ≥ 0.5), and weak (r < 0.5). Moreover, as a general guideline, the main clusters in Fig. 2 are those comprising the largest number of samples that exhibit strong to moderate correlations with one another.

CATPCA of the FTIR spectra

The CATPCA-derived correlation matrix of the FTIR spectra for the 30 mg sample series (Correlation Matrix S1; variance explained: 84.2%) reveals a pronounced clustering of most inorganic substrates, reflected by the predominance of strong positive correlations (r ≥ 0.85), as illustrated in Fig. 2a.

This pattern indicates a largely shared spectral response across these experimental conditions. A particularly coherent group is observed among MSI30, MFO30, MAL30, MBA30, MAN30, MZI30, MSA30, and MMR30, which exhibit near-unity correlations (r up to 0.97). The control experiment (CE) also shows strong correlations with this group (r ≈ 0.88–0.93), indicating that these minerals do not significantly alter the FTIR spectral profile of this nitrile region relative to the mineral-free system. In contrast, a subset of samples, most notably MVI30, MTE30, and MIM30, displays weaker or even negative correlations with the main cluster, pointing to distinct spectral signatures. MVI30 correlates strongly only with MIM30 (r = 0.94), while MIM30 also shows a high correlation with MPY30 (r = 0.80). Interestingly, iron-containing minerals define a secondary cluster that includes MMA30, MHE30, MSD30, and MPY30 (r > 0.75). Similarly, MCA30 correlates highly only with MKA30 (r = 0.81), whereas MKA30 itself shows high correlations with most members of the iron-containing mineral cluster (r > 0.75). The CATPCA dimensional structure supports this interpretation: the first two components account for most of the variance (eigenvalues of 11.8 and 4.2, respectively, Correlation Matrix S1), indicating that the observed spectral variability is largely governed by a limited number of chemically meaningful factors, while higher-order components contribute only marginally.

CATPCA of the HPLC-FLD chromatograms

The CATPCA-derived correlation matrix of the HPLC–FLD chromatograms for the 30 mg sample series (Correlation Matrix S2; variance explained: 77.3%) shows overall high positive correlations across most experimental setups, indicating a broadly shared chromatographic response and comparable reaction outcomes under the investigated conditions (Fig. 2b). A large subset of samples, including MSI30, MFO30, MAL30, MAN30, MSD30, MPY30, MVI30, MZI30, MSA30, and MMR30, exhibits consistently from strong to high correlations (typically r ≈ 0.75–0.94), suggesting similar distributions of derivatized fluorescent products. The control experiment follows this general trend, displaying moderate-to-strong correlations with most substrates. Compared to the FTIR analysis, the HPLC–FLD correlation structure is more homogeneous, with fewer extreme correlations and less sharply defined clustering, as it is showed clearly to compare the groupings of the Fig. 2a, b and the Correlation Matrices S1 and S2. Nevertheless, a distinct subgroup comprising MBA30, MHE30, and MMA30 is characterized by strong mutual correlations (r ≈ 0.88–0.90) and comparatively weaker correlations with the main cluster, indicating a differentiated chromatographic profile likely reflecting substrate-specific effects on product formation or chromatographic behavior. As observed in the FTIR analysis, MTE30 does not show significant correlations with other samples, while MCA30 correlates highly only with MKA30 (r = 0.82). In this case, however, MKA30 does not cluster clearly with the iron-containing minerals but instead shows a high correlation with MZI30 (r = 0.80). The CATPCA dimensional structure corroborates these observations: the first component dominates the variance (eigenvalue = 12.7, Correlation Matrix S2), whereas the second and third components contribute more modestly (eigenvalues of 2.0 and 1.0, respectively), indicating that HPLC–FLD variability is largely governed by a single underlying chemical factor, with secondary components capturing finer substrate-dependent differences.

CATPCA of the GC-MS chromatograms (Acid hydrolysis. Overall detection of polar organic compounds)

The CATPCA-derived correlation matrix of the GC–MS chromatograms obtained after acid hydrolysis for the 30 mg sample series (Correlation Matrix S3; variance explained: 72.1%) reveals a structured with a less homogeneous correlation grouping pattern than those observed for the FTIR and HPLC–FLD datasets, indicating increased diversification of chemical outcomes following hydrolytic treatment (Fig. 2c). A main cluster of samples, including MFO30, MAL30, MBA30, MAN30, MHE30, MZI30, MSA30, and MMR30, exhibits from strong to high mutual correlations (typically r ≈ 0.80–0.90), suggesting comparable distributions of hydrolysis-derived polar products. The control experiment also shows high correlations with this group, particularly with MAL30 and MZI30, indicating that the dominant post-hydrolysis chemical signature is largely preserved even in the presence of inorganic substrates. In contrast, several experimental setups, most notably MCA30, MSD30, MVI30, and MTE30, (without significant correlations among them) display systematically weaker correlations with the main cluster (r generally < 0.45), pointing to statical distinct GC–MS profiles. The CATPCA dimensional structure supports this interpretation: although the first component remains dominant (eigenvalue = 12.0), the second and third components retain non-negligible contributions (eigenvalues of 1.7 and 1.1, respectively, Correlation Matrix S3), indicating that multiple chemically meaningful factors are required to account for the variability of the hydrolyzed samples.

CATPCA of the GC-MS chromatograms (Basic hydrolysis. Detection of specific N-heterocycles)

Finally, the CATPCA-derived correlation matrix of the GC–MS chromatograms obtained after basic hydrolysis for the 30 mg sample series (Correlation Matrix S4; total variance explained: 67.1%) displays a moderately organized but markedly less homogeneous correlation structure than that observed after acid hydrolysis (Correlation Matrix S3), indicating enhanced diversification of chemical profiles (Fig. 2d). Although positive correlations still dominate, their overall magnitude is reduced and more unevenly distributed, suggesting that basic hydrolysis might accentuate substrate-dependent chemical differences. A principal subset of samples, including MFO30, MHE30, MSD30, MZI30, and MSA30, shows high mutual correlations (typically r ≈ 0.73–0.82), with particularly high correlations between MHE30 and MSD30. These samples also correlate moderately to highly with MAN30, MAL30, and MPY30 (r ≈ 0.60–0.80), with the correlation between MPY30 and MAN30 being especially pronounced. In contrast, MCA30 and MTE30 exhibit consistently weak correlations with most of the dataset (r generally < 0.30), reflecting distinct GC–MS profiles after basic hydrolysis. MVI30 shows an intermediate response, correlating moderately with a limited subset of samples, including MBA30 and MMR30, which themselves exhibit a strong mutual correlation. Similarly, MKA30 displays a selective and intermediate correlation pattern. It does not cluster with the main group of strongly correlated samples, yet it shows from moderate to weak correlations with MBA30, MSD30, MHE30, and MFO30 (r ≈ 0.48–0.56), while its correlations with most other substrates and with the control experiment remain weak. The control experiment shows from moderate to weak correlations with most samples (r ≈ 0.48–0.70) but does not integrate clearly into any specific cluster, indicating that, in contrast to acid hydrolysis results, the presence and nature of inorganic substrates might exert a more pronounced influence on the chemical profiles generated under alkaline conditions to identified N-heterocycles. The CATPCA dimensional structure supports these observations. Although the first component remains dominant (eigenvalue = 10.3, Correlation Matrix S4), it accounts for a smaller fraction of the total variance than in the FTIR, HPLC–FLD, and acid-hydrolyzed GC–MS datasets. The second and third components retain substantial contributions (eigenvalues of 2.0 and 1.3, respectively, Correlation Matrix S4), indicating that multiple chemically meaningful factors are required to describe the variability induced by basic hydrolysis. Overall, these results suggest that basic hydrolysis may be particularly sensitive to specific mineral–organic interactions, potentially enabling the emergence of chemical divergences among substrates that are less evident under other analytical conditions.

Therefore, the CATPCA analyses reveal that the influence of inorganic substrates on cyanide-derived chemical complexity is strongly mineral-dependent and method-sensitive. Across FTIR and HPLC–FLD datasets, several minerals and mixtures (notably silica (SI), forsterite (FO), alumina (AL), anatase (AN), zirconia (ZI), Saharan dust (SA), and Martian regolith (MR)) display high correlations with the control experiment, indicating that their presence has a largely negligible effect on the global molecular signatures detected under these conditions. In contrast, other substrates, including calcite (CA), tenorite (TE), vitriol of Mars (VI), and, to a lesser extent, kaolinite (KA), consistently deviate from the control, particularly in GC–MS analyses after acid and basic hydrolysis, pointing to distinct transformation pathways or product distributions. Iron-containing minerals (pyrite (PY), hematite (HE), maghemite (MA) and siderite (SD)) further define differentiated responses, forming coherent but control-divergent clusters in several datasets, particularly when their FTIR spectra are considered.

As a general result, the CATPCA analyses of the 30 mg mineral series indicate that the nature and composition of the sol fractions, derived from cyanide polymerization reactions, are influenced by mineral identity. Whereas some substrates behave as largely inert matrices, respect to the control experiment (CE), others modulate cyanide reactivity under alkaline aqueous aerosol conditions, leading to statistically significant distinct spectroscopic and chromatographic signatures. Moreover, these differences are selectively influenced by the post-treatment reaction of the samples and the analytical technique chosen.

Effect of mineral loading on aqueous cyanide polymerization (comparison 30 and 150 mg series)

Building on the findings of the previous sections, we next investigated how increasing mineral loading influences these potentially abiotic reaction systems. Assessing loading effects, as amount in weight of the minerals in the simulated prebiotic environment, is essential to determine whether mineral-induced signatures observed at low loading are simply intensified at higher abundance, progressively dampened, or qualitatively transformed as mineral–solution interactions become more prominent. To address this question in a statistically robust and chemically meaningful manner, a representative subset of mineral substrates was selected based on the results of the initial CATPCA screening.

Silica (SI) and alumina (AL) were chosen as effectively inert reference materials, as their presence at 30 mg did not induce statistically significant deviations from the control experiment (CE) in either spectroscopic or chromatographic datasets. Martian regolith (MR), a multicomponent assemblage representative of complex natural mineralogy, was also included because it similarly exerted only minor influence on cyanide-derived chemical signatures at low loading. On the other hand, calcite (CA), blue apatite (BA), and hematite (HE) were selected as reactive substrates, as they displayed pronounced deviations from the control experiment (CE) across multiple analytical techniques, suggesting a strong potential to modulate cyanide reactivity.

To evaluate how mineral effects evolve with increasing mineral loading, CATPCA analyses were performed by directly comparing the datasets obtained at 30 mg with those generated at 150 mg mineral loading for each selected substrate (Figs. S7–S10). This paired-experiment approach allows for a systematic assessment of loading-driven effects while minimizing confounding factors related to mineral identity or analytical variability.

FTIR spectroscopy: mineral-dependent sensitivity to loading

Correlation analysis of the FTIR spectra reveals a clear mineral-dependent effect of loading on spectral similarity (Correlation Matrix S5; total variance explained: 91.1%; Fig. 3a). For silica- and alumina-bearing systems, the spectra obtained at 30 mg and 150 mg mineral concentrations are strongly correlated (r ≈ 0.91–0.95 for MSI and r ≈ 0.83–0.95 for MAL). These high correlation coefficients indicate that increasing mineral loading does not significantly alter the dominant vibrational features of the system. In these cases, the chemical environment probed by FT-IR remains effectively invariant with concentration, supporting the interpretation that silica (SI) and alumina (AL) act as largely passive substrates that do not perturb the bulk chemical structure of cyanide-derived polymers. In contrast, the remaining minerals exhibit a more pronounced sensitivity to mineral loading. For blue apatite (BA), hematite (HE), and Martian regolith (MR), correlations between the 30 mg and 150 mg paired experiments are markedly lower, indicating that changes in mineral loading lead to detectable modifications in the FTIR response. At the same time, strong intercorrelations emerge among several 150 mg samples, most notably MBA150, MCA150, and MHE150 (r > 0.87), suggesting partial convergence of bulk vibrational features at high mineral abundance. This effect is particularly pronounced for calcite-based systems. The correlation between MCA30 and MCA150 is only moderate, and MCA30 displays weaker correlations with the rest of the dataset (Correlation Matrix S5), evidencing a substantial concentration-driven modification of the FTIR signature. Notably, MCA150 correlates strongly only with MBA150 (r = 0.91), indicating that high calcite loading induces a distinct chemical environment that partially overlaps with that of other reactive minerals only at elevated loading.

Consistent with these observations, the CATPCA dimensional structure shows that the first two components account for most of the variance (eigenvalues 7.7 and 3.2, Correlation Matrix S5), indicating that spectral variability is dominated by a limited number of chemical factors that are strongly modulated by mineral identity and, for specific substrates, by mineral concentration. These results show that while silica (SI) and alumina (AL) are largely insensitive to loading changes at the level of bulk vibrational features, mineral loading constitutes a key factor shaping the FTIR signatures of blue apatite- (BA), hematite- (HE), Martian regolith- (MR), and especially calcium carbonate (CA)-bearing systems.

HPLC-FLD: enhanced sensitivity of soluble products to mineral loading

Correlation analysis of the HPLC-FLD chromatograms reveals a markedly different pattern respect to the FTIR spectra data (Correlation Matrix S6; variance explained: 70.1%; Fig. 3b). This behavior points to a higher sensitivity of chromatographic profiles to mineral concentration, reflecting the responsiveness of soluble cyanide-derived products to changes in mineral loading. Across the correlation matrix, coefficients are predominantly moderate, confirming that variations in mineral loading exert a stronger influence on the distribution of fluorescent species than on bulk vibrational features. For all minerals, correlations between the 30 mg and 150 mg paired experiments are consistently moderate rather than strong. Even for silica (SI) and alumina (AL), which appear largely inert in FT-IR, MSI30–MSI150 and MAL30–MAL150 correlations fall within the range r ≈ 0.64–0.77. This indicates that, although overall chemical reactivity may not be dramatically altered, mineral loading affects the relative abundance of soluble products resolved by HPLC-FLD. Consequently, paired experiments cannot be considered chromatographically equivalent, even for nominally inert substrates. Among the more reactive minerals, blue apatite and hematite display particularly clear loading-dependent effects. While MBA30 and MHE30 are strongly correlated with each other (r ≈ 0.90), their correlations with the corresponding 150 mg samples are substantially lower. This pattern indicates that increasing mineral loading leads to a reorganization of soluble reaction products rather than a simple amplification of the low-loading profiles. At higher mineral concentration, correlations among MBA150, MHE150, and MMR150 remain only moderate (r ≈ 0.61–0.75) and are significantly weaker than those observed in FTIR. This suggests partial convergence toward similar chromatographic patterns without the emergence of a fully shared chemical signature. Calcite (CA) again exhibits a distinctive behavior: MCA30 shows moderate correlations with most samples, whereas MCA150 correlates more consistently with other 150 mg experiments. The lack of a strong MCA30–MCA150 correlation points to a qualitative modification of soluble cyanide-derived species as calcite abundance increases, consistent with the progressive dominance of calcite-mediated processes in solution.

The CATPCA dimensional structure reinforces these conclusions, with the first component accounting for most of the variance (eigenvalue 7.1, Correlation Matrix S6), while subsequent components capture finer distinctions related to mineral identity and concentration. Overall, the HPLC-FLD dataset highlights mineral loading as a decisive factor in shaping soluble reaction products, with calcite (CA) representing the most pronounced case.

GC–MS after acid hydrolysis: partial homogenization of molecular signatures

Correlation analysis of the GC–MS chromatograms obtained after acid hydrolysis reveals an intermediate behavior between FTIR spectra and HPLC-FLD chromatograms, in relationship with the grouping of the samples (Correlation Matrix S7; variance explained: 77.0%; Fig. 3c). The CATPCA model is dominated by the first two components (eigenvalues 7.1 and 2.1), indicating that a limited number of chemical factors govern the distribution of hydrolysis-released compounds. In general, correlation coefficients are predominantly moderate to high, suggesting that acid hydrolysis partially renders uniform molecular signatures while preserving mineral- and concentration-dependent effects. For silica (SI) and alumina (AL), correlations between the 30 mg and 150 mg experiments remain moderate (MSI30–MSI150, r = 0.65; MAL30–MAL150 r = 0.77), indicating that increasing mineral loading introduces quantitative changes without fundamentally altering the nature of the compounds detected by GC–MS. These systems also display strong intercorrelations with MBA30 and MHE30. Blue apatite (BA) and hematite (HE) exhibit clearer mineral loading dependence. While BA30 and HE30 correlate strongly with each other and with several low-loading samples, their correlations with the corresponding 150 mg experiments decrease, indicating that higher mineral abundance leads to redistribution of hydrolysis products rather than simple scaling. Calcite-bearing systems again show the most distinctive behavior: although MCA30 and MCA150 are moderately correlated, MCA150 correlates more strongly with other 150 mg samples, particularly MBA150 and MMR150, suggesting a dominant calcite-mediated influence at high concentration. To conclude, GC–MS after acid hydrolysis reveals a common structural pool of cyanide-derived compounds while retaining mineral- and concentration-specific modulations, positioning this technique between FTIR spectroscopy and HPLC-FLD in sensitivity to mineral loading.

GC–MS after basic hydrolysis: amplification of concentration-dependent divergence

A distinct pattern emerges from correlation analysis of GC–MS chromatograms obtained after basic hydrolysis (Correlation Matrix S8; variance explained: 65.5%; Fig. 3d). The lower eigenvalues of the first two CATPCA components (5.5 and 2.4) and the increased contribution of higher-order components indicate a chemically more diverse and less convergent dataset. Correlation coefficients are predominantly low to moderate, evidencing substantial dispersion and heightened sensitivity to both mineral identity and mineral loading. Unlike acid hydrolysis, paired experiments consistently show weak correlations, indicating that increasing mineral loading under basic conditions induces qualitative changes in the molecular inventory. Even silica (SI) and alumina (AL) display low paired correlations (MSI30–MSI150 r = 0.26; MAL30–MAL150 r = 0.45), demonstrating that basic hydrolysis amplifies loading-dependent differences even in nominally inert systems. Reactive substrates show particularly strong divergence. Blue apatite (BA) and hematite (HE) exhibit weak correlations between low- and high-loading experiments, while their 150 mg samples correlate more strongly with other high-loading systems than with their 30 mg counterparts. Calcite again stands out: MCA30 correlates weakly across the matrix, whereas MCA150 shows stronger correlations with other 150 mg samples, notably MHE150 and MMR150, indicating a dominant carbonate-mediated control under alkaline conditions. Taken together, basic hydrolysis preserves and amplifies mineral- and loading-specific differences, yielding the highest chemical dispersion observed across all analytical approaches.

As summary, across all paired experiments, the combined analyses from Correlation Matrices S5–S8 and CATPCA biplots (Fig. 3) demonstrate that the impact of mineral loading on cyanide polymerization is strongly technique-dependent. FT-IR spectroscopy is relatively insensitive to mineral loading for inert minerals but reveals pronounced mineral-loading effects for reactive substrates, particularly calcite (CA). HPLC-FLD highlights the decisive role of mineral loading in shaping soluble products, even for inert minerals. GC–MS further reveals that post-reaction treatment critically modulates mineral loading effects, with acid hydrolysis partially homogenizing molecular signatures and basic hydrolysis amplifying divergence. Collectively, increasing mineral loading does not simply scale reaction outputs but reorganizes chemical pathways. In strong agreement, similar results can be observed after paired samples test from the experiments carried out using different mineral loads. Paired contrast is significant for all the six minerals considered and for the all the techniques used herein (Tables S2–S5).

Assessment of analytical fidelity and internal consistency in multi-technique experimental data

To evaluate the statistical robustness and internal consistency of the CATPCA results discussed above, a reliability analysis based on standardized Cronbach’s alpha coefficients was performed for all spectroscopic and chromatographic datasets, using the triplicate measurements acquired for each sample and analytical technique (Table S6, Text S3). Cronbach’s alpha provides a quantitative measure of the extent to which replicated measurements capture a coherent and reproducible underlying signal46,47 and therefore represents a critical validation step for multivariate approaches such as CATPCA.

The reliability analysis confirms that the CATPCA results are statistically well supported for the FTIR and HPLC–FLD datasets, robust and readily interpretable for GC–MS chromatograms obtained after acid hydrolysis, and chemically informative, albeit intrinsically more variable, for GC–MS analyses following basic hydrolysis. Notably, the systematic decrease in Cronbach’s alpha from FTIR spectroscopy to HPLC–FLD and ultimately to GC–MS under alkaline conditions mirrors the progressive increase in chemical specificity introduced by successive sample-handling steps and by the analytical techniques employed. This trend reflects the enhanced sensitivity of the multivariate analyses to subtle variations in molecular composition as the analytical focus shifts from bulk spectral features to increasingly resolved and chemically selective molecular fingerprints. The close correspondence between analytical reliability and CATPCA-derived dispersion therefore reinforces the main conclusions of this study, indicating that the observed mineral- and concentration-dependent trends arise from genuine chemical effects rather than experimental artefacts or methodological noise.

Principal components analysis (PCA) from soluble organic matter fixed and molecular diversity

The CATPCA results discussed above clearly demonstrate that minerals exert a statistically significant influence on cyanide polymerization under aqueous aerosol conditions, as revealed by the comparative analysis of complementary spectroscopic and chromatographic datasets. A key question that follows is whether these multivariate differences can be quantitatively linked to (i) the amount of organic matter fixed as soluble products and (ii) the molecular diversity generated within the different cyanide–mineral systems.

Relationship between soluble organic matter yield and molecular diversity

Figure 4a (Table S7) summarizes the amount of organic matter fixed as semi-volatile and non-volatile soluble compounds (black square). As expected, these values correlate directly with the final mass balance of the corresponding cyanide polymerization reactions (Fig. 4a, red cycles). In all cases, the mass balance is negative, likely reflecting the dominance of elimination reactions involving decyanation and deamination processes, in agreement with previous studies42. Relative to the control experiment (CE), three distinct groups are identified: (i) minerals yielding amounts of soluble matter comparable to the control (highlighted in light red); (ii) minerals exhibiting significantly lower yields (highlighted in violet), with calcite (CA) showing the most pronounced effect; and (iii) minerals displaying reduced mass loss and consequently higher final amounts of fixed soluble material, including tenorite (TE), vitriol of Mars (VI), and maghemite (MA).

a Weight of soluble organic matter fixed as semi-volatile and non-volatile compounds in the 30 mg mineral series (Table S7); b PCA biplot for the 30 mg series considering the following variables (blue vectors): weight of soluble organic matter (Weight), final mass balance of the cyanide polymerization reactions (Mass balance), number of chromatographic peaks detected by GC–MS after acid (GC–MS (AH)) and basic (GC–MS (BH)) hydrolysis, and by HPLC–FLD (HPLC–FLD); c Comparison between the weight of soluble organic matter collected as semi-volatile and non-volatile compounds (black squares) and the final mass balance (red circles) for the 30 mg (filled symbols) and 150 mg (open symbols) series (Table S7); d PCA biplot for the comparative analysis of the 30 and 150 mg series, considering the same variables as in (b), with the addition of mineral loading (Mineral concentration) as a descriptor. Colored ellipses are included solely as visual guides, have no statistical significance, and are drawn based on the trends discussed in the main text. Mass balance was calculated as follows: [((mg of NaCN + mg of NH4Cl + mg of mineral)-(mg of raw reaction))/ (mg of NaCN + mg of NH4Cl + mg of mineral)]*100. The mg of raw reaction was referred to the total freeze-dried mass collected. The mean values shown in (a, c) were calculated from n = 3 independent experiments, and the error bars represent the corresponding standard deviations.

A closely analogous grouping emerges from the PCA biplot shown in Fig. 4b when the number of chromatographic peaks is included as a descriptor (Table S7). The total number of peaks could provide a proxy for molecular diversity, with higher counts corresponding to a more diverse molecular inventory. The PCA model explains 83.7% of the total variance, with the first two principal components accounting for most of this variability (PC1 = 55.5%, PC2 = 28.2%), indicating that the dominant chemical trends are well described in a low-dimensional space. PC1 is primarily associated with the diversity of GC–MS-detectable compounds, showing strong positive loadings from both acid- and base-hydrolyzed GC–MS peak sets. PC2 captures a secondary contrast related to the balance between HPLC–FLD and GC–MS results. In the score space, most samples cluster near the origin or at moderate PC1 values, indicating broadly comparable molecular outputs across many mineral systems. The control experiment (CE) and samples containing inert substrates such as silica (MSI30), alumina (MAL30), and zirconia (MZI30) occupy central positions, confirming their limited impact on the general chemical signature. In contrast, MCA30, MTE30, MVI30, and MMA30 are displaced along PC1 and/or PC2, indicating distinct molecular fingerprints. Calcite (CA) shows one of the strongest deviations, consistent with its differentiated behavior observed across FTIR, HPLC–FLD, and GC–MS-based CATPCA analyses. Examination of the loading vectors indicates that GC–MS variables contribute most strongly to PC1, underscoring the sensitivity of mass spectrometry to mineral-driven changes in reaction pathways, whereas HPLC–FLD variables mainly contribute to finer discrimination along PC2. Bulk metrics exhibit shorter loading vectors, indicating a secondary contribution to variance. Notably, except for maghemite (MA), samples with the highest and lowest yields of soluble matter show the lowest molecular diversity, indicating no direct relationship between global soluble compounds yield and molecular organic diversity under the conditions explored.

In this way, this PCA shows that mineral-dependent chemical diversity is not an artefact of categorical encoding in CATPCA but emerges robustly from continuous multivariate analysis. The strong agreement between PCA and CATPCA supports the conclusion that a limited number of chemically meaningful factors, dominated by mineral reactivity and modulated by analytical selectivity, govern cyanide-derived molecular profiles.

Effect of mineral loading on soluble organic matter and molecular diversity

A comparison of the final weight and mass balance of selected samples from the 30 mg and 150 mg series is shown in Fig. 4c (Table S7). In general, increasing mineral loading results in lower amounts of soluble organic matter fixed as semi-volatile and non-volatile compounds, with silica (SI) showing negligible variations and calcite (CA) exhibiting the opposite trend. Moreover, the strong correlation between weight and mass balance observed for the 30 mg series (Fig. 4a, b) is no longer evident for the 150 mg series, as shown in Fig. 4c and further supported by the PCA biplot in Fig. 4d, indicating a clear effect of mineral loading on the fixation of soluble matter.

Consistent with the CATPCA results, the PCA biplot in Fig. 4d demonstrates that these trends reflect continuous, mineral-load-dependent chemical behavior rather than categorical artefacts. The first two principal components explain 75.4% of the total variance (PC1 = 39.7%, PC2 = 35.7%), enabling a robust interpretation of variable relationships and sample clustering. The loading structure reveals a pronounced inverse correlation between mineral loading and the number of chromatographic peaks, indicating that increasing mineral load reduces the molecular diversity detected by GC–MS and HPLC–FLD (Table S7). A similar inverse relationship is observed between mineral loading and the final weight of the soluble phase, linking higher mineral loads to both reduced molecular diversity and lower recoverable soluble organic matter. This effect is particularly evident for the hematite samples, with MHE150 displaying the lowest molecular diversity. In the score space, two well-defined clusters corresponding to the two mineral loading regimes are observed. Samples from the 30 mg series cluster in regions associated with higher chromatographic peak counts, consistent with enhanced molecular diversity at lower mineral load. In contrast, samples from the 150 mg series occupy the opposite region of the biplot, reflecting a systematic reduction in both molecular organic diversity and soluble matter at higher mineral loading. This separation reinforces the groupings identified by CATPCA and confirms that mineral loading significantly affects cyanide polymerization pathways. Within the 30 mg series, calcite exhibits a distinctive trend, with MCA30 displaced relative to other samples at comparable mineral load. Despite the generally higher molecular diversity of the 30 mg series, MCA30 shows a diminished chromatographic response and reduced soluble organic yield, indicating that calcite exerts a strong inhibitory effect on chemical diversification at low mineral concentration.

These results highlight how mineral identity and loading jointly modulate cyanide-derived molecular organic complexity.

Overview of the effect of minerals on cyanide polymerization reactions in alkaline aqueous aerosols: insights for prebiotic chemistry research

At low mineral loading (30 mg series), the presence of silicon-bearing phases, including amorphous silica, quartz, aluminosilicates, and forsterite (SI, VA, SA, MR, and FO substrates, Table S1), does not induce significant changes in spectroscopic or chromatographic profiles relative to the mineral-free control experiment (CE). However, increasing mineral loading (150 mg series) amplifies these differences, an effect particularly evident in the CATPCA analysis of the Martian regolith series (MR) and clearly reflected in the PCA results, where molecular diversity decreases systematically with increasing mineral load. A comparable trend is also observed for the alumina (AL) series, indicating that higher mineral loading generally constrains cyanide-derived molecular complexity. By contrast, iron-bearing mineral systems (MA, PY, HE, VI, and SD substrates, Table S1) induce marked variations in spectroscopic and chromatographic profiles even at low mineral loading. These effects are especially pronounced for MVI30 (Figs. 2 and 4a, b), where a soluble iron sulfate was used as substrate. In this case, the observed differences are consistent with modifications of cyanide polymerization pathways driven by the formation of soluble ferrocyanide complexes and by redox processes mediated by HCN-derived oligomers, such as diaminomaleonitrile (DAMN)48. Accordingly, the higher yield of soluble matter recovered for MVI30 can be directly linked to the formation of soluble ammonium and/or sodium ferrocyanides coexisting with organic products detected by chromatographic techniques. The lower solubility of the remaining iron-bearing substrates results in a less pronounced, though still significant, effect on molecular profiles, which becomes increasingly evident with higher mineral loading (likely due to a variation in the reactivity of the system related with variations in the superficial area of the system and also with the formation of insoluble organic-inorganic hybrid materials), as observed for the hematite (HE) series (Figs. 3 and 4b). These results confirm that the suppressive effect of mineral loading on cyanide-derived molecular diversity is not system-specific but reflects a more general feature of cyanide–mineral interactions.

Notably, siderite (SD) aligns with the iron-bearing mineral series rather than with calcite (CA), despite both being carbonate minerals. This behavior underscores the dominant influence of iron-mediated redox chemistry over carbonate-driven effects during cyanide polymerization. In contrast, the distinctive response observed for calcite (CA) is likely linked to its higher solubility. The release of CO₂ from carbonate during the bubble–aerosol–droplet cycle, promoted by the acidic nature of microdroplets49, would inhibit cyanide polymerization, favoring the formation of volatile products28 and ultimately resulting in lower yields and reduced molecular diversity. Despite the widespread occurrence of carbonate minerals in natural environments, their role in cyanide polymerization has not been systematically explored, with experimental evidence largely limited to the effect of bicarbonate on DAMN formation24. A similarly distinctive response is observed for tenorite (TE), which displays unique spectroscopic and chromatographic profiles. As in the case of iron-bearing minerals, this behavior can be plausibly attributed to the formation of soluble cyano-cuprate complexes, leading to higher yields of soluble matter and molecular signatures clearly differentiated from those of the control experiment (CE).

Taken together, these results highlight the largely unexplored role of redox processes during cyanide polymerization in the presence of transition metals. While iron-mediated effects have been only sparsely investigated48, copper-driven redox chemistry in this context remains essentially unexplored, beyond early reports on its catalytic role in HCN tetramerization24. Both iron and copper exhibit a strong affinity for cyanide50, readily forming complex salts that, under prebiotic conditions, could act either as cyanide sinks23,51 or as reservoirs for subsequent chemical transformations52,53. The present results therefore underscore the need for detailed characterization of the soluble phases generated during cyanide polymerization, with particular attention to the identification of complex salts and the redox mechanisms leading to organic product formation. An additional question emerging from this work concerns whether the observed decrease in molecular diversity at higher mineral loadings is accompanied by increased molecular selectivity toward compounds of potential prebiotic relevance. Although aqueous aerosols are known to enhance molecular diversity relative to conventional hydrothermal conditions21,31, the present results demonstrate that specific mineral substrates, such as calcite (CA) and especially tenorite (TE), can counteract this effect, even in aerosol-mediated systems. The extent to which reduced diversity translates into increased chemical efficiency or functional selectivity remains an open question. Importantly, mineral solubility, and therefore its chemical impact, depends on environmental parameters such as pH, temperature, and pressure, while mineral loading has been shown here to be a critical factor modulating chromatographic profiles. Together, these variables are likely to modulate both molecular diversity and selectivity in cyanide–mineral systems.

Finally, it should be emphasized that mineral effects depend not only on mineral identity but also on their amounts and environmental context. These effects become particularly evident when probed using highly selective analytical techniques, albeit at the cost of increased data dispersion. Nonetheless, the combined spectroscopic, chromatographic, and multivariate approach employed here provides a robust framework for disentangling these effects and for advancing our understanding of mineral-controlled chemical evolution in prebiotic environments.

Conclusions

As described extensively in the literature cyanide polymerization reactions result in the generation of highly complex mixtures of organic compounds (e.g., refs. 8,12). The composition of these mixtures is statistically significantly influenced by the presence of minerals during polymerization processes, both by the nature of the mineral and by its loading, as demonstrated in this work through an integrated statistical analysis that did not focus on individual organic molecules. Moreover, it is important to note that the magnitude of the effects attributed to minerals depends on both analytical selectivity and statistical resolution; that is, the mineral effect observed relative to the control experiment (polymerization reaction carried in the absence of inorganic substrates) depends on the post sample handling protocol and the selective methodology employed for a given analytical technique. Therefore, the clusters observed in the different CATPCA analyses vary depending on the analytical technique used. However, despite that we can draw general conclusions from the present study: (i) The statistical framework employed here demonstrates that while several silicate- and alumina-rich substrates, and others such as anatase and zirconia, behave as largely inert minerals, iron- and copper-bearing minerals and carbonates induce statistically significant deviations in molecular signatures and soluble organics yields, respect to the control experiment; and (ii) In general, an increase in mineral loading partially inhibits the increase in molecular diversity, highlighting that mineral concentration is a key parameter in the production of organic compounds through cyanide polymerization reactions in simulated prebiotic depositional environments.

Overall, this study highlights the power of multivariate, target-agnostic analytical methodologies to disentangle environmentally conditioned chemical complexity and provides a quantitative framework for exploring mineral-controlled chemical evolution in prebiotic environments.

Methods

Simulations of the aqueous environments

Equimolar aqueous solutions of NaCN (247 mg) and NH4Cl (267 mg), each prepared at 1 M concentration and combined to a final volume of 5 mL, were prepared using reagents from EMPLURA® (Merck KGaA) and PanReac AppliChem, respectively. These solutions underwent reaction within an active bubble-aerosol-droplet cycle (following the procedure described in refs. 21,31). The experiments were conducted under an inert atmosphere of nitrogen in the presence of eighteen distinct minerals; each tested at 30 mg and some of them at 150 mg as well (Table 2). After allowing the reactions to proceed for four days, the mixtures were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min. Then, the supernatants were freeze-dried and subjected to various spectroscopic and chromatographic techniques. All reactions were conducted at least in triplicate. All the minerals used in this work are listed in Table S1, which shows their provenance and structural composition (by XRD). A more detailed discussion about the simulated prebiotic conditions checked is indicated in the Text S4.

FTIR spectroscopy

Diffuse reflectance spectra, for both sol fractions and inorganic substrates, were acquired in the spectral 4000-450 cm−1 region using a FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet Thermo Fisher®, model Nexus 670, MA, USA, software OMNIC). Using a DRIFT reflectance accessory (Harrik, model Praying Mantis DRP, New York, USA) mounted inside the instrument compartment. The spectral resolution was 2 cm−1. The FTIR spectra of the sol fractions were directly measurement after freeze-drying, whereas for the inorganic substrates, their FTIR spectra were recorded using CsI pellet. KBr or CsI are typically used to prepare solid samples as pellets due to their infrared transparency. However, when halite (NaCl) is present, it can act as a suitable matrix, allowing direct measurement without the addition of other IR-transparent salts. Therefore, no CsI pellets were required for these samples, as they already contained NaCl.

The FTIR spectra in the selected region were normalized and smoothed using 20 points before the statistical analysis using Origin Pro 2024.

HPLC-FLD analysis

The analysis was carried out using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), applying the AccQ•Tag derivatization method from Waters manufacture43, adapted to the available chromatographic system and column in our laboratory. No hydrolysis step was applied, and no specific identification of compounds was performed. An Agilent 1260 HPLC system equipped with a fluorescence detector was used. Separation was achieved using an InfinityLab Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (3.0 mm × 150 mm, 4 μm particle size). The excitation and emission wavelengths were set at 248 nm and 395 nm, respectively. The column was maintained at 38 °C, with a flow rate of 0.592 mL/min and an injection volume of 5 μL. Under these chromatographic conditions, and following the derivatization procedure described below, reproducible chromatographic profiles were obtained. Liquid samples (sol fraction) were directly derivatized using the Waters AccQ-Fluor™ reagent kit. Briefly, 20 μL of each liquid sample was transferred to a clean HPLC vial, followed by the addition of 60 μL of AccQ-Fluor borate buffer (Vial 1 of the kit) and vortex mixing. Then, 20 μL of reconstituted AccQ-Fluor reagent in acetonitrile was added, vortexed again, and the mixture was allowed to incubate at room temperature for 1 minute. Finally, the reaction mixture was heated at 55 °C for 10 min. This procedure enabled the generation of fluorescent derivative profiles that were used for comparative analysis across samples.

GC-MS analysis

Hydrolysis

For all the cases of the two experimental series (Table 2), 15 mg of sample was hydrolyzed. These hydrolysis reactions were carried out in sealed ampules. After the reaction time, the hydrolyzates were freeze-dried until a constant weight was achieved. Acid hydrolysis: 500 μL of 6 M HCl (Panreac) were added to each sample and the mixtures were heated at 110 °C for 24 h. Basic hydrolysis: Each sample was treated with 500 μL of 5 N NH₄OH (Panreac) and subjected to thermal hydrolysis at 110 °C for 24 h.

Derivatization of the samples: trimethylsilyl derivatives

The hydrolyzed and freeze-dried samples were derivatized by adding 100 μL of N,O-Bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) containing 1% trimethylchlorosilane (TMCS) (Thermo Scientific). The mixture was heated at 80 °C for 3 h to form the corresponding trimethylsilyl derivatives prior to GC-MS analysis.

GC-MS methods

Once both types of hydrolysis were completed, the derivatized samples were subjected to GC-MS analysis as follows: Analyses were conducted using an Agilent 6850 gas chromatograph coupled to a 5975 VL MSD triple-axis mass selective detector operating in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV. The system was equipped with an HP-5 MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm) and helium as the carrier gas. Method 1 (for acid hydrolysis): Initial temperature of 60 °C held for 1.5 min; ramp to 130 °C at 5 °C/min with an 11-min hold; ramp to 180 °C at 10 °C/min with a 10-min hold; then ramp to 220 °C at 20 °C/min with a 15-min hold. One microliter of sample was injected in splitless mode at an injector temperature of 275 °C. The detector temperature was 300 °C, and the carrier gas flow rate was 1.1 mL/min. The monitored mass range was 50–650 amu. Method 2 (for basic hydrolysis): Initial temperature of 80 °C held for 1.5 min; ramp to 230 °C at 5 °C/min with a 5-min hold; then ramp to 300 °C at 25 °C/min with a final hold of 10 min. Two microliters were injected in splitless mode. Injector and detector temperatures were 300 °C, with a carrier gas flow rate of 0.9 mL/min.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed in collaboration with the Applied Statistics Unit Service of the Spanish National Research Council (CSIC). Rigorous procedures for sample preparation, data acquisition, and data handling were applied to minimize experimental uncertainty and to ensure the robustness and reproducibility of the results. The internal consistency of the measurement instruments was evaluated by calculating Cronbach’s alpha, including its standardized form, using IBM SPSS Statistics (v30). Paired-sample correlation analyses were conducted, and the statistical significance of the correlation coefficients was assessed by calculating p-values derived from Student’s t distribution using the same software. Categorical Principal Components Analysis (CATPCA) was also performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (v30). Spectral and chromatographic data processing and normalization (from 0 to 1) were carried out using Origin (OriginPro 2024), which was additionally employed for Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Optimal scaling was applied to the continuous variables observed in the FTIR dataset prior to CATPCA analysis. Numerical variables were discretized into seven categories by grouping their values into intervals, resulting in an approximately normal distribution. These categories were treated as ordered and equally spaced (interval level), such that both the rank order and the relative distances between category values were preserved in the optimally scaled variables. Under these conditions, the category quantifications lie along a straight line (vector) through the origin. When all variables are treated at the numerical level, CATPCA is mathematically analogous to standard principal component analysis. However, CATPCA was preferred in this study due to deviations from normality in the original variables, as optimal scaling provides a more flexible framework for handling non-normally distributed data while preserving the underlying structure of the dataset.
