A cross-species proteomic assessment of cost-effective platforms for depleting high-abundant proteins from blood serum
Authors: Zongkai Peng, Shakya Wije Munige, Deepti Bhusal, Isabella L. Yang, Zhibo Yang, Nagib Ahsan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood proteome analysis is becoming increasingly popular in veterinary research because many animal models have been used to study a range of human diseases. Most of the commercial high-abundance protein (HAP) depletion kits are optimized for human serum, whereas proteins in animal serum may not be present in human serum or may be present at different abundances. There are no previous studies investigating the efficacy of using various HAP kits for proteome analysis of animal serums.MethodWe used three commercial serum abundant protein depletion (SAPD) kits (i.e., ion exchange-based Norgen kit (ProteoSpin™), antibody-based Thermo Albumin Depletion Kit (Pierce™), solubility-based kit (Minute™), and a cost-effective method (i.e., perchloric acid (PerCA) HAP precipitation) to assess their effectiveness to process serums from five different species (i.e., mouse, chicken, dog, goat, and guinea pig). Protocols of the commercial kits were adopted from manufacturers’ guidelines with minor modifications for optimized performance. Following HAP depletion, proteins from all species were digested using a Trypsin/Lys-C enzyme mix, desalted, and subjected to label-free quantitative bottom-up proteomics analysis via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The raw data were processed using the Andromeda search engine integrated into MaxQuant, and peptide identification was performed by searching against the UniProt-reviewed protein database. Advanced bioinformatics tools were employed to facilitate data analysis and visualization, ensuring comprehensive interpretation of the depletion efficiency and comparative performance of the methods across species.ResultWe determined their capabilities of protein identification (Norgen kit > Minute kit > PerCA precipitation > Thermo kit), depletion efficiencies of HAPs (Minute kit > Norgen kit > PerCA precipitation > Thermo kit), and cost-effectiveness (PerCA precipitation > Minute kit > Norgen kit > Thermo kit). Our results show that the PerCA precipitation method, which is > 20 times cheaper than commercial kits, outperforms other methods in depleting HAPs, especially in mouse serum. While Norgen kit excels in mouse and goat serum, the PerCA precipitation method offers broader applicability and reveals unique low abundant proteins. Protein pathway analysis highlights distinct biological processes affected by different depletion methods.DiscussionOverall, our studies provide valuable insights into protein depletion techniques, with the PerCA depletion method emerging as a cost-effective and versatile option for proteomics research across various serums.

Introduction

Proteomics has emerged as a powerful tool in translational medicine, offering promising avenues for discovering biomarkers and diagnosing diseases. Proteomics analysis of serum further enhances its utility to investigate the integrate protein compositions [1]. Understanding the serum proteome is challenging due to the broad range of protein concentrations and post-translational modifications [2]. In particular, accurately identifying low-abundance biomarkers in serum is difficult because of the presence of substantial levels of high-abundance proteins (HAPs) such as albumin (ALB) and immunoglobulin G (IgG). HAPs tend to dominate the serum proteome, masking the signals from less abundant proteins that could hold valuable clues for disease diagnosis [3]. Removing HAPs is crucial for effective measurement and identification of low-abundance proteins in biological samples, including serum, plasma, and biofluids. Commercial serum abundant protein depletion (SAPD) kits, which can cost between $11 to $136 per sample [4], often hinder research with large sample sizes. While an acid-based protein precipitation method offers an alternative, there is a lack of comparative studies between this method and commercial kits. Therefore, it is crucial to develop more sensitive proteomic techniques to identify these low-abundance proteins of interest. Among all methods, HAP depletion is an effective strategy to improve identification efficiency by simplifying sample complexity, allowing researchers to focus on specific proteins or pathways of interest.

Animal proteomics is becoming increasingly prominent, attracting attention not only within veterinary domains [5–7] but also in the utilization of animal models for researching human diseases [8]. However, serum protein profiles vary among different species, adding a layer of challenge for comprehensively studying animal serums. Similar to human serum, HAPs in animal serum can mask the presence of low-abundance proteins, making it difficult to sensitively detect and accurately quantify them [9]. Depleting HAPs provides multiple benefits for animal serum proteomics studies. First, depleting HAPs enhances the detection sensitivity of these less abundant proteins, which may be crucial biomarkers or indicators of disease states in animal models, allowing for the investigation of species-specific proteins that could be important for understanding physiological processes or diseases unique to those animals [10]. Second, depleting HAPs in animal serum allows for more accurate comparisons among different animal models or experimental conditions. By normalizing the protein composition of serum samples, researchers can better discern differences or similarities in protein expression patterns across different species or experimental groups [10]. Third, cross-species depletion conserves resources by utilizing a single depletion method for different animal species, facilitating comparative proteomic analysis to identify conserved or species-specific protein signatures associated with physiological or pathological conditions.

Commercial HAP depletion kits offer researchers convenient and efficient solutions for enhancing the sensitivity and depth of proteomic analyses. Most of these kits generally use affinity-based mechanisms to specifically remove a protein, such as ALB or IgGs, from complex biological samples, including serum and plasma [11]. By employing specific ligands or antibodies immobilized on solid supports, these kits effectively capture and remove targeted proteins, thereby reducing sample complexity and uncovering low-abundance proteins of interest. Methods used in earlier studies, such as dye ligand affinity chromatography (Dye-LAC) and protein ligand-based approaches, specifically target and remove ALB and IgG in plasma [5, 12–15]. However, with the advent of more sophisticated depletion methods, these approaches have become less favored. In the realm of plasma proteomics, many researchers rely on commercial immunoaffinity-based kits. Immunoaffinity depletion stands out as the preferred approach to eliminating HAPs in plasma proteomics, primarily owing to its remarkable specificity and reproducibility [16, 17]. This strategy entails capturing specific proteins by binding them to antibodies affixed to resin beads, thereby helping to purify the sample. Some of the popular commercial products for immunodepletion include Agilent’s Multiple Affinity Removal System (MARS) columns, Millipore’s Seppro IgY kits, Thermo Fisher Scientific’s Pierce depletion columns, and Sigma’s ProteoPrep kits [11]. Additionally, these commercial kits often offer compatibility with downstream proteomic workflows, allowing for seamless integration into mass spectrometry (MS)-based analyses for comprehensive protein identification and characterization. However, animal serum contains proteins that may not be present in human serum or may be present at different abundances. Commercial HAP depletion kits are generally designed to analyze human serum, and therefore they may not be ideal for animal serum. To the best of our knowledge, however, there are no reported studies evaluating the effectiveness of using these commercial HAP depletion kits to process animal serums. The current study examined five animal groups: Swiss Webster mice, Leghorn chickens, unspecified dogs, unspecified goats, and unspecified guinea pigs. The total serum protein concentrations and major protein compositions in relevant animal groups have been previously reported and measured in the current studies (Table 1). It is evident that the total serum protein concentrations significantly vary among different animal species as well as among different strains within the same animal species such as mice. However, the abundant proteins consist of similar protein groups (e.g., albumin and globulin) among different animal species. These reported studies likely indicate that it is challenging to use a single method to deplete abundant proteins from different animal serums.

There is an ongoing exploration of new HAP depletion methods, such as using precipitation and novel nanomaterials, to improve the efficiency of protein depletion [11, 28, 29]. Precipitation-based techniques provide a commonly employed substitute for immunodepletion in eliminating high-abundance proteins from biological samples. These methods involve the addition of a variety of different substances, including salt (e.g., ammonium sulfate (AS) [30]), acid (e.g., trichloroacetic acid (TCA), hydrochloric acid (HCl), and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)) [28, 29], organic solvent (e.g., acetonitrile, acetone, and methanol) [29, 31], organic solvent containing acid (e.g., acetone/TCA [32] and isopropanol/TCA [33]), and others (e.g., dithiothreitol (DTT) [34] and polymer polyethylene glycol (PEG) [35]), to induce protein aggregation and subsequent precipitation. Notably, these methods efficiently remove a significant portion of HAPs while preserving the low-abundance proteins, making them suitable for proteomic applications where cost efficiency and preservation of target proteins are crucial considerations.

In a prior study, we evaluated the efficacy of several commercial kits to extract HAPs from human serum samples [4]. As far as we are aware, no comparative analysis of these commercial kits has been conducted to determine their efficacy in removing HAPs from blood samples from animal models. To address this gap, we propose a comprehensive evaluation of various cost-effective extraction kits on serum samples derived from different animal models. In order to ascertain the most cost-effective method for removing HAPs from animal model serum samples, we employed the Norgen kit, which was found to be a low-cost commercial kit with better performance from our earlier work [4], in conjunction with two other commercial kits, namely Minute and Thermo kits. In addition, we also used an acid-based precipitation approach to remove HAPs from serum samples from model animals. The total amount of proteins in each serum sample (30 µL) is within the depletion capacity of each commercial kit (10–50 µL for Norgen, 5–50 µL for Thermo, and < 100 µL for Minute), considering that the total protein concentrations of animal serums (31 − 99.2 mg/mL Table 1) are largely comparable to those of (60–83 mg/mL) human serum [36].

These commercial HAP depletion methods, which are designed for human serum, have different working mechanisms. The Minute kit dissolves HAPs and then precipitates the low-abundance proteins. The Norgen kit (ProteoSpin) uses ion exchange mechanisms to separate proteins based on their charges and then remove HAPs. The Thermo kit (Pierce Albumin Depletion Kit) is an antibody-based method designed for the depletion of human HAPs thus improving the protein identification using LC-MS/MS analysis [33]. Immunoaffinity depletion is selected for eliminating HAPs in plasma proteomics because of its high specificity and reproducibility [16, 37]. On the other hand, acid-based method, such as modified perchloric acid (PerCA) precipitation method, is a cost-effective and efficient approach to depleting HAPs in human plasma [38]. PerCA can denature and precipitate major serum proteins, except for glycoproteins and alkaline proteins. Proteins have a specific isoelectric point (pI), where they carry no net charge. When the pH drops below the pI, proteins become positively charged. This disrupts the hydration sphere surrounding proteins, leading to structural destabilization and aggregation of high-abundance proteins [39, 40], leaving most lower-abundance proteins in the supernatant [38, 41, 42]. Thus, the aim of this study is to evaluate the above methods to address the critical challenges of enhancing the depth and sensitivity of serum proteomic analyses of animal model systems.

Methods

Serum sample acquirement

The current studies are focused on five distinct groups of animals commonly utilized in various research contexts. Serum samples of Swiss Webster mice (lot# S-245407), leghorn chickens (lot# S-245408), unspecified dog (lot# S-245406), unspecified goat (lot# S-245405), and unspecified guinea pig (lot# S-245404) were purchased from BioChemed Services (VA, USA). (Clinical trial number: not applicable).

HAP depletion methods and serum protein extraction

For serum protein extraction, three commercially available HAP depletion kits were utilized: the ProteoSpin™ Abundant Serum Protein Depletion Kit (Cat# 17300, Norgen Biotek, ON, Canada) (referred to as Norgen kit), the Pierce™ Albumin Depletion Kit (Cat# 85160, Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA) (referred to as Thermo kit), and the Minute™ Albumin Depletion Reagent for Plasma and Serum (Cat# WA-013, Invent Biotechnologies, MN, USA) (referred to as Minute kit). All depletion procedures were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Additionally, the PerCA precipitation method was tested to evaluate its depletion efficiency in comparison to the commercial kits. In fact, the PerCA precipitation method has been proven as an efficient way of depleting HAPs in human serum or plasma [28, 29]. Here, we adopt a similar approach with minor modifications to simplify the sample preparation steps, allowing for direct digestion of the supernatant proteins without accumulation. Briefly, 30 µL of serum samples were treated with 30 µL of 7% (v/v) PerCA (Fisher Chemicals, lot# 112008, Fair Lawn, NJ, USA) to achieve a final PerCA concentration of 3.5% (v/v). The mixture was kept on ice for 15 min to allow the HAPs to settle. The suspension was then centrifuged (5402 Refrigerated Centrifuge, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 14,000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. Both the supernatant and pellet were separated and treated with 150 µL of 8 M urea (Amresco, lot# 1265C360, VWR, Solon, OH, USA) prepared in optima water (lot# 232303, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To adjust the pH to 8, we added 50 mM and 200 mM Tris buffer to the pellet extract and supernatant, respectively. Protein quantification was performed using a NanoDrop Microvolume Spectrophotometer (NanoDrop One, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 2 µL of supernatant from each sample was measured three times at 280 nm. The results are summarized in Table 1.

Protein digestion and desalting

Protein digestion was performed once protein quantification was accomplished. Based on total protein concentration (Table 1), a total of 100 µg of serum proteins from each sample (3 replicates/species) were utilized for subsequent experimental procedures. 100 mM Tris buffer, 50 mM Tris buffer, and 8 M urea were freshly prepared. The dilution was performed to maintain the concentration of urea at 6 M and pH 8. Proteolytic digestion of the samples was carried out in solution using Trypsin/Lys-C mix (Cat# V5071, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) with protein/trypsin of 50:1 (w: w). Following digestion, the peptides were desalted using C18 Sep-Pak Plus cartridges (Cat# WAT023590, Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The peptides, once dried, were resuspended in 100 µL of water (0.1% formic acid, v/v). An equal amount of (2 µg) peptides per sample was injected for LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

The LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted using a Dionex UltiMate® 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). LC-MS/MS was conducted according to our previously published protocols [4, 43]. RAW files were searched against the UniProt reviewed Swiss Webster mice (Uniprot ID: UP000000589, Taxon ID: 10090, accessed 07/09/2023), leghorn chickens (Uniprot ID: UP000000539, Taxon ID: 9031, accessed 07/08/2023), dog (Uniprot ID: UP000805418, Taxon ID: 9615, accessed 08/31/2023), goat (Uniprot ID: UP000291000, Taxon ID: 9925, accessed 07/08/2023), and guinea pig (Uniprot ID: UP000005447, Taxon ID: 10141, accessed 09/02/2023) protein database using the Andromeda Search engine in MaxQuant_2.0.3.1 (Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Martinsried, Germany) [44]. The database search was performed with following settings: Trypsin enzyme cleavage specificity, two possible missed cleavages, 10 ppm mass tolerance for precursor ions, and 0.02 Da mass tolerance for fragment ions. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification, while N-terminal acetylation of proteins and methionine oxidation were designated as variable modifications. For protein levels, the database search was filtered down to a 1% false discovery rate (FDR). Under the match between run feature, the parameters were configured as: 0.4 min for the match time window and 20 min for the alignment time window. Maxquant LFQ [45, 46] (label-free quantification) algorithm was used for normalization, which was achieved by calculating a set of normalization factors for each sample based on the total intensity of all identified peptides. These factors were then applied to adjust the intensities of individual peptides, ensuring accurate quantification. For statistical significance, a 1.5-fold change increased or decreased in abundance with a p-value < 0.05 was considered.

Bioinformatics

All pathway analyses were performed using ShinyGO 0.80 bioinformatics platform [47]. Heatmaps, volcano plots, and Upset plot were generated by SRplot (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en), a free online platform for data analysis and visualization. Venn diagram was generated using Molbiotools (https://molbiotools.com/listcompare.php). Experiments workflow graph was generated using Biorender (https://www.biorender.com/). All the bar plots were generated with GraphPad Prism version 7.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA (https://www.graphpad.com). The remaining parts were generated by Microsoft Office PowerPoints and Excel 365.

Results

Evaluation of HAP depletion methods to cross-species serum proteome

First, we evaluated the cost-effectiveness of these methods. In this study, three commercially available kits and one in-house lab-based method (PerCA) were used to evaluate proteome profiles of five different animal species (mice, chicken, dog, goat, and guinea pig) (Fig. 1). Figure 2 illustrates the estimated price per sample of four different HAP depletion methods: two solvent-based methods ($0.25 for PerCA precipitation and $1.50 for the Minute kit for) are more cost-effective than two commercial ones ($11.48 for the Norgen kit and $15.71 for the Thermo kit).Fig. 2Cost per sample of each HAP depletion method

Cost per sample of each HAP depletion method

Next, we assessed the number of proteins and peptides identified and quantified in different species before and after depletion. Compared with the undepleted group, if higher numbers of proteins were identified in the corresponding depleted group, this depletion method was regarded as effective. Overall, some HAP depletion methods were effective to improve the number of identified proteins in mouse, goat, and dog serums (Fig. 3). Particularly, the Norgen kit overall showed the best performance of improving protein detection from all species. This is because the Norgen kit is based on the ion exchange mechanism and is not specific to any species, although it was initially designed for human serum. Because a depletion method may have different influence on specific samples, the number of identified proteins and peptides varies significantly across different species and methods. Peptides detected from the undepleted groups were primarily from those abundant proteins. Although depleting HAPs might lead to smaller numbers of total peptides, this process allowed for higher chances to detect unique peptides from less abundant proteins. For example, compared with undepleted mouse serum, samples processed by Minute kit resulted in more identified proteins but fewer total number of peptides.

Last, we compared the changes in two abundant proteins (ALB and IgG) before and after depletion. Unlike those previously reported results (Table 1), although we are unable to obtain the absolute concentrations of proteins in the current studies, both ALB and IgG, which is the most common class of globulin, are among the abundant proteins. Our measurements of total protein concentrations in these animal serums are in a reasonably good agreement with published results: ALB is the most abundant protein in all species, whereas globulins are also abundant in many of them (Table 1). Our results indicate that the Minute kit provided the highest depletion efficiency of ALB, with the Norgen kit being the second most efficient for all five serums; however, both Thermo kit and PerCA precipitation were generally less effective (Fig. 4A). In addition, excellent reproducibility of ALB depletion efficiency (i.e., small error bars) has been observed in nearly all experiments. Although the reproducibility was less than ideal for the mouse serum processed by the Themo kit, likely due to the inefficiency of the Thermo kit and high concentration of ALB (Table 1), depletion using this kit had no obvious influence on the reproducibility of proteins’ profiles (Fig. 5A). The inefficiency of the Thermo kit can be attributed to its antibody-based mechanisms designed for human ALB removal, but it is not efficient for depleting animal ALB due to their differences among different species. We also investigated the abundance of IgG in mouse serum before and after using these depletion methods (Fig. 4B). Our results showed that all depletion methods had minor or even inverted effects on decreasing IgG in all serums. Compared with its abundance (33.3%) in undepleted mouse serum, the PerCA precipitation slightly reduced its percentage to 27.3%; however, the other three methods increased its abundance: Norgen kit (37.9%), Minute kit (40.7%), and Thermo kit (38.5%). These results can be attributed to differences in these methods: the three commercial kits are not designed for IgG removal, whereas PerCA has no specific target protein (i.e., IgG can be precipitated along with other HAPs). Label-free comparative quantitative proteomics

Principal component analysis (PCA) shows excellent reproducibility of our proteomics results, with the analytical replicates being highly grouped (Fig. 5A). In the PCA plots for all five species, we noticed a similar trend between different HAP depletion methods. The Minute kit, Norgen kit, and PerCA precipitation showed significantly different proteomes compared to the undepleted groups, whereas the Thermo kit had no obvious change in proteome profiles (Fig. 5A). These trends are also reflected in the heatmaps: depletion using the Minute kit, Norgen kit, and PerCA precipitation method resulted in improved detection of different proteins in the same serum as well as across all species (Fig. 5B). These differences can be attributed to different depletion mechanisms of these methods: the Minute kit dissolves the abundant proteins, the Norgen kit uses ion exchange-based cartridges to remove abundant proteins, and PerCA precipitates abundant proteins. As for the Thermo kit, it does not show much difference from the undepleted group, indicating that the kit might not be suitable for serums from these five species (Fig. 5B).

Cross-species comparison between proteins in animal serum and Human Protein Atlas

Biofluids (e.g., serum or plasma) are important and commonly used in clinical and therapy-related studies. However, it is challenging to comprehensively conduct large-scale experiments with human body fluids. As a result, many researchers start with animal serums, but there are no reported studies comparing results across different species. In the current work, we compared proteome in all five depleted species with the Human Protein Atlas database. The top 26 abundant proteins from the Human Protein Atlas were chosen, and their regular concentrations were shown (Fig. 6A). ALB was removed from the comparison to better illustrate trends of other remaining proteins. We performed the cross-species comparison for all depleted serums from five species and the top 26 serum human proteins using an UpsetR plot (Fig. 6B). We found four common proteins (i.e., AHSG, APOB, RBP4, and CP) in all species matched the top 26 atlas data (Fig. 6B and C). Although none of our depletion methods could successfully deplete AHSG from all species, the PerCA precipitation method showed excellent depletion efficiencies for the remaining three proteins (APOB, RBP4, and CP) (Fig. 6C). Our results indicate that the PerCA precipitation can be potentially suitable for researchers to use these animal species for human serum studies such as protein biomarker discovery.

Discovery of potential pathways and biomarkers after HAP depletion

One of the key points of this work is to determine whether commercially available human HAP kits can be also used for different animal species. Additionally, it is important to know which depletion method is more suitable for the discovery of specific pathways and relevant proteins. We compared the identified proteins in depleted serums from five species, and we found certain unique proteins were associated with specific depletion methods (Fig. 7A). Among all depletion methods, the Norgen kit provided the highest numbers of both total proteins and unique proteins in all species: mouse (39 proteins), chicken (35 proteins), goat (22 proteins), and guinea Pig (18 proteins) (Fig. 7A). We then performed human reactome pathways analysis to determine which pathway-related proteins were enriched by Norgen kit depletions in mouse, goat, and guinea pig serums (Fig. 7B). Surprisingly, we discovered common enriched pathways between mouse and goat (10 pathways) as well as between mouse and guinea pig (8 pathways) (Fig. 7B and C).

Discussion

This study aims to examine the influence of different types of HAP depletion methods on serum proteome of animal models. We tested three commercially available kits (i.e., Norgen, Thermo and Minute kits) and the PerCA precipitation method for comparative studies. Our results indicate that the PerCA precipitation method was very efficient for mouse serum and had a moderate depletion effect on other species. The Thermo kit had no obvious effects on mouse and chicken serums but exhibited moderate efficiency for the other three serums. The unsatisfactory performance of the Thermo kit is likely due to the fact that this antibody-based depletion kit is designed for ALB depletion from human serum, whereas ALB in other species has different sequences, resulting in lower binding efficiencies. The Norgen kit performed well in cross-species serum depletion. This kit is based on ion-exchange mechanisms, and a smaller proportion of HAPs bind to the cartridge, whereas most unbound HAPs can be easily washed out while low-abundant proteins are retained, making it suitable for most species that do not rely on specific sequences.

Another purpose of this work is to understand if these depletion methods can improve the detection of low-abundant proteins and help identify potential biomarkers in discovery proteomics studies. Previously, human serum has been analyzed after HAP depletion, revealing that many low-abundance proteins, such as immunoglobulin variants and histone proteins (H2BC21), can only be identified post-depletion using the Norgen kit [4]. In this study, proteins detected from these five depleted species were compared with the top 26 proteins in the Human Protein Atlas database (Fig. 6). Four common proteins (i.e., AHSG (Alpha 2-HS Glycoprotein), APOB (Apolipoprotein B), RBP4 (Retinol Binding Protein 4), and CP (Ceruloplasmin)) in this database were found across all five species after depletion. Different depletion methods exhibited varying efficiencies in enriching low-abundance proteins. Overall, the Norgen kit had the best performance in detecting these four common proteins (Fig. 6C). The detection of AHSG was improved by most methods, whereas APOB, RBP4, and CP showed different preferences depending on the animal species.

Different HAP depletion methods resulted in different protein profiles. In five species, PerCA precipitation, Norgen kit, and Minute kit resulted in different profiles of retained low-abundance proteins, whereas the Thermo kit has limited influence on the protein profiles (Fig. 5). Depletion of HAPs can benefit proteomics analysis by increasing the number of identified proteins, including unique proteins, allowing for improved protein pathway analysis. The Venn plots indicate that depletion by the Norgen kit led to the identification of the most unique proteins, followed by the PerCA precipitation, across all species (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, the Norgen kit resulted in the largest number of proteins that are sufficient for pathway analysis. Multiple immune system-related enriched pathways were discovered in the mouse serum depleted by the Norgen kit; this is expected because this kit enriches IgG proteins. In addition, hemostasis pathways and post-translational protein phosphorylation pathways are unique to both the mouse and goat species depleted by the Norgen kit, indicating that the Norgen kit is suitable for studies of hemostasis pathways and phosphorylation-related proteins in mouse and goat serums. Our results imply that specific HAP depletion methods can be used to process serum based on the research focus. Except for the Thermo kit, the rest methods (i.e., the Norgen kit, Minute kit, and PerCA precipitation) are suitable for cross-species animal serum depletion. Based on cost and research focus, the specific depletion method can be chosen. However, to obtain a complete view of the influence of HAP depletion methods on serum proteomics, more comprehensive studies are still needed to investigate the performance of more depletion techniques and serums of other species.

Conclusions

In our previous research, we discovered that an ion-exchange commercial kit was cost-effective and yielded a higher number of proteins/peptides in human serum samples [4]. In fact, there is an increasing need in studies to extend human serum to other species’ blood serums, which are valuable for human disease studies such as diagnosis, prevention, and treatment. Most commercially available serum depletion kits are designed for the analysis of human serum, necessitating alternative methods for researchers focusing on other types of blood serum. Here, we performed comparative studies using three commercial SAPD kits and the PerCA precipitation method to deplete HAPs in serum samples from five different species. We then carried out label-free quantitative bottom-up MS proteomics analysis to determine their capabilities for protein identification and cost-effectiveness. With high reproducibility of protein identification and quantification for each method, we have the following conclusions. The commercially available antibody-based kits (Thermo), while effective on human proteins, showed the least efficiency for depleting HAPs in mouse serum, possibly due to differences in ALB protein sequences across species. Heat maps revealed that the PerCA precipitation method accumulated unique low-abundant proteins compared to other kits. Solubility-based kits (Minute) also successfully depleted HAPs in mouse serum but identified fewer proteins compared to the ion-exchange-based kit (Norgen). Both the ion-exchange-based kit and the PerCA precipitation method are suitable for mouse serum depletion. For other species’ serums, the ion-exchange-based method excelled at goat serum depletion, tripling the identified peptides compared to non-depleted serum. In this study, we measured the overall protein and peptide numbers, and we evaluated the unique proteins from samples processed by each HAP depletion method. Because there is no gold standard for detecting proteins in animal serums, we do not have a positive control for all depletion methods. Establishing the standard with a target number of proteins to be detected from animal serums would allow researchers to better evaluate the performance of their HAP depletion methods, but these studies are beyond the scope of current work. In conclusion, our modified PerCA HAP depletion method is cost-effective, requires less sample preparation, and shows great potential for label-free proteomics studies of various serums. This study offers valuable insights into the comparative performance of four different protein depletion techniques and their potential applications in proteomics research.
