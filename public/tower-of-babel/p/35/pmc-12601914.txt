Induction of apoptosis and growth inhibition by silodosin and abiraterone acetate in PC-3 human prostate cancer cells
Authors: Lukman Hakim, Zakaria Aulia Rahman, Furqan Hidayatullah, Anthony Chi-Fai Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeProstate cancer that progresses despite androgen deprivation therapy, known as metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) is notoriously difficult to treat. While abiraterone acetate (AA) is a standard therapy, repurposing drugs like silodosin, a selective α1A-adrenoceptor antagonist, offers promising potential.Materials and MethodsPC-3 human prostate cancer cells were treated with silodosin, AA, or their combination. Cell viability was assessed via CCK-8 assay, apoptosis by Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry, and drug interaction using CompuSyn to determine combination index (CI) and dose reduction index (DRI).ResultsBoth silodosin and AA reduced cell viability concentration-dependently (half-maximal inhibitory concentration [IC50]: 44.16 mM and 66.90 µM, respectively). Combination therapy yielded the lowest viability (18.03%±5.73%), significantly outperforming AA (41.79%±13.11%) and silodosin (45.35%±11.51%) alone. Silodosin induced greater apoptosis (80.94%±15.88%) than AA (22.93%±7.41%), while the combination further enhanced apoptosis (87.41%±12.25%). At ¼ IC50 doses, the combination retained efficacy using markedly reduced concentrations—16.72 µM AA (8-fold reduction) and 11.04 mM silodosin (5-fold reduction). CI values across fractional effects remained <1, confirming synergism, with favorable DRI values indicating enhanced efficacy at lower doses.ConclusionsSilodosin exhibits potent effects in reducing cell viability and promoting apoptosis in androgen-insensitive prostate cancer cells, comparable to AA. When combined, both agents demonstrate synergistic effects with reduced dosing requirements. These findings support further investigation of silodosin as a potential therapeutic in mCRPC.

INTRODUCTION

Prostate cancer remains a major global health challenge with significant worldwide incidence and mortality rates [1]. The shift from hormone-sensitive to castration-resistant disease correlates with the continued activation of the androgen receptor (AR) signaling pathway despite systemic androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) [2]. In metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), ongoing androgen production by extragonadal sources such as the adrenal glands and the tumor microenvironment further complicates effective treatment, making ADT agents are invaluable in clinical practice [3].

Abiraterone acetate (AA) has demonstrated meaningful efficacy in mCRPC, including higher prostate-specific antigen response rates and longer progression-free outcomes in the pre-chemotherapy setting [4]. In parallel, another trials demonstrating improved overall survival in patients with disease progression following docetaxel-based chemotherapy [56]. AA have been shown to improve metastasis free and overall survival outcomes in real-world non-mCRPC cases, regardless of tumor aggressiveness [7]. AA is a key for mCRPC therapy that inhibits CYP17A1 and androgen biosynthesis, also affects PC-3 cell survival via non-androgenic pathways, possibly involving glucocorticoid receptor signaling or stress responses [89]. Research indicates that AA significantly reduces viability and promotes apoptotic activity in PC-3 cells, supporting its relevance even beyond its conventional AR-dependent framework [10].

Silodosin, initially indicated for managing lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) secondary to benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), exerts highly selective α1-adrenergic blockade [11]. Compared to earlier α1-blockers like tamsulosin, silodosin provides similar relief of voiding and storage symptoms with superior efficacy against nocturia [12]. It also exhibits pro-apoptotic and anti-proliferative effects in bladder and prostate cancers, likely via inhibition of androgen-stimulated viability, downregulation of ELK1 and c-fos, and reduced ELK1 activity [1314].

PC-3 was derived from a bone metastasis in mCRPC and serves as a validated model of resistance to androgen-deprivation therapy [15]. Despite advances in systemic therapy, resistance and progression remain major challenges. Repurposing drugs used for benign urologic disease is one approach. Silodosin is a standard treatment for LUTS/BPH and has proposed antineoplastic activity [1314]. In men undergoing low-dose-rate brachytherapy, a randomized trial showed better disease-specific quality of life with silodosin than with naftopidil, particularly for bowel function and sexual bother [16]. A prospective study also found silodosin more effective than naftopidil and tamsulosin for brachytherapy-related LUTS, with improvements in International Prostate Symptom Score and postvoid residual volume [17]. These findings suggest a role for silodosin in prostate cancer that extends beyond symptom control.

The cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects of silodosin in PC-3 cells remain underexplored. Considering the frequent coexistence of LUTS and prostate cancer, silodosin may offer dual benefits—relieving urinary symptoms while exerting antitumor activity. This pharmacological duality provides compelling rationale for a comprehensive preclinical investigation to elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of these effects and evaluate their translational significance in clinical oncology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study design

This in vitro experimental study utilized PC3 cells, a human mCRPC cell line, divided into four groups: control, AA, silodosin, and a combination of silodosin and AA. For each applied dose, the experiments were repeated at least six times to ensure reproducibility and reliability of the data. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) determination of silodosin and AA in this study was based on values and methodologies previously described in earlier studies [18]. In the viability assay, concentration-dependent effects were assessed using four concentrations of each drug (¼ IC50, ½ IC50, 1×IC50, and 2×IC50). For the combination group, the IC50 concentrations of both drugs were applied to evaluate synergistic effects. In the apoptosis assay, treatments were administered at IC50 doses. Cell viability and apoptosis were evaluated after 72 hours of drug exposure using the CCK-8 assay and flow cytometry.

2. Cell culture and materials

PC-3 cells, along with F-12K medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), penicillin/streptomycin, and phosphate-buffered saline, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Upon arrival, cells were thawed at 37℃ for 2 minutes, centrifuged at 125g for 5 minutes, and cultured in complete F-12K medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% DMSO. Cells were maintained at 37℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and subcultured at 70%–80% confluence. Silodosin and AA were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry. The Annexin V apoptosis detection kit and CCK-8 assay kit were obtained from Elabscience. All tissue culture plasticware was supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific.

3. CCK-8 assay for cell viability

A total of 100 mL of cell suspension (5,000 cells/well) was seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 hours. Subsequently, 10 mL of the respective treatment concentrations was added to each well, followed by incubation for 72 hours. Thereafter, 10 mL of CCK-8 solution was carefully added to each well, avoiding bubble formation to prevent interference with optical density readings. The plate was further incubated for 4 hours. Cell viability was assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using an RT-2100C microplate reader (Rayto Life and Analytical Sciences Co., Ltd.). Viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control group.

Cell viability (%)=(absorbance of control well - absorbance of treated well)×100

4. Quantitative measurement of cells apoptosis

An apoptosis assay was conducted to evaluate the effect of silodosin only and in combination with AA on PC-3 cell death. Four groups were assessed: untreated control, silodosin (IC50), AA (IC50), and their combination. PC-3 cells were washed twice with Cell Stain Buffer (BioLegend) and resuspended in Annexin V Binding Buffer at 1×107 cells/mL. A 100 mL cell suspension was incubated with 5 mL FITC Annexin V and 10 mL propidium iodide (PI) for 15 minutes at room temperature in the dark, followed by addition of 400 mL Binding Buffer. Apoptosis was analyzed using flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur; BD Biosciences), with 10,000 events recorded per sample. Annexin V-FITC and PI staining distinguished early apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI-), late apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI+), and necrotic (Annexin V-/PI+) cell populations.

5. Statistical analysis

Dose-response curves were constructed to illustrate the relationship between treatment concentration and cell viability based on absorbance values from the CCK-8 assay. Absorbance at each concentration was plotted to assess drug efficacy. Apoptotic populations were quantified by summing early and late apoptotic cells (lower and upper right quadrants) identified via flow cytometry, representing the total apoptosis induced by treatment. A normality test was performed to determine the data distribution. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was utilized. A later multiple comparison test or post hoc test was conducted. The statistical analysis was performed utilizing IBM SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp.). Synergistic interaction analysis was performed using a lobogram-based approach with the aid of CompuSyn software (ComboSyn, Inc.), which facilitated the calculation of the combination index (CI) and dose reduction index (DRI) based on isobologram-derived data. In accordance with established thresholds, a CI<1 was interpreted as synergistic, CI=1 indicated an additive effect, and CI>1 was considered antagonistic as described previously.

RESULTS

1. Silodosin, AA and both combination reduce viability of PC-3 cells

Cell viability in each experimental group was quantitatively evaluated using spectrophotometry at a wavelength of 450 nm after the addition of 10 mL of CCK-8 to each well. The IC50 concentrations of AA and silodosin in this study were established based on previously published research on PC-3 cells, which reported values of 66.90 µM and 44.16 mM, respectively [18]. Spectrophotometry results provided an overview of the absorbance of PC-3 cells based on each treatment. Subsequently, the treatment groups of PC-3 cells viability was assessed by calculating the ratio of absorbance values in each treatment group relative to those in the control group, allowing for normalization and comparative analysis. The control group exhibited the highest cell viability at 99.72%±12.75%, serving as the baseline for comparison. Treatment with AA alone resulted in a substantial reduction in cell viability to 41.79%±13.11%, while silodosin treatment led to a similar viability of 45.35%±11.51%. Notably, the combination of AA and silodosin demonstrated the most pronounced inhibitory effect, reducing cell viability to 18.03%±5.73% (Fig. 1). Statistical analysis was then conducted to differentiate the degree of total viability in PC-3 cells.

Normality was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which confirmed that the data met the assumption of normal distribution (p>0.05). A statistically significant difference was observed in the one way ANOVA method with a p-value less than 0.05 (Table 1). Due to equal variance with Levene test >0.05, Bonferroni post hoc test was performed to investigate differences between each group. The findings indicated a significant differences in apoptosis levels in the group that was administered silodosin, AA and the combination of silodosin+AA if compared with control group. There were no statistically significant differences between silodosin, AA and the combination of silodosin+AA (Table 2).

2. Silodosin, AA and their combination induced apoptosis of PC-3 cells

Flow cytometry results provided an overview of the distribution of PC-3 cells based on cell size and complexity. Gating processes were conducted to separate PC-3 cells from cell debris. Subsequently, the treatment groups of PC-3 cells were separated based on their emitted fluorescence intensity into four distinct quadrants (Fig. 2). The control group exhibited a baseline apoptosis with 20.47%±3.61%. Treatment with AA resulted in a slight increase in apoptosis to 22.93%±7.41%. In contrast, silodosin treatment led to a prominent rise in apoptosis, reaching 80.94%±15.88%. The highest level of apoptosis was observed in the combination treatment group (AA+silodosin), with a mean apoptosis of 87.41%±12.25% (Fig. 3).

Statistical analysis was then conducted to differentiate the degree of total apoptosis (early and late apoptosis) in PC-3 cells. Normality test was performed using Shapiro–Wilk test, with results indicating a normal distribution (p>0.05). A statistically significant difference was observed in the one way ANOVA method with a p-value less than 0.05 (Table 3). Due to variance was not equal with Levene test <0.05, Games-Howell post hoc test was performed to investigate differences between each group. The findings indicated a significant differences in apoptosis levels in the group that was administered with silodosin and the combination of AA+silodosin but not with AA only if compared with control group, as well as the group that received the AA only when compared to silodosin only and combination of AA+silodosin. Silodosin only group was not statistically difference if compared with the combination of AA+silodosin (Table 4).

3. Synergistic effect of silodosin and AA in reducing PC-3 cells viability

To further investigate the synergistic interaction between AA and silodosin, cell viability data from each treatment group were subjected to quantitative analysis using CompuSyn software (ComboSyn, Inc.). The analysis demonstrated synergism across a wide range of fractional effects, as indicated by CI values below 1 (Table 5, Fig. 4). Notably, at one-quarter of the IC50 concentration, the AA-silodosin combination yielded a CI of 0.323, with corresponding drug concentrations of 16.72 µM for AA, representing an 8-fold reduction compared to its monotherapy, and 11.04 mM for silodosin, which is a 5-fold reduction relative to its standalone.

DISCUSSION

Silodosin, a selective α1A-adrenergic blocker used for managing BPH/LUTS, has shown emerging off-target effects on prostate cancer cell lines, including PC-3 [1319]. In this study, we examined silodosin’s impact on the proliferation and viability of PC-3 cells—an androgen-independent line commonly used to model CRPC [15]. CRPC is an advanced form of prostate cancer that progresses despite androgen suppression and AA remains one of the key systemic therapies. However, resistance to AA and its limited long-term efficacy highlight the need for combinatory strategies that target diverse mechanisms to enhance therapeutic outcomes [2021].

This study investigated the cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects of silodosin, AA and their combination on PC-3 cells. Both agents reduced cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner, with IC50 values of 44.16 mM for silodosin and 66.90 µM for AA [18]. Their combination produced the greatest reduction in viability, suggesting a synergistic effect and supporting the cytotoxic potential of α1-blockers and antiandrogens in prostate cancer therapy [51320].

Silodosin exhibits potent antiproliferative activity by modulating adrenergic pathways linked to cell growth and survival [131422]. Its potential as a repurposed adjuvant in prostate cancer therapy offers a basis for combining with hormonal agents like AA to target complementary pathways. In this study, silodosin showed a measurable IC50 against PC-3 cells and reduced viability in a concentrationdependent manner, indicating cytotoxic effects. These findings support its role in inhibiting CRPC cells and lay the foundation for further investigation into its molecular mechanisms.

Silodosin likely reduces cell viability by antagonizing the α1A-adrenergic receptor, which regulates proliferation, survival, and migration via MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways [23]. The MAPK/ERK pathway regulates transcription factors like ELK-1 and is crucial for cell cycle progression, differentiation and apoptosis. As a downstream effector, ELK-1 promotes proliferation while suppressing apoptosis [24]. This inhibited pathway may prevent the activation of ELK-1, thereby disrupting cell survival signals and contributing to reduced cell viability [1314]. The PI3K/AKT pathway supports cell growth and survival and is often activated in cancers, including prostate cancer. Silodosin may suppress this pathway, further reducing proliferation by disrupting AKT-driven signals that control metabolism, the cell cycle and resistance to apoptosis [25]. Furthermore, previous studies in LNCaP prostate cancer cells have shown that protein kinase C (PKC) activation can also trigger apoptosis through a RhoA/ROCK-dependent mechanism, leading to cytoskeletal remodeling and upregulation of the cell cycle inhibitor p21Cip1. This pathway promotes apoptotic signaling independently of AR modulation and may represent an additional mechanism by which silodosin enhances the proapoptotic stress response in prostate cancer cells [26].

The viability of PC3 cells was substantially reduced with the combination treatment of silodosin and AA, as opposed to either agent alone. This suggests a synergistic interaction rather than a merely additive effect. The findings indicate that silodosin and AA produce a synergistic cytotoxic effect in PC3 cells, demonstrated by a markedly greater decrease in cell viability compared to each monotherapy. This suggests simultaneous impairment of complimentary survival pathways. Silodosin showed limited direct effect on hTERT expression in PC-3 cells, but its combination with metformin and AA enhanced hTERT suppression, suggesting potential additive effects in multi-drug regimens for mCRPC [18]. Though silodosin’s direct impact on hTERT is limited, these findings support its role in multi-drug strategies for mCRPC.

Abiraterone primarily functions by inhibiting CYP17A1, an enzyme essential for androgen production. This results in the inhibition of circulating and intratumoral androgens, thereby restricting the activation of the AR [2728]. Despite the fact that PC3 cells are classified as AR-negative or androgen-independent, there is an increasing consensus that CRPC cells can maintain partial AR pathway responsiveness through noncanonical or intracrine mechanisms, or activate AR-independent proliferative programs in response to AR suppression [29]. The disruption of metabolic and transcriptional systems linked to AR signaling caused by AA may contribute to cellular vulnerability even in AR-low or AR-null models. Additionally, recent evidence suggests that AA can enhance cytotoxic responses independently of AR status, partly by impairing DNA repair pathways and altering cell cycle regulation in AR-negative prostate cancer models [30].

Silodosin may potentiate this vulnerability by simultaneously blocking G protein-coupled receptor-mediated activation of intracellular survival signaling [31]. The α1A-adrenergic receptor associates with Gq/11 proteins, which activate phospholipase C, resulting in elevated intracellular calcium and the activation of PKC [3233]. This then initiates the downstream activation of the Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK (MAPK/ERK) cascade. The convergence of both disruptions—AA’s interference with the AR pathway and silodosin’s suppression of adrenergic-driven MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways—may result in cumulative stress within cancer cells. This may encompass endoplasmic reticulum stress resulting from defective protein folding and degradation, mitochondrial depolarization caused by oxidative stress imbalance, and cell cycle arrest due to the dysregulation of cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases [34]. The interconnected and complementary processes likely explain the observed synergistic cytotoxicity of the silodosin—AA combination in PC3 cells.

This study presents early evidence that silodosin amplifies the ability of AA to reduce cell viability in PC-3 prostate cancer cells. The reduced proliferation highlights adrenergic blockade as a potential complement to androgen biosynthesis inhibition in mCRPC treatment. Notably, the combination did not significantly increase apoptosis, suggesting the synergy may act through cell cycle disruption, stress responses, or metabolic pathways rather than classical apoptotic mechanisms.

Apoptotic responses in PC-3 prostate cancer cells were evaluated following treatment with silodosin, AA and their combination at IC50 concentrations. The findings revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cell populations across all treatment groups. The apoptosis assay further corroborated the cytotoxic effects of these treatments, as evidenced by the marked increase in apoptotic cell populations following drug exposure. Treatment with silodosin alone resulted in a substantial apoptotic response (80.94%±15.88%), whereas AA exhibited a comparatively modest effect (22.93%±7.41%). The most pronounced apoptotic activity was observed in the combination treatment group (87.41%±12.25%), indicating enhanced efficacy when both agents were administered concomitantly. The underlying mechanism may involve the inhibition of AR-mediated survival pathways, which have been implicated in prostate cancer progression and resistance to apoptosis.

These findings support further exploration of repurposing non-oncologic drugs with adrenergic activity for advanced prostate cancer. Silodosin’s potential underscores the need to examine off-target effects of approved medications that may enhance existing therapies. However, this study is limited by the absence of molecular profiling, leaving the mechanisms behind the observed synergy speculative. Because this is a cell-line study, clinical safety cannot be inferred; combining AA with silodosin could entail AA related mineralocorticoid effects (hypertension, hypokalemia, edema) and liver enzyme elevations, while silodosin commonly causes reversible orthostatic symptoms, dizziness, ejaculatory dysfunction [412]. Future work should define mechanisms, assess safety, drug interactions, and identify biomarkers of response.

CONCLUSIONS

These preliminary results are promising but they must be evaluated cautiously. The evidence indicates a possible supplementary function of α1A-adrenergic inhibition in mCRPC treatment, highlighting measurable effects on prostate cancer cell viability and apoptosis. These observations contribute to the growing body of evidence on non-androgenic targets in prostate cancer and underscore the relevance of adrenergic signaling in therapeutic modulation.
