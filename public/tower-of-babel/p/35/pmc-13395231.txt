Coping with male factor infertility in India: A call for culturally contextualized biopsychosocial frameworks
Authors: Prarthana Pai, Deviga Subramani
Published: 2026

Infertility has globally gained recognition as a public health concern, according to the WHO guideline for the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of infertility (2025).[1] Based on a review done by Agarwal et al.,[2] male factor infertility contributes to approximately half of all infertility cases reported worldwide. In addition to this, India reports the highest increase in its burden in South Asia over the past three decades, particularly among men in the 25–29 age group.[3] Despite this, the psychosocial dimensions of male infertility remain understudied in the Indian context. Existing literature predominantly focuses on biomedical aspects of diagnosis and treatment, while the lived experiences of men, specifically how they cope with the diagnosis, remain largely invisible.[4]

In November 2025, the WHO released its first-ever global guideline on infertility that covers female factor infertility, male factor infertility, and unexplained infertility. This is the first time male reproductive health has been formally integrated into an international public health clinical framework. Crucially, the guideline explicitly recognizes the psychosocial burden of infertility and calls for ongoing access to psychosocial support as a core component of care, yet it does so without providing any culturally specific guidance on how that support should be delivered for men in South Asian contexts.[1]

Recent evidence from India substantiates this concern. Mor et al.[5] found that 59.5% of men with infertility reported stress symptoms, 51% reported anxiety, and 46% reported depressive symptoms at a tertiary care hospital in South India. These findings emphasize the psychosocial burden experienced by men. Coping was assessed in this study using the Brief COPE scale with no exploration of indigenous or culturally specific practices, and with limited engagement with the masculinity norms that shape how Indian men experience and respond to infertility.

This indicates a critical gap. While literature may highlight models of coping that are Western-centric, it may not fully capture practices that stem from the socio-cultural context including the use of Ayurvedic remedies, faith-based rituals, and consultations with AYUSH practitioners. Baranwal and Chattopadhyay found that more than one-third of men undergoing infertility treatment in Mumbai consulted AYUSH practitioners as part of their coping process, yet such practices remain largely absent from the quantitative literature on coping.[4] Awareness of these culturally attuned coping practices among mental health professionals and clinicians would improve the cultural relevance and therapeutic effectiveness of their interventions.

In addition to this, the role of masculinity norms in shaping how men cope is also unexplored. For men, infertility challenges ideas of masculinity and produces feelings of inadequacy, especially because of the fertility-virility linkage[6] that refers to the dominant conception of masculinity in that being a “breadwinner” and being “tough” are seen as key components.[7] These norms produce a specific burden of shame, silence, and reluctance to seek formal psychological support, discouraging men from accessing the mental health care they need.[7]

We therefore argue for a shift from purely biomedical and symptom-focused frameworks toward a biopsychosocial approach that integrates physiological, psychological, and socio-cultural dimensions of coping. Such a framework, grounded in the Indian socio-cultural context, would better serve mental health professionals, clinicians, and policy makers in designing interventions that are not only evidence-based but culturally accessible and acceptable to men in India.

Male infertility in India is not experienced in a clinical vacuum. It is lived within a web of cultural expectation, masculine identity, and social silence. The frameworks we use to understand and respond to it must reflect that complexity.
