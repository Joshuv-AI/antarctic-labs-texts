The potential of plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles from Centella asiatica leaves (CA-PDENs) for anti-inflammation and prevention of UVB-induced photoaging
Authors: Anggraini Barlian, Youngkwan Cho, Yulia Rahmah, Annisa Nurrahmi Az Zahra, Hanifa Azka Maulidisya, Noviana Vanawati, Safira Meidina Nursatya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to isolate plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles from Centella asiatica leaves (CA-PDENs) and evaluate their anti-inflammatory and anti-photoaging potential.Materials & methodsCA-PDENs were isolated via differential centrifugation and polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation, then characterized physically and proteomically Anti-inflammatory effects were assessed in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages. Introduction

Currently, the beauty industry is one of the fastest-growing industries worldwide. According to [1], the beauty market in Indonesia alone is projected to generate revenues of USD 9.17 billion in 2024, with an expected annual growth rate of 4.39%, or approximately USD 402.56 million, until 2028. To reach market targets and remain competitive, cosmetic companies are striving to improve the quality of their products. One promising approach that has recently attracted increasing attention in the industry is nanotechnology. This approach involves the application of nanoscale materials, devices, or systems in cosmetics development [2,3]. Due to their unique physiochemical characteristics, nanotechnology-based products are often considered more effective and efficient than conventional formulations, where nano-based formulations can overcome their limitations particularly regarding penetration, stability, and controlled release of active ingredients [3,4]. Furthermore, nanomaterials and nanosystems can enhance dispersibility and improve the texture quality of products [5]. Among these emerging nanosystems, plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PDEN) have gained increasing interest in dermatological and cosmetic applications.

Plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PDEN) are plant-originated nanovesicles resembling mammalian exosomes that have emerged as promising cell-free therapeutic agents in regenerative medicine. They exert therapeutic effects by delivering molecular cargos specifically to target cells and tissues, enabling efficient cellular communication, while offering high penetration and compartmentalization, with relatively safer and low production costs [6,7]. PDEN are reported to share similar properties, morphology, and size distribution with mammalian exosomes [8], but are often considered superior due to their higher biocompatibility and specificity, lower immunogenicity (relatively nontoxic), and large-scale producibility [9].

Beyond their biological cargo, the natural vesicular structure of PDEN in general may also contribute to their advantages as carriers of plant-derived metabolites. PDEN typically exhibit particle sizes ranging from approximately 20–200 nm, a size range known to facilitate more efficient penetration across biological barriers. Furthermore, their lipid bilayer structure resembles biological cell membranes, which may promote favorable interactions with cellular membranes and enable effective delivery of encapsulated compounds without significant lipophilic or hydrophilic barriers [10,11]. Given these structural characteristics and delivery advantages, PDEN derived from medicinal plants may represent promising natural delivery systems for dermatological and nanocosmetic formulations. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of PDEN for various skin-related benefits, including anti-photoaging effects in ginseng-derived PDEN [12], skin barrier enhancement from green tea PDEN [13], brightening effects from Sargassum fusiforme PDEN [14], and anti-inflammatory activity from blueberry-derived vesicles [15]. These findings support PDEN as versatile bioactive nanocarriers for cosmetic and therapeutic applications.

Centella asiatica (commonly known as pegagan or gotu kola) is a medicinal plant that has been utilized in traditional medicine across Asian countries for over 3,000 years. It has been known for its ability to treat various diseases with inflammation, accelerate wound healing, and enhance cognitive functions [16]. Its pharmacological activities are associated with a rich content of bioactive compounds, including triterpenoids, flavonoids, polyphenols, amino acids, and phytosterols [16,17]. Historically, Centella asiatica has been reported to treat conditions such as leprosy, lupus, gastritis, diarrhea, fever, eczema, gastric ulcers, and psoriasis [18,19]. More recently, it has gained attention in modern medicine as an alternative therapeutic option for neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) [19–21], endocrine disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity [19,22,23], cardiovascular diseases including hypertension [24], gastrointestinal conditions such as tract damage [25], and respiratory diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis [26]. Moreover, Centella asiatica has demonstrated substantial potential in dermatology and the beauty industry.

Extracts from Centella asiatica leaves possess anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating inducible NO synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) [16,17,20,27,28]. They also inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TLR2, IL-6, and TNF-α, through suppression of the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways [17,27–29]. Beyond these general immunomodulatory activities, Centella asiatica extracts additionally display antibacterial effects against Cutibacterium acnes by disrupting bacterial metabolic pathways, including cell-cycle regulation and protein processing [16,17,30]. These combined anti-inflammatory and antibacterial actions highlight their relevance in conditions characterized by excessive inflammation, including acne-associated inflammation [16,17,20,27,28,30,31]. These effects are attributed to bioactive compounds such as triterpenoids (madecassoside, asiaticoside, asiatic acid), flavonoids, and polyphenols [16,17]. Furthermore, Centella asiatica leaves contain ferulic acid, gulonic acid, quercetin, and kaempferol, which exhibit antioxidant properties through mechanisms including the enhancement of antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD), regulation of the Nrf2-ARE pathway, and modulation of ROS [20,32,33].

Madecassoside and asiaticoside in Centella asiatica have also been reported to provide anti-photoaging benefits, particularly in wrinkle reduction and maintenance of skin elasticity [16,20,34]. Additionally, Centella asiatica extracts have proven effective in accelerating wound healing and scar management due to their asiaticoside and madecassoside content, primarily by enhancing collagen synthesis [16,20]. Although extensive research exists on the bioactivity of Centella asiatica extracts, studies focusing specifically on PDEN derived from this plant remain limited. Given that PDEN may encapsulate bioactive metabolites and enzymes associated with the plant’s therapeutic properties [35,36], investigating Centella asiatica-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (CA-PDENs) could provide novel insights for developing effective anti-inflammatory and photo-protective nanocosmetic products. This study aimed to isolate and characterize CA-PDENs and evaluate their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-photoaging effects using macrophage and fibroblast models. Additional assessment of antibacterial activity against Cutibacterium acnes was included to explore potential relevance to acne-associated inflammation. The findings may support the development of CA-PDENs as a multifunctional, plant-derived nanocosmetic ingredient.

Materials and methods

Isolation of plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (CA-PDENs)

The isolation of CA-PDENs from Centella asiatica leaves was conducted using filtration and differential centrifugation, followed by precipitation with polyethylene glycol (PEG) 6000. The procedure was adapted from a previously described method Kalarikkal et al. [37] with modifications. A total of 350 g of fresh Centella asiatica leaves, separated from stems and roots, were obtained from a local market in Bandung. The leaves were thoroughly washed and blended with 300 mL of distilled water to obtain a crude extract. To separate CA-PDENs from leaf tissue, gradual filtration was carried out. First, using porous cloth (Paris voile fabric), followed by sequential nylon mesh filters of 100 µm and 40 µm.

The filtrate was subjected to stepwise centrifugation at 4 °C: 2000 × g for 10 min, 6000 × g for 20 min, and 10,000 × g for 40 min. The supernatants from each centrifugation stage were separated from the pellet containing leaf tissue. PEG 6000 (50%) was then added to the final supernatant to reach a final concentration of 15%, vortexed, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The mixture was centrifuged at 8000 × g for 1 hour at 4 °C. The supernatant was separated, and the pellet was resuspended in double-distilled water (ddH2O) and vortexed until fully dissolved. The suspension was sterilized by sequential syringe filtration using 0.45 µm and 0.22 µm (Minisart, Sartorius) pore filters to obtain the final CA-PDENs filtrate, which was stored at 4 °C.

Characterization of CA-PDENs

CA-PDENs were characterized based on morphology, size, and concentration. Morphology was observed using a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) (Hitachi, Japan) with negative staining. Particle size measurements of CA-PDENs were performed using a Particle Size Analyzer (PSA) and Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) (Horiba, Japan). Protein concentration was determined using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). The method used follows the method in the kit. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) standards were prepared by serial dilution, while the BCA working solution was prepared by mixing Reagent A and Reagent B at a 50:1 ratio. CA-PDENs sample and BSA standards (25 μL each) were placed in 96-well plates, followed by 200 μL of BCA working solution (in three repetitions). Samples were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, then the absorbance value was measured at 595 nm. Additionally, CA-PDENs were characterized by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/HRMS) to detect related compounds.

CA-PDEN stability assay

A stability assay was performed to determine the optimal storage conditions that maintain the structural and functional integrity of CA-PDENs. Isolated CA-PDENs were stored at −20 °C, 4 °C and room temperature. Their physical characteristics were analyzed weekly for a total of four weeks using a PSA.

Cell culture 1BR3 and RAW 264.7 cells

1BR3 cells (human fibroblast cell line) and RAW 264.7 cells (mouse macrophage cell line) were cultured in flasks containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). 1BR3 cells were maintained in DMEM-low glucose, while RAW 264.7 cells were maintained in DMEM-high glucose. Both media were supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution to prevent microbial contamination. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, with medium changes every 2–3 days. Cells were subcultured at 60–80% confluency and used between passages 19–25. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

Analysis of CA-PDENs internalization into 1BR3 and RAW 264.7 cells

CA-PDENs were fluorescently labeled using the PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells/well and incubated for 24 hours in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following incubation, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. PKH 67-labeled CA-PDENs, were then added to the cells and incubated for 2 and 6 hours for RAW 264.7 cells, and 3 and 6 hours for 1BR3 cells. For each cell line, parallel control groups were maintained without exposure to the labeled CA-PDENs. Following the designated incubation periods, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes. Cells were then washed with PBS before counterstaining the nuclei with DAPI. The internalization of CA-PDENs into the cytoplasm was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Olympus, Japan).

Cytotoxicity assay of CA-PDENs

Cytotoxicity assay was evaluated to measure toxicity in vitro based. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells/well in 100 µL medium and incubated for 24 h. Untreated cells cultured in complete medium served as the cell-only control, representing 100% viability. Different CA-PDENs concentrations were added and incubated for another 24 hours. Concentration 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 100, 200, and 150 µg/mL for RAW 264.7 cells, and 10, 20, 40, 100, 200 and 400 µg/mL for 1BR3 cells. After medium aspiration, 10 µL of MTT reagent (final concentration 0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The MTT reagent was removed, and 100 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Results were expressed as percentage viability relative to untreated controls.

Nitric oxide production assay in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells treated with CA-PDENs

This assay was performed to evaluate the ability of CA-PDENs to suppress nitric oxide (NO) production in polarized M1 macrophages, thereby assessing its anti-inflammatory potential. The procedure was adapted from a study of [38]. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was used to induce a standardized inflammatory response in RAW 264.7 macrophages, while dexamethasone was included as a positive anti-inflammatory control, as it is known to suppress LPS-induced inflammatory mediators [39,40]. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at 7.5 × 104 cells/well in 24-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS in serum-free DMEM to induce an M1-like inflammatory phenotype. CA-PDENs were administered at concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL to LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells. Cells treated with LPS alone served as the inflammatory (negative) control, while dexamethasone (1 µg/mL) was used as a positive anti-inflammatory control.

After 24 hours of incubation, 150 µL of supernatant from each well was transferred into a 96-well plate. To each well, 20 µL of Griess reagent (N-naphthyl-ethylenediamine and sulfanilic acid, 1:1; Invitrogen, USA) and 130 µL distilled water were added. A photometric reference sample was prepared by mixing 20 µL Griess reagent with 280 µL distilled water. Standard NaNO2 solutions (0, 10, 25, 50, 75, and 100 µM) were prepared in the same manner. Samples and standards were incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA), and NO concentration was calculated based on the standard curve equation. Data were expressed as mean ± SD from three independent experiments (n = 3).

Flow cytometry analysis of LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells treated with CA-PDENs

Flow cytometry was performed to assess the expression of macrophage surface markers associated with pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes, specifically CD80 and CD206, respectively. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 hours. LPS-stimulated cells (100 ng/mL) without further treatment served as the inflammatory control, while cells treated with LPS and dexamethasone (1 µg/mL) were used as the positive control. Treatment groups consisted of LPS-stimulated cells exposed to CA-PDENs at concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL, followed by an additional 24 hours of incubation. Following incubation, cells were harvested using a cell scraper, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour at room temperature. Cells were then stained with FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD80 (clone 16-10A1, Armenian hamster IgG; BioLegend, USA) and Alexa Fluor-conjugated anti-mouse CD206 (clone C068C2, rat IgG; BioLegend, USA) for 30 minutes at 4 °C in the dark. After staining, cells were washed with sterile PBS, resuspended, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Appropriate isotype controls were used to define gating strategies. Data were collected from at least 10,000 events per sample and analyzed using FlowJo software.

IL-6 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells treated with CA-PDENs

This assay was conducted to measure the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 by RAW 264.7 cells following CA-PDENs treatment. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at 3.8 × 104 cells/well in 24-well plates and incubated for 24 hours at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To assess the preventive effect, cells were pretreated with CA-PDENs at concentrations of 20 and 40 µg/mL. After 24 hours of incubation, cells were stimulated with 10 ng/mL LPS and incubated for an additional 6 hours. IL-6 levels in the cell-free culture supernatant were measured using a Mouse Interleukin-6 ELISA kit (BT LAB, China), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA).

Antibacterial activity of CA-PDENs against Cutibacterium acnes

Antibacterial assay of CA-PDENs was evaluated using a broth microdilution assay [41]. First, CA-PDENs sample were diluted 1/10 in sterile Mueller–Hinton Broth (MHB) in 15 mL tubes. Then, 100 µL of the mixture was transferred into the second column of a 96-well plate. Serial two-fold dilutions were performed across columns 2–11 by transferring 50 µL from the previous well to the next. Column 12 served as the bacterial control, and column 1 as the blank containing only medium. Untreated bacterial cultures (medium + bacteria) served as negative control. No antibiotic control was included. Therefore, this assay was intended as a preliminary screening of bacteriostatic activity rather than definitive antibacterial efficacy. Bacterial growth was assessed by measuring optical density at the appropriate wavelength after incubation. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Cutibacterium acnes inoculum was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (≈1.5 × 108 CFU/mL), then further diluted 1/50 in MHB to achieve 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Bacterial suspension (50 µL) was added to columns 2–12. Rows without bacterial inoculation were used as sample controls. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 hours. Bacterial growth was assessed by measuring turbidity at 595 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC).

DPPH assay of CA-PDENs

The antioxidant activity of CA-PDENs was evaluated using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay [42]. In a 96-well plate, 100 µL of the methanolic CA-PDENs solution (6,25–400 µg/mL) was added to each well, followed by the addition of 100 µL of the methanolic 0.5 mM DPPH working solution. Ascorbic acid (vitamin C) within the same concentration range was used as positive control. A methanolic solution of DPPH served as the negative control, while methanol alone was used as the blank. After incubation for 30 minutes at 37 °C in the dark, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-rad, USA). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay in UVB-induced 1BR3 cells

An intracellular ROS assay was performed to measure oxidative stress levels in in response to UVB exposure. 1BR3 cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/well in black-walled, clear-bottomed 96-well plates and incubated for 24 hours 37 °C in complete medium. Cells were grouped into untreated control cells without UVB exposure, UVB-exposed cells without pretreatment (negative control), and CA-PDENs treated cells at concentrations of 20, 40, and 100 µg/mL in serum-free medium. After a 24 hour treatment incubation, the medium was removed, and 100 µL ROS Deep Red Working Solution (Abcam, UK) was added to each well and incubated for 1 hour in the dark. The wells were aspirated, washed with PBS, and fresh PBS was added. Cells were subsequently exposed to UVB irradiation (Lucky Herp, China) at a dose of 151 mJ/cm2 for 30 minutes. Fluorescence intensity was measured immediately using a multimode microplate reader (Promega, USA) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 520/605 nm.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) assay for collagen type I in UVB-induced 1BR3 cells

An ICC assay was performed to evaluate the expression and distribution of collagen type I within cells following UVB exposure. 1BR3 cells were seeded on 35-mm glass-bottom dishes at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells/dish and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until reaching 80% confluency. Cells were pretreated with CA-PDENs (40 µg/mL and 100 µg/mL) for 24 hours, while untreated cells served as controls. Cells were then exposed to UVB irradiation at a dose of 151 mJ/cm2 for 30 minutes; control cells were protected from UVB using aluminum foil. Following irradiation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes, permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100 for 30 minutes, and blocked with 1% BSA for 45 minutes. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mouse anti-collagen type I primary antibody (1:500 in 1% BSA; Invitrogen, USA), followed by incubation with an Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:500 in 1% BSA; Abcam, USA) for 60 minutes in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Olympus, Japan).

Collagen type I enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in UVB-induced 1BR3 cells treated with CA-PDENs

Collagen type I levels were quantified using ELISA. 1BR3 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 7.5 × 104 cells/well and incubated until confluency. Cells were treated with CA-PDENs at concentrations of 40 and 100 µg/mL for 24 hours, while untreated cells served as controls. Cells were then exposed to UVB irradiation at a dose of 151 mJ/cm2 for 30 minutes, with control cells protected using aluminum foil. After irradiation, cells were incubated for an additional 48 hours in serum-free media to allow protein synthesis. Culture supernatants were collected, and collagen type I levels were measured using a Collagen Type I ELISA kit (FineTest, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA).

Cell migration observation through scratch assay on 1BR3 cells

The effect of CA-PDENs on cell migration was evaluated using an in vitro scratch assay. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After reaching confluency, the medium was aspirated, and cells were washed with PBS. A uniform scratch was made across the cell monolayer using a sterile 100 µL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing once with PBS. Cells were then treated with medium containing CA-PDENs at concentrations of 40 and 100 µg/mL, while serum-free medium was used as the control. Cell migration and wound closure were monitored for 96 hours using an automatic cell culture monitoring system (Olympus, Japan). The percentage of wound closure was analyzed by measuring the distance between the wound edges using ImageJ software equipped with the Wound_healing_size_tool plugin.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments (n = 3). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Bartlett’s test. For data that were normally distributed and homogeneous, statistical significance was analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for single-factor comparisons or two-way ANOVA for two-factor comparisons, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For data that did not meet the assumptions of normality or homogeneity, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Results

Characterization of CA-PDENs

CA-PDENs were successfully isolated using a combination of differential centrifugation and PEG 6000 precipitation. The final precipitation step using 15% PEG 6000 yielded a visible, compact white pellet (Figure 1A). Particle size analysis (PSA) revealed that CA-PDENs had an average particle size of 154.17 ± 1.59 nm, with a polydispersity index (PI) of 0.24 and a count rate of 62 kCPS, indicating a uniform dispersion and reliable measurement quality (Table 1). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) corroborated these findings, demonstrating an average particle size of 186 ± 0.27 nm, a modal size of 135 nm, and a particle concentration of 3.6 × 1010 particles/mL within the 100–300 nm range (Table 2; Figure 1B). The slight size difference observed between the PSA and NTA measurements was not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Stability of CA-PDENs

The stability of CA-PDENs was evaluated by monitoring changes in the average particle diameter using PSA at weeks 1, 2, 3, and 4 under three distinct storage conditions: fresh storage at 4 °C(fresh), −20 °C (fresh), and 20–25 °C (freeze-dried at room temperature). As illustrated in Figure 1E, CA-PDENs stored at −20 °C remained highly stable over the four-week period, showing no significant alterations in particle size, although this group consistently exhibited a slightly larger initial average diameter compared to the other conditions. Conversely, CA-PDENs stored at 4 °C maintained the smallest average particle diameter during the first two weeks; however, significant size increases (aggregation) were observed in weeks 3 and 4 (p = 0.013 and 0.016, respectively). Freeze-dried CA-PDENs stored at room temperature exhibited intermediate particle diameters, and a significant size increase was recorded by week 4 (p = 0.009). Therefore, while CA-PDENs can be adequately stored at 4 °C for short-term use (1–2 weeks), cryopreservation at –20 °C is strongly recommended to maintain structural integrity for long-term storage.

Cytotoxicity effects of CA-PDENs in RAW 264.7 and 1BR3 cells

The in vitro cytotoxicity of CA-PDENs was assessed in RAW 264.7 and 1BR3 cells using the MTT assay following 24-hour treatment period. According to standard ISO criteria, a substance is considered non-cytotoxic if cell viability remains above 80% [44]. In RAW 264.7 cells (Figure 2D), CA-PDENs maintained cell viability above the 80% threshold at concentrations up to 150 µg/mL. Notably, within the lower concentration range (2.5–60 µg/mL), cell viability exceeded 100%, indicating a potential stimulatory effect on metabolic activity or cell proliferation. Specifically, treatments with 2.5, 5, and 10 µg/mL resulted in statistically significant increases in cell viability compared to the untreated control (p-= 0.0004, p = 0.0067, and p = 0.0161, respectively).

Similarly, CA-PDENs exhibited excellent cytocompatibility in 1BR3 fibroblasts at high concentrations up to 400 µg/mL, with all treatment groups sustaining viability above 80% (Figure 2E). Statistical analysis confirmed that no treatment group differed significantly from the untreated control (p > 0.05), Indicating an absence of toxic effects. Collectively, these findings establish that CA-PDENs are highly biocompatible and safe for use in subsequent functional in-vitro assays.

The effect of CA-PDENs on macrophage surface marker expression was evaluated by analyzing CD80 (M1-associated marker) in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells using flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 3B, treatment with CA-PDENs at 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL was associated with a decreasing trend in the percentage of CD80-positive cells compared to the LPS control. Notably, the highest CA-PDENs concentration showed a greater numerical reduction in CD80-positive cells than the dexamethasone control (15.63%). However, one-way ANOVA indicated that these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Overall, CA-PDENs treatment did not induce statistically significant changes in CD80 expression but demonstrated a modest, concentration-dependent trend toward reduced M1-associated marker expression.

Analysis of CD206 expression (Figure 3C) showed a modest increase following treatment with 10 µg/mL CA-PDENs (22.22%) compared to the LPS control (20.95%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). CA-PDENs decrease IL-6 expression in RAW 264.7 cells

The anti-inflammatory activity of CA-PDENs was further evaluated by measuring IL-6 secretion using ELISA. Unlike the NO and CD marker assays, in which cells were treated after LPS induction, this assay assessed the preventive effect of CA-PDENs by treating RAW 264.7 cells prior to stimulation with 10 ng/mL LPS. Treatment with CA-PDENs at 20 and 40 µg/mL resulted in IL-6 levels of 172.88 pg/mL and 159.61 pg/mL, respectively. In comparison, the LPS control and dexamethasone-treated groups exhibited IL-6 levels of 177.90 pg/mL and 182.91 pg/mL. However, no significant differences were observed between the two doses (p > 0.05). These results suggest that CA-PDENs may modulate inflammatory signaling rather than fully suppress cytokine production.

Discussions

Isolation and physicochemical characterization of CA-PDENs

This study successfully isolated plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (CA-PDENs) from Centella asiatica and demonstrated that these vesicles exhibit physicochemical and biological characteristics consistent with previously described PDENs from edible and medicinal plants. The nanoscale diameter, low polydispersity, and bilayered vesicle morphology observed through PSA, NTA, and TEM confirm that the combination of differential centrifugation and PEG-assisted isolation workflow effectively enriched exosome-like fractions. Differential centrifugation is widely recognized as the “gold standard” for exosome isolation, as it enables the processing of large sample volumes while achieving high purity of the isolates [46]. Meanwhile, PEG is a low-toxicity hydrophilic polymer that precipitates exosomes by reducing their solubility through interactions with surrounding water molecules [47–49]. PEG-based precipitation can yield more exosomes and higher exosomal DNA content than commercial kits such as ExoQuick and PureExo, while remaining simple, cost-effective, and scalable [50]. The selection of 15% PEG 6000 was based on a previous study [51] demonstrating that this concentration is optimal for obtaining CA-PDENs isolate with favorable characteristics.

The particle sizes obtained for CA-PDENs fall within the typical PDEN range of 50–500 nm [52], supporting the structural integrity of the isolated vesicles. The polydispersity index (PI) was 0.24, which is well below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.3 for lipid-based nanocarriers [53], indicating a uniform and homogeneous particle size distribution suitable for therapeutic applications. In addition, the PSA count rate (62 kCPS) exceeded the minimum quality threshold of 50 kCPS [54], supporting the reliability of the measurements. CA-PDENs displayed a spherical morphology surrounded by a lipid bilayer membrane, with particle diameters ranging from approximately 40–140 nm under TEM. These observations are consistent with the morphological characteristics of CA-PDENs previously reported by [35].

Proteomic analysis provided a deeper characterization of the molecular cargo of CA-PDENs, revealing that CA-PDENs contains metabolic and biosynthetic proteins, including 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate synthase (DXS) and chalcone synthase (CHS), which are involved in flavonoid and terpenoid biosynthesis. These pathways are associated with many of the bioactivities historically attributed to Centella asiatica, including anti-inflammatory [17,55–58], wound healing [59], and antibacterial effects [60]. These findings suggest that CA-PDENs encapsulates molecular cargo reflective of Centella asiatica’s therapeutic profile. Consistent with vesicles from other plants, these protein cargos may contribute to the functional bioactivities of PDENs, reinforcing their potential as natural therapeutic nanocarriers.

Stability and cellular uptake of CA-PDENs

The stability of CA-PDENs was strongly dependent on storage conditions. For example, leaf-derived vesicles from Dendropanax morbifera showed that storage at −20 °C for up to 4 weeks preserved vesicle size distribution and membrane integrity better than storage at 4 °C, 25 °C, or 45 °C, which caused size increases, altered surface charge, decreased protein content, and reduced cellular uptake [61]. The present results similarly indicate improved stability at −20 °C compared to 4 °C over a four-week period, consistent with earlier reports [38,51]. Although short-term storage at 4 °C may be acceptable, medium- to long-term preservation requires −20 °C or preferably −80 °C [62]. Repeated freeze–thaw cycles and freeze-drying without appropriate cryoprotectants can induce aggregation, size shifts, or cargo degradation and should therefore be minimized [63].

Confocal microscopy demonstrated efficient, time-dependent internalization of CA-PDENs by RAW 264.7 macrophages and 1BR3 fibroblasts, with uptake detectable as early as 2 hours in RAW 264.7 cells and 3 hours in 1BR3 cells. These observations are consistent with known PDEN internalization pathways, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis, phagocytosis, micropinocytosis, and in some cases membrane fusion [47]. The uptake mechanism of PDEN in general can be influenced by its particle size [64]. The internalization process also may be influenced by the presence of phosphatidic acid (PA) in the CA-PDENs membrane, which supports membrane fusion and fission and regulates cellular membrane-transport systems [65,66]. In the context of topical application, the nanoscale size and lipid-bilayer structure of CA-PDENs may also facilitate transdermal delivery through trans follicular, intercellular, and intracellular routes [47].

Cytocompatibility of CA-PDENs in mammalian cells

CA-PDENs also exhibited no cytotoxicity up to 150 µg/mL, maintaining cell viability above the 80% safety threshold [67], and even promoting viability at lower concentrations in RAW 264.7 cells. Similarly, CA-PDENs remained nontoxic to 1BR3 cells at concentrations up to 400 µg/mL. These results indicate that CA-PDENs are well tolerated by mammalian cells under the tested conditions, supporting their suitability for subsequent biological assays.

Based on these findings, concentrations of 10,20, and 40 µg/mL were selected for further anti-inflammatory evaluation [68]. Meanwhile, the concentrations of 20, 40, and 100 µg/mL used for the anti-aging assays were selected with consideration of the DPPH IC50 results and previous studies employing comparable concentration ranges of Centella-derived preparations in similar skin-related cellular models [69]. The use of similar concentration ranges was intended to facilitate the comparison of biological responses among PDENs derived from different plant sources.

Anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of CA-PDENs

The biological activities observed for CA-PDENs are closely linked to key mechanisms involved in inflammatory responses. Macrophages act as central regulators of inflammation by producing mediators such as nitric oxide (NO), reactive oxygen species (ROS), IL-6, and TNF-α upon activation, and their polarization state strongly influences whether inflammation is amplified or resolved [17,70]. Excessive M1-driven responses contribute to prolonged inflammatory signaling, whereas a shift toward M2-associated phenotypes promotes resolution and tissue repair [70–72]. These regulatory pathways are broadly relevant across diverse inflammation-associated conditions, including skin disorders where microbial stimuli and immune activation intersect. Acne vulgaris represents one such condition, in which Cutibacterium acnes proliferation and amplified inflammatory responses within the pilosebaceous unit act synergistically to drive lesion formation [73–76]. Thus, therapeutic candidates capable of modulating inflammatory responses while providing auxiliary antimicrobial effects, such as CA-PDENs, may have broad relevance in inflammation-associated skin conditions [77,78].

Evaluation of anti-inflammatory activity showed that CA-PDENs significantly reduced NO production in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages across all tested concentrations, with a greater reduction than that observed in dexamethasone-treated cells. NO is an early and rapidly inducible pro-inflammatory mediator generated through inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) activity and interactions between reactive oxygen and nitrogen species during macrophage activation [79,80]. The observed reduction in NO levels suggests attenuation of early inflammatory signaling. Previous studies have reported that triterpenoids from Centella asiatica, including asiaticoside and madecassoside, can modulate upstream inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK signaling [29]. However, as these signaling pathways were not directly examined in the present study, the involvement in CA-PDENs–mediated effects remains to be confirmed. Nevertheless, the magnitude of NO reduction observed here is consistent with earlier reports demonstrating that Centella asiatica extracts suppress NO production in LPS-stimulated macrophages [74], indicating that CA-PDENs retain anti-inflammatory bioactivity associated with their plant origin.

Flow cytometry analysis further revealed non-significant but consistent directional trends in macrophage polarization following CA-PDENs treatment in the present study. CA-PDENs tended to reduce CD80 expression, a marker associated with M1 activation, while slightly increasing CD206, suggesting a shift toward an M2-associated phenotype. This biphasic response pattern is consistent with a hormetic dose–response behavior, in which lower doses may elicit stimulatory effects while higher doses exert suppressive effects, a phenomenon commonly reported for natural product–derived immunomodulators [81]. Previous studies have shown that bioactive compounds from Centella asiatica can influence macrophage polarization by suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including MAPK and NF-κB signaling, leading to reduced p65 nuclear translocation and decreased expression of downstream inflammatory mediators [29]. Such signaling modulation has been associated with a transition from an M1 pro-inflammatory state toward an M2 pro-resolving phenotype. In addition, plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles have been reported to modulate macrophage signaling networks through their lipid, protein, and miRNA cargos [82,83]. However, as these molecular pathways were not directly investigated in the present study, the observed polarization trends should be interpreted with caution.

Similarly, CA-PDENs induced a modest reduction in IL-6 secretion compared with the LPS-stimulated control. While triterpenoids derived from Centella asiatica have been reported to downregulate IL-6 production via NF-κB–related mechanisms [84], the relatively modest magnitude of IL-6 modulation observed here suggests that cytokine regulation may be less sensitive to CA-PDENs under the tested conditions. These conditions may include vesicle concentration, exposure duration, or cargo abundance. When considered together with the NO data, these findings suggest a hierarchy of inflammatory responsiveness, in which nitric oxide, an early and rapidly inducible inflammatory mediator [85], appears more readily modulated than cytokine secretion or macrophage phenotypic shifts. Cytokine regulation and macrophage polarization involve complex signaling networks integrating multiple receptors, transcription factors, and feedback mechanisms, and may therefore require stronger stimuli, higher concentrations, or prolonged exposure to achieve statistically significant modulation [86,87]. In this study, the evaluation of macrophage inflammatory markers was intended as an initial exploratory assessment of the immunomodulatory potential of CA-PDENs. The treatment concentrations were selected based on previously reported studies of plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles to enable comparison across different PDEN sources [68]. However, although these directional changes were observed, differences in CD80, CD206, and IL-6 did not reach statistical significance. Nevertheless, the observed trends are consistent with an expected immunomodulatory response. Further studies with larger sample size and extended durations are required to confirm these findings and to better characterize the immunomodulatory effects of CA-PDENs. Overall, the present findings provide preliminary insight into the interaction between CA-PDENs and macrophage inflammatory signaling pathways.

Antibacterial activity related to microbe-associated cutaneous inflammation

Beyond macrophage-mediated pathways, CA-PDENs also exhibited biological activity relevant to the microbial components of cutaneous inflammation. Although antibacterial activity was not the primary focus of this study, CA-PDENs demonstrated a moderate, concentration-dependent inhibitory effect of Cutibacterium acnes growth using a broth microdilution assay [88]. The observed bacteriostatic activity aligns with previous reports indicating that plant-derived nanovesicles typically exert bacteriostatic rather than bactericidal effects under standard experimental conditions [74].

This antibacterial activity is likely supported by the bioactive cargo of CA-PDENs, including enzymes such as chalcone synthase (CHS) and 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate synthase (DXS), which are involved in the biosynthesis of flavonoid and terpenoid compounds known to possess antimicrobial properties [60]. Although modest, this activity is biologically relevant, as microbial components of C. acnes can amplify inflammatory cascades through the activation of Toll-like receptor–mediated pathways, leading to increased cytokine and reactive oxygen species production [73,75,77,89]. Thus, even partial inhibition of bacterial proliferation may contribute to limiting downstream inflammatory amplification. Taken together, CA-PDENs integrate the intrinsic bioactivities of Centella asiatica with the cellular interaction and delivery advantages of plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles. These findings extend current knowledge on plant-derived nanovesicles by providing new insight into the biological activity of nanovesicles derived from Centella asiatica, a medicinal plant whose vesicular forms remain relatively underexplored. The pronounced suppression of NO production, accompanied by modest immunomodulatory trends and moderate bacteriostatic activity, suggests that CA-PDENs preferentially influence early inflammatory events while also providing secondary antimicrobial effects. These properties are particularly relevant to inflammation-associated skin conditions, including acne, where microbial cues and excessive immune activation jointly contribute to disease pathology [90]. Although modulation of IL-6 secretion and macrophage polarization did not reach statistical significance, these trends complement the more robust NO suppression and antibacterial effects observed. Future studies focusing on vesicular cargo characterization, mechanistic signaling pathways, and in vivo validation will be essential to further define the therapeutic relevance of CA-PDENs in inflammatory and skin-related conditions.

Antioxidant activity and protection against oxidative stress

Premature skin aging is closely linked to elevated intracellular ROS levels induced by UV exposure. These ROS typically include derivatives of superoxide anions (O2−), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and non-radical molecules such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Excessive accumulation of H2O2 activates mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), which regulates the biosynthesis of activator protein-1 (AP-1). Elevated AP-1 increases the production of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), a class of enzyme responsible for extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling by cutting up collagen type I fibers, ultimately leading to its degradation and a subsequent loss of skin quality. In parallel, elevated ROS levels induces IκB ubiquitination, promoting NF-κB translocation into the cell nucleus and further increasing MMP-1 expression. The reduction of collagen and elastin compromises ECM structure, and in epidermal cells, this results in skin sagging and wrinkle formation [91–93].

The general antioxidant activity of CA-PDENs was initially evaluated based on its ability to scavenge DPPH free radicals, where it exhibited strong radical-scavenging activity. This result align with the previous findings that reported that Centella asiatica nanovesicles isolated using glacial acetic acid with similar IC50 value [94]. The antioxidant activity observed can be attributed to Centella asiatica leaves natural antioxidant constituents’ interaction with the DPPH radical. This interaction is strongly influenced by the structural conformation of the compounds, particularly the presence of hydroxyl groups that act as electron donors to neutralize free radicals and inhibit subsequent radical propagation. Specifically, polyphenols with aromatic rings oh -OH or -OCH3 substituents can act as potent radical scavengers by electron donors to the free radical [95]. Flavonoids such as kaempferol, quercetin, and rutin in Centella asiatica also act as major antioxidants that reduce H2O2-induced cytotoxicity in cells [32]. In addition, the four major triterpenoid compounds of Centella asiatica, known as centelloids, asiatic acid, madecassoside, madecassic acid, and asiaticoside are also known to contribute significantly to its antioxidant activity [96].

While the DPPH assay provides a valuable baseline of CA-PDENs chemical potency, a more biologically relevant measure of efficacy is essential to assess its ability to neutralize ROS at the intracellular level. Therefore, further evaluation was performed on UVB-induced 1BR3 fibroblasts, one of the key cell types involved in maintaining the skin’s line of defense against sun exposure. A significant reduction in intracellular ROS was detected in CA-PDENs–treated cells, further supporting its role in mitigating UVB-induced oxidative stress. At the cellular level, antioxidant compounds from Centella asiatica, such as terpenoids, act as ROS scavengers by neutralizing superoxide and hydroxyl radicals via electron donation. Although cells possess intrinsic antioxidant defense systems, radical scavenging molecules such as nanovesicle, in this case CA-PDENs, offer an advantage due to their small size, which enables efficient membrane diffusion and localization near potential ROS-generating targets [97].

Furthermore, the observed decrease in ROS may also be associated with the upregulation of endogenous antioxidant enzymes. Previous studies have reported that Centella asiatica fractions can increase the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), which subsequently reduces malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, an oxidative stress marker in cells [98]. In addition, triterpenoid saponins from CA-PDENs have been reported to inhibit the oxidation of thioredoxin-1 (Trx 1), a key regulator of the MAPK pathway, thereby preventing intracellular oxidative stress [92]. Together, these results highlight the antioxidant potential of CA-PDENs at both chemical and cellular levels.

Pro-collagen and anti-photoaging effects of CA-PDENs

Collagen type I degradation is a key process underlying photo-induced skin aging. Therefore, the anti-photoaging potential of CA-PDENs was evaluated based on its ability to prevent the degradation of collagen type I, an essential component of extracellular matrix (ECM) responsible for maintaining dermal integrity and elasticity. Immunocytochemistry (ICC) analysis showed that CA-PDENs showed remarkable collagen preservation in UVB-induced cells and may also promote its synthesis under stressful conditions, potentially mediated by its bioactive constituents. Previous studies have reported that asiaticoside in Centella asiatica exhibits inhibitory activity against matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1, MMP-3, and MMP-9) compared with related compounds [96]. Based on these findings, CA-PDENs are proposed to mitigate collagen degradation, potentially through the inhibition of these enzymes, which could partly explain the protective effects observed. However, further investigation of the specific molecular mechanisms underlying CA-PDENs-mediated regulation of MMPs is required to gain further insight into the pathways involved in photoprotection.

Beyond preventing degradation, quantitative analysis further indicated that CA-PDENs actively enhanced collagen expression, suggesting that CA-PDENs not only protects existing matrix collagen from breakdown but may also stimulate the synthesis of new collagen, an essential process in tissue repair and regeneration. Notably, collagen levels in the UVB-only group were also higher than those in untreated controls. This response may be attributed to the pivotal role of fibroblasts as the primary source of collagen and other ECM components. Under normal conditions, collagen expression is maintained at relatively stable levels. However, in response to cellular stress or injury, fibroblasts upregulate collagen synthesis to initiate ECM repair [99]. Thus, the elevated collagen levels observed in the UVB-induced group likely represents a natural cellular response to restore ECM integrity following UVB-induced damage.

The stimulatory effect of CA-PDENs on collagen synthesis are likely due to the action of its active compound, particularly asiaticoside. Previous studies have reported that this compound enhances collagen synthesis in human dermal fibroblasts via Smad signaling pathways independent of transforming growth factor-β receptor 1 kinase [96]. Although these pathways were not directly investigated in the present study, they may partly explain the pro-collagen effects observed following CA-PDENs treatment. Therefore, CA-PDENs may provide dual protection against photoaging by limiting collagen degradation while simultaneously promoting collagen synthesis under UVB-induced stress conditions.

Wound healing potential of CA-PDENs

Following its pro-collagen activity, CA-PDENs also exhibited wound healing–associated effects, as evidenced by the scratch assay results. The enhanced wound closure suggests increased fibroblast migratory activity, a key process in tissue regeneration, as fibroblasts migrate toward the injured site and contribute to ECM reconstruction through the production of structural proteins such as collagen [100]. This observation aligns with previous studies reporting that the asiatic acid content in Centella asiatica promotes fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, and re-epithelialization during the proliferative and remodeling phases of wound healing [101]. Altogether, consistent with the antioxidant activity observed in this study, CA-PDENs appears to induce a coordinated cellular response that facilitates ECM repair by promoting collagen synthesis and supporting fibroblast migration under oxidative stress conditions.

Conclusion

CA-PDENs were successfully isolated and characterized, exhibiting size, morphology, and homogeneity consistent with established criteria for plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles. Proteomic profiling confirmed the presence of enzymes involved in flavonoid and terpenoid biosynthesis, supporting their potential as bioactive plant metabolites carriers. CA-PDENs were efficiently internalized by both RAW 264.7 macrophages and 1BR3 fibroblasts and demonstrated low cytotoxicity to both cell types even at high concentrations. Functionally, CA-PDENs exhibited pronounced early-phase anti-inflammatory activity through significant nitric oxide suppression at 40 µg/mL, and a consistent downward trend in pro-inflammatory markers, including CD80 expression and IL-6 secretion, while the M2-associated marker CD206 was most evident at 10 µg/mL. Although these changes did not reach statistical significance, they suggest a potential modulatory effect on macrophage activation. Beyond macrophage-associated pathways, CA-PDENs also displayed moderate, concentration-dependent bacteriostatic effects against Cutibacterium acnes, highlighting its relevance for microbe-associated inflammation. In UVB-induced photoaging assays, CA-PDENs demonstrated notable protective effects through high antioxidant activity, significant reduction of intracellular ROS levels, preservation of collagen type I levels following UVB exposure, and improvement of wound closure in fibroblast culture, with these effects most pronounced at an optimal concentration of 100 µg/mL.

Collectively, these findings highlight CA-PDENs as a multifunctional nanovesicle system integrating anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties. Consistent with previous reports that plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles can modulate inflammation and oxidative stress in mammalian cells, although effects may vary with plant sources, this work provides integrated evidence of both immunomodulatory and photoprotective effects from a single vesicle system. Overall, the results support the hypothesis that CA-PDENs can serve as a bioactive nanocarriers for cosmetic and dermatological applications targeting inflammation, photoaging, and microbe-associated skin conditions. Future in vivo studies are warranted to validate these effects and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying their biological activity, paving the way for practical applications.
