Experimental evaluation of an integrated focused ultrasound and electroencephalography approach for developing activation-informed neuroimaging
Authors: Ye Yuan, Jun You Li, Yongzhi Zhang, Sonia E. Vader, Tongsheng Zhang, Gösta Ehnholm, Jue Wang, Nathan McDannold, Yoshio Okada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We experimentally evaluated the possibility of using an integrated focused ultrasound (FUS) - electroencephalography (EEG) approach to develop activation-informed neuroimaging for human functional brain mapping, enhancing the power of conventional approaches based solely on recording methods. A brief beam of FUS (50 μs, 4.0 MHz) with a focal transverse profile diameter of 0.4 mm determined in water was applied to the barrel cortex of rat in vivo on the dura, skull and scalp and evoked activity was recorded with EEG. We found using a laminar depth profile analysis and specific channel blockers that the stimulation within a safe pressure range could directly evoke a presynaptic spike (~1 ms) in layer IV, followed by postsynaptic spikes (2.5-7 ms) in layer V and an excitatory wave (8-12 ms), both regulated by fast inhibition, and a major robust excitatory wave (20-30 ms) regulated by slow inhibition. Stimulus artifact was <1 ms, much shorter than for transcranial magnetic stimulation. All components were reliably detected from the scalp, skull, and dura using the 4 MHz and 2.2 MHz transducers. Thus, the FUS-EEG approach is capable of activating neurons transcranially and noninvasively recording a rich spectrum of excitatory and inhibitory activity starting immediately after stimulation. These characteristics can advance neuroimaging since this approach can identify normal electrophysiology and pathophysiology in known locations anywhere in the brain with precise timing and could possibly enhance the conventional approaches by validating and improving their spatial and temporal accuracy of localization.

Introduction

Although the major neuroimaging methods including functional MRI (fMRI), magnetoencephalography (MEG) and electroencephalography (EEG) are powerful for functional brain mapping, none can stimulate the brain. Adding stimulation or neuromodulation could greatly advance our understanding of human brain functions since it could then directly probe the physiological properties of local and brain-wide circuits in ways not possible with recording techniques alone.

Some have recognized this benefit and have proposed a stimulation-recording (S-R) approach combining transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and EEG (Ilmoniemi et al., 1997; Ilmoniemi and Kičić, 2010; Rogasch and Fitzgerald, 2013; Lioumis and Rosanova, 2022). But their potentials have not been fully realized in part due to several basic limitations, which include: (1) limited depth of penetration of the induced electric field, (2) relatively poor focality for stimulating deep regions and (3) long-lasting artifacts that may be 30 ms or longer unless great care is used.

Focused ultrasound (FUS) is becoming useful for noninvasive brain stimulation and neuromodulation (Yu et al., 2021; Mohammadjavadi et al., 2022; Yoo et al., 2022; Kosnoff et al., 2024). It can transcranially activate the brain with safe low-energy levels (Tyler et al., 2008; Tufail et al., 2010, 2011; Legon et al., 2014). In contrast to TMS, a whole-head FUS system is capable of stimulating multiple focal regions with a focality independently of depth in any region of the brain (McDannold et al., 2024). Thus FUS is beginning to be combined with functional MRI based on BOLD signals (cf. Raghunath et al., 2025). However, it is still not well known whether FUS has properties essential in developing an S-R system with a millisecond temporal resolution and a millimeter spatial resolution.

In this study, we experimentally evaluated the possibility of using an integrated focused ultrasound (FUS)-electroencephalography (EEG) approach for developing a new type of neuroimaging. We chose EEG for a FUS-based S-R system because we are interested in developing a new neuroimaging approach with a millisecond resolution. Today, EEG and MEG are the only two techniques uniquely capable of measuring electrographic signals directly related to neuronal currents with a millisecond temporal resolution (Hari and Puce, 2023; Ilmoniemi and Sarvas, 2019; Okada, 2020). This FUS-EEG approach could possibly advance neuroimaging since the locations stimulated by FUS can be known in advance and thus there is no ambiguity in referring EEG signals back to the generators in the brain. We carried out an in vivo study to determine whether the activity elicited in the cortex by FUS and recorded non-invasively on the scalp with EEG has characteristics ideal for developing a new type of neuroimaging.

We found that a brief highly focal beam of FUS applied to the barrel cortex of rat in vivo could produce a repeatable, reliable robust sequence of neuronal activity that can be recorded intracortically and on the exposed dura. We were able to develop a FUS stimulation parameter that produces a very brief stimulus artifact lasting <1 ms. This enabled us to detect a presynaptic spike with virtually no delay generated by axons in layer IV of the barrel cortex, which was followed by a cascade of postsynaptic spike trains and excitatory waves in layer V that could be sometimes observed in real time. The postsynaptic spikes and the first excitatory wave activated a fast type of inhibition, whereas the second excitatory wave activated a slow type of inhibition. A systematic series of experiments elucidated the physiological bases of these components. We then showed that the full sequence of events can be activated transcranially and all can be recorded noninvasively using scalp EEG, indicating that this approach could be used to develop a new type of neuroimaging, which we term “activation-informed neuroimaging”. We will below discuss how these characteristics can be used to develop the new type of neuroimaging method, which could enhance the spatial and temporal precision of the conventional neuroimaging approaches such as MEG and EEG that are based solely on recording techniques.

Methods

Animal surgery and preparation

Sprague Dawley male rats (250-350 g) (Charles River Laboratories) were used under an approved protocol at Brigham and Women’s Hospital in Boston, MA, USA.

The anesthetic was changed from a combination of ketamine/xylazine used in our previous study (Tripathi et al., 2020) and by others (Han et al. 2018) to a combination of α-chloralose and isoflurane in order to evoke stronger neuronal responses from the cortex. Ketamine reduces FUS-evoked responses as the dosage is increased, eventually blocking the response completely (Han et al., 2018). We, therefore, switched the anesthetics to a combination of isoflurane and α-chloralose or α-chloralose alone. Both types of anesthetics are used commonly in functional MRI studies (Austin et al., 2005; Huttunen et al., 2008). Isoflurane enhances effects of the fast γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors (GABAA) (Jones et al., 1992). Since it also blocks N-methyl-D aspartate (NMDA) receptors, like ketamine, we reduced isoflurane from 1% to 0% and mostly used α-chloralose, which also enhances GABAA receptor actions (Garret and Gan, 1998). Most of the data reported in this paper are based on α-chloralose alone. Although α-chloralose is used as an effective anesthetic in fMRI research, we do not know of any ultrasound studies that have used this anesthetic.

Anesthesia was induced by placing the rat (Sprague Dawley, 250-350 g) in an induction chamber and delivering 5% isoflurane for 4-1/2 minutes. The animal was then placed on the platform of a stereotactic head holder modified from a commercial head holder (Stoelting). The platform was mounted on a concentric aluminum cylinder so that it could be rotated continuously and smoothly for optimally placing the FUS transducer on a target region of the barrel field over the left hemisphere. Immediately after the animal was placed on the platform, a nose cone was placed on the animal to deliver 2% isoflurane. A heat pad was inserted between the animal and the platform to maintain body temperature. Buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg i.p.) as analgesic and Dexmedetomidine (0.5 mg/kg i.p.) as sedative were injected intraperitoneally. After waiting for 5 min, sufficient for the analgesics to take their effects, the head was fixed in the head holder using ear bars and a bite bar. The scalp was shaved and cleaned with depilatory cream. A bolus (25 mL) of irrigation saline was injected subcutaneously behind the neck and in the caudal region of the body to prevent dehydration during the experiment.

In the main series of experiments, we were interested in determining the full spectrum of electrophysiological activities in the barrel cortex that can be elicited by FUS. Therefore, the transducer was placed on the dura in order to avoid potential complications associated with transcranial stimulations (Guo et al., 2018; Sato et al., 2018; Mahammadjavadi et al., 2019). In these experiments, the scalp was cut with a razor blade along the midline to expose the skull. The temporal muscles on the left side were cut away to expose the temporal cranial bone. The animal with the head holder was then moved inside a 2-layer, homemade rf cage equipped with an operating microscope. The craniotomy was performed using a hand-held burring drill under the view of the microscope to expose a large section of the left lateral hemisphere wider than the entire barrel field. The skull area removed extended from 2 mm anterior to the bregma to the lambdoid suture, 1 mm lateral to the midline, extending laterally down to the bottom of the exposed temporal cranial bone. The dura was kept intact, except during the intracortical depth profile analysis. The exposed dural surface was cleaned with irrigation saline. The barrel column receiving whisker C2 (2.5 mm posterior to the bregma and 5.5 mm lateral from the midline (Tripathi et al., 2020) was determined with the stereotactic head holder. Once the craniotomy was completed, the anesthetic was switched to a solution of α-chloralose mixed in polyethylene glycol. This anesthetic was administered intraperitoneally at a concentration of 30 mg/kg, while 1% isoflurane was delivered. Spontaneous brain activity was measured continuously with an EEG electrode in order to monitor the level of anesthesia. The electrode was the same as the one used for epidural recording of LFP. It was placed approximately 2-3 mm away from the center of the FUS transducer tip with the reference electrode below the scalp on the right side of the head. We looked for sleep spindles, sometimes called anesthesia spindles, in the spontaneous EEG, which is the marker of sleep stage II (Fernandez and Luthi, 2020; You et al., 2024). Approximately 10-15 min later, as spontaneous EEG became stable in sleep stage II, isoflurane delivered with medical grade air was reduced slowly to 0%.

In the follow-up second series of experiments, we tested whether the full spectrum of activities observed with the transducer above the exposed dura can be elicited transcranially and recorded non-invasively. Thus, the C2 area of the cortex was stimulated with a FUS transducer placed on the dura, skull or scalp and evoked activity was recorded on the dura, skull or scalp. Once the head was secured in the head holder, the dorsal portion of the scalp was shaved and depilated as above. Then, the scalp was cut around a hemi-circle leaving the left side of the scalp uncut. The scalp was flipped and the connective tissue on the skull was removed. The location receiving projection from whisker C2 (2.5 mm posterior to the bregma and 5.0 mm to the left of the midline) was marked for FUS stimulation. This location was 0.5 mm closer to the midline than in the main series in order to stimulate the cortex away from the corner of the cranial bone where it was thicker. A slit was made in the temporal wall of the cranial bone, just lateral to the center of the FUS beam, for recording the LFPs on the dura for comparisons with the LFPs on the skull and scalp. As soon as the preparation was completed, the anesthetic was switched to α-chloralose, isoflurane was gradually reduced to 0% while monitoring continuous EEG. Measurements normally lasted 2-6 hrs. When the data collection was completed, the animal was euthanized with sodium pentobarbital in this acute, nonsurvival study (100 mg/kg. i.p.)

There were 42 rats in this entire study: 9 rats showing the strong W2 response (Fig. 1), 6 rats showing activation of a slow inhibitory system for W2 (Fig. 2), 5 rats showing activation of a fast inhibitory system for Spike 2, 3 and W1 (Fig. 3), 3 rats in the TTX experiment (Fig. 4), 4 rats in the Kyna experiment (Fig. 4), 5 rats in the study of the laminar depth profile of the LFP (Fig. 5), 7 rats for the study of duration effects (Fig. 6), and 5 rats for the study of pressure effect and 3 rats for the transcranial study.

Experimental Design

This study consisted of one main series of experiments and a second follow-up series of experiments, all using a within-subject design, which eliminates the subject variability and reduces the number of animals needed. Fig. 1A shows the experimental arrangement in the main study. The barrel column receiving projection from whisker C2 was activated by mechanical stimulation of a single whisker C2 or by direct stimulation of the barrel column using a FUS beam oriented vertically over the exposed intact dura. These two modes of stimulation were used to compare the waveforms produced by stimulation of C2 area in the cortex with FUS and mechanical stimulation of the whisker. In this main study, we discovered that FUS was capable of producing a cascade of short-latency responses, as short as 1 ms in peak latency, that had not been reported previously. Thus, we carried out the second series of experiments to determine whether the full spectrum of activities can be found when the cortex is stimulated transcranially and the LFPs are recorded non-invasively.

Mechanical stimulation of single whiskers and recording of evoked responses

In some of the experiments in the main series, a single whisker was mechanically stimulated and the evoked LFP’s were recorded on the epidural surface. Whisker C2 was mechanically stimulated by inserting the whisker into a gauge 22 syringe needle that was fixed to a piezoelectric bender (a rectangular stick with a 28 mm free length and maximum displacement at the edge of 450 μm, model PB4NB2S, ThorLabs). The bender was deflected by 200 μm for 1 ms to stimulate the whisker. The movement was too small to see with naked eyes, but it was sufficient to produce robust LFP’s. LFP’s were recorded with a Ag-AgCl electrode on the epidural surface. The potential sensed by the electrode was amplified using an extracellular amplifier (Neurodata ER98) with a filter bandwidth of 0.1 Hz to 3000 Hz and a gain of 1000. The amplified signal was digitized at 10 kHz using a National Instrument data acquisition system and was analyzed using an in-house acquisition program written in the LabView environment.

FUS stimulation of the barrel cortex and recording of evoked activity

In most of the first series of experiments, the barrel column receiving projection from whisker C2 was stimulated with a focal beam of 4 MHz FUS using an in-house transducer (Fig. 1B). The FUS design and characteristics of the transducer used in this study were described in detail in our previous publication (Tripathi et al., 2020). Briefly, the transducer consisted of a rectangular piezoelectric bar (Del Piezo Specialties, DL-47 4 MHz) encased in an electromagnetically shielded brass casing. The piezo plate was glued to a ¼ wave filter for increasing the bandwidth in order to use short bursts as short as 5-6 μs. The rise time of the pressure profile was very short, the pressure reaching the maximum in 2 μs. The filter was glued to a plastic non-conducting 7-mm diameter rod (Rexolite, Sand Diego Plastics, National City, CA, USA). The tip of the rod was spherically concave filled with silicon rubber (Wacker Elastosil RT601) that acted as a lens to focus the ultrasound beam. A 200-μm tip hydrophone (Onda HNC-200, Onda) in a water tank was used to calibrate the transducer and define the transverse and longitudinal pressure profiles. The transducer produced a narrow focal beam of acoustic pressure with a diameter of 400-μm on the transverse plane and 1 mm along the radial axis. The maximum pressure was at a depth of 2.7 mm below the transducer. The maximal pressure zone (>3 MPa) extended over approximately 1 mm, while the secondary pressure zone (>1.5 MPa) extended over 2 mm.

The delivery of each FUS burst was controlled by a data acquisition computer. Our custom-made program in LabView delivered a trigger to an event controller (Master 8, A.M.P.I.) to start each epoch of stimulation and recording. This controller sent a trigger to a function generator (Keysight 33500, Keysight Technologies, Santa Rosa, CA, USA). The output of the function generator was sent to a power amplifier (E&I 2100 L, 50 dB, Electronic Navigation Industries, Inc., Rochester, NY, USA), which activated the piezoelectric FUS transducer.

The energy delivered by each burst of FUS is an important parameter for evaluating possible tissue heating and safety. It was calculated using the quantity called Ispta (spatial peak temporal average intensity). Ispta is the average energy over time delivered during each trial by a single burst of 4 MHz FUS for 50 μs every 1.6 s. We used a very short duration in order to obtain a low duty cycle. We calculated Ispta as equal to Isptp*duty cycle (Cullion et al., 2018), where Isptp (= ΔP2/(2ρc), ΔP = peak negative pressure PNP of the FUS burst in Pascal, ρ = material density (1080 kg/m3 for brain), c = propagation velocity of ultrasound in brain (1560 m/s), and duty cycle is 50 μs/1.6 s = 0.003125%). For underated PNP = 3.97 MPa, Ispta = 468 W/cm2*duty cycle = 14.6 mW/cm2. Due to the small duty cycles, the average energy level was 2% of the maximum safe value of 720 mW/cm2, according to the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) standard for ultrasound imaging (Center for Devices and Radiological Health, 2017). Thus, we do not expect any tissue damage due to tissue heating.

We also computed the mechanical index (MI) of the pressure at a specific depth in the cortex using the derated pressure based on the formula of 0.3 dB/MHz/cm. MI is equal to the peak negative pressure (PNP) of the FUS divided by a square root of FUS frequency f, i.e. MI = PNP/√f. This parameter is used to assess possible cavitation of cell membrane. The maximum safe value of MI for ultrasound imaging set by the FDA is 1.9. The maximum PNP used in this study was 3.84 MPa, with MI = 1.9.

The transducer was placed vertically over the left barrel cortex with its center positioned over the projection area of whisker C2. The space between the transducer and the cortical surface was filled with either saline or ultrasound gel in order for the ultrasound pressure to go into the cortex without unwanted reflections. The bottom of the transducer did not touch the exposed cortical surface so as not to put pressure on the brain, which was important for not irritating the brain and maintaining the shape of the brain throughout each experiment. The linear beam of FUS pressure activated the deeper layers of the barrel cortex. Stimulations were carried out using a continuous 4 MHz FUS burst at a duration of 50 μs in most of the experiments. The duration was varied between for 5 μs and 1000 μs in one experiment. In some experiments, FUS-evoked LFP’s were recorded using the same Ag-AgCl electrode used for whisker response recordings. In other experiments, the LFP’s were recorded with a sharpened tungsten electrode (AM-Systems) placed in the cortex.

We found two important features that significantly reduce the FUS-evoked stimulus artifact. (1) The tip of the recording electrode on the dura or inside the cortex was always outside of the FUS beam in order to avoid recording the vibration artifact that can be produced immediately after the stimulation when the tip is inside the beam of acoustic pressure. (2) The artifact can be quite strong and long lasting if the burst duration is longer because the artifact at the onset and offset of the burst do not mutually cancel. By reducing the duration, the mutual cancellation increases. In addition the net artifact produced by the on and off artifacts will contain frequencies higher than the upper frequency cutoff of the recording apparatus as the duration is made shorter. The sum of the onset and offset artifacts should have a frequency content in the range above the high cutoff frequency in order to obtain a brief and small artifact. These two considerations enabled us to obtain a very short artifact of <1.0 ms using a burst duration of 505 μs.

The depth profile analysis was carried out by inserting the tungsten electrode from the left lateral side into the brain after cutting the dura with a pair of scissors. The electrode was positioned horizontally and the tip was moved to the locations along the depth of the cortex where the LFP was to be measured. As mentioned above, this location was outside of the pressure beam to avoid artifacts.

In a follow-up study, we tested whether the cascade of events produced by the transducer placed above the exposed dura can be also elicited transcranially with the transducer placed on the skull or scalp. In this study, we first used the same 4 MHz transducer as in the first series of experiments for comparison. This transducer was placed on the skull with the recording electrode (silver wire) inserted into the slit in the lateral temporal bone in order to record LFPs on the dura or on the skull just above the slit. The scalp was then placed back on the skull, the transducer was lowered to touch the scalp and the LFPs were recorded on the skull or scalp.

We also used an in-house 2.2 MHz transducer since FUS penetrates the skull more easily at lower frequencies. This 2.2 MHz transducer was the same as the one we have used in our in vitro studies (Yu et al., 2022; 2023). The piezo element was conical in shape with a conically shaped Rexolite as acoustic guide to focus the beam at 6.7 mm below the bottom of the guide. The transducer was calibrated with a 400-um diameter tip hydrophone (Onda HNC-400, Onda). We used a pressure of 1.2 MPa, which has a mechanical index (MI = 0.81) below the safety limit of 1.9. The value of Ispta was 1.3 mW/cm2, again well below the maximum safe energy level. The FWHM of the pressure intensity was 1.1 mm in the transverse plane and 5.0 mm in the longitudinal plane. The rise time of the pressure wave was 4 μs. The transducer was placed on a plastic tube of 4-5 mm in height filled with ultrasound gel in order to place the focal area of the FUS beam onto the target region in the barrel cortex.

This 2.2 MHz transducer was placed on the skull and LFPs were measured on the dura. Then, the transducer was placed on the scalp and LFPs were recorded on the dura or scalp. The scalp was then flipped back again, the transducer placed on the skull and LFPs were recorded on the dura to ascertain that changes in the LFPs measured with the transducer on the scalp were not due to changes in physiological conditions of the animal.

In all the FUS experiments, the head was rotated by 15 degrees counter-clockwise, seen from the front of the animal, in order to place the FUS transducer as radially as possible to the target area of the barrel cortex, but with the FUS beam missing the thalamus entirely.

Data analysis

The FUS-evoked LFPs were analyzed by first averaging the raw data over 100 or 200 epochs of stimulation, in most cases 200 epochs. These waveforms were filtered with an analog filter between 0.1 Hz and 3000 Hz with a gain of 1000. To analyze the short-latency responses (Spike 1-3 and W1), we applied a digital high-pass FIR (Finite Impulse Response) filter provided by the Labview to eliminate the distortion of W1 due to the overlap between W1 and the rising slope of W2. In the FIR highpass filter with a Hanning window, the passband frequency is the frequency above which the signal is passed unattenuated and the stopband frequency is the frequency below which the signal is attenuated by 44 dB. Narrowband 30-3000 Hz signals were constructed by passing the analog filter through a highpass FIR filter with a passband frequency of 30 Hz and a stopband frequency of 20 Hz. The attenuation by 44 dB at 20 Hz is comparable to the attenuation by an analog filter with an amplitude roll-off of 48 dB/octave. The 30-3000 Hz highpass filter was found to provide the best compromise for eliminating this distortion without reducing the amplitudes of Spike 1-3 and W1 by the filtering. We report the raw data with a bandpass filter of 0.1-3000 Hz as wideband waveforms and the high-pass filtered waveforms as narrowband waveforms with a bandwidth of 30 – 3000 Hz since the analog signals were bandpassed with an upper cutoff frequency of 3000 Hz. In some cases, we computed the multi-unit activity (MUA) signals in order to visualize the action potentials produced by a population of synchronized neuronal activity. The MUA was calculated by first filtering the raw data with a highpass FIR filter of 300 Hz (passband 300 Hz and stopband 200 Hz), then rectifying these waveforms and averaging them. We refer to these signals as MUA waveforms with a banwidth of 300 – 3000 Hz.

The statistical analyses were carried out using repeated-measures within-subject ANOVA with 2 or 3 factors, animals as replication and Student’s t test for independent samples or paired comparisons. We used the 1-tailed t test when we had a clear hypothesis about the direction of the difference, whereas we used the 2-tailed t test when we wanted to know if the difference was statistically different from zero regardless of sign of the difference.

Results

FUS Evokes short-latency responses in the rat barrel cortex

Focused ultrasound (FUS) stimulation of the barrel column in the rat cortex evoked novel short-latency electrophysiological responses in vivo (Fig. 1C, red trace). The LFPs were recorded on the epidural surface with an electrode tip 3 mm laterally away from the center of the transducer tip in order to avoid vibration artifacts from the FUS beam. The FUS-evoked LFPs under α-chloralose consisted of a spike train with 2-4 spikes with short peak latencies (1-5 ms), followed by the first wave (W1) (peak latency 8-12 ms) and the second wave (W2) (peak latency 20-30 ms). The spikes and W1 were not seen in our previous study under ketamine/xylazine (Tripathi et al., 2020), even though the same transducer was used and the maximum pressures were comparable. The spikes and W1 were not seen in the LFP evoked by mechanical stimulation of whisker C2 under α-chloralose (Fig. 1C, black trace), using a 1-ms, 200-μm movement of C2 made by the piezoelectric actuator described in Methods.

We attribute the appearance of these new components to the use of α-chloralose as anesthetic. As mentioned above, this agent enhances GABAA receptor activity. Under this anesthetic, W2 was much stronger (as much as 170 μV) than under ketamine/xylazine (<25 μV in most cases in the previous study reported by Tripathi et al (2020)). Under α-chloralose, this component was strong enough sometimes to be observable in real time. It was often comparable in amplitude to the W2-like response evoked by mechanical whisker stimulation, demonstrating the enhanced excitatory response facilitated by α-chloralose. The mean amplitude of W2 in 9 rats was 126 μV with a standard deviation of 24 μV for mechanically evoked LFPs and a mean of 98 μV with a standard deviation of 28 μV for the LFPs evoked by FUS in an independent group of 9 rats. The mean for the FUS-evoked W2 was 78% of the mean for the mechanically evoked LFP. The mean amplitude of W2 was were statistically stronger for the whisker-evoked responses compared to the FUS-evoked LFPs, according to an independent sample Student 1-tailed t test (t= 2.257, df = 16), which was significant at the p=0.02 level. However, the FUS-evoked W2 amplitude was within one standard deviation of its variability (28 μV). In comparison, the maximum amplitude of 25 uV seen under ketamine/xylazine in Tripathi et al (2020) study is more than 2SD’s below the mean of the FUS-evoked W2 under α-chloralose. We suggest that this enhanced population activity provided by this anesthetic explains the appearance of the short-latency spike train and W1, which are all novel.

Burst repetition effects on the LFP components

To investigate the physiological processes underlying the novel LFP components, we used a paired-pulse paradigm to assess the influence of inhibition on neuronal responses (Fig. 2). In this paradigm, a conditioning stimulus (CS) excites neurons within an excitatory/inhibitory network, leading to a subsequent inhibitory response, schematically shown in Fig. 2A (Waldeck et al., 2000; Waldbaum and Dudek, 2009; Boulogne et al., 2016). The exact waveform of inhibition depends on relative contributions of GABAA and GABAB receptors. The fast inhibitory response, mediated by GABAA receptors, peaks at 10-50 ms after CS (McCormick, 1989; Davies et al., 1990), while the slower inhibitory response, mediated by GABAB receptors, peaks at 100-300 ms (McCormick, 1989; Davies et al., 1990). We modified this paradigm to examine the effects of FUS burst repetition on the LFP components. A train of four 50 μs FUS bursts was presented with Inter-Burst Intervals (IBIs) of 100, 200, and 500 ms, every 2 seconds. Fig. 2B shows the averaged LFPs across 200 epochs (0.1-3000 Hz bandwidth) for the three IBIs. There were no significant changes in the shape or amplitude of the spikes or W1 across all three IBIs. However, the amplitude of W2 significantly decreased with FUS repetition at IBIs of 100 and 200 ms, with minimal effect observed at IBI 500 ms. Fig. 2C quantifies the effects for W2 amplitude (n = 6 animals), showing a 70-80% reduction at IBI = 100 ms and a 55-65% reduction at IBI = 200 ms. Statistical analysis using repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant effects of both IBI and FUS repetition as well as their interaction (p < 0.001; IBI – F(df = 2/10) = 88, FUS – F(df = 3/15) = 72, IBI x FUS – F(df = 6/30) = 20). Further post hoc analysis with a Student’s t-test indicated that the amplitudes of LFPs evoked by FUS 2, 3, and 4 were significantly smaller than those evoked by FUS 1 at p < 0.001 (t = 10, 19, 19 for FUS 2, 3, and 4 relative to FUS 1, df = 5).

In summary, while the Spikes and W1 remained largely unaffected by FUS repetition, the amplitude of W2 was markedly reduced by 50-80% at IBI = 100 and 200 ms. This attenuation peaked around 100-200 ms after the initial FUS pulse, corresponding to the range of IBIs where GABAB-mediated inhibition is maximum in a paired pulse paradigm (McCormick, 1989; Davies et al., 1990). Thus, we infer that the W2 evoked by a FUS may activate slow GABAB-type inhibitory processes.

Effects of burst repetition on short-latency components: spikes and W1

We used the modified paired-pulse paradigm to assess the inhibitory modulation of the Spikes and W1. A shorter IBI range was used, as these components were not affected by IBIs between 100 and 500 ms. W1 sometimes overlapped with the rising slope of W2 (Fig. 3A, top), complicating accurate amplitude estimation. To resolve this, we filtered the averaged waveforms between 30 and 3000 Hz (Fig. 3A, bottom).

Narrowband waveforms were examined as a function of FUS repetition across different IBIs. Fig. 3B shows the initial 10 ms segment of the LFPs for bursts 1-5, separately for three IBIs. No clear decrease in Spike 1 amplitude was observed, but Spike 2 and 3 as well as W1 showed reduced amplitudes with FUS repetition. These reductions are illustrated in Fig. 3C. The response amplitude for FUS1 was normalized to 1.0 and the responses to the subsequent FUS bursts were expressed relative to FUS 1 amplitude. Response patterns were similar across all three values of IBI (three-factor repeated-measures ANOVA: F (df = 2/8) = 1.03, p > 0.05). Although the small reduction in Spike 1 amplitude with FUS repetition (4-12% for FUS 2-5 relative to FUS1) was statistically significant, the decrease was minimal (Student’s t: 3.6-18.2, df = 4, p < 0.05). In contrast, Spike 2 and 3 as well as W1 exhibited clear amplitude reductions. The amplitude decrease was most pronounced for FUS 3 at IBI = 10 ms (Student’s t (df = 5): p < 0.05 for the FUS2-FUS3 and FUS3-FUS4 pair-wise comparisons) with a significant difference between FUS 2 and FUS 3 at IBI = 15 ms (p > 0.05). However, no significant difference was found between FUS 2 and 3 at IBI = 20 ms (p > 0.05).

In summary, while Spike 1 showed minimal attenuation with FUS repetition, Spikes 2 and 3 and W1 were significantly suppressed with the greatest suppression occurring 15-20 ms after FUS1. According to the previous studies (McCormick, 1989; Davies et al., 1990), this range of IBI at which the suppression was maximum suggests that Spike 2 and 3 as well as W1 may have evoked fast GABAA-type inhibition.

All LFP Components originate from neuronal activity

To confirm the neuronal origin of all the components, we locally injected tetrodotoxin (TTX), which blocks voltage-sensitive sodium channels, in the region of strongest FUS pressure (Fig. 4A) (n = 3 animals). Prior to TTX injection, a 50-μs FUS burst was applied as shown in Fig. 4A. Evoked LFPs were recorded at location shown in Fig. 4A (“rec”), away from the FUS beam to avoid FUS-related vibration artifacts. The top trace in Fig. 4B (“Control”) shows the measured wideband and narrowband waveforms. The wideband signal was dominated by W2, which was negative at this depth in this experiment. The narrowband signal clearly shows Spikes 1-3, followed by W1. TTX was then injected in the center of the beam as shown in Fig. 4A, first at location 1 and then subsequently at location 2. A 8-μL bolus of 5 μM TTX was injected into location 1 at a depth of 3 mm below the transducer acoustic guide over a period of 2 min. The 2-mm diameter circle indicates the region of tissue within which TTX was expected to diffuse. Then, we measured the LFP labeled as TTX 1, 30 min after the injection. The first injection reduced the spikes and W1, but it did not abolish these components completely. The same 8-μL bolus of 5 μM TTX was thus injected into location 2 at a depth of 1 mm over 2 min. Since these two injections covered the entire region of strong FUS pressure, we expected the LFP to completely disappear as observed if all the components were neuronal in origin. To confirm that the flat response was not some type of non-neuronal artifact such as due to some changes in physiological condition of the animal, we measured the LFP during the washout period. There was a partial recovery 2 hrs after the last post-TTX LFP measurement (“Washout”), showing that all components were neuronal in origin.

Pre- and postsynaptic spikes

The results in Fig. 3 suggest that Spike 1 is presynaptic, while Spike 2 and 3 as well as W1 are postsynaptic. To validate these hypotheses, we carried out an experiment using a non-specific blocker of excitatory amino acid receptor activation (kynurenic acid) in four rats (n = 4 animals). In the control condition, the same region as in the TTX experiments was stimulated and the evoked LFPs were recorded at the same location as in the TTX experiments. The top traces in Fig. 4C (“Control”) shows the wideband, narrowband and MUA waveforms obtained in the control condition. The fast components are not clearly visible in the wideband signal at this scale, but they are clear in the narrowband signal and MUA. The narrowband waveform in Control shows a triphasic Spike 1 at this recording location, followed by more spikes and the beginning of W1. The MUA shows an initial strong activity followed by additional spikes with no MUA during W1.

After this control recording, a bolus of kynurenic acid (Kyna, 4 μL, 20 mM) was injected into the same two locations as in the TTX experiments, first at location 1, followed by injection at location 2, using the same injection protocol as in the TTX experiments. The injection volume was reduced to 4 μL to evaluate whether this dose sufficed to abolish the LFP. The waveforms in the second row of Fig. 4C (“Kyna”) show the LFPs measured after the second Kyna injection. Subsequently, TTX (4 μL, 5 μM) was injected into location 2, then in location 1 following the same injection protocol. The waveforms in the third row (“TTX”) were obtained after the second TTX injection.

Since kynurenic acid is expected to block excitatory amino acid synaptic transmissions, we predicted that Spike 1 should be insensitive to Kyna if it was presynaptic, whereas Spikes 2 and 3 and W1 should be eliminated by Kyna if they were postsynaptic. In Kyna, the LFP recording obtained 30 min after the second Kyna injection shows a strong triphasic Spike 1, but the subsequent spikes and W1 are reduced in the narrowband waveform. The MUA clearly shows that Kyna did not reduce Spike 1 amplitude, but it did nearly eliminate the subsequent spikes and W1. The “TTX” traces obtained 30 min after the second TTX injection replicate the TTX-only study in Fig. 4B, showing that all the components were eliminated after TTX injection. The traces in Kyna and Control conditions are superimposed at bottom to demonstrate that Kyna did not affect Spike 1, but eliminated the subsequent components.

We carried out a statistical test in the data obtained from 4 rats (n = 4 animals) to see if spike 1 was insensitive to Kyna, whereas spike 2 and 3 are. The mean and standard deviation of spike 1 were 1.95±1.27 μV in Control and 1.90±1.24 μV in Kyna. This difference between Control and Kyna was not statistically significant at p=0.05 according to a paired 2-tail Student t test (t = 2.1, df = 3). The mean and standard deviation for spike 2 were 0.97±0.61 μV in control and 0.47±0.46 μV in Kyna. The mean and standard deviation for spike 3 were 0.92±0.22 μV in control and 0.22±0.11 μV in Kyna. These differences between Control and Kyna were statistically significant at p=0.05 for both spike 2 and 3, according to a paired 2-tail Student t test (spike 2: t = 3.85, df = 3; spike 3: t = 12.58, df = 3). These results support our conclusion that spike 1 is presynaptic, while spike 2 and 3, along with W1, are postsynaptic.

Generation sites of the pre- and postsynaptic spikes

To identify the generator locations of Spikes 1, 2, and 3, we conducted a laminar depth profile analysis. A single sharp-tip tungsten electrode was inserted horizontally from the lateral side of the cortical surface with the animal’s brain tilted by 15° from the horizontal orientation to direct the FUS beam away from the thalamus. This setup ensured that the FUS beam remained confined to the cortex and underlying white matter (Fig. 5A). The tungsten electrode tip was 1.0 mm away from the FUS beam’s center to avoid vibration artifacts that could interfere with short-latency measurements.

Fig. 5B shows the depth profile of the wideband LFP obtained from one animal. The spike train is not clear with this vertical scale. W2 appeared positive superficially and negatively deeper in the cortex with its polarity reversing at a depth between 1400 and 1600 um below the cortical surface. Since the recording electrode track was oblique to the radial axis of the cortical layers, their radial depths from the cortical surface along the line perpendicular to the overlying cortical tangential surface are between 991 and 1132 um, which corresponds to layer IV for whisker C2 according to Meyer et al (2013). Fig. 5C shows the corresponding narrowband signals. The vertical dashed lines indicate the positive peak of the three spikes at the cortical surface (Spike 1-3 latencies = 1.0, 2.2, 3.0 ms). The waveform was monophasic with a positive peak in the superficial region and a negative peak in deeper regions at the same latencies. The waveforms became triphasic between 800 and 1000 um for Spike 1 and between 1400 and 1600 um for Spike 2 and 3.

Fig. 5D shows the depth profiles for all rats to demonstrate reproducibility across subjects. The dots represent potentials from individual animals, while the solid curve represents the group average for each spike. The average location of polarity reversal for Spike 1 occurred at 963±23 μm (mean±SEM, n = 5 rats), and those for Spikes 2 and 3 were at 1604±67 μm and 1624±38 μm (n = 5 rats), respectively. The location for Spike 1 was different from the location of Spike 2 according to 2-tail, paired Student t test (t=8.89, df = 4, p=0.001). The location for Spike 2 and 3 were not significantly different (t=0.41, df = 4, p=0.701).

Their radial depths from the cortical surface along the line perpendicular to the overlying cortical tangential surface were 703 μm for Spike 1 and 1172 μm for Spike 2 and 1186 μm for Spike 3. The depth of 703 μm is in layer IV and the depth of 1172-1186 μm are in layer V for whisker C2 according to Meyer et al. (2013).

The genesis of Spike 1-3 can be inferred from the depth profiles. The profile for Spike 1 was dipolar with the positive potential toward the brain surface and negative potentials below with the polarity reversal in layer IV, where the thalamocortical fibers terminate. The quadrupolar potential profile produced by action potentials in the axons in deeper layers transforms into a dipolar profile as the action potentials reach the axonal terminals. The measured profile suggests that Spike 1 was produced by an equivalent current dipole in the axonal terminals of the thalamocortical fibers. The FUS could have also activated axons of pyramidal cells in layer V and VI, antidromically producing Spike 1. However, it is unlikely that these axons generated Spike 1 since the polarity reversal was seen in layer IV instead of layer V or VI. Spikes 2 and 3 were produced postsynaptically according to the Kyna results. The postsynaptic origin rules out the possibility that they were produced antidromically by activation of pyramidal cell axons in layer V or VI. Their peak latencies were 1.2 to 2.0 ms longer, respectively, than the peak latency of Spike 1. These latencies suggest that they were generated by synaptic activation of pyramidal cells in layer V via presynaptic activation of the thalamocortical axons in layer IV.

Temporal precision of activation

The data in Fig. 5 demonstrate that FUS can reliably generate Spike 1 with a peak latency around 1.0 ms. Since the beam duration was always 50 μs, it was ambiguous whether the spike was precisely timely locked to the onset, rather than the offset, of the FUS beam. To clarify whether the spike was time locked to the onset of the FUS beam, the duration of a single continuous burst of FUS was varied over a range of 5 to 1000 μs. The 4 MHz transducer was capable of producing full steady-state pressure for durations as short as 5-10 μs since the pressure rise time was approximately 2 μs (Fig. 6A).

Fig. 6B shows waveforms obtained from one animal in two replications, all of which contained Spike 1, 2 and 3, along with W1. Importantly, the peak latency remained independent of the burst duration, indicating that Spike 1 was produced by FUS onset. Spike 2 and 3 as well as W1 appeared to be synaptically driven by Spike 1 since their latencies were time-locked to the onset of Spike 1. The peak latency was 0.98 and 1.00 ms for the two replications, consistent with the values observed in Fig. 5C. In another set of 5 animals, the mean peak latency of Spike 1 was 1.13 ±0.15 ms (SEM) for a 50-μs burst (derated PNP = 3.8 MPa). These findings confirm that the onset of FUS burst can produce presynaptic spikes with a small jitter of 0.15 ms, indicating the temporal precision of neural activation.

The threshold durations for producing Spike 1-3 and W1 were determined by a linear regression fit to the data over the range of durations where these components were visible. The duration at which the regression line crossed zero amplitude was determined for all 5 animals. They were 7.43±0.09 μs (mean±SEM) for Spike 1, 7.67±0.21 μs for Spike 2, 7.85±0.48 μs for Spike 3, and 7.59±0.50 μs for W1. These threshold durations were statistically the same for all the spikes (ANOVA, F (df=3/12) = 0.254, p==0.857). Thus, The range of 7.4-7.9 μs is the threshold duration for activating these short latency responses.

Pressure needed for activating cortical axons

We investigated the pressure range required to activate cortical spikes since this information is essential for extending our work to transcranial activation of cortical neurons. Fig. 6C shows the LFP’s produced by a single 50-μs burst of 4 MHz FUS as a function of derated PNP for two out of five animals studied. Spike 1, 2 and 3, as well as W1, were clearly visible at pressures around 1.9 MPa, nearly half of the maximum safe pressure. No spikes were detectable below 1.5 MPa across 5 animals. The data relating the LFP amplitude to the pressure were fitted with a line using a linear regression analysis and the threshold pressure was found for Spike 1, 2 and 3 in 5 animals. They were 2.04±0.6 MPa, 1.37±0.15 MPa, and 1.37±0.15 MPa for these spikes, respectively. These values are consistent with in vitro results. Action potentials can be elicited in single crayfish axons in vitro at pressures above 1 MPa (Lin et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2022, 2023) and at 1 MPa in primary cultured rat neurons (Vassan et al., 2022).

Transcranial activation

We tested whether FUS could transcranially activate all the components that were observed with direct brain surface stimulation. This is a critical step for using FUS as a non-invasive stimulation method for human functional brain mapping. In this series of experiments, an FUS transducer was placed on the exposed skull and the LFPs were measured on the dura or skull (Fig. 7, left column), then on the scalp and LFPs measured on the dura, skull or scalp (Fig. 7, middle column), and again on the skull and LFPs measured on dura (Fig. 7, right column).

This study was at first carried out with the same 4.0 MHz transducer used in the main series for comparison. Since the focal area of strong pressure was 2.7 mm below the acoustic guide, the transducer tip was placed directly on the skull or scalp as before after coating the surface with ultrasound gel. Fig. 7B shows the data for one animal. We were able to detect all the components observed with direct brain surface stimulation, including the spikes, when the transducer was placed transcranially over the skull and scalp. The amplitudes (n = 3) were 3.3±1.2 (mean±SEM) for Spike 1, 5.2±1.5 for Spike 2 and 19.0±0.5 μV for W2 in the skull-dura condition in which FUS was placed on skull and recording electrode was placed on dura. The mean amplitudes were 2.8±0.4 (Spike 1), 5.0±0.5 (Spike 2) and 14.0±2.5 μV (W2) for the skull-skull condition (n = 4) in which FUS was on skull and recording was on skull. The amplitudes were 2.6±0.8, 2.3±0.6, 6.7±0.9 μV for FUS on scalp and recording on skull (n = 5), and 3.4±0.1, 3.2±0.0, and 3.8±0.5 μV for FUS on scalp and recording on scalp (n = 2). The amplitudes were generally lower when the transducer was placed on the scalp, but they were all clearly above the prestimulus values indicating that FUS was capable of transcranially activating Spike 1-3, W1 and W2, even at 4 MHz.

We repeated this study using the 2.1 MHz transducer since FUS can penetrate the skull more easily at lower frequencies. Since the pressure was maximum 6.7 mm below the bottom of the acoustic guide for this transducer, the acoustic guide was positioned above a 4 mm high plastic tube filled with ultrasound gel placed on the skull or above a 3 mm tall tube for the stimulation above the scalp in order to optimally target the same cortical layers as for the 4 MHz transducer. The waveforms from one animal are shown in Fig. 7C. The LFPs including W2 are shown in the upper panel and Spikes 1-3 are shown expanded in the lower panel with a shorter time scale. The spike train and W2 were clearly detectable with the transducer above the skull or scalp and the recording electrode on the dura or scalp. The amplitudes of Spikes 1 and 2 and W2 averaged over 6 replications across 3 animals were 1.8±0.3, 3.2±0.5, 72.2±13.1 μV, respectively, for FUS on skull and recording on dura, 1.1±0.1, 1.6±0.4, 11.1±2.4 μV for FUS on scalp and recording on scalp and 2.3±0.3, 4.6±0.8, 74.2±16.1 μV for FUS again on skull and recording on dura. Their amplitudes were reduced when FUS was placed above the scalp, but they were all clearly above the prestimulus noise level as was the case for the 4 MHz transducer. The means and SEM’s of the LFP with the 2.2 MHz transducer above the scalp and EEG recording electrode above the scalp were 1.07±0.15 μV, 1.60±0.40 μV, 11.08±2.64 μV for Spike 1, Spike 2 and W2, respectively. These values were all significantly larger than zero at the o.oo5 level according to a 1-tail Student t test (t =7.26 (df = 5) for spike 1, t =4.04 (df = 5) for spike 2, and t =4.2 (df = 5) for W2). Thus, we can conclude that a FUS-EEG system could evoke neuronal responses with the FUS transducer placed transcranially and the recording electrode placed noninvasively on the scalp.

In summary, these results demonstrate that FUS at 2.2 MHz and 4 MHz can transcranially elicit all the components including Spike 1-3 and W2 that were present when FUS was applied over the exposed dural surface of the barrel cortex. Importantly, these responses were detectable noninvasively from the scalp as well as from the skull and dura, highlighting the potential of transcranial FUS for non-invasive brain stimulation and functional mapping applications.

Discussion

Characteristics of FUS-evoked short-latency cortical activity

This study revealed a cascade of electrophysiological activity in the cortex that can be elicited non-invasively by FUS starting immediately after stimulation. We found FUS parameters that produced a very short FUS artifact lasting less than 1 ms, enabling us to detect the possible activation of neurons with no delay. The sequence of events started with a spike (Spike 1) produced directly by FUS in layer IV with a peak latency of ~1.0 ms with a small jitter of 0.2 ms, which we identified as presynaptic. It was followed by a postsynaptic spike train of which the first two spikes (Spike 2 and 3) produced in layer V with peak latencies of ~2 and ~3 ms were most clear. These spikes were quite small in amplitude (2-5 μV) and thus they may appear insignificant or negligible, but their presence is very significant since they show for the first time that FUS can activate neurons immediately after stimulation. The peak latency of Spike 1 is much shorter than in previous studies. In the primary motor cortex of a mouse in vivo it was 27 ms (Tufail et al., 2010). The latency of the initial cortical spike was 16-35 ms in guinea pig in vivo (Guo et al., 2018). The onset latency in our previous study was <7 ms (Tripathi et al., 2020), much shorter than those in earlier studies, but still quite long since many events can take place within the first several milliseconds. The precisely time-locked activation with little delay enables the study of temporal dynamics of network activation with a millisecond resolution. They were followed by an excitatory wave (W1). The synaptically activated spikes and W1 were both modulated by fast inhibition with a maximum suppression at the characteristic latency between 15-20 ms. This range of IBI matches the range of inter-pulse intervals for maximum inhibition in a paired-pulse study found for GABAA-type inhibition (McCormick, 1989; Davies et al., 1990). They were followed by a major excitatory wave (W2), strong enough to be observed in real time during some epochs, that was modulated by a slow type of inhibition with a maximum suppression at the characteristic latency between 100-200 ms. This range of IBI with maximum suppression corresponds to the values of maximum inhibition mediated by the GABAB-type inhibition (McCormick, 1989; Davies et al., 1990). We thus hypothesize that a GABAA-type inhibitory network was activated by the short-latency spikes and W1 and a GABAB-type inhibitory network was activated by W2.

We were able to elicit all the components transcranially above the skull and scalp with a 2.2 MHz and a 4 MHz transducer using pressures within the safe range and record them with an electrode on the dura, skull and non-invasively on the scalp. Thus, it is possible to activate a cascade of activity starting immediately after FUS stimulation and record the activity non-invasively with scalp EEG electrode without interference from FUS stimulation.

In our view, there are three candidate mechanisms underlying Spike 1 based on this work and related earlier studies by our group and others: (1) radiational force modulating the membrane capacitance Cm, which produces capacitive current, which in turn depolarizes the membrane potential Vm, (2) radiation force activating a mechanosensitive receptor, thereby depolarizing Vm, and (3) FUS opening nanopores in the phospholipid membrane bilayer and depolarizing Vm. Our in vitro study of crayfish motor axon preparation (Lin et al., 2019; Yu et al, 2022, 2023) indicates that the mechanism of activation of a single axon depends on acoustic pressure. When the pressure is relatively weak (0.1-0.6 MPa), a burst of FUS always hyperpolarizes the axon (Yu et al., 2023). The hyperpolarization may be based on reduction in Cm due to rectified increase in the gap in the lipid bilayer produced by the cavitation force (Plaksin et al., 2014; Lemaire et al., 2021). The reduction in Cm leads to membrane hyperpolarization when the capacitive charges remain constant. As the pressure of the burst is gradually increased, the axon starts showing depolarization when the pressure reaches around 0.6 MPa. As the pressure is raised further, the depolarization leads to a single action potential or a train of spikes (Lin et al., 2019). In our in vivo study, Spike 1 was elicited by pressures above 1.5 MPa for the 4 MHz transducer. The pressure used for spike activation was 1.2 MPa for the 2.1 MHz transducer, although we did not determine the threshold for activating Spike 1 for this latter transducer. In a study by another group using cultured neurons, an action potential could be elicited with a pressure of 1 MPa (Vasan et al., 2022). Taken all together, it is possible that the radiation force depolarizes and activates action potentials when the FUS pressure is higher than about 1 MPa. The peak latency of Spike 1 was time-locked to the onset of FUS burst. We suppose that the radiation force produced at the onset of FUS appears to generate Spike 1. According to Mechanism 1, the radiation force could produce capacitive current strong enough to depolarize the axons (Prieto et al., 2013; Vassan et al., 2022). According to Mechanism 2, the radiation force could mechanically shake the membranes of the axons, thereby activating a mechanosensitive channel such as Piezo 1 (Coste et al., 2012; Zhu et al., 2023), which is known to be present in the cortex and is capable of producing depolarization with a latency of around 1-2 ms (Zeitchel and Lechner, 2024). As the third possibility, the cavitation or radiation force could open nanopores in the lipid membrane with a latency of <1 ms as found in the crayfish axons (Lin et al. 2019), thereby depolarizing Vm. These possibilities need to be experimentally evaluated in future studies.

Both the postsynaptic spikes generated in layer V and the excitatory wave W1 were modulated by the same type of fast inhibitory network since they were all maximally suppressed at 15-20 ms after the initial FUS stimulation. We could not identify the underlying network. Nevertheless, this property may be useful in neuromodulation studies since it specifies the optimal time window for neuromodulation involving the fast inhibitory network.

In contrast to the postsynaptic spikes and W1, the major excitatory wave W2 activated a slow inhibitory network since it was maximally suppressed at 100-200 ms after the FUS stimulation. Again, we could not identify the specific network involved in generating W2, but this property may be practically useful for neuromodulation selectively involving the slow inhibitory network.

Development of activation-informed neuroimaging based on the FUS-EEG approach

The characteristics we have identified in this study are useful for advancing neuroimaging in several ways. In a simple and straightforward way, this approach is useful for identifying the electrophysiology of each local circuit since EEG signals can be referred to known locations in the brain. Since FUS activates both excitatory and inhibitory networks, it can be used to identify the excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance in local circuits as well as oscillatory dynamics, which depend on the E/I balance (Buzsaki and Wang, 2012). The postsynaptic spikes and W1 could be used to evaluate the E/I balance involving GABAA-type inhibition and W2 could be used to evaluate the E/I balance involving GABAB-type inhibition. Each target region can be stimulated repetitively in a repetitive FUS (rFUS) mode analogous to rTMS to determine the oscillatory dynamics, for example in the gamma range.

This simple mode of application can be also used to identify functions of long-range neural circuits if their locations are known in advance. In this case, each node of the network can be stimulated with FUS and the reciprocal connectivity can be identified by measuring EEG and using this information to image activity in each node. The highly precise temporal pattern of activity we found is essential for studying the propagation with a millisecond time scale. Since the locations are known in advance, their locations can be used to constrain the inverse solutions, enabling this approach to identify functions of networks of interest without the ambiguity associated with the conventional EEG source estimation (Ilmoniemi and Sarvas, 2019). It may be thus useful for studying the electrophysiology of brain-wide circuits, for example by using the phenomenon of cross-frequency coupling (Palva et al. 2005; Canolty and Knight, 2010; Hyafil et al., 2015).

This approach can be combined with conventional neuroimaging approaches to enhance the power of neuroimaging. In the conventional approaches, regions of the brain are activated with an external stimulus or with a task. Due to non-uniqueness in their inverse solutions, imaging of the brain activity using MEG or EEG is difficult when the spatial distribution of activity is complex and involves subcortical regions and deep regions in the cortex outside of sensitivity of these techniques. This FUS-EEG approach can improve the localization based solely on MEG or EEG since FUS can stimulate any region of the brain regardless of depth with the same degree of focality and the evoked EEG signals can be referred back to known locations. The accuracy of MEG- or EEG-based localization can be validated by stimulating the locations hypothesized to be active based on the conventional approach at suitable moments around the time of activation produced by an external stimulation or an endogenous task and testing whether the activity interacts with the activity evoked by FUS. The sharp focus that can be achieved with FUS can enhance spatial precision. The precisely stimulus-locked responses with little jitter we found provides temporal precision in this interaction technique. The activity evoked by FUS may suppress the activity evoked by an external stimulation or a task since we found FUS activate two types of inhibitory network which strongly suppress evoked responses. The FUS-EEG approach can expand the depth sensitivity of both MEG and EEG, enabling the imaging of subcortical sources and deep sources in the cortex outside of normal sensitivity, since each region hypothesized to be active, even in deep regions outside of sensitivity of MEG or EEG alone, can be repeatedly stimulated with FUS in conjunction with an external stimulation or a task and recording average scalp EEG signals until the signal-to-noise ratio becomes high enough for detecting the interaction. This interaction technique can be, thus, used to validate the accuracy of MEG or EEG-based source localization, thereby improving the spatial and temporal precision of neuroimaging.

This FUS-EEG approach can be used to identify neuropathophysiology in patients since it is known that E/I balance and oscillatory dynamics are abnormal in schizophrenia, epilepsy, autism spectrum disorder and depression (Fries, 2015; Uhlhaas and Singer, 2006; 2012). For example, this approach can become transformative in localizing epileptic regions in human patients if it can identify the epileptic tissues in pharmacologically intractable epilepsy in whom surgical and pharmaceutical treatment options are very limited. The identification of epileptogenic tissues and circuits is perhaps the central problem in clinical epilepsy. This approach could be used to identify the epileptic tissues by measuring the E/I balance and identifying tissues with hyperexcitability. Hyperexcitability can be used as the biomarker for identifying the epileptic circuits. Since FUS can focally activate any regions regardless of depth and the stimulated locations are predetermined, the localization is accurate. Hyperexcitability can be measured with scalp EEG.

In conclusion, the characteristics of FUS-evoked EEG activity measured in this study show that an integrated FUS-EEG approach holds promise for developing a new type of activation-informed neuroimaging.
