Caring for yourself, caring for nature: a pro-environmental mechanism of self-compassion and intervention study
Authors: Weijian Fu, Jiandong Fang, Baorui Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-compassion can enhance an individual’s willingness to engage in pro-environmental behavior, but its underlying mechanisms have been limitedly explored. The purpose of this study was to explore the effects of self-compassion on pro-environmental behavior and the underlying psychological mechanisms using correlational study and experimental intervention.MethodsStudy 1 utilized self-compassion scale, materialism scale, and pro-environmental behavior scale to conduct a correlational study among 712 middle school students, preliminarily exploring the interrelationships among these three constructs. Study 2 randomly assigned participants to an intervention group and a control group, employing an intervention study method to intervene in the participants’ self-compassion over a period of 7 days, to investigate the causal relationship between self-compassion, materialism and pro-environmental behavior.ResultsThe results of study 1 revealed that self-compassion, materialism, and pro-environmental behavior were significantly and positively interrelated; self-compassion significantly and positively predicted pro-environmental behavior; and materialism mediated the relationship between self-compassion and pro-environmental behavior. Study 2 found that as the level of self compassion increasesed, the level of materialism among participants decreased and their willingness to participate in pro-environmental behavior significantly increases.ConclusionThis study examines the potential benefits of self-compassion in promoting pro-environmental behavior and explores its underlying psychological mechanisms, which may offer insights into the social implications of self-compassion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03389-2.Keywords: Self-compassion, Pro-environmental behavior, Materialism, Intervention

Introduction

Compassion refers to the emotional resonance with the misfortunes of others, coupled with unconditional tolerance towards them [1]. In the context of Buddhist psychology, compassion for oneself is considered as important as compassion for others [2]. Building on this concept, Neff introduced the notion of Self-Compassion, which is the ability to maintain a kind and tolerant attitude towards one’s own negative states during times of suffering, disappointment, or other adverse emotional experiences [3]. Self-compassion comprises six dimensions: Three positively framed components—self-kindness (treating oneself with warmth rather than criticism), common humanity (recognizing one’s experiences as part of shared human existence), and mindfulness (maintaining balanced awareness of present-moment experiences)—and three inversely scored components—self-judgment (harsh self-criticism), isolation (feeling alone in suffering), and over-identification (excessive absorption with negative emotions).

In recent years, the beneficial outcomes of self-compassion and how to cultivate it have attracted increasing attention from researchers [4]. Research on self-compassion has also shown a trend towards defearization, viewing self-compassion as a positive self-awareness attitude [5, 6], which is a positive evaluation of reality, including oneself. Studies have shown that self-compassion can bring a series of positive effects to individuals, not only improving their psychological adaptation functions [7], promoting the ability to self-regulate psychological states [8], but also bringing spiritual pleasure [9], thereby enhancing individuals’ prosocial behavior [10]. For instance, individuals with higher levels of self-compassion tend to be more generous and helpful compared to others [11], willing to devote more energy to charitable activities [12]. After interventions to cultivate self-compassion, individuals also show a higher willingness to be prosocial compared to before the intervention [13]. As a special form of prosocial behavior [14], will pro-environmental behavior(PEB) also be positively affected by self-compassion? What is the psychological mechanism involved? There is currently a lack of research in this area.

Furthermore, adolescents represent a critical population for investigating pro-environmental mechanisms, as their value systems and consumption habits are highly malleable during this developmental stage [15]. Prior interventions targeting youth have demonstrated the efficacy of brief psychosocial protocols—such as mindfulness-based school programs [16] and intervention based on the theory of planned behavior [17]—in promoting sustainable behaviors. By extending this work to self-compassion, our study tests the unique responsiveness of adolescent samples to psychological interventions. This study will delve into the above issues based on a review of relevant literature.

Theoretical review and research hypothesis

Self-compassion and pro-environmental behavior

PEB refers to the conscious actions taken by individuals that are beneficial to the natural environment, enhance environmental quality, or promote the sustainable use of natural resources [14]. It is generally categorized into two aspects: public-sphere PEB and private-sphere PEB. The former pertains to an individual’s engagement in public environmental protection activities, while the latter refers to the environmental attitudes and lifestyle exhibited by individuals in their daily lives [18]. PEB often necessitates an individual’s concern for the welfare of others and transcendence of personal interests, sharing the same connotations with the universalism dimension within the self-transcendence value orientation of the circumplex model of values [19]. In the circumplex model of values, the closer the positions, the more similar the types of motivations, which typically guide individuals to produce the same judgments [20]. Self-compassion requires not only self-tolerance and consolation but, more importantly, acknowledging oneself as part of the human collective, embracing the world with love and kindness, enhancing the sense of connection with the outside world, and accepting and caring for all people [3], which aligns with the perspective of self-transcendence [21]. Both occupy the same position in the circumplex model of values and exert the same direction of influence on individual behavior. In other words, the motivational types underlying self-compassion correspond with those behind PEB, hence, the level of self-compassion positively predicts PEB.

Furthermore, self-compassion inherently encompasses the characteristic of “mindfulness”, defined within this construct as the balanced, non-judgmental awareness of one’s present-moment experiences, including distressing thoughts and feelings related to environmental issues, without over-identification or suppression [3, 22]. This mindful awareness facilitates a critical process of “re-perception” [22]. It enables individuals to objectively acknowledge environmental realities and associated risks by creating cognitive space through the non-reactive observation of internal emotional reactions (e.g., eco-anxiety, guilt). By reducing the overwhelming influence of these emotions, individuals can more clearly perceive and process external information about environmental challenges (e.g., scientific data on climate impacts) and mitigate cognitive distortions like denial or wishful thinking, leading to a more accurate assessment of threats [23, 24]. Simultaneously, the mindful component, synergistically interacting with self-kindness and the recognition of common humanity, empowers individuals to gradually detach from key psychological barriers that hinder proactive environmental engagement [25]. This detachment manifests by reducing the defensive need to justify past unsustainable choices due to sunk costs (through acceptance fostered by mindfulness and reduced self-blame via self-kindness) [3], alleviating feelings of isolation and generalized distrust (by fostering a sense of shared struggle through common humanity, potentially increasing openness to collective action or credible information) [26]. Therefore, by promoting clearer perception of environmental challenges through re-perception and by diminishing the influence of internal barriers like sunk cost fallacy and distrust, the mindfulness dimension of self-compassion contributes significantly to fostering more flexible, informed, and proactive responses to environmental issues [27].

A number of current studies demonstrate the possibility that self-compassion positively influences PEB. First of all, self-transcendence itself seems to promote PEB in individuals [28], and correlational studies have shown that self-compassion is not egocentric, but rather it removes the boundaries between the self and the other, and has self-transcendent attributes, which may make the individual more able to perceive the changes in the outside world and the surrounding environment [29, 30] and may promote PEB in individuals. Second, a study of a self-compassion-based meditative yoga practice found that participants who took part in a meditative yoga practice would exhibit stronger environmental behaviors and more positive climate policy supportive attitudes compared to those who did not, which analytically may be caused by increased self-compassion [31]. Moreover, this predictive effect may be stable, as a 3-year follow-up study of the relationship between self-compassion and willingness to engage in pro-social behaviors showed that self-compassion positively predicted adolescents’ pro-social behaviors, as well as positively predicting their habit of keeping the environment clean and tidy [11]. In summary, study hypothesis 1: self-compassion positively predicts PEB.

The mediating effect of materialism

Materialism, a value orientation where individuals emphasize the importance of possessing material wealth for personal significance [32], may account for the potential mediating mechanism between self-compassion and PEB. In the circumplex model of values, values that are relatively positioned motivate opposite types of behaviors and judgments [20]. Researchers have found through multidimensional scaling analyses that materialism belongs to the self-enhancement dimension [33], and similar results have been validated in different countries such as the United States, Turkey, Germany, and Canada [34, 35], demonstrating cross-cultural consistency. Since self-compassion shares attributes similar to self-transcendence [28], and the self-transcendence dimension is positioned opposite to the self-enhancement dimension in the circumplex model of values, the influence on individual behavior is converse. In other words, self-compassion itself is not in line with the motivational types behind materialism; therefore, the level of self-compassion will negatively predict individual’s materialism. The core tenet of materialism suggests [32] that when materialistic individuals fail to achieve their high materialistic aspirations, they engage in self-criticism. Individuals may excessively self-criticize and view these as necessary conditions to maintain high standards. However, the self-compassionate beliefs held by individuals can effectively reduce their motivation to excessively pursue goals. Self-compassion leads individuals to recognize that the meaning of life stems from within themselves, not from external goals and material wealth. By lowering these standards, it alleviates the emergence of self-critical behaviors [36], thereby reducing materialistic levels. Furthermore, the relative deprivation felt by individuals is an important trigger for materialism, where individuals feel a sense of deficiency compared to others and thus must focus on acquiring wealth and status as compensation [32]. The core component of self-compassion, “common humanity,” makes individuals aware that everyone fails or makes mistakes, and failure is a shared human experience. It does not view one’s experiences in isolation, emphasizing the connection between the individual and others [37], which can to some extent alleviate feelings of relative deprivation [38], thereby suppressing the emergence of materialism. Correlational study provides support, such as study using self-report measures indicating a negative correlation between self-compassion and materialism [39]. Recently, Wang et al. found in their study on the impact of self-compassion meditation intervention on impulsive consumption that self-compassion not only improves individuals’ impulsive consumption behavior but also inhibits materialistic levels [40].

Individuals with higher levels of materialism exhibit lower pro-environmental attitudes and behaviors [41]. The value belief-norm theory, proposed based on the circumplex model of values, suggests that values influence individuals’ PEB by affecting their beliefs about human-environment relationships and the attribution of responsibility for the consequences of environmental actions [42]. When individuals hold egoistic values such as materialism, they may fail to recognize the importance of environmental issues and make correct judgments about the consequences of their environmental behaviors, leading to a reduced sense of environmental responsibility and a lower willingness to engage in PEB, thus resulting in fewer pro-environmental actions [43]. The relationship between materialism and PEB has been amply demonstrated by numerous studies [41]. For instance, correlational studies using self-report measures have found a negative relationship between materialism and the willingness to engage in PEB [44, 45]. Another laboratory study using a priming method to activate materialism showed that participants who were primed with materialism felt less responsible for water scarcity issues [46]. A meta-analysis also revealed a significant negative correlation between materialism and PEB [47](r=-0.22).

Materialism, which emphasizes self-enhancement, can lead to numerous negative consequences for individuals, such as self-centered tendencies and a reduction in PEB [32]. Due to the antagonistic effects of self-transcendence and self-enhancement [20], self-compassion may inhibit the development of materialism in individuals, leading them to consider both their own and external needs, and to engage in more PEB. Furthermore, higher levels of self-compassion may reduce external social comparison and competition [3], which are both significant causes and consequences of materialism [48]. Therefore, the enhancement of self-compassion may effectively mitigate its negative impacts, reduce materialism, and increase PEB [40, 49]. In summary, study hypothesis 2 is proposed: materialism mediates the relationship between self-compassion and PEB.

The intervention of self-compassion

The extensive research on self-compassion suggests that self-compassion may be a parsimonious and effective indicator of psychological interventions and treatments [3]. Self-compassion is not only a personality trait, but also a skill that can be learned and practiced [9], and researchers have explored self-compassion interventions using a variety of modalities and have demonstrated significant gains in self-compassion levels with the implementation of these interventions [50]. Due to the long duration of intervention of the earliest traditional self-compassion interventions, such as the 8-week-long positive thought self-compassion program [3] and compassion center therapy [51]. The development of self-compassion interventions has seen a trend toward streamlining, such as participant-centered self-compassion intervention approaches that last a week or even less [52]. Furthermore, most current research on self-compassion interventions has been conducted in Western countries in individualistic cultures, with one 2019 meta-analysis suggesting an 86% share [53], and while this share may have decreased in recent years, it is still relatively uncommon for validation in different cultural contexts [3]. So is a short self-compassion intervention also feasible in the Chinese cultural context? Does self-compassion after the intervention enhance PEB? This study will further examine the causal relationship between self-compassion and PEB.

The present study

The purpose of this study was to examine the relationship between self-compassion and PEB as well as their psychological mechanism and intervention effect. Study 1 examined the relationship between self-compassion and PEB and the mediating mechanism through a questionnaire method. Study 2 examined the effect of self-compassion on materialism and PEB through a one-week self-compassion intervention training. The theoretical model is shown in Fig. 1 below.

Study 1: correlation between self-compassion and pro-environmental behavior

Participants

This study conducted an offline survey among students in the Chinese school, collecting a total of 712 questionnaires. Adolescence marks a formative period for both materialism internalization and environmental identity development [15], allowing robust examination of the proposed mediation model. After excluding 29 invalid responses that did not pass the attention test, 683 valid responses remained, yielding a valid response rate of 95.93%. Among the participants, there were 331 males and 352 females, with an average age of 16.85 years (SD = 0.48). Sensitivity analysis using G*power 3.1 indicated that the sample provided sufficient statistical power (80%) to detect an effect size as small as d = 0.11 in a one-sample T-test.

Measurement and procedure

All participants in this study reported their responses through the completion of a paper-based questionnaire survey. Specifically, the survey questionnaire in this study consisted of a series of instruments, including measures of self-compassion, materialism, PEB, and demographic variables (gender, age, socioeconomic status). Following survey completion, each participant received a small gift as a token of appreciation.

Study material

Self-compassion scale

This study employed the Self-Compassion Scale-Short Form (SCS-SF), revised by Raes et al. [54], to measure participants’ self-compassion levels. The 12-item short form was selected over the original 26-item version [36] for a near-perfect correlation with the long form SCS (r ≥ 0.97 all samples) and reduced participant burden. The scale utilizes a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores indicate higher levels of self-compassion. In this study, the scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.76.

Materialism scale

The Chinese version of the Materialism Scale [55], adapted from Richins and Dawson’s Material Values Scale (MVS), was used. This 13-item version was preferred to the 18-item MVS due to its validated cross-cultural applicability in East Asian contexts and stronger factorial invariance in adolescent populations. In this study, the scale exhibited a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.73.

Pro-environmental behavior scale

This study employed the Private and Public Environmental Orientation Scale [56] to measure participants’ PEB. The scale comprises two dimensions: public and private spheres. Each dimension is assessed through three items. A 7-point Likert scale is used for scoring (1 = completely disagree, 7 = completely agree). Higher scores indicate a higher level of PEB. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for public PEB was 0.81; for private PEB, it was 0.73.

Results and analysis

Common method bias test

This study collected data using a questionnaire method, which may be susceptible to common method bias. To statistically control for this, a Harman’s single-factor test was employed in subsequent processing. The results indicated that there were eight factors with eigenvalues greater than one, with the largest explained variance of the first factor being 15.15%, which is below 40%. This suggests that there is no significant common method bias issue in this study.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

Correlation analysis was conducted for all variables, with gender represented by a dummy variable (male = 1, female = 0). Self-compassion also showed a significant negative correlation with materialism (r= -0.12, p = 0.002). Materialism exhibited a significant negative correlation with public-sphere PEB (r=-0.20, p < 0.001) and private-sphere PEB (r=-0.21, p < 0.001).

Discussion

The results of Study 1 indicate that individuals with higher levels of self-compassion exhibit lower levels of materialism and demonstrate higher levels of PEB in both public and private spheres, thus validating Hypotheses 1 and 2. However, as this study is correlational in nature, it does not provide support for causal relationships [57]. Therefore, in Study 2, we will investigate whether the level of PEB increases with the cultivation of self-compassion through intervention, aiming to provide further causal evidence for the hypotheses.

Study 2: an intervention study of self-compassion influencing pro-environmental behavior

Participants

Students from Chinese school were recruited to maintain consistency with Study 1’ s population and leverage educational institutions as naturalistic settings for intervention, students were recruited offline and randomly assigned to an intervention group (71 participants) and a control group (70 participants), consisting of 57 males and 84 females, with an average age of 17.69 years (SD = 1.91). A pretest was conducted with all participants before the intervention to assess the levels of self-compassion and PEB reported by the two groups. Independent samples T-tests revealed no significant differences in levels of self-compassion (t=-0.11, p = 0.91), materialism (t=-1.19, p = 0.24), private-sphere PEB (t=-0.40, p = 0.69), or public-sphere PEB (t=-0.19, p = 0.85) between the two groups, indicating that the randomization was effective. Intervention content and methodology

In accordance with previous intervention study paradigms [58], participants were subjected to a 7-day self-compassion intervention. This paradigm primarily involves having participants recall and write about negative experiences from their past, and then extending compassion and care towards themselves during those times, thereby exercising and enhancing self-compassion. It is anticipated that, compared to the control group, participants in the intervention group will exhibit higher levels of self-compassion and a greater tendency towards PEB following the intervention.

A 2 (Time: Pretest vs. Posttest) × 2 (Group: Intervention vs. Control) mixed-factor experimental design was utilized, with time being a within-subjects factor and group being a between-subjects factor. The intervention was formally implemented after participants completed the pretest. The specific content is as follows.

Participants in the intervention group were informed as follows: In the coming days, we invite you to engage in a special writing exercise. Please recall an experience that once caused you pain and frustration, whether it was a failure in an exam, the loss of a cherished possession, or challenges in interpersonal relationships. Allow yourself to immerse in the emotions of that time and feel those feelings. Subsequently, we ask you to write a letter to your past self. In this letter, include the words you wished to hear at that time. Use a tone of warmth, understanding, and encouragement to convey support and comfort to your past self. Each line of the letter is an embrace and strength you give to yourself. Remember, there is no right or wrong content in the letter; they are a reflection of your inner world. This is not only a journey of self-healing but also an opportunity for self-discovery and growth. We encourage you to write a letter each day for seven consecutive days without repetition.

Participants in the control group were instructed as follows: Recall and record a life event that occurred on that day; the event should not be judged as good or bad, right or wrong. Record one event per day (without repetition) for a total of 7 days.

Each letter is required to be at least 50 words long. And we stated to all participants before starting the intervention that the letters written each day must be non repetitive in content. Specifically, different letters written by the same participant must point to different events. In addition, we require that the events written are based on personal experiences in the past, rather than being based on one’s own imagination or stories that did not actually happen.

After the intervention, a post-test was conducted with all participants, and both the intervention and control groups completed the measurement questionnaires and feedback information forms again. To test the effectiveness of the intervention, self-compassion and PEB were measured in both the pre-test and post-test. Finally, the purpose of the study was disclosed to the participants. And participants were compensated with a small gift upon concluding all experimental sessions.

Pro-environmental behavior scale

The PEB questionnaire in this study was adapted from the 2013 CGSS(Chinese General Social Survey) survey questionnaire on environmentally friendly behavior, comprising a total of 10 items divided into two dimensions: private-sphere and public-sphere PEB, with each dimension measured by 5 items. In this study, the original phrases “in the past year” and “often” were modified to “in the future.” For example, “In the future, I am willing to reuse plastic bags” (private-sphere), “In the future, I am willing to actively participate in environmental protection activities organized by environmental groups” (public-sphere). The questionnaire uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating a greater willingness of individuals to engage in PEB. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for private-sphere PEB was 0.81; for public-sphere PEB, it was 0.80.

Results and analysis

Intervention feasibility and acceptability test

In this study, we assessed the feasibility of the intervention based on the criteria [59]: participants were considered to have successfully completed the intervention tasks and were eligible for subsequent research analysis only if they completed the prescribed intervention exercises for 5 or more consecutive days (i.e., at least 75% of the entire 7-day intervention period). During the implementation of this study’s intervention, a total of 141 participants joined the intervention program. In the intervention group, 2 participants failed to meet the requirement of completing the intervention tasks for more than 5 days. In the control group, we encountered 2 cases of missing data. After screening, we ultimately obtained 137 valid datasets, with an efficiency rate of 97.16%.

Additionally, to assess participants’ acceptance of the intervention measures, we employed the Intervention Acceptability Scale [59]. The scale consists of two items designed to measure participants’ experiences and feelings. The main criterion for acceptability is that the average score of participants’ responses to the two items is greater than or equal to 4. The two items are: “Overall, I really enjoyed the self-compassion exercises this week”; “I would recommend this exercise to others.” A 5-point Likert scale is used for scoring (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). In this study, the average score for intervention acceptability was 4.25, indicating that the intervention was effective and acceptable.

This suggests that the 7-day intervention significantly enhanced participants’ levels of private-sphere PEB.

The analysis of participants’ public-sphere PEB levels was conducted, and the results showed a significant main effect of the intervention condition (F(1, 132) = 12.49, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.09); a significant main effect of measurement time (F(1, 132) = 5.13, p = 0.02, η2p = 0.04); and a significant interaction between the intervention condition and measurement time (F(1, 132) = 11.54, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.08). Simple effects analysis indicated no significant difference in public-sphere PEB levels between the intervention and control groups at pre-test (F(1, 132) = 0.05, p = 0.82); however, a significant difference was observed at post-test, with the intervention group showing significantly higher levels of public-sphere PEB compared to the control group (F(1, 132) = 30.86, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.19). This suggests that the 7-day intervention significantly improved participants’ levels of public-sphere PEB.

In Model a, self-compassion positively predicted public-sphere PEB (β = 0.46, p < 0.001) and negatively predicted materialism (β=-0.23, p = 0.006). Additionally, materialism significantly predicted public-sphere PEB (β=-0.20, p = 0.008), indicating a significant partial mediation by materialism with an indirect effect of 0.08 (95% CI= [0.02, 0.15], excluding zero) that accounted for 9.88% of the total effect. General discussion

Self-compassion and pro-environmental behavior

This study examined the relationship between self-compassion and PEB through complementary methodologies. Study 1 revealed significant modest correlations between self-compassion and PEB engagement (public sphere: r = 0.14; private sphere: r = 0.18). Study 2 demonstrated that enhancing self-compassion via intervention yielded small-to-moderate increases in PEB willingness (η2p = 0.19–0.24). These effects, while statistically significant, align with typical magnitudes for distal psychological predictors of complex socio-behavioral outcomes like PEB, where single factors rarely explain large variance portions [14]. Critically, Self-compassion functions not as a direct driver but as an affective enabler that reduces ego-defensiveness [3] and mitigates self-threat barriers (e.g., guilt-driven avoidance). This creates cognitive-emotional capacity to engage with environmental concerns. Being self-compassionate enhances connection with the external environment not through sheer motivation magnitude, but by removing psychological barriers to environmental receptivity [60]. The intervention’s effects, though limited individually, hold practical significance given the low-cost, scalable nature of brief self-compassion protocols. When deployed population-wide, such gains could yield meaningful ecological impact—paralleling ‘nudge’ approaches in environmental policy where small aggregate changes create substantial effects [61]. Furthermore, Self-compassion may amplify established PEB drivers. By buffering climate anxiety [62], it reduces message avoidance and enhances engagement with environmental information. Through its self-transcendence correlates [21], it reinforces universalistic values that underpin sustained PEB. Thus, it may operates as a catalytic factor within integrated intervention frameworks rather than a standalone solution.

On the one hand, self-compassion is not selfish; being kind and caring towards oneself does not mean being less concerned about the outside world [3]. In fact, self-compassion enhances the connection with the external environment [63], and individuals with high levels of self-compassion are more willing to care for others [64], actively participate in public welfare activities, and provide social support [22], thus being more likely to engage in PEB. On the other hand, self-compassion is not laziness or lethargy; it is an important motivational source for individual self-improvement, stemming from the desire to become better [65]. Individuals with high levels of self-compassion are likely to have higher moral goals [66] and a greater sense of social responsibility [53], reducing the occurrence of behaviors that violate social norms.

While no studies have directly examined the impact of self-compassion on PEB, research indicates that individuals with higher levels of self-compassion tend to have positive self-perceptions and are more supportive of environmental social policies [67]. Additionally, self-compassion is significantly positively correlated with self-transcendence [21], which is an important positive predictor of the willingness to engage in PEB [19]. Previous studies have suggested that positive self-cognition and evaluation can increase PEB, such as recycling in the private sphere [68]. Individuals who are friendly, universalistic, and concerned with the welfare of others show more care for the natural environment in both private and public spheres [69]. As a positive self-cognition attitude that transcends the narrow self-interest [70], self-compassion increases attention to the interests of others and interactions with the natural environment, reducing the excessive pursuit of self-interest [3]. Therefore, self-compassion is conducive to enhancing individuals’ willingness to engage in PEB.

The mediating effect of materialism

According to the research findings, materialism is significantly negatively correlated with self-compassion and PEB, and materialism mediates the relationship between self-compassion and PEB. That is, self-compassion can indirectly increase PEB by reducing the level of materialism. This is consistent with previous research findings [40, 47]. Materialism motivation theory suggests that the internalization of materialism often stems from the unfulfilled basic psychological needs (a sense of autonomy, competence, and relatedness), leading to a lack of psychological security, and then attempts to alleviate the lack of security through material wealth [32]. However, self-compassion can reverse this process. On the one hand, self-compassion increases individuals’ contemplation of life; when individuals perceive life as meaningful, they are more willing to care for life, help others, and show concern for the external world, even sacrificing themselves for the sake of others [71]. On the other hand, self-compassion involves unconditional self-worth and non-threatening comparison, which is essentially opposite to the materialistic approach of measuring self-worth by material wealth and threatening social comparison [28, 33]. Researchers studying psychological coping mechanisms during turbulent times (unemployment, deflation) have found that compared to an excessive desire for material wealth, a more adaptive psychological coping mechanism is to move away from materialism and consumer culture and embrace self-compassion [72]. The literature on materialism consistently indicates that personal suffering (depression, anxiety) is a trigger for materialism, and in order to maintain an unclear sense of self-meaning [73], self-compassion itself has a buffering effect on negative self-feelings [65], thereby inhibiting the development of materialism. In addition, according to the social mentality theory [74], self-compassion can deactivate the threat system (related to the sense of defense and the limbic system) and activate the self-soothing system (related to the sense of secure attachment and the oxytocin-opioid system). In contrast, materialism is considered to represent an evaluation of superiority and inferiority, which helps stratify social hierarchy and is associated with motivational impulses and dopamine activation [32, 75], and the two may differ at the physiological level. Therefore, the characteristics of self-compassion itself reduce individuals’ excessive pursuit of materialism, thereby promoting individuals’ willingness to engage in PEB.

Significance and value of the study

This study offers preliminary theoretical and practical insights. Theoretically, it explores a potential link between self-compassion and PEB, suggesting a possible extension of self-compassion applications into socio-ecological domains. By implementing a self-compassion intervention in a Chinese adolescent sample, this work provides initial cross-cultural evidence regarding the framework’s potential adaptability. Furthermore, these findings contribute to the ongoing discussion on the adaptive functions of self-compassion and lend modest support to the potential value of brief, scalable interventions in this context.

From a practical perspective, in light of increasing environmental challenges—frequently associated with materialistic values [41]—this study explores potential actionable pathways. The 7-day self-compassion writing intervention, piloted in this study with adolescents, may be adaptable for use by educators, environmental NGOs, or corporate sustainability programs to encourage eco-friendly attitudes. To broaden its reach, digital formats such as mobile applications or online modules could offer a low-cost means of delivering this intervention to wider audiences, including adults in high-consumption settings [76]. Schools, businesses, and community organizations might consider incorporating such approaches into existing well-being or environmental initiatives, potentially supporting both mental health and ecological awareness. By helping reduce materialistic tendencies—which often inhibit pro-environmental behavior—the intervention could potentially promote consumption-related actions (e.g., mindful purchasing, reusing products, reducing waste) and foster participation in collective efforts (e.g., environmental advocacy). Therefore, cultivating self-compassion may offer a promising approach to simultaneously support individual wellness and engage people in sustainability efforts, though further practical implementation studies are needed [77].

Limitations and future directions

This study confirms the impact of self-compassion on the willingness to engage in PEB and its underlying mechanisms, yielding some valuable results. However, there are still the following limitations.

First, the reliance on self-reported measures of behavioral intention rather than observed behaviors may introduce social desirability bias and limits insight into actual behavioral outcomes. Future studies would benefit from incorporating objective behavioral measures, such as observational data or tracked behavioral outcomes, to validate the relationship between self-compassion and tangible PEB [78].

Second, in our intervention study, the effect of self-compassion on materialism was less pronounced than its impact on PEB. We attribute this to a possible floor effect [79]. Participants in Studies 1 and 2 were primarily high school students in China, aged 16–17. At this developmental stage, adolescents typically have limited exposure to and understanding of material wealth and the significance of money, resulting in a relatively not high baseline level of materialism [80]. Consequently, self-compassion interventions had limited room to further reduce already low levels of materialistic values. Nonetheless, our findings provide robust support for Hypothesis 2, confirming the mediating role of materialism in the relationship between self-compassion and PEB. Future studies should replicate this research with adult populations, where stronger effects may be observed, thereby broadening the practical applicability of self-compassion-based interventions.

Third, although the proposed theoretical model incorporates materialism as a mediator, it does not directly examine the potential role of self-transcendence values, which may further clarify the connection between self-compassion and PEB. Subsequent research could explicitly test the influence of self-transcendence to provide a more comprehensive understanding of these motivational pathways [81].

Fourth, while our findings illuminate self-compassion’s role in promoting PEB, we acknowledge potential ideological influences in PEB measurement [82]. Critically, although all participants were recruited from China, and Study 2 utilized a PEB scale adapted from the 2013 Chinese General Social Survey (CGSS)—an instrument developed within China’s sociopolitical context that may reflect specific national priorities—Study 1 employed the rigorously validated Private and Public Environmental Orientation Scale [56]. This scale has demonstrated robust cross-cultural applicability across 20 nations and politically diverse subgroups (e.g., Northern Ireland/Great Britain, East/West Germany), attesting to its capacity to capture core environmental behaviors transcending singular ideological frameworks. Consequently, the convergence of effects across both studies suggests that self-compassion’s relationship with PEB is unlikely to be an artifact of political bias embedded in measurement tools. Nevertheless, future research should develop updated, comprehensive PEB scales that explicitly minimize value-laden terminology and incorporate culturally nuanced public/private behaviors. Replicating this model with such neutral instruments would further verify the generalizability of our findings in increasingly polarized societies [83].

Conclusion

This study confirms that self-compassion can positively predict the willingness to engage in PEB, and materialism serves as a mediator between self-compassion and the willingness to engage in PEB. Specifically, individuals with higher levels of self-compassion are likely to have lower levels of materialism, thereby increasing the likelihood of engaging in PEB. The research also found that intervention method can enhance individuals’ levels of self-compassion, inhibiting the level of materialism, fostering a mindset of “caring for oneself and caring for nature,” which in turn promotes individuals’ willingness to engage in PEB.
