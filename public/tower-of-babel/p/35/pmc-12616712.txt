Release and Reuptake Sites for Norepinephrine Are Differently Distributed Across Layers of the Primary Visual Cortex in Macaque Monkeys
Authors: Corey Roach, Rachel Rios, John Pearson, Anita A Disney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNorepinephrine (NE) is released in cortex by axons arising from the locus coeruleus (LC). The cortical innervation patterns from LC have been mapped previously using antibodies directed against the vesicular synthetic enzyme for NE, dopamine β‐hydroxylase (DBH), which provides information about putative release sites. We sought to quantify putative NE reuptake sites by immunolabeling the norepinephrine transporter (NET), focusing on the primary (V1) and secondary (V2) visual cortex of macaque monkeys. We used stereological methods to determine the density of axon segments immunoreactive for NET (NET+ segments) in V1 and V2, and non‐stereological methods to compare the laminar distribution of NET+ segments and to compare the distribution of NET+ segments in V1 with segments immunoreactive for DBH (DBH+). We also developed a novel analysis approach on the basis of Bayesian statistics that allowed us to combine these quantitative data with qualitative data from previously published studies to generate and compare updated estimates of LC axon density. In line with prior reports based on DBH immunoreactivity, we find that LC axon density is higher in V2 than in V1 and has a similar laminar pattern across both areas. We also find that densities of DBH+ and NET+ segments differ across the layers of V1. This indicates that sites of NE release and reuptake may not be colocated and suggests a role for differing scales of diffusion in cortical modulatory effects mediated by NE.Keywords: immunohistochemistry, macaque, noradrenaline, statistical methods, stereology

Introduction

Sensory cortices, such as primary (V1) and secondary (V2) visual cortex, filter and transform incoming data about the environment and route the resulting patterns of activity to other brain regions. During this processing, the presence in the circuit of neuromodulatory molecules, such as norepinephrine (NE), acetylcholine, and serotonin, modifies neuronal excitability and synaptic strength via membrane‐associated receptors (Kaczmarek and Levitan 1987; Hasselmo 1995; Katz 1999). In this way, neuromodulators can adjust sensory processing as environmental conditions and the state of the animal change (Disney 2021).

NE, also known as noradrenaline, is a catecholaminergic neuromodulator that is released into cortex from axons originating in the locus coeruleus (LC), the only cortically projecting noradrenergic nucleus. Accordingly, antibodies directed against dopamine β‐hydroxylase (DBH), the enzyme that converts dopamine into NE, have been used to identify axons in cortex that originate in the LC (Foote and Morrison 1984; Kosofsky et al. 1984; Morrison and Foote 1986). More specifically, because DBH expression is largely restricted to vesicles (Olschowka et al. 1981; Smiley et al. 1999), axon segments immunoreactive for DBH (DBH+ segments) represent putative sites of NE release.

On the basis of DBH immunoreactivity, prior studies have reported that, in macaque monkeys, the LC innervates the layers of V1 in two bands: one spanning Layers 2–4B, and the other, Layers 5 and 6 (Kosofsky et al. 1984; Morrison and Foote 1986). In between these two bands of relatively dense staining is the most distinct feature of DBH immunoreactivity in V1: the sparse labeling of Layer 4C, the primary recipient layer for visual information from the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus (LGN). Closer inspection of published images of DBH immunoreactivity in Layer 4C reveals a few DBH+ axons in upper 4C (4Cα), whereas there appear to be none at all in lower 4C (4Cβ; Kosofsky et al. 1984). Thus, in V1, noradrenergic modulation seems directed toward specific stages of visual processing (i.e., specific layers). In V2, the density is higher than in V1 but still roughly presents as a trilaminar banding pattern (Morrison and Foote 1986).

We set out to quantify the noradrenergic innervation of V1 and V2 (the previous studies were qualitative), and to do so using a different measure: immunoreactivity for the norepinephrine transporter (NET). Like DBH, NET is expressed at putative sites of NE release where it participates in a well‐described physical interaction with syntaxin 1A, a presynaptic soluble N‐ethylmaleimide‐sensitive‐factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) protein involved in vesicular fusion (Sung et al. 2003). It is not known whether NET is only expressed at sites of NE release, or whether it is found at all of these sites.

We first quantify the density of axon segments immunoreactive for NET (NET+ segments) stereologically and then compare the laminar distribution of these segments between areas V1 and V2. We also develop a new analysis method that allows us to compare these quantitative data with prior qualitative data on the distribution of DBH+ segments. Finally, we directly compare (quantitatively) the laminar distribution of NET+ segments with the distribution of DBH+ segments in V1 and find evidence that NET and DBH are not always colocated.

Materials and Methods

Tissue Preparation

Brain tissue from three adult macaques was used in this experiment: a 14‐year‐old male Macaca nemestrina (A7), an 11‐year‐old male Macaca mulatta (A8), and a 7‐year‐old female M. mulatta (A9). These animals had been used previously in unrelated electrophysiology experiments (Oristaglio et al. 2006; Lovejoy and Krauzlis 2010; Hafed et al. 2011). All premortem and tissue harvest procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at the relevant institution (The Salk Institute for Biological Studies for A7 and A9, Columbia University for A8) and were performed in accordance with National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Animals were euthanized by intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital (∼60 mg/kg). Following the abolition of corneal and pedal reflexes, a ball‐tipped rat gavage needle was inserted through an incision at the lowest tip of the left ventricle of the heart; this positions the needle tip up beyond the aortic arch. After exsanguination with chilled, heparinized 0.01 M phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, minimum 1000 IU heparin/liter), chilled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4) was run at a flow rate of ∼125 mL/min for no less than 40 min. A9 was euthanized in the same way, but the fixative used was 0.15% glutaraldehyde and 4% PFA in PB. Brains were harvested and postfixed overnight at 4°C in 4% PFA in PB. For A7 and A9, prior to postfixation, the hemispheres were separated and the unrecorded hemisphere blocked in an approximate coronal plane at the level of the intraparietal sulcus. A8 was postfixed without blocking (whole brain). Following postfixation, the brains were moved to 30% sucrose in PBS as a cryoprotectant and stored at 4°C.

Data were collected from the (unrecorded) right hemisphere of each brain. Tissue was sectioned in a roughly coronal plane, from the occipital pole to the posterior tip of the intraparietal sulcus, at a thickness of 50 µm, on a freezing microtome. For A9, because glutaraldehyde had been used as a fixative, sectioning started 48 h after perfusion, and the resulting sections were immediately reacted in 1% sodium borohydride in PB for 30 min then rinsed 6 × 15 min in PB. A7 and A8 were sectioned after the tissue had sunk beneath the surface of the cryoprotectant solution and were not reacted with sodium borohydride. Three 1‐in‐6 series were processed as reference sets for use in determining boundaries between cortical areas and cortical layers: a silver stain for myelin (modified Gallyas method; Pistorio et al. 2006), a cresyl violet stain for Nissl bodies, and antibody staining directed against the neurofilament protein, SMI‐32. The remaining sections were stored free‐floating at 4°C in PBS with 0.05% sodium azide added.

Immunohistochemistry

Norepinephrine Transporter

Tissue sections for stereological quantification of NET immunoreactivity comprised a 1‐in‐12 series selected via systematic random sampling (Mouton 2002) on the basis of a random starting well and included the entire volume of both V1 and V2. Sections for non‐stereological analyses were selected pseudo‐randomly from this set. All sections were first rinsed 3 × 5 min in PBS to remove the sodium azide. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating in 1% hydrogen peroxide in PBS for 30 min, followed by 3 × 5‐min rinses in PBS and a 60‐min incubation in a blocking buffer comprising 6% normal donkey serum (NDS; Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 017‐000‐121, RRID:AB_2337258) and 0.5% Triton X‐100 (MilliporeSigma, RRID: CHEBI_9750) in PBS. Tissue was then incubated for 3 days on a rocker at 4°C in a solution containing a monoclonal primary antibody raised in mouse against a segment of the human NET protein (anti‐NET; Table 1) diluted 1:500 in 6% NDS in PBS. This concentration (1:500) of anti‐NET yielded the highest stereological density (SpaceBalls probe, see below) of immunoreactive segments in a pilot study that tested six concentrations (1:100, 1:200, 1:300, 1:500, 1:1000, and 1:2000).

Dopamine β‐Hydroxylase

Tissue sections for the quantification of DBH immunoreactivity were pseudo‐randomly selected: One section was chosen from each of A7 and A8, and three sections were chosen from a 1‐in‐6 series for A9. Tissue processing differed between animals. The protocol used for A8 and A9 yielded lower background staining of the neuropil and appeared qualitatively different, but the quantitative data did not differ between animals, which were combined for analysis. Processing for A8 and A9 began with an antigen retrieval protocol modified from Schuerger and Balaban (1999). A saturated sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution was made by mixing 40–80 g of NaOH salt (MilliporeSigma) into 50 mL of ultrapure (type 1) water at 22°C. The saturated NaOH solution was allowed to sit for 1–2 h to allow excess NaOH to precipitate, then filtered through P8 grade filter paper, and diluted 1:3 with 100% methanol. Tissue sections were dipped in ultrapure water and then incubated in the NaOH‐methanol solution for 30 min. Following antigen retrieval, the tissue was rinsed 2 × 7.5 min in 100% methanol, 2 × 7.5 min in 50% methanol, and 3 × 5 min in PBS.

Tissue sections for all animals were processed to block endogenous peroxidases as described for NET processing (above) and were incubated for 3 days on a rocker at 4°C in a solution containing a polyclonal antibody raised in sheep against a segment of the human DBH protein (anti‐DBH; Table 1). For A8 and A9, the blocking solution and antibody diluent was 6% NDS in PBS. For A7 it was 1% IgG‐free bovine serum albumin (BSA, Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 001‐000‐173, RRID:AB_2336947) with 0.5% Triton X‐100. The antibody concentration used for all animals (1:500) was selected on the basis of a qualitative assessment of staining intensity from a dilution series conducted under the processing conditions used for A7 that tested four concentrations (1:250, 1:500, 1:1000, and 1:2000).

Following primary incubation, sections were rinsed 3 × 15 min in PBS before being transferred into an F'ab fragment biotinylated donkey anti‐sheep IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 713‐066‐147, RRID:AB_2340717) diluted at 1:1000 in 6% NDS in PBS for A8 and A9, in 1% BSA in PBS for A7. After a 1‐h incubation in the secondary antibody solution, the tissue was rinsed and labeling visualized using the DAB procedure described above. Sections were mounted, dehydrated, and coverslipped as described above.

SMI‐32

The 1‐in‐6 tissue series set aside for SMI‐32 visualization was rinsed in PBS and endogenous peroxide activity quenched as described above. Tissue sections were then transferred into a blocking buffer comprising 1% IgG‐free BSA, 5% normal goat serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 005‐000‐121, RRID:AB_2336990), 0.1% Triton‐X 100, and .05% sodium azide (MilliporeSigma) in PBS for 1 h. Tissue was incubated for 24 h on a rocker at room temperature in a solution containing a monoclonal primary antibody raised in mouse against purified human neurofilament H (anti‐SMI‐32; Table 1) diluted at 1:2000 in the blocking buffer. After primary incubation, tissue sections were washed 3 × 10 min in PBS before being transferred into an F(ab’)2 fragment biotinylated goat anti‐mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 115‐066‐003, RRID:AB_2338576) diluted 1:1000 in 1% BSA in PBS. After a 1‐h incubation in the secondary antibody solution, the tissue was rinsed in PBS, labeling was visualized by ABC‐DAB, and the sections were mounted and coverslipped as described above.

Control Studies for Polyclonal Antibodies

A previously published western blot analysis conducted on human adrenal gland homogenate reports that the anti‐DBH primary antibody yields a band at 75 kDa, as predicted for the human DBH protein. No band was produced when the antibody solution was pretreated with the DBH antigen (Nagatsu et al. 1990). In our own preadsorption control experiment using macaque cortical tissue sections, the anti‐DBH antibody was preincubated with a saturating concentration of the immunogen (including C‐terminal amidation, GenScript Piscataway, NJ) prior to use at 1:500, as described for A8 and A9 above. Under these conditions, staining was abolished.

Data Analysis

Defining Cortical Area Boundaries

Light micrographs of immunostained tissue were captured using a Zeiss AxioCam MRc camera driven by the StereoInvestigator software suite (MBF Bioscience; RRID:SCR_002526). Settings for the contrast and brightness of micrographs were chosen to match the image as it appeared through the ocular lenses. Borders of V1 and V2 were traced at 1.25× magnification from myelin and SMI‐32 reference sections adjacent to the (antibody‐stained) sections from which data was collected. V1 borders were identified by the presence (in V1) of the line of Gennari. V2 borders with areas V3 and PO were identified on the basis of the appearance of the bands of Baillarger in the myelin stain (Ungerleider and Desimone 1986; Colby et al. 1988) and by SMI‐32 immunoreactivity (Hof and Morrison 1995). Data were not collected within a confidence boundary centered on the estimated border, the width of which was determined by the subjective ease of defining each border; less distinct borders were assigned wider boundaries. For each animal, confidence boundaries were determined separately for each border type (e.g., V2/PO) and the same boundary applied to all sections. The width of each of these boundaries varied, with the V1/V2 border being the easiest to distinguish (∼80 µm), and V2/V3 boundaries the most difficult (∼240 µm).

Stereological Quantification of NET± Axon Segment Density

The total length (mm) of NET+ axon segments in V1 and V2 was quantified under Köhler illumination at 63× magnification using the SpaceBalls probe (Calhoun and Mouton 2001; Mouton et al. 2002; Calhoun et al. 2004) implemented in StereoInvestigator. Spherical “probes” (regions within which counting would occur) of radius 8 µm were overlaid (by the software) on an image of the tissue. Mounted section thickness was measured at every probe location; the average measured thickness of the mounted and dehydrated tissue was 18 µm. A 1‐µm guard zone was applied at the top and bottom of the tissue (leaving 16 µm in the z axis for counting). To be counted, an axon segment had to be in focus and at least 1 µm in length (Figure 1a). For segments that intersected a given probe more than once, each intersection was counted. For segments lacking continuous inter‐varicose staining, the “segment” needed to consist of at least three varicosities or two varicosities plus an inter‐varicose filament, and those structures needed to follow a (subjective) Gestalt principle of good continuity (Figure 1b).

Laminar Quantification of Immunoreactive Axon Segment Density

To obtain images for offline counting and estimation of the length of NET+ axon segments as a function of cortical layer in V1 and V2, “montages” of light micrographs—spanning all layers of cortex (from pia to white matter)—were collected at pseudo‐random locations at 63× magnification. Three montages were collected per animal for V1, and three for V2, from sections separated by 6000 µm anteroposteriorly (one montage at each anteroposterior position), resulting in 18 image montages (2 brain areas × 3 animals × 3A−P positions). Images of DBH+ axon segments in V1 were collected from available tissue, which was not selected by systematic random sampling and did not cover the full extent of V1. One pia‐to‐white matter image montage was taken from each available section, yielding one montage each from A7 and A8, and 3 from A9: a total of 5 montages.

Laminar boundaries were traced on images of adjacent Nissl‐stained reference sections and overlaid on the data (i.e., immunostained) images using Adobe Photoshop (v. 2022). Confidence intervals of 10 µm were placed on both sides of each laminar boundary.

Two‐dimensional (2D) circular probes of diameter 20 µm and line thickness 0.5 µm (Figure 1a) were randomly placed on the micrographs using custom code (ImageJ). Similar to the three‐dimensional (3D) quantification described above, intersections between axon segments and (now circular not spherical) probes were counted. 2D sampling is appropriate for quantifying one‐dimensional (1D; number), but not 2D (length) properties, so we report results as counts per probe, by layer. Counts were converted to intersections per 100 probes for χ 2 goodness‐of‐fit testing against a uniform distribution using the chi2gof function in MATLAB (Mathworks).

Critical to a valid 2D adaptation of the 3D SpaceBalls approach is demonstrating that axon segments are oriented in the tissue in a manner that is isotropic in 3D (i.e., that reducing to a circle from a sphere will not introduce bias). Our tissue was hand‐blocked, so the plane of section differs between animals. We assessed isotropy by measuring the angle of incidence of individual axon segments upon probe boundaries with respect to the horizontal plane of the image (Figure 1a, inset). We then measured the angle of the pial surface with respect to this horizontal plane for each image and adjusted all of the measured angles such that the pia served as a common reference plane for the entire dataset. Tests for isotropy in angle of incidence made use of circular statistics (circ_rtest, circ_wwtest, MATLAB; Mathworks, RRID:SCR_001622).

Bayesian Model and Statistical Analysis

Traditional statistics do not offer the ability to directly test our hypotheses (see below). Furthermore, our data violate assumptions (e.g., independence) and requirements (sample size) for the proper application of frequentist statistics. We therefore adopted a Bayesian approach that let us quantify our ability to diminish uncertainty (represented by the prior) using data and to perform comparisons between models representing different hypotheses for the non‐stereological studies of NET+ and DBH+ axon segment density as a function of cortical layer. Our initial Bayesian model (Equation 3) states that the observed number of probe/segment intersections (Qr) is a Poisson variable determined by (1) factors operating at the level of whole tissue sections (αs) such as individual differences between animals and differences introduced by tissue processing; (2) true differences in density between cortical layers (βl ); and (3) the number of probes applied (Rsl).

In the simplest models, αs and βl were modeled as Gamma (k,b) distributions with shape (k) and rate (b) parameters both set to 1.

When qualitative data from Kosofsky et al. (1984) were passed to the model as data, they were treated as observations obtained from βl using an ordinal categorization (layer density classification: −, +, ++, etc.) with unknown cutoff points, f. These qualitative density assessments were, of course, reported with no uncertainty estimate; to address this, we assigned a probability distribution over categories. In doing so, we assumed that high confidence (p = 0.98) accompanied assignment to the extreme categories (“−” and “++++”); a slightly lower confidence (p = 0.9) was attributed to all other classifications (see Table 2).

Results

The purpose of this study was to (1) quantify the density and laminar distribution of axon segments immunoreactive for the NET+ segments in V1 and V2 of macaque monkeys and (2) understand the quantitative relationship between immunoreactivity for NET (putative NE reuptake sites) and for dopamine ß‐hydroxylase (DBH, putative NE release sites) in V1. We began with a stereological quantification of the overall (i.e., across layers) density of NET+ segments in V1 and V2. By the SpaceBalls method, the density of NET+ axon segments in V2 (mean = 614.8 mm/mm3, SD = 101.3) was higher than that in V1 (mean = 505.8 mm/mm3, SD = 62.6; t(2) = 4.492, p = 0.046, two‐tailed; see Table 3 for individual animal data, CE, and the number of tissue sections counted). This accords with a prior study of DBH immunoreactivity (Morrison and Foote 1986) and confirms what can be seen by eye in axon tracings (Figure 2).

Laminar Density Patterns for NE Axons

If anatomical data such as these are to be used to predict function, knowing which layers (and thus which stages of visual processing) are targeted by the noradrenergic system can be helpful. To achieve this, we counted, by layer, intersections (Qr ) between axon segments and circular “probes” of fixed radius and normalized by the number of probes (R). This yields axon intersections per probe (Qr /R) per layer. We accounted for axons that either lacked, or intermittently lacked, inter‐varicose staining with a counting criterion based on subjective “good continuity” (Figure 1b).

To assess whether this 2D adaptation of the (3D) SpaceBalls method introduced bias, we compared across animals the distribution of angles of incidence of axon segments upon probes (Figure 1a, inset). Due to hand blocking of the tissue, the true plane of section differed between animals and from the intended coronal plane. We reasoned that if bias was introduced in the loss of the dimension perpendicular to the plane of section (z axis), it would appear in our data as differences between animals in the angles of incidence of axon segments upon probes and differences in apparent segment length. The latter would result in count differences between animals, which we assess in the analyses below. The angles of incidence of NET+ axon segments upon probes were uniform (Figure 3; Rayleigh test: z = 2.8135, p = 0.0599). Furthermore, when we fit individual animal data with von Mises functions, the best fit location parameters are similar and close to an angle of incidence of zero (indicating most axons run ∼parallel to the pial surface; A7 μ = 3.46°; A8 μ = 6.88°; A9 μ = 3.00°). These von Mises fits also do not differ (Watson–Williams test; F = 0.89, p = 0.4114).

We counted segment/probe intersections (Qr /R) over a 4.62 mm2 region of V1 stained to visualize NET+ sites using 1886 counting probes and a 2.03‐mm2 region of V2 using 1328 probes. As expected from the stereological analysis above, when collapsed across layers the mean number of intersections per probe was higher in V2 (Qr /R = 0.30) than in V1 (Qr /R = 0.14). A 2D probe is appropriate for estimating a 1D property (number), so we did not calculate a length estimate. When we broke the data out by V1 layer, we found that, in V1, the average number of intersections per probe ranges from 0.08 (Layers 4Cβ and 5) to 0.21 (Layer 1; Table 4), and the pattern of intersection frequencies across layers was not uniform (χ 2 = 21.49, 8 df, p = 0.006). In V2, layer averages ranged from 0.20 (Layer 2) to 0.40 (Layer 3), with a pattern that was also not uniform (χ2 = 21.00, 5 df, p = 0.0008).

Two prior studies made qualitative assessments of the noradrenergic innervation of macaque V1 and V2, on the basis of DBH immunoreactivity, and our data broadly agreed with their observations. Specifically, Morrison and Foote (1986) reported that the density of DBH+ axons in V2 is higher than in V1 and is nonuniform, and Kosofsky et al. (1984) reported that the distribution of DBH+ axons in V1 is also nonuniform. We wanted to more directly compare our data with these reports, but our study differed from theirs in two regards: (1) the antibody used (anti‐NET vs. anti‐DBH) and (2) the analysis approach (qualitative vs. quantitative).

Laminar Density Pattern, V1

As a first step toward the desired comparison, we counted segment/probe intersections in V1 tissue stained to visualize DBH+ sites. On the basis of a count of intersections over a 1.16‐mm2 region of tissue, using 717 probes, we found that the pattern of intersection frequencies for DBH+ segments is (like that for NET+ segments) not uniform across layers (χ 2 = 68.99, 8 df, p << 0.00001) and ranges from 0.21 (Layer 4Cβ) to 0.93 (Layer 6; Table 4). Interestingly, although as an overall result this comports with Kosofsky et al. (1984)—in that there were laminar differences in the pattern of DBH immunoreactivity—we found the highest rate of probe intersections in Layer 6, followed by Layer 2, whereas Kosofsky et al. report that the highest density is in Layer 4B.

Ideally, an understanding of the noradrenergic innervation of V1 would be informed by both our quantitative data (i.e., NET+ and DBH+ counts) and prior qualitative data, in a rigorous fashion. To achieve this, we used both data types (i.e., qualitative and quantitative) to perform inference for laminar densities using a Bayesian statistical model (Equation 3; see Table 2 for qualitative data). We then used model comparison to understand the relationships between datasets. To do so, we used both quantitative and qualitative data (see Section 2 and Supporting Information section for details) to fit five models initialized with weak priors on all variables. Each model fits a variable, α, that captures differences at the level of tissue sections (this will include individual differences and “batch” effects from processing) and a variable, ß, that captures differences between layers.

Models 1 and 2 serve as validation benchmarks for this analytical approach and leverage additional qualitative data on the density of serotonergic (5‐HT+) axons in V1 from the same publication as the DBH data (Kosofsky et al. 1984). In that paper, and discernable by eye from their figures, a striking difference is noted in the laminar pattern of serotonergic versus noradrenergic axons. We reasoned that a model that asserts that a single density pattern can account for all of the data (i.e., for NET+, DBH+, and 5‐HT+ axons) or asserts that DBH+ segment densities are more like 5‐HT+ than like NET+ densities should be less likely (by our analysis) than models that allow distinct density patterns for serotonergic (5‐HT+) and noradrenergic (DBH+ and NET+) axons. Thus, the first two models assert that:

All three axon markers have the same laminar pattern; that is, DBH = NET = 5‐HT, and

All three axon markers have the same laminar pattern; that is, DBH = NET = 5‐HT, and

DBH+ and 5‐HT+ axons have the same density pattern, NET+ is different; DBH = 5‐HT ≠ NET.

DBH+ and 5‐HT+ axons have the same density pattern, NET+ is different; DBH = 5‐HT ≠ NET.

Model 3 then asserts that in quantifying DBH+ and NET+ segments, we effectively counted the same objects in the tissue (putative release sites), twice with the same sensitivity:

3.DBH+ and NET+ axons have the same density pattern, 5‐HT+ is different; DBH = NET ≠ 5‐HT.

DBH+ and NET+ axons have the same density pattern, 5‐HT+ is different; DBH = NET ≠ 5‐HT.

Model 4 is a variant of Model 3 with a global variable, ρ, that allows NET+ and DBH+ to be scaled versions of each other. This is equivalent to asserting that in quantifying NET+ and DBH+ segments we were counting the same biological structures, with different sensitivity. Model 4 states that:

4.DBH+ and NET+ axons have the same density pattern, but different absolute densities, 5‐HT is different; DBH ∝ NET ≠ 5‐HT.

DBH+ and NET+ axons have the same density pattern, but different absolute densities, 5‐HT is different; DBH ∝ NET ≠ 5‐HT.

Note that Model 4 contains Model 3 (when ρ = 1), so would only be favored when the more parsimonious Model 3 is disfavored. Finally, Model 5 asserts that the densities are all different:

5.All three axon markers have different patterns; DBH ≠ NET ≠ 5‐HT.

All three axon markers have different patterns; DBH ≠ NET ≠ 5‐HT.

In comparing model likelihoods (Figure 4), we find that Models 1 and 2 are highly unlikely. This is reassuring, as these models assert the highly unlikely cases in which all axon density patterns are identical (Model 1) or that the pattern of DBH axon densities is more like that for serotonergic axons than for NET+ axons (Model 2). A priori we would expect that one measure of NE axon density (e.g., DBH) would be similar to another (NET) and would differ from a measure of 5‐HT axon density. Beyond this, we find that the model asserting that the density patterns of DBH+ and NET+ segments are different is favored [Model 5DBH ≠ NET; relative log p(model|data) = 0] over models 3 [DBH = NET; relative log p(model|data) = −17.18] and 4 [DBH ∝ NET; relative log p(model|data) = −11.18].

To begin to understand the underlying relationship between counts of NET+ and DBH+ intersections, we can look at a variable, ρ, that was calculated under Model 4 as the ratio (NET+/DBH+) of posteriors on α. As α captures section‐level effects in segment counts, this ratio accounts for overall differences in density shared across all layers. The median value of ρ for the fits to Model 4 was 0.3462, suggesting that—when differences between NET+ and DBH+ densities are ignored—the data are typically best fit with a model in which we encountered roughly one‐third as many NET+ segments as DBH+ segments. To take this overall difference in density into account, we normalized the segment/probe (Qr /R) counts for NET+ and for DBH+ segments in each V1 layer (Figure 5). This reveals distinct laminar patterns for the two proteins, suggesting different quantitative relationships between putative release (DBH+) and reuptake (NET+) sites across cortical layers, consistent with the higher likelihood of Model 5.

In all layers, DBH+ segments outnumber NET+ segments by roughly 2:1 (Figure 6 and ρ = 0.3462). We also compared the laminar pattern of NET+ segment counts between V1 and V2. Our stereology data (above) and past reports (Morrison and Foote 1986) indicate that V2 is more densely innervated by the LC than is V1; none of these data speak to differences in laminar density pattern. We ran Model 5 against these data, now positing a different density pattern between V1 and V2. In doing so, we combined data for Layers 4A and 4B of V1 with Layer 3 for comparison with Layer 3 of V2 and combined data for Layers 4Cα and 4Cβ for comparison with Layer 4 of V2. Discussion

We have shown that the density of noradrenergic innervation differs between layers of the primary visual cortex (V1) and between the primary and secondary (V2) visual cortices, in line with previous reports (Kosofsky et al. 1984; Morrison and Foote 1986). In using a different anatomical marker for these axons, we have also shown that the relative density of putative release (DBH+) and reuptake (NET+) sites differs by layer in V1. Finally, in addressing some of the challenges inherent in working with small datasets (common in studies of primate brains, in particular) and in comparing quantitative and qualitative data, we have developed a novel analysis approach, on the basis of Bayesian statistics.

What might differences in the density of noradrenergic innervation between V1 and V2 mean, functionally? We first caution that, although we reported stereological data as length in mm, there are limits on directly interpreting those data biologically because the tissue was dehydrated with ethanol and xylenes before counting. This dehydration leads to shrinkage and, because the tissue is mounted on slides at the time, the deformation is different in the x/y axes (supported by the slide) compared with the z‐axis. Relative differences are, however, interpretable. Innervation density is commonly used to infer neuromodulatory “tone,” which—in this context—encapsulates a sense of the actual or potential extracellular concentration of a molecule in the tissue. This is not identical to the idea of “tonic” (as opposed to “phasic”) firing measured at the cell body, although the concepts are clearly related, and sometimes confused. Under the idea of tone that is addressed by innervation density, either (or both) basal levels (related to tonic activity) or peak levels (related to a combination of tonic and phasic activity) of NE is expected to be higher in V2 than in V1, given the higher density of axons. Drawing this conclusion requires making some assumptions, including a positive correlation between axon density and release site density, and equivalent numbers of vesicles, quantal content, and probability of release per release site. We are not aware of data that speak to most of these assumptions, but the connection between density and “tone” is presumably stronger for DBH than for NET, if the two are not measuring the same thing (see below).

DBH is an enzyme directly related to NE synthesis and is found inside NE‐containing vesicles, so prior studies that compared DBH immunoreactivity in V1 and V2 arguably directly address release site (as distinct from general axon) density. If so, then (assuming equal tortuosity and isotropy) the distance from any point in the tissue to a source of NE is expected to be shorter in V2 than in V1 (and in Layer 4B of V1 compared to layer 4Cβ). From any of these point sources, NE (which is “volume transmitted”) diffuses away until it is removed from the extracellular space (ECS) by NET.

The NET+ segments we counted, then, are like “point sinks” (sites of reuptake) in the tissue that can contribute to anisotropic diffusion (Syková and Nicholson 2008). That DBH and NET are not colocated (cannot be if their relative densities differ by area and layer) implies volume transmission by NE in V1 and V2. This is one of a number of anatomical signatures of volume transmission found in the literature on neuromodulatory systems in the mammalian central nervous system. Other signatures include the lack of a synaptic cleft or postsynaptic membrane specialization in close apposition to a release site, a difference in density and/or lack of colocation between release sites and receptors, and the existence of membrane‐associated extrasynaptic receptors. Such signatures have been reported for multiple modulatory systems (Zoli et al. 1998; Özçete, Banerjee, and Kaeser 2024). For the catecholamines specifically, a mismatch between release and reuptake site localization for the dopaminergic system has been reported in prefrontal cortex (Lewis et al. 2001) thalamus (García‐Cabezas et al. 2009) and amygdala (Freedman and Shi 2001). Sadly, the latter study also looked at serotonin reuptake transporters, but not at NET. A previous study of the noradrenergic system in macaque thalamus (Pérez‐Santos et al. 2021) did make use of antibodies directed against both DBH and NET but treated them as alternative axon markers and so did not address their relative distribution or density. Our study is then, to our knowledge, the first to consider the uncoupling of release and reuptake sites for the noradrenergic system in any species. The question of species is critical here; as has been noted previously, specifically with regard to monoamine transporters (Smith and Porrino 2008), species differences abound. We have argued elsewhere (Coppola and Disney 2018; Disney 2021; Disney and Robert 2019) that neuromodulatory systems must be understood in specific species and in distinct brain systems within a species.

When release and reuptake are uncoupled, the resulting concentration gradients in space and time would be expected to interact with receptor affinity to create a dynamic signaling landscape, potentially on a fine‐grained scale (Coppola et al. 2016). A reasonable hypothesis arising from this work is that the increase in NET+ segment density from V1 to V2 will be proportional to the increase in DBH+ segment density, potentially shortening the length scale of diffusion in V2 and yielding a finer‐grained V2 modulatory landscape. We did not quantify DBH+ segments in V2, and so our data do not speak directly to this hypothesis.

The density of noradrenergic axons in V1 differs across layers, and Figure 5 shows that the ratio of NET+ to DBH+ segments in V1 is layer specific, suggesting different laminar relationships between release and reuptake. Specifically, there is an “excess” of NET+ segments in Layers 3 and 4A, which might again be expected to shorten the length scale of diffusion, and perhaps a deficit in Layer 6, allowing diffusion over longer distances.

Overall, we also find that there are more DBH+ segments than NET+ segments in V1. So, are antibodies directed against NET and DBH not assaying the same biological structure(s)? It is reasonable to assume that, in cortex, DBH is only found in axons from the LC, as these are the only known local NE sources. NET, on the other hand, could be expressed by other structures. We did not see structures that looked like cell bodies, dendrites, or glia in tissue processed to visualize NET immunoreactivity. To further assess the likelihood that local cortical cells express NET, we pulled V1 data from a publicly available single‐nucleus RNAseq database for M. mulatta (Chiou et al. 2023). With no cells excluded (i.e., no quality control), a single copy of the mRNA transcript for NET (SLC6A2) was detected in each of 12 (of 313,628) cells across four animals (0.00004%); two of these cells were neurons. In a similar dataset from the middle temporal gyrus of M. mulatta (Jorstad et al. 2023), no copies of SLC6A2 were detected in 75,644 neurons or in 13,492 glia. It thus seems unlikely that NET is expressed by cortical neurons, and so these were not the structures we were counting.

An alternative possibility is that NET is expressed on axons arising from cell bodies that reside in a structure other than the LC or V1, perhaps one of the other monoaminergic nuclei. We cannot rule this out, but it is unlikely to contribute importantly to our results. The other source of cortically projecting catecholaminergic neurons in macaques, the ventral tegmental area (VTA), only projects to layer 1 of V1 in macaques (reviewed by Disney 2021); we do not observe particularly high NET+ segment densities in Layer 1. If serotonergic axons in V1 expressed NET, our Bayesian analysis would have more strongly favored Model 1 or 2, both of which asserted that NET = 5‐HT.

It is most likely, then, that NET+ and DBH+ segments are nonidentical domains within the same parent structure: an LC axon. If, on the other hand, NET is being expressed by structures other than LC axons, then the conclusion that counting NET+ and DBH+ segments is not counting the same thing twice—and thus that differences in expression may represent an important functional measure relevant to NE diffusion—only gets stronger. This conclusion would be weakened, however, by a difference in antibody detection failure for the two proteins (specifically by high failure for the NET), or by counting bias. With respect to our counting method, bias (if present) would seem likely to operate in the opposite direction. In our tissue, DBH+ axon segments appeared narrower and more frequently lacked inter‐varicose immunoreactivity than did NET+ segments (Figure 9). Disconnected varicosities, of the type commonly encountered in tissue stained to visualize DBH immunoreactivity, were only counted on the basis of “good continuity” (see Section 2). Any counting bias, then, more likely favors an undercount of DBH+ segments.

Antigenic sites for DBH are inside vesicles; if our antigen retrieval protocol either damaged a large number of vesicles and their contents (over‐processing) or did not open enough vesicles up to the antibody (under‐processing), either could lead to false negatives. Again, this would tend to suggest undercounting for DBH (note: we optimized primary antibody concentration, but not antigen retrieval, see Methods).

The antigenic site on the NET protein is on the cytoplasmic N‐terminal tail that is a locus for protein–protein interactions, for example, with syntaxin 1A in support of cell surface expression (Sung et al. 2003; Binda et al. 2006). The epitope might then be masked in vivo, leading to a systematic, but presumably unbiased, undercounting. Given that we are not at ceiling (we see higher NET+ segment densities in V2 than V1, and qualitatively higher again in areas V3, V3A, and V4; not shown) or at floor (except perhaps in layer 4Cβ of V1), the fact that both NET+ segment density and the ratio of NET+ to DBH+ segments differ by layer suggests that, even if epitope masking lowered NET detection, this does not entirely explain the patterns in the data.

If the differences we observe cannot be entirely explained by methodology, then it seems likely that NET+ and DBH+ segments represent nonidentical parts of the same LC axons. The quantitative analysis cannot distinguish between a case in which there are more DBH+ segments versus one in which DBH+ segments are longer than NET+ segments. We attempted a co‐labeling study to understand the extent of physical colocation, but the processing requirements for the two antibodies are incompatible (the antigen retrieval for DBH results in almost no NET detection, the Triton X‐100 used for NET yielded very high background for DBH). Our use of “good continuity” to rule‐in DBH+ segments for counting favors the former conclusion (more, not longer, segments). Differences in the precision of antigen localization, based on intracellular diffusion of the DAB reaction product, are unlikely to account for our observations if both antigens are presumed to be in the same parent structure (an axon originating in the LC). It is, however, possible that antigen retrieval for DBH disrupted the intracellular milieu more than did the protocol for NET detection, allowing greater spread of the DAB reaction product away from the original antigenic site. Again, in this case we might expect to see more inter‐varicose immunoreactivity, not less. We also cannot, from light microscopic data, determine whether the differences in subcellular localization represent differences in trafficking; NET and DBH can be trafficked independently (Matthies et al. 2009). A study at the electron microscopic level would be needed to resolve these questions, although the processing protocols we found necessary for good detection (aggressive antigen retrieval and/or high Triton X‐100 concentrations) seem unlikely to yield good ultrastructural data.

Further arguing against the idea that the different densities we observe arise from measurement artifacts, rather than true biological differences, are the differences in overall density, and in the NET/DBH ratio as a function of layer in V1. We see no reason for a laminar bias in any of the above technical considerations. What, then, can one learn from laminar differences? Based on connectivity with other brain areas (Felleman and van Essen 1991), layers in the V1 cortical circuit (Callaway 1998) are classically ascribed distinct functions. Looking at laminar differences in innervation density, then, has been used to develop hypotheses about preferential “targeting” of modulatory effects to particular visual functions. Under this framework: Layer 4C/4 (in V1/V2, respectively) receives ascending input from the previous stage of processing; the superficial layers (2−4B in V1, 2 and 3 in V2) are sites of significant local lateral connectivity and also make longer range connections with areas “higher” in the processing hierarchy; layer 5 mediates connectivity with the pulvinar and motor structures (and perhaps other cortical areas via thalamus; Sherman and Guillery 2011); and Layer 6 mediates “feedback” connectivity with “lower” areas.

If we take the density of DBH+ segments as an indication of innervation density (and NET+ sites as an indication of diffusion length scales), then we can look to the posteriors on β for DBH (Figure 6, light gray) to understand laminar differences in noradrenergic innervation. The generally high axon density in Layers 2–4B would suggest that noradrenergic modulation in V1 is directed toward layers supporting interactions with other cortical areas (2−4B) over layers mediating interactions with subcortical structures (4C−6), including the thalamus. It is interesting to note here that the LGN, which provides the major ascending visual input to V1, does not receive a projection from the LC (Hubel and Wiesel 1972; Blasdel and Lund 1983; Disney 2021).

Interestingly, if we consider the densest and least dense LC innervation in V1, we find that noradrenergic modulation appears directed toward Layer 4B—which is the V1 output to the motion‐selective visual area MT and thus considered part of the magnocellular pathway—and away from 4Cβ, which is the thalamic recipient sublayer for the parvocellular visual pathway. The magnocellular‐recipient sublayer, 4Cα, is more densely innervated than is 4Cβ, further reinforcing the idea that the noradrenergic system preferentially modulates what has been called the “where” (primarily magnocellular recipient) pathway over the “what” (parvocellular recipient) pathway (Mishkin and Ungerleider 1982; Mishkin et al. 1983; Ungerleider and Haxby 1994). Differential targeting of modulatory systems to elements of the magnocellular and parvocellular pathways is a common, and not well understood, motif in the neuromodulation of early visual processing (Disney 2021).

Although the overall structure of the LC innervation to V1 appears to have a “feedforward” bias (preferentially targeting layers that interact with other cortical areas) within the layers mediating interactions with subcortical structures, the innervation density is higher in Layer 6, which participates in a feedback loop to the LGN. One way to think about this overall pattern is that noradrenergic modulation does not appear poised to control the “gate” to cortex (Layer 4C), instead allowing visual information to pass into cortex with minimal modification, although perhaps with some differentiation between magno‐ and parvocellular information, as noted above. Once visual information is in V1, the emphasis (from the point of view of the noradrenergic system) then appears to be perception, image formation, and spatial cognition (feedforward from 2−4B to cortex, feedback from 6 to LGN) over “vision for immediate action” (the fast feedforward pathway from Layer 5 to the superior colliculus, with collaterals to brainstem).

Conclusion

The findings in this study suggest that release and reuptake sites for NE need not colocalize, suggesting a role for diffusion (i.e., volume transmission) in noradrenergic signaling. Furthermore, the ratio of release to reuptake sites appears layer‐specific, suggesting that diffusion length scale might differ on quite fine scales in cortex. If future anatomical studies can overcome the challenges of co‐labeling NET and DBH, ultrastructural reconstruction of noradrenergic axons would be invaluable in precisely localizing sources and sinks, and allowing modeling of NE diffusion and of how resulting NE concentrations interact with receptor affinity, supporting a better understanding of the ways that NE shapes cortical processing. Understanding the action of any neuromodulator in any given brain system will call for the consideration of many variables. Here, we have focused on anatomical markers related to the presence and persistence of a signaling molecule in the tissue. Our data suggest that diffusion length scale is almost certainly a variable that differs across circuits of the primate neocortex on multiple scales. This is rarely considered in conceptual or computational models of modulatory action in any species; we suggest this may be an error. The other part of the process—through which receptors that signal is received, and on which compartments of which cell types those receptors are expressed—is beyond the scope of this study. For recent reviews that consider these variables, and the broader (if perhaps somewhat problematic (Disney 2021) question of what NE “does” in cortex, the interested reader can find a number of classic and recent reviews (Fuxe et al. 2015; Agnati et al. 2010; Edeline 2012; Zhou and Chu 2025; Berridge and Waterhouse 2003; Aston‐Jones and Cohen 2005; Disney 2021).

The novel statistical approach developed for this study solves two problems commonly encountered by primate neuroanatomists. First, small sample sizes limit the rigorous use of frequentist statistics. The Bayesian approach we propose here is not only robust to small sample size but also allows comparison of conditions that are not independent of one another (e.g., in this study, single axons that traverse and are thus counted in, multiple layers). Second, our approach allows qualitative data to be integrated with quantitative data to generate updated estimates of biological measurements. This is particularly helpful in the study of less common model species for which anatomical studies might be conducted several decades apart and so span periods of time over which there may be substantial changes in practice.
