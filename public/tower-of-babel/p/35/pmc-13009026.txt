L. carnitine modulates oxidative stress, steroidogenic gene disturbance, and testicular histopathology induced by Bisphenol A in male rats
Authors: Nafisa Esmail Emam, Ola A Habotta, Amal Abdelmonem Halawa, Mamdouh Mohamed Abouelmagd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBisphenol A (BPA) is a widely used endocrine disruptor that has been implicated in reproductive toxicity. L-carnitine (LC), has shown potential protective impacts against various toxicities.AimThis study aims to assess the protective role of LC against BPA-induced reproductive toxicity in male Sprague Dawley rats.MethodologySixty adult male Sprague Dawley rats (180–250 g, 3.5 months old) were divided into six groups (n = 10). Group I (control) administered of corn oil, Group II administered LC (500 mg/kg/day), Group III administered BPA (100 mg/kg/day), Group IV administered BPA (300 mg/kg/day), Group V was co-treated with BPA (100 mg/kg/day) and LC , and Group VI administered co-treatment with BPA (300 mg/kg/day) and L-carnitine. After 70 days of treatment, sperm count, motility, and morphology were assessed. Reproductive hormone levels (Follicle Stimulating Hormone (FSH), Luteinizing Hormone (LH), and testosterone) were measured via ELISA. Oxidative stress markers as Malondialdehyde (MDA), Glutathione (GSH), Nitric Oxide (NO), Catalase (CAT), and Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), the expression levels of StAR (Steroidogenic Acute Regulatory Protein), 3β-HSD (3β-Hydroxysteroid Dehydrogenase), Cyp17a (Cytochrome P450 17A1), and aromatase were evaluated. Testicular histopathological examination was performed.ResultsBPA exposure significantly reduced sperm quality, hormone levels, and antioxidant enzyme activities, while increasing oxidative stress markers. LC co-treatment ameliorated these impacts, improving sperm count, motility, and morphology, and partially restoring hormone levels. Gene expression of steroidogenic proteins was partially restored with LC treatment, and histopathological analysis revealed less severe testicular damage compared to the BPA-alone groups.ConclusionL-carnitine demonstrates a protective role against BPA-induced reproductive toxicity in male rats.Keywords: Toxicology, L-Carnitine, Reproductive toxicity, Bisphenol A, oxidative stress

Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA) is an industrial chemical that is known to be an endocrine disruptor and is commonly found in plastic products. BPA is known to affect reproductive health in humans and animals. This includes the hormonal balance, functioning of the testes, and overall fertility in a person (Elgawish et al. 2020; Pirozzi et al. 2020). BPA is known to mimic estrogen and disrupt the endocrine system. Such mimicry disrupts the body’s oxidative system and the functioning of mitochondria. This worsens the reproductive health making the exposure to BPA is a danger to public health, especially for women in pregnancy, infants and in the case of occupational exposure, working adults (Fang et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2020).

Oxidative stress is one of the main ways by which BPA can affect organism’s reproductive health. BPA exposure has been known to trigger the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsible for causing lipid peroxidation, DNA injury, and cellular suicide in reproductive cells (Gu et al. 2021; Barghash et al. 2022). These effects impact on sperm and egg fertility, and secretion of key hormones (Sun et al. 2020). These harmful impacts have also been observed in animal studies, where the oxidative stress caused as a result of BPA exposure was found to be responsible for the dysfunction of ovaries and testes along with posing a risk to fetal developmental changes. These changes, in addition to affecting pregnancy outcomes, increase the long-term health risks for the offspring (El Henafy et al. 2020).

L-carnitine (LC), in conjunction with other nutrients, aids in the transportation of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria, where they are oxidized to produce energy (Kooshesh et al. 2023). This process is important for energy supply in cells, especially for organs with higher energy demands such as the heart and muscles (Mateus et al. 2023). It is made in the body from the amino acids lysine and methionine and is concentrated in the fatty acid-using tissues, like skeletal muscles, the heart, and liver. It serves the important function of maintaining energy metabolism in the cells and, therefore, is basic to improve metabolic health. It is worth noting that LC may also be ingested from dietary sources, particularly from meats and other animal derivatives (Li et al. 2021). LC also aids in the elimination of other metabolic waste products in excess, such as acetyl-CoA, which is produced inside the cells and needs to be removed. Moreover, LC has been associated with oxidative stress modulation, improving mitochondrial activity, and enhancing overall response to exercise and endurance training (Mateus et al. 2023; Petrillo et al. 2021).

In this regard, our study aims to evaluate the protective impact of LC against BPA-induced reproductive toxicity in male Sprague Dawley rats. We focused on sperm quality (count, motility, and morphology), hormone levels (FSH, LH, testosterone), oxidative stress markers (MDA, GSH, NO, CAT, SOD), expression of steroidogenesis-related genes (StAR, 3β-HSD, Cyp17a, Aromatase), and histopathological analysis of the testes.

Materials and methods

Tested compounds

BPA was purchased from Sigma Chemical Co., Germany. LC was obtained from Mepaco-MediFood Co., Egypt.

Experimental animals and design

Sixty (60) apparently healthy mature male Sprague Dawley rats, each weighs between 200 and 250 g and approximately 3.5 months old, were obtained from the Medical Research Institute at Mansoura University. They were kept in plastic cages under the environmental conditions were kept at 23 °C, 14-hr light and 10-hr dark cycles. During the experiment, the rats were given standard lab pelleted feed in addition to water, which was freely available. The rats were kept in the lab conditions for 2 weeks prior to the experiment.

After acclimatization, rats were randomly divided into six groups (n = 10 each). Sixty (60) mature male Sprague Dawley rats were randomly separated into six groups;

Group I administered 5 mL/kg of corn oil orally as a control.

Group II administered 500 mg/kg/day of LC via intraperitoneal (I.P) (Lebda et al. 2020).

Group III was treated orally with BPA at 100 mg/kg body weight dissolved in corn oil for 70 consecutive days (Abdul-Hassan and Hussein 2022).

Group IV administered orally with BPA at 300 mg/kg body weight dissolved in corn oil for 70 consecutive days (Sharf-El Deen et al. 2015).

Group V administered BPA (100 mg/kg orally) and LC (500 mg/kg/day i.p.).

Group VI administered BPA (300 mg/kg/day orally) and LC (500 mg/kg/day i.p.).

The experimental period of 70 days was selected based on previous studies, including that of Edres et al. (2018), who investigated the impact of LC on Bisphenol A-induced kidney damage in rats. In their work, Bisphenol A was administered to induce toxicity, and LC was used as a therapeutic agent for a different organ system. Their findings demonstrated that this duration was sufficient to establish consistent Bisphenol A-induced toxicity while also providing an adequate treatment period for LC to exert its protective effects. The 70-day experimental period was also selected because it approximately corresponds to a full spermatogenic cycle in rats, allowing us to evaluate the effect of the administered drugs on reproductive (spermatogenic) cells and on the entire process of sperm formation.

Samples collection and preparation

Samples were collected at 70 days’ post-experiment initiation. Animals from each group were lightly anesthetized using isoflurane (induction 3–4% in oxygen via chamber, maintenance 1.5–3% via nose cone) in accordance with AVMA guidelines (Jiron et al. 2019‏). At the end of the study, all animals were euthanized 24 h after the final injection of thiopental sodium (100 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection. The collected samples consisted of whole blood (for serum biochemistry and reproductive hormones), testes (used for homogenates, oxidative-stress assays, RT-qPCR, and histology), and cauda epididymides (for evaluation of sperm motility, count, and morphology).

Preparation of tissue homogenate

The testes were excised and washed with saline solution. One gram from each testis was blended with 9 ml of ice-cold phosphate buffer solution (PBS) at pH 7.4 in Falcon tubes, using a homogenizer. The homogenates were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The Supernatants were carefully siphoned off, aliquoted, and stored at − 20 °C until their antioxidant enzyme activity was assessed.

Sperm motility, sperm count, and sperm morphology

In assessing sperm motility, the caudal portion of the epididymis was isolated and a sperm suspension was made in phosphate-buffered saline, which was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, then observed in phase contrast microscope at 400x magnification. Sperm motility was categorized as motile and non-motile, and the percentage of motile sperm was obtained for each rat. For sperm count, the cauda epididymis was cut in PBS, and sperm were counted in a hemocytometer at 200 magnifications with sperm count per milliliter calculated. Sperm morphology was evaluated with the preparation of a sperm smear which was eosin-nigrosin stained, air-dried, and examined in a light microscope at 1000x magnification. Sperm morphology was categorized as normal and abnormal based on head, midpiece, or tail defects, and the percentage of abnormal sperm was computed for each rat. For the experimental groups of each rat, the results of sperm motility, count, and morphology were averaged for each group.

Estimations of reproductive hormones

Plasma hormonal concentrations of testosterone (Cat. no. ab108666; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), luteinizing hormone (LH; Cat. no. ENZ-KIT107-0001; Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH; Cat. no. KA2330; Novus Biologicals, CO, USA) were evaluated utilising designated assay kits via ELISA, adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The optical densities were measured at 450 nm using a reference wavelength of 620–630 nm using a spectrophotometer (BioTek Inc., Germany).

Assessment of oxidative stress and anti-oxidant enzymes activities

Testicular homogenate samples were analyzed for malondialdehyde (MDA), reduced glutathione (GSH), nitric oxide (NO), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD). The lipid peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit from Cayman Chemical, based in the USA, is commonly used to assess oxidative stress. The catalog number is 10,009,055. Reduced glutathione (GSH) colorimetric assay kit (AssayGenie, Dublin, Ireland) was used to determine the GSH content following the manufacturer’s instructions. Nitric oxide concentration was measured by the enzymatic colorimetric method. The catalase activity assay kit from Sigma-Aldrich, headquartered in the USA (catalog number MAK381), was measured. The SOD assay kit from Dojindo Laboratories in Japan (catalog number S311) provides a spectrophotometric method to measure the activity of superoxide dismutase.

Molecular analysis

The study utilized Real-Time PCR to determine the levels of gene expression in treated and untreated cells. Total RNA was extracted using the PureLink® RNA Mini Kit, followed by cDNA synthesis using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit. For Real-Time PCR amplification, the Maxima SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix was used, with primers designed for the target genes StAR (Steroidogenic Acute Regulatory Protein), 3β-HSD (3β-Hydroxysteroid Dehydrogenase), Cyp17a (Cytochrome P450 17A1), Aromatase and GAPDH as an internal control. The process involved RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and PCR amplification, with primer sequences specific for each gene of interest. The CT values obtained for each gene were normalized against GAPDH. The results were analyzed by comparing the transcription levels of the target genes to those of the control group.

Histopathology evaluation

Tissue specimens were kept in 10% buffered formalin solution for 48 h for fixation. Specimens were then dehydrated with alcohol, cleared using xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. A microtome (Leica RM 2155, England) was used to slice the paraffin wax blocks into sections of 5 𝜇m thickness. Sections were stained using H&E. The grading of histopathologic lesions was done based on the composite of quantitative and qualitative changes of tissue structures in five fields in each section.

Statistical analysis

As mean ± SE (Mean ± Standard Error) was utilized to express the results, the treatment groups’ impact on the individual parameters was assessed through a one-way as well as a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a post hoc Duncan multiple test. The level of significance set at P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For all statistical analyses and charting, Graph Pad Prism version 8.0.2 and SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp, NY, USA) were utilized.

Impact of LC on reproductive hormones in BPA-exposed rats

The data presented in Table 2 demonstrate that BPA exposure significantly impairs reproductive hormone levels in male rats, with dose-dependent impacts. Both FSH and LH levels were markedly reduced in the BPA 100 and BPA 300 groups compared to the control, with the BPA 300 group showing the most severe suppression (FSH: 0.1775 ± 0.005 mIU/ml; LH: 0.1135 ± 0.08 mIU/ml), indicating profound disruption of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. Similarly, testosterone levels significantly declined in the BPA 100 (2.150 ± 0.61 ng/ml) and BPA 300 (1.030 ± 0.09 ng/ml) groups, confirming the androgenic toxicity of BPA. However, co-administration of LC (LC) with BPA partially restored hormone levels in both dose groups, with BPA 300 + LC rats showing significantly improved FSH (0.2935 ± 0.009), LH (0.2235 ± 0.029), and testosterone (2.985 ± 0.74) levels compared to BPA 300 alone. These findings suggest that LC has a protective role against BPA-induced endocrine disruption.

Impact of LC on testicular oxidative stress in BPA-exposed rats

The results in Fig. 1 highlight the oxidative stress induced by BPA exposure and the protective role of LC in male rats. MDA was significantly elevated in the BPA 100 (23.89 ± 1.60) and especially in the BPA 300 group (36.75 ± 1.45), indicating dose-dependent oxidative damage. Correspondingly, antioxidant defenses such as GSH, CAT, and SOD were markedly reduced in these groups, with GSH decreasing to 1.557 ± 0.26 in BPA 100 and further to 0.685 ± 0.04 in BPA 300, and CAT and SOD showing significant declines as well. Nitric oxide which can contribute to nitrosative stress, was significantly increased, particularly in the BPA 300 group (5.270 ± 0.46). In contrast, co-treatment with LC substantially mitigated these impacts. For example, the BPA 300 + LC group showed a reduction in MDA (21.20 ± 2.70), partial recovery in GSH (1.312 ± 0.42), and improved CAT (3.240 ± 0.30) and SOD (158.6 ± 20.4) activities compared to BPA 300 alone. These findings indicate that LC supplementation ameliorates BPA-induced oxidative damage by enhancing antioxidant defenses and lowering lipid peroxidation and NO levels.

Impact of LC on reproductive related genes in BPA-exposed rats

Overall, LC treatment alone significantly increased the expression of StAR, 3β-HSD, and Cyp17a compared to control, with the highest levels observed in LC for these genes. Conversely, BPA exposure (BPA 100 or BPA 300) markedly reduced the expression of these genes, in a dose-dependent manner, with BPA 300 showing the most pronounced. Co-treatment with LC partially restored expression levels in both BPA 100 + LC and BPA 300 + LC groups, though the recovery was incomplete compared to LC alone. Interestingly, Aromatase mRNA expression showed an opposite trend; BPA exposure, especially at 300 mg/kg, significantly upregulated its expression, while LC alone did not alter it relative to control. Co-treatment with LC moderated this BPA-induced elevation, indicating a potential normalizing impact (Fig. 1)(Fig. 2).

Discussion

BPA is an endocrine-disrupting chemical that is commonly found in plastics and poses a significant risk to reproductive health by disrupting hormones, inducing oxidative stress, and causing mitochondrial dysfunction. BPA is known to impair fertility, developmental health, and pose toxic risks to vulnerable populations. One of the mechanisms of BPA toxicity is stimulation of oxidative stress and the secretion of ROS that harm reproductive cells and disrupt hormonal secretions (Cao et al. 2020). Alharthi et al. (2020) reported that LC, a compound essential for mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, is protective against BPA-induced damage by increasing energy metabolism and reducing oxidative stress.

BPA exposure lowered sperm counts, with the BPA 300 group displaying the lowest count. This confirms BPA’s spermatotoxicity, likely due to oxidative stress disrupting spermatogenesis. This is supported by Rafiee et al. (2021) who documented diminished sperm counts due to BPA and subsequent mitochondrial damage. Administering LC replenished the decline, with BPA 100 + LC and BPA 300 + LC increasing, respectively. Improvements are in line with Said et al. (2023) who confirmed LC ’s protective impact on spermatogenesis. In contrast, Nguyen et al. (2022) studied bovine oocytes and spermatozoa reported oxidative stress without notable declines in sperm counts at lower BPA exposures, indicating possible species-specific or dose-dependent threshold impacts.

Sperm motility was severely affected by BPA exposure. This already confirms the impact that BPA has on the mitochondrial energy metabolism of spermatozoa (Nguyen et al. 2022). The motility improvement marks its significance in LC-treated groups. The data suggest that LC plays the role of partially improving sperm motility. The protective role of LC in partially restoring motility as demonstrated in the BPA + LC groups, strongly aligns with Shalaby et al. (2022) who reported that LC has been able to improve sperm motility in rats exposed to dimethoate due to its antioxidative and mitochondrial protective impacts. Mateus et al. (2023) also reported the positive impact of LC on sperm motility and energy metabolism in male infertility clinical settings. BPA exposure significantly increased the proportion of sperm with abnormal morphology, with the highest value in the BPA 300 group compared to the control.

The aforementioned increase is consistent with Salehabadi et al. (2022) findings on BPA exposure’s impact on DNA fragmentation and spermatozoan abnormalities. LC supplementation did improve clinically significant morphological changes, in the BPA 100 + LC group and BPA 300 + LC group, demonstrating its partial corrective influence. This is in agreement with Alvi et al. (2022) where LC was shown to protect against oxidative damage and sperm deformities. Using transcriptomic analyses, Sun et al. (2020) demonstrated the ability of BPA to disrupt critical genes for the development of spermiogenesis and acrosomal structures in zebrafish, which explains the increased severity of the morphological abnormalities. Hadi et al. (2020) reported increased sperm deformity and DNA fragmentation in BPA-exposed infertile men. The LC mitigating impact in the BPA + LC groups is consistent with findings of Ezati et al. (2020) where LC was shown to decrease abnormal sperm forms from formalin exposure in mice due to increased chromatin stability and decreased apoptosis. Supporting Elokil et al. (2021) with transcriptomic profiling, found that LC directly boosts normal spermatogenesis and morphology by upregulating relevant genes.

BPA exposure caused a significant suppressive impact on serum FSH levels with a clear dose dependent line. This corroborates the impacts of BPA exposure on the function of the HPG axis with FSH, as Frobenius FSH is an essential hormone in spermatogenesis. Awad et al. (2024) identified reduced gonadotropin secretions due to BPA exposure which ultimately negatively affects the reproductive capabilities of the exposed individual. This negative impact was somewhat mitigated when LC was co-administered, especially in FSH levels in the BPA 100 + LC group and the BPA 300 + LC group. LC seemed to have provided some level of functional restoration as described in Salama et al. (2021) and Said et al. (2023), where LC restored functioning after a stress. This is in contrast to Malmir et al. (2021) where some imbalance restoration was posited to be the impact of some incorporated antioxidants to some elagic toxins which would restore hormonal balance, suggesting that the restorative impact of LC would be negligible under high toxic exposure.

This was especially clear in the BPA 300 group, where the LH levels in this group fell compared to the control group, which illustrates the BPA-induced disruption of the signal that stimulates Leydig cells to produce testosterone. This was also clear in the BPA 100 + LC and BPA 300 + LC groups, where LC mitigated the impact of BPA as the LH levels in those groups were in the expected range. Building on these findings, Al Malahi et al. (2022) and Iftikhar et al. (2022) demonstrated the ability of LC to reinstate the functionality of poisoned pituitary and Leydig cells.

Post exposure to BPA, there was a significant decrease in testosterone levels in both the BPA 100 and BPA 300 groups. This finding is consistent with the observations of Alvi et al. (2022) and Salehabadi et al. (2022), who reported the suppression of testosterone levels post BPA exposure due to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress within Leydig cells. LC showed a significant restoration in testosterone levels, suggesting enhanced steroidogenic activity. Similar conclusions regarding LC’s impacts related to toxic stress alleviation and improved mitochondrial function were made by Abd Elkader et al. (2024).

BPA exposed rats showed even greater levels of the MDA marker for peroxidation and oxidative damage in the BPA 300 group compared to the control group, suggesting oxidative damage to the testicular tissue is dose dependent. LC again was effective in reducing MDA in the BPA 100 + LC and BPA 300 + LC groups. This finding was consistent with Salama et al. (2021) finding that LC protects against peroxidation and damage to membranes of biological tissues in reproductive tissues suffering from oxidation stress. The reduction of MDA value in BPA 300 + LC following LC treatment was consistent with Shalaby et al. (2022), which reported LC reduced MDA levels of dimethoate-treated rats by inhibiting lipid peroxidation. This demonstrates LC’s capacity to enhance restoration of membrane damage sustained as a result of oxidative stress.

BPA exposure drastically decreased the controls, GSH levels in BPA 100, and in BPA 300, which shows the loss of intracellular antioxidants. The administration of LC restored GSH levels remarkably, especially in BPA 100 + LC which exceeded the control value, signifying its effectiveness at replenishing the thiol antioxidant reserves. Al Malahi et al. (2022) reported similar findings which proved LC’s ability to restore GSH levels and reduce oxidative stress damage. The recovery of GSH in the BPA 300 + LC group corroborates the findings of Khedr and Werida (2022) where LC was shown to restore GSH content in testes with oxidative damage by increasing the activity of glutathione reductase. These findings support the hypothesis of LC assisting in the restoration of intracellular redox balance during BPA toxicity.

BPA significantly increased the testicular levels of NO, marking nitrosative stress, in BPA 100 and in BPA 300 versus the controls. There is a possibility that NO in excess can yield peroxynitrite, thereby contributing to cellular damage and fragmentation of sperm DNA. Co-treatment with LC mitigated NO levels in the BPA 100 + LC and BPA 300 + LC groups respectively, which indicates inhibition of inflammatory and oxidative pathways. These findings are in agreement with the findings of Iftikhar et al. (2022) where normalization of NO levels with LC was reported in models of reproductive stress induced by deleterious chemicals.

BPA impacted CAT activity, with the BPA 300 group showing the greatest reduction compared to the controls, suggesting a weakened response in the enzymatic protection system against hydrogen peroxide. LC resulted in greater CAT levels in both treated groups, in BPA 100 + LC and in BPA 300 + LC, a finding that indicates a partial recovery of the enzyme. These results corroborate the results of Said et al. (2023) that LC is capable of activating key antioxidant enzymes and restoring the function of enzymatic defense systems in tissues that have undergone oxidative stress.

Moreover, SOD activity demonstrates the profound impact BPA had on SOD activity, where SOD activity was measured at BPA 300 which is around a third of the control group, which indicates oxidative damage to the mitochondria. LC co-treatment at BPA 300 + LC and BPA 100 + LC showed LC co-treatment SOD values respectively, indicating sustained enhancement of mitochondrial function. These results align with Badawy et al. (2022) when assessing the impacts of LC on mitochondrial SOD, where LC was shown to enhance SOD activity stimulating mitochondrial functions and gene expression.

Our study’s findings showed that BPA exposure, especially at the higher dose significantly diminished the expression of steroidogenic genes, StAR, 3β-HSD, and Cyp17a, demonstrating altered steroidogenesis within the testes. Co-treatment with LC partially normalized the expression of these genes; greater restoration was observed at the lower dose (BPA 100 + LC), while recovery at the higher dose (BPA 300 + LC) was more limited. Notably, the expression of aromatase was upregulated significantly by BPA exposure, but exhibited a contrasting pattern of aromatase expression decline. Compared to the control, LC did not significantly change aromatase expression, but in the presence of BPA, LC co-treatment mitigated the BPA-enhanced expression of aromatase, suggesting some form of normalizing control.

Shooree et al. (2023) regarding BPA exposure and negative impacts on steroidogenesis through key ovarian tissue enzymes, aromatase, and several others. In our case, BPA exposure similarly affected the expression of steroidogenic genes StAR, 3β-HSD, and Cyp17a. In contrast to Shooree et al. (2023) which noted a reduction in aromatase expression, our study documented a dose-dependent upregulation of aromatase following BPA exposure. Co-treatment with LC diminished this upregulation, indicating a possible normalizing impact of LC on aromatase gene expression in the testes. Additionally, Risalde et al. (2021) noted that BPA exposure significantly altered the immunohistochemical expression of aromatase enzymes (cyp19a1a in the ovary and cyp19a1b in the brain) in zebrafish, indicating endocrine-disrupting activity. These findings demonstrate that BPA interferes with estrogen synthesis pathways in both reproductive and neural tissues in a dose-dependent manner.

Pötzlet al (2024) demonstrated that BPA exposure altered steroidogenic gene expression within the gonads of rare minnows, especially StAR and 3β-HSD. This aligns with our findings that BPA exposure decreased the expression of the same steroidogenic genes in male rats. The protective impact of LC in reversing these gene expression changes is supported by Alyasari and Selman (2023) showing that LC-loaded nanoparticles attenuated cypermethrin/induced reproductive toxicity along with steroidogenic gene expression changes. We, along with Ahmed et al. (2014), demonstrated that LC mitigated gamma irradiation/testicular dysfunction, indicating that LC may provide significant reproductive protective modulating toxic changes by gene expression regulation, particularly steroidogenesis. Collectively these studies confirmed the critical role of genes StAR, 3β-HSD, Cyp17a, and aromatase along with LC provides as a therapeutic agent to restore toxic damage through normal gene expression.

The histological findings from Group B and the BPA 100 + LC group clearly demonstrate a protective impact of LC, as indicated by the relatively preserved seminiferous tubule architecture, minimal vacuolation, and presence of luminal sperms. These observations align with multiple studies affirming the antioxidant and cytoprotective properties of LC against testicular toxicants. For instance, Bushra and Bastwrous (2022) reported significant improvement in spermatogenic integrity and seminiferous tubule structure following LC co-treatment in cisplatin-exposed rats. Similarly, Alm-Eldeen et al. (2020) found that LC preserved germinal epithelium and minimized necrosis, comparable to our findings in the BPA 100 + LC group. Additionally, Shalaby et al. (2022) demonstrated that LC counteracts insecticide-induced vacuolation and germ cell loss by enhancing testicular antioxidant status and reducing ROS-mediated damage, reinforcing its histological benefits in mild-to-moderate toxicity models.

Conclusion

The study revealed that BPA exposure in adult male rats adversely affected reproduction, with a marked deterioration in sperm parameters, hormonal imbalance, oxidative damage, and severe oxidative damage and histopathological deterioration in the testicular tissues. These adverse impacts were also dose-dependent with the higher BPA dose causing more significant damage. Addition of LC co-administration counteracted some of these adverse changes by improving spermiogram parameters, correcting hormonal changes, improving the antioxidant defense system, gene expression of steroidogenic proteins was partially restored with LC treatment, and protecting the architecture of testicular tissue. Though LC could not completely counteract the toxic impacts of BPA, it conferred marked protective impacts, underscoring the importance of considering it as a possible supportive therapeutic agent in environmentally induced male infertility.
