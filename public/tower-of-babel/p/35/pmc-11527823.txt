Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibition through henagliflozin ameliorates cognitive impairment in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Shujun Zhang, Fen Wang, Lei Xie, Jialu Xu, Xiaoqing Song, Jing Tao, Juan Chen, Delin Ma, Xuefeng Yu, Xiaoli Shi, Yan Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims/IntroductionTo assess whether the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, henagliflozin, improves cognitive impairment in patients with type 2 diabetes.Materials and MethodsWe carried out a prospective study on 290 patients with type 2 diabetes and cognitive impairment. Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores and plasma phosphorylated tau181 levels were used to assess cognition. The association between henagliflozin use and changes in cognition was examined using multivariable logistic regression analysis.ResultsMontreal Cognitive Assessment scores at enrollment and after 6 months were 21 (interquartile range [IQR]19–23) versus 22 (IQR 20–25; P < 0.0001) in all patients, 21 (IQR 19–23) versus 24 (IQR 22–26; P < 0.0001) in the henagliflozin group and 21 (IQR 19–22) versus 21 (IQR 19–23; P > 0.05) in the non‐sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor group. Logistic regression analysis showed that henagliflozin treatment was associated with Montreal Cognitive Assessment score improvement independent of potential confounders (odds ratio [OR] 3.670, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.224–6.056, P < 0.0001). Additionally, plasma phosphorylated tau181 levels significantly decreased at 6‐month follow up in all patients (OR 11.5, 95% CI 9.9–13.7 vs OR 10.1, 95% CI 7.8–12.9, P < 0.0001) and in the henagliflozin group (OR 11.5, 95% CI 10.3–13.0 vs OR 9.2, 95% CI 7.1–10.7, P < 0.0001), but not in the non‐sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor group. Henagliflozin treatment was independently associated with decreased phosphorylated tau181 levels (OR 3.670, 95% CI 1.598–4.213, P < 0.0001).ConclusionsHenagliflozin treatment was independently associated with improvements in Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores and plasma phosphorylated tau181 levels, indicating significant beneficial effects on cognitive impairment in patients with type 2 diabetes.Keywords: Cognitive impairment, Henagliflozin, Type 2 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic cognitive impairment generally refers to cognitive impairment in patients with diabetes 1 . The main risks of diabetes are acute and chronic complications. Diabetes poses risks of both acute and chronic complications, with cognitive dysfunction particularly noted in type 2 diabetes patients. Type 2 diabetes is characterized by insulin resistance and progressive dysfunction of pancreatic β‐cells, affecting learning, memory, mental flexibility and mental speed 2 . Individuals with type 2 diabetes have a 1.5–2‐fold higher risk of developing dementia than people without diabetes 3 . The relationship between diabetes and dementia is complex and multifactorial. Possible mechanisms include brain insulin resistance, excessive amyloid‐β deposition, elevated phosphorylated tau (P‐tau) levels, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress and blood–brain barrier dysfunction 4 .

A wide range of medications are available for the management of type 2 diabetes, including metformin, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, acarbose, dipeptidyl‐peptidase 4 inhibitors, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is), glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists and insulin. Due to their multiple effects in lowering blood glucose, as well as improving insulin resistance and inflammation, the influence of antidiabetic drugs on type 2 diabetes‐associated cognitive dysfunction has attracted increasing attention. Studies have shown that different antidiabetic drugs have varying effects on cognitive dysfunction 5 , 6 , 7 . Nevertheless, a Cochrane review did not find compelling evidence supporting the effectiveness of any specific treatment in preventing or delaying cognitive impairment in patients with type 2 diabetes 8 .

SGLT2is represent a newer class of antidiabetic agents for type 2 diabetes treatment. SGLT2is exert their antidiabetic effect by inhibiting SGLT2 in the renal tubules, reducing glucose reabsorption from urine and promoting urinary glucose excretion, thus lowering blood glucose levels 9 , 10 . Dapagliflozin, canagliflozin, ertugliflozin and empagliflozin have been approved for the treatment of adults with type 2 diabetes since 2013. Research has shown that SGLT2i treatment improves glycemic control and reduces bodyweight 11 , 12 . Interestingly, recent studies also showed the neuroprotective effects of SGLT2i treatment 9 , 13 . Animal studies showed that empagliflozin ameliorated cognitive decline by reducing cerebral oxidative stress, elevating cerebral brain‐derived neurotrophic factor and conferring beneficial effects on the neurovascular unit 14 , 15 . A randomized controlled trial involving 39 older patients with type 2 diabetes treated with SGLT2i and incretin found no evidence of cognitive function deterioration with either agent 16 . Furthermore, recent studies showed that empagliflozin improves cognitive impairment in frail older adults with type 2 diabetes 17 , 18 , also supported by a meta‐analysis 19 .

Henagliflozin is the first SGLT2i independently developed in China, and has been approved for the treatment of adults with type 2 diabetes since 31 December 2021. The phase III trial carried out in an all‐Chinese type 2 diabetes population showed significantly beneficial effects, including reduced hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C) from enrollment, as well as improvements in bodyweight and blood pressure 20 , 21 . In the present study, we sought to investigate the effect of henagliflozin on cognitive function in patients with type 2 diabetes and cognitive impairment. P‐tau181 was used in the present study as a quantitative biomarker of cognitive decline 22 , 23 .

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants and study design

Consecutive patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes from Tongji Hospital, Wuhan, China, admitted between January 2023 and December 2023 were enrolled in this prospective observational study. The inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥45 years with a confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. The exclusion criteria included a history of stroke and/or acute myocardial infarction and a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) >26, and patients who had used SGLT2i drugs. We included 135 participants who used henagliflozin during 6‐month follow up, and 155 participants who were not treated with regular drugs except for SGLT2i. Finally, 290 participants were included in the analysis. This study adhered to medical ethics standards and was approved by the Ethical Committee of Tongji Hospital (Approval No.: TJ‐IRB20230809). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection

Clinical data from hospitalized patients were collected at enrollment, including variables, such as age, sex, body mass index, smoking history, drinking history, course of diabetes, education level (lower: 6–12 years, higher: ≥12 years), diabetic macroangiopathy (defined as coronary heart disease, transient ischemic attack, peripheral artery disease or heart failure), diabetic microangiopathy (defined as diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy and peripheral neuropathy) and other complications (excluding those listed in the exclusion criteria). Medication information was obtained from clinical records. Information on the usage of SGLT2i (henagliflozin), as well as metformin, insulin and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists, was specifically extracted from pharmacy records. Based on whether they used henagliflozin during follow up, participants were divided into the SGLT2i group and non‐SGLT2i group.

Laboratory data collected included total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein and HbA1C. Fasting blood samples were collected after a minimum overnight fast of 10 h and analyzed for biochemical measurements. TC and low‐density lipoprotein were measured using an auto‐analyzer (Hitachi 7600, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). HbA1C was measured using a high‐performance liquid chromatography system (model HLC‐126723 G7; Tosoh Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Evaluation of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed at enrollment and after 6 months using neuropsychological assessments (MoCA) and P‐tau181. The MoCA is a standardized questionnaire utilized for rapid screening for mild cognitive impairment. It includes 11 items across eight cognitive domains: attention and concentration, executive function, memory, language, visual structure skills, abstract thinking, calculation, and orientation 24 . MoCA scores range from 0 to 30; a score of ≥26 is considered normal. The plasma levels of P‐tau181 were measured using the Simoa HD‐1 (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) 22 , 23 .

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Normality Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to analyze data distributions. Normally distributed variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using Student's t‐test. Variables with skewed distributions are expressed as the median (interquartile range), and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, and were analyzed using the χ2‐test. Logistic regression analysis was carried out to calculate odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for improvement in MoCA score and P‐tau181. A two‐tailed P < 0.05 is considered significant.

Participants were stratified into two groups based on antidiabetic treatment: the SGLT2 group received 10 mg henagliflozin in addition to standard therapy (n = 135), and the non‐SGLT2i group received standard therapy alone (n = 155). There were no significant differences at enrollment between the two groups in terms of age, sex, type 2 diabetes duration, body mass index, BP, HbA1C, TC, low‐density lipoprotein, education and complications (all P > 0.05). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in terms of metformin, insulin or GLP‐1 receptor agonists use between receiving henagliflozin and those not receiving SGLT2is (all P > 0.05). Importantly, there were no statistically significant differences in MoCA scores and P‐tau181 levels between the two groups at enrollment (all P > 0.05).

Beneficial effects of henagliflozin on cognitive impairment

The medians (interquartile range) of MoCA scores at enrollment and 6‐month follow up were 21 (19–23) versus 22 (20–25; P < 0.0001) in all patients, 21 (19–23) versus 24 (22–26; P < 0.0001) in the SGLT2i group and 21 (19–22) versus 21 (19–23) (P = 0.274) in the non‐SGLT2i group (Figure 1). We then carried out a multivariable logistic regression analysis using the improvement in MoCA score as the dependent variable while controlling for model potential confounders (Table 2). The results showed that henagliflozin treatment was associated with improvement in MoCA score (OR 3.670, 95% CI 2.224–6.056, P < 0.0001) independent of potential confounders, indicating a significant effect of henagliflozin on ameliorating cognitive impairment.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we found that henagliflozin treatment was associated with improvements in MoCA scores and P‐tau181 levels independent of potential confounders in patients with type 2 diabetes and cognitive impairment. Our results suggest a beneficial effect of henagliflozin on cognitive impairment in this population.

The MoCA is a standardized questionnaire utilized for rapid screening for mild cognitive impairment 24 , but it involves an evaluation that can be subjective. In contrast, P‐tau181 is a plasma or cerebrospinal fluid biomarker that has been associated with memory decline and the incidence of dementia, suggesting its potential as a cost‐effective and promising tool for rapid diagnosis of cognitive impairment 22 , 23 . Previous studies have shown a negative correlation between P‐tau181 levels and MoCA scores in patients with Alzheimer's disease, mild cognitive impairment or other types of cognitive declines 22 , 23 , 25 , 26 , including in patients with type 2 diabetes 27 , suggesting that a higher level of P‐tau181 might indicate an increased risk of cognitive impairment in this population.

Cognitive impairment caused by diabetes is often neglected due to its diverse and nonspecific clinical manifestations. Patients with cognitive impairment frequently experience impaired learning and memory abilities, as well as reduced performance in information processing speed, attention and executive function 28 . In severe cases, there might be a noticeable decline in the ability to carry out daily activities, adversely impacting quality of life. Cognitive impairment might contribute to poor treatment compliance among patients with diabetes, potentially leading to an increased risk of severe hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia and other acute complications associated with diabetes. Severe hypoglycemia can aggravate cognitive impairment, whereas dementia might obscure the clinical symptoms of hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia. Diabetes and cognitive impairment affect and promote each other, thereby forming a vicious circle. In addition to early detection of risk factors, optimizing blood glucose control and prompt intervention for cognitive impairment with conventional treatment, it is also important to consider the use of appropriate antidiabetic drugs to address cognitive impairment in diabetes.

Antidiabetic agents improve hyperglycemia, insulin resistance and cellular metabolism, and reduce tissue inflammation and oxidative stress associated with insulin‐resistant states. These agents might also positively impact cellular metabolism in the brain and improve cognitive performance. Studies have shown that inhaled insulin can increase the insulin concentration in the cerebrospinal fluid, thereby improving cognitive status 29 . Additionally, metformin and GLP‐1 receptor agonists might improve cognitive function and reduce the risk of dementia 30 , 31 . Furthermore, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, dipeptidyl‐peptidase 4 inhibitors and SGLT2i might have a protective effect on cognitive function. However, evidence from clinical studies is insufficient. Under appropriate conditions, glycemic management in patients with diabetes and cognitive impairment should prioritize the use of these drugs, along with close monitoring of changes in cognitive function. In the present study, the use of metformin and GLP‐1 receptor agonists was 81.4 and 40.7%, respectively. The use of metformin, insulin or GLP‐1 receptor agonists was comparable between the henagliflozin and non‐SGLT2i groups, thereby eliminating the potential effects of these drugs on cognition. The present results suggest that henagliflozin has a beneficial effect on cognitive function that is independent of other antidiabetic drugs.

SGLT2i treatment has been shown to improve cardiovascular energetics, reduce vascular tone and blood pressure, and decrease systemic inflammation 29 , 30 , 32 , 33 . Recent studies using a mouse model of type 2 diabetes reported that glycemic control with empagliflozin significantly ameliorates cognitive decline 14 . Previous research also suggested a beneficial effect of SGLT2i treatment on cognitive function in patients with type 2 diabetes. Specifically, SGLT2i improves cognitive and physical impairment in frail older adults with diabetes and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction 17 . Additionally, a study investigating the relationship between SGLT2i use and Repeatable Battery for Neuropsychological Status score changes in patients with type 2 diabetes showed a previously unobserved association between prolonged SGLT2i use for ≥3 years and improved cognitive scores globally, as well as in the language and executive function domains 31 . A study carried out in frail older adults with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease observed beneficial effects on both cognitive and physical function after SGLT2i treatment 18 . Further studies are needed to validate these findings.

Individuals with type 2 diabetes have a higher risk of dementia compared with people without diabetes 3 . The exact mechanisms are still incompletely understood, but might involve brain insulin resistance, excessive amyloid‐β deposition, elevated P‐tau levels, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress and blood–brain barrier dysfunction 4 . Previous studies have shown that different antidiabetic drug classes have some effects on cognitive dysfunction, possibly through dealing with type 2 diabetes‐related issues, such as insulin resistance, systemic inflammation and oxidative stress related to hyperglycemia 5 , 6 , 7 . SGLT2is improve glycemic control and reduce bodyweight 11 , 12 , and their hypoglycemic effects should contribute to the protection of cognitive functions. Potential mechanisms for the beneficial effects of SGLT2is on cognition include their protective effect on oxidative stress and atherosclerosis, as well as reducing vascular damage, as shown in animal studies 32 , 34 . Recent evidence also suggests pleiotropic effects, such as the improvement of mitochondrial function and antioxidative effects 35 . The neuroprotective effects of SGLT2is could also involve reduced inflammation, M2 polarization, Janus kinase 2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 and NOD‐, LRR‐ and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 inflammasome inhibition, and a reduction in carotid intima‐media thickness 36 . A recent meta‐analysis also supports the cognitive protective effects of SGLT2is 19 . In addition, SGLT2is might exert direct neuroprotective effects to improve cognitive function, such as inhibiting acetylcholinesterase and increasing brain‐derived neurotrophic factor levels 36 . Nevertheless, the present study was not designed to address the mechanisms of the neuroprotection. The pathophysiological mechanisms of the neuroprotective effect of SGLT2i require further investigation.

The present results hold significant clinical implications for the development of management strategies for patients with type 2 diabetes‐associated cognitive impairment. Raising awareness among clinicians regarding the potential protective effects of antidiabetic medications, such as SGLT2is, on improving cognitive decline could help prevent the onset of dementia. To further establish SGLT2i as a disease‐modifying treatment for alleviating cognitive dysfunction in patients with type 2 diabetes, future randomized controlled trials should be carried out.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study. First, MoCA scores and P‐tau181 levels were used to assess cognitive function, lacking brain imaging data to confirm the effects of SGLT2i on brain structure and function. Second, some potential risk factors for cognitive decline were not available for analysis, such as physical, social and mental stimulating activities, hearing impairment, and psychotropic drugs. Third, the relatively short follow‐up period might have limited our ability to detect substantial changes in MoCA scores and P‐tau181 between enrollment and follow up. Admittedly, a 6‐month follow up might not reflect long‐term cognitive improvement, but studies of the effect of SGLT2i on cognitive function are lacking. The DECODE‐CKD study explored the effect of SGLT2i on cognitive function in CKD patients only among the secondary endpoints, also with a 6‐month follow up 37 . Of course, SGLT2is are recent drugs, limiting the scope of the data. Additional studies with a longer‐term follow‐up period are needed for validation. Finally, all patients on SGLT2i therapy in the present study were taking henagliflozin, as per the eligibility criteria. The data available in the literature does not allow a comparison of henagliflozin with other SGLT2is regarding the cognitive protective effects. It will have to be examined in future studies.

In conclusion, the present findings are the first to show an independent and positive association between henagliflozin treatment, increased MoCA scores and decreased P‐tau181 levels in patients with type 2 diabetes and cognitive impairment. This indicates the favorable effect of henagliflozin on ameliorating cognitive impairment. Further investigations are warranted to explore the potential clinical benefits of SGLT2is in improving cognitive decline.
