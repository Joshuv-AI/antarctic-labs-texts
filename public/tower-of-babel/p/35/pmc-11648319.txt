Kaiser Permanente Los Angeles Annual Research Week 2023: Research as a Pillar for Well-Being in Graduate Medical Education and Beyond
Authors: Joon S Park, Davida Becker, John J Sim
Published: 2024

Introduction

Attainment of well-being in medicine has been elusive but seems more important than ever given the high rates of burnout and the “Great Resignation.” Physician well-being investment in graduate medical education is imperative for the future and sustainability of the clinical workforce. Initial strategies, spurred by the Bell Commission and later Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) requirements, centered around reducing burnout.1 Later in the 2000s and 2010s, motivated by the newly passed Affordable Care Act,2 discussions focused on physician well-being as being essential for quality health care. Well-being was viewed as something that could be cultivated through mindfulness, resilience training, and self-care activities via learning modules and meetings.3 In 2015, ACGME hosted the first of three, Physician Well-Being Symposia. Through the new Common Program Requirements in 2017, ACGME developed a more concrete framework and provided the common vocabulary needed to discuss physician well-being at the national level.4 This work was followed by a National Plan for Health Workforce Well-Being from the National Academy of Medicine’s Clinician Well-Being Collaborative. They described seven Priority Areas for achieving a thriving health workforce: 1) create and sustain positive work and learning environments and culture; 2) invest in measurement, assessment, strategies, and research; 3) support mental health and reduce stigma; 4) address compliance, regulatory, and policy barriers for daily work; 5) engage effective technology tools; 6) institutionalize well-being as a long-term value; and 7) recruit and retain a diverse and inclusive health workforce.5

The COVID-19 pandemic brought a sense of urgency for translating these frameworks into real and concrete means. During the pandemic, it was evident that traditional means targeting well-being were either ineffective or insufficient. Many physicians struggled with feelings of being ineffective, sense of a loss of control over their lives, and isolationism. These contributed to physicians retiring early or leaving the profession altogether.6,7 In response, physicians across the nation spontaneously converged on the idea that well-being is not simply an absence of burnout,8,9 and sought multidimensional models for addressing fulfilment and burnout, simultaneously 10 11. They specifically identified the losses precipitated by COVID-19, and developed more practical, hybrid models for attaining well-being.12

At the Kaiser Permanente Los Angeles Medical Center (LAMC), physicians were not immune to the effects of the pandemic. However, we realized early on that research and quality improvement provided a means to well-being, giving us a sense of community13 and hope that we could treat and control COVID-19.3 Over the course of the pandemic, we witnessed perhaps the greatest period of rapid scientific innovation in human history from the Recovery Trial, ACTT-1, REMAP-CAP, and ACTT-2 to the rapid development and rise of mRNA vaccines, Paxlovid, and more.14–18 These brought hope and inspiration. At Kaiser Permanente Southern California, we were led by clinician-researchers contributing to the worldwide effort, such as William Towner and the Kaiser Permanente Southern California clinical trials teams as they studied the efficacy of remdesivir19 and eventually the COVID-19 vaccine’s efficacy.18 We later saw Cheng-Wei Huang, assistant program director for research of the Internal Medicine residency program and fresh out of residency, spearheading the data gathering on convalescent plasma and remdesivir and later producing practice-relevant research on COVID-19 management.20,21 The hospitalist group came together with the postdischarge team to innovate a pilot for COVID-19 Home Monitoring when hospitals were flooded with patients. We became energized by these seemingly daily innovations as we sought to contribute to collective knowledge, test new findings, and improve care for patients. Research was a way for us to take back control, to engage with each other as a community (even if remotely), and to educate the public and to fight unprecedented levels of disinformation.22

The May 2022 LAMC Research Week took on new meaning when it returned as one of the first large, in-person events of the postpandemic era after a 2 year virtual hiatus. This event has been an opportunity for physicians and other allied health professionals to showcase their work in original research, quality improvement, education, and exceptional cases.23 The residents, who perservered through the pandemic, showcased their impactful contributions (Supplementals). Kirollos Zaki’s work on the “Comparison of Safety and Outcomes Related to Remdesivir Treatment Among Dialysis Patients Hospitalized with COVID-19”24 expanded upon earlier work and provided justification to extend therapeutics to higher risk subgroups. Anthony Simone reported one of the first cases of “Acute Myocarditis Following a Third Dose of COVID-19 mRNA Vaccination in Adults.”25 He would achieve first place in the prestigious Oral Abstract Competition that year. The presented studies highlighted Kaiser Permanente’s unique ability to leverage practice-based experiences to produce high-impact, translational research. It also demonstrated the breadth of topics, ranging from health disparities to hospital operations to cutting edge technology utilization, and finally to physician well-being. Research in this context was a step back toward normalcy, a means for residents to regain a sense of control over their practice and their future careers. It was also an avenue for meaningful interaction and community.

Over the following year, we further realized that attaining wellness required removing barriers in areas that physicians valued such as community, achievement, and autonomy.26,27 Barriers to research were also lessened. Our hospital leadership prioritized physician research and helped galvanize the culture of research. They provided resources to help with logistical planning including project managers and biostatistical support. They help pay for publication expenses and secured provisions to travel for research conferences and presentations. In a similar effort, the graduate medical education program supported and worked to reduce barriers to research for the residents and medical students.

The result was a larger-than-ever 24th Annual LAMC Research Week showcasing a record number of submissions and participants. We welcomed students from the Kaiser Permanente Bernard J. Tyson School of Medicine who participated for the first time at this event. We are grateful to the amazing body of work from the Research Week team, whom we affectionately refer to as “The Committee.” Their dedication to building and sustaining LAMC’s research infrastructure, including research training, logistics, mentorship, and leadership, has been crucial to the continued growth and development of research, and hence physician well-being.

In this issue of The Permanente Journal, we proudly share with you the 24th Annual LAMC Research Week 2023 abstract presentations. These abstracts represent the “virtuous cycle” of physician/clinician-led research28 and exemplify the tenets of training and practicing medicine within Kaiser Permanente: leadership, scholarship, and discovery pertinent to integrated health delivery systems; health care modeling and innovations; value-based and high-value care; applied research in health disparities; and equity, inclusion, and diversity in medicine.
