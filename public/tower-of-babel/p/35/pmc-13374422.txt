BRIGHT pathways: recommendations to improve the developmental follow-up of children with CHD in Canada – protocol for a nominal group study
Authors: Marie-Eve Bolduc, Brahmdeep S Saini, Andrea M Patey, Eric Sadiku, Steven P Miller, Marie-Noelle Simard, Marie Brossard-Racine, Annette Majnemer, Linh G Ly, Renee Sananes, Mike Seed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSystematic developmental follow-up for children with congenital heart disease (CHD) is essential for the timely detection of developmental delays and disorders and to put in place the supports and resources necessary to maximise function and support family well-being. Best practice guidelines recommend follow-up for children with CHD at high risk of developmental delays. However, many countries, including Canada, have faced barriers such as limitations in financial and human resources which have limited their ability to implement these important neurodevelopmental follow-up programmes. The aim of this project is to develop recommendations for evidence-based care pathways for the developmental follow-up of children and adolescents with CHD that are adapted to the Canadian health care context.MethodsThis study includes two steps: (1) a narrative review of current literature on the developmental follow-up of children with CHD and (2) a nominal group process involving patients, families, healthcare workers, researchers, policy-makers and government representatives that will be used to achieve consensus on practice recommendations. Purposive sampling will be used to select eligible participants so that all interested parties are well represented and reflective of a variety of experiences and opinions. Two to three groups of approximately 10 participants will be held via videoconferencing. The nominal groups will consist of a five-step process that will be repeated for each topic: (1) question; (2) idea generation; (3) discussion; (4) ranking; and (5) consensus.AnalysisData from the different groups will be merged. Ideas for which a consensus was reached (>80% agreement) during the meetings will be summarised.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained from The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Canada (#1000081399) and McGill University, Montreal, Canada (#25-09-006). The findings of this study will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journals and conferences, presented at local, national and international conferences and disseminated to local, provincial and national governments to inform policy.

Introduction

It is now well accepted that children and adolescents with congenital heart disease (CHD) present a high risk of developmental impairments.13 These challenges can have a significant impact on the person with CHD, their family and society.46 To address these concerns, the American Heart Association (AHA) released, in 2012, a statement to optimise developmental follow-up practices.7 This statement describes an extensive series of recommendations that include developmental evaluations for all children with CHD at high risk of developmental challenges at 12–24 months, 3–5 years and 11–12 years of age.7 Children historically considered at high risk of developmental delays include those requiring early open-heart surgery, with other cyanotic heart lesions that do not require open-heart surgery, and with associated conditions, including but not limited to prematurity, genetic abnormality/syndrome, heart transplant, and history of mechanical support. In a recent series of articles, the Cardiac Neurodevelopmental Outcome Collaborative (CNOC) identified new time points for evaluation at 6 months, 18 months, 3 years, 5 years, 8–9 years, 10–11 years, 13–14 years and 18 years.8 9 Specific recommendations were also made for assessments and screening tools to be used to assess each developmental domain at risk of being affected by CHD.8 9 The 2012 statement was recently updated in 2024 to include new risk factors such as socioeconomic status and parental mental health.1

The AHA and CNOC statements have been instrumental in raising awareness of the impact of CHD on child functioning and the importance of an organised and proactive approach to developmental follow-up for children with CHD. However, the current recommendations identify a large and growing number of high-risk children and suggest the use of extensive assessment batteries. However, the implementation of the proposed care pathway has faced notable barriers in various countries due to limitation in human and financial resources.1013 To address the contextual differences and limitations in resources, different institutions, states/provinces and countries have adapted the recommendations to meet the needs and resource constraints of their specific environment.10 12 Hence, the structure of the follow-up programmes, eligibility criteria, frequency of follow-ups and the type of assessments (screening vs evaluation) greatly vary across countries and institutions.10 12 13

In Canada, only half of tertiary care centres that perform open-heart surgeries have a systematic developmental programme that includes screening and evaluation for children with CHD.13 Even when present, these services are only accessible to a subset of children with CHD considered at high risk of developmental delays and do not necessarily offer ongoing services through childhood transition points.13 Healthcare professionals involved in the developmental follow-up of children with CHD in Canada have identified limited human and financial resources as the main barriers to optimal developmental follow-up care.13 Furthermore, parents of children with CHD and youth with CHD themselves have identified important gaps in the developmental follow-up care that they have experienced.4 6 The identified limitations included the absence of systematic and responsive developmental follow-up programmes, inadequate communication and coordination among services, and the limited resources available to support child development and function within healthcare and educational systems. The parents of children with CHD expressed that this has resulted in increased parental burden and stress while support for children and youth with CHD remains insufficient.4 6 Youth with CHD have also identified transition to adult care as a point of discontinuity in their follow-up care.6 These gaps in CHD developmental follow-up are in contrast with the services offered to other groups of high-risk children who present similar developmental profiles such as the children born prematurely.1417 For children born very preterm, there is a Canadian Neonatal Follow-up Network that includes 26 tertiary care centres and where a developmental evaluation is systematically offered at 18–24 months corrected age. In addition, these centres voluntarily collaborate to enter assessment data in a collective database that can be used for quality improvement and research.16 This model, however, would need to be adapted to the specific needs of children with CHD, who experience the cumulative effects of their cardiac condition, such as multiple open-heart surgeries during infancy or chronic hypoxia.

Timely identification of developmental impairment and functional challenges is essential to ensure that children and youth with CHD can access the resources that support their development and functioning and to limit the impact of these challenges on academic, social and daily functioning.18 19 Unfortunately, there is no uniform care pathway or standardised set of developmental and domain-specific assessments currently recommended across Canada for children and adolescents with CHDs. Hence, current developmental follow-up practices may not allow for the timely detection of developmental challenges, and important opportunities for interventions and for providing support to families may be lost.20 The aim of this study is to develop recommendations for evidence-based care pathways for the developmental follow-up of children and adolescents with CHD and their families that are adapted to the Canadian context.

Methods and analysis

The development of context-specific recommendations through a consensus process has been selected as a strategy to improve developmental follow-up for children with CHD. Consultation with relevant interested parties (parents, youth, healthcare providers, researchers, administrators and policy-makers) in the form of a nominal group technique will be used to develop a consensus around the recommendations for evidence-based care pathways for the developmental follow-up of children and adolescents with CHD that are adapted to the Canadian context.

Step 1 (conducted between November 2023 and October 2025)

Specific aim 1

To summarise the current literature about the best follow-up practice necessary for the development of Canadian recommendations for the developmental follow-up of children with CHD.

Aim 1 study design

A targeted narrative review of current literature on (1) the prevalence of developmental delays and the domains affected in children with CHD at key stages of child development; (2) the factors associated with a higher risk of developmental delays in children with CHD; (3) the various pathways that have been implemented around the world as well as inner and outer setting barriers and facilitators to their implementation21; (4) the tools used for the developmental follow-up in children with CHD; (5) the tools used to assess parental/family mental health; and (6) the steps required for a successful transition to adult care. This initial step is essential to ensure that the participants in the consensus group (described below) will be provided with all the evidence necessary to select the recommendations of care pathways that are evidence-informed and adapted to the Canadian context. The review was designed as a pragmatic, non-systematic synthesis rather than a comprehensive evidence review given its extensive scope. Consultations with community members and other interested parties will complement the information obtained through the literature search.

Aim 1 methods

A narrative review will be used to synthesise the evidence. The steps for conducting the narrative literature review will include: (1) identifying keywords and search terms; (2) conducting the search in various databases (PubMed and MEDLINE); (3) reviewing abstracts and articles, focusing on the most recent evidence and when possible, synthesis of evidence (ie, scoping or systematic reviews) and (4) documenting results. A single reviewer screened abstracts and articles, consistent with the review’s role as a preparatory tool rather than a formal evidence synthesis. The content will be subsequently reviewed with input from content experts and patient partners to ensure clinical relevance, completeness and clarity.

Step 2 (planned from June 2026 to December 2026)

Specific aim 2

To develop a consensus around recommendations to improve the developmental follow-up of children and adolescents with CHD in Canada.

Aim 2 study design

This study involves a nominal group process that includes patients, families, health care workers, researchers, policy-makers, administrators and/or government representatives to achieve consensus on practice recommendations. A nominal group technique was selected to obtain a consensus on recommendations to improve the developmental surveillance of children and adolescents with CHD because when ideas are generated anonymously, the influences of power dynamics or other outside factors are minimised.22 23

Aim 2 methods

Two to three nominal groups will be held and will each be composed of about 10 participants from different backgrounds.22 24 The heterogeneous nature of these groups will allow for the generation of varied and creative ideas.22 The nominal groups will be moderated by members of the research team, and when possible, a study partner. One study team member will serve as the note taker and will provide assistance as needed. The role of the lead moderator will be to maintain the focus of the discussions, clarify opinions and ideas and guide the decision-making process.22 24 The questions asked to participants will be co-developed with the study partners and will serve to guide the formulation of recommendations related to the follow-up of children and adolescents with CHD, the transition to adult care, and the promotion of parental mental health. For example, one question may be around the components needed of a standardised developmental follow-up pathway for children (0–18 years old) with CHD in Canada, ensuring equitable access to developmental follow-up while another question would be on the essential components of mental health and family support for parents of children with CHD. The questions will be iteratively refined through ongoing consultation with our team of parent and youth partners to ensure clarity, relevance and appropriateness.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria include diverse interested parties involved in the developmental follow-up of children and adolescents with CHD. Across two to three nominal groups, we will aim to recruit: 6–9 health care professionals (cardiologists, paediatricians, cardiothoracic surgeons, psychologists, nurses, occupational or physical therapists) and researchers from at least two different institutional settings; 6–9 parents of children or adolescents with CHD; 3–7 youth (13–25 years) or young adults with CHD and 3–7 policy-makers, administrators and/or government representatives. We will exclude those that lack proficiency in English as they may face challenges in effectively participating and contributing to the discussions.

Recruitment and consent process

Purposeful sampling will be used to ensure that all interested parties are well represented (parents, youth, healthcare professionals, researchers, administrators and policy-makers) and that a variety of experiences and opinions are conveyed. The participants will also be selected to maximise the heterogeneity of the groups in terms of gender and living/working location (province; rural, urban, suburban) and to have representatives from minority groups. Participants will be asked to complete a short demographic questionnaire to ensure diversity among the participants and to describe our sample. Participants will be recruited through the research team network, emails to associations, organisations and institutions, direct contact via information obtained on institutional/organisational websites (eg, for non-patient participants) and through posts on social media channels (X, Instagram, LinkedIn and Facebook) of relevant healthcare institutions, organisations and advocacy groups (eg, SickKids, CHILD-BRIGHT Network, Canadian Congenital Heart Alliance, Fondation En Coeur and Heart MoM), and posters will be placed in cardiology and neonatal neurodevelopmental follow-up clinics at the institutions where co-investigators of this study have primary affiliations. The posts and posters will include a link/QR code to a REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture)25 26 study summary page where interested participants will find more information on the study and contact information of research team members.

Patient and public involvement

Interested parties will be involved throughout the project. The research question arose from a previous project in which parents of children and youth with CHD highlighted the need for a more systematic developmental follow-up programme in Canada. A team of six partners, including parents of children with CHD and other brain-based disabilities and young adults with CHD, will be part of the research team for this project. They participated in the idea generation for the current protocol during online, individual and group meetings. Partners will participate in the development of questions for the nominal group, recruitment material and knowledge mobilisation strategies. They will also provide comments and suggestions on the presentation that summarises the results of the narrative review. Partners may be asked to facilitate the consensus groups if they are interested. The time contributed by the study partners for different steps of the project will be tracked and tallied in a spreadsheet and compensation issued biannually.

Evidence from the literature search described in Step 1 will be summarised and presented to the group at the beginning of the session, prior to the consensus exercise. During the nominal groups, the participants will develop recommendations, discuss them and assess their feasibility, utility and practicality. A voting process will be used to develop a consensus on which recommendations are to be selected.

A three-level ranking was preferred over full rank-ordering to reduce cognitive burden and facilitate discussion around the acceptability and feasibility of each recommendation among participants with diverse backgrounds. Although the nominal group technique was specifically designed to mitigate power imbalances, active facilitation remains essential. To ensure all voices are heard and power dynamics are minimised, the moderator will establish explicit ground rules at the outset, use active moderation to ensure equitable speaking time, and use targeted prompts to elicit input from quieter participants.

The consensus groups will be held online via a teleconferencing platform (Microsoft Teams). Participants will be offered compensation for their time. Each consensus group session should last approximately 3 hours. Participants will be given a break in the middle of the process. The consensus groups will be recorded, a transcript will be automatically generated using the available feature on the virtual platform and saved, and notes will also be taken during the meeting to document the discussion and interactions. After the consensus group, the transcript will be reviewed by the same research team members who conducted the session, and any needed edits will be made for accuracy. The recorded video/audio will be replayed, and the notes taken during the session will be used in conjunction to facilitate the transcript review and finalisation process. The audio/video recording will be deleted within 60 days from the date of the consensus group, and the finalised transcript will be de-identified by removing any identifiers and replacing them with the participant study code.

Data analysis

We will consider that a consensus has been reached if 80% of the participants rated the recommendation as a 3 (green). This is consistent with the recommended 70%–80% range28 but set at the higher end of the range to reflect the group’s heterogeneity and to ensure feasible and sustainable recommendations. Data from the different groups will then be merged. This analysis will follow a three-step process described by McMillan et al29: (1) all the recommendations for which a consensus was reached will be coded and grouped into themes in a spreadsheet by at least two members of the research team to facilitate comparison across groups; (2) the total number of votes for each theme will then be calculated; (3) priorities will be ranked based on the number of votes, and ideas that were in the top five for any group will be identified. Transcripts will be analysed to identify elements that provide additional context and the perspectives of participants from different geographical areas or groups will be compared to identify possible variations in the needs across participant groups so that these are taken into consideration in the final recommendations. Recommendations for which a consensus has not been reached will also be synthesised and the reasons for disagreements will be described.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval was obtained from The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Canada (#1000081399) and McGill University, Montreal, Canada (#25-09-006). Written informed consent will be obtained from each participant prior to holding the nominal group. Assent will be obtained from all participants who may lack the capacity to consent, and consent will be obtained from their parents or substitute decision-makers. All participants’ data (consent forms, demographic data, notes, transcripts, ideas discussed and their voting results) will be kept in a password-protected institutional server and/or network (OneDrive) for a period of 7 years following the end of the study with the exception of the recording, which will be deleted within 60 days of the consensus groups, when the transcript has been verified and finalised. The recording will be used only for verifying the accuracy of the transcript and no analysis of facial expressions and visual cues will be performed. Only the members of the research team will have access to this data. All personal health information and de-identified study data about participants will be stored in a secure and confidential location for 7 years after the completion of the study as per institutional policies. All publications and presentations will be anonymised so that participants and their institutions cannot be identified. No adverse effects on the participants are expected from this study.

In addition to following a rigorous process to ensure the quality of the research, health research must also follow standards of moral defensibility such that health research must only be conducted when necessary. The high prevalence of developmental challenges in children and adolescents with CHD and their significant impact on daily functioning combined with the known limitations of the current developmental follow-up practices warrants the need for this study to develop recommendations that address the service delivery gaps for this growing population in Canada. This research also has important clinical value and may translate into important changes in practice.

The findings of this study will be disseminated via various avenues including publications in peer-reviewed journals and presented at local, national and international conferences. Additionally, we aim to share our results on social media and organise webinars, as well as lunch and learn sessions for healthcare professionals, administrators and policy-makers. Finally, the recommendations will be distributed to local, provincial and national governments to inform policy.

Limitations

Participants with CHD will not be screened for neurodevelopmental conditions, which may limit interpretation of their experiential insights. This decision reflects the challenges of accurate identification via a single time-point screening survey and the study’s focus on healthcare navigation rather than individual neurodevelopmental status. Targeted recruitment of indigenous or recent immigrant participants was not possible under the approved protocol. To address these important considerations, we plan to consult with equity-seeking communities following the analysis of the nominal group data to ensure their perspectives inform the final recommendations.
