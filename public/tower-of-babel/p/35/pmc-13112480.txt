Quad‑polarization synthetic aperture radar dataset for marine oil spill and look‑alike segmentation
Authors: Sohail Jamal, Guangmin Sun, Yu Li, Abdul Qadir Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quad‑polarization oil spill detection dataset is a collection of fully polarimetric L‑band synthetic aperture radar scenes acquired by the NASA/JPL uninhabited aerial vehicle synthetic aperture radar (UAVSAR) over the U.S. Gulf of Mexico from 2010 to 2022. The dataset comprises 41 multi‑look complex SAR scenes with a spatial resolution of roughly 6 m × 4 m and incidence angles between 27° and 67° For each scene, a 3 × 3 polarimetric coherence matrix is computed using the quad polarization channels, (HH, HV, VH, VV) namely horizontal transmit horizontal receive, horizontal transmit vertical receive, vertical transmit horizontal receive, and vertical transmit vertical receive. This matrix is then represented as a nine channel real valued feature set. Pixel‑level segmentation masks annotate four classes: sea surface, oil spill, look‑alike phenomena and land with >80 million labelled pixels. Ground‑truth labels were generated with a semi‑automated workflow combining manual labelling, Random Forest‑based label propagation and manual refinement. QPOSD is intended to enable reproducible research, benchmarking and polarimetric feature analysis for SAR‑based oil spill and look‑alike segmentation. The dataset is accessible at DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19258036.Keywords: Remote sensing, Environmental monitoring, Polarimetric coherence matrix, Oceanic pollutants, Deep learning

Background

Marine oil releases are a severe ecological hazard and need quick and precise identification to reduce the long-term effects on the ecosystem and economy [2]. Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) is also becoming a useful instrument in this regard because it is not dependent on sunlight and is not very sensitive to weather conditions [3]. The oil slicks are seen in SAR imagery as regions with reduced backscatter, which are due to the damping of the surface capillary waves. Nevertheless, there are a number of natural effects, including biogenic surface films, algal blooms, and low-wind patches, which can create the same backscatter patterns, and thus it is hard to distinguish between oil spill and look-alikes [4]. Although automated analysis is increasingly required [[5], [6], [7]], commonly the publicly available SAR datasets do not have the polarimetric richness and categorical diversity necessary to generate algorithms. Most datasets are single or dual-polarized, have few annotations, and cannot be used with more intricate machine learning methods. To overcome these shortcomings, this study developed a Quad-polarization Oil Spill Detection dataset through a systematic workflow to transform raw aerial SAR data into the high-quality pixel-based semantic masks. It consists of region-of-interest selection, coherence matrices processing, expert assisted labelling, and supervised learning from June 2010 – June 2022.

To clarify the contribution of the proposed dataset and better position QPOSD within the existing landscape of SAR oil-spill datasets, Table 1 provides a comparative summary of its main characteristics alongside several representative publicly used datasets. Most previous datasets are based primarily on Sentinel-1 single-polarization C-band imagery and are intended mainly for binary oil-spill detection or patch-level classification tasks. For example, the CSIRO and Yang et al. datasets focus on oil versus non-oil discrimination using single-polarization data, while the Deep-SAR dataset combines Sentinel-1 and ALOS PALSAR imagery but remains limited to single-polarization observations. Although the M4D dataset provides pixel-level masks for multiple classes, it is still derived from single-polarization C-band Sentinel-1 data. In contrast, QPOSD is constructed from full quad-polarization UAVSAR L-band data and provides pixel-level semantic annotations for four classes, namely sea surface, oil spill, look-alike, and land. This full-polarimetric configuration enables the use of richer scattering information and the extraction of physically meaningful polarimetric descriptors that cannot be directly derived from single-polarization datasets. Although QPOSD contains fewer scenes than large patch-based datasets, it contributes a distinct and practically important benchmark by combining full-scene annotation, dense pixel-level labeling, and complete quad-polarization information in a fully accessible public release. As such, QPOSD fills an important gap for reproducible research on physics-informed and polarimetry-aware oil-spill segmentation.

Data Description

The QPOSD dataset comprises 41 quad-polarization UAVSAR scenes and their corresponding pixel-level semantic segmentation masks. The data were accessed from the NASA/JPL UAVSAR archives via the Alaska Satellite Facility (ASF). Each sample is provided as a Multi-Look Complex (MLC) product and includes the four polarimetric channels (HH, HV, VH, VV). From these measurements, a 3 × 3 polarimetric coherence matrix (T3) is derived per pixel. To enable reuse in conventional real-valued learning-based models, the off-diagonal terms are split into real and imaginary components to form the nine-channel representation reported in Table 2. The released nine-channel representation was generated directly from the UAVSAR multi-look complex (MLC) polarimetric data through the corresponding matrix-element representation, and no additional per-channel min-max normalization, z-score standardization or log transformation was applied during dataset preparation. This preserves the original relative scattering information contained in the processed MLC product. However, this does not imply that further normalization is unnecessary for all machine-learning frameworks. Depending on the chosen model architecture and training pipeline, users may still apply task-specific normalization or standardization during model development. For each SAR scene, a corresponding pixel-level semantic segmentation mask is provided in PNG format using the fixed RGB encoding. Table 3 summarizes the class distribution of QPOSD at the pixel level. The dataset contains a total of 79,961,077 labeled pixels, of which sea surface is the dominant class (76.44%), followed by oil spill (15.61%), look-alike (5.98%), and land (1.98%). These statistics highlight the inherent class imbalance of the dataset and provide useful guidance for model development and evaluation. While the dataset was employed in [1] for algorithm development, this article provides the first complete public release, documentation, and standardized formatting of the full QPOSD dataset.

Recommended usage

To support reproducible benchmarking on QPOSD, a practical data partitioning strategy is to divide the dataset into 80% training and 20% validation/test subsets using a stratified random split at the patch level, with a fixed random seed of 60. This recommended split may serve as a reference setting for future comparative evaluation. Moreover, to facilitate dataset usage, the Zenodo repository now includes an example Python code snippet demonstrating how to load the released .mat feature files and visualize the corresponding RGB segmentation masks.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The QPOSD dataset was constructed through a multi-stage workflow designed to transform raw quad-polarization airborne SAR data into a standardized, pixel-level annotated dataset suitable for supervised learning and benchmarking. The workflow consists of (i) data acquisition and scene selection, (ii) region-of-interest extraction, (iii) polarimetric feature derivation and formatting, (iv) semi-automated annotation, and (v) expert refinement and dataset packaging (Fig. 2).

Workflow used to construct QPOSD: UAVSAR acquisition, ROI extraction, T3 processing and nine-channel formatting, initial manual labeling, Random Forest-based label extension, and expert refinement to produce final pixel-level masks.

Data acquisition and scene selection

All scenes were acquired by the NASA/JPL UAVSAR instrument in L-band quad-polarization mode over the U.S. Gulf of Mexico from 2010 to 2022. Scenes were selected to include confirmed oil slicks and look-alike conditions together with surrounding sea surface and coastal land areas. Region-of-interest extraction

Each UAVSAR scene was inspected to identify oil spills, look-alike regions, and representative background classes. A region of interest (ROI) was defined as a spatial subset of the full scene selected for labeling and analysis. ROI dimensions were not fixed across the dataset; instead, their size varied depending on scene content in order to capture the relevant target classes while preserving contextual information near class boundaries. In the released dataset, the smallest ROI has dimensions of 750 × 750 pixels, whereas the largest ROI has dimensions of 2050 × 2050 pixels. ROIs were extracted using PolSARpro, which was also used to generate the per-pixel 3 × 3 coherence matrix T3 from the multi-look complex data following standard polarimetric SAR processing procedures [15]. This step focused the annotation process on relevant areas containing the four target classes; sea surface, oil spill, look-alike, and land, while preserving sufficient contextual information around class boundaries. In addition, PolSARpro was used to derive the H/A/α (entropy/anisotropy/alpha) decomposition products to support detailed visual interpretation of the scenes.

Polarimetric feature derivation and formatting

After extraction of the T3 matrix, its components were visually inspected and cross-checked in SNAP to support careful scene interpretation and reduce annotation errors. The diagonal and off-diagonal matrix elements were then arranged into a nine-channel real-valued representation using MATLAB and saved in .mat format for dataset release. The channel order used in the released data is

(T11, Im{T12}, Re{T12}, Im{T13}, Re{T13}, T22, Im{T23}, Re{T23}, T33).

Semi-automated annotation procedure

Pixel-level semantic segmentation masks were generated using a semi-automated workflow implemented in ImageJ Fiji. Specifically, the Trainable Weka Segmentation plugin was used for annotation and Random Forest-based [16] label propagation. Representative pixels for each class; sea surface, oil spill, look-alike, and land were first manually labeled on polarimetric intensity representations derived from the SAR data. These labeled samples were then used to train the built-in Random Forest classifier in Fiji, which propagated class labels to the remaining pixels within each ROI. The main Random Forest settings used in this workflow were 200 trees, maximum depth = 0 (unlimited depth), 2 features per split, and random seed = 146098729. Batch size was set to 100, and computation used 12 threads. The classifier output was used only for preliminary label propagation; all generated masks were subsequently reviewed and manually refined before final release.

Quality control and manual refinement

All automatically generated segmentation maps were subsequently reviewed and refined under expert supervision. Manual correction focused particularly on misclassified regions, class boundaries, and visually ambiguous areas where oil slicks and look-alike phenomena exhibited similar backscatter characteristics. The Random Forest propagation step was used only to accelerate preliminary mask generation and was not adopted directly as final ground truth. Instead, all propagated labels were manually inspected and corrected before release, improving spatial consistency and reducing labeling artefacts introduced during automated propagation. Formal inter-annotator agreement statistics were not recorded because the annotations were not produced by multiple independent annotators, and the standalone validation accuracy of the Random Forest propagation step was not archived during the original dataset curation process.

Final dataset packaging

For each sample, the dataset includes (i) the processed nine-channel polarimetric feature array and (ii) the corresponding pixel-level segmentation mask stored in PNG format using a fixed RGB encoding. Files are organized by sample to support reproducible data loading for supervised segmentation, benchmarking, and transfer-learning experiments.

Limitations

QPOSD is limited to 41 scenes acquired by the UAVSAR instrument over the U.S. Gulf of Mexico between 2010 and 2022; it does not capture other geographic regions or more recent conditions. In addition, all observations were collected at L-band, which may limit the direct generalizability of models developed on this dataset to SAR sensors operating at other frequencies, such as C-band or X-band, where scattering behavior and polarimetric responses can differ. Only four surface classes (sea surface, oil spill, look‑alike and land) are annotated; other phenomena (e.g., ship wakes, ice or coastal infrastructure) are not labelled. While geographically limited, the dataset provides a controlled and well-annotated benchmark that is particularly suitable for comparative algorithm evaluation and transfer-learning studies to other regions or sensors.

QPOSD has an imbalanced class distribution consistent with real marine SAR observations. Among the 79,961,077 labeled pixels, sea surface accounts for 76.44%, followed by oil spill (15.61%), look-alike (5.98%), and land (1.98%). While this distribution increases the realism of the dataset, it may also influence model training and evaluation by favoring the dominant background class. Accordingly, users should consider reporting class-sensitive metrics such as per-class IoU, mean IoU, F1-score, and confusion matrices when benchmarking on QPOSD.

Ethics Statement

This work did not involve human participants, animals or personal data. All information presented is derived from remotely sensed SAR observations acquired over marine and coastal environments, and no ethical approval or informed consent was required.
