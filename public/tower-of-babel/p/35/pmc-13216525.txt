AHP integrated geospatial application for identifying groundwater recharge zones accounting for temporal variations through multi-criterion weighted overlay analysis
Authors: Kanwarpreet Singh, Aashish Sharma, Abhishek Sharma, Rupesh Gupta, Abhijit Bhowmik, Amenjor Senagah, Nadhir Al-Ansari, Gaurav Juneja, Mohamed A. Mattar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ground water is critical in supporting agricultural, domestic and industrial demands in mountainous and water scarce areas. Nevertheless, it is difficult to detect the groundwater potential zones (GPZs) of structurally complicated terrains of Himalayas because of the insufficiency of the hydrogeological data and time delays in recharge conditions. This paper seeks to map the recharge areas on the groundwater of the Kinnaur District in Himachal Pradesh, India, by applying a multi-criteria decision-making model based on GIS. To create a groundwater recharge potential map as well as to take into consideration the temporal variation, 9 hydrogeologically significant parameters including rainfall, slope, curvature, drainage density, land use/land cover, lineament density, soil, aspect, and topographic wetness index were examined and weighted with the help of the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). The study incorporates nine critical criteria—rainfall, curvature, slope, drainage density, land use/land cover, lineament density, soil, aspect, and topographic wetness index—to analyze and map the spatial distribution of GPZs. Through combining such criteria through AHP in a GIS framework, the research is able to consider the aspect of time variation, not to mention multi-criteria features that are crucial in proper GPSZ identification. The groundwater fluctuation zone of the study area has been categorized into five different classes: Very Low (24%), Low (31%), Medium (21%), High (18%), and Very High (6%). The delineated map was tested with the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) technique, and the map was found to have a good correlation with the water bodies (AUC = 71.7%). This will give a complete picture of the groundwater recharge dynamics, and this will be of great help on how to use ground water sustainably. The resulting map of the generated ground water recharge can be a solid decision-support guide in the exploration of the ground water resource, its prioritization, and sustainable management of mountainous areas with limited data.

Introduction

Groundwater is one of the most valuable natural resources, particularly in areas where surface water is scarce. It is a valuable natural freshwater source for human health, regional economic development, biological diversity, and agricultural sustainability1. However, its availability is limited. Groundwater resources, like surface water bodies, are unevenly distributed2. The situation is exacerbated by climate change, which influences rainfall patterns and groundwater recharge rates, further intensifying the pressure on this finite resource3. In recent years, the demand for groundwater has increased significantly in both developed and developing nations to meet the water needs of agriculture, industry, and households4. A limited understanding of the complex groundwater system has led to decreased success rates in drilling groundwater production wells, particularly in developing countries5. Over 65% of agricultural activities worldwide rely on groundwater for irrigation. India is the world’s largest user of groundwater. It consumes around 230 cubic kilometers of groundwater annually, which accounts for more than 25% of the global total6. There is a lot of pressure on its appropriate usage because groundwater use is gradually increasing due to high population density, an expanding area of irrigated farmland, and less ecologically significant economic development7,8. As a result, there is an urgent need to investigate effective approaches for identifying areas with high potential for groundwater extraction. This can be accomplished by using modern methodologies for data preparation and integration, as well as a unique decision-making techniques9,10. Various researchers have performed water based studies using various techniques11–13. Delineating Groundwater Potential Zones (GPZs) involves a systematic analysis of the various factors influencing groundwater availability to identify areas that can support sustainable extraction14. In mountainous regions, slope gradients, drainage density, and topography play a crucial role in determining how effectively water is captured and recharged into groundwater systems15. Understanding these factors is essential for accurately assessing groundwater potential and managing its use effectively. This process typically utilizes geological, hydrological, and land use data to create comprehensive maps that define zones based on their groundwater potential. Identifying these zones facilitates strategic planning and prioritization of resources, enabling water managers to focus on areas with the highest potential for sustainable groundwater extraction1. When the groundwater demand is rising, understanding the spatial distribution of GPZs can help mitigate resource depletion and promote sustainable agricultural practices14,16.

The integration of the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) technologies offers a powerful framework for groundwater management and GPZ delineation17. The AHP, developed by18, is a widely used method in multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) for resolving socioeconomic decision-making challenges19,20. When combined with GIS, it provides a visual and spatial representation of the groundwater system, allowing for a more detailed understanding of spatial relationships among various factors14,21. This integration facilitates the identification and prioritization of groundwater potential zones by enabling researchers and policymakers to analyze multiple data layers, including land use, drainage patterns, and climate data, leading to more informed decision-making17. The AHP model and GIS are computer-based methodologies used to collect, analyze, and visually represent geographical data to solve complex problems efficiently22–25. Furthermore, ground truth data and RS satellite imagery have been widely used to provide baseline information for identifying locations with potential groundwater26. Thus, this study employed the widely recognized and highly dependable AHP technique to investigate potential zones for groundwater.

Groundwater is also one of the most important sources of freshwater in the world with a large percentage of the available freshwater on the earth being ground water. According to recent global evaluations, the contemporary groundwater storage is an important factor in maintaining ecosystems, drinking water supply, and the production of agriculture27,28. Nevertheless, an increasing climatic variability and anthropogenic pressures are having a major impact on groundwater recharge processes and spatial sustainability29.

Water availability and processes of water recharge are also further regulated by hydrological partitioning processes, such as the interaction between climate variability and vegetation seasonality, especially in the Budyko framework30. Megafocus water budgets also indicate the sophistication of the evapotranspiration, runoff formation, and subsurface storage in various landscapes31. In addition, the variability of hydrological and geochemical processes in river basins in space and time proves the dynamic connection between the surface water and groundwater systems32.

Attempts have been made to merge primary and quantitative data sources with GIS to extract more themed layers33. The formulation’s efficacy varies throughout research, with some being more effective, accurate, time-saving, and cost-efficient than standard techniques that are known to be time-consuming2. In Kinnaur, Himachal Pradesh, the application of AHP and GIS in delineating GPZs is particularly relevant given the region’s unique hydrogeological conditions and heavy reliance on groundwater for agricultural practices14. In the research region, no systematic definition of groundwater potential has been carried out using GIS-based MCDA and AHP approaches. Although their quantitative qualities and benefits are well recognized, experts in the current discipline have done little research on these algorithms.

Many past investigations of ground water potential zonation with AHP-GIS were mostly carried out in rather homogeneous landscapes like alluvial plains, basalt plateaus, or semi-arid sedimentary basins with relatively stable hydrogeological conditions and less complex structures of recharge process. Contrarily, mountainous areas, especially in the Himalaya setting, are steep-sloped, fractured lithology, high runoff, strong structural controls and large temporal rainfall variability, which have a significant effect on the recharge processes. In contrast to most studies worldwide that use mere conventional parameters like slope, drainage density, and land use to describe the topography, the current study incorporates other terrain sensitive indices like curvature and Topographic Wetness Index (TWI), structural and climatic variability parameters to explain the recharge heterogeneity in rugged terrain with enhanced accuracy. Moreover, the use of ROC-based validation enhances the methodology strength, which is a weakness of some of the similar studies conducted in the region, where quantitative accuracy measure is weak or missing34.

Groundwater is among the most important freshwater resources in the world, as it contains almost half of drinking water and provides an irrigated agriculture in the world which is estimated at 40 percent. It is critical to maintaining water security, ecology, as well as counteracting effects of climate variability and drought. In the developing world, groundwater is one of the major sources of domestic and agricultural water since it is relatively predictable relative to surface water35.

In India, groundwater forms an important source of irrigation, rural drinking water and industrial purposes, especially where the resources of surface water are small or seasonally fluctuate. Himalayan states like Himachal Pradesh are extensively dependent on springs and shallow aquifers to provide water locally and thus groundwater analysis is important in planning finances on sustainable resources. Rising population stress, climatic shifts and changing land-use policies also underscore the importance of undertaking systematic mapping of ground water potentials at regional levels.

This paper aims at producing thematic maps that reflect on the driving variables which are of importance in groundwater recharge. It also uses the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) methodically to identify the best rankings and weights of the chosen parameters. The groundwater potential zone (GPZ) map is created using the weighted overlays index (WOI) method in a GIS environment using these weighted factors. The obtained GPZ map is then tested with an appropriate spatial modeling method to provide reliability and accuracy of analysis.

In India, it is an essential aspect of the rural and agricultural water security. Complex terrain, structural controls and climatic variability affect water availability negatively in mountainous areas like Kinnaur. The springs and shallow aquifers in the local communities play a major role in supporting the local communities hence the systematic groundwater potential assessment is necessary in long-term water security planning and sustainability of the resources in the district.

This paper will elaborate the description of the study area and data sources, and then give a thorough account on the methodology, including the creation of thematic layer, AHP based weighting, and weighted overlay analysis as a part of a GIS environment. The outcomes of groundwater potential zonation and their confirmation with the help of ROC analysis are then discussed. Lastly, the research ends by drawing conclusions of main research findings and outlines the implications of the research findings to sustainable groundwater management practice in mountainous areas.

Study area

The research area stretched from 31°30′12″ N to 32°22′16″ and 77°40′16″ E to 79° E, and it is situated in Himachal Pradesh’s western Himalayas36, as shown in Fig. 1. Kinnaur is a region in northeastern Himachal Pradesh that shares its eastern boundary with Tibet37. It features three prominent mountain ranges, namely the Zanskar and Himalaya, which encompass the Satluj, Spiti, and Baspa valleys, along with their tributaries. The district encompasses a total area of 6401 km238. The altitudinal spectrum of the study area spans from 1193 to 6399 m39.Fig. 1Map of the study area: Kinnaur district.

Map of the study area: Kinnaur district.

The area is rugged in mountainous land with some sections of Greater Himalaya and Zanskar. The big river systems are the Satluj River and its tributaries (Baspa and Spiti rivers) that have a significant impact on the drainage pattern and geomorphology. The dramatic slopes and excavated valleys regulate the surface runoff and ground water recharge processes to a great extent. Kinnaur has its climatic conditions in cold desert to temperate mountain climates. The valleys have moderate summers and cold winters, whereas the high elevations are covered with snow throughout the long periods of time. Spatial distribution of precipitation is uneven with the monsoonal rainfalls prevailing at the lower level and snowfall at the higher altitude. This climate variability is significant in recharge dynamics40. The soils within the district are mostly shallow, coarse textured and poorly developed because of the steep grades and the erosion process taking place. In elevated areas, skeletal and glacial soils are predominant and in valley areas there are alluvial and colluvial deposits that have comparatively good infiltration properties. These are the differences in soils that have a direct effect on the storage and recharge of ground water41.

The vegetation is of temperate forests (pine, deodar, and oak) in the low areas and alpine shrubs and sparse cold desert vegetation in the high areas. The agricultural activity is also concentrated in the valley bottoms where there are orchards of apple or seasonal crops. Endangered species found in the fragile ecological environment include the wildlife of Himalayan fauna, that comprises of ibex and snow leopard. Kinnaur is an appropriate area to conduct systematic groundwater potential assessment because of the complex topography, fractured geology, weather variability, and reliance of people in the area on springs and shallow aquifers.

Methodology

All groundwater conditioning maps were made in ArcGIS 10.8. GIS was used to blend thematic layers to identify groundwater-suitable zones in the research region. The investigation procedures are outlined in Fig. 2. The inventory of locations with significant groundwater potential is obtained by marking the locations of water bodies on Google Earth Pro. The groundwater potential map was confirmed by the locations of water bodies. Weighting thematic layers with AHP and WOI methods identified possible groundwater zones affected by environmental variables.Fig. 2Flow diagram illustrating the methodology employed in developing the groundwater zone map.

Flow diagram illustrating the methodology employed in developing the groundwater zone map.

Saaty et al.45 developed the AHP, a decision-making system that ranks various factors in determining groundwater zones. The final groundwater potential zone map was created using weighted overlay analysis on thematic layers. This was done by overlaying each theme map with WOI in GIS. The thematic layers were prepared using the conventional geoprocessing and spatial analyst. Slope, aspect, curvature, drainage network, and Topographic Wetness Index (TWI) were obtained by using the DEM (ALOS, 30 m). In particular, a terrain derivatives generation tool (the Slope and Aspect and Curvature tools of the Spatial Analyst toolbox) was used. The Fill, Flow Direction, Flow Accumulation, and Stream Order functions were used to extract drainage networks and calculating drainage density was done using Line Density tool. Data on rainfall that was available on CHRS was processed to a continuous rainfall surface through the Inverse Distance Weighted (IDW) interpolation algorithm. The Landsat OLI-TIRS images were used to prepare Land Use/Land Cover (LULC) maps using Supervised Classification (Maximum Likelihood Classifier), which was then done by assessing accuracy. Calculation of lineament density was done based on digital geological lineaments in the Line Density tool. The Polygon to Raster conversion tool was used to convert the soil maps into the raster format and georeferenced. The reclassification of all thematic layers into suitability classes in accordance with the Reclassify functional was done at a uniform spatial resolution (30 m). In it, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) was used as a means of determining weights of each of the thematic layers by comparing them in pair and their consistency was checked by the Consistency Ratio (CR). The completed groundwater potential map was created through the tool of Weighted Overlay of the tool Spatial Analyst that combined all the reclassified layers weighted by the weight that was given to each. The output map was validated through a Receiver Operating Characteristic ROC curve analysis by comparing the predicted water body locations with known locations of water bodies obtained through Google Earth Pro.

Analytic hierarchy process (AHP)

Over the past several decades, numerous researchers have utilized the AHP model for various applications, including the evaluation of groundwater quality, landslide susceptibility, and resource extraction. The AHP is frequently employed as the primary technique in MCDA Analysis43,46. This approach enables the seamless incorporation of all thematic layers to generate the final GPZs map. Within the MCDA methodology, each pertinent aspect of the study subject is allocated a specific weight47. The relative relevance values, based on Saaty’s scale (1–9), were used. Weights for this study were established through a review of the literature and consideration of groundwater variables43,48. According to49, the concept of judgmental uncertainty is expressed by the principal eigenvalue and the consistency index (CI) within the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). For the Consistency ratio (CR) level, the permissible value is less than 0.1044.

Despite the popularity of the AHPGIS framework in the context of assessing the potential of groundwater, the framework has some weaknesses. The AHP technique is based on the subjective judgments in the pair wise comparisons and thus it adds subjectivity in allocating weights in spite of consistency ratio verifications. Weighted overlay method presupposes that the parameters are linearly related and it is not always able to account for complicated non-linear interaction of the hydrogeology. As well, data reliance is quite sensitive to quality and spatial resolution of input data layers in determining the accuracy of the results. The little field validation information and time change in the conditions of recharges in mountainous areas can also affect model reliability. Consequently, although AHP offers a clearer and consistent structure of decision making process, their results must be viewed in the light of these inherent limitations.

Weighted overlay indices

Once the ultimate weights for the criterion are established through the AHP process, which includes performing pairwise comparisons, developing a comparison matrix, and verifying an acceptable level of consistency. The WOI has been employed within a GIS platform to generate a map of the GPZs. WOI is a methodology used to perform a comprehensive assessment by assigning a specific set of values to input elements derived from the outcomes of AHP analysis50. When assigning weightage to a parameter, we evaluate the impact of various factors on groundwater conditions48. Consequently, the weights for this study were assigned based on a comprehensive review of the literature and an understanding of the factors affecting groundwater2,43,44,51.

Multi-Criterion Weighted Overlay method of AHP-GIS is most appropriate to use in the current research since the area is mountainous with various factors interacting to affect recharge of groundwater including: slope, geology, drainage, land use and rainfall. AHP offers a rational and clear method of weight allocation by making a pair-wise comparison and minimizes the arbitrary determination witnessed in simple weighted overlay methods. The AHP-MCWO model is more applicable in the Himalayan areas with data-limited, which are characterized by a scarcity of data compared to data-intensive machine learning modeling applied in a few GWPZ studies, because it combines the knowledge of experts with spatial analysis and is computationally-inexpensive and interpretable. Its reliability is enhanced by the fact that it incorporates terrain specific parameters and ROC-based validation thus making it a strong and an appropriate procedure in assessing the ground water potential of a region.

Assessing groundwater potential

Lineament density

Lineament density is the sum of the length of linear geological features like faults, fracture and joints in a unit area51. These features significantly influence groundwater dynamics by facilitating water infiltration into subsurface aquifers52. Rainfall

Rainfall is a key climatic factor influencing groundwater availability, as it directly contributes to recharge processes54. Understanding the spatial distribution of rainfall is crucial in assessing (GPZs). District climate is characterized by a temperate climate prevailing at high elevations (1500 m-6735 m), with long winters from October to May and short summers from June to September38. Figure 3b shows a GIS-generated rainfall map created using the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) method. Annual rainfall ranges from 944 to 1061 mm.

Land use land cover

Land use and land cover (LULC) play a crucial role in influencing groundwater dynamics, as anthropogenic activities such as deforestation, soil erosion, groundwater depletion, and degradation of soil quality have adverse impacts on ecosystems55. Changes in LULC are significant drivers of groundwater variation. In this study, supervised LULC classification was performed using the Gaussian Maximum Likelihood Classifier Algorithm (GMLCA) within ArcGIS 10.8. The weights in this study, as shown in Fig. 3c, were assigned based on potential groundwater recharge using LULC data. Water bodies and dense forest areas were assigned less importance than snow mountain areas, residential areas, agricultural land, and barren land.

Soil

Soil plays a vital role in determining groundwater potential zones (GPZs) due to its influence on water infiltration. The ability of topsoil to allow water to percolate is significantly affected by factors such as grain structure, size, texture, shape, and porosity, which collectively enhance water infiltration9,56. Soil type is also influenced by the conditions of pore saturation and dryness, which affect the entry of water into the subsurface57. Soil composition analysis reveals a wide variety of soil types in the study region Fig. 3d. Sandy and coarse-grained soils exhibit higher porosity and permeability, allowing for rapid infiltration, whereas clayey loam and clay soils show significantly lower rates of infiltration9,58. Accordingly, higher weights were assigned to sandy soils in the determination of GPZs to reflect their superior water infiltration characteristics.

Slope

It is defined as the rate of maximum change in elevation between adjacent cells in a Digital Elevation Model (DEM). The slope of a watershed has a significant influence on water availability, runoff, and infiltration. Areas with steep slopes are characterized by higher runoff volumes and reduced infiltration rates due to the rapid movement of water over the surface24. The natural breaks jenks method has been used for classification of the slope map. In contrast, regions with gentle slopes facilitate greater infiltration and lower runoff, making them more suitable for groundwater recharge42. Curvature

The curvature is a measure of how far a surface or linear function deviates from being perfectly flat51. The surface contour, which might be convex or concave upward, is determined by curvature61. Topographic Wetness Index (TWI)

An important metric in hydrogeological investigations is the Topographic Wetness Index (TWI), which was first presented in 197963. The topographical factors affect the amount and location of water sources that cause surface runoff64,65. Based on terrain features and their influence on the topography of the area, TWI can also be used to evaluate the possibility of groundwater penetration66. When identifying possible groundwater zones, many researchers have used the TWI (Fig. 4d). Very low, low, medium, high, and very high are the five groups into which TWI values are divided in this research. The regions with the highest TWI values, indicating a high potential for recharge, are given greater importance.

Drainage density

The Drainage Density (DD) is calculated by dividing the total length of all streams in the basin by its surface area51. The closeness of steam channels is what constitutes DD,67. The drainage density, which is inversely proportional to permeability, must be considered when computing the GPZs. The potential for groundwater buildup is significantly enhanced in areas with low drainage densities due to the increased infiltration capacity in such places68. Kumari et al.42 found that an increase in drainage density has a direct effect on recharge capacity since it increases runoff and decreases infiltration. Discussions

Despite its critical role in supporting life, groundwater levels have been steadily dropping. Nevertheless, due to anthropogenic activities and imbalanced patterns of development, its recharge rate has declined dramatically over the past four to five periods51. In this study, we utilized RS satellite data to create nine thematic layers that impact the groundwater potential of the subject area. To obtain the optimal rankings and weights for the nine theme layers, the AHP method was employed. To create the final groundwater map, these layers were developed within a GIS framework and classified using the AHP technique.The ArcGIS environment assigns the GPZs weightage to individual variables of thematic layers.

The weighting scheme of the AHP shows that there is a rational prioritization of the recharge-controlling factors within the mountainous landscape of the Kinnaur District. Rainfall (23.7) and Land Use/Land Cover (23.5) were found to be the most impactful factors as the predominant role of the climatic input and surface conditions as the main one that controls the recharge processes. Weight was also considerably given to slope (17.0%), indicating that it has a high level of control on the processes of runoff and infiltration in steep Himalayan topography. The significance of the structural permeability and surface runoff properties is displayed by the lineament density (9.0%) and drainage density (8.4%), and additional refinements in the infiltration and moisture retention capability are provided by soil (6.3%) and TWI (5.2). Curvature (2.9) and aspect (4.0) had poorer weights indicating their secondary but complementary role in groundwater recharge. The obtained λmax value of 9.68 yielded a Consistency Index (CI) of 0.133 and Consistency Ratio (CR) of 0.092, which falls short of the acceptable level of 0.10, and proved the internal consistency and reliability of the pairwise comparison matrix. Generally, the weighting distribution is hydrogeologically realistic and it matches the predominant terrain and climatic controls of the high altitude areas.

Although the current study uses a traditional AHP-GIS weighted overlay methodology because it is the most transparent, interpretable, and works well in areas with scarce data such as mountainous regions, new methods of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) highlight enormous opportunities in improving predictive ability in future studies. Randome Forest (RF), Support Vector machine (SVM), Gradient Boosting, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and deep learning models are advanced algorithms that have the capability to identify complex non-linear relationships among conditions of the hydrogeology and the occurrence of groundwater. Such data methods minimize the subjectivity in the weighting process, and can lead to better model performance, especially where there are adequate training and validation data (e.g., borewell yield, spring inventory data). The combination of expert knowledge based on AHP and ML optimization methods can also be further reinforced by hybrid models to enhance robustness in predictions. To further enhance the accuracy of the results, future research in the area of Kinnaur may utilize ML/DL frameworks and compare their performance to the existing AHP system based on AUC-ROC, precision-recall, and kappa metrics, as well as determine their applicability to high-relief Himalayan sites. This kind of integration would elevate the scientific soundness and forecast factors of groundwater potential mapping.

Limitations, constraints, and future perspectives

The research is largely based on the secondary spatial data (e.g., DEM, rainfall products, geological and soil maps), which can be characterized by built-in resolution constraints and classification ambiguity. The 30 m DEM resolution which is good on a regional scale, may not be able to adequately represent the micro-topographic changes in a steep Himalayan land. In the same way, rainfall datasets are gridded estimates and are not necessarily a source of localized orographic variability. Hydrogeological accuracy is also hampered by the lack of detailed lithological data on parameters of the aquifer (e.g., transmissivity, storage coefficient).

The AHP-based weighted overlay methodology incorporates judgement in weighting and this aspect of the method brings certain degree of subjectivity even though the method allows acceptable consistency ratios. It is a model that is not dynamic and it does not consider the time variation of recharge processes and the effects of climate change. Future studies can be conducted with the additions of higher-resolution datasets, lithological/geomorphological map details, field-based verification (spring discharge, borewell yield data) and time-series analysis. The combination of data-driven models including machine learning, ensemble modeling as well as hybrid frameworks might also enhance predictive performance and minimize subjectivity. Furthermore, it would be necessary to provide groundwater potential mapping with the climate variability and land-use change scenarios to make it more applicable in long-term water resource planning in mountainous areas.

Validation

Validation is of utmost importance because it ensures that the results are scientifically relevant69,70. To verify the results, an inventory of locations with significant groundwater potential has been created by marking the locations of the water bodies on Google Earth Pro. Validation of GPZ mapping studies often employs the ROC approach, a parametric analytical tool71,72. Applying the ROC curve and computing the area under the curve (AUC) validated the map’s accuracy. According to relevant studies, GPZs maps with AUC values between 0.7 and 0.9 are considered to have satisfactory accuracy73,74. An AUC of 71.7% was achieved (Fig. 7), which is comparable to other studies in the literature42,44, demonstrating that the GPZs map in the research area was generated with outstanding accuracy as a result of the methodologically integrated approach between AHP and GIS.Fig. 7AHP model derived GPZs map ROC and AUC curve.

Conclusions

Using a multi-criteria decision-analysis approach based on integrated GIS, this study identifies groundwater potential zones in the Kinnaur region of Himachal Pradesh. Using satellite imagery and supplementary datasets, thematic layers were created. The resultant map would categorize the area into five possible zones of which about 45 percent fell in to medium to very high groundwater potential, which means that the area is favorable to sustainable groundwater development. ROC analysis (AUC = 71.7%), and surface water body verification of model validation indicated satisfactory predictive behaviour. The study region was divided into five classifications on the groundwater potential map that came out: Very Low (24%, 1536.24 km2), Low (31%, 1984.31 km2), Medium (21%, 1344.21 km2), High (18%, 1152.18 km2), and Very High (6%, 384.06 km2).

Google Earth Pro verification of the location of surface water bodies ensures the soundness and feasibility of the proposed model in resource assessment and management in groundwater. Furthermore, validation on the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve indicates that the accurateness is 71.7, when an inventory of locations with high potential of ground water was employed. The resulting potential map of ground water can be applied as a crucial decision support model in sustainable groundwater exploration, site-based well planning and resource prioritization.

This study, in comparison with most of the prior groundwater potential investigations, which either apply only the morphometric parameters or restrict themselves by a few thematic layers, incorporates various hydrogeological, topographic and climatic variables into an organized system of AHP-GIS modeling that is adapted to a high altitude, Himalayan topography. Namely, the problem of complex geomorphological and structural controls of a cold desert-mountain setting is specifically investigated in the study that is still not well represented in the literature on groundwater. Also, the dual validation with ROC-based analysis and verification of surface water bodies ensure the high credibility of the model as opposed to the research based on single validation methods.

The study has limitations though it is very strong. The AHP approach entails subjective use in weight allocation that can be subject to biasness despite consistency ratio tests. This model too relies on the spatial resolution and quality of inputs data especially that of DEM and thematic layers. The fact that the processes of temporal variability of the recharges and lack of long-term groundwater level monitoring data may also affect the quality of predictions. Time-series groundwater level data, pumping tests, and hydrochemical analysis should be included in the future research to improve on model validation. Predictive accuracy could be enhanced through the combination of newest data-driven models, which include machine learning-based ensemble models, and the use of higher-resolution remote sensing datasets. Moreover, the climate change conditions, and seasonal variability of recharge must be studied to comprehend the long-term groundwater sustainability in the weak Himalayan ecosystems.
