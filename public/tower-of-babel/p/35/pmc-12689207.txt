Multidisciplinary perinatal care for women with bleeding disorders
Authors: Vanessa Giuliano, Natalya E. O’Neill, Vincent So, Shamshah Aratia, Shalene Wong, Allison Rupnaraine, Rebecca Sampat, Filomena Meffe, Jillian M. Baker, Jeffrey Wassermann, Grace H. Tang, Michelle Sholzberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen with bleeding disorders face increased bleeding risks during the perinatal period, making coordinated care essential to prevent and manage bleeding complications.ObjectivesThe study aimed to characterize the perinatal experiences of women with bleeding disorders and assess the impact of multidisciplinary care and care plans on patients and healthcare providers.MethodsWomen aged >18 years with bleeding disorders who were provided a multidisciplinary care plan and underwent labor and delivery were recruited from St. Michael’s Hospital Multidisciplinary Clinic for Women with Bleeding Disorders. Semistructured interviews were conducted until thematic saturation was reached, and transcripts were analyzed using descriptive qualitative analysis. Provider experiences were assessed using survey methods.ResultsFourteen participants were interviewed. Four primary themes were identified: 1) the impact of bleeding disorders on pregnancy, 2) challenges with the healthcare system, 3) St. Michael’s Hospital Multidisciplinary Clinic for Women with Bleeding Disorders delivered patient-centered care, and 4) experience with the care plan. Eighteen healthcare providers completed the survey, with 89% supporting that the care plan was helpful and all agreeing that it met clinical needs.ConclusionOur work demonstrates that although women with bleeding disorders face unique perinatal challenges, multidisciplinary care and individualized care plans improve confidence and empowerment and support advocacy-based, patient-centered care.

Introduction

Women with bleeding disorders face various hemostatic challenges during the perinatal period, placing them at increased risk of bleeding-related complications [1,2]. During this period, individual risk must be evaluated in the context of a patient’s diagnosis and bleeding history. Careful anesthetic planning must ensue to minimize the risk of epidural hematomas [3]. Care teams must also carefully consider bleeding risks associated with various modes of delivery and manage the subsequent risk of postpartum hemorrhage [3]. Further, the risk of bleeding in the neonate, due to acquired and inherited factors, must also be considered [1,4]. These risks to both the birthing individual and neonate make coordinated care between multiple subspecialists crucial for safety in the perinatal period [2].

The benefits of multidisciplinary care have been widely recognized for a host of complex medical conditions [5]. An integrated approach to care for chronic diseases has been shown to decrease mortality and emergency room visits and improve overall function [5]. Multidisciplinary care provided through hemophilia treatment centers, which have historically focused on the care of men with bleeding disorders, has shown benefits, including tailored care to manage bleeding symptoms and pain as well as increased patient empowerment [6,7]. In women with bleeding disorders, a multidisciplinary approach to care addresses bleeding symptoms that are typically overlooked, such as heavy menstrual bleeding, and improves quality of life [[8], [9], [10]], with international guidelines supporting its integration [11]. Despite this, the adoption of multidisciplinary perinatal care for women with bleeding disorders has been less widely implemented [2].

The purpose of this study was to characterize the experiences of women with bleeding disorders during the perinatal period and to assess the impact of multidisciplinary perinatal care. We further evaluated how the use of individualized multidisciplinary perinatal care plans influenced the experiences of both patients and their healthcare providers (HCPs).

Methods

In this article, we use the term “women” to refer to women with bleeding disorders during the perinatal period, and “female” in reference to the biological capacity to menstruate, become pregnant, give birth, and lactate. We recognized that these terms may be exclusive and may not extend to all individuals with the anatomy capable of menstruation, pregnancy, and childbirth, including girls, transgender men, intersex people, and gender nonbinary individuals [9,12]. Throughout this review, we use the Canadian Institutes of Health Research defintion of sex as “a set of biological attributes in humans and animals that is primarily associated with physical and physiological features including chromosomes, gene expression, hormone levels, and reprodutive/sexual anatomy” and gender as “the socially constructed roles, behaviours, expressions, and identities of girls, women, boys, men and gender-diverse people” (CIHR citation inputted in query comments).

This was a mixed-methods study. We conducted a qualitative interview study to explore the experiences of women with bleeding disorders and multidisciplinary perinatal care plans during labor and delivery at St. Michael’s Hospital, Toronto, Canada. The Multidisciplinary Clinic for Women with Bleeding Disorders (MCWBD) was formed in response to a critical need for comprehensive care for women with bleeding disorders. The MCWBD is composed of a hematologist, an obstetrician, a pediatric hematologist, an anesthesiologist, a specialized nurse practitioner, and a pharmacist. Patients cared for by the MCWBD are provided with individualized care plans that optimize care for the patient and fetus throughout pregnancy, during anesthetic interventions, at delivery, and in postpartum/postnatal care. These plans were designed to provide HCPs with prophylactic and reactive guidance on potential hemostatic challenges throughout the perinatal period.

Individuals aged 18 years and over who had been provided with a perinatal care plan by the MCWBD between 2015 and 2024 were eligible for participation. A purposeful sampling approach was used to identify participants. Eligible participants were those with acquired or inherited bleeding disorders, such as von Willebrand disease, hemophilia, platelet function disorders, and immune thrombocytopenia, as all are managed by the care team of the MCWBD. Participants were recruited via telephone and email. Semistructured interviews were conducted by one of the female authors (V.G., resident physician, MSc, MD). V.G. had no prior relationship to any of the participants. Participants were made aware that the interviewer was a resident physician conducting interviews of patients’ experiences with the MCWBD. Interviews were conducted virtually, which allowed participants to join from a private location of their choosing without others present. A qualitative interview guide was developed by the MCWBD team and used to maintain consistency across interviews. No repeat interviews were conducted. Field notes were made during interviews to capture contextual details. Questions focused on participants’ lived experiences with their bleeding disorder diagnosis, pregnancy, interactions with the MCWBD clinic, and the provided care plans. Demographic data of the participants were also collected.

All interviews were recorded, transcribed, and reviewed for accuracy. Transcripts were not reviewed by the participants for comment or correction. Thematic analysis was employed to identify themes in the transcripts. The transcripts were then independently coded by 2 investigators (V.G. and V.S.), using NVivo software (Lumivero, version 15), with discrepancies in coding resolved through discussion. The initial coding scheme was developed by analyzing the first 5 transcripts and modified as needed thereafter. Thematic saturation was achieved following 10 interviews, and an additional 4 interviews were conducted to ensure no new themes were identified. Participants did not provide feedback on thematic analysis.

To assess the experiences of HCPs who used the care plan, an online survey was developed. The survey used a Likert scale to evaluate key outcomes, including (1) HCPs' perceived value of the care plan, (2) satisfaction with the care plan, and (3) adherence to the care plan. Eligible HCPs were identified by their affiliation with their respective hospital divisions/departments at St. Michael’s Hospital. Eligible HCPs were sent a study email invitation that provided the link to the online survey.

Results

Patient qualitative interviews

A total of 14 patient participants were interviewed. The interview time ranged from 11 to 42 minutes. The median age of the participants at the time of interview was 35 years (range, 32-45 years). The median age at the time of pregnancy and connected to the MCWBD was 33 years (range, 27-40 years). The diagnoses of our participants included immune thrombocytopenia (3 participants), type 1 von Willebrand disease (3 participants), Gray platelet syndrome (1 participant), aspirin-like platelet function disorder (1 participant), concurrent factor (F)VII deficiency and type 1 von Willebrand disease (1 participant), FV deficiency (1 participant), FVII deficiency (1 participant), concurrent FVII and FXI deficiency (1 participant), FXI deficiency (1 participant), and hemophilia A carrier (1 participant).

Theme 1: burden of disease: impact of bleeding disorder on pregnancy

Pregnancy unveils the significance of bleeding disorders

Eleven patients were diagnosed with a bleeding disorder prior to becoming pregnant. Eight out of 11 (73%) participants reported being minimally impacted by their condition prior to the perinatal period. These patients emphasized that they were unaware of the potential impact of their condition on their family planning or life in general until they attempted to become pregnant or experienced bleeding complications during pregnancy.

Anxiety related to a bleeding disorder

Eleven of the 14 (79%) participants described anxiety related to the impact of their bleeding disorder on the perinatal period. This anxiety was present at various stages of pregnancy, including preconception planning, concerns about potential complications for both the fetus and the patients themselves during pregnancy, and anxiety surrounding postpartum bleeding. Many women described distress at the possibility of genetically (or passively, in the case of immune thrombocytopenia) passing their condition to their child. While some participants feared the unknown, others who had experienced multiple pregnancies were apprehensive about reliving past traumas from their earlier pregnancies.

Trauma associated with the perinatal experience

Nine of the 14 (64%) participants reported experiencing trauma associated with the perinatal period, with most trauma stemming from bleeding complications and associated management by HCPs external to the MCWBD. Participants described feeling that their condition took away their ability to have what they perceived as a “normal pregnancy,” such as being unable to witness the birth of their child due to the need for general anesthesia in the case of a required cesarean section or being unable to experience a natural delivery.

Participants who had a family history of inherited bleeding disorders recounted intergenerational trauma, living in fear of encountering the same severe bleeding complications as their relatives. One participant noted that her traumatic experience would prevent her from pursuing future pregnancies. Participants also outlined the psychosocial impacts of their bleeding disorders, with one participant describing how the stigma of her diagnosis within her community led to social isolation and negative mental health consequences (participant 6).

The impact of bleeding on daily life in the perinatal period

Ten of the 14 (71%) participants recounted significant bleeding events that impacted their daily living. These complications included miscarriages with excessive bleeding, significant vaginal bleeding during pregnancy, postpartum hemorrhage, and thrombocytopenia. Bleeding symptoms interfered with normal life due to the frequent emergency department visits, frequent investigations (blood work and ultrasound), and the need for treatments (infusions of blood products, intravenous iron, and the administration of steroids) with associated side effects.

Theme 2: challenges with the healthcare system

Dismissal, misrecognition, and disbelief of bleeding symptoms by HCPs

Ten of the 14 (71%) participants described past experiences that included dismissal, disbelief, or misrecognition of their bleeding symptoms by HCPs. When the significance of bleeding symptoms was not recognized by HCPs in a timely manner, this often led to diagnostic delays, with participants suffering from symptoms for years before being referred to a hematologist and obtaining a diagnosis. Participants reported that their bleeding symptoms, particularly heavy menstrual bleeding prior to pregnancy, were minimized or ignored by healthcare professionals. One patient reported that even when she presented her treatment wallet card to physicians and a physician provided a document that validated her condition and provided treatment guidance, her bleeding symptoms during pregnancy remained dismissed and were left unmanaged.

Perceived HCP discomfort in providing necessary care

Seven of the 14 (50%) participants reported that they perceived HCPs outside of the MCWBD as uncomfortable managing their bleeding disorder and lacked understanding of their condition. One participant highlighted how she had difficulty finding an obstetrician in her served geographic region who would follow her during pregnancy, as they were uncomfortable providing care within the context of her bleeding disorder diagnosis. One participant stated that her rare condition made her feel like a "lab rat" during pregnancy, studied by the HCPs around her.

Participants reported advocating for referrals to specialized care for the purposes of obtaining a diagnosis and receiving treatment during acute bleeding events. In some cases, participants would directly contact their own hematologist when they were concerned that their care was unsafe.

Participants described feeling exhausted and frustrated from the constant need to advocate for care, with some fearing what would happen if they were ever unable to do so. One patient, who was an HCP herself, voiced concerns about how someone without a medical background could possibly advocate to the same extent she was required and equipped to.

Theme 3: the MCWBD delivered effective, patient-centered care

The MCWBD provided patient support and advocacy

All 14 participants (100%) described feeling supported by the MCWBD during the perinatal period. Participants reported feeling validated as their symptoms were taken seriously and were reassured knowing that they had an expert point of contact should they experience bleeding complications.

Eight of the 14 (57%) participants expressed that the MCWBD unburdened them by acting as an advocate. These participants said that when they felt at risk, expert providers of the MCWBD were able to directly advocate for them to receive safe care. Some recounted instances where their HCPs disproportionately considered the care of the fetus, and MCWBD team members were able to advocate to ensure the care of the mother was equally carefully considered.

The MCWBD increased access to appropriate, patient-centered care

Nine of the 14 (64%) participants in our study outlined that their connection to the MCWBD increased their access to care. One participant stated that the providers in the MCWBD were instrumental in referring them to relevant subspecialists, as they were equipped to identify aspects of their medical history that could pose a health risk during pregnancy in the context of their bleeding disorder. Participants who lived remotely recounted how their connection to the MCWBD allowed them to receive care at their home hospital, a hospital they preferred for its familiarity, using a shared care model and virtual visits. Some participants noted that their connection to the MCWBD allowed them to avoid emergency department visits, as they had a reliable point of contact who could provide care in a timely manner.

Connection to the MCWBD improved care in subsequent pregnancies

Four participants had at least 1 pregnancy without MCWBD care, followed by a pregnancy with MCWBD care. All 4 of these participants stated that their perinatal experience was significantly improved with support from the MCWBD in comparison with their earlier pregnancy.

Theme 4: experience with a care plan

The care plan increased patient confidence

Twelve of the 14 (92%) participants stated that having a care plan increased their confidence in the labor and delivery process. Participants described that the care plan provided them with a sense of control, knowing that the team had thoughtfully planned their management and outlined what to expect. Participants particularly appreciated the inclusion of contingency plans to address multiple potential scenarios in their care.

The care plan empowered the patient to actively participate in their own care

Eight of the 14 (57%) participants said that having a care plan allowed for the opportunity to contribute actively to their own labor and delivery experience. Additionally, many found that the care plan helped them advocate for their own care. When they felt their care was being compromised, they could draw attention to their bleeding disorder by referring to the care plan, which included management guidance. The care plan also empowered them to learn more about their condition. By reading the provided care plan, participants said they could engage in discussions and seek explanations about their treatment, increasing their understanding and sense of empowerment.

Barriers to care plan implementation

Five of the 14 (36%) participants described scenarios in which they felt that at least 1 member of their care team at the time of labor and delivery (outside the MCWBD) was either unaware of the care plan or did not follow the suggestions provided.

Discussion

Our study aimed to characterize the perinatal experiences of women with bleeding disorders and describe the impact of perinatal care plans on both patients and HCPs. Key themes surrounding the perinatal experience of participants included the emotional burden of their disease, the perceived dismissal and mismanagement of their bleeding symptoms by HCPs, and the need for self-advocacy. Participants described that multidisciplinary teams promoted patient-centered and advocacy-based care, while care plans increased confidence, empowerment, and engagement in their care. HCPs reported that these care plans provided clarity, guidance, and coordination of care, while facilitating prompt management of bleeding symptoms.

The experiences of women with bleeding disorders are markedly underrepresented in the literature [13]. Few studies have used qualitative approaches to understand their lived experiences, and even fewer have assessed their experiences during pregnancy, labor, and delivery. Previous work [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]] on the experiences of women with bleeding disorders outside of pregnancy highlights challenges in accessing care, misdiagnoses, the need for self-advocacy, and describes the emotional burden of bleeding disorders on daily life. A recent systematic review reinforced these themes [19], emphasizing that women with bleeding disorders struggle to access appropriate care and experience significant psychosocial impacts. VanderMeulen et al. [10] explored the postpartum experiences of women with bleeding disorders, where challenges in recognizing excessive postpartum bleeding and accessing proper perinatal care were described. Our findings build on this literature, offering unique insights into the entire perinatal experience of this patient population.

Interestingly, many participants endorsed that pregnancy unveiled the significance of their bleeding disorder and the validity of their experience to them personally, despite having been diagnosed before pregnancy, and later recounting symptoms such as heavy menstrual bleeding in early life. This likely reflects prepregnancy normalization of heavy bleeding, a commonly described occurrence among women with bleeding disorders [9,16,17], with pregnancy enhancing awareness of their condition and its potential impact on their health and the health of their baby.

Our work overall provides evidence that multidisciplinary care models and perinatal care plans effectively address many perinatal challenges faced by women with bleeding disorders by increasing patient self-confidence and access to care, and by fostering a sense of support and empowerment. While the use of multidisciplinary care for men with bleeding disorders has been widely implemented, its adoption for women has been less widespread [20]. Emerging evidence, including findings from this study, supports its global application for women, as advocated by international guidelines [5,8,11,20,21]. Prior studies have similarly noted a positive impact of multidisciplinary care for women with bleeding disorders, including increased patient empowerment during the postpartum period [10] and patient satisfaction [22]. This further aligns with initiatives from the Foundation for Women and Girls with Bleeding Disorders Clinics of Excellence model, which aims to promote best practices in delivering multidisciplinary care to females with bleeding disorders [23].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the use of multidisciplinary care plans during the perinatal period. Although patient feedback on the care plans was overall positive, some participants expressed that some HCPs were unfamiliar with the plans or did not adhere to their recommendations, suggesting inadequate integration of the care plans into patient care and an opportunity for knowledge translation. While our HCP survey data contrasts with this view, with all surveyed HCPs stating that they followed the care plan and 89% of HCPs agreeing that their colleagues followed the care plan, this may be biased, as all responding HCPs work at the same hospital as the MCWBD. Future work should focus on understanding the barriers and facilitators to the application of care plans across various hospital settings, both within and outside hemophilia treatment centers, so that interventions and implementation plans are properly mapped to them.

Strengths of our study include the use of one-on-one interviews that facilitated in-depth discussions, diverse representation of both inherited and acquired bleeding disorder diagnoses, the inclusion of women who experienced pregnancies with and without the involvement of the MCWBD, and the inclusion of survey data from HCPs who used the care plans. Several limitations should be acknowledged in our study. All study participants were recruited from a single tertiary center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Further, our interviews were conducted virtually. While this format increased accessibility for participants (such as those living outside the Toronto area), it is possible that it may have limited our ability to observe nonverbal cues, establish rapport, and minimize environmental distractions. Finally, although our study represented a large breadth of patients with different bleeding disorder diagnoses, only 1 patient, who was a hemophilia carrier, was recruited. Hemophilia carriers face unique challenges as their bleeding symptoms are often dismissed and underestimated due to the “carrier” label, which may falsely signal to HCPs that they are asymptomatic carriers [17]. This makes representation of the perinatal experiences of hemophilia carriers in future work critical for understanding their unique experiences.

In summary, our work demonstrates that a multidisciplinary approach to perinatal care not only addresses the barriers experienced by women with bleeding disorders but also significantly improves care for patients and HCPs alike. Given these positive impacts, we believe that the widespread adoption of this care model should be prioritized, in alignment with the accepted standard of care that is already available globally for men with bleeding disorders.
