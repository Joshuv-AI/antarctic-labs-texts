Improvement of directivity in plasmonic nanoantennas based on structured cubic gold nanoparticles
Authors: Shiva Moazen Dehkordi, Hamidreza Mohammadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn array of metallic nanoparticles can diffract or concentrate the incident electromagnetic wave and behave as an antenna. In this paper, the effects of the inner sub-wavelength structure of nanoparticles are studied on the directivity of the plasmonic nanoantenna, which is coated on the output of a waveguide. Three 5*5 element configurations are analyzed: nanocubes, nanoshells, and nanoframes array. Numerical results are obtained using the 3D FDTD technique. The results show that structured nanoantennas can improve the antenna's directivity due to the plasmonic properties and hybridization mechanism. Between the three configurations investigated in the 250–800 nm wavelength range, the nanoshell array exhibits maximum and minimum amounts of its directivity at 321.5 nm and 591 nm, respectively. At 558 nm, nanoframes and nanoshells' arrays show the same amount of directivity, and from the wavelength greater than 558 nm, the nanoframe array has the best performance. The results may help design and fabricate directive optical fiber endcaps.Subject terms: Photonic devices, Nanophotonics and plasmonics

Introduction

Plasmonic nanostructures offer new possibilities for controlling and localizing electromagnetic fields at subwavelength scales1,2. These optics effectively augment the capabilities of various optical instruments, such as sensors and antennas, thereby improving their overall performance. The nanoantenna is the primary element of an optical antenna array that enables the manipulation of light direction in free space. Beam steering in optical antenna arrays is achieved by precisely controlling the phase of light emitted by each nanoantenna according to the interference feature3–5

Plasmonic-based nanoantennas are proposed to reduce the size and increase the radiation efficiency of antennas6–9. Plasmonic nanoantennas (PNAs) consist of both metal and dielectric materials10–17. When an electromagnetic wave impinges on a metal–dielectric interface, it causes the electrons on the metal to oscillate due to coupling with the wave. Consequently, a hybrid particle known as a surface plasmon polariton (SPP) emerges. Metallic nanostructures exhibit two plasmonic modes: surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) and localized surface plasmon polaritons (LSPs)18. When certain phase-matching conditions are met, surface plasmon polariton (SPP) waves can propagate along the interface between a metal and a dielectric material. In contrast, localized surface plasmons (LSPs) are strongly confined standing waves on the nanoparticle surfaces18. Due to their curved surface, direct illumination can excite LSP in nanoparticles without phase-matching conditions. Localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) is caused by incident light that resonates with coherent electron oscillations18. The generation of LSPR allows the establishment of electromagnetic fields and the absorption and scattering of light, which depends on the shape, size, chemical composition, and environment of the nanoparticles2,19,20. The LSPR radiation can be directly coupled to free space radiation, as can conventional R.F. antennas. PNAs possess unique capabilities to manipulate light-matter interactions at subwavelength levels, enabling them to serve as valuable tools in various technological applications such as solar energy harvesting16,17, sensing21–23, nanomedicine24, imaging25, communication26,27, spectroscopy28–32, single-molecule analysis33,34, optical trapping35, light emission36–41, photocatalysis42,43. Recent research has demonstrated that the plasmonic properties of nanoparticles may be significantly enhanced through the utilization of hollow metallic particles, including nanoshells, nanoframes, and nanocages, via the plasmon hybridization mechanism42. The plasmon hybridization theory, as proposed by Pordan et al., presents a physical model for analyzing plasmon resonances observed in metallic particles of hollow shapes. This model considers a nanoshell with different resonance frequencies, comprising an inner cavity and an outer surface. The coupling between cavity (hybrid) and sphere plasmons depends on the nanoshell's finite thickness.

The strength of this particular interaction is inherently linked to the thickness of the nanoshell. The hybridization mechanism of nanoshells is known to split their plasmonic oscillations into symmetric and asymmetric modes, with the former occurring at lower frequencies and exhibiting lower energy levels compared to the latter. The asymmetric oscillation, dark mode, does not show any coupling with far-field radiation. Conversely, the symmetric oscillations exhibit a significant coupling level with the external optical fields, as previous research indicates43. The hybridization mechanism presents a promising explanation for various challenges encountered in plasmonics. By leveraging this insight, researchers can develop innovative plasmonic designs with the potential to enhance the sensitivity of sensors44, plasmon-enhanced solar cells, photodetectors, drug delivery, and cancer therapy as well as nanolasers, invisibility cloaks, and quantum computing, through changing the distribution of surface charges45–48.

Optical antennas have many applications in microscopy, metrology, sensing, and broadcasting. These antennas can efficiently collect and direct light toward or spread a specific target. Using nanoantennas equipped with localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) effectively concentrates electromagnetic energy from a wide range of wavelengths to minimal volumes on the Nanoscale18. Moreover, these nanoantennas can efficiently radiate energy back to the continuum10. A measure that qualifies this antenna property is named the “directivity” of the antenna.

Particular literature articles focus on the directivity of nanoantennas, specifically PNAs14,49, and50. For example, in 2021, Qaisar Hayat et al. studied the behavior of an array of active coated spherical nanoparticles mediated by a passive dielectric to solve the instability problem in the core–shell nanoantenna and improve its directivity simultaneously. They also claim that their configuration can improve the optical characteristics and is superior to conventional configurations for nano-amplifier and nanosensor applications51. Recently, Phung et al. have considered an array of dipole antennas and calculated their directivity from R.F. to optical frequencies via the finite difference time domain (FDTD) method. Their results show that the notion of directivity differs for R.F. and microwave concerning the optical region. Despite R.F. and microwave antennas, a single dipole optical antenna can be crucially shorter than half wavelength due to plasmonic effects, (which) arose in this regime. In addition, they report beam shaping with an optical dipole array for improving the antenna's performance in applications such as solar energy conversion, spectroscopy, and nanophotonics52.

Further research is necessary to comprehend how nanoparticles' surface structure and hybridization mechanisms affect the directivity of plasmonic nanoantennas (PNAs). These variables have yet to be thoroughly examined and necessitate additional investigation. In our study, we sought to assess the directivity of PNAs, which consist of cubic nano-frames, nanoshells, and nanocubes. We conducted simulations at various wavelengths ranging from 250 to 800 nm to evaluate their performance. To achieve this objective, we utilize the 3D FDTD method to numerically solve Maxwell's equations with appropriate boundary conditions for various PNA structures. Our findings indicate that the directivity of PNAs can be improved by customizing the hybridization mechanism.

Conclusion

Structured nanoparticles, such as nanocubes, nanoshells, and nano-frames, have been utilized to create plasmonic nanoantennas that couple electromagnetic waves from waveguide to free space. Numerical calculations were done using the 3D FDTD technique in the 250–800 nm wavelength range. It has been shown that all structures scatter light into free space. The highest and lowest directivity values were related to the nanoshell array at wavelengths 591 and 321.5 nm. Moreover, nanoframes exhibit excellent directivity from wavelengths greater than 558 nm due to the hybridization mechanism. Among the investigated cases, the nanoantenna based on the nanoframes always showed a more directional directivity. This study's findings offer valuable insight into constructing efficient optical fiber endcaps.
