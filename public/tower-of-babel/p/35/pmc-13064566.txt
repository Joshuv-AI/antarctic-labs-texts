High-throughput transposon sequencing identifies HprR as a key regulator of the LEE operon and virulence in E. coli O157:H7
Authors: Miaomiao Liu, Pan Wu, Tao Luo, Linxing Li, Yuchen Wang, Jingliang Qin, Dan Liu, Xiaoya Li, Binbin Xiang, Yuanyuan Niu, Lu Wang, Bin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli (EHEC) comprises a prominent group of extracellular human pathogens that specifically colonize the colonic epithelium, leading to severe gastrointestinal diseases and representing a significant global public health threat. During infection, EHEC O157:H7—the most prevalent serotype – tightly adheres to intestinal epithelial cells and induces the formation of characteristic attaching and effacing (A/E) lesions. However, the molecular mechanisms governing this close interaction remain incompletely understood. In this study, we employed transposon-directed insertion-site sequencing (TraDIS) to conduct a genome-wide screen for genes essential for EHEC O157:H7 adherence. Among the identified candidates, disruption of hprR (encoding the response regulator of the HprS/HprR two-component system) resulted in markedly reduced epithelial colonization. Subsequent mechanistic analyses revealed that HprR positively regulates expression of genes within the locus of enterocyte effacement (LEE) pathogenicity island in a Ler-dependent manner, thereby promoting type III secretion system (T3SS) activity and epithelial colonization. Additionally, during luminal survival, HprR was found to directly bind to the promoter region of hiuH, a gene encoding a transthyretin-like protein, thereby enhancing bacterial resistance to oxidative stress and promoting persistence in the colonic lumen. Collectively, this study defines a comprehensive genomic framework for understanding EHEC O157:H7 colonization and identifies HprR as a dual-function regulatory protein that coordinates both epithelial adherence and luminal survival, thereby shaping host-pathogen interactions.

Introduction

Enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli O157:H7 (EHEC O157:H7) is a highly virulent foodborne pathogen responsible for both sporadic infections and large-scale outbreaks worldwide [1]. Epidemiological studies have shown that EHEC O157:H7 infection can lead to a wide range of clinical outcomes, from mild watery diarrhea to hemorrhagic colitis and, in severe cases, life-threatening hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), which carries a mortality rates of 2–5% [2,3]. Currently, there is no approved vaccine or pathogen-specific treatment available, and the use of antibiotics is generally contraindicated, as it may exacerbate toxin release and worsen clinical outcomes [4]. These challenges highlight the urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying EHEC O157:H7 pathogenesis to guide the development of effective therapeutic and preventive strategies [5].

Within the host, the pathogen initiates colonization by forming characteristic attaching and effacing (A/E) lesions – a hallmark of EHEC infections. These lesions are defined by localized destruction of intestinal microvilli and actin cytoskeleton rearrangements that generate pedestal-like structures beneath adherent bacteria. The ability of EHEC O157:H7 to colonize the large intestine depends on a complex arsenal of virulence factors, such as type III secretion system (T3SS) [6–8]. The T3SS is encoded within the locus of enterocyte effacement (LEE), a 35-kb pathogenicity island comprising five major transcriptional units (LEE1-LEE5) [9]. The LEE region also encodes structural components of the T3SS translocon (EspA, EspB, and EspD) [10,11], the outer membrane adhesin intimin, and a suite of effector proteins that are essential for intimate bacterial attachment to host epithelial cells [12,13]. Beyond the LEE-encoded T3SS, EHEC O157:H7 employs additional adhesins to promote epithelial attachment, such as curli fibers-amyloid-like structures assembled from subunits encoded by the csgBAC and csgDEFG operons-that facilitate surface adhesion and biofilm formation [14,15]. In parallel, EHEC produces Shiga toxins 1 and 2 (Stx1 and Stx2), which are responsible for systemic manifestations such as hemorrhagic colitis and hemolytic uremic syndrome [6,8].

To thrive in the hostile environment of the gastrointestinal tract, bacterial pathogens have evolved sophisticated regulatory networks that sense environmental cues and precisely coordinate the spatial and temporal expression of virulence genes [16]. Among these, two-component systems (TCSs) act as primary signaling modules linking environmental or host-derived stimuli to the transcriptional modulation of downstream targets [17]. Notably, the PhoQ/PhoP, BaeS/BaeR, and FusK/FusR systems facilitate intestinal colonization by modulating LEE gene expression in response to distinct environmental signals [18,19]. In addition, the BasS/BasR two-component system promotes survival of EHEC O157:H7 in the intestinal lumen by sensing bile salts and activating arnT expression, thereby enhancing resistance to host-derived antimicrobial peptides [20].

These examples illustrate that EHEC relies on intricate regulatory networks to sense and respond to diverse environmental challenges [21]. We therefore hypothesize that transcriptional regulators, particularly TCSs, play central roles in coordinating virulence factor synthesis and bacterial adaptation during infection. While an increasing number of such regulators have been implicated in virulence in recent year, many remain to be discovered. To identify such previously unrecognized regulators, we performed a genome-wide transposon mutagenesis screen in EHEC O157:H7.

Transposon-directed insertion-site sequencing (TraDIS), a high-throughput Tn-seq-based method, enables genome-wide mapping of gene essentiality and fitness with single-nucleotide precision. TraDIS has been successfully applied to identify essential and virulence-associated genes in several bacterial pathogens, including Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Salmonella enterica [22–27]. In EHEC O157:H7, this approach has previously been used to identify genes required for intestinal colonization in cattle [28,29], but its application to dissect epithelial colonization mechanisms remains unexplored.

In this study, we applied an unbiased TraDIS screen and identified hprR, a transcriptional regulator previously characterized in other contexts but not associated with bacterial adhesion. We demonstrated that HprR plays a previously unrecognized role in regulating adhesion and enhancing host colonization by EHEC O157:H7.

Results

Construction of a high-resolution transposon library for screening genes associated with adherence in EHEC O157:H7

To systematically screen for genes involved in adhesion of EHEC O157:H7 EDL933, we generated a genome-wide Tn5 transposon mutant library following previously published procedures [30,31] (Figure 1(a)). Approximately 5 × 10^ [5] transposon mutants were generated; the library size was estimated from colony-forming unit (CFU) counts on selective plates and further verified by the number of unique insertion sites detected by TraDIS. Sequencing analysis identified 1,072,936 (input_1), 1,128,422 (input_2), 833,562 (output_1), and 798,548 (output_2) unique insertion sites, which were distributed genome-wide with only minor gaps (Figure 1(b)). High reproducibility between replicates confirmed the robustness and comprehensive coverage of the Tn5 library for high-throughput screening of adherence-associated genes (Figure 1). Normalized insertion frequencies per gene were calculated to generate input/output (I/O) ratios. Based on a 2-fold cutoff (log2 fold change > 1 or <−1, FDR < 0.05), 336 genes were enriched and 346 genes were depleted in the output pool compared to input, suggesting potential roles in modulating bacterial adherence (Figure 1(c), Table S1). Using a VFDB-based pipeline, we identified 31 adhesin-associated genes (Table S2), most of which are involved in initial bacterial attachment [32,33]. Among these, 10 genes showed significantly reduced transposon insertion frequencies in the output library, indicating a likely contribution to adherence. In addition, the LEE, which encodes the primary attachment machinery of EHEC, was markedly affected: 26 of the 40 LEE genes exhibited decreased insertion frequencies in the output pool, indicating selective pressure during adherence. Several known regulatory genes affecting LEE expression, including fadR, argR, exuR [34–36], were also depleted, supporting their involvement in bacterial adhesion (Table S1).

To validate the TraDIS screen, ten regulatory genes were randomly selected from those showing significant changes in mutant abundance for experimental verification. Adherence assays revealed trends consistent with the TraDIS data, supporting the reliability of the screen (Figure 1(d)). Among the candidates exhibiting substantial abundance changes, hprR-a regulatory gene previously characterized but not reported to influence bacterial adhesion – was selected for detailed functional analysis in this study.

To elucidate the biological pathways involved in bacterial adherence, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for the 682 genes identified through TraDIS. As shown in Figure 1(e), significantly enriched pathways included the bacterial secretion system, flagellar assembly, pathogenic Escherichia coli infection, glycerophospholipid metabolism, mismatch repair, sulfur relay system, and aromatic compound degradation. These enrichments indicate that bacterial motility, host interaction mechanisms, and structural remodeling are key contributors to adherence phenotypes.

HprS/hprr TCS is required for EHEC O157:H7 adherence in vitro and in vivo

As described above, the TraDIS results showed that hprR gene, encodes cognate response regulator of the HprS/HprR TCS [37,38], exhibited significantly reduction in the count of transposon-genome sequences from the output pool relative to the input pool. So, we speculated that the HprS/HprR TCS might affect EHEC O157:H7 adherence capacity to host cells.

To evaluate the role of HprR in the adherence of EHEC O157:H7, a ΔhprR mutant and its complemented strain (ΔhprR+) were constructed for HeLa cell adherence assays. The results demonstrated that the ΔhprR mutant exhibited a significantly reduced adherence ability compared to both the wild-type (WT) and the complemented strain (Figure 2(a)). These results suggest that HprR positively regulates the adhesion of EHEC O157:H7 to host cells, which is consistent with our Tn5 libraries results. Meanwhile, WT, ΔhprR, and ΔhprR+ shared similar growth curves in vitro (Figure 2(b)), indicating the difference in adherence ability between these strains was not due to defects in growth. As ΔhprR exhibited a marked reduction in adherence, we hypothesized that HprR might influence the bacterial adhesion through affecting A/E lesion formation, which is the most important pathogenic feature associated with EHEC O157:H7 adherence to host epithelial cells [39]. The fluorescent actin staining (FAS) assay was conducted to evaluate whether HprR affected A/E lesion formation. The results showed that a smaller proportion of HeLa cells formed pedestals when infected with the ΔhprR strain (23%) compared to WT (47%) and ΔhprR+ (44%) strain respectively (Figure 2(c, d)). Consistently, the number of pedestals per infected HeLa cell was significantly lower in the ΔhprR group (4.3) than in the WT (12.4), whereas complementation with hprR partially restored pedestal formation (9.5) (Figure 2(e)). Cumulatively, these experiments illuminated the critical role of HprR in enhancing the adherence capacity of EHEC O157:H7 through its promotion of A/E lesion formation.

To investigate whether the effect of HprR on cellular adherence could be recapitulated in vivo, we assessed the colonization capacity of the relevant strains in vivo through animal experiments. The results of mouse colonization experiments showed that the number of ΔhprR bacteria recovered from the colon was significantly reduced compared to the WT and ΔhprR+(Figure 2(f)). We further conducted an infant rabbit competition assay, and the data revealed that the competitive index (CI) of ΔhprR versus WT was 0.13 in rabbit colon (Figure 2(g)), demonstrating HprR plays a significant role in EHEC O157:H7 colonization in vivo.

HprR promotes LEE genes expression in vivo and in vitro

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of the HprR-mediated virulence regulation in EHEC O157:H7, the high-throughput RNA-seq of WT and ΔhprR strains grown in DMEM medium were performed. In total, we obtained 25,138,778 and 25,877,814 reads for WT and ΔhprR strains (after filtering low quality reads), with mapping rates of 95.89% and 95.93% to the reference genome respectively. The MA plot of RNA-seq results showed that a total of 371 genes were found differentially expressed between WT and ΔhprR, with 288 downregulated genes and 83 upregulated genes (FDR < 0.05, |log2 fold change|≥1) (Figure 3(a), Table S3). To validate the reliability of the transcriptome data, 10 of the 371 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected randomly for RT-qPCR under the same culture conditions, and the results were highly consistent with our RNA-seq data (Fig. S2), suggesting the RNA-seq data is highly reliable and repeatable. The detailed RNA-seq analysis showed that the expression of most LEE genes was significantly decreased in ΔhprR compared to WT (Table S3). Consistently, the RT-qPCR results showed that the relative expression of seven representative LEE genes (ler, eae, tir, escT, escN, escC, espB) was markedly decreased in ΔhprR compared to WT and ΔhprR+ (Figure 3(b)). To define the effect of HprR in vivo, the RNA from colonic tissue of infant rabbit infected with WT or ΔhprR strains were isolated for RT-qPCR. The results showed that the expression of LEE genes in ΔhprR was 1.92–2.38-fold lower than that in WT (Figure 3(c)). These data indicate that HprR promotes the expression of the LEE genes in vivo and in vitro.

Furthermore, western blotting showed that the production of the Intimin (encoded by eae) and its translocated receptor (encoded by tir) exhibited a significant reduction in ΔhprR compared to WT in DMEM medium. Complementation with hprR restored the production of both proteins to WT levels (Figure 3(d, e)). These data reinforce that the activation of LEE mediated by HprR is essential for host colonization of EHEC O157:H7.

HprR directly binds to the promoter of ler

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism by which HprR contributes to bacterial pathogenicity, a ChIP-seq analysis was performed. The results revealed 41 high-confidence HprR-binding peaks (fold enrichment > 2; p ≤ 0.05) that were significantly enriched in the HprR-ChIP sample compared to the mock-ChIP control (Table S4). Notably, one of the most enriched peaks corresponded to the promoter region of ler (PLEE1), which exhibited a 4.3-fold increase in the HprR-ChIP sample relative to the control. This observation was further validated by ChIP-qPCR, which confirmed a 4.3-fold enrichment of the ler promoter in the HprR-ChIP sample. In contrast, no significant enrichment was detected for the promoter regions of PLEE2/3, PLEE4 and PLEE5, nor for the coding region of rpoS (used as a negative control) (Figure 4(a)). To further verify whether HprR modulates ler expression, a bioluminescent reporter assay was conducted to assess the promoter activity of ler (LEE1). Remarkably, both the WT and the complemented strain (ΔhprR+) exhibited approximately 2-fold higher LEE1-lux activity compared to the ΔhprR mutant (Figure 4(b)). Furthermore, electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) were conducted to assess the interaction between HprR and the LEE1 promoter. As shown in Figure 4(c), increasing concentrations of purified HprR protein led to a clear mobility shift of the PLEE1, indicating specific binding. In contrast, no shifted bands were observed for the PLEE2/3, PLEE4 and PLEE5, nor for the coding region of rpoS (negative control) (Figure 4(d); Fig. S3), suggesting that HprR specifically binds to the LEE1 promoter. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HprR specifically associates with the ler promoter both in vivo and in vitro, thereby positively regulating its transcription.

HprR induces LEE genes expression via ler

Given that Ler is the master regulator encoded within the LEE1 operon, we further investigated whether HprR regulates LEE gene expression and A/E lesion formation through Ler. To test this, the Δler, ΔlerΔhprR, and ΔlerΔhprR+ strains were constructed and used to infect HeLa cells. Compared to the WT strain, the Δler mutant exhibited significantly reduced adherence in the HeLa cell adherence assay. HprR enhances the survival of EHEC O157:H7 in the host by directly regulating hiuH expression

Our findings demonstrated that the colonization capacity of the ΔhprR strain was reduced by 9.9-fold in the murine colon, whereas a 4.06-fold decrease in adherence was observed in the in vitro HeLa cell assay. This discrepancy suggests that HprR may have additional roles in promoting EHEC O157:H7 colonization in the intestinal environment. Previous studies have shown that HprR enhances E. coli fitness under oxidative stress by activating the expression of hiuH, a gene involved in resistance to hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative damage [40]. HiuH has been well characterized as a detoxifying enzyme that reduces the accumulation of oxidative products, which is essential for bacterial survival both in vitro and in vivo [41]. Moreover, EHEC O157:H7 is known to employ multiple oxidative stress response pathways to enhance its survival within the host intestine [42]. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that HprR may promote the survival of EHEC O157:H7 in the colon by upregulating hiuH expression. To test this hypothesis, RT-qPCR was performed on WT, ΔhprR, and ΔhprR+ strains grown in DMEM. The results revealed that hiuH expression was significantly repressed in the ΔhprR mutant, while complementation in ΔhprR+ restored hiuH expression to levels comparable to the WT strain (Figure 6(a)), consistent with RNA-seq data (Table S2). Furthermore, intestinal lumen survival assays were performed using WT, ΔhprR, ΔhiuH, and ΔhprRΔhiuH strains, and viable bacterial loads were measured daily in fecal samples from day 1 to day 8 post-infection. Although ΔhprR showed slightly higher bacterial loads than the other two mutant strains, no statistically significant differences were observed among the ΔhprR, ΔhiuH, and ΔhprRΔhiuH groups, suggesting that HiuH functions downstream of HprR and mediates its role in promoting bacterial survival in the intestinal environment.

To further confirm that HiuH enhances the survival capacity of EHEC O157:H7 by conferring resistance to oxidative damage, an in vitro oxidative stress survival assay was performed. WT, ΔhprR, ΔhiuH, and ΔhprRΔhiuH strains were exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and their survival was monitored over time. The WT strain exhibited significantly greater tolerance to H2O2 compared to all three mutant strains at each time point. However, no notable differences in survival were observed among the ΔhprR, ΔhiuH, and ΔhprRΔhiuH strains, suggesting that HiuH contributes to EHEC O157:H7 survival specifically through oxidative stress resistance (Figure 6(c)).

To investigate the mechanism by which HprR regulates hiuH expression, ChIP-seq data were re-analyzed. The results revealed significant enrichment of the hiuH promoter region in the HprR-ChIP sample, with a 27-fold increase compared to the mock-ChIP control (Table S4). Consistently, ChIP-qPCR analysis confirmed a 14.66-fold enrichment of the hiuH promoter in the HprR-ChIP sample (Figure 6(d)), indicating that HprR directly binds to the hiuH promoter in vivo. In addition, EMSAs showed that increasing concentrations of phosphorylated HprR protein resulted in distinct mobility shift bands for the hiuH promoter, confirming specific binding in vitro (Figure 6(e–f)). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HprR directly binds to the hiuH promoter and activates its transcription, thereby enhancing the survival capacity of EHEC O157:H7 in the host intestine.

Discussion

EHEC O157:H7 colonizes the host colon and is a major causative agent of enteritis and diarrhea, with severe infections potentially progressing to HUS [43]. Adherence to intestinal epithelial cells is a critical early step in establishing infection, primarily mediated by T3SS and its effectors encoded within LEE pathogenicity island [7,8]. TraDIS is a high-throughput genetic screening approach that enables genome-wide identification of genotype – phenotype relationships in transposon mutant libraries, offering insights into the structural, regulatory, and functional roles of bacterial genes [44]. In this study, we applied a high-density TraDIS screen under cellular adhesion conditions and identified HprR as a novel positive regulator that promotes both epithelial adherence and in-host survival of EHEC O157:H7.

Based on these findings, we propose a model for HprR-dependent regulation of virulence (Figure 7). Upon entering the intestinal lumen, EHEC activates hiuH expression through HprR to counter oxidative stress and promote luminal persistence. As the bacterium contacts the epithelial surface, HprR directly activates ler, which in turn induces expression of the LEE genes and assembly of the T3SS. This regulatory cascade promotes the formation of A/E lesions and efficient colonization of the epithelium. Our findings establish HprR as a dual-function regulator that coordinates oxidative stress resistance and epithelial attachment, thus advancing our understanding of the spatially organized regulatory mechanisms governing EHEC pathogenesis. Our TraDIS results also confirmed the critical role of T3SS in promoting tight epithelial adherence. In particular, the transposon insertion frequency within T3SS-encoding genes was significantly reduced following host cell infection, indicating strong selective pressure for these loci. Notably, several known regulatory genes that influence LEE gene expression, such as baeR, exuR, and argR [34–36], also exhibited markedly decreased transposon insertion frequencies after infection. These findings support the robustness and biological relevance of our Tn5 mutant libraries. Beyond canonical virulence regulators, our preliminary screening also revealed a large number of candidate genes associated with diverse cellular processes, including transcription, amino acid transport and metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, signal transduction, and energy production. This highlights that bacterial adhesion is a complex, multifactorial process governed by a network of regulatory pathways. The roles of other adherence-associated genes uncovered in this study merit further investigation to fully elucidate their contributions to EHEC O157:H7 colonization and pathogenicity. While our in vitro and in vivo colonization data provide strong evidence that the transcriptional regulator HprR governs bacterial adhesion and persistence, we acknowledge a key limitation inherent to the mouse model used. Mice are not natural hosts for EHEC O157:H7, and this model cannot fully replicate the human disease pathology, such as the progression to HUS. Our use of the model was narrowly focused on assessing the impact of HprR on competitive intestinal colonization efficiency, a well-established indicator for adhesion capacity in EHEC studies. Future investigations will employ the Citrobacter rodentium model, which better recapitulates the A/E lesions caused by EHEC pathogenesis, to further validate our findings.

During infection, pathogenic bacteria must adapt to a range of distinct microenvironments within the host. TCSs enable bacteria to rapidly sense and respond to environmental changes [45]. Previous studies have demonstrated that several TCSs enhance the fitness and survival of EHEC O157:H7 during its passage from the stomach to the intestinal tract [46]. For example, the RstA/RstB system modulates the expression of acid resistance genes (hdeAB, asr, and gadEWX), thereby promoting bacterial survival under highly acidic gastric conditions [47]. Similarly, the BasS/BasR system promotes transcription of the arn operon to modify lipid A and confer resistance to human α-defensins, including HD-5 and HNP-1 [20]. In addition, the FusK/FusR system senses fucose and represses unnecessary virulence gene expression, optimizing energy expenditure and supporting bacterial survival in the intestinal lumen [18]. In this study, we identified the response regulator HprR of the HprS/HprR TCS as a novel factor contributing to EHEC O157:H7 pathogenesis. Specifically, HprR activates the expression of hiuH, which encodes a transthyretin-like protein, thereby enhancing bacterial resistance to reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), encountered in the colonic lumen. These findings uncover a previously unrecognized role for HprR in promoting bacterial survival under oxidative stress. HprR and HiuH are widespread in pathogenic E. coli [37,48–52]. We therefore speculate that the HprR-dependent activation of hiuH may represent a broader, conserved survival strategy among enteric E. coli strains. This possibility warrants further investigation to assess its prevalence and functional significance in other pathotypes.

RNA-seq analysis revealed that deletion of hprR also altered the expression of multiple genes involved in carbon and nitrogen metabolism, including the eut operon, which mediates ethanolamine utilization [53,54]. In addition, our transcriptomic data showed that the expression of several genes involved in DNA repair, protein secretion, and flagellar biosynthesis was significantly affected in the hprR mutant, indicating that HprR likely functions as a pleiotropic regulator. Further studies are needed to dissect the specific roles of HprR in these broader biological processes. HprS, the sensor kinase of the HprS/HprR two-component system, is likely to detect specific environmental cues within the intestinal milieu and activate HprR accordingly. Although the upstream activating signals of HprS were not delineated in this study, previous evidence indicates that HprS primarily senses reactive chlorine species [38,]. Future work will explore whether HprS in EHEC O157:H7 responds to chlorine-derived or other yet unidentified host signals.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nankai University (protocol no. IACUC 2,016,030,502) and were conducted in accordance with national regulations and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Neonatal rabbits and mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, in strict accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition) [55]. Animals were housed in individually ventilated cages (IVC) under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (24 ± 2°C, 50 ± 5% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals used. This study was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Animal health status was monitored daily throughout the experimental period, including assessments of body weight, general appearance, behavior, and activity level. Only data from healthy animals were included in the statistical analysis. No animals exhibited signs of severe discomfort during the experiments, and no data were excluded from the final analysis.

Bacterial strains, plasmids, primers, and growth conditions

A complete list of bacterial strains, plasmids, and oligonucleotides employed in this study is available in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6. Using the λ red recombinase system, mutant genetic strains were constructed by substituting the relevant genes with chloramphenicol or kanamycin resistance cassettes [56]. The plasmid used in the hprR complementary assay was generated through cloning the hprR coding sequence and its promoter region into a pACYC184 plasmid, and the resulting plasmid was electroporated into the ΔhprR strains. All strains were cultured in LB broth or on solid LB medium unless otherwise mentioned. When antibiotics were required, a following final concentration was added: chloramphenicol, 25 μg/mL; kanamycin, 50 μg/mL; ampicillin, 100 μg/mL; and nalidixic acid, 50 μg/mL, respectively.

Preparation of random transposon-mutant library

The EHEC O157:H7 transposon-mutant library was generated as described previously with minor modification [29]. In short, the pRL27-mini-Tn5 plasmid DNA, possessed a hyperactive Tn5 transposase for generating a large number of random transposon mutants, which was mixed with 100 μL EHEC O157:H7 electrocompetent cells, and transferred into 0.1 cm gap cuvette. The cuvette was inserted into the electroporation chamber and electroporated at 1.8 kV. 1 mL of SOC medium was immediately added to the cell suspension and the sample was incubated for recovery at 37 °C for 2 h. Finally, 200 μL bacteria suspension were plated onto the previously prepared LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16 ~ 20 h. Following the incubation, Colonies with mutants were harvested by scraping and pooling approximately 5 × 10^ [5] colonies. The mutant library was stored in 20% glycerol at −80°C.

Screening of the transposon-mutant by adhesion assay

The adhesion assay was performed in this study from a previous protocol [18]. As shown in Figure 1a, the transposon-mutant library prepared before was treated as the input sample and the bacteria mutants retrieved after the adhesion assay were treated as the output sample. Prior to infection, HeLa cells were washed three times with pre-warmed PBS, and the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM without antibiotics and FBS. Monolayers of HeLa cells were infected with 2 mL exponential phase mutant library per well suspended in DMEM and incubated for 3 hours in CO2 incubator. Then, the infected cells were washed six times with pre-warmed PBS to remove unattached bacteria. After lysing the cells in 0.05% Trition, the bacteria were collected by centrifugation. Two independent replicates were performed.

TraDIS sequencing and analysis

Mutant libraries and sequencing data analysis were performed following the transposon-directed insertion site sequencing (TraDIS) protocol described before [57]. Briefly, genomic DNA of Input and Output sample was extracted using NGS Fast DNA Library prep set for Illumina (CWBIO, CW2585M), and the DNA Purification was performed using the MagBead DNA Purification Kits (CWBIO, CW2508S). The primers used for PCR amplification of fragments containing transposons and customized sequencing are listed in Table S5. Sequences data were generated using HiSeq150 Illumina sequencing platform, generating approximately 2 million 150-bp paired-end reads per sample. TraDIS data were analyzed using the BioTraDIS pipeline [58] with the EHEC O157:H7 strain EDL933 reference genome NCBI Reference genome: GenBank accession GCF_000006665.1) as the mapping reference.

Identification of adhesin-related genes and integration with TraDIS data

The complete genome sequence of EHEC O157:H7 strain EDL933 (GenBank accession GCF_000006665.1) was used as the reference genome. Protein-coding sequences were extracted from the annotated genome for virulence factor annotation. Adhesin-related genes were identified by homology searches against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) core dataset, which contains experimentally validated bacterial virulence factors. Protein sequence similarity searches were performed using BLASTp implemented in the BLAST+ suite (v2.15.0). To ensure high-confidence annotations, only BLASTp hits meeting the following criteria were retained: (i) amino acid sequence identity ≥ 70% and (ii) alignment coverage ≥ 70%, defined as the proportion of the VFDB reference protein sequence covered by the BLAST alignment. Based on VFDB functional classifications, virulence factors annotated as adhesins or adhesion-associated proteins were selected. For proteins with multiple VFDB hits, the annotation with the highest sequence identity and coverage was retained as the best match.

The resulting set of adhesin-related genes identified in the EDL933 genome was then cross-referenced with the transposon sequencing dataset. For each adhesin gene, transposon insertion indices and associated statistical parameters were extracted from the TraDIS analysis to evaluate their contribution to bacterial fitness during host-cell adhesion.

RNA isolation and RNA-sequencing

Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted 1:100 into fresh LB medium and grown at 37°C with shaking until they reached an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of approximately 0.6. Cells were then harvested for RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15,596,026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and further purified with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74,104). RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA integrity was assessed by formaldehyde-denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis.

RNA library construction, high-throughput sequencing, and primary data processing were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Clean reads were aligned to the EDL933 reference genome (GenBank accession GCF_000006665.1), and gene-level read counts were generated using HTSeq (v0.6.1). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted with edgeR (v4.4.2) based on raw count data. Genes exhibiting an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 (|log2FC| >1) and FDR < 0.05 were defined as significantly differentially expressed.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‑qPCR)

Total RNA was processed for downstream analysis as previously described. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, 18,080–051). RT-qPCR was carried out on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4,367,659). An expression fold change of target gene was calculated using a 2−ΔΔCt method [59]. All data were normalized to EHEC O157:H7 housekeeping gene dnaK RNA levels [60]. At least three biological replicates were performed for each analysis.

Cell culture and adherence assay

The HeLa cells were grown in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37°C under 5% CO2 incubator. Bacterial adherence to the HeLa cells was evaluated as described previously with minor modifications. Briefly, HeLa cells were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to reach approximately 90% confluency. EHEC O157:H7 overnight cultures were diluted into fresh, antibiotic-free DMEM and grown to mid-logarithmic phase. Bacteria were added to the monolayer at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100:1, followed by a 3-hour co-incubation at 37°C in 5% CO2. Non-adherent bacteria were removed by washing the wells five times with pre-warmed PBS. HeLa cells were then lysed using 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, X100-5 mL), and the resulting lysates were serially diluted and plated on LB agar to quantify adhered bacteria. All experiments were conducted with a minimum of three biological replicates.

Fluorescent actin staining (FAS)

HeLa cells were seeded onto glass coverslips placed in 6-well culture plates and infected with bacteria as described. After 3 hours of incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 [18]. To visualize cellular structures, filamentous actin was stained with FITC-conjugated phalloidin (Sigma, P5282), and nuclei were counterstained with propidium iodide (PI; Beyotime, ST511). Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides and examined under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880). Quantification of infected cells and enumeration of actin-rich pedestals per infected cell were performed.

Mouse experiments

A total of 68 six-week-old female BALB/c mice were used in this study, including cohorts for intrarectal infection, fecal shedding, and colonization experiments. All mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Mice were fasted and deprived of water for 22 hours prior to infection. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups based on body weight to ensure comparable baseline conditions. Mice were infected via oral gavage with approximately 10^ [9] CFU of each bacterial strain. For the intrarectal infection experiment, mice were anesthetized with Avertin (2,2,2-tribromoethanol, 0.04 g/mL, intraperitoneal injection) and euthanized by cervical dislocation at 6 hours post infection. The distal colon was excised, washed, and homogenized in ice-cold PBS. Homogenates were serially diluted and plated on LB agar plates to determine bacterial loads after overnight incubation at 37°C. For the colonization assay in the intestinal tract, each group consisted of 8 mice. At designated time points post infection, mice were euthanized and their colons were harvested for bacterial enumeration as described above. For the fecal shedding experiment, each group consisted of 3 mice. Fecal samples were collected on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 8 post infection. To collect feces, mice from each group were placed individually in sterile, empty cages. Fecal pellets were homogenized in PBS, serially diluted, and plated on LB agar plates containing appropriate antibiotics to enumerate CFUs. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Bacterial competition experiments in infant rabbit

Infant rabbit infection experiments were performed following previously established procedures [60]. Three-day-old female New Zealand White (NZW) rabbits were obtained from Beijing Huabukang Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed in individually ventilated cages (IVC) under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, temperature of 24 ± 2°C, and relative humidity of 50 ± 5%. Two bacterial strains were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and administered orally to neonatal rabbits at a dose of 5 × 10^ [8] CFU per ~90 g body weight. After seven days, animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation in strict accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition). The entire colon was collected; colonic contents were gently expressed and weighed, and the tissue was rinsed and homogenized in PBS. Homogenates were serially diluted and plated on LB agar plates to determine colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of colon tissue. A total of 9 neonatal rabbits were used in this competition assay. No grouping or repetition was performed, as all animals received the same mixed inoculum.

Western blot assays

Overnight bacterial cultures in LB broth were subcultured into DMEM medium and grown until OD600 = 1.0. Bacterial cells were harvested, washed and treated with RIPA lysis buffer. In equal amounts, proteins were denatured and then separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transblotted onto a PVDF membrane at 4°C. The blots membrane was blocked for 35 minutes at room temperature in QuickBlocKTM Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, P0252) with gentle shaking. The blots membrane was probed with polyclonal antisera (mouse) against Tir (~80 kDa) or Intimin (~96 kDa), and with anti-DnaK antibody (1:10000 dilution; Abcam, ab69617) for 2 h and the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was added for another 1 h incubation at room temperature (1:5000 dilution for the goat anti-mouse-HRP; CWBIO; CW0102). The protein signals were visualized with the ECL enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (CWBio, CW0048M), and relative protein levels were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software V2.0.0. The molecular weight marker used was Precision Plus Protein™ Dual Color Standards (Bio-Rad, 26,616). Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Luminescence reporter assay

For luminescence assays, the ler promoter was cloned into the pMS402 plasmid, which harbors the lux reporter system. The lux fusion reporter plasmid PLEE1-pMS402 was transformed into EHEC O157:H7 strains. The reporter strains were cultured in DMEM medium until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, and were measured as counts per second (cps) of light production with multifunctional microplate reader. The ler promoter activity was acquired by CPS/OD600.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR)

CHIP assays were performed as previously described with minor adaptations as follows [61]. TheΔhprR strain harboring the hprR-3×FLAG-pTrc99a plasmid was cultured in LB medium until OD600 reached 0.6, then induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside for 30 minutes at 37°C. To crosslink proteins to DNA, bacterial treated with 1% formaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and cross-linking reaction was stopped by adding 0.5 M glycine. Bacterial pellets were washed twice with PBS and then resuspended lysis buffer (Tris-HCl 50 mM; pH7.5, NaCl 100 mM, EDTA 1 mM, lysozyme 20 mg/mL, RNase A 0.5 mg/ml, PMSF 1 mM) at 37 °C for 30 minutes, and 500 μl sonication buffer (Tris-HCl 100 mM; pH7.5, NaCl 200 mM, EDTA 1 mM, TritonX-100 2%) was further added. The cross-linked cells were sonicated on ice to generate DNA sizes of 200–500 bp. Insoluble cellular debris was removed by centrifugation and the supernatant was aliquoted into two Eppendorf tubes. One of them used as ChIP sample for the immunoprecipitation of protein – DNA complexes. ChIP sample were incubated with 50 μL anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich; F1804) and protein A magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0202) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. As a negative control, mock-ChIP sample was performed on the other aliquot without the addition of antibodies. The magnetic beads were washed three times and resuspended in 300 mL elution buffer (Tris-HCl 50 mM; pH8.0, EDTA 10 mM, SDS 1%). Eluted samples were then reverse cross-linked overnight by heating at 65 °C. The RNA contamination was removed via incubation with RNaseA (10 μg/mL). The DNA sample was subsequently purified with a PCR purification kit (Qiagen, 28,104). To calculate the enrichment folds of each target sequence in the ChIP sample relative to the mock-ChIP sample, qPCR was conducted, and the experiment procedure and data statistical were prepared as described in previous section.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The recombinant plasmid pET32a-hprR was introduced into E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells for protein expression. Transformed strains were cultured in LB medium at 37°C until OD600 reached 0.6, followed by induction with 0.1 mM IPTG. Cells were incubated at 16°C for 16–20 hours to promote soluble protein production. The HprR-His fusion protein was then purified from the supernatant of bacterial lysates using Ni2+-NTA affinity chromatography (Beyotime, P2235). Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) were performed with minor modifications to previously described methods [62]. Briefly, 30 ng of purified DNA fragment was incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes with increasing concentrations (0–40 nM) of recombinant HprR-His protein in a 20 μL binding reaction containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.8), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM CuCl2, 3 mM magnesium acetate, and 30 mM acetyl phosphate. Binding reactions were separated on a 6% native polyacrylamide gel in 0.5×TBE buffer. After electrophoresis, DNA bands were stained with GelRed (Biotium, 41,000) and visualized using a gel imaging system (Tanon, China).

Bacterial resistance to H2O2

Bacteria was cultured to mid-log phase in DMEM medium. Bacteria were pelleted, washed twice with PBS, and adjusted to a density of 10^ [9] CFU/mL in fresh DMEM medium. H2O2 solution (Sigma) was added to the culture to a final concentration of 6 mM and incubated for 0.5, 1, 2 h at 37°C. All samples were immediately collected at the indicated times and serially diluted in PBS followed by plating on LB plates. The plates subsequently were incubated overnight at 37°C and viable bacterial CFUs were counted.

Statistical analysis

All statistical evaluations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9. Unpaired Student’s t-tests were applied for comparisons between two groups. For colonization assays in mice and rabbits, the Mann – Whitney U test was employed to assess statistical significance. The number of biological replicates and animals used in each experiment is detailed in the respective figure legends. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
