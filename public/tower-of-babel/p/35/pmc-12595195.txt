Metformin’s multifaceted role in colorectal cancer: mechanisms of action and synergy with standard treatments
Authors: Haniye Rahimi Kolour, Nastaran Bahrami, Ehsan Nazemalhosseini-Mojarad, Amir Sadeghi, Mehdi Shamsara, Nayeralsadat Fatemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite advancements in treatment, colorectal cancer (CRC) prevalence, mortality rates, and therapeutic resistance persist as global health concerns. The review delves into metformin’s multifaceted effects on molecular pathways implicated in cancer progression and resistance, including apoptosis, autophagy, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters. A particular focus is placed on metformin’s promising role in targeting cancer stem cells (CSCs), known for their contribution to chemoresistance, metastasis, and relapse. The potential synergy of metformin with standard CRC treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, emerges as a pivotal theme. The review underscores the importance of elucidating metformin’s mechanisms of action and optimizing its utilization through further research, potentially leading to personalized, precision medicine approaches.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Therapeutic resistance, Metformin, Apoptosis, Autophagy, Epithelial–mesenchymal transition, ATP-binding cassette transporters, Cancer stem cells

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer and it is the second deadliest cancer globally. According to GLOBOCAN data, approximately 9.4% of all cancer-reported deaths were related to CRC, in 2020. However, due to the significant increase in cases diagnosed at older ages, it is estimated that the global incidence of CRC will increase more than double by 2035, especially in less developed countries [1].

CRC occurs exclusively in the colon or rectum and it is designated by the aberrant proliferation of glandular epithelial cells in the intestine. There are different types of CRC, including sporadic, hereditary, and colitis-associated. Environmental and genetic factors determine the risk of CRC. Diet, lifestyle, family history, and chronic inflammation are other significant risk factors [2].

CRC develops following genetic or epigenetic changes in intestinal epithelial cells [3]. These aggressively proliferating cells form a benign adenoma that can progress to cancer and ultimately metastasis through several distinct pathways, including microsatellite instability (MSI), chromosomal instability (CIN), and serrated neoplasia [4]. When an adenocarcinoma becomes aggressive, it can spread to other body parts through the blood and lymphatic vessels [5].

Different treatments are available for CRC, including chemotherapy, surgery, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. Surgery and chemotherapy are the two main treatments for this cancer. Surgery is considered a standard treatment option for stages 0 to II of CRC. Stage III requires surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, and stage IV requires a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy [6]. Currently, there are relatively limited treatment options with varying toxicity and side effects for CRC [7].

The critical thing about CRC is that 25–30% of CRC cases eventually metastasize, and there is no effective treatment at this stage. For this reason, adjuvant treatments are often used during or after surgery to kill any remaining cancer cells, including cancer stem cells. Therefore, in order to prevent tumor recurrence and metastasis, targeting the signaling pathways of stemness is seen as a logical approach [8].

In addition, patients in the metastatic stage often show multiple genetically heterogenic tumors, which might be resistance to chemotherapy. With the emergence of such resistance tumors, patients respond to higher doses of chemotherapy drugs, which increases the side effects of treatments and reduces the quality of life of patients. Chemotherapy resistance in CRC is conducted by the disruption of signaling pathways, such as Wnt, PI3K/Akt, Hedgehog, ErbB, RHOA, Notch, BMP, Hippo, AMPK, NF-kB, MAPK, and JNK [9].

One of the ways to deal with these challenges is to design new drugs, but designing new drugs is time-consuming, expensive, and laborious. Moreover, the percentage of defeat in this path is high. Alternatively, drug repurposing, which is the use of a drug for a different indication than the original indication, can be applied as an effective and safe strategy. Using drug repurposing, also, it is possible to control the high side effects of chemotherapy drugs and reduce the resistance to these drugs [10]. Many drugs, such as Metformin [11], Celecoxib [12], Aspirin [13], Diclofenac [14], Cimetidine [15], Disulfiram [16], Doxycycline [17], Niclosamide [18], Chloroquine [19] and Artesunate [20] have been used for this purpose. In this review article, we aim to comprehensively assess the significance of metformin as an adjuvant in CRC chemotherapy. We will deal with its impact on various signaling pathways that contribute to chemotherapy resistance.

Metformin anticancer effects

Metformin is the first line of treatment for type 2 diabetes due to its ability to inhibit hepatic gluconeogenesis and stimulate glucose uptake in skeletal muscles. This drug has controllable toxicity, and its pharmacokinetics is well known [21].

Epidemiological evidence shows that exposure to metformin may reduce cancer incidence and improve cancer patient’s prognosis [22]. Also, clinical studies show that metformin may have anticancer properties in a range of malignancies and effects on preventing tumor formation [23–25].

In 2004, the first report on the association between metformin administration and CRC risk was published [26]. After that, it has been found that there is an inverse relationship between the long-term use of metformin and the risk of CRC. Therefore, in addition to the effect of reducing glucose levels of plasma, metformin has significant anticancer potentials [23]. Moreover, metformin consumption in CRC patients with diabetes is associated with a significant reduction in mortality, indicating the possibility of metformin auxiliary role [27–29]. Metformin can also suppress the resistance of cancer stem cells to conventional chemotherapy which cause cancer recurrence and metastasis. These evidences support the use of metformin as an adjuvant agent in CRC [30].

Metformin at cellular level conducts its anticancer effects by inducing apoptosis, autophagy [31], cell cycle arrest [32], deviation of energy metabolism, suppression of epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) [33], senescence, immune system activation [34], inactivation of stem cells, and other biological outputs. Metformin can make cancer cells sensitive to the effects of conventional chemotherapy agents and other treatment strategies [35].

At molecular level, metformin activates AMPK and inhibits cell proliferation and protein synthesis by inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway. Activating this pathway can result in the inhibition of aerobic glycolysis [36]. Furthermore, metformin causes cell death by activating caspase-dependent and caspase-independent cell apoptosis [37].

The decrease in ATP production by metformin prevents the growth of cancer cells, and it seems to cause the loss of TP53. For this reason, cancer cells lose their ability to respond to metabolic changes and no longer have the ability to grow in unfavorable conditions [38].

Smad2 and Smad3 are activated by TGFβ and then combined with Smad4 to form the Smad complex. After entrance into the nucleus, this complex induces the expression of genes involved in the EMT signaling pathway. However, metformin by inhibiting the expression of TGFβ1 prevents the induction of the EMT pathway and metastasis [39]. Moreover, TGFβ can activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, while metformin inversely inhibits the mTOR pathway [40]. Also, by binding to TGFβ1, metformin prevents heterodimer formation and downstream signaling pathways [41]. By reducing the expression of β-catenin in colon cancer cell lines and chemotherapy-resistant xenograft models, metformin suppresses tumor migration, proliferation, and growth [42].

Cancer stem cells

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a subpopulation of tumor cells characterized by their capacity for self-renewing and the ability to differentiate into various cell types, which are key features in sustaining tumor growth and progression [43]. In CRC, CSCs have been identified as major contributors to tumor initiation, metastasis, and notably, resistance to conventional chemotherapy, often leading to tumor recurrence [44]. These cells generate heterogeneous populations within the tumor, which adds complexity to treatment and poses a significant challenge to achieving long-term remission [45]. CSCs in CRC express specific surface markers such as CD44, CD133, Lgr5, CD166, ALDH1, and EPCAM [46], which can serve for both CSC identification and isolation and potential therapeutic targets [47].

CSCs are also known to activate multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, including Notch [48], Hedgehog [49], Wnt-βcatenin [50], PI3K/Akt [51], TGF-β [52], and mTOR [53], all of which contribute to their survival, self-renewal, and resistance phenotypes. Based on accumulating evidence, metformin has garnered considerable attention as a therapeutic agent for targeting CSCs in CRC [54, 55]. For instance, metformin activates AMPK, a central energy sensor that negatively regulates the mTOR pathway, which is critical for CSC proliferation and maintenance [56]. Through AMPK activation and subsequent mTOR inhibition, metformin disrupts cellular metabolism, inhibits self-renewal, and reduces the stemness of CSCs.

In addition to its impact on signaling pathways, metformin decreases the CSC population in CRC by downregulating key stemness markers such as OCT4 and CD44 [57, 58]. It also promotes apoptosis in CSCs, thereby enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents. Furthermore, metformin selectively impairs mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, a metabolic vulnerability in CSCs, making them more sensitive to chemotherapy-induced stress [59].

By selectively targeting CSCs, metformin may also contribute to a reduction in tumor recurrence and metastasis, which are primarily driven by this subpopulation of cells. As CSCs are known to survive after conventional therapies and initiate new tumor growth at distant sites, eliminating them may improve long-term treatment outcomes and reduce the likelihood of relapse in CRC patients [60].

Moreover, growing evidence supports the potential of combination therapies involving metformin to enhance treatment efficacy against CRC. Combined with conventional chemotherapeutic agents, metformin may enhance the sensitivity of CSCs to cytotoxic drugs, thereby overcoming drug resistance and reducing tumor relapse. Similarly, combining metformin with immunotherapy has shown promise in modulating the tumor microenvironment and enhancing immune-mediated tumor clearance. These combination strategies aim to simultaneously target both CSCs and non-stem cancer cells, thereby achieving more comprehensive and durable treatment outcomes [61–63].

Despite encouraging preclinical evidence, the clinical application of metformin as a CSC-targeted therapy in CRC remains to be fully established. Future research should focus on well-designed clinical trials to evaluate the impact of metformin on CSC-specific markers, treatment response, recurrence rates, and long-term patient outcomes. Understanding the pharmacodynamics and optimal dosing of metformin in the context of CSC eradication is also essential. These efforts will help to translate promising laboratory findings into effective clinical strategies for improving CRC management [64, 65].

ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters

ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters constitute a group of membrane transporters that harness energy to transfer substrates across the membrane [66]. These transporters are integral to maintaining normal cellular function and tissue protection [67]. Also, they role encompasses essential functions such as eliminating toxic substances from the cell, facilitating nutrient absorption, and transporting ions P-glycoprotein (P-gp), also known as MDR1 or ABCB1, is of particular interest to researchers and is the most widely studied ABC transporter [68].

The upregulation of ABC transporters in many types of cancer, including CRC, confers resistance to chemotherapy by limiting drug absorption or enhancing drug efflux [66, 68]. These transporters mediate the pumping out of drugs, such as anthracyclines, alkylating agents, vinca alkaloids, and others, from the cell [67]. Moreover, ABC transporters in cancer cells facilitate the translocation of tumorigenesis signaling molecules [66].

The high expression of ABC transporters has been identified in CSCs by staining the cells with Hoechst 33,342 dye and Rhodamine 123 dye using flow cytometry. Because of removal of the dye from the cells due to the high expression of ABC transporters, the CSCs are observed as an unstained population in flow cytometry [69]. ATP binding cassette (ABC) transporters are highly expressed in CSCs. Using ATP, these pumps help to transfer chemotherapy drugs out of CSCs and cause multi-drug resistance in these cells. This event preserves cell viability and stemness [70].

Although the role of ABC transporters in resistance to chemotherapy has been established, there has been no proof of the effect of metformin on the expression of their markers. The effect of metformin on this pathway has been investigated in many cancers such as breast, pancreas, prostate, and CRC [71–73]. Gene silencing of ABCC3 and ABCC1 has been associated with increased cell sensitivity to anticancer drugs and reduced stemness of cancer cells in breast cancer [74]. Zhang et al. synthesized a polymer of metformin named polymeth, polyethylene glycol conjugated with dicyandiamide, and investigated its effect on cells. Their results demonstrate the potency of polymeth in preventing MDR by inhibiting drug removal by ABC transporters [75]. Cancer cells use oxidative phosphorylation chain to generate high-energy phosphate bonds in the form of ATP. But metformin can disturb cellular pathway for ATP production by targeting mitochondria. Since the ABC transporters need ATP to work, metformin can control the activity of ABC transporters and increases the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemotherapy by limiting the drug efflux from the cancer cell [75].

On the other hand, by inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation pathway and decreasing ATP level, the tumor cells force to compensate ATP deficiency via glycolysis. However, metformin consumption decreases the glucose level of serum through the enhancement of glucose absorption by the muscle cells. In this condition, the access of tumor cells to the glucose is limited and the glycolysis pathway is inhibited in consequence [76]. The mentioned events cause CSCs to weaken and increase their sensitivity to traditional cancer treatments, including chemotherapy [77].

Considering that so far, no study has been conducted on the effect of metformin on ABC transporters in CRC chemoresistance, further research in this field can have effective results.

Epithelial–mesenchymal transition

EMT is a dynamic biological process through which epithelial cells lose their characteristic features, such as cell-cell adhesion, apical-basal polarity, and tight junctions, and acquire mesenchymal traits, including enhanced motility, invasion, resistance to apoptosis, and extracellular matrix remodeling. While EMT plays an essential role in physiological processes such as embryonic development and tissue regeneration, its aberrant activation in malignancies contributes to tumor progression, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [78, 79].

EMT is classified into three types based on their distinct roles in normal development and pathological conditions. Type 1 is associated with embryogenesis and organ development, Type 2 is seen during wound healing and fibrosis, and Type 3 is associated with tumorigenesis and metastasis in various solid cancers like CRC [80].

At the molecular level, EMT is characterized by decreased expression of epithelial markers such as E-cadherin, and increased expression of mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin, vimentin, and fibronectin [71, 81].

In CRC, several signaling pathways including Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β pathways play pivotal roles in EMT activation. Additionally, transcription factors such as Snail, Slug, Zeb1, Zeb2, and Twist contribute to EMT by repressing E-cadherin expression and promoting mesenchymal genes [71]. Moreover, EMT can regulate the expression of drug transporters, thereby modulating drug uptake and efflux, and contributing to multidrug resistance. Pathways such as NF-κB, Notch, Wnt, and Hedgehog are also implicated in regulation of drug resistance [71]. The dysregulation of these pathways resulting the change of drug transporter expression contribute to drug resistance in various cancers, highlighting the importance of targeting EMT pathways in cancer therapy.

Several studies have investigated the potential of metformin in modulating EMT and CRC. Wang et al. reported that metformin suppresses EMT by negatively regulating the SNAIL/miR-34 and ZEB/miR-200 axes, thereby increasing the proportion of hybrid epithelial/mesenchymal (E/M) phenotype cells [82]. Another study discovered that metformin through the deactivation of the Wnt3a/β-catenin pathway attenuates the EMT process in CRC cells resulted in reducing vimentin and increasing the epithelial marker such as CD44, Nanog, and ALDH [83, 84]. One more study conducted by Kang et al. demonstrated that IL-6-induced EMT in colon cancer cells could be reversed by metformin, highlighting its ability to counteract inflammatory-driven EMT [85]. Moreover, metformin has been shown to inhibit EMT and regulate chemoresistance in rectal cancer cells. This is achieved by inhibiting the expression of the TGFβ type 2 receptor [86].

Taken together, these findings suggest that metformin may serve as an effective EMT modulator in CRC, offering promising therapeutic benefits. In addition to suppressing EMT through regulation of transcription factors and signaling axes, metformin is known to activate AMPK, thereby inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway, a key pathway promoting EMT and cancer progression [87]. It also targets CSC-like traits, which are often enhanced during EMT, thus reducing tumor invasiveness. By another mechanism, metformin inhibits EMT through modulating the tumor microenvironment leading to declining inflammation and altering cellular metabolism [88]. Importantly, EMT is strongly associated with resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapies in CRC by promoting drug efflux, inhibiting apoptosis, and enhancing DNA repair mechanisms. By reversing EMT, metformin may restore drug sensitivity and improve therapeutic outcomes, making it a promising adjunct to conventional treatment regimens in CRC [88]. These multiple mechanisms highlight metformin’s potential as a multifunction therapeutic agent, although further clinical studies are needed to confirm its efficacy and safety in CRC management. Given the promising preclinical evidence, future clinical studies are essential to evaluate the impact of metformin on EMT markers and correlate these changes with patient outcomes in CRC. Such investigations will help to clarify the therapeutic potential of metformin as an adjunct treatment and to optimize clinical protocols targeting EMT-associated drug resistance.

Autophagy

Autophagy is a cellular process that breaks down and recycles cellular components to maintain a balanced cellular environment [89]. Its role in cancer, particularly CRC, is complex, as it can either promote cell survival or induce cell death. One-way autophagy is involved in CRC is through its impact on chemoresistance and chemosensitivity [89]. One proposed mechanism suggests that autophagy can be triggered by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, which regulates cell survival and proliferation [89]. Metformin can reduce the activity of this pathway, leading to autophagy and subsequent cell death [89].

Metformin may also affect the expression of specific microRNAs, such as mir-26a, which can modulate autophagy and apoptosis in CRC cells [90, 91]. Additionally, combining metformin with other drugs like doxorubicin and sodium oxamate in a triple therapy approach has synergistic effects in reducing tumor growth and promoting apoptosis and autophagy in CRC cells [89]. Another study exploring the combined effect of metformin and imatinib revealed that metformin alone induced autophagy and its activation enhanced when used with another drug. This study also examined the impact of inhibiting autophagy on CRC treatment and found that inhibiting autophagy increases the effectiveness of anticancer drugs and reverses drug resistance in CRC cells [92]. A separate study conducted by Mogavero et al. in 2017 showed that metformin treatment in HCT116 cells increased levels of both isoforms of LC3B, which serve as markers for autophagy activation. This suggests the potential induction of autophagy in this particular cell line. These findings highlight the potential of targeting autophagy, particularly in combination with metformin, as a promising approach for improving CRC treatment outcomes.

Apoptosis

Apoptosis plays a crucial role in CRC resistance. Dysregulation of apoptosis pathways can contribute to the CRC tumor growth and resistance to therapy. Aberrant expression or mutations in key apoptotic regulators, such as p53, Bcl-2 family proteins, and caspases can lead to disrupted apoptosis signaling and cell death evasion. Additionally, dysregulation of apoptotic pathways can impact response to chemotherapy and targeted therapy by promoting cell survival [93]. In a study conducted in 2022, it was discovered that resistance to butyrate in CRC cells leads to an increase in phospho-Akt, followed by a decrease in phospho-AMPK and phospho-ACC. This inhibits fatty acid synthesis and promotes resistance to apoptosis. However, treatment with metformin defeats this resistance as metformin increases caspases 3/7, induces cell apoptosis, and activates the AMPK and Akt pathways while inhibits ACC and mTOR pathways [94]. Another study by Li and colleagues focused on non-vascular CRCs resistant to chemotherapy. Surprisingly, metformin increased microvessel density by inhibiting endothelial cell apoptosis. Contrary to expectations, this drug restored the vascular structure of this CRC type, facilitating the entry of more drugs and immune cells into the tumor [37]. A study examining the effects of metformin on CRC cell lines revealed the selective impact of metformin on healthy and cancer cells. Although metformin has no impact on healthy cells, it affects the viability of CRC cell lines depending on the drug concentration [95].

Examining metformin effect on xenograft models indicated its potential as an antimetastatic drug, inhibiting the spread of CRC to other organs. By increasing the expression of miR-676-3p, metformin activates pro-apoptotic proteins and cell cycle regulation, ultimately leading to increased apoptosis and cell cycle arrest [96]. Additionally, metformin has shown potential in reducing ATP in doxorubicin-resistant cells and inhibiting tumor growth. It achieves this by decreasing oxygen consumption, activating the AMPK pathway, and inducing apoptosis. When combined with doxorubicin in triple therapy, metformin stimulates apoptosis by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT pathway [89].

The multifaceted effects of metformin on apoptosis and resistance in CRC highlight its therapeutic potential in addressing these crucial aspects of CRC treatment.

Metformin combined with standard treatments on CRC

Traditional cancer treatments such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy are limited in their effectiveness due to their non-specific nature and the occurrence of secondary side effects. Prolonged exposure to chemotherapy drugs can lead to toxicity and the development of drug resistance. To address these challenges, combination therapy has been proposed as a strategy to improve the efficacy of chemotherapy while minimizing side effects [89, 97]. Recent research has shown a growing interest in combining metformin with standard chemotherapy drugs in treating CRC (Table 1). Metformin is also being studied for its potential effectiveness against CRC in clinical trials (Table 2). The objective is to minimize toxicity and improve the overall therapeutic efficacy. Signaling pathways through which the combination of metformin and other chemotherapy drugs acts in colorectal cancer

Metformin and 5-fluorouracil

Despite significant tumor shrinkage after 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) treatment, some tumor cells survive treatment and cause disease recurrence. These cells, which are resistant to the 5-FU, show increased expression of pyruvate kinase M1 (PKM1) and suppression of PKM2, which is associated with suppression of the pentose phosphate pathway, decreased NADPH levels, and antioxidant defenses, leading to PKM2 oxidation and stemness acquisition [98]. In a study on the functional and metabolic changes of colon cancer in vivo and in vitro, using a combination of 5-FU and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) inhibitors such as metformin, rotenone, antimycin A, etc., the findings demonstrated that the survival rate of resistant cells significantly reduces [98]. In another study, similar results obtained with an in vivo situation and metformin along with 5-FU brings more effectiveness. The data of this study showed that after 40 days of treatment, the combination of metformin and oxaliplatin possessed a more pronounced effect on inhibiting tumor growth and weight than using these drugs alone [99]. Another study showed that metformin plays an inhibitory role in the proliferation and metastasis of CRC and combined metformin with 5-FU has a synergistic effect in both in vitro and in vivo [100]. Genome-wide transcriptional profile analysis of 5-FU-resistant cells showed that the Wnt signaling pathway is one of the pathways involved in metastasis and resistance to chemotherapy in CRC. To inhibit resistance to chemotherapy, the researchers used metformin together with ICG-001, an inhibitor of the Wnt signaling pathway. Unexpectedly, they observed that CRC cells resistant to 5-FU have overexpression of CSC markers and formed clones. However, when ICG-001 was used together with metformin, the expression of CSC markers decreased, while autophagy and apoptosis increased synergistically. Therefore, this combination enhances the cytotoxic potential of the chemotherapy drug and effectively eliminated resistance to chemotherapy in these cells [101]. To understand the efficacy of metformin combined with 5-FU, a phase 2 clinical trial was conducted on advanced and chemotherapy-resistant metastatic CRC (mCRC) patients. The patients were observed for one year, and their tumor size was measured every eight weeks. Finally, moderate activity was observed in 5-FU-resistant mCRC. Additionally, a subgroup of obese patients and those with a longer duration of 5-FU achieved disease stabilization and prolonged survival [102]. Also, from 2010 to 2012, 187 patients with type 2 diabetes were used to investigate the simultaneous effect of these two drugs on CRC. Metformin treatment caused an increase in CD133 and β-catenin protein expression in type 2 diabetes patients. Also, this drug had a negative impact on metastasis and poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma [103].

Metformin and Irinotecan

Irinotecan (CPT-11) is an essential chemotherapeutic agent used for CRC treatment. It acts through the inhibition of topoisomerase I, an enzyme critical for DNA unwinding during replication [104]. The drug binds covalently to the enzyme’s catalytic complex, preventing the re-ligation of single-strand breaks during DNA replication [105]. This results in the accumulation of DNA damage, ultimately leading to apoptosis of cancer cells. Metformin might enhance the toxic effects of DNA-damaging agents like irinotecan on cancer cells, since it can result apoptosis following activation of AMPK and thereby inhibition of mTOR pathway [106].

Alternatively, metformin by modulating the expression of transporters such as ABC family proteins and enzymes like carboxylesterases and UGTs (uridine diphosphate-glucuronosyltransferases), can enhance the intracellular accumulation of active Irinotecan metabolites or reduce their detoxification [107, 108]. Metformin has been shown to promote transient cell cycle arrest, particularly at the G0/G1 phase, through AMPK activation and downregulation of key regulators such as cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) [109]. Rather than causing permanent arrest, this modulation can synchronize cancer cells in early phases of the cell cycle. Upon release from this arrest or in fluctuating tumor microenvironments, a larger fraction of the synchronized cells may simultaneously enter the S-phase, during which they become more susceptible to DNA-damaging agents like Irinotecan [110].This synchronization effect, along with metabolic stress induced by Metformin, may sensitize tumor cells to Irinotecan-induced cytotoxicity and enhance its overall efficacy.

Combining metformin with irinotecan also offers a promising approach to overcoming resistance mechanisms in CRC [111, 112]. Resistance often develops through alterations in signaling pathways and metabolic adaptations such as increased efflux transporter expression or mutations in topoisomerase I [113]. The combination of these drugs may help circumvent such resistance by downregulating pathways associated with tumor growth, such as PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling, and by modulating tumor metabolism to favor apoptosis. Moreover, this strategy may enhance drug retention and efficacy within cancer cells.

Recent preclinical studies support this therapeutic approach. For instance, Khader et al. demonstrated that adding metformin to CRC cells increased their sensitivity to the cytotoxic effects of Irinotecan. Metformin and irinotecan were shown to reduce cell viability and inhibit the growth of irinotecan-resistant cancer cells [110]. In a clinical trial conducted in 2021, the simultaneous administration of irinotecan and metformin was investigated for the first time in patients with CRC who were resistant to standard treatments. Forty-eight patients participated in this clinical trial, and in the end, forty-one patients remained. The primary objective was to achieve disease control within 12 weeks, while secondary objectives included evaluating the toxicity of the drugs and assessing the patient’s quality of life. The trial findings indicated that combining these two drugs proved to be an effective treatment regimen for patients with treatment-resistant CRC, demonstrating a favorable safety profile [111].

Metformin and oxaliplatin

Oxaliplatin is a chemotherapy drug containing platinum that is commonly used for various types of cancers, including CRC. In vitro studies have demonstrated that metformin has a synergistic effect when combined with oxaliplatin [114]. Research conducted by Song et al. has shown that a liposomal nanomedicine formulation combining oxaliplatin and metformin can effectively suppress tumor growth in CRC. Metformin can regulate cellular oxygen consumption and alleviate tumor hypoxia, while oxaliplatin can induce immunogenic cell death [115]. Patients with diabetes have a higher risk of developing CRC, and long-term insulin treatment can lead to chemotherapy resistance, particularly oxaliplatin. However, metformin has been found to reverse the chemoresistance induced by insulin in CRC cells. It achieves this by inhibiting the expression of Bcl-2through the activation of the AMPK pathway, ultimately promoting mitochondrial apoptosis [116]. High mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1) is a molecule involved in cell death and survival regulation and is associated with resistance to cytotoxic drugs. Huang et al. demonstrated that treatment with oxaliplatin could increase the expression of HMGB1 in human CRC DLD-1 cells. However, metformin enhanced the cytotoxicity of oxaliplatin while inhibited HMGB1. The combined treatment of metformin and oxaliplatin has been shown to reduce cell proliferation and decrease HMGB1 expression [117]. Although the combination of metformin and oxaliplatin shows promising potential in overcoming resistance in CRC treatment, further research and clinical trials are warranted to fully understand the mechanisms involved and optimize the therapeutic benefits of this combination in CRC patients.

Metformin and doxorubicin

Doxorubicin (anthracycline antitumor antibiotic) is a DNA replication inhibiting chemotherapy agent drug used for treating various cancers. It works by blocking the topoisomerase II enzyme, thereby impeding the growth of cancer cells. However, doxorubicin has limited effectiveness when uses alone [97]. In 2016, González et al. conducted a triple therapeutic approach using metformin, doxorubicin, and sodium Oxamate. Their findings demonstrated that the combination of these drugs could reduce tumor size and quantity in mice, induce autophagy and apoptotic cell death in CRC cells, and influence the mTOR and glycolysis pathways. Moreover, the triple therapy was shown to decrease the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in endothelial cells, including INFγ, TNFα, IL-1β, and IL-6 [97]. In 2021, Hernández et al. further investigated the triple therapy of metformin, doxorubicin, and sodium oxamate and its effects on CRC-derived cells compared to doxorubicin alone. Their study revealed that the triple therapy enhanced apoptosis and autophagy while reducing cell proliferation more effectively than doxorubicin. The major challenge in cancer treatment lies in chemoresistance, which occurs when cancer cells adapt through factors like hypoxia, increased glycolysis, and dysregulated energy metabolism. However, the triple treatment approach reversed chemoresistance by decreasing lactate production through oxamate and reducing ATP production through metformin. Doxorubicin targets DNA replication, while oxamate and metformin address abnormal metabolic processes, leading to apoptosis and autophagy in cancer cells. Additionally, the triple therapy regulated the expression of mir-26a, an oncomir often overexpressed in CRC [89]. Overall, the triple therapy approach holds potential as an effective strategy for combating cancer and overcoming chemoresistance in CRC.

Metformin and cisplatin

Cisplatin is a highly effective platinum-based chemotherapy drug widely utilized for treating various solid cancers [118, 119]. Its anticancer activity primarily involves forming DNA adducts, which interfere with DNA replication and transcription, ultimately leading to apoptosis [120, 121]. Cisplatin also induces cell cycle arrest, predominantly at G2/M and S phases [122]. Despite its efficacy, the development of resistance to cisplatin remains a significant challenge in CRC management [123]. Resistance mechanisms include enhanced DNA repair, drug efflux, and evasion of apoptosis.

To overcome cisplatin resistance, combination therapies have been explored. Recent studies investigate the synergistic effects of combining cisplatin with metabolic modulators such as metformin. In vitro studies using CRC cell lines (SW480 and SW620) have demonstrated that co-treatment with metformin and cisplatin significantly inhibits cell viability, reduces colony formation, and induces apoptosis more effectively than either agent alone [123–125]. These effects are partly mediated through the activation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent pathways, notably the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, which enhances cancer cell sensitivity to cisplatin [124].

Emerging evidence suggests that metformin can enhance the cytotoxic effects of cisplatin through several complementary mechanisms. One key pathway involves the inhibition of DNA repair processes. Metformin has been shown to downregulate critical DNA repair proteins, including BRCA1 and BRCA2, thereby impairing homologous recombination and increasing the susceptibility of cancer cells to cisplatin-induced DNA damage [126, 127]. Additionally, metformin appears to reduce the population of CSCs, which are often associated with therapy resistance and tumor relapse. This effect is mediated in part through suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, leading to diminished stemness and improved treatment response when combined with cisplatin [128, 129]. Furthermore, the combination of metformin and cisplatin has been found to elevate ROS levels, which promotes apoptosis in CRC cells. This pro-apoptotic effect is associated with activation of ROS-mediated PI3K/Akt signaling, further sensitizing tumor cells to cisplatin’s cytotoxicity [130].

An additional benefit of combining metformin with cisplatin is its potential to mitigate some of the adverse side effects associated with cisplatin, particularly nephrotoxicity [131, 132]. Cisplatin-induced kidney damage is a common limiting factor in its clinical use [133]. Emerging evidence suggests that metformin may have nephroprotective properties, possibly through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, which could reduce oxidative stress and renal cell injury caused by cisplatin [132, 134, 135]. This interaction might make the combination more tolerable for patients, facilitating higher or more sustained dosing without compromising renal function. Further research is needed to fully elucidate this protective role and its clinical implications, but current data indicate promising potential for metformin to improve the safety profile of cisplatin-based chemotherapy.

Metformin and radiotherapy

Radiotherapy plays a crucial role in treating CRC by using high-energy radiation to target and destroy cancer cells in the affected area, helping to control tumor growth and improve patient outcomes [136]. Metformin, when combined with radiotherapy, has shown promising potential in enhancing the sensitivity of CRC cells to radiation treatment, leading to improved therapeutic outcomes [35]. For instance, a study investigated the combined effects of ionizing radiation (IR) and metformin on CRC by examining the reduction of P53 protein levels and the repair of DNA damage caused by IR. The findings showed that metformin further decreased clonogenic survival and increased sensitivity to radiation. This effect was attributed to metformin inducing cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase and reducing the expression of DNA repair proteins. Interestingly, these effects were more pronounced in HCT116 p53-/- cells and xenograft models compared to p53 +/+ cell lines [35]. Numerous in vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated the comparable efficacy of combining metformin with radiotherapy to the concurrent use of radiotherapy and 5-FU in disrupting tumor growth [137, 138]. In a study using a zebrafish model of induced rectal cancer, the effects of metformin and radiotherapy were compared with those of 5-FU and radiotherapy. Patient-derived rectal cancer samples were utilized to induce cancer in these models. The results indicated that metformin exhibited promising effects as a sensitizer to radiotherapy, suggesting its potential as an alternative to the standard 5-FU protocol [137, 138]. Furthermore, metformin has been found to mitigate the toxicity associated with radiation therapy by optimizing mitophagy, which is dependent on AMPK. It enhances the sensitivity to radiotherapy, particularly in CRCs carrying the p53 mutation [139]. Overall, the synergistic effects of metformin and radiotherapy in CRC treatment highlight the possibility of utilizing metformin as an adjunct therapy to enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy and overcome resistance in CRC patients.

Metformin and immunotherapy

To date, limited studies have explored the potential synergistic effects of metformin combined with immunotherapy in CRC, and this remains an area requiring further extensive research. A clinical trial initiated in 2019 (NCT03800602) investigated the combination of metformin with nivolumab in patients with stage IV microsatellite stable (MSS) CRC. The findings indicated that although the combination was well tolerated and demonstrated some immunomodulatory activity in patient tissues, its clinical efficacy was limited in this particular population of metastatic MSS-CRC patients who are typically resistant to immunotherapy [140, 141].

Preclinical studies provide additional insights into the mechanisms by which metformin may enhance anti-tumor immunity. For instance, Scharping et al. (2017) showed that metformin can reduce tumor hypoxia and modulate the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing the efficacy of PD-1 blockade [142, 143]. Similarly, Eikawa et al. (2015) demonstrated that metformin promotes metabolic reprogramming of T cells via AMPK activation, improving their anti-tumor functions [144].

Despite these promising mechanistic findings, it is important to interpret current clinical data with caution due to the limited sample size and focus on a single CRC subgroup in the existing trial. The complex interactions between metformin, the immune system, and the tumor microenvironment, along with potential differential effects across various CRC subtypes, remain incompletely understood. Thus, further well-designed clinical studies involving larger and more diverse patient cohorts are essential to elucidate the potential of metformin to enhance immunotherapy efficacy in CRC and to define its role within combination treatment strategies.

Conclusion and future perspectives

This review highlights the multifaceted role of metformin in colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment, emphasizing its potential as an adjuvant agent capable of targeting several hallmarks of cancer progression and therapy resistance. Metformin exerts anti-tumor effects by modulating CSC populations, inhibiting ABC transporters, suppressing EMT, inducing autophagy and apoptosis, and enhancing the efficacy of conventional chemotherapeutic agents including 5-fluorouracil, irinotecan, oxaliplatin, doxorubicin, and cisplatin.

Preclinical studies consistently support metformin’s ability to overcome chemoresistance and improve therapeutic outcomes. Its synergistic potential has also been explored in combination with radiotherapy and, to a more limited extent, immunotherapy. In particular, recent preclinical findings suggest that metformin may modulate the tumor microenvironment and immune response, although clinical evidence—especially in microsatellite stable CRC—remains insufficient and inconclusive.

Despite its promising effects, the clinical translation of metformin as an adjuvant agent in CRC requires further investigation. Current clinical trials are limited in scale and scope, and many questions remain regarding its mechanisms of action in humans, optimal treatment combinations, and the specific patient populations that are most likely to benefit. Advancing this field will require comprehensive clinical studies that integrate mechanistic research, biomarker identification, and personalized treatment design.

As a widely available and low-cost drug with a well-established safety profile, metformin offers a compelling opportunity to improve CRC treatment strategies. However, its successful integration into standard care depends on robust clinical validation. Continued investigation into metformin’s role in modulating molecular pathways, reprogramming tumor metabolism, and enhancing therapeutic response will be essential for realizing its full potential in oncology.

In conclusion, metformin holds substantial promise as an adjunctive therapeutic agent in colorectal cancer. Through its diverse biological effects and potential to enhance conventional treatment modalities, it may contribute to the development of more effective, personalized strategies for CRC management—particularly in the face of advanced disease and therapeutic resistance.
