Air quality forecasting using a spatiotemporal hybrid deep learning model based on VMD–GAT–BiLSTM
Authors: Xiaohu Wang, Suo Zhang, Yi Chen, Longying He, Yongmei Ren, Zhen Zhang, Juan Li, Shiqing Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The precise forecasting of air quality is of great significance as an integral component of early warning systems. This remains a formidable challenge owing to the limited information of emission source and the considerable uncertainties inherent in dynamic processes. To improve the accuracy of air quality forecasting, this work proposes a new spatiotemporal hybrid deep learning model based on variational mode decomposition (VMD), graph attention networks (GAT) and bi-directional long short-term memory (BiLSTM), referred to as VMD–GAT–BiLSTM, for air quality forecasting. The proposed model initially employ a VMD to decompose original PM2.5 data into a series of relatively stable sub-sequences, thus reducing the influence of unknown factors on model prediction capabilities. For each sub-sequence, a GAT is then designed to explore deep spatial relationships among different monitoring stations. Next, a BiLSTM is utilized to learn the temporal features of each decomposed sub-sequence. Finally, the forecasting results of each decomposed sub-sequence are aggregated and summed as the final air quality prediction results. Experiment results on the collected Beijing air quality dataset show that the proposed model presents superior performance to other used methods on both short-term and long-term air quality forecasting tasks.

Introduction

In recent years, air pollution has emerged as one of the primary environmental concerns in urban areas. Prior study1 has indicated that air pollution can trigger respiratory issues in individuals, and those exposed to prolonged air pollution are at a higher risk of cardiovascular diseases. Additionally, it poses potential threats to the nervous and immune systems, and extended exposure to air pollution may reduce life expectancy and even lead to fatalities. According to the report2 of World Health Organization (WHO), air pollutants such as PM2.5 fall into the category of carcinogens, with over seven million deaths attributed to air pollution annually. Government investments in air pollution control have ranked as the foremost among three major pollution control actions, and these investments have exhibited a yearly increase. Consequently, the issue of air pollution remains a matter of concern. Air pollution also directly impacts the competitiveness of urban areas. Liu et al.3 analyzed the influence of air pollution on residents’ well-being and their choice of residential cities. The results indicate that air pollution significantly diminishes the well-being of residents in cities. As a result, accurate forecasting of air quality is very beneficial to present an early warning in air pollution control, government decision-making, as well as public health management.

At present, air quality forecasting methods can be categorized into two major classes: traditional forecasting methods and recent deep learning methods. The traditional forecasting methods encompass numerical modeling methods, statistical methods, and traditional machine learning methods, as described below.

Numerical modeling methods4–6 are an approach closely aligned with traditional weather forecasting methods. They are grounded in the disciplines of atmospheric physics and chemistry, utilizing computer-simulated data and a system of equation sets to mathematically model the variations in air pollution data, whether in the vertical or horizontal dimensions. Statistical methods7–9, on the other hand, leverage historical meteorological and air pollution data, assuming a degree of linearity and stationarity, to establish statistical models based on parameter estimation and curve fitting for predictive purposes. Traditional machine learning methods in air quality forecasting encompass Multiple Linear Regression (MLR)10, Support Vector Regression (SVR)11, Random Forest Regression (RFR)12, and so on. For instance, Castelli et al.13 employed SVR with radial basis function to predict air quality indices and PM2.5 levels in California. Lee et al.14 proposed a machine learning approach based on Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)15 to forecast PM2.5 concentration, incorporating additional industry-related features into the input. This approach exhibited improved predictive performance compared to traditional methods. However, due to the intricate and dynamic temporal patterns in air quality, these traditional machine learning techniques struggle to accurately extract features, and thus present limitations in capturing long-term pollution patterns, resulting in their highly limited performance on air quality forecasting tasks.

Deep learning methods16, as one of recently-developed machine learning techniques, are capable of automatically learning the underlying patterns within samples and capture high-level features from input data. So far, deep learning methods have exhibited promising performance in audio and text signal processing17, object detection and segmentation18, pattern recognition19–21, and so on. In this sense, several typical deep learning methods, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), serve as a more comprehensive and powerful approach suited for air quality forecasting22–27. Jin et al.22 introduced a novel deep learning framework based on Nested Long Short-Term Memory networks (NLSTM)28. Zhang et al.29 provided a CNN-LSTM multi-model for forecasting Beijing’s air quality index, outperforming Autoregressive Moving Average (ARMA), LSTM, and other models. Kow et al.24 fused multiple CNNs and Back-propagation Neural Networks (BPNN) for predicting PM2.5 levels across 74 sites in Taiwan for the next 72 h. Their proposed model achieved robust long-term forecasting capabilities within a relatively short computational complexity.

It is noted that the majority of air pollution data originates from different monitoring stations in close physical proximity. This indicates that air pollution data usually have both spatial and temporal characteristics. In this case, the above-mentioned methods consider using either spatial or temporal characteristics of air pollution data for air quality forecasting. This oversight leads to reducing their prediction stability and accuracy. Hence, it is necessary to comprehensively account for both temporal and spatial features of air pollution data when performing air quality forecasting tasks. Ge et al.30 presented a multi-scale spatiotemporal graph convolution network, consisting of a multi-scale block, several spatial–temporal blocks and a fusion block, for air quality forecasting. Shi et al.31 designed a multi-scale and multi-site modeling approach, called the multivariate empirical mode decomposition and spatial cosine-attention-based long short-term memory (MEMD–SCA–LSTM), to model the air pollution process.

To alleviate the above-mentioned issue, this work proposes a spatiotemporal hybrid deep learning model, called VMD–GAT–BiLSTM, for air quality forecasting. Specially, the proposed model integrates the advantages of variational mode decomposition (VMD), graph attention networks (GAT) and bi-directional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) for PM2.5 concentration forecasting. The VMD is employed to decompose original PM2.5 data into a series of relatively stable sub-sequences, mitigating the impact of unknown factors on the model’s predictive capability. Then, a GAT is constructed for each sub-sequence to capture deep spatial relationships among different monitoring sites. Subsequently, a BiLSTM is leveraged to capture the temporal characteristics of each decomposed sub-sequence. Finally, we aggregate the obtained prediction results of each decomposed sub-sequence with a simple summation as the final air quality forecasting results.

Materials

Study area

In order to assess the proposed model’s ability to capture spatiotemporal dependencies of air quality data, a total of 30 meteorological monitoring stations in Beijing, China were selected as the study objects (http://www.bjmemc.com.cn/). Accurate prediction results can be obtained by considering the spatiotemporal properties of air quality data collected from these monitoring stations. These prediction results can significantly benefit vulnerable populations and help formulate appropriate air pollution control policies.

Data collection

The collected data contains historical air quality data from 30 air quality monitoring stations in Beijing, recorded at an hourly resolution, spanning from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2020. The five specific air pollutants include particulate matter (PM2.5), inhalable particles (PM10), carbon monoxide (CO), ozone (O3), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2). Additionally, historical meteorological data and timestamp data are also included. The six meteorological factors are temperature, dew point temperature, air pressure, precipitation, eastward wind speed, and northward wind speed. Also, in the construction of graph feature maps, both distance data and similarity data are utilized.

Method

The overall structure of the proposed VMD–GAT–BiLSTM model is depicted in Fig. 1. As shown in Fig. 1, our model primarily consists of five key parts: an input layer, a decomposition layer, a graph construction layer, a spatiotemporal layer, and an output layer. In particular, the input layer aims to collect historical air quality data, historical meteorological data, and timestamp data, and then normalize them as the input of our model, denoted by X ∈ RT×S×F. Here, T represents the time dimension, S represents the number of monitoring sites, and F represents the feature dimension. The decomposition layer employs variational mode decomposition (VMD) to decompose the PM2.5 sequence data into K sub-sequences, often referred to as Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs) denoted by X = IMF1, IMF2, … IMFK. In this context, each IMF obtained by VMD corresponds to a specific frequency range in the original PM2.5 sequence data. Higher-order IMFs typically capture higher-frequency components, while lower-order IMFs represent lower-frequency components. These sub-sequences are individually utilized for downstream graph construction tasks. The graph feature maps differ among the IMFs because each IMF captures distinct frequency components of the PM2.5 sequence data. As a result, the patterns, properties, and graph-based features extracted from the constructed graphs will vary across the IMFs, reflecting the different frequency characteristics present in the data. Subsequently, based on the geographical locations of air monitoring sites and their correlation, K graph feature maps G = G1, G2, …, GK are constructed separately. These graph feature maps correspond to the spatial relations between air quality monitoring sites. Next, a spatiotemporal layer, which combines Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM), is designed to learn spatiotemporal correlations from the constructed graph feature maps. This yields K outputs corresponding to the prediction results of each sub-sequence. Finally, at the output layer, these predicted results for all individual sub-sequences are aggregated with a summation operation to produce the ultimate PM2.5 forecasting results. Decomposition Layer

The decomposition layer employs variational mode decomposition (VMD)32 to decompose PM2.5 data into multiple sub-sequences, each of which is independently used for prediction. VMD is a signal processing technique, which is capable of decomposing a signal into several relatively stable Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs). Leveraging the favorable localization properties and frequency resolution of VMD, it can effectively handle data with complex frequency structures. The computational approach of VMD involves in formulating a variational problem to decompose PM2.5 data into K sub-sequences, where each sub-sequence Si(t) possesses a unique central frequency. The constraint of the variational problem is that the sum of these sub-sequences equals to the original signal. This is achieved through iterative minimization of the objective function.

Graph construction layer

The adjacency matrix describes the connections between nodes, and its selection significantly affects the performance of our model. Let the adjacency matrix be denoted as A ∈ RN×N, where ai,j represents the relationship between monitoring stations vi and vj. When ai,j = 0, it signifies the absence of spatial relationship between these two stations. Existing methods of constructing graphs between stations mainly rely on either geographical distances between monitoring sites or correlations between them35. The graph construction layer takes into account both the geographical distances between sites and the intersite correlations of air quality. This is achieved by adjusting hyperparameters β that control the weights of geographical distance and correlation. Spatiotemporal layer

The spatiotemporal layer is composed of a cascade of Graph Attention Networks (GAT)36 and Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) in parallel, as depicted in Fig. 1. Here, a GAT is a type of neural network architecture designed for processing graph-structured data. A GAT aims to capture the relationships and dependencies between nodes in a graph by leveraging attention mechanisms. Unlike traditional graph convolutional networks, which aggregate information from neighboring nodes using fixed weights, a GAT introduces self-attention mechanisms that allow the model to weigh the importance of each neighbor when aggregating information. This enables the GAT to focus on more relevant nodes and capture complex relationships within the graph. Therefore, a GAT possess the advantages of attention mechanisms, enabling parallel processing of the entire graph and resulting in faster computation speeds. This makes GATs particularly suitable for handling large-scale graph data. GATs offer the flexibility to dynamically learn spatial relationships between each pair of nodes, rather than relying on fixed rules. After learning spatial characteristics of graph feature maps, BiLSTMs are used to learn temporal characteristics of graph feature maps, leading to the obtained spatiotemporal relationships of air quality data.

Experiment Results and Analysis

Experiment Setting

The open source Pytorch tools are utilized to implement all machine learning models for air quality forecasting. When performing deep learning models, we employ the Adam optimizer, and set the initial learning rate to be le−4. The batch size is set to 512, the maximum epoch number is 100. Mean Squared Error (MSE) is leveraged as the loss function.

Baseline

In order to evaluate the effectiveness of our model, we choose several deep learning models as the baseline methods for comparison. These comparing methods contain three typical models such as Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)37, BiLSTM38, and CNN-LSTM39 models. Additionally, another three recent deep models, such as Transformer40, Graph Convolutional LSTM (GCN-LSTM)41 and Spatiotemporal Graph Convolutional Networks (STGCN)42, are also employed for comparison.

The effect of VMD module

The number of decomposed sub-sequences K in decomposition layer is the most crucial parameter in variational mode decomposition (VMD) module. It typically falls within the range of 1 to 10. A higher K results in a greater number of decomposed sub-sequences, enabling the extraction of more detailed frequency information. However, if K is excessively large, it could lead to frequency overlap among the decomposed sub-sequences, potentially leading to overfitting and reduced prediction accuracy. Table 2 provides the performance comparisons of results with different numbers of decomposed sub-sequences K ranging from 3 to 7 when forecasting PM2.5 concentrations over the next 1 to 6 h. As shown in Table 2, it can be observed that our model with 6 decomposed sub-sequences, K = 6, yields the best performance on air quality forecasting tasks in terms of MAE, RMSE, and IA. Therefore, in this work the number of decomposed sub-sequences K in VMD module is set to 6 for its best performance. The effect of GAT module

The number of graph attention layers in Graph Attention Networks (GAT) module significantly influences the performance of the proposed model. When the number of graph attention layers is shallow, GATs can only capture relatively simple graph structure information, and fail to extract deeper, higherlevel graph structure information. As the number of graph attention layers increases, GATs gradually acquire deeper levels of graph structure information, thereby enhancing their capability to model complex graph structures. To evaluate the effect of the number of graph attention layers in GAT module, experiments were conducted using stacked 1, 2, 3 graph attention layers in GAT module, and the experimental results are presented in Table 3. From Table 3, it is evident that the best predictive performance is achieved with a stacking of 2 graph attention layers in GAT module. However, as the number of stacked graph attention layers increases to 3, there is a slight decrease in prediction accuracy. Deeper layers of graph structure information could potentially introduce irrelevant and weakly correlated information, which might not be beneficial for predicting the decomposed sub-sequences. Hence, it is observed that a stacking of 2 graph attention layers in GAT module is the optimal choice. Conclusions

In this work, we propose a spatiotemporal hybrid deep learning model called VMD–GAT–BiLSTM for predicting PM2.5 concentration levels. Due to integrating the advantages of VMD, GAT, and BiLSTM, our model achieves superior performance to other used models in both short-term and long-term air quality forecasting tasks. Experiment results on the collected Beijing dataset show that our model is a powerful tool to present highly accurate air quality performance. In future, it is interesting to explore more advanced spatiotemporal deep learning models for air quality forecasting. Additionally, it is also meaningful to adopt SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)43, which is a Shapley-value-based explanation method rooted in coalitional game theory, to quantify the importance of different predictor variables.
