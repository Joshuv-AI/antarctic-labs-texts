Measurement of twenty-one serum steroid profiles by UPLC-MS/MS for the diagnosis and monitoring of congenital adrenal hyperplasia
Authors: Joon Hee Lee, Kyunghoon Lee, Sun-Hee Jun, Yun Jeong Lee, Choong Ho Shin, Young Ah Lee, Junghan Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•UPLC-MS/MS method for 21 steroids, including 11-oxygenated androgens.•Precision, linearity, and recovery acceptable for CAH diagnostics.•Differentiates CAH subtypes by identifying distinct steroid profiles.•Provides a reliable tool for CAH diagnosis and monitoring.

Introduction

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) encompasses a group of autosomal recessive disorders characterized by various enzymatic defects in the adrenal steroidogenesis pathway. The most common form of CAH, 21-hydroxylase deficiency (21-OHD), due to mutations in CYP21A2, accounts for over 95 % of all CAH cases globally [1]. Other forms of CAH, such as deficiency of the steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (lipoid CAH, due to mutations in StAR), 11β-hydroxylase (11β-OHD), 17α-hydroxylase/17,20-lyase (17-OHD), 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-HSD) type 2, cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (PORD), and P450 cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme (P450scc) are very rare [2].

Early and accurate diagnosis of CAH is essential to prevent life-threatening adrenal crisis and minimize androgen excess. The classic salt-wasting form of 21-OHD is characterized by clinical features of adrenal insufficiency due to glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid deficiencies, and androgen excess due to elevated adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) levels and accumulation of precursor steroids [3]. The phenotypic spectrum of 21-OHD ranges from the classic (salt-wasting or simple virilizing) form to the mild or non-classic form, which features variable degrees of postnatal androgen excess without glucocorticoid deficiency [1]. Traditionally, newborn screening programs have relied on immunoassays to measure 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17OHP) levels to detect 21-OHD, known as the most common type of CAH. However, these methods have been criticized for their lack of specificity for 17OHP, especially in preterm infants, leading to numerous false-positive results [4]. Furthermore, 17OHP may be elevated in other enzyme defects, particularly 11β-OHD, and more rarely, 3β-HSD or PORD [2].

To improve diagnostic accuracy and permit diagnosis of other forms of CAH, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) has emerged as a more reliable technique, allowing for the simultaneous measurement of multiple steroid hormones with greater sensitivity and specificity. In recent years, with the application of LC-MS/MS in the detection of steroid hormones, early diagnosis and classification of 21-OHD CAH have become possible [[5], [6], [7]]. Recent studies have demonstrated the clinical utility of steroid profiling using LC-MS/MS in differentiating various forms of CAH, particularly in identifying rare forms such as 11β-OHD, 17-OHD, PORD, and lipoid CAH ​[2,8,9]. In addition, a second-tier LC-MS/MS newborn screening test for CAH offers significant improvements to screening specificity without any other changes to screening protocols [4,[10], [11], [12]].

The 11-oxygenated androgens originate from the adrenal cortex due to the adrenal-specific expression of cytochrome P450 11β-hydroxylase and are further converted by the action of 17β-HSD, 11β-HSD and 5α-reductase enzymes [13]. Recent clinical evidence suggests that 11-oxygenated androgens have emerged as a relevant biomarker of androgen excess in the monitoring of 21-OHD [14].

Thus, the purpose of this study was to develop a core method for the simultaneous measurement of 21 steroid hormones, including 11-oxygenated androgens, for the differential diagnosis and monitoring of various forms of CAH, i.e., pregnenolone (Preg), progesterone (P), 17-hydroxypregnenolone (17OHPreg), 17OHP, dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), androstenedione (A4), 11-hydroxyprogesterone (11OHP), 21-deoxycortisol (21DF), testosterone (T), 11β-hydroxyandrostenedione (11OHA4), 11-ketoandrostenedione (11KA4), 11β-hydroxytestosterone (11OHT), 11-ketotestosterone (11KT), 5α-dihydrotestosterone (DHT), 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC), corticosterone (B), aldosterone (Aldo), 11-deoxycortisol (11DF), cortisol (F), cortisone (E) and estrone (E1).

Materials and methods

Chemical and Reagents

HPLC-grade methanol and water were obtained from J.T. Baker (Phillipsburg, NJ, USA). Aldo, DHEA, Preg, 17OHPreg, d7-E, d4-F, d8-21DF, 13C3-B, d5-11DF were purchased from Cerilliant Co. (Round Rock, TX, USA). F, 21DF, 11DF, 17OHP, and d7-A4 were obtained from Steraloids Inc. (Newport. RI, USA). 11OHT were purchased from SY Innovation (Pyeongtaek-si, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea).

P, 11OHP, A4, T, 11KA4, 11OHA4, 11KT, DHT, DOC, B, E, E1, d9-P, d5-DHEA, d4-11OHA4, d4-11OHT, d3-DHT, 13C3-DOC, d4-Aldo, 13C3-E1, formic acid, zinc sulfate, hydroxylamine, charcoal and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were purchased from Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). d4-Preg, d4-17OHPreg, d8-17OHP and d2-T were purchased from C/D/N Isotopes Inc. (Pointe-Claire, Quebec, Canada). d3-11KT was obtained from Cambridge Isotopes.

Preparation of standards and quality control samples

Stock solutions of various analytes were prepared in 90 % methanol at the following concentrations: 11KA4(0.04 μg/mL), 11OHA4(0.04 μg/mL), 11KT (0.04 μg/mL), 11OHT (0.04 μg/mL), E1 (0.05 μg/mL), Aldo (0.2 μg/mL), 21DF (0.2 μg/mL), B (0.2 μg/mL), 11DF (0.2 μg/mL), A4 (0.2 μg/mL), DOC (0.2 μg/mL), T (0.2 μg/mL), P (0.2 μg/mL), 11OHP (0.2 μg/mL), DHT (0.2 μg/mL), Preg (0.4 μg/mL), 17OHPreg (0.4 μg/mL), DHEA (0.5 μg/mL), 17OHP (1 μg/mL), E (2 μg/mL) and F (4 μg/mL). These stock solutions were stored at −70 °C until use. All stock solutions were combined and serially diluted in 2 % BSA in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to create working standards. Calibration curves were constructed with seven concentrations of each analytes as follows: 11-KA4, 11OHA4, 11KT and 11OHT at 0.005, 0.01, 0.04, 0.1, 0.2, 1 and 2 ng/mL; E1 at 0.00625, 0.0125, 0.05, 0.125, 0.25, 1.25 and 2.5 ng/mL; Aldo, 21DF, B, 11DF, A4, DOC, T, P, 11OHP and DHT at 0.025, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 5 and 10 ng/mL; Preg and 17OHPreg at 0.05, 0.1, 0.4, 1, 2, 10 and 20 ng/mL; DHEA at 0.0625, 0.125, 0.5, 1.25, 2.5, 12.5 and 25 ng/mL; 17OHP at 0.125, 0.25, 1, 2.5, 5, 25 and 50 ng/mL; E, at 0.25, 0.5, 2, 5, 10, 50 and 100 ng/mL; F at 0.5, 1, 4, 10, 20, 100 and 200 ng/mL. Three quality control samples with low, medium and high concentrations were prepared by spiking different lots of stock solutions to 2 % BSA in PBS.

For internal standards (IS), working combined solutions were prepared in 90 % methanol and water with the following concentrations: d4-11OHA4(5 ng/mL), d3-11KT (5 ng/mL), 13C3-E1 (5 ng/mL), d4-Aldo (50 ng/mL), d8-21DF (15 ng/mL), 13C3-B (25 ng/mL), d5-11DF (10 ng/mL), d7-A4 (10 ng/mL), 13C3-DOC (20 ng/mL), d2-T (10 ng/mL), d9-P (10 ng/mL), d3-DHT (15 ng/mL), d4-Preg (10 ng/mL), d4-17OHPreg (20 ng/mL), d5-DHEA (10 ng/mL), d8-17OHP (100 ng/mL), d7-E (1,000 ng/mL) and d4-F (50 ng/mL). For the quantification of 11OHP, 11OHT and 11-KA4, d8-17OHP, d3-11KT and d4-11OHA4 were used as ISs, respectively.

Sample preparation

Aliquots of 250 μL of calibrators, controls, or patient serum obtain from the BD Vacutainer SST II™ Advance tube (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) were transferred into glass tubes. To each tube 50 μL of the working combined IS and 100 uL of acetonitrile were added. The samples were extracted using 1 mL of methyl t-butyl ether (MTBE). The organic phase was transferred into a glass vial and evaporated under nitrogen at 50 °C. The dried residues were derivatized with 100 uL of 0.1 M hydroxylamine and incubated at 60°C for 60 min. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 12,000 rpm. Finally, 20 uL of the reaction mixture was injected into UPLC-MS/MS system for analysis.

HPLC-MS/MS conditions

After extraction, 20 μL of the reconstituted sample was injected into a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH column (C18, 1 mm × 50 mm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) using an ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phases were 2 mM ammonium acetate with 0.1 % formic acid in deionized water (A) and 2 mM ammonium acetate with 0.1 % formic acid in methanol (B). The mobile phase gradient (A) was set as follows: 0–2 min, 66–55 %; 2–4 min, 55–52 %; 4–5 min, 52–50 %; 5–7 min, 50–35 %; 7–7.6 min, 35–30 %; 7.6–8.5 min, 30–2 %; 8.5–9.5 min, 2 %; 9.5–9.8 min, 2–66 %; 9.8–11 min, 66 %. The total runtime for each sample was 11 min with a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min.

The HPLC system was coupled to a Waters Xevo TQ-S tandem mass spectrometer. Quantification was achieved by multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) in positive-ion mode. Analytical performance

Analytical performance parameters such as precision, linearity, recovery, ion suppression, carry over, and method comparisons were evaluated in accordance with US Food and Drug Administration Center for Drug Evaluation and Research Guideline for Bioanalytical Method Validation [15] or CLSI Guidelines [16,17], with minor modifications.

The precision of the method was assessed by analyzing three concentrations of control samples. Within-run precision was determined by 10 replicated analyses of controls and between-run precision was measured over 9 consecutive days.

To evaluate linearity, we made lower limit of the measuring interval (LLMI) and upper LMI (ULMI) samples by spiking the standards into the 2 % BSA in PBS. LLMI sample was mixed with ULMI sample at predefined ratios (4:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:4) according to the CLSI EP06-A [18]. Each sample was measured in quadruplicate.

Recovery was determined by analyzing charcoal stripped serum spiked with low, medium and high standards. Absolute recovery was calculated by dividing the peak area obtained from standards spiked in the charcoal stripped serum by the peak area after post-extraction spiking of the same amounts of standards. The post-extraction addition consisted of spiking the standards onto the MTBE fraction obtained from the workup of charcoal stripped serum. We also calculated relative recovery by comparing the observed difference in the IS-corrected peak area within the same samples.

The limit of detection (LOD) was defined as the minimum concentration with a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) greater than 3, while the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was set at concentration where both precision and accuracy relative to the expected value were within 20 %.

Ion suppression was evaluated by the post-column infusion method [19]. Briefly, an IS solution was continuously infused directly into the mass spectrometer while five patient serum samples were injected into the HPLC column. Significant changes in detection levels were noted as points of ion suppression.

Carryover was evaluated by injecting a reconstituted solvent blank (70 % methanol in water) immediately after the ULMI of the standard curve. The acceptance criterion for carryover was set as the concentration of the solvent blank with a response of less than 20 % of the LLOQ.

When selecting an IS, the degree of stable isotope labeling should ensure that interference from naturally occurring isotopes in the target analytes at the ULMI is less than 5 % of the IS response [16]. As a general rule, for molecules with a mass of less than 1,000 amu, isotopic enrichment of + 3 amu is typically sufficient. However, due to strict import regulations enforced by the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, obtaining testosterone compounds is extremely challenging. Therefore, we used d2-T, which had been previously purchased and was available for use. To assess potential interference from naturally occurring isotopes of T on the d2-T signal, we measured the relative intensity of the d2-T peak in a ULMI-level sample without IS, originating by natural isotope of T, compared to d2-T peak intensity in a blank sample with IS.

Our laboratory participated in the proficiency testing for sex hormones (2023 trials, Y-B) conducted by the College of American Pathologists (CAP). The results of T, A4, 17OHP, P and 11DF measured by our method were compared with group mean values from other laboratories using mass spectrometry. Deming regression analysis was performed for comparison (MedCal Version 22.018, Ostend, Belgium).

Lastly, the distributions of steroid hormone profiles in healthy children and patients with four different forms of CAH were evaluated. The samples from 34 healthy children (17 boys and 17 girls, age = 5.7 ± 2.3 years) were leftover samples from patients undergoing minor procedures such as exotropia or tonsillectomy. Only samples with normal laboratory findings (including complete blood counts, liver function tests, renal function tests, and C-reactive protein) were included. Samples from six patients with CAH were also analyzed (Table 2): two boys with 21-OHD (age 6.4 and 9.5), one girl with 17-OHD (age 17), one girl with PORD (age 7.5), and two girls with lipoid CAH (age 7 for both). Mann-Whitney test (independent samples) (MedCalc® Version 22.018, Ostend, Belgium) was used to compare the 21 steroid hormone concentrations between normal controls and each CAH patient group. Results

All target analytes were effectively separated and detected using the developed method. Representative chromatograms are displayed in Fig. 1. Oxime derivatives of some steroids were separated into syn and anti isomers, showing two peaks [20]. Steroids displaying syn- and anti-oxime isomers included A4, 21DF, T, 11OHA4, 11KA4, 11OHT, 11KT, B, Aldo, 11DF, F, and E. When peaks were completely separated, the larger peak was selected for quantification. When peaks were closely eluted (e.g., Aldo), their combined area was used for quantification. Since an IS was employed, the results remained consistent regardless of whether the larger peak alone or the combined area was used. The analytes 11KA4, 11OHA4, 11KT and 11OHT exhibited linear responses over the range of 0.005 ∼ 2 ng/mL with R2 values > 0.99; E1 over 0.00625 ∼ 2.5 ng/mL with R2 values 0.998; Aldo, 21DF, B, 11DF, A4, DOC, T, P, 11OHP and DHT over 0.025 ∼ 10 ng/mL [R2 values > 0.99 (except B, R2 value = 0.9853)] Preg and 17OHPreg over 0.05 ∼ 20 ng/mL (R2 values > 0.99); DHEA over 0.0625 ∼ 25 ng/mL (R2 value = 0.9987); 17OHP over 0.125 ∼ 50 ng/mL (R2 value = 0.9988); E over 0.25 ∼ 100 ng/mL (R2 value = 0.999); F over 0.5 ∼ 200 ng/mL (R2 value = 0.9967), respectively.

No significant ion suppression or enhancement was observed by the post-column infusion of stable isotope labeled IS into chromatographed eluates from extracted serum samples (Fig. S1).

Absolute recoveries ranged from 57 % to 86 %, while relative recoveries of spiked analytes ranged from 99 % to 111 %, which was deemed acceptable (Table 3). No significant carryover was observed for any steroid hormones; all solvent blank concentrations were less than 20 % of the LLOQ.

To evaluate the potential interference from naturally occurring isotopes of T on the d2-T signal, we measured the intensity of the d2-T peak in a ULMI-level sample without IS (A) and compared it to the d2-T intensity in a blank sample containing IS (B). The A/B ratio was 1.7 %, which is below the 5 % threshold [16]. Therefore, interference from naturally occurring isotopes of T on the d2-T response was considered negligible.

The overall correlations were strong (Pearson correlation coefficient r = 0.9993 for T, 0.9986 for 17OHP, 0.9909 for A4, 0.9440 for 11DF and 0.9991 for P. Deming regression analysis indicated proportional biases especially for A4, P and 11DF (Fig. S2), where values obtained by our method were lower than survey mean values.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed a highly sensitive and specific LC-MS/MS method for the simultaneous quantification of 21 steroid hormones including 11-oxygenated androgens, demonstrating its utility in diagnosis and monitoring of CAH. The method performed particularly well in distinguishing between normal controls and patients with different forms of CAH, with several key steroids showing significant differences between these groups. Our method also demonstrated robust analytical performance, with excellent linearity, precision (except 11KA4), and minimal ion suppression. A potential reason for the relatively high imprecision of 11KA4 is the absence of a dedicated stable isotope-labeled IS. In our study, d4-11OHA4 was used as a surrogate IS for 11KA4. Furthermore, the generally low serum concentrations of 11KA4 in many individuals may have contributed to the higher variability. Importantly, as shown in Fig. 2, 11KA4 levels were markedly elevated in patients with 21-OHD and significantly decreased in those with lipoid CAH. Given the substantial differences between these groups, we believe that the observed repeatability issue does not interfere with the clinical utility of 11KA4 in the differential diagnosis of CAH subtypes.

The comparison with the CAP proficiency testing results revealed that our LC-MS/MS correlated well with the peer group means of other laboratories using LC-MS/MS. However, there were notable negative biases for A4, P and 11DF. Although the CAP peer group for these analytes consists of laboratories using LC-MS/MS methods, significant inter-laboratory variation is still observed, particularly for analytes lacking commercially available calibrators. For both A4 and 11DF, no standardized reference materials are universally available; thus, laboratories—including ours—must prepare in-house calibrators, often using different sources, matrices, and preparation protocols. This inevitably leads to variability in calibration and quantification. Indeed, the between-laboratories variations from the CAP survey ranged from 9.7 to 16 % for A4, and 19.9 to 104.4 % for 11DF, indicating significant differences between laboratories using LC-MS/MS. The Clinical Standardization Program for steroid hormones by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the US was primarily focused on testosterone and estradiol. Based on this comparison study, international harmonization or standardization efforts for other steroid hormones will be necessary [21].

Feng et al. utilized microbore LC-MS/MS instead of conventional LC-MS/MS, which proved to be more suitable for analyzing steroids [18]. The greatest advantages of microbore column UPLC combined with ESI-MS lies in its suitability for samples with limited amounts or volumes and its low volumetric flow rates, which increase sample ionization efficiency and enhance detection sensitivity, leading to identification of a great number of steroids [22]. In this study, we applied microbore column systems. Additionally, we employed chemical derivatization with hydroxylamine to further increase sensitivity [23]. The combination of microbore column UPLC and hydroxylamine derivatization enabled excellent chromatographic separation and enhanced sensitivity of all compounds of interest.

Steroid hormone profiling, distinct from that of normal controls, was observed in each patient according to the type of enzyme defect associated with the adrenal steroidogenesis pathway. For patients with 21-OHD (non-classic form), androgen precursors and androgens (17OHPreg, 17OHP, DHEA, A4, and 11-oxygenated androgens) and 21DF were significantly elevated, in consistent with previous studies [[5], [6], [7]]. For a patient with 17-OHD, Preg, P, 17OHPreg, 17OHP, 11OHP, T, DOC and B showed an increasing tendency, while 21DF and E showed a decreasing tendency [8]. POR is the electron donor for three P450 enzymes, including P450c17 (17α-hydroxylase/17, 20-lyase), P450c21 (21-hydroxylase), and P450aro (aromatase). For a patient with PORD, DHT, Preg, P, 17OHPreg and 17OHP showed an increasing tendency, although B, Aldo, F, and E levels were also elevated by blood collection during hydrocortisone treatment. Lipoid CAH, the most severe form of CAH with all steroid deficiencies due to a StAR enzyme defect in which the initial step of adrenal and gonadal steroidogenesis is impaired [2]. In our patients with lipoid CAH, 17OHPreg, 17OHP, 21DF, T, 11OHA4, 11KA4, 11KT, DOC, B, and 11DF were significantly decreased compared to the normal controls. A preliminary differential diagnosis of different types of CAH can be made through analysis of the steroid hormone profile in this study.

The adrenals are also the source of 11-oxygenated androgen steroids including 11OHA4, 11OHT, 11KA4, 11KT, 11-hydroxydihydrotestosterone, and 11-ketodihydrotestosterone [13,14]. Numerous studies have focused on the role of 11-oxygenated androgens in human health and disease, implicating them as role players in conditions such as castration resistant prostate cancer, CAH, polycystic ovary syndrome, Cushing's syndrome, and premature adrenarche [13]. The 11-oxygenated androgens may have a role as useful biomarkers for the monitoring of disease control in 21-OHD [14]. In this study, we successfully measured the four 11-oxygenated androgens (11OHT, 11KT, 11OHA4, and 11KA4). 11OHA4 is the most abundant 11-oxygented androgen in circulation, followed by 11KA4, 11KT and 11OHT. The circulating levels of latter three compounds were very low, highlighting the importance of method sensitivity. The LLOQ of 11OHA4, 11KA4, 11KT and 11OHT were 0.01, 0.01, 0.02 and 0.01 ng/mL, respectively, which was sufficient sensitivity for the diagnosis and monitoring of CAH.

Despite the strengths of our devised method, our study has some limitations. Although steroid hormone profiles may differ by age and sex, we did not perform steroid hormone analysis for large-scale normal controls. In addition, due to the limited number of patients diagnosed with rare diseases, comparative analysis could not be performed by taking blood samples from all different types of patients at diagnosis to fully verify the clinical usefulness of steroid profiling for differential diagnosis of CAH. Currently, we are conducting nationwide studies focused on establishing the reference intervals based on age and sex, and exploring the method’s applicability in monitoring treatment efficacy in CAH patients.

In conclusion, we successfully developed a simultaneous assay for 21 steroids. The method demonstrated reliable quantitative performance for the profiling of adrenal hormones in the CAH patients. Specifically, we observed reliable analytical performances in terms of precision, ion suppression, sensitivity, linearity and carryover. The LC-MS/MS method developed in this study provides a comprehensive and reliable tool for steroid profiling in CAH. Its superior performance compared to immunoassays, particularly in the specificity of steroid measurements, makes it an ideal candidate for use in clinical laboratories to enhance the accuracy of CAH diagnosis.
