The impact of genome duplication on agronomically valuable traits in Ribes nigrum L.: a morpho-anatomical, physiological and cytogenetic evaluation
Authors: Aleksandra Machlańska, Agnieszka Marasek-Ciołakowska, Małgorzata Podwyszyńska, Stanisław Pluta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlackcurrant (Ribes nigrum L.), which is known for its nutrient richness, faces agricultural challenges, such as environmental stress and limited genetic diversity. Polyploidisation to increase the number of chromosome sets is a promising approach for enhancing plant traits but is underexplored in blackcurrant breeding. This study investigated the effects of chromosome doubling on blackcurrant cultivars ‘Polares‘ and ‘Gofert‘, examining growth, cyto-anatomical structures, and photosynthesis of 5- and 6-year-old bushes, to understand how genome duplication influences plant phenotypes and the potential of autotetraploids in breeding programs.ResultsCompared to diploids, tetraploid blackcurrant showed reduced growth and delayed flowering, with changes in leaf morphology, such as a larger area, increased stomatal size, lower density, and thicker epidermal and mesophyll layers. The flowers of tetraploid plants were larger, but their number per inflorescence was reduced, and pollen viability was lower. Although the berry size and weight were comparable between ploidy levels, tetraploids were characterised by reduced seed set and fruit yield. Tetraploids had a higher chlorophyll content and increased photosynthetic parameters. The cell and chloroplast ultrastructure observed through transmission electron microscopy differed between ploidy levels. The number and intensity of 5S and 35S rDNA loci also varied, as shown by fluorescence in situ hybridisation.ConclusionsInduction of tetraploid in blackcurrant resulted in significant phenotypic changes. Although tetraploidisation reduced plant vigour and fruit yield, it enhanced leaf and floral traits and photosynthetic efficiency. These findings highlight the complexity of the effect of chromosome doubling on blackcurrant performance, emphasising the need for research to optimise the benefits of polyploidisation in crop improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08514-w.Keywords: Autotetraploid, Blackcurrant, Chloroplast ultrastructure, Crop improvement, Chlorophyll content, Growth and development, Leaf anatomy, Photosynthesis efficiency, rDNA loci

Introduction

The genus Ribes, particularly Ribes nigrum L. (blackcurrant), is of significant economic and nutritional importance, with over 150 species available worldwide [1, 2]. Blackcurrant berries are prized for their high vitamin, mineral, and antioxidant contents, making them valuable for fresh consumption and processing into products, such as juices, jams, and dietary supplements [3–5]. Despite the known benefits of blackcurrants, their cultivation faces challenges, such as drought, waterlogging, flower susceptibility to late spring frosts, and biotic stressors, which negatively affect plant health, yield, and fruit quality [6]. The demand for improved R. nigrum cultivars has led to research on enhancing desirable traits [7, 8]. However, current Ribes breeding programs are limited by the genetic diversity of the collected germplasm [9, 10].

Polyploidisation, a process that increases the number of chromosome sets in an organism, has emerged as a promising method for crop improvement [11]. This approach can be achieved through two main routes: interspecific hybridisation followed by chromosome doubling, in which allopolyploids combine genomes from different species, and intraspecific whole-genome duplication (WGD), which multiplies the existing genome in a single species [12–14]. Both methods have shown the potential for enhancing desirable traits in plants. WGD often results in organ enlargement, stronger and thicker stems, increased plant height and number of leaves, a more compact plant habit, and increased growth vigour [15–18]. WGD influences the leaf thickness, stomatal size, and density [17, 18]. Changes in the leaf structure and stomatal characteristics significantly affect photosynthetic efficiency and water use in polyploid plants [19]. Increased leaf thickness often results in a higher chloroplast content per unit leaf area, potentially enhancing the carbon fixation rate. Alterations in stomatal size and density affect gas exchange and transpiration, improving drought tolerance in some polyploid species [17]. Additionally, changes in the chloroplast ultrastructure, including the number and size of chloroplasts per cell, and an increased photosynthetic pigment content affect the photosynthetic efficiency and, consequently, plant growth and productivity [20]. The increase in the genome size leads to the reorganisation of the nuclear architecture, affecting gene expression patterns [16, 21]. Moreover, changes in the number of rDNA loci influence ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis, thereby affecting cellular metabolism and growth [22]. Polyploidy also affects the production of secondary metabolites [23] or tolerance to abiotic and biotic stressors [24–26]. For example, tetraploid Lonicera japonica demonstrated increased heat tolerance following genome duplication [25]. Similarly, tetraploid Malus domestica has shown enhanced drought stress tolerance [17] and resistance to apple scab (Venturia inaequalis) [16]. Tetraploid Oryza sativa exhibited improved root resistance to salt stress compared to its diploid progenitor [27].

Despite the potential benefits of polyploidisation observed in other crops, its effects on the blackcurrant genome are limited because native species of the genus Ribes and the blackcurrant cultivars available on the market are diploids (2n = 2x = 16) [5]. To date, limited research has been conducted on the effects of artificial polyploidisation on blackcurrant, with most studies focusing on early stage neopolyploids [21, 28, 29]. Studies have shown that 6-month-old tetraploid blackcurrants have shorter shoots, fewer leaves, and a higher chlorophyll index than diploid plants [29]. Additionally, research on blackcurrant seedlings grown in vitro has demonstrated that neotetraploids are dwarfed with shorter internode lengths, thicker stems, larger and rounder leaves, decreased stomatal density, and larger stomata [21].

There is a knowledge gap regarding whether polyploidisation improves the agronomically valuable traits of blackcurrant cultivars under field conditions. Therefore, the current study aimed to address this gap by focusing on two Polish blackcurrant cultivars, ‘Polares’ and ‘Gofert‘ , which were developed at the National Institute of Horticultural Research (NIHR) in Skierniewice, Poland. These cultivars were selected for genome doubling because they are well-adapted to the Polish climate and have low susceptibility to powdery mildew (Podosphaera mors-uvae Schw.) and white pine blister rust (Cronartium ribicola Fish.) [29–31].

This study compared diploid (2n = 2x = 16) and induced tetraploid (2n = 4x = 32) plants of ‘Polares‘ and ‘Gofert‘ in their 5th and 6th growing seasons. This timeframe allowed for a comprehensive assessment of the long-term effects of polyploidisation on mature plants. We examined various aspects of diploid and tetraploid plant performance and quality, including cytological characteristics (cell size, chloroplast ultrastructure, and 35S and 5S rDNA loci), anatomical features (leaf thickness, stomatal size, and density), morphological traits (leaf, flower, and fruit characteristics), fruit yield, and photosynthetic parameters. By comprehensively evaluating these characteristics, this study aimed to provide valuable insights into the effects of polyploidisation on blackcurrant performance and quality. The findings of this study have the potential to optimise blackcurrant breeding strategies and cultivation practices. If chromosome doubling proves beneficial, it could lead to the development of new cultivars with improved traits, such as a larger fruit size or better adaptation to environmental stressors. Furthermore, the results of this study may have broader implications for the Ribes genus and other berry crops. Understanding the impacts of genome duplication on blackcurrant could provide a foundation for similar research on related species, potentially advancing the breeding and cultivation of a range of economically important fruit crops.

Methods

Plant materials

The plants material used in the present study were obtained from the maintained germplasm collection of the Department of Horticultural Crop Breeding, NIHR, Skierniewice, Poland. The plants material comprised diploid blackcurrant cultivars ‘Polares’ and ‘Gofert’ each with four autotetraploid clones (each genotype with three plants as replications). The tetraploids were obtained using an in vitro polyploidisation technique [29]. To date, only the basic morphological parameters of juvenile, six-month-old ‘Gofert‘ plants grown in a greenhouse have been evaluated [29]. After acclimatisation in a greenhouse, these plants were planted in a field in 2018 at a density of 3.5 m × 0.75 m, with a spacing of 1.2 m between plots, within the field experiment conducted at the Pomological Orchard in Skierniewice, Poland, under the auspices of NIHR (51° 57‘ 44.678” N, 20° 9‘ 40.305” E). The experimental plots were maintained in accordance with standard agrotechnical practices for blackcurrant cultivation. The current study was conducted from 2021 to 2022, corresponding to the 5th and 6th growing seasons. Both the diploid and tetraploid blackcurrant plants employed in the field experiment were derived from in vitro propagation. The ploidy level of the clones was repeatedly confirmed during plant growth using flow cytometry, following the method described by Podwyszyńska and Pluta [29] (Fig. S1). Additionally, the ploidy level was verified by determine the number of chromosomes according to the method described by Marasek-Ciołakowska [32] (Fig. S2). Weather conditions during the vegetation seasons were assessed using meteorological data from a METOS-COMPACT station located in the Pomological Orchard at NIHR (Table S1). The 2021 season experienced more summer rainfall than 2022, although the average temperatures were similar across both seasons.

Evaluation of growth parameters

In the 2021 and 2022 vegetative seasons, parameters including bush size (width and height), and annual shoot growth were assessed at the end of the growing season., Annual shoot growth was assessed on each individual bush. Ten current-year shoots were randomly selected and permanently marked on each bush, and the length of annual shoot growth was measured at the end of the growing season. Measurements obtained from multiple shoots were treated as repeated (technical) measurements within each bush.

Leaf sampling and morphological analysis

For leaf area analysis, ten fully developed leaves located fifth from the apex of the shoot were collected from each individual bush. Thus, a total of 30 leaves were analysed for diploid plants (three clones), and 120 leaves were analysed for tetraploid plants (four clones, three bushes per clone) for each cultivar. Leaf measurements were treated as technical replicates nested within bushes and clones. The leaf area, leaf length (LL), and leaf width (LW) were measured using a planimeter (Area Meter AM350, ADC BioScientific Ltd., Hoddesdon, UK). Based on these measurements, the leaf length-to-width ratio (LR) was calculated using the following formula: LR = LL / LW. Images captured with a VHX-7000 N KEYENCE (Japan, Osaka) digital microscope (a total of 30 microscopic images were analyzed for diploid plants, and 120 microscopic images were analyzed for tetraploid plants for each cultivar) were used to assess the number of secretory glands per square centimetre on the abaxial side of the leaves.

Micromorphological analysis of leaf surfaces using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis, leaf samples (10 mm × 10 mm) were excised from the fifth fully developed leaf from the top of the shoot and fixed in chromic acid, acetic acid, and formalin solution (CrAF). Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (20, 40, 60, 80, 90, and 100%), desiccated via critical point drying with CO2, and sputter-coated with gold, as described by Pathan et al. [33]. The micromorphology of the abaxial leaf surface was examined using a JEOL JSM 6390LV scanning electron microscope (JEOL, Peabody, Massachusetts, USA Inc.). Electron micrographs were acquired at magnifications of 50× and 1000×. In total, 17 electron micrographs were analysed for diploid plants and twenty-four for tetraploid plants.

Observation of stomatal morphology and leaf anatomical structure

To examine the stomatal characteristics, the abaxial epidermis was extracted from mature leaves with similar growth stages using adhesive tape, stained with 2% toluidine blue, and mounted on microscope slides. The stomatal density per square millimetre (n = 5 replicates per genotype) and length (n = 3 plants × 100 stomata per genotype) were quantified. Paraffin sections were prepared for leaf anatomical observations as described by Marasek-Ciolakowska et al. [34]. Leaf segments (5 mm × 10 mm) were excised from the middle of the fully developed fifth leaf from the top of the shoot, with five leaf samples collected per genotype. Briefly, the samples were fixed in CrAF solution at room temperature in the dark for 48 h and dehydrated in a graded alcohol series (20, 40, 60, 80, 90, and 100%). The samples were treated with xylene and embedded in paraffin. Transverse sections(12 μm in thickness) were prepared using a rotary microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and stained with 1% Safranin-O and 0.5% Fast Green. The sections were mounted on Canada balsams. Microscope slides were analysed using a light microscope (LM) (Eclipse 80i, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with NIS-Elements Br version 2.30 imaging software (Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan) for photographic documentation and measurement purposes. Measurements included the thickness of the abaxial and adaxial epidermal layers and the palisade and spongy mesophyll layers. For each analysed anatomical trait, the following numbers of measurements were performed. For the cultivar ‘Gofert’, a total of 180 measurements were obtained, including 20 measurements for diploid plants and 160 measurements for tetraploid plants (four clones × three bushes per clone). For the cultivar ‘Polares’, a total of 100 measurements were obtained, including 20 measurements for diploid plants and 80 measurements for tetraploid plants. In all cases, bushes were treated as biological replicates, and multiple measurements obtained from each anatomical section were averaged prior to statistical analysis.

Observation of the chloroplast ultrastructure using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Mature leaves were collected from the branches exposed to sunlight. Leaf fragments (2 mm × 2 mm) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 12 h in a 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4). Subsequently, the material was treated with a 1% aqueous osmium tetroxide solution for 4 h. After dehydration in ethanol and propylene oxide [35], the material was embedded in an epoxide resin mixture [36]. The material was sectioned into semi-thin sections of 2 μm and ultrathin sections (approximately 80 nm) using a Leica UTC ultramicrotome. Ultrathin sections were contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate according to Reynolds [37] and analysed using an FEI268D Morgagni transmission electron microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, Oregon, USA) equipped with a Morada SIS camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). In total 345 micrograph were acquired. Three biological samples were analysed for each genotype. For each biological sample, plastoglobuli were counted in 10 chloroplasts. Thus, a total of 30 chloroplasts were analysed for diploid plants (3 samples × 10 chloroplasts). In tetraploid plants, four clones were examined, each represented by three biological samples; plastoglobuli were counted in 10 chloroplasts per sample, resulting in 30 chloroplasts analysed per clone and a total of 120 chloroplasts analysed per cultivar (120 for ‘Gofert’ and 120 for ‘Polares’).

Phenotype characteristics during flowering and fruiting

Stamen size, number of flowers per strig (inflorescence), and pollen grain size and quality were evaluated during the flowering period. Morphological measurements of floral organs were obtained from digital images captured using a VHX-7000 N KEYENCE digital microscope. For floral trait analysis, three plants per genotype were used as biological replicates. For each biological replicate, images of 10 flowers were acquired and analysed, resulting in a total of 30 flower images for diploid plants (three clones, one plant per clone) and 120 flower images for tetraploid plants (four clones, three bushes per clone) for each cultivar. These images were used to measure flower diameter as well as pistil and stamen dimensions.

Anthers were collected on the day of anthesis from 10 randomly selected flowers per plant to study pollen grain size and fertility. For pollen morphometric analysis, pollen grains stained with Alexander’s stain were imaged, and the diameter of 100 pollen grains per biological replicate was measured on microscope slides. Three plants were used as biological replicates for each genotype. In total, 300 pollen grain images were analysed per genotype for pollen size determination.

The same slides were also used to assess pollen fertility based on cytoplasmic staining with Alexander’s stain. Pollen stainability was determined by evaluating at least 300 pollen grains per genotype in 10 microscopic fields of view at 10× magnification. The pollen grain diameter and viability were assessed using a light microscope (Eclipse 80i; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and NIS-Elements Br ver. 2.30 imaging software (Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was used for photo documentation and measurement. Pollen grain germination was evaluated on agar medium supplemented with 150 g/L sucrose after 6 h of incubation at room temperature in the dark. For each biological replicate, pollen grains from 10 randomly selected flowers were plated on the germination medium. The percentage of germinated pollen was determined by observing pollen tube growth under a VHX-7000 N KEYENCE digital microscope. Pollen grains with pollen tubes exceeding twice the grain diameter were considered viable. Fruit yield was recorded for each clone at harvest (g per clone). Fruit size and mean berry weight were determined using 10 randomly selected berries per plant, with three biological replicates per genotype. Seed number and seed weight were assessed in the same berry samples. Representative images of fruits and seeds were acquired for documentation and morphometric analysis.

Evaluation of the relative chlorophyll content and photosynthetic parameters

The relative chlorophyll content index (CCI) and photosynthetic parameters were evaluated in July during the 5th growing season. For diploid plants, three bushes were evaluated (n = 3 bushes in total). For tetraploid plants, four clones were evaluated, each represented by three bushes (n = 12 bushes in total). From each bush, 10 fully expanded, healthy leaves were selected for physiological measurements. Consequently, measurements were performed on a total of 30 leaves for diploid plants (3 bushes × 10 leaves) and 120 leaves for tetraploid plants (4 clones × 3 bushes × 10 leaves). CCI was measured using a CCM-200 Plus chlorophyll content meter (Opti-Sciences Int., Hudson, NH, USA). Chlorophyll a fluorescence was measured using a MINI PAM Fluorimeter (Walz, Switzerland). The maximal fluorescence (Fm) and minimal fluorescence (Fo) were recorded. The maximal photochemical efficiency of photosystem II (PSII) (Fv/Fm) and the potential photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fo) were calculated as Fv/Fm = (Fm − Fo)/Fm and Fv/Fo = (Fm − Fo)/Fo, as described by Kramer et al. [38]. Gas exchange was measured using a portable LCpro+ photosynthesis measurement system (ADC BioScientific Ltd., Hoddesdon, UK). Measurements were conducted between 09:00 and 12:00 under controlled chamber conditions: photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) was set to 800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ using an internal light source, reference CO₂ concentration was maintained at 385 ppm, leaf temperature was stabilized at 35 °C (corresponding to ambient leaf temperatures recorded under field conditions and was identical for all genotypes and ploidy levels), and relative humidity inside the chamber (H2O partial pressure) was maintained at approximately 23 mbar. Leaves were allowed to equilibrate for approximately 120 s prior to data recording. Measurements included the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), internal stomatal conductance (Gs), and transpiration rate (Tr).

Double-target Fluorescence in situ Hybridisation (FISH)

Tips of young roots (1–1.5 cm in length) were harvested from hardwood cuttings and treated with 2 mM 8-hydroxyquinoline for 6 h (2 h at 4 °C and 4 h at room temperature). Subsequent procedures followed the methodology described by Marasek-Ciołakowska et al. [32]. Briefly, the roots were fixed in freshly prepared Carnoy’s solution (ethanol: acetic acid, 3:1 v: v) and digested in an enzyme mixture (4% (v: v) pectinase and 2% (v: v) cellulase in 2 mM citrate buffer, pH 4.5) at 37 °C for 90 min. Root tips were squashed in a drop of 45% (v: v) acetic acid under a cover slip. Following freezing in liquid nitrogen, the cover slips were removed using a razor blade, and preparations were dehydrated in absolute ethanol, air-dried, and stored at − 20 °C. In situ hybridisation techniques were adapted from Marasek-Ciołakowska et al. [32, 39]. Ribosomal DNA probes included a 410-bp-long wheat clone, pTa794, to visualise 5 S rDNA and a 2.3-kb ClaI subclone of the 35S rDNA coding region of Arabidopsis thaliana to detect 35S rDNA loci. The latter probe localised the 18S–5.8S–25S rRNA genes (35S rDNA). The 5S and 35S rDNAs were labelled with tetramethyl-rhodamine-5-dUTP and digoxigenin-11-dUTP (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), respectively, via nick translation. The hybridisation mixture contained 50% deionised formamide, 20% dextran sulphate, 2× saline sodium citrate (SSC), and salmon sperm-blocking DNA in 50–100× excess of labelled probes. Ribosomal DNA probes were combined to approximately 2.5 ng/mL and pre-denatured at 80 °C for 10 min. Chromosomal slides were denatured in the mixture at 70 °C for 4.5 min and hybridised at 37 °C for 18 h. Post-hybridisation washes were performed in 2× SSC at room temperature, 0.1× SSC at 42 °C (73% stringency), and 2× SSC at room temperature. Immunodetection was performed using the fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated anti-digoxigenin antibody (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Preparations were mounted in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) with 2 µg/mL 4´,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). The slides were analysed using an epifluorescent microscope Optiphot-2 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with an image analysis system (NIS-Elements Basic Research ver. 4.00 (Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and appropriate filter blocks at excitation wavelengths of 350 nm for DAPI, 495 nm for fluorescein isothiocyanate, and 550 nm for rhodamine. In total, 228 images were acquired; for each clone, at least 3–5 distinct metaphases were examined for hybridization. Images were captured using a digital CCD camera PS-Fi1 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed separately for each cultivar. Each tetraploid clone and diploid variety was treated as a separate genotype. Bushes were treated as biological replicates for statistical analyses. A linear model in hierarchical classification was used for data analysis. Comparisons between the diploid and the average effect of tetraploids were made using planned contrasts. Comparisons between genotypes were performed using Duncan’s multiple range test at p = 0.05. For all traits, F-values, degrees of freedom (df), exact p-values and sample sizes (n) are reported in the corresponding tables or provided in Supplementary Table S2 and Table S3. All values presented in tables and figures represent means ± standard error (SE).

All calculations were done using STATISTICA 13.1 package (Dell Inc. 2016).

Results

Comparison of growth traits

Morphological analysis of diploid and tetraploid blackcurrant ‘Polares‘ and ‘Gofert‘ revealed significant differences in growth under field conditions, with a clear distinction observed between genotypes with different ploidy levels (Figs. 1A, B and S3). This comparison was systematically quantified, focusing on the critical parameters of bush height and width as well as the annual shoot growth rate across growing seasons.

In the 5th growing season (2021), a significant disparity in growth vigour was evident between tetraploid and diploid plants of both cultivars. Specifically, tetraploid plants exhibited a reduction in both height and width, indicating a less robust growth pattern than diploid plants (Fig. 1C, D). For ‘Gofert‘, the height and width were reduced by 26 and 22%, respectively (Fig. 1C). The reduction was even more pronounced in ‘Polares‘, with tetraploids showing a 47% decrease in height and a 58% reduction in width (Fig. 1D). Additionally, during this season, diploid plants of both cultivars demonstrated approximately 40% longer shoots than their tetraploid counterparts.

The 6th growing season showed a consistent trend, with tetraploid bushes maintaining smaller dimensions in terms of height and width. However, the degree of difference in growth parameters varied between cultivars. Although ‘Gofert‘ showed no significant differences, ‘Polares‘ tetraploids were notably smaller, with a 41% reduction in height and a 52% reduction in width compared to diploid plants, indicating a cultivar-specific response to ploidy level changes (Fig. 1C, D).

An interesting shift was observed in the annual growth rates in 2022. In contrast to the patterns observed in previous seasons, tetraploid plants of ‘Polares‘ and ‘Gofert‘ exhibited 27 and 12% higher growth rates, respectively, compared to diploids (Fig. 1C, D).

Leaf morphological and anatomical characteristics

The morphological and anatomical characteristics of the leaves of the tetraploid clones were compared to those of their diploid counterparts of cultivars ‘Polares’ and ‘Gofert’ (Figs. 2, 3 and S4A, B, Table 1). Although the leaf shape and length-to-width ratio remained consistent between ploidy levels, tetraploid clones exhibited a larger leaf area, which increased by approximately 14% for ‘Gofert’ and 12% for ‘Polares’ (Fig. S4; Table 1). SEM showed that tetraploid leaves had a smoother surface with trichomes primarily concentrated along the midrib and lateral veins, whereas diploid leaves displayed trichomes on minor veins and veinlets. Tetraploid plants also demonstrated a higher density of secreting glands on the abaxial leaf surface, by approximately 7% for ‘Gofert’ and 16.5% for ‘Polares’ (Table 1).

Notably, the stomatal characteristics differed between ploidy levels, with tetraploids exhibiting larger guard cells (increased by approximately 46% in both cultivars) but a lower stomata density (decreased approximately 30–35%) (Figs. 2C–G and S4C, D). In diploid ‘Gofert’, the highest percentage of stomata (50%) measured between 18 and 20.99 μm in length. In contrast, the majority of stomata (25%) in tetraploids of this cultivar were within the 24–26.99 μm range. For cultivar ‘Polares’, the highest percentage of stomata (45%) in diploids ranged from 21 to 23.99 μm, while in tetraploids, 32% of stomata were between 33 and 35.99 μm (Fig. 2F).

Anatomically, tetraploid leaves showed significant differences compared to their diploid counterparts (Fig. 3; Table 1). The leaves were thicker in tetraploids by approximately 22.45% for ‘Gofert’ and 11.85% for ‘Polares’, with increased thickness in the epidermis, palisade mesophyll, and spongy mesophyll layers (Table 1). The midrib diameter was larger in tetraploid leaves, with a larger vascular tissue area, and they displayed a thicker cuticle layer on both the midrib and adaxial epidermis (Fig. 3A, B). Tetraploid leaves featured 2–3 layers of palisade mesophyll cells, compared to 2 distinct layers in diploids. The spongy mesophyll in tetraploids was more compact, with fewer intercellular spaces (Fig. 3C, D). The anatomical structures of the secreting glands were similar between diploids and tetraploids (Fig. 3E, F).

Chloroplast ultrastructure

The ultrastructure of leaf mesophyll cells in diploid and tetraploid blackcurrants was analysed using TEM (Fig. 4). Chloroplasts of diploids (Fig. 4A–C) frequently contained large starch grains, whereas there was almost no starch in the chloroplasts of tetraploids (Fig. 4D–F). Additionally, chloroplasts in diploids had larger grana and tight arrangements of their thylakoid lamella (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the thylakoid lamella was thicker in tetraploids, but less regularly arranged, and grana were less visible (Fig. 4F). Tetraploids had a higher number of plastoglobuli than their diploid progenitors. Specifically, the number of plastoglobuli increased in ‘Gofert’ from 11.80 ± 5.4 in the diploid to 16.15 ± 5.9 in the tetraploid and in ‘Polares’ from 16.43 ± 5.4 in the diploid to 26.47 ± 8.42 in the tetraploid.

Phenotype characteristics during flowering and fruiting

Induced tetraploidy in blackcurrant resulted in significant changes in reproductive traits and fruit characteristics. Tetraploid plants exhibited delayed flowering by 2–3 days and produced larger flowers with enlarged floral organs, including anthers and pistils (Table 2; Fig. 5A–F). Compared to diploids, the rachis was shorter in tetraploids, and the number of flowers per raceme was reduced by 14.5% in ‘Gofert’ and 7.72% in ‘Polares’ (Table 2; Fig. 5A, B). Pollen grains of tetraploid plants were approximately 22% larger in diameter than those in diploids. In diploids, most pollen was between 30 and 31.99 μm in length, whereas in tetraploids, it ranged from 36 to 39.99 μm (Fig. 5G, H). Although pollen of tetraploids exhibited reduced viability, with stainability nearly 10% lower and germination rates 35% lower than their diploid counterparts (Figs. 5I, J and S5), the proportion of germinated pollen grains remained high. Pollen of ‘Gofert’ tetraploids achieved a germination rate of approximately 50%, while it reached 60% in ‘Polares’ tetraploids.

Chromosomal distribution of 35 S and 5 S rDNA loci

Chromosome numbers were evaluated for both diploid and tetraploid R. nigrum plants (Fig. S2). In diploid ‘Polares’ and ‘Gofert’ , the somatic chromosome number was 2n = 2x = 16. Tetraploid plants had double the number of chromosomes (2n = 4x = 32). The chromosome counts showed uniformity across all examined cells. Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) was performed using 35S and 5S rDNA probes to localise ribosomal DNA sites and compare their distribution between diploid and tetraploid plants (Fig. 8). In diploid plants of both cultivars, two loci each of 35S and 5S rDNA were detected. The 35S rDNA loci were located at the telomeric positions on the short arms of one of the chromosomes, and the sizes of the in situ hybridisation signals were similar. 5S rDNA loci were located on the long arm of the same chromosome near the centromeres, but the signals differed in strength, with one major locus and one minor locus. In tetraploids, the number of 35S rDNA loci were doubled compared to that in diploid genotypes, and the signal strength and their position on chromosomes were stable in all analysed clones. In contrast, the 5S rDNA loci exhibited polymorphism, with variations in the number and intensity of loci ranging from 2 to 4 between clones.

Discussion

Polyploidisation has diverse and sometimes contrasting effects on plant growth, depending on the species, genomic stability, and physiological adjustment following WGD [40]. Previous studies have shown that genome duplication enhances the vigour, organ size, and biomass accumulation in certain plants, including Arabidopsis thaliana [41], Morus alba L [42]., and Oryza sativa L [43]. Conversely, in other polyploid species, such as Allium sativum L [44]., Luculia pinceana Hook [45]., Malus × domestica Borkh [16, 17]., and Citrus limonia Osbeck [46], it leads to growth reduction or dwarfism.

The induction of tetraploidy in R. nigrum cultivars ‘Polares’ and ‘Gofert’ caused alterations in growth dynamics and plant morphology. Compared to their diploid counterparts, tetraploid blackcurrants exhibited reduced vegetative growth, which was pronounced in ‘Polares’. This aligns with previous observations in juvenile blackcurrant plants [21, 29]. Several factors may underlie this growth reduction. First, despite the increase in cell size, the reduced growth rate could be attributed to a slower cell division rate [47, 48]. This slowdown in cell proliferation could limit organ lengthening and biomass accumulation and delay developmental processes, including flowering. Second, genome duplication often triggered transcriptomic and epigenetic reprogramming, including altered regulation of growth-related hormones [49, 50]. Altered hormonal balance, particularly decreased levels of active gibberellic acids, indoleacetic acid, and brassinosteroids, was associated with dwarf phenotypes in other polyploid species, including apple [51, 52]. Interestingly, the sixth growing season revealed an adaptation phase in which tetraploid blackcurrant plants demonstrated an increased annual growth rate, hinting at a complex interaction between genome duplication and growth regulation over time.

Chromosome doubling in R. nigrum resulted in characteristic polyploidy-induced changes in leaf morphology and anatomy. Tetraploid blackcurrant plants produced thicker leaves of a similar shape to diploids, with a reduced number of larger stomata, a thicker cuticle along the midrib, and larger mesophyll cells forming a more compact spongy parenchyma. This modification corresponded well with the ‘gigas’ effect commonly reported in polyploid plants, as cell and organ enlargement arise from the increase in the nuclear DNA content [53]. Similar structural enlargements were observed among species, including Citrus limonia [46] and Eucalyptus urophylla [54], as autotetraploids developed larger and thicker leaves. In contrast to our results, a smaller leaf area was observed in tetraploid Populus alba L [55]. and Citrullus lanatus [56]. WGD was often accompanied in other species by changes in leaf shape and morphology. Compared to diploids, changes in leaf shapes were observed in tetraploids of Malus × domestica Borkh [17]. and Luculia pinceana Hook [45]., and in the latter, leaf deformation occurred [45]. Our observation also revealed a difference in leaf morphological architecture between ploidy levels in regard to the frequency of secreting glands and the localisation of trichomes.

The inverse relationship between stomatal size and density recorded for autotetraploid blackcurrant is a common cytological consequence of genome duplication, as observed in polyploids of other species [17, 18, 54, 57, 58]. This inverse relationship is thought to be a compensatory mechanism that allows plants to maintain optimal gas exchange and water use efficiency, despite changes in cell size [59]. According to Allario et al. [46], the low density of large stomata optimise leaf stomatal conductance. In our study, this aligns with observations in tetraploid ‘Gofert,’ although no statistically significant differences in stomatal conductance were found in ‘Polares’ with different ploidy levels.

The anatomical features of tetraploid leaves in R. nigrum, such as increased thickness of the epidermis and parenchyma tissues, larger mesophyll cells, a more compact arrangement of the spongy parenchyma, and a thicker cuticle, are consistent with observations in other woody and herbaceous species [17, 42, 46]. This consistency across diverse plant species suggests a common adaptive response to environmental factors. The anatomical changes observed in blackcurrant leaves have significant implications for plant physiology. The thicker cuticle and enlarged mesophyll cells likely contribute to altered leaf rigidity, improved water balance, and enhanced light capture efficiency. Numerous studies have shown that thicker cuticles reduce leaf water loss [46, 60]. These adaptations may improve the plant’s resilience to environmental stressors by optimising water use and carbon assimilation. Additionally, anatomical reconfiguration may enhance the plant’s defence mechanisms against pathogens by creating more robust physical barriers [61].

A key finding of this study is the improved photosynthetic capacity in tetraploid blackcurrants, as evidenced by the higher chlorophyll content and enhanced photosynthetic parameters. The increased CCI and quantum efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm and Fv/Fo) suggest more efficient light harvesting and utilisation in tetraploids. These improvements, coupled with the anatomical changes in the leaf structure, likely contribute to the enhanced net photosynthetic rates observed in tetraploid ‘Gofert’. Similar enhancements in the photosynthetic efficiency have been reported in other polyploid crops and are often associated with increased biomass production and stress tolerance [20]. These physiological advantages likely stem from the combination of anatomical changes and modifications in the chloroplast structure. Chloroplasts serve as the primary site of photosynthesis in plants, as thylakoid membranes absorb, transport, and transform light energy during photosynthesis. Their structure therefore has a direct impact on photosynthesis and leaf characteristics [62]. Compared to their diploid counterparts, an increase in the number of chloroplasts without changes in size was described in tetraploid Echinacea prupurea [63] and Thymus vulgars L [64]., whereas in tetraploid Chrysanthemum nankingense, the size and density of chloroplasts were increased [65]. According to Warner and Edwards [66], the number and volume of chloroplasts were positively correlated with cell size.

In our study, analysis of the chloroplast ultrastructure revealed differences between ploidy levels, including less regular arrangement of the thylakoid lamellae, reduced starch accumulation, and an increased number of plastoglobuli in tetraploids. The absence of large starch grains in tetraploid chloroplasts indicates more efficient carbon export from chloroplasts or altered source–sink relationships. The increased number of plastoglobuli could be related to enhanced lipid metabolism or stress responses [67]. These changes in the chloroplast ultrastructure and function warrant further biochemical and molecular investigations to understand their implications for plant performance. A significant difference in the internal structure of chloroplasts was observed in tetraploid Lilium plants, which contained larger grana with a thicker, more regularly, and tightly arranged thylakoid lamellae than that in diploids. These chloroplast traits were correlated with a higher Pn and maximum net photosynthetic rate (Pmax) in tetraploid plants [68, 69]. Similarly, in tetraploid Acer buergerianum ‘Xingwang’, chloroplasts had a thicker thylakoid and more closely arranged lamellae than in diploids, while in the latter, more starch grains and plastoglobuli were observed [70]. Acer buergerianum tetraploids were characterised by a higher chlorophyll content, Fv/Fm, and Fv/Fo compared to diploids.

Given the intricate modifications in the chloroplast ultrastructure observed among tetraploid species, as outlined above, it is evident that these cellular-level changes translate into significant physiological advantages for polyploid plants. Alterations in the chloroplasts are crucial for enhancing photosynthetic processes, which reveal a comprehensive strategy aimed at optimising photosynthetic efficiency and water utilisation when considered alongside the anatomical changes observed in tetraploids, including increased leaf size and stomatal dimensions and decreased stomatal density. The enhanced CCI was another important factor that improved the photosynthetic parameters in tetraploid R. nigrum. The relationship between these anatomical alterations and photosynthetic performance aligns with findings in other polyploid species [68, 70, 71]. For instance, Populus davidiana tetraploids demonstrated significant improvements in chlorophyll fluorescence metrics, with Fv/Fm and the photochemical yield of PSII surpassing those of diploids by 1.08 and 2.5 times, respectively [72]. Similarly, tetraploids of Populus alba L [55]. and Luculia pinceana Hook [45] exhibited a superior photosynthetic capacity compared to their diploid counterparts. Parameters of gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence were also higher in tetraploids of apple ‘Hanfu’ [73]. Cao et al. [68] reported that tetraploid Lilium FO (L. × formolongi × Oriental hybrid) hybrids exhibited a higher Pn and Pmax than diploid plants. These findings collectively suggest that polyploidisation may confer advantages to photosynthetic efficiency across diverse plant species, potentially through similar mechanisms of anatomical and physiological adaptations.

Genome duplication significantly affected the reproductive biology of R. nigrum. Tetraploid plants flowered later than diploids and produced larger individual flowers, but exhibited fewer flowers per inflorescence and a lower flowering intensity. In addition, pollen viability was significantly reduced, and fruit was of a comparable size but contained fewer seeds, resulting in a lower total yield per plant.

These reproductive changes align with observations in other polyploid species. For instance, delayed flowering has been reported in tetraploid Malus × domestica [17, 73], Hemerocallis sp [74]., and Tulipa gesneriana [75], which could be attributed to altered developmental gene expression following genome duplication [76].

The larger flower size in tetraploid blackcurrant is consistent with the ‘gigas’ effect characteristic of polyploids, in which the increased cell volume contributes to organ enlargement [53]. Similar to our results, flowers and pollen were larger in tetraploid blackberries [77] and apples [17] than those in diploid plants. However, this structural enhancement was accompanied by decreased pollen fertility, which may be attributed to various factors, including chromosomal abnormalities during meiosis and alterations in gene expression due to the doubled chromosome set. Meiotic chromosome behaviour was not directly examined in this study. Therefore, the cytogenetic mechanisms underlying the observed reproductive impairment cannot be conclusively determined. In newly induced autotetraploids, reduced fertility is frequently associated with irregular chromosome pairing during meiosis, including multivalent formation, univalent chromosomes, lagging chromosomes, and chromosome mis-segregation at metaphase I and anaphase I, which result in the production of unbalanced gametes [78, 79]. Such meiotic irregularities have been widely reported as a primary cause of reduced pollen viability and seed set in neopolyploid plants. While the reduced reproductive performance observed in tetraploid blackcurrant is consistent with these general patterns, the absence of direct meiotic cytological analyses precludes definitive attribution of fertility reduction to specific meiotic abnormalities. Future studies incorporating detailed meiotic chromosome pairing analyses at metaphase I and II will be essential to elucidate the cytogenetic basis of reproductive impairment in autotetraploid R. nigrum.

Reduced fertility in newly formed autotetraploids is not consistent. For example, in tetraploid Luculia pinceana Hook., pollen fertility was higher than that observed in diploid plants [45].

In the present study, the comparable fruit size and weight but reduced seed number in tetraploid blackcurrant suggest that fruit development proceeded normally despite compromised fertilisation efficiency. According to Kaldmäe et al. [80], there is a positive correlation between fruit weight and the number of seeds and poor pollination in blackcurrants, leading to fewer seeds, which can restrict berry size. However, it seems that the number of seeds is only one of several factors influencing the final fruit size since a high number of seeds does not necessarily result in larger berries [81]. The drastic reduction in fruit yield observed in blackcurrant tetraploids is a major concern from an agronomic perspective. This is likely due to a combination of factors including the reduced number of flowers, lower pollen viability, potential issues with fertilisation or embryo development, and diminished seed set. Fewer seeds per fruit in blackcurrant tetraploids could be seen as a positive trait for consumer preference but may affect the plant’s reproductive potential and genetic diversity in subsequent generations. Reduced reproductive success, as evidenced by lower pollen viability and seed set, presents a challenge commonly observed in newly induced polyploids across various species [17, 75, 82].

From an agronomic perspective, these findings highlight a common trade-off associated with the production of autotetraploids. Although vegetative vigour and photosynthetic potential may increase, reproductive efficiency and yield can decline due to developmental and meiotic disturbances. Nevertheless, such tetraploid forms may still be valuable as breeding materials, particularly for developing triploid, seedless, or parthenocarpic blackcurrant cultivars, as demonstrated in other crops.

Cytogenetic analysis of rDNA loci provided additional insight into the genomic consequences of polyploidisation in R. nigrum. FISH revealed a doubling of 35S rDNA loci in tetraploids, consistent with complete genome duplication, whereas the number and intensity of 5S rDNA signals were more variable. This polymorphism aligns with observations in other polyploid species, indicating a broader genomic response to polyploidisation [83, 84]. Variability in the tandem repeats of rDNA, which is reflected in fluctuating FISH signal strengths and loci numbers among individuals, may suggest differential amplification, partial silencing, or chromosomal rearrangements affecting 5S rDNA regions during post-duplication genomic stabilisation [85–87]. Such variation, potentially culminating in the loss of the 5S rDNA locus due to a decrease in copy number below FISH detection thresholds [88], emphasises the dynamic nature of rDNA loci in polyploids [89, 90]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying rDNA reorganization in tetraploid R. nigrum cannot be resolved based on the present data. Complementary quantitative approaches, including qPCR-based estimation of rDNA copy number and analyses of rRNA gene expression, will be required to determine the functional significance of the observed cytogenetic patterns.

Importantly, an increased number of rDNA loci in polyploids does not necessarily translate into enhanced ribosomal biogenesis or altered gene expression, as rDNA activity is strongly influenced by epigenetic regulation, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin remodelling [91]. Therefore, any potential links between rDNA locus reorganization and plant growth or physiological performance remain speculative and should be addressed in future studies integrating cytogenetic, molecular, and transcriptional analyses.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that genome duplication in Ribes nigrum cultivars ‘Polares’ and ‘Gofert’ induces a broad range of morphological, anatomical, physiological, and reproductive changes. Tetraploid plants exhibited distinct leaf anatomical traits, including increased leaf thickness, enlarged stomata, and altered mesophyll organization, accompanied by higher chlorophyll content index and improved photosynthetic performance under the growth conditions examined. These features indicate substantial physiological reprogramming following whole-genome duplication. Despite these physiological and anatomical modifications, tetraploid blackcurrant plants showed reduced vegetative growth and markedly impaired reproductive performance, including delayed flowering, lower pollen viability, reduced seed set, and significantly decreased fruit yield. These findings indicate that genome duplication imposes both potential advantages and constraints on plant performance.

Although the observed reductions in fertility are consistent with reproductive disturbances commonly reported in newly induced autotetraploids, the cytogenetic mechanisms underlying these effects cannot be conclusively determined in the absence of meiotic chromosome behaviour analyses. Consequently, interpretations regarding the causes of reproductive impairment remain preliminary and require confirmation through future studies incorporating detailed meiotic cytology.

The observed leaf anatomical traits and photosynthetic characteristics may influence plant responses to environmental conditions; however, their functional significance in conferring enhanced abiotic stress tolerance was not directly tested in this study. Experimental validation under controlled stress conditions will be necessary to determine whether these traits translate into improved stress resilience in tetraploid R. nigrum.

From a breeding perspective, the results indicate that while induced tetraploidy can introduce potentially beneficial physiological traits, it may simultaneously compromise reproductive efficiency and yield. Therefore, tetraploid blackcurrant genotypes should be considered primarily as breeding materials rather than as immediate candidates for commercial cultivation. Future breeding strategies may benefit from utilizing tetraploid lines in hybridization programs aimed at generating triploid or seedless cultivars, combining desirable physiological traits with acceptable reproductive performance.
