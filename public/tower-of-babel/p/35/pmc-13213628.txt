Cost savings associated with the adoption of lower-dose everolimus in patients with advanced neuroendocrine tumors
Authors: G.R. de Almeida, R.P. Riechelmann, A.B. Brito, R.G. Taboada, A.L.M. da Silva, R.F. Weschenfelder, A.P.B.S. Etges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEverolimus is effective in advanced neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) but is associated with substantial toxicity and high long-term costs. Real-world data suggest that lower doses (≤6 mg/day) achieve comparable efficacy with reduced toxicity compared with standard-dose therapy. This study aimed to quantify the cost savings associated with a lower-dose everolimus strategy in a middle-income country setting.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective microcosting analysis from the Brazilian private provider perspective using patient-level data from two referral centers. Adults with advanced, well-differentiated NET who received everolimus were stratified by mean daily dose: high-dose (HD, >6 mg; n = 61) or low-dose (LD, ≤6 mg; n = 14). Direct medical costs comprised everolimus acquisition (2024 institutional acquisition cost, excluding margins) and toxicity-related hospitalizations. Costs were calculated in Brazilian reais (R$) and converted to US dollars (US$) (2024 average R$5.39 = US$1). The primary outcome was monthly cost. Both groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Multivariable γ-log regression with heteroskedasticity-consistent-robust standard errors was used to estimate the adjusted cost ratio.ResultsSeventy-five patients were included (HD = 61; LD = 14). The mean length of time using everolimus was 41 months (HD) and 35 months (LD). Drug acquisition accounted for 91% of total expenditure, while hospitalizations accounted for 9%. Direct mean monthly cost was US$2696 [standard deviation (SD) 2032] in the HD group versus US$1558 (SD 144) in the LD group (42% lower; P < 0.001). After adjustment for age, NET grade, primary site, line of therapy, and concurrent somatostatin analogue use, HD remained independently associated with an 88% higher monthly cost [adjusted cost ratio 1.88, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.27-2.70, P = 0.002]. A sensitivity analysis excluding two influential HD outliers yielded a conservative estimate of 54% higher monthly costs (cost ratio 1.54, 95% CI 1.35-1.77, P < 0.001) compared with the LD group.ConclusionIn a real-world Brazilian cohort, with previous evidence suggesting no compromise in efficacy, lower-dose everolimus (≤6 mg/day) was associated with a potential cost saving of US$1137 per patient-month in patients with advanced NET, reflecting a 46% decrease in cost. Deliberate dose optimization strategies can substantially improve the affordability and sustainability of NET care, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are heterogeneous neoplasms that arise from neuroendocrine cells, most commonly within the gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) tract. Although prognosis varies according to tumor stage, NET grade, and site of origin, among other factors, many patients with advanced disease live for several years and thus require sequential therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, results from several randomized clinical trials conducted in the last few decades have led to many drug approvals for this group of diseases. Such scientific advances have improved patients’ outcomes but are associated with incremental costs for patients and health care systems alike.

The economic burden of patients with advanced NETs is considerable and multifactorial. Cost-of-illness studies describe persistent spending driven by medications, surveillance, hospitalizations, and management of carcinoid syndrome complications.1, 2, 3, 4 Given the typically indolent course and prolonged survival of many patients, cumulative treatment costs can be substantial. For example, a population-based analysis of 3827 patients with NETs in Canada showed that mean per-patient costs increased across phases of care, from CA\$5601 (prediagnostic), CA\$26 756 (diagnostic), and CA\$27 282 (postdiagnostic) to CA\$59 075 annually in the prolonged postdiagnostic (PPostDx) phase.5 Notably, when compared with colon cancer controls, before the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors for microsatellite unstable tumors, costs in the PPostDx phase were significantly higher for patients with NETs than for patients with colon cancer. The primary driver of this difference was drug costs, accounting for 41% of total cost in NET during the PPostDx phase versus only 16% in colon cancer (P = 0.011).

To reduce financial toxicity, dose optimization strategies are increasingly needed in NETs, and some have been proposed. For example, in pancreatic NETs, capecitabine and temozolamide at 150 mg/m2 appears comparable to 200 mg/m2, potentially lowering long-term risk of dysplastic syndrome, and for cabozantinib, recognition that nearly two-thirds of patients required dose reductions in a phase III trial has prompted a clinical trial on a start-low, adapt-to-tolerability strategy (NCT05263050).6

Everolimus, an oral inhibitor of the mammalian target of rapamycin, is an effective option in second or later lines for patients with advanced NETs. The pivotal placebo-controlled RADIANT 4 trial showed that 10 mg once daily significantly improved progression-free survival (PFS) in nonfunctional gastrointestinal or lung NETs, with a median of 11 versus 3.9 months. Yet, toxicity incurred by everolimus is substantial. Grade 3/4 adverse events prompted dose reductions or interruptions in 60%-67% of patients with NET in phase III trials,7, 8, 9 with discontinuations in 12%-13% of patients. For real-world patients, rates of serious adverse events appear to be even higher. A retrospective study of 122 patients reported an incidence of everolimus-induced pneumonitis of 23%, compared with 12%-17% in the pivotal clinical trials.10 In another study led by our group, 111 patients with NET were followed up for a median of 33 months, 22% experienced grade 3-4 infections, and 3.6% of patients died from infections.11 Previously, our group reported a multicenter retrospective cohort comparing everolimus at a mean daily dose of >6 mg [high-dose (HD)] versus ≤6 mg (low-dose), with no significant difference in time to treatment failure [adjusted hazard-ratio 1.24, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.68-2.25], suggesting that lower daily doses may preserve effectiveness with potential less toxicity.12

From an economic perspective, standard-dose (10 mg/day) everolimus has been deemed cost-effective in some high-income countries. In the United States, it yielded an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of US dollars (US$41 702) per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) versus sunitinib for pancreatic NET, and in Canada, the ICER was Canadian dollars (CA$145 670/QALY) for gastrointestinal or lung NET relative to best supportive care (BSC).13,14 In the UK, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends everolimus for GEP or lung NET, as its cost-effectiveness falls within the £20 000-30 000/QALY threshold for routine commissioning.15 Nevertheless, a UK cost-utility analysis concluded that everolimus at list price is unlikely to be cost-effective across multiple indications when compared indirectly with sunitinib, 177Lu-DOTATATE, or BSC, largely because confidential discounts available to NICE were not reflected in this analysis.16

Despite these findings, cost-effectiveness evidence from low- and middle-income countries remains scarce, and the high acquisition cost of the 10 mg/day regimen remains a significant barrier to access in resource-limited settings. A lower-dose strategy (mean dose of ≤6 mg/day) may improve affordability and access in these settings.12 Because no study has yet quantified the economic impact of dose reduction in advanced NETs, we aimed to estimate the potential cost savings of adopting a lower dose of everolimus, providing pharmacoeconomic evidence relevant for both high-income and resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design

We carried out a retrospective microcosting analysis of direct medical costs from the perspective of a Brazilian private health care provider using patient-level data from two referral centers: A.C. Camargo Cancer Center (São Paulo) and Hospital Moinhos de Vento (Porto Alegre). The study was approved by the ethics committees of both institutions. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to minimal risk and the use of de-identified data. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable local regulations.

Participants

Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with histologically confirmed, unresectable locally advanced or metastatic, well-differentiated NETs (GEP, lung, or unknown primary) who received ≥1 dose of everolimus between August 2011 and September 2023 to treat progressive disease.12 Mixed neuroendocrine and nonneuroendocrine neoplasms were excluded. Concomitant somatostatin analogs for functioning syndrome were permitted. Patients were classified by higher mean daily dose (HD, >6 mg) and low-dose (LD, ≤6 mg). The rationale for these two dose groups has been detailed previously12; in summary, it is based on phase I clinical trials and retrospective studies.17, 18, 19 Considering that many patients who start at 10 mg daily will require dose reductions, we stratified the dose groups according to the mean daily dose, instead of categorizing patients based on the starting dose. Patients with missing or incomplete data were excluded.

Cost analysis

Costs were estimated from the private provider perspective. To ensure internal consistency of unit costs, data were sourced from a single hospital’s cost accounting system, representative of private hospitals serving insured patients at both centers. For each patient, the cost of everolimus was calculated by multiplying the treatment duration in days, the mean dose in mg, and the mean acquisition cost for 10- and 5-mg tablets, excluding profit margins. The hospitalization costs were calculated by multiplying each patient's length of stay in the ward and ICU by the respective cost per day for each unit. Daily unit costs included clinical staff, depreciation, taxes, general material, and others, divided by bed-time capacity, assuming 24 h/day and 20% expected idleness based on the hospital’s 2024 mean idleness and national microcosting guidance.20,21 All costs were calculated in Brazilian reais (R$) and converted to US$ for comparability using the 2024 average exchange rate (R$5.39 per US$1), according to data from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis The primary economic outcomes were total cost over the on-treatment period and the monthly mean cost (total cost divided by the number of months on therapy). Costs were summarized separately for the LD and HD groups. Outpatient costs (emergency visits without admission, supportive medications, laboratory tests, and clinic visits) were not captured.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean [standard deviation (SD)] or median [interquartile range (IQR)], and categorical variables as counts and percentages (%). Baseline characteristics between LD and HD groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney U) test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test (when expected cell counts were <5) for categorical variables. Total and monthly treatment-related costs were compared between dose groups using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test due to right-skewed distributions and the presence of extreme values.

The primary analysis of monthly cost was carried out using a multivariable generalized linear model with the γ family and log link. We used a γ distribution because treatment costs are strictly nonnegative and positively skewed, making the γ model appropriate and consistent with established health-economic guidelines. Predictors included dose group (LD ≤ 6 mg versus HD > 6 mg), age (z-scored), tumor grade (G1/G2 versus G3), primary site (pancreatic versus nonpancreatic gastrointestinal/other), and concurrent somatostatin analog use (yes/no). Model fit was assessed via Akaike information criterion (AIC), dispersion parameter, residual plots, and Cook distance. Collinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Heteroskedasticity-robust heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors were applied to ensure valid inference. A sensitivity analysis excluding influential cases identified on diagnostic plots was carried out. All tests were two-sided, and P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were carried out using R Studio (Waltham , MA) v2024.04.2+764.

Results

Of these, 61 (81%) received a mean daily dose >6 mg (HD), and 14 (19%) received ≤6 mg (LD). The majority had grade 2 tumors (overall 84%; 87% in HD versus 79% in LD). Patients in the LD group were significantly older than those in the HD group (median 71.5 versus 53.0 years, P = 0.023). Other baseline characteristics were well balanced (Table 1). The mean time on therapy was 41.39 months in the HD group and 34.81 months in the LD group. Discussion

In this multicenter microcosting study, a lower-dose strategy for everolimus in advanced NETs was associated with lower per-patient monthly costs (US$1558 versus US$2696), saving US$1137 per patient per month. The direct drug acquisition cost accounted for, on average, 91% of spend, whereas hospitalizations accounted for only 9% in both groups. Although patients in the LD group were significantly older (median 71.5 versus 53 years), age was not a significant cost-driver in multivariable analysis. Only HD independently predicted greater monthly cost, yielding 54%-88% higher adjusted cost.

Our cost-minimization analysis complements two published cost-effectiveness evaluations of everolimus. In the United States, a semi-Markov model comparing everolimus with sunitinib for pancreatic NET yielded an ICER of US$41 702 per QALY, driven primarily by differences in drug and medical resource costs between groups.13 A Canadian model evaluating everolimus versus BSC in patients with gastrointestinal or lung NET reported an ICER of CA$145 670 per QALY (incremental 0.616 QALYs, CA$89 795), with dose intensity and unit price among the most influential parameters in sensitivity analysis.14 Using the RADIANT-4 dose intensity (79%) reduced the ICER to ∼CA$117 000 per QALY, underscoring the impact of lower doses on cost-effectiveness.

This microcosting analysis quantifies these savings in a Latin American private provider setting with linear tablet pricing (5- and 10-mg tablets priced proportionally), demonstrating a 54%-88% increase in monthly cost with HD after adjustment. In jurisdictions with flat tablet pricing (as in the Canadian analysis), absolute per-mg savings may be smaller, but reduced dose intensity still yields a meaningful budget impact. In our study, the cost differential was driven predominantly by drug acquisition, consistent with both prior models, where drug cost accounted for most total expenditures. Although we captured only inpatient toxicity costs, lower doses likely reduce additional outpatient resource use—including clinic visits, laboratory monitoring, and supportive medications—which were not measured in this analysis. Our finding may be useful and potentially applicable to countries where everolimus price is also based on dosage, such as the United States, China, and Malaysia.

Cost-effectiveness analyses of everolimus have yielded conflicting conclusions depending on willingness-to-pay thresholds and whether confidential discounts were incorporated. Using list prices only, the 2018 PenTAG review for NICE reported ICERs of ∼£45 000/QALY for pancreatic and gastrointestinal/lung NETs, and ∼£199 000/QALY for midgut NETs (the latter highly uncertain due to limited subgroup data), all versus BSC. At these list-price ICERs, everolimus would not be considered cost-effective under NICE’s £20 000-£30 000/QALY threshold range.16 Nevertheless, NICE recommends everolimus for routine commissioning, enabled by patient-access discounts that reduced the effective ICERs to within acceptable limits.15 Our data provide direct pharmacoeconomic evidence that dose reductions might be a pragmatic solution, potentially without compromising outcomes.

Our study had some limitations inherent to retrospective designs: selection bias (some patients in the LD group were older or frailer) and modest sample size (especially in the LD group), which limits precision. Our perspective was a private provider and included only drug and hospitalization costs, excluding outpatient toxicity management, emergency visits without admission, imaging, and productivity, which likely underestimates total savings from LD. Prices excluded profit margins and reflect a single market; generalizability to public payers or countries with different rules (e.g. flat tablet pricing) requires caution. Finally, our economic outcomes focused on costs and budgetary impact.

Despite the appealing results of observation dose optimization studies, results from randomized clinical trials are warranted to confirm that LD strategies with everolimus or other cancer-directed therapies can be implemented in patients. Our group has initiated the EVENET trial (NCT06472388), a multicenter randomized near-equivalence phase II study comparing 10 versus 5 mg daily in patients with pretreated grade 1/2 GEP or lung NET, with PFS rate at 12 months as the primary endpoint. This trial is enrolling patients from Brazil, Chile, and India. By embedding real-world dose distributions from this cohort into a future cost-effectiveness analysis, it will be possible to generate robust ICER estimates and inform health policy in resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion

In this real-world Brazilian microcosting analysis, the adoption of an LD of everolimus can result in a cost saving of US$1137 per patient-month in advanced NET, driven predominantly by drug acquisition (91% of expenditure). Prospective validation via the ongoing EVENET randomized trial (NCT06472388) of 5 versus 10 mg daily everolimus for patients with NETs and future cost-utility modeling incorporating these dose distributions are warranted to guide policy, enhance access, and promote sustainable care for patients with NET.
