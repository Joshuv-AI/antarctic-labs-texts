Positioning Community Pharmacies in Nigeria as a Clinical Trial Partner: A Forward Perspective
Authors: Adeboye O Bamgboye, Ibrahim A Hassan, Babatunde M Jinadu, Luke A Alade, Tobechi B Nnanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDecentralized clinical trials (DCTs) employ innovative approaches to conduct research outside traditional clinical sites. Low‐ and middle‐income countries like Nigeria, with limited infrastructure, insufficient manpower, and inadequate financial support for trials, can benefit from DCTs. By leveraging non‐traditional infrastructure, such as community pharmacies, DCT approaches could overcome some infrastructural and recruitment challenges common in clinical trials in African countries. The extensive community pharmacy network in Nigeria could be strategically positioned as partners in DCT efforts.Keywords: clinical trials, community pharmacy, decentralized trials, Nigeria, research infrastructure

Overview

Africa suffers from a double burden of communicable and non‐communicable diseases. Despite the high disease burden and the large number of individuals who would benefit from new therapeutics, less than 4% of global clinical trials are conducted on the continent [1]. Furthermore, the push to expand personalized medicine research underscores the need for tailored clinical studies in African populations. However, despite ongoing efforts to promote diversity in clinical trials, persistent barriers continue to limit African representation in these trials.

Clinical trials are resource‐intensive, presenting challenges for resource‐constrained settings typical of many African countries. The median cost of a clinical trial for new therapeutics is $19 million [2]. Manpower shortages, rigid regulatory frameworks, poor infrastructure, and patient recruitment challenges are additional bottlenecks to improving clinical trials in most African countries.

Decentralized clinical trials (DCTs) are an emerging trial paradigm that reduce the logistical constraints of the traditional model. DCTs include trials that employ digital tools like e‐consent, telemedicine, remote monitoring, and care in local settings to reduce the burden of participation [3]. The DCT model affords broader access to diverse patient populations, reduces travel time and costs, and is suitable for Phase II, III, & IV studies involving large populations. DCTs are effective for chronic disease‐related trials where patient mobility may be a concern, such as neuromuscular conditions; where logistical barriers are present, such as with rare disorders; where telemedicine services are established, such as in diabetes; or in low‐risk investigational device studies, such as those using wearable biosensors [3].

Nigeria (and other African countries), with limited infrastructural and financial resources for traditional clinical trials, could benefit from investing in DCTs. Decentralized approaches that leverage non‐traditional health establishments can help address infrastructure and patient accessibility challenges. The community pharmacy network in Nigeria, including approximately 5600 registered retail pharmacies [4], could be positioned as DCT partners. In this perspective, we examine several considerations for positioning community pharmacies (CPs) as key players in Nigeria's evolving trial landscape. Why Integrate Nigerian Community Pharmacies in DCTs?

CPs in Nigeria have an established track record of delivering decentralized clinical and public health interventions. Nigerian CPs have contributed to the successful implementation and penetration of contraceptive services, differentiated antiretroviral therapy services, routine immunization [4], mental health services, and tuberculosis case‐finding. These contributions have been possible because CPs are highly accessible and trusted. A study found that 71% of respondents turned to CPs first for minor health issues or questions about medicines [5].

Additionally, pharmacists are well‐trained, knowledgeable about clinical studies, and embrace emerging digital health technologies [6]. Their competencies in patient‐facing, operational, and regulatory compliance duties, leveraged in previous expanded service roles, map directly to the transferable duties of traditional clinical trial pharmacists. Hence, CPs can serve as DCT nodes or micro‐sites in a “hub and spoke” model, with individual CPs as spokes and a tertiary hospital as the central hub.

CPs within a DCT model can provide services such as patient screening and recruitment, sample collection, handling and dispensing investigational drugs, data management, and safety reporting. Positioning CPs in this way improves reach to underserved and rural populations, boosting recruitment and representation. Community pharmacists interact with diverse patients, enabling them to identify individuals who may meet inclusion criteria for DCT‐applicable studies. Furthermore, through initial screening, community pharmacies can reduce enrollment burdens. A study by Lal et al. [7] showed that willing Australian CPs can be trained to function as alternatives to traditional clinical trial pharmacies. In their study, which compared CPs to conventional sites for HIV pre‐exposure prophylaxis delivery, CPs accounted for 83% of the total dispensing volume and recorded a 2% error rate. These findings highlight the potential of CPs in DCTs.

Given Nigeria's rapidly expanding community pharmacy infrastructure and significant growth in IT, the CP‐DCT model offers a valuable option to enhance clinical trial participation.

Considerations for Partnering With CPs in a DCT Model in Nigeria

The Need for a Regulatory Framework Expansion for DCT

Regulatory compliance is essential to maintain ethical standards, protect participant rights, and produce credible data [8]. Nigeria's existing regulatory framework was designed for conventional, site‐based research and does not adequately address the technological, ethical, and data governance requirements of decentralized models.

Several national bodies, such as the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC), the Pharmacy Council of Nigeria (PCN), the National Health Research Ethics Committee (NHREC), the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA), the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN), and the Federal Ministry of Health (FMoH), oversee various aspects of clinical trial conduct. However, none of these agencies have DCT‐specific policies, leaving regulatory gaps in remote trial, digital tools, participant monitoring, and data protection. While each regulatory body has unique responsibilities, the growing complexity of clinical trials requires a more collaborative approach for oversight. PCN and MDCN can advise on patient safety, trial feasibility, and professional training required for trial clinicians. NHREC, in collaboration with NAFDAC, can provide guidance on the conduct of DCTs, including approval procedures, while FMoH, in collaboration with NPHCDA, can advise on the priority areas for research to ensure that trial efforts are consistent with national policy goals while creating an environment that allows for the revision of health policies based on trial results.

While NAFDAC regulates investigational products for safety and quality, the current framework is insufficient for DCT oversight. Updated regulations must also consider remote drug delivery, home storage, telepharmacy counseling, and the use of digital devices for data capture. Similarly, PCN should develop a competency framework for pharmacists in DCTs, outlining training requirements, permissible activities, documentation standards, and the necessary technology and infrastructure for pharmacies operating as DCT nodes.

As Nigeria's ethics authority, NHREC faces new challenges in evaluating DCT protocols. Digital literacy, telemedicine, and electronic consent require updated review processes. Individuals with limited access to technology risk exclusion from DCTs. NHREC must therefore provide updated guidance on digital education and access, remote safety monitoring, and inclusive recruitment strategies.

Pharmaceutical companies and Contract Research Organizations (CROs) remain central stakeholders. Their involvement in remote monitoring, digital data collection, and technology‐enabled operations highlights the need for clear regulations to ensure transparency and data quality.

Data governance within the IT infrastructure is a critical priority for DCT regulation. DCTs rely on distributed digital platforms, which introduce new risks. Robust data‐governance frameworks that cover data flow mapping, access control, reconciliation, and secure storage are essential to safeguard participant privacy and maintain the integrity of trial data.

Patient safety is another crucial consideration. Safety monitoring plans should be developed [3] to address adverse event (AE) reporting channels from CPs to physicians, as well as minimum reporting requirements for AEs according to trial protocols and regulatory guidelines.

Training Needs

Clear benchmarks are required to define the qualifications needed for community pharmacists to participate in DCTs. The Joint Task Force for Clinical Trial Competency (JTF) is a multidisciplinary group of professionals that defines and refines competency standards for clinical trial professionals [9]. The most recent JTF framework includes eight competency domains and 47 competency statements [9]. The competency domains include scientific concepts and designs; ethical and participant safety considerations; investigational product development and regulation; clinical study operations; study site management; data management and informatics; leadership and professionalism; and communications and teamwork.

Pharmacy training in Nigeria already develops many of the competencies required for clinical trial professionals. Further training in clinical trial conduct could be integrated into the Mandatory Continuing Professional Development (MCPD) program administered by the Pharmacy Council of Nigeria. The specific training needs will depend on the regulatory framework established for community pharmacy involvement in DCTs.

The scope of additional training requires consultation with other stakeholders, including NAFDAC, NHREC, patient representatives, the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN), and clinical trial sponsors. The JTF Framework [9] can enhance clinical trial education in pharmacy curricula and the MCPD program. Physician‐pharmacist collaboration is crucial to ensure comprehensive safety oversight, greater trust from sponsors, and improved care transitions when needed.

Remuneration

Community pharmacies engaged in DCT services require equitable and transparent compensation structures that recognize their contributions. However, developing fair compensation networks for CPs in Nigeria is challenging because there is no standardized remuneration system for clinical pharmacy services in the community setting. Funding from the sponsors or contract research organizations (CROs) that flow to the trial pharmacists is the most practical option.

Alternative funding sources include Public‐Private Partnerships (PPPs) through the Ministry of Health, NAFDAC, and international funders, as well as academic or hospital co‐funding. These mechanisms could create a more sustainable funding structure for pharmacists' involvement in DCTs.

Furthermore, since some trial‐related tasks, including documentation, measurement of vital signs, and handling of medications, are currently performed by community pharmacists in Nigeria [6], CPs could update the job descriptions and remuneration of hired pharmacists to account for time spent on clinical trial‐related work. Recruitment‐based incentives could cause undue pressure to recruit participants and should generally be discouraged. Furthermore, pharmacists obtaining informed consent from their patients should be restricted to prevent coercion. Protocols must include safeguards to ensure that participants are well‐informed about trials before consenting.

Community Buy‐In Considerations

Community buy‐in will likely depend on trust and clear benefits. Incorporating research into community pharmacies requires patient education and robust safeguards to ensure continued trust. DCT advocates emphasize that ethical oversight, data privacy protections, and stakeholder training are crucial for maintaining confidence [10]. Furthermore, framing trial participation as an extension of care and aligning with public health goals could further legitimize pharmacies as research partners and encourage better participation.
