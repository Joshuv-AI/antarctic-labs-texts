Targeting SPP1+TAMs associated with liver metastasis reverses immunosuppression and synergizes with immunotherapy in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yilin Lin, Zhihua Chen, Shidong Zhao, Long Zhao, Changjiang Yang, Mingxuan Zhu, Yingjiang Ye, Shan Wang, Zhanlong Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are critically involved in colorectal cancer (CRC) progression, yet their spatial and metabolic heterogeneity across primary and metastatic microenvironments remains poorly defined, limiting therapeutic development.MethodsIntegrated single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on 998,204 cells from a multicenter cohort encompassing primary CRC, adjacent normal tissue, liver and lymph node metastases, and peripheral blood. Computational analyses included uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering, pseudotime inference, RNA velocity, and CellChat. Findings were validated using multiplex immunohistochemistry, spatial transcriptomics, and in vivo preclinical models.ResultsThis study identified two distinct TAM populations: SPP1+ and SEPP1+ TAMs. SPP1+TAMs, associated with angiogenesis, immune evasion, and liver metastasis, were linked to poorer prognosis. This population exhibited tissue-specific differentiation trajectories and comprised two metabolic subtypes: a glycolytic ATP5F1E+ subtype enriched in primary tumors and lymph nodes, and an oxidative phosphorylation-dominant MT-CO1+subtype specific to liver metastases. Spatially, MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs localized to oxygen-rich invasive margins, while ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs resided in hypoxic tumor cores. Functionally, SPP1+TAMs drove CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and spatial exclusion. In preclinical models, combined regorafenib and anti-programmed cell death protein 1 therapy reduced SPP1+TAM infiltration and suppressed tumor growth and metastasis.ConclusionThis study delineates the spatial, developmental, and metabolic heterogeneity of SPP1+TAMs in CRC, identifying SPP1+TAMs as key mediators of immunosuppression and metastasis. Targeting these populations with combination therapy effectively enhances antitumor immunity, presenting a novel translational strategy for CRC immunotherapy.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Macrophage, Colorectal Cancer

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in colorectal cancer (CRC) are highly heterogeneous and critically shape tumor progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance. SPP1+TAMs have been implicated as a protumorigenic subset, but their tissue-specific distribution, developmental origins, and metabolic features across primary and metastatic sites remain poorly defined.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study integrates single-cell data from 420 samples spanning primary CRC, matched normal tissues, liver metastases, normal liver, lymph nodes, and blood, and identifies SPP1+TAMs as a tissue-enriched subset associated with liver metastasis. It delineates tissue-specific differentiation trajectories and metabolic subtypes (ATP5F1E+ and MT-CO1+) of SPP1+TAMs with distinct spatial localizations and metabolic programs. In vivo, it demonstrates that regorafenib reduces SPP1+TAM infiltration and enhances anti-programmed cell death protein 1 efficacy.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignant tumor globally, with 1.93 million new cases and 900,000 deaths reported in 2022.1 Approximately 25% of patients present with synchronous liver metastases at initial diagnosis, while 30–50% of stage II–III patients develop metachronous liver metastases after surgery. Liver metastasis-related deaths account for over 60% of all CRC-related mortality.2 3 Despite advances in systemic chemotherapy, anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)/vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) targeted therapy, and hepatic resection techniques, the 5-year survival rate for patients with resectable liver metastases remains around 50%, dropping to less than 10% for those with unresectable disease.4 Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms driving liver metastasis and developing novel therapeutic strategies remains critical challenges in CRC research.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play a central role in the initiation, progression, and immune regulation of CRC liver metastasis.5 6 A high density of TAMs is frequently observed in liver metastatic lesions,7 where these cells secrete a variety of immunosuppressive and pro-angiogenic factors—such as vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), interleukin-10, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)—that promote tumor angiogenesis, remodel the extracellular matrix, and directly suppress the function of cytotoxic T cells.8 9 These mechanisms significantly accelerate metastatic progression and foster a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Notably, TAMs not only facilitate the colonization and growth of CRC liver metastases but also broadly modulate responses to various immunotherapeutic strategies.10

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown significant efficacy in microsatellite instability-high/mismatch repair-deficient (MSI-H/dMMR) CRC.11 In MSI-H/dMMR CRCs, TAMs may still impair treatment outcomes by upregulating immune checkpoint molecules and promoting T-cell exhaustion.12 13 In contrast, among patients with microsatellite-stable tumors—which account for more than 85% of all CRCs and exhibit primary resistance to ICBs—the immunosuppressive microenvironment mediated by TAMs is considered a key factor contributing to the lack of response to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 monotherapy.14 Substantial preclinical and clinical evidence indicates that high TAM infiltration is significantly associated with poorer responses to immunotherapy and worse patient survival outcomes.1015,17 Therefore, targeting TAMs and their related signaling pathways may be a promising strategy to overcome ICBs resistance and inhibit metastasis.

TAMs have long been simplified into two polarized states: the antitumor M1 phenotype and the protumor M2 phenotype.18 19 However, with the widespread application of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies, it has become evident that this traditional M1/M2 dichotomy fails to fully capture the high heterogeneity of TAMs within the TME.14 20 In recent years, multiple single-cell studies have progressively revealed functionally distinct TAM subpopulations. For instance, FOLR2+ resident-like macrophages are often co-localized with CD8+T cells and are generally associated with favorable prognosis,21 whereas SPP1+TAMs have been shown to significantly promote advanced metastasis and immunosuppression across various solid tumors.22 23 Additionally, other functionally diverse subsets have been identified, including TREM2+TAMs associated with angiogenesis,24 and IL1β+TAMs that highly express inflammatory factors.25 However, inconsistencies remain in the functional definitions of specific marker gene sets across different studies. These discrepancies may stem from the lack of uniform cell identification standards, biases introduced by limited sample sizes, as well as spatiotemporal dynamics within the TME, variations in individual immune status, and differences in cell clustering thresholds and bioinformatic methods employed across studies. These observations highlight the need for larger cohort studies to establish more robust classifications. Furthermore, most current single-cell studies fail to adequately account for the developmental and lineage heterogeneity of the same TAM subpopulation across different metastatic sites. In addition, due to the heavy reliance on cell dissociation protocols in many studies, spatial information is lost, resulting in insufficient characterization and quantification of spatial distribution and functional heterogeneity within microregions—even for the same subpopulation. Therefore, multi-omics integrative analysis, along with functional validation through in vivo and in vitro experiments, is essential to confirm TAM subpopulation classifications and establish causal relationships, thereby providing reliable targets and strategies for precise immunotherapy targeting specific TAM subsets.

This study seeks to characterize the spatiotemporal dynamics and differentiation trajectories of TAM subsets across primary colorectal tumors, liver metastases, and matched normal adjacent tissues. Furthermore, it aims to elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which SPP1+TAMs drive metastatic progression and foster an immunosuppressive TME. Finally, this study evaluates the impact of regorafenib in combination with PD-1 blockade on the abundance of SPP1+TAMs. These findings may reveal novel therapeutic targets and provide rational combination strategies for overcoming CRC metastasis and resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis of human tissues

Human scRNA-seq data were integrated from multiple sources, including previously published studies by our research group,14 work by Ignasius Joanito et al,26 and publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (accession numbers GSE205506, GSE225857, GSE164522, and GSE178341). A total of 420 samples were collected, including 248 CRC samples, 103 adjacent normal colorectal tissues (ANCT), 18 colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CLM) tissues, 18 adjacent normal liver tissues (ANLT), 18 metastatic lymph nodes (LN) and 15 peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples. Raw gene expression matrices were processed using the Scanpy toolkit (V.1.9.0) in Python. Low-quality cells were filtered based on the following criteria: cells with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 5% were excluded to remove potential apoptotic or damaged cells. Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes, or more than 6,000 genes were discarded to eliminate low-information droplets and multiplet captures. Genes detected in fewer than three cells were also removed. Doublet detection was performed using Scrublet (V.0.2.3) methods. Subsequent normalization and logarithmic transformation were applied to the raw counts. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using 30 principal components for dimensionality reduction. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony (V.0.1.0). Cell clusters were identified through Leiden clustering with a resolution parameter of 0.8. Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes derived from literature and database references.14

The number of observed cells and the expected number of cells in the tissue were analyzed by Ro/e.27 The Augur method was used to analyze the cell types that are most sensitive to biological disturbances in different tissue sample types.27 The scTour was used for the analysis of cell development trajectories.28 The rank_genes_groups function in Scanpy and the FindMarkers function in Seurat were employed to identify differentially expressed genes across cell subpopulations. A non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for intergroup comparisons. This analysis was restricted to genes expressed in at least 10% of cells within any given subpopulation to ensure the detected signals possessed sufficient biological prevalence. Differentially expressed genes were filtered using thresholds of an adjusted p value (Benjamini-Hochberg method) <0.05 and an absolute average log2 fold change (avg_log2FC) >0.25. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analyses were used to explore the potential functions of cell subpopulations based on the clusterProfiler package.29

Gene set enrichment analysis of cell subpopulations in bulk transcriptomic data

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data were obtained from the cancer genome atlas (TCGA) database (colon adenocarcinoma and rectum adenocarcinoma) and the GEO database under accession numbers GSE39582, GSE41568, GSE17356, GSE72968 and GSE41568. These datasets comprise transcriptomic profiles derived from CRC tissue samples. The normalized gene expression matrices were downloaded and processed for subsequent analysis. To estimate the relative abundance of specific immune cell subsets within the TME, single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA)30 was applied. Signature gene sets for each cell subpopulation were defined based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) derived from single-cell clustering analysis. DEGs were selected using a threshold of adjusted p value<0.05 and |log₂ fold change|>0.1. The analysis was performed using the ssGSEA method within the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) package in R, with default parameters and 1,000 permutations for significance testing.

Enrichment scores were compared across clinical subgroups—including tumor stage, metastatic status, MMR status, and molecular subtypes using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests, as appropriate. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Statistical analyses and visualization were conducted in R V.4.2.0.

Spatial transcriptome sequencing analysis of human colorectal cancer tissues

The raw VisiumHD dataset for three CRC samples (Accession: GSE280315) was retrieved from the GEO database. Data processing was performed using Scanpy in Python. The downloaded data, which included the filtered feature-barcode matrix, spatial coordinates of each barcode spot, and the accompanying high-resolution tissue image, were loaded into an AnnData object. Quality control was conducted by filtering out spots with an unusually low number of detected genes or a high proportion of mitochondrial transcript counts. Global expression normalization was applied to correct for library size variations across spots, followed by a logarithmic transformation. Highly variable genes were selected for downstream analysis.

Cell type annotation was conducted using a marker-based approach consistent with established scRNA-seq classifications for CRC.

The spatial distribution of each identified cell population was visualized directly on the spatial transcriptomics array. For each major cell type, the computed gene signature score was plotted over the spatial coordinate system of the VisiumHD slide using the sc.pl.spatial function. This allowed for the direct mapping of inferred cell type localization onto the original tissue capture areas.

Analysis of cell–cell communication

Cell–cell communication analysis was performed using the CellChat package (V.1.6.1) in R. Ligand-receptor interactions were inferred from established databases, and signaling pathways were analyzed to identify biologically significant communication networks between cell subtypes. Differential interaction strength and signaling flux were compared between experimental conditions using built-in permutation tests.

Construction of subcutaneous tumor models in mice

The colon adenocarcinoma cell line MC38 was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. On reaching 80% confluence, the cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed, and resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final concentration of 5×10⁶ cells per 200 µL for inoculation. Female C57BL/6 mice aged 8 weeks, maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions, were used in this study. To establish the subcutaneous tumor model, 5×10⁶ cells in 200 µL PBS were injected into the left flank of each mouse. 1 week post-inoculation (Day 7), mice with palpable tumors were randomly assigned to six treatment groups including NC, anti-CSF1R, anti-PD-1, anti-CSF1R combined with anti-PD-1, regorafenib, and regorafenib combined with anti-PD-1, with four mice per group. Anti-CSF1R (clone AFS98, Bio X Cell, #BE0213) and anti-PD-1 (clone RMP1-14, Bio X Cell, #BE0146) antibodies were diluted in PBS. Regorafenib (Bayer Pharma) was prepared in a vehicle consisting of 5% DMSO, 45% PEG300, and 50% saline. Treatments began on Day 7 and were administered every 3 days. The monoclonal antibodies were delivered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 200 µg per mouse in sterile PBS, while regorafenib was administered by oral gavage at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight. Tumor size was measured every 3 days using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume (mm³)=(length×width²)/2. The study was terminated when the tumor volume in any mouse reached the maximum permitted size of 1,000 mm³. The mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation. At the endpoint, all tumors were surgically resected, photographed for gross morphological documentation, and prepared for subsequent ex vivo analyses.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of human colorectal cancer tissues and subcutaneous tumors in mice

A retrospective cohort of 108 untreated primary CRC tissues and 92 matched adjacent normal tissues (collected at least 5 cm from the tumor margin) was analyzed in this study. All specimens were acquired from patients who underwent surgical resection at Peking University People’s Hospital between 2020 and 2024. No patient had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy prior to surgery. Among these, a subset of 32 primary tumor samples from patients with synchronous liver metastases was specifically examined. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University People’s Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Subcutaneous tumor models were established in mice, and tumors were excised after reaching a predefined volume. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of our institution.

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections from human and murine samples were sectioned at 4 µm thickness. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using the tyramide signal amplification technique. Primary antibodies, including anti-CD8A (Cat. No. ab316778, Abcam, USA), anti-CD68 (Cat. No. ab303565, Abcam, USA), anti-SEPP1 (Cat. No. GB114931, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), anti-SPP1 (Cat. No. ab283656, Abcam, USA), and anti-Pan-CK (Cat. No. GB122053, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), were applied at optimized dilutions ranging from 1:200 to 1:400. Following each round of staining, antibody complexes were efficiently removed by microwave-based antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 98°C for 15 min. This process effectively denatured and dissociated bound antibodies while preserving tissue antigenicity for subsequent staining cycles. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Cat. No. G1012, Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

All stained slides were digitized using a high-throughput fluorescence imaging system (PerkinElmer Vectra). Murine tissue images were visualized and preliminarily evaluated with CaseViewer (3DHistech, Hungary). Human tissue sections were quantitatively analyzed using TissueFAXS Viewer and associated software (TissueGnostics, Austria). Immune cell infiltration density and spatial distribution were assessed, and spatial proximity between specific immune cell subsets was measured to evaluate potential cell–cell interactions.

Establishment of a murine colorectal cancer liver metastasis model

MC38 cells were cultured under the above conditions and harvested when the cells reached a 70%–80% fusion degree. The cells were washed with PBS, resuspended at a concentration of 1×107 cells/mL, and 50 µL of the suspension was used for each intraperitoneal injection. 6–8 weeks old C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized with Avitan (15 µL/g, Promocor, China), and the cell suspension was slowly injected into the spleen sac using a syringe. After about 10 min of intraperitoneal injection, a partial splenectomy was performed. After confirming hemostasis, the abdominal wall and skin were sutured layer by layer with absorbable sutures for closure. The mice were placed on a heating pad until full recovery from anesthesia. Then, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (PBS group, regorafenib group, and regorafenib combined with anti-PD-1 group), with four mice in each group. The treatment was initiated 7 days after tumor cell injection, and the administration route and dose were the same as those used in the subcutaneous tumor model. The treatment was given once every 3 days. 1 month after the injection of tumor cells, the mice were euthanized under carbon dioxide anesthesia. The liver was collected for gross observation, H&E staining, and multiplex immunofluorescence staining.

H&E staining

Tissue specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. After deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol to distilled water, sections were stained with hematoxylin, rinsed in running tap water, differentiated, and blued according to standard procedures, and subsequently counterstained with eosin. Slides were then dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a resin-based mounting medium. H&E-stained sections were examined and imaged using a light microscope.

Flow cytometry analysis of subcutaneous tumor tissues in mice

Subcutaneous tumors were aseptically dissected from euthanized mice and mechanically dissociated. The resulting tissue fragments were digested in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium containing 1 mg/mL collagenase D (Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 U/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C with constant agitation. The digested cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer and treated with ACK lysing buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove red blood cells. The cells were then washed and resuspended in PBS, and the cell concentration was adjusted to 1×10⁷ cells/mL.

For surface marker staining, cells were incubated with the following fluorescently conjugated anti-mouse antibodies (all from BioLegend) at manufacturer-recommended concentrations for 30 min on ice in the dark: Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD45 (Clone 30-F11, Cat. No. 103138), FITC anti-mouse CD8a (Clone 53–6.7, Cat. No. 100706), PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse F4/80 (Clone BM8, Cat. No. 123114), and Brilliant Violet 605 anti-mouse/human CD11b (Clone M1/70, Cat. No. 101257). After staining, cells were washed two times with PBS.

For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, cells were stained with allophycocyanin (APC) anti-human/mouse granzyme B recombinant antibody (Clone QA16A02, Cat. No. 372204) and phycoerythrin (PE) anti-mouse interferon (IFN)-γ antibody (Clone XMG1.2, Cat. No. 505808; both from BioLegend) at recommended concentrations for 30 min on ice in the dark. Finally, the cells were washed and resuspended in PBS for acquisition on a BD flow cytometer. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (V.10.8.1).

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis of subcutaneous tumors in mice

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from dissociated subcutaneous tumor tissues as previously described for flow cytometry analysis. Briefly, tumors were digested using a solution of 1 mg/mL collagenase D (Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 U/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich), filtered through a 70 µm strainer, and treated with ACK lysing buffer to remove red blood cells. To enrich for immune cells, the resulting cells were stained with Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD45 antibody (BioLegend, Cat. No. 103138) and CD45+ cells were isolated using fluorescence-activated cell sorting.

ScRNA-seq was performed by LC-Biotechnology (Hangzhou, China) using the 10x Genomics platform. Raw sequencing data were processed with Cell Ranger (V.7.1.0) for alignment, barcode assignment, and Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) counting. Downstream analysis was conducted in Python using the Scanpy toolkit. Cells with fewer than 200 genes or more than 5,000 genes were filtered out. Mitochondrial gene content was restricted to <10%, and potential doublets were excluded based on UMI and gene count distributions. Normalization and log-transformation were performed followed by highly variable gene selection. PCA was conducted with 30 components, and batch effects were corrected using Harmony. Cell clusters were identified through Leiden clustering, and cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes described in previous literature. Cell–cell communication analysis was performed in R using the CellChat package (V.1.6.1). Ligand-receptor interaction databases and signaling pathways were analyzed to infer intercellular communication networks between major cell subtypes.

Statistical analysis

Tumor volume comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons in GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean±SEM. Differences in immune cell frequencies between groups were assessed using unpaired Student’s t-test (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA (for multiple groups). Non-parametric alternatives (Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis’s test) were applied when data did not meet normality assumptions. ssGSEA was implemented using the GSVA R package to evaluate enrichment scores of cell subpopulations. Differences in enrichment scores among groups were tested using the Kruskal-Wallis’s test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons. For bulk RNA-seq data involving three or more groups, differential expression was analyzed using DESeq2 with a generalized linear model. Post hoc comparisons between specific groups were performed using the Wald test with Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between gene expression levels and cell subpopulation enrichment scores. Correlation coefficients and corresponding p values were calculated, with multiple testing correction applied using the FDR method. The significance of the Ro/e score was evaluated through Fisher’s exact test.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (V.4.2.0), Python (V.3.9), and GraphPad Prism (V.10.0). A significance threshold of p<0.05 was used for all analyses unless otherwise specified.

Results

Single-cell landscape of the human colorectal cancer primary and metastatic immune microenvironment

A comprehensive scRNA-seq analysis across multiple tissue types from a multicohort dataset, including CRC, ANCT, CLM, ANLT, LN, and PBMC, to characterize the cellular heterogeneity of CRC and its metastatic microenvironment was performed (figure 1A). After quality control, filtering, and pre-processing, a total of 998,204 cells were retained. Using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for dimensionality reduction and unsupervised clustering, we successfully integrated the multitissue transcriptomic data and identified eight major cell populations (online supplemental figure S1A): B cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, myeloid cells, plasma cells, and T/natural killer (NK) cells.

The compositional distribution of these cell types across sample categories was displayed in a stacked bar plot (online supplemental figure S1B). Further dissection of cell subset proportions revealed elevated myeloid cell levels in CRC compared with ANCT. In contrast, CLM showed a lower proportion of myeloid cells relative to ANLT. Plasma cells were more abundant in ANCT than in CRC, potentially indicative of an active humoral immune response in normal intestinal mucosa. Additionally, fibroblasts were markedly enriched in liver metastases compared with normal liver tissues, suggesting that stromal activation may facilitate metastatic dissemination through the formation of a fibrotic microenvironment. Lymph node tissues exhibited a pronounced reduction in myeloid cells and were predominantly composed of T/NK cells and B cells (online supplemental figure S1C). To quantify cell-type-specific variations across samples, a bar chart was used to illustrate proportional differences in each major cell population highlighting considerable tissue-level heterogeneity (online supplemental figure S1D). Subsequently, this study employed the Augur algorithm to assess sample-specific gene expression perturbations per cell type. Fibroblasts exhibited the highest area under the curve (AUC) among stromal cells, while among immune cells, plasma cells and myeloid cells showed the highest AUC values (figure 1B).

Given the prominent distribution shifts of myeloid cells between CRC and ANCT, as well as between CLM and ANLT—coupled with their notable reduction in LN—these findings imply that myeloid cells may play a pivotal role in modulating the immune microenvironment of CRC and its metastatic sites.

The dynamic evolution of myeloid cell subsets in colorectal cancer and its metastatic microenvironment

Based on the above preliminary results, this study further investigated the heterogeneity of myeloid cells across different sample types. Myeloid cells were classified into five major functional categories—neutrophils, monocytes, dendritic cells (DC2, DC3), and TAMs—and further subdivided into 13 distinct clusters (C0–C12). These included eight monocyte subsets (C1-HLA-DRB+Mon, C2-FCN1+Mon, C5-EREG+Mon, C6-MMP12+Mon, C7-MT2A+Mon, C8-AREG+Mon, C10-GBP1+Mon, and C12-HSPH1+Mon), two TAM subsets (C0-SEPP1+TAM and C4-SPP1+TAM), two dendritic cell clusters (C3-DC2 and C11-DC3), and one neutrophil cluster (C9-Neo) (figure 1C). A stacked bar plot illustrates the proportional distribution of these cell subsets across different sample types, revealing pronounced heterogeneity in myeloid cell composition among tissues (figure 1D).

To assess tissue-specific enrichment patterns of myeloid subpopulations more accurately, this study performed Ro/e analysis (figure 1E), which delineates the distribution and infiltration characteristics of cell clusters across tissues, providing insight into immune mechanisms underlying CRC and its metastasis. The results demonstrated that SPP1+TAMs (C4) were more enriched in tumor tissues (CRC: Ro/e=1.40; CLM: Ro/e=0.63) compared with normal tissues (ANCT: Ro/e=0.27; ANLT: Ro/e=0.13). In contrast, SEPP1+TAMs (C0) showed lower enrichment in tumors (CRC: Ro/e=1.17; CLM: Ro/e=0.16) than in normal tissues (ANCT: Ro/e=2.18; ANLT: Ro/e=0.27). Among monocyte subsets, HLA-DRB+Mon (C1, Ro/e=3.53) and HSPH1+Mon (C12, Ro/e=2.13) were highly enriched in CLM. Additionally, both DC2 (C3, Ro/e=2.02) and DC3 (C11, Ro/e=3.42) were significantly enriched in LN, underscoring the potential role of dendritic cells—alongside lymphocytes—in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment, suggesting their relevance as potential immunotherapeutic targets. UMAP visualization enabled intuitive observation of the tissue-specific distribution of various myeloid cell subsets (online supplemental figure S2). Consistent with these findings, marked variations in infiltration abundance were observed for both SPP1+TAMs and SEPP1+TAMs across different tissues.

Subsequent focus on TAMs revealed that the infiltration ratio of SEPP1+TAM was significantly lower in CRC compared with ANCT, a trend not observed between CLM and ANLT. Conversely, SPP1+TAM infiltration was significantly higher in CRC than in ANCT and elevated in CLM compared with ANLT (figure 1F). To validate these infiltration patterns, this study performed multiplex immunohistochemistry on a tissue microarray (figure 1G). Co-localization analysis using the macrophage marker CD68 confirmed that CD68+SEPP1+TAMs were significantly enriched in ANCT, whereas CD68+SPP1+TAMs were predominant in CRC.

These findings suggest distinct functional and regulatory roles for these TAM subsets in CRC development and progression, providing new perspectives for understanding TAM heterogeneity and its therapeutic implications.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling reveals distinct developmental trajectories and functional specialization of SEPP1+TAMs and SPP1+TAMs in colorectal cancer

To further characterize the heterogeneity and developmental origin of SEPP1+TAMs and SPP1+TAMs, this study performed differential gene expression and functional enrichment analysis across myeloid subsets. Transcriptional profiling revealed distinct gene signatures between subsets (figure 2A). GO analysis indicated that SEPP1+TAMs were predominantly enriched in immune processes related to B-cell activation and major histocompatibility complex class II antigen presentation, whereas SPP1+TAMs were strongly associated with ERK signaling pathway activation (figure 2B).

Given these functional differences, this study next reconstructed the developmental trajectories of these TAM populations. The overall trajectory inference illustrated continuous transitions from monocytic states toward mature TAM subsets (figure 2C). When analyzed by sample type, TAMs were notably absent in peripheral blood (PBMC), consistent with their tissue-resident identity (figure 2D). Both SEPP1+ TAMs and SPP1+TAMs were found to originate from C2-FCN1+ monocytes in circulation. Notably, the differentiation pathways of these TAMs exhibited variation. SPP1+TAMs developed through multiple routes: in CRC, differentiation occurred either directly from FCN1+ monocytes or via C8-AREG+monocytes and C10-CXCL10+monocytes as intermediate monocytic stages; in CLM, they arose directly from FCN1+ monocytes; in LN, they underwent a multistep differentiation through C8-AREG+monocytes and C10-CXCL10+monocytes. In contrast, SEPP1+TAM differentiation was more uniform, consistently proceeding through C7-MT2A+monocytes and C6-MMP12+ monocytes as intermediates across all tissue contexts (figure 2E).

Based on the above analyses, this study systematically reveals the remarkable heterogeneity in the composition, distribution, and functional states of myeloid cells, particularly SEPP1+ TAMs and SPP1+TAMs, within CRC and its metastatic niches. The results demonstrate that SPP1+ TAMs are specifically enriched in tumor tissues, whereas SEPP1+ TAMs are predominantly present in normal mucosal tissues, suggesting that these two subsets may play distinct roles in tumorigenesis and immune regulation. The tissue-specific microenvironment (primary tumor, metastatic site, and LN) exerts a substantial influence on the selective enrichment and spatial distribution of myeloid subpopulations, further underscoring the critical role of the microenvironment in shaping immune cell phenotypes and functions.

Metabolic and developmental diversification of SEPP1+TAMs and SPP1+ TAMs into tissue-specific functional subsets in colorectal cancer and metastatic niche

To further dissect the intrinsic heterogeneity of SEPP1+ TAMs and SPP1+ TAMs populations, this study performed subclustering analysis specifically on these cells. This approach revealed that each population could be clearly subdivided into two distinct subclusters: C1 (ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs), C2 (ATP5F1E+SEPP1+TAMs), C3 (MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs), and C4 (MT-CO1+SEPP1+ TAMs), with clear separation in UMAP space (figure 3A). Differential expression analysis confirmed elevated expression of ATP5F1E in clusters C1 and C2, while MT-CO1 was predominantly expressed in clusters C3 and C4 (figure 3B). This study next investigated the tissue-specific distribution of these subpopulations across different microenvironment contexts. All four subsets were present within primary CRC tissues. In ANCT, there was observed a notable reduction in C1 (ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs) and C3 (MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs), consistent with the overall decreased infiltration of SPP1+TAMs in non-malignant tissues. In contrast, CLM and ANLT were predominantly populated by MT-CO1-high subsets (C3 and C4), while lymph node metastases showed exclusive enrichment of ATP5F1E-high subsets (C1 and C2) (figure 3C). This tissue-specific distribution pattern was further validated by Ro/e analysis (figure 3D). To elucidate the developmental relationships among these TAM subsets, this study performed pseudotime trajectory analysis. The results indicated that MT-CO1-high TAMs (C3 and C4) likely represent a more mature differentiation state (figure 4E). RNA velocity analysis suggested potential developmental progression from ATP5F1E+SEPP1+TAMs (C2) toward ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs (C1), while MT-CO1-high subsets (C3 and C4) appeared to follow independent developmental trajectories, indicating possible lineage divergence (figure 3F).

Functional and spatial heterogeneity of TAM metabolic subsets in colorectal cancer microenvironment

Previous findings indicated distinct developmental trajectories of SEPP1+TAMs and SPP1+TAMs across different samples. To investigate whether these subpopulations exhibit divergent functional characteristics, this study evaluated their resemblance to classical M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (protumor) macrophage phenotypes. Expression analysis revealed consistently high levels of CD163 across all four subtypes—ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs, ATP5F1E+SEPP1+TAMs, MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs, and MT-CO1+SEPP1+TAMs (figure 4A). Gene set scoring for M1 and M2 signatures showed no significant differences between ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs and ATP5F1E+SEPP1+TAMs, or between MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs and MT-CO1+SEPP1+TAMs (figure 4B). However, heatmap analysis demonstrated that both SPP1+TAM clusters (C1 and C3) exhibited elevated expression of VEGFA, MMP19, and MMP9 (figure 4C), suggesting a potential M2-like phenotype.

To further delineate functional differences, GSVA enrichment analysis was performed. ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs and MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs were significantly enriched in metabolic processes including the renin-angiotensin system, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway, mannose and galactose metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism, and sucrose metabolism (figure 4D). Notably, ATP5F1E+ macrophages exhibited stronger enrichment in these pathways compared with MT-CO1+ macrophages, indicating a more pronounced metabolic phenotype. This pattern may be attributed to the central role of ATP5F1E in mitochondrial ATP synthesis, which supports high energy demand and anabolic processes in tumor-promoting macrophages. In contrast, ATP5F1E+SEPP1+TAMs and MT-CO1+SEPP1+TAMs were enriched in immune-related pathways such as coagulation cascades, IgA production, type I diabetes mellitus, infection responses (Vibrio cholerae, Leishmania), asthma, autoimmune thyroid disease, allograft rejection, graft-versus-host disease, and viral myocarditis (figure 4D). MT-CO1+ macrophages showed stronger association with these pathways, likely due to the involvement of MT-CO1 in oxidative phosphorylation and immune activation, which may enhance inflammatory and immune regulatory functions.

To explore the spatial context of these TAM subpopulations in CRC, this study performed spatial transcriptomic analysis. Mitochondrial genome-encoded genes, particularly those involved in the electron transport chain, were predominantly localized at the tumor periphery (figure 4E,F). This suggests that MT-CO1+SEPP1+TAMs and MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs are primarily situated in oxygen-rich regions along the tumor edge, where mitochondrial oxidative respiration is highly active. This spatial distribution is mechanistically linked to their functional phenotypes: the tumor periphery provides ample oxygen supply, favoring oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). The high OXPHOS activity not only supports energy-intensive immune processes but also influences macrophage polarization and effector functions. Furthermore, the abundance of MT-CO1+TAMs in the liver can be explained by the liver’s physiological reliance on oxidative metabolism, making it a conducive microenvironment for macrophages with high mitochondrial respiratory activity. Thus, the spatial positioning of TAMs according to their metabolic requirements (aerobic vs anaerobic) further elucidates the functional segregation between ATP5F1E+ and MT-CO1+ subsets.

Infiltration abundance and clinical relevance of SPP1+TAMs in colorectal cancer

Based on the previously identified heterogeneity in developmental trajectories and tissue residency of SPP1+TAMs, this study next quantified their infiltration abundance in CRC. Analysis of an scRNA-seq dataset demonstrated that SPP1+TAMs had a median infiltration proportion of approximately 56% among total TAMs, a level significantly higher than that of SEPP1+TAMs (figure 5A). This finding was further validated using multiplex immunofluorescence staining in an independent CRC cohort (figure 5B). Subsequent analysis assessed whether SPP1+TAM enrichment varied across different CRC subtypes. Analysis of the large-scale single-cell cohort in this study indicated a trend toward a higher median proportion of SPP1+TAMs in right-sided CRC compared with left-sided CRC; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (figure 5C). The lack of significance may be attributable to the relatively limited number of left-sided CRC cases in the cohort, suggesting a need for future studies with expanded sample sizes. Notably, SPP1+TAM infiltration was significantly elevated in proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) tumors compared with dMMR tumors (figure 5C), implying a potential association between SPP1+TAM abundance and the immune contexture of CRC.

A particularly interesting observation was the significantly higher proportion of SPP1+TAMs in liver metastatic lesions compared with SEPP1+TAMs, a phenomenon not observed in metastatic LN (figure 5D). This finding prompted the hypothesis that SPP1+TAMs may play a functional role in CRC liver metastasis. To test this, the GSE41568 cohort was analyzed, which includes primary tumors from patients with CRC without liver metastasis and those with synchronous liver metastasis. Primary tumors from patients with liver metastasis contained a significantly greater proportion of SPP1+TAMs than those from non-metastatic patients (figure 5E). This result was corroborated by multiplex immunofluorescence experiments in the validation cohort (figure 5F).

These results demonstrate that SPP1+TAMs are the predominant TAM subset in CRC and are associated with pMMR status. More importantly, the strong correlation between SPP1+TAM abundance and liver metastasis suggests this subset may actively participate in the metastatic process and shape the metastatic niche in CRC.

Prognostic and predictive value of SPP1+TAM abundance in large colorectal cancer cohorts

Given that prior investigations into the tissue-specific distribution of SPP1+TAMs were primarily based on scRNA-seq cohorts with limited sample sizes, this study sought to validate these findings in a large bulk transcriptomic cohort. Using the GSE39582 dataset, the infiltration levels of SPP1+TAMs across different clinical stages were analyzed. SPP1+TAM abundance was significantly lower in stage I disease but was notably elevated in stages II through IV, although no significant differences were observed among these later stages (figure 6A). This stage-dependent increase suggests that SPP1+TAM infiltration may be triggered by tumor progression and potentially contributes to the advancement of CRC. This suggests that high SPP1+TAM infiltration is a common feature from stage II onward. Subgroup analysis further revealed that SPP1+TAM levels were significantly higher in pMMR tumors compared with dMMR tumors, a finding consistent with the earlier single-cell cohort results (figure 6A). The preferential enrichment of SPP1+TAMs in pMMR tumors implies their potential role in establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment (figure 6A). Then, this study evaluated the abundance of SPP1+TAMs across different TCGA molecular subtypes. Significant variation was observed, with the highest infiltration levels found in the C2 and C4 subtypes, and the lowest in C1 and C3 subtypes (figure 6A). The distinct distribution pattern across molecular subtypes further emphasizes the heterogeneity of SPP1+TAM infiltration and its context-dependent roles. This pattern strongly implies that SPP1+TAMs are implicated in both the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment (characteristic of the C2 subtype) and intrinsic tumor aggressiveness (C4 subtype).

To further analyze the impact of the infiltration level of SPP1+TAM on prognosis, this study conducted an analysis in the GEO dataset. When all samples were included, the high content of SPP1+TAM was significantly associated with a lower overall survival rate (figure 6B). Further stratified analysis revealed that in the early stage of the tumor, high SPP1+TAM still had a significantly adverse prognosis (figure 6B). However, in patients with distant metastasis, high SPP1+TAM had a poor prognosis, but this trend was not significant, which might be related to the small sample size (figure 6B). The above results indicate that SPP1+TAM infiltration may be a potential adverse prognostic factor in CRC. The high correlation between SPP1+TAM level and patient prognosis highlights its clinical relevance and potential value as a risk stratification biomarker.

The preliminary results suggested that SPP1+TAM might be associated with liver metastasis of CRC. To assess the predictive power of SPP1+TAM levels for liver metastasis, the GSE41568 cohort was analyzed. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis demonstrated a strong predictive capability, with an AUC of 0.847 (figure 6C). The high predictive accuracy suggests that SPP1+TAM infiltration could serve as a valuable non-invasive indicator for assessing metastatic risk, highlighting its potential clinical applicability in patient management.

These findings prompted further investigation into the potential functions of SPP1+TAMs in CRC. Subsequent correlation analysis revealed that the infiltration level of SPP1+TAMs was significantly positively correlated with the expression of multiple pro-angiogenic factors and immune checkpoint molecules, while showing a significant negative correlation with the expression of various interleukin factors (figure 6D,E). These findings position SPP1+TAMs as a potential critical nexus linking malignant proliferation and immune evasion in CRC.

SPP1+TAMs drive CD8+ T-cell dysfunction and foster an immunosuppressive niche

To further delineate the immunosuppressive microenvironment orchestrated by SPP1+TAMs, this study leveraged a large scRNA-seq cohort of CRC tissues. Samples were stratified into high and low SPP1+TAM groups based on the median infiltration ratio of SPP1+TAMs among total TAMs. UMAP visualization and subsequent quantitative analysis revealed a significantly higher abundance of CD4+ T cells and CD8+T cells in the low SPP1+TAM group (figure 7A). To dissect the specific impact on T-cell subsets, clustering analysis was performed, identifying 10 distinct CD4+T cell subsets and 8 distinct CD8+T cell subsets (figure 7B). Differential abundance analysis demonstrated that the low SPP1+TAM group was markedly enriched for EEF1A1+CD4+central memory T cells, MT-CO1+CD8+effector memory T cells (TEM), and CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM). Conversely, the high SPP1+TAM group exhibited a significant increase in exhausted CD8+ T cells (TEX) (figure 7C). This inverse correlation between SPP1+TAM abundance and cytotoxic T-cell infiltration was further validated by multiplex immunofluorescence (figure 7D) and spatial transcriptomic analysis, the latter showing reduced CD8A expression in regions with high SPP1+TAM infiltration (figure 8A). These findings strongly suggest that SPP1+TAMs function as immunosuppressive regulators in CRC, potentially by impairing CD8+ T-cell infiltration and function.

To investigate the mechanistic basis of this suppression, cell–cell communication analysis was conducted using the CellChat package. In the high SPP1+TAM group, interaction frequency between SPP1+TAMs and TEX was significantly enhanced, while communication with CD8+ TEM and TRM subsets was substantially reduced. The opposite pattern was observed in the low SPP1+TAM group (figure 8B). Further dissection revealed that this differential networking was primarily driven by outgoing signals from SPP1+TAMs (figure 8C). Functional analysis corroborated that the low SPP1+TAM group was enriched for pathways related to T-cell activation and immune signaling, indicating a more immunocompetent microenvironment (figure 8D). Finally, multiplex immunofluorescence staining combined with spatial proximity analysis demonstrated that the number of CD8+ T cells within a 100 µm radius of SPP1+TAMs was drastically decreased in high SPP1+TAM samples, a phenomenon not observed in low SPP1+TAM samples (figure 8E).

In summary, these results indicate that SPP1+TAMs contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment by inhibiting CD8+ T-cell activity and promoting T-cell exhaustion. This effect is likely mediated through altered cellular communication, whereby SPP1+TAMs selectively enhance interactions with exhausted T cells while reducing crosstalk with cytotoxic memory subsets, ultimately leading to localized spatial exclusion of effector T cells.

Remodeling the SPP1+TAM-mediated microenvironment through regorafenib combined with immune checkpoint blockade therapy

Building on the established role of SPP1+TAMs in promoting immunosuppression through multiple mechanisms, this study sought to develop a therapeutic strategy to counteract their function. Given the clinical limitations of anti-CSF1R monotherapy in targeting SPP1+TAMs and this study’s discovery of their dual expression of pro-angiogenic (VEGFA) and immunosuppressive (PD-1) molecules, this study next investigated a combination approach simultaneously addressing both pathways. Specifically, this study hypothesized that regorafenib—a clinically approved multikinase inhibitor targeting both VEGFR and CSF1R—could effectively reprogram the SPP1+TAM-enriched immunosuppressive niche and synergize with anti-PD-1 therapy. To test this hypothesis, a murine MC38 subcutaneous colon cancer model was established. Notably, regorafenib combined with anti-PD-1 demonstrated significantly enhanced tumor growth suppression compared with either monotherapy or control groups (figure 9A,B). To gain deeper insight into the underlying immunological changes, single-cell transcriptomic profiling of treated tumors was performed. This analysis revealed substantial remodeling of the immune microenvironment (figure 9C), characterized by a marked reduction in Spp1+TAM frequency accompanied by a concurrent increase in Cd8+ T-cell infiltration in the combination treatment group (figure 9C). These findings were further validated by multiplex immunofluorescence, which visually confirmed decreased SPP1+TAM areas alongside increased CD8+ T-cell density following combination therapy (figure 9D).

To further explore whether inhibiting SPP1+TAMs can improve liver metastasis of CRC, this study established a mouse liver metastasis model. In this study, a mouse liver metastasis model was established. The results showed that the regorafenib group and the regorafenib combined with anti-PD-1 group significantly inhibited the formation of liver metastases, and the latter had a better effect (figure 9E,F). Multiplex immunological analyses further revealed that regorafenib treatment inhibited the infiltration of Spp1+TAMs, and that combination with anti-PD-1 therapy increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration (figure 9G).

To further quantify these immune population changes, flow cytometric analysis was conducted. The results demonstrated that combination treatment significantly increased total tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (figure 10A), reduced the F4/80+ macrophage compartment (figure 10B), and expanded the CD8+ T-cell population more effectively than either agent alone (figure 10C). To elucidate the mechanistic basis for these observations, cell–cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat. This investigation revealed that regorafenib treatment effectively disrupted immunosuppressive crosstalk by diminishing both the frequency and intensity of Spp1+TAM-to-Cd8+T cell interactions (figure 10D). Further ligand-receptor analysis identified significant inhibition of the Spp1-(Itgav+Itgb5) axis—a key molecular pathway mediating TAM-induced suppression of T-cell function—following treatment (figure 10E). Functionally, the combination therapy robustly enhanced Cd8+T cell cytotoxicity, as evidenced by significantly increased frequencies of both IFN-γ+ and granzyme B+CD8+T cells (figure 10F). Importantly, this enhancement of cytotoxic function was most pronounced in the regorafenib plus anti-PD-1 group, indicating synergistic restoration of antitumor immunity.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that dual treatment of angiogenic and myeloid signaling pathways with regorafenib effectively modulates the SPP1+TAM-driven immunosuppressive microenvironment and significantly enhances the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in CRC, providing a strong rationale for clinical translation of this combination strategy.

Discussion

CRC remains a major clinical challenge, and its progression is closely associated with the functional states and spatial architecture of immune cells within the TME. TAMs, as key regulatory components of the TME, have been shown by numerous studies to be involved in tumor metastasis, immune regulation, and therapy resistance.8 31 However, despite the widespread application of single-cell sequencing technologies that have greatly expanded our understanding of TAM heterogeneity, several major limitations remain in the field. A lack of integrated multisite analyses has prevented authoritative and consistent conclusions regarding the context-dependent properties of TAMs in developmental trajectories, spatial distribution, and functional adaptation.14 32

This study conducted an in-depth analysis of a large-scale single-cell cohort encompassing primary CRC, liver metastases, lymph node metastases, and normal tissues, systematically identifying and characterizing two functionally distinct macrophage subtypes: SPP1+TAMs and SEPP1+TAMs. This finding revealed that SPP1+TAMs are the dominant infiltrating TAM subpopulation in CRC tissues, with significant enrichment observed in liver metastases.33 Integrated analysis of single-cell transcriptomic and bulk RNA-seq data indicated that high infiltration levels of SPP1+TAMs in primary lesions may serve as a potential biomarker for predicting CRC liver metastasis. These results are consistent with accumulating evidence implicating SPP1 as a critical mediator in cancer metastasis processes across multiple cancer types.34 Particularly, a study has revealed the role of SPP1 signaling in promoting tumor metastasis.35

Although SPP1+TAMs and SEPP1+TAMs exhibit significant heterogeneity across tissues, the developmental origins and differentiation trajectories of these tissue-resident immune cells warrant further investigation. This developmental trajectory analysis revealed that SPP1+TAMs arise through distinct differentiation pathways depending on their microenvironment: in primary tumors, they may originate via direct differentiation from FCN1+ monocytes or through intermediate AREG+ and CXCL10+ monocytic stages, while in liver metastases they primarily develop through direct differentiation. This finding resonates with groundbreaking research by Zhang et al in tumor immunology, who first mapped the complete differentiation landscape of myeloid cells in the CRC microenvironment.27 This context-dependent developmental plasticity suggests profound influences of local microenvironmental signals on TAM fate determination,36 while also providing potential explanations for the inconsistent responses observed in previous TAM-targeted therapies.8 31 37 Important discoveries by Locati et al in microenvironment research support this concept, demonstrating that different tissue microenvironments induce distinct differentiation fates in macrophages.36 However, these previous studies only explored the differentiation trajectory of SPP1+TAMs and did not delve into the proportional differences of SPP1+TAMs across different tissues or whether their differentiation exhibits heterogeneity. This study provides innovative findings that build on earlier research.

Further analysis uncovered substantial heterogeneity within the SPP1+TAM population, which could be subdivided into two functional subsets: ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs and MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs. These subsets exhibited distinct spatial distribution patterns: ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs were predominantly enriched in primary lesions and metastatic LN, while MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs dominated the liver metastasis microenvironment. Recent spatial transcriptomic research by Karin Pelka et al provided important evidence supporting this observation, revealing subtype-specific distribution patterns of TAMs across different metastatic sites.38 This distribution pattern reflects the profound impact of different metastatic microenvironments on selective pressures and metabolic reprogramming of TAM subsets. Pioneering research on tumor metabolic microenvironments by Reinfeld et al offered mechanistic explanations for this phenomenon.39

As a key component of mitochondrial ATP synthase, the high expression of ATP5F1E suggests that this subset enhances mitochondrial ATP synthesis to meet the energy demands of rapidly proliferating tumor cells in primary lesions, potentially supporting protumor activities such as angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling through energy provision. A comprehensive review by Stine et al on cancer metabolism therapy detailed this mechanism.40 Recent studies further indicate that lactate gradients in primary CRC lesions may reinforce the metabolic characteristics and functions of TAMs through epigenetic modifications such as histone lactylation.41 Conversely, as a crucial subunit of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, high expression of MT-CO1 indicates this subset’s reliance on highly active OXPHOS. Systematic research on cancer metabolism by Vasan et al elucidated the critical role of OXPHOS in tumor progression.42 The liver, as a metabolically highly active organ with relatively adequate oxygen supply, may provide an ideal environment for OXPHOS-dominated metabolic modes.42 In-depth research on liver metabolic microenvironments by Vander Heiden and DeBerardinis supports this concept.43 The unique hypoxic conditions within hepatic sinusoids, combined with high concentrations of metabolites such as bile acids and ammonia, collectively establish a specialized microenvironment conducive to the survival and functional differentiation of TAMs.44 Emerging evidence indicates that nutrient availability within this niche drives metabolic reprogramming of TAMs, thereby modulating their functional phenotypes and enhancing their protumoral activities.31 Functionally, the spatial heterogeneity of TAM subsets suggests distinct roles depending on their anatomical location. In primary tumors, ATP5F1E+SPP1+TAMs appear to facilitate initial tumor growth and local invasion. In contrast, MT-CO1+SPP1+TAMs, which are predominantly enriched in liver metastases, play a critical role in establishing the metastatic niche and mediating liver-specific immunosuppression.45 Clinically, high infiltration of SPP1+ TAMs is significantly correlated with increased angiogenesis, suppression of T-cell function, and poorer patient prognosis.46

Integrative analysis using single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and multiplex immunofluorescence revealed that SPP1+TAMs interact closely with CD8+ T cells via immune checkpoint signaling pathways, including the Spp1–(Itgav/Itgb5) axis. This interaction promotes T-cell exhaustion and physical exclusion from the TME, further compromising antitumor immunity.38 Given these mechanisms, this study evaluated the immunomodulatory potential of regorafenib, a multikinase inhibitor. By concurrently targeting VEGFR and CSF1R signaling pathways, regorafenib effectively suppresses SPP1+TAMs and reshapes the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that this intervention significantly enhances CD8+ T-cell infiltration and reinvigorates antitumor immune responses. Supporting these findings,47 Fukuoka et al reported robust synergistic effects when regorafenib was combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors in CRC.48

Recently, Shen et al reported that tumor cell-derived SPP1 promotes CRC liver metastasis and resistance to immunotherapy by stimulating CXCL12 production in cancer-associated fibroblasts.49 Their study delineates a distinct cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF)-centered mechanism in which SPP1 produced by tumor cells acts on CAFs to enhance CD44 signaling and upregulate CXCL12 transcription, thereby establishing a pro-metastatic and immunosuppressive microenvironment. By contrast, the present study, through single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses, identifies SPP1+TAMs as a major SPP1-producing population in both primary and metastatic lesions, which likely interact directly with CD8+T cells via integrin and immune checkpoint pathways. Notably, SPP1+TAMs also secrete SPP1 protein, suggesting that SPP1 derived from both tumor cells and TAMs may operate in parallel cooperating to construct a physical immune barrier and to generate an immunosuppressive niche that facilitates the establishment of the liver metastatic microenvironment. This may provide more perspectives and avenues for exploring the mechanism of action of SPP1+TAMs in the future.

This study has several limitations. First, the findings are primarily based on retrospective cohort data and single-cell analyses, and thus require prospective validation in larger, independent cohorts, as well as mechanistic experiments to clarify the causal roles of SPP1+TAM subpopulations in CRC progression and therapy resistance. Second, although regorafenib was employed as a representative multikinase inhibitor targeting both VEGFR and CSF1R, its pleiotropic nature makes it difficult to dissect the relative contributions of TAM modulation versus anti-angiogenic effects to the observed therapeutic benefit, and potential additional, non-canonical pathways contributing to its immunomodulatory activity cannot be excluded. Third, recent reports of a CAF–SPP1–CXCL12 axis suggest that targeting CAF-derived CXCL12 or its upstream SPP1 signaling may offer a stroma-focused, complementary strategy to disrupt the metastatic microenvironment. Future work in suitable preclinical models will be essential to systematically evaluate selective interventions against SPP1+TAM-derived SPP1 and its effects on CAFs, direct blockade of SPP1/ligand-receptor interactions, combinations of CSF1R inhibition with anti-angiogenic therapy, and CAF–CXCL12-targeted approaches, to define optimal combination regimens and develop effective strategies to overcome immunotherapy resistance in CRC.

In conclusion, this study systematically reveals the high heterogeneity of TAMs in CRC and its metastatic niches from three dimensions: spatial distribution, metabolic features, and functional states. It particularly elucidates the specific adaptive mechanisms of the pro-metastatic SPP1+TAM subset within distinct anatomical locations and metabolic microenvironments. Through preclinical experiments, this study further demonstrates that the combination of regorafenib and immune checkpoint inhibitors effectively reduces the infiltration of SPP1+TAMs, providing a novel therapeutic strategy for clinically reversing the SPP1+TAM-mediated immunosuppressive microenvironment and enhancing antitumor immunity.
