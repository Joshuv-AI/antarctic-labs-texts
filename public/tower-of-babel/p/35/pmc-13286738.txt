How well is messaging about the importance of vaccination for people living with dementia being communicated? A jurisdictional scan of National Immunization Technical Advisory Groups and dementia advocacy organizations
Authors: Melissa K. Andrew, Shanna C. Trenaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaccination is particularly important for people living with dementia (PLWD), who are at risk for meaningful declines in function and cognition after acute illnesses including vaccine‐preventable infections. Even so, vaccine uptake remains suboptimal. Optimal tailoring of messaging relies on awareness in advisory, decision‐making, and advocacy settings that PLWD are an important population for immunization. This policy forum paper discusses why vaccination is particularly important for PLWD and whether this is translated into public‐facing and policy audience messaging. Jurisdictional scans investigated three questions: the inclusion of geriatrics expertise in a sample of eight National Immunization Technical Advisory Groups (NITAGs; Australia, Canada, France, Germany, Switzerland, the UK, United States, World Health Organization), whether PLWD are included in high‐risk groups particularly recommended to receive adult vaccinations, and whether dementia advocacy organizations from these same jurisdictions discuss and recommend vaccination. We conclude that there remain important opportunities for improvement in understanding and communicating the benefits of vaccination for PLWD, and present policy recommendations for NITAGs, dementia advocacy organizations, and clinicians.

INTRODUCTION

Vaccination is particularly important for people living with dementia (PLWD). Even so, vaccine uptake remains suboptimal, and messaging may not be optimally tailored to populations likely to receive the most benefit. Awareness of and attention to PLWD as an important population for immunization may also be limited in public health settings, including in the work of National Immunization Technical Advisory Groups (NITAGs) and dementia advocacy organizations.

This policy forum paper investigates why vaccination is particularly important for PLWD and how well this message is being translated to policy audiences and to the public.

To investigate these steps along the decision making to recommendation pathway, the present paper starts with a discussion of why vaccines are particularly important for PLWD. This is followed by presentation of a jurisdictional scan conducted to consider (1) geriatrics representation in NITAG membership, (2) whether dementia is considered a high‐risk condition in NITAG guidance, and (3) whether national dementia advocacy organizations (e.g. Alzheimer's Association/Society) specifically recommend vaccination as part of messaging on living well with dementia.

Adult vaccine‐preventable disease burdens of illness and evidence re: vaccines

The vaccines considered here are the ones which are generally considered adult vaccines—influenza, COVID‐19, pneumococcus, herpes zoster (shingles), and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). Each of these infections has a risk profile that increases with older age. Although their outcomes and burden of illness are traditionally considered in the short term (e.g., a 2‐week self‐limited viral illness in the case of influenza and RSV, a pneumonia which is treated and recovers, or an episode of shingles which then resolves), as we review below, they are also associated with longer term poor outcomes, which are especially important to consider for PLWD.

Influenza occurs in yearly epidemics (and occasional pandemics). It is estimated that each year globally > 32 million people experience influenza infections, and > 5.6 million are hospitalized (with the highest risk being observed in adults > 65 years of age). 1 Influenza is also increasingly understood to be associated with exacerbations of chronic conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure, and chronic renal disease. Influenza can trigger events such as myocardial infarction and stroke, and there is evidence that influenza vaccination is effective in preventing these outcomes. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Influenza is also an important cause of falls, fractures, delirium, and functional decline. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 PLWD are often older and frail, and they have a vulnerable brain that predisposes to delirium when ill and also to incomplete functional recovery. They often have underlying chronic conditions such as those listed above, which can be exacerbated, sometimes permanently, by an influenza illness. Incomplete functional recovery may lead to increases in frailty going forward, 12 and to increased reliance on formal and informal caregiving assistance for basic and instrumental activities of daily living (ADLs and IADLs). For some, this functional decline may lead to incident admission to long‐term care (LTC) facilities (LTCF). 9 , 13

COVID‐19 has also disproportionately impacted older adults, residents of LTCF, and PLWD. 14 , 15 Dementia has been found to predict poor outcomes from COVID‐19, 16 , 17 and COVID‐19 has been shown to be a risk factor for developing cognitive impairment and dementia. 18 , 19 As with other respiratory infections, COVID‐19 is associated with cognitive and functional decline and institutionalization in PLWD. 17

Community acquired pneumonia (CAP) and invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) are important causes of morbidity and mortality, especially for older and immunocompromised adults. 20 This includes infection with potentially vaccine‐preventable strains of pneumococcus. 21 Older age and dementia have both been found to be risk factors for CAP illness, hospitalization, and mortality. 20 , 22 Neurodegenerative conditions may specifically increase risk for pneumonia through impairments in clearance of secretions: PLWD who have impaired swallowing and/or difficulties in maintaining oral hygiene, for example, are more susceptible to pneumonia. 23 There is also evidence that pneumonia is a risk factor for subsequently developing dementia. 24

Older age is one of the key risk factors for herpes zoster. 25 Although shingles episodes can be self‐limited with complete resolution, up to 30% of cases are complicated by post‐herpetic neuralgia (PHN), which can have a debilitating impact on function and well‐being. 26 , 27 Although dementia in and of itself has not been found to be an independent risk factor for shingles, the pain and functional decline seen with PHN likely have a disproportionate impact on PLWD, particularly as many pain medications are sedating and anticholinergic. Notably, herpes zoster ophthalmicus has been identified as a risk factor for dementia. 28

RSV has only recently become a vaccine‐preventable infection for older adults, with the introduction of vaccines recommended for this population in 2023. 29 Because of the historic paucity of surveillance in adults, there is less evidence about the burden of RSV among adults. Even so, RSV has been shown to contribute substantial morbidity and mortality in older adults, including those living with dementia. 30

Is dementia itself vaccine preventable?

There is increasing research into whether dementia could be vaccine preventable. This is not referring to dementia‐specific vaccines (e.g., vaccines targeting amyloid), but rather to the potential benefits of vaccines that are “non‐specific” to their target infections. The mechanisms that might contribute to protection against dementia could include both direct effects (acting through prevention of the targeted illness and hospitalization/complications) and indirect/non‐specific effects. Indirect or non‐specific effects of vaccination could occur via various mechanisms including cross‐reactive adaptive (i.e., cell‐mediated) immunity and trained innate immunity in which inflammatory pathways activated by vaccination might play a role in protecting against insults other than the vaccine‐specific one (possibly both infectious and non‐infectious). 31 , 32 For example, in one large US study using administrative data, influenza vaccination has been associated with reduced subsequent risk of developing dementia, with a number needed to vaccinate as low as 29 people to prevent one case of dementia. 33 , 34 Similar findings have been reported for pneumococcal and zoster vaccine, 35 with a 2025 meta‐analysis of 21 studies representing > 104 million participants finding that zoster (relative risk [RR] 0.76, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.69–0.83), influenza (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.77–0.99), pneumococcal (RR 0.64, 95% CI 0.47–0.87), and tetanus/diphtheria/pertussis (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.54–0.83) vaccination were each associated with reduced dementia risk. 36 This evidence base continues to evolve, and notable limitations include residual contribution arising from incomplete adjustment for confounding (e.g., by healthy vaccinee bias or confounding by indication), limitations in data sources (e.g., use of administrative data which lack detailed clinical characterization and dementia diagnostic criteria), and uncertainty in estimates of effect.

Why is vaccination particularly important for PLWD?

Having reviewed the evidence of the importance of vaccine‐preventable diseases (VPDs) for PLWD, it is important to approach the question of why vaccines are particularly important for PLWD from multiple perspectives. The importance of older adult vaccination has been recognized by aging and frailty advocacy groups, including the International Federation on Ageing (IFA) and the Canadian Frailty Network (CFN). The IFA has a Vaccines4Life program which communicates and advocates about the importance of vaccines for healthy aging. 46 The CFN includes vaccination in its AVOID frailty framework of five things that people can do to age well, avoid and/or mitigate frailty (the components of AVOID are Physical Activity, Vaccinate, Optimize medications, Social Interaction, and Healthy Diet). 47

Jurisdictional scan #1: geriatrics representation in NITAG membership

Perspective is important, and having a seat at the decision‐making table matters. Immunization is traditionally viewed as important for pediatrics, and it is true that vaccination is a critical part of optimizing health for infants and children. As more vaccines become available for infections that impact adults, there is an increasing interest in optimizing protection across the adult life course as well, including into older age. When it comes to the specific question of whether expertise on and awareness of conditions of aging, and specifically dementia, is included at the table, presence of a geriatrician is an important indicator. This was starkly communicated in a report published by the IFA in 2023 that found that of 34 NITAGs surveyed, only 3 included expertise in adult vaccination, geriatrics, or population aging, with clinical expertise in most NITAGs weighted toward pediatrician perspectives. 48

A jurisdictional scan was thus conducted among NITAGs in the following countries: Canada, United States, the UK, Australia, Germany, France, Switzerland, and for the World Health Organization (WHO). These jurisdictions were selected to adequately represent countries that have a well‐established NITAG, are comparable in terms of resource status, and similar in that they have aging populations and comparability in terms of the scope of adult vaccination programs. Language was also a consideration; the review was focused on English, French, and German to enable comprehensive searches of websites and documents. Selected countries also had to have a national dementia advocacy organization, for the paired second part of the jurisdictional scan. The WHO's Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization NITAG was included as it brings an intentionally international lens, though in doing so it has to be attentive to the needs of countries across the full spectrum of resource, vaccination programming, and population age structure.

Lists of each NITAG's voting members were reviewed, including institutional affiliations and biographies, via links provided on the NITAG website and supplementary internet searches. Geriatrics representation was determined by reviewing the institutional/departmental affiliations and professional training background of all listed voting members for each NITAG to identify whether any voting members had training or stated expertise in geriatrics or gerontology.

The jurisdictional scan was originally conducted January 8, 2025, and was fully repeated and updated on March 5, 2026. One notable exception relates to the intervening changes in the US Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), in the context of ongoing important changes in the vaccine policy landscape in the United States. The original jurisdictional scan search was done prior to the replacement of the ACIP committee. The results on ACIP composition and vaccine recommendations have been left reflecting the findings from January 2025, given that the ACIP in its prior membership format was responsible for many of the recommendations on adult vaccines which still stand and which remain a reasonable basis for informing messaging through public health and dementia advocacy organizations.

Our finding was that four of the eight NITAGs (Canada, United States, France, Germany) included a geriatrician voting member. (The United States's geriatrician member was subsequently replaced in June 2025 along with the rest of the ACIP committee.)

Jurisdictional scan #2: Is dementia considered a high‐risk condition in NITAG guidance?

Having reviewed the reasons why vaccines are particularly important for PLWD, we next turned our attention to whether this is reflected in NITAG advice. NITAGs generally have two main ways of framing recommendations around adult vaccines. One is an age‐based approach (e.g., “vaccination is strongly recommended for all adults over [a certain age]”). For age‐based recommendations, the specific age groups recommended were abstracted to help inform assessment of whether universal age‐based recommendations would likely include most PLWD. The second is framing (which may supplement an age‐based recommendation to denote people at particularly high risk), is to make risk‐based recommendations which usually specify a list of high‐risk conditions for the infection in question. In general these risks tend to be seen as intrinsic (e.g., increased vulnerability to severe outcomes once infected) or extrinsic (social or other factors which would increase risk of exposure or transmission or predispose to poor outcomes) and so both types of factors may be included on lists of high‐risk conditions. For the purposes of this study, either “dementia” or “neurodegenerative condition” in lists of high‐risk conditions were taken to indicate specific inclusion of PLWD, and recommendations for vaccination of LTC residents were also noted as they would apply to those PLWD in these settings.

In this second stage of the jurisdictional scan, published recommendations from the eight NITAGs were reviewed for five adult vaccines (COVID‐19, influenza, pneumococcal, RSV, and herpes zoster) to determine whether dementia or related conditions appeared on lists of people for whom vaccination is particularly recommended, and whether there were age‐based or LTC recommendations. All documents were retrieved through online searches of the websites of the relevant public health authority where NITAG recommendations are published for each jurisdiction. Details of the source documents and publication dates are presented in Table S1 in supporting information.

Findings are summarized in Table 1. All jurisdictions had variations on age‐based recommendations for adult vaccines. Some included specific mention of dementia as a high‐risk condition (e.g., UK, Germany, and France for COVID, Canada for RSV), and several included specific mention of chronic neurological conditions (sometimes with central nervous system or neurodegenerative as descriptors), or residence in LTC settings.

Jurisdictional scan #3: Do national dementia advocacy organizations specifically recommend vaccination as part of messaging on living well with dementia?

Finally, to understand whether NITAG recommendations and the existing evidence base and rationale for why vaccination would be particularly important for PLWD is being communicated to interest holders and the public, websites of dementia advocacy organizations in each of the seven countries were searched for information relating to vaccines, and any specific recommendation on the importance of vaccination for PLWD. Each organization's website was browsed with attention to pages giving advice for reducing risk and living well with dementia, and each site was searched for occurrences of the words vaccine, vaccines, vaccination, immunization(s), jab, and the phrase “should I get vaccinated” as well as the names of the vaccines/illnesses: influenza, pneumococcal, pneumococcus, respiratory syncytial virus, RSV, COVID, coronavirus, SARS‐CoV‐2, shingles, zoster (with French and German translations as appropriate). The Alzheimer's Association USA included a recommendation to get COVID vaccine in the context of recommendations listed in a newsletter‐style story on “Healthy Holidays,” and Alzheimer Suisse included content from early in the pandemic advocating that PLWD should be prioritized for receipt of COVID vaccine. The US Alzheimer's Association linked to news stories describing research studies showing that influenza and pneumococcal vaccines may prevent dementia. The Alzheimer Suisse site included content on how shingles vaccine may reduce dementia risk. None of the organizations discussed vaccination as a specific recommendation for living well with dementia.

DISCUSSION

Given the many reasons why vaccination is an important determinant of healthy aging and is particularly important for PLWD, and the fact that PLWD are generally included in groups recommended to receive these adult vaccines (either on the basis of age as are generally seen for all of the vaccines, or based on specific inclusion in lists of high‐risk conditions as was seen for COVID vaccine in the UK, Germany, and France, and RSV vaccine in Canada), communication to the public would be an important next step. This includes aging advocacy organizations (which are already taking up the torch) and, importantly, dementia advocacy organizations, which (as noted in the present study) generally have yet to do so. Given the trusted position of Alzheimer's associations and societies in their various jurisdictions, not clearly emphasizing the benefits of vaccination for PLWD and the importance of vaccination for preventing and/or living well with dementia represents a missed opportunity. For example, is there a reason not to include a message to “get vaccines that are recommended for you” in lists of recommendations that currently focus mainly on the importance of exercise, social activity, cognitive stimulation, and balanced diet? Do we need to wait for the perfect randomized controlled trial evidence (especially given that PLWD are often excluded from trials)? 49 We do not and indeed should not. Vaccines are a recommended intervention because they have been determined to be safe, effective, and beneficial for their primary purpose (protecting against infections). As stated in the discussion of why vaccination is particularly important for PLWD, these are people who have heightened vulnerability to adverse outcomes such as delirium and functional decline after infections, so it is important that we do anything that we can do to prevent infectious insults and their sequalae. The evidence base is continually building, but we currently have sufficient evidence that infections can have catastrophic impacts on functional independence, well‐being, and health for older adults and specifically for PLWD; any additional benefits for dementia prevention or other indirect benefits would be supplementary. So why wait? Here we can take inspiration from the “precautionary principle,” which posits that it is not necessary to wait for complete evidence before action is taken to mitigate the effects of a reasonably suspected threat to public health. 50 Applying this to vaccines: even if there is always more to learn about the specific benefits and mechanisms at play, we know that these adult vaccines are safe and beneficial and recommended for populations at risk including PLWD, so identifying opportunities to enhance communication about vaccine recommendations messaging and policy stands to contribute to optimizing well‐being, independence, and outcomes even as the evidence base continues to be strengthened.

Policy recommendations

This discussion of the importance of vaccination for PLWD has implications for policy and practice. NITAGs should engage geriatrics or gerontology expertise to inform recommendations pertaining to vaccines for older adults. Dementia advocacy organizations should incorporate messaging on the importance of vaccination for PLWD. Keeping up to date with recommended vaccination should be specifically included in discussion of strategies for living well with dementia. For example, messaging such as: “Keeping up to date with recommended vaccines is an important part of living well with dementia, to help prevent infections which can cause declines in memory, thinking, and daily functional abilities. For details of recommended vaccines, talk to your health‐care providers and see [link to jurisdiction specific recommendations, e.g., the country's public health agency or equivalent].” Clinicians (including pharmacists, primary care clinicians, and dementia specialists) should advise patients/clients living with dementia to keep up to date with recommended vaccinations, including specifically naming each recommended vaccine target infection. This will optimally include making a clear and actionable plan with the patient/family to actually receive the vaccine, either by administering the vaccination, providing a prescription, or recommending that the patient/family make an appointment with a vaccine provider according to local access pathways.

Limitations

This study was limited to the eight jurisdictions reported here and is thus not a comprehensive investigation of all countries. Online searches were initially completed on January 8, 2025, and were updated (with the exception of ACIP membership and US vaccine recommendations as noted above) on March 5, 2026. Language of review was limited to English, French, and German. Even so, the sample focused on high‐income countries where populations are aging and adult vaccination is available and recommended. Expansion to other jurisdictions represents a future direction for this work. NITAG membership changes over time as volunteer members complete their terms and are replaced. Also, most NITAGs would have working groups focused on specific vaccine targets and policy questions which conduct the detailed work to be presented to the main committee for voting. The present investigation was limited to reviewing the lists of voting members so it is possible that some of the working groups might have had geriatrician representation. Advocacy organizations were not contacted to verify their position on vaccination, or to validate the findings of the website searches. Some content could have been missed. Even so, this is felt to be a more real‐world representation of what interest holders in the public, including PLWD and caregivers, would see when they search the websites themselves with these questions. Notably, several countries sites link to the American Alzheimer's Association, making them a major messaging stakeholder.

CONCLUSIONS

Vaccination is particularly important for maintaining function and well‐being for PLWD. Although geriatrics representation is increasingly part of NITAGs, and PLWD are generally included in age‐based recommendations and sometimes included among high‐risk groups specifically recommended to receive vaccination, translation into public health and dementia advocacy organization messaging remains suboptimal. This represents a missed opportunity and an area for improvement given the benefits of vaccination for PLWD, and has policy and practice implications for NITAGs, dementia advocacy organizations, and clinicians.
