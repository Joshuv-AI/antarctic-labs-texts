Efficient phenol removal by δ-MnO2 synthesized in situ on a self-supporting membrane
Authors: Yi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryManganese oxides are effective environmental coupling oxidants for the conversion of phenol; however, previous research has mainly focused on their powdered rather than immobilized forms. To address this gap, the oxidative removal of phenol from aqueous solutions was investigated using δ-MnO2, which was synthesized in situ and immobilized on a tailorable self-supporting membrane carrier (MnO2/M). The Freundlich isotherm provided the best fit for adsorption, whereas Sips successfully indicated the highest adsorption capacity. Kinetic analyses showed that the pseudo-second-order and electron transfer control models represented the reaction dynamics, with intra-particle diffusion and liquid film diffusion playing crucial roles. Physicochemical calculations confirmed the exothermic and spontaneous nature of the reaction (ΔG = −31.65 kJ/mol; 298 K). This research also clarifies and elucidates the two-electron direct oxidative transfer processes involved in the oxidative transformation of phenol by the MnO2/H+ heterogeneous system, establishing a framework for advancing environmental remediation using Mn-based materials.Subject areas: Applied sciences, Materials science, Materials synthesis

Introduction

The detection of organic pollutants in ecological water bodies is of significant environmental concern, especially considering that the persistence of these pollutants has become a severe threat to the ecosystem.1,2 For instance, the presence of antibiotics and endocrine-disrupting chemicals in hospital sewage can promote drug resistance and damage the endocrine system,3,4,5 while the dyes and additives originating from industrial wastewater can lead to reproductive or neurological disorders.6,7,8 Some of the most hazardous organic compounds in refinery wastewater are phenolic derivatives, a significant class of environmental pollutants.9,10,11 Phenol, a representative of hydroxylated aromatics, persists in water and soil, and exposure through inhalation, ingestion, or dermal contact can lead to toxicity in various organs and tissues, including the skin and the mucosal, cardiovascular, and nervous systems.9,12,13 Consequently, various approaches have been developed for the elimination of phenol from wastewater.14,15,16,17,18,19,20,21

Although photocatalytic oxidation and vapor generators have been developed as advanced treatment methods, they tend to require continuous light irradiation at a specific wavelength,18,22 in addition to specialist equipment, such as filters to prevent thermal effects, and large-scale vapor-capturing apparatus for condensation. Despite claims of breakthroughs in state-of-the-art membrane research,23,24 conventional membranes are still subject to trade-off effects and fouling, and more advanced membranes can be expensive and relatively inaccessible. A number of other challenges also exist, such as those related to the molecular design of the system (which ensures selective screening), the film formation process (which is based on miscibility thermodynamics and is affected by many variables), the temperature and pressure dependence of the process, the environmental impact caused by the large volumes of cleaning solvents, and the downstream ancillary processes. It is therefore imperative to pursue cost-effective and efficacious remedial measures for phenol removal.

In the ecological environment, the environmental mobility of hydroxylated aromatics is often regulated by oxidative coupling, a process frequently responsible for their immobilization in subsurface environments.25 Oxidative coupling can be catalyzed by naturally occurring minerals and biological factors within soils and sediments, generating reactive intermediates, such as radicals or quinone species, which subsequently undergo self-/cross-coupling reactions, contributing to the formation of both dissolved and adsorbed organic matter. The resulting hydrophilic humic substances undergo diagenesis over extended geological periods: their H/C and O/C atomic ratios progressively decrease, whereas their sp2/sp3 carbon hybridization ratios increase, ultimately transforming into insoluble and structurally condensed kerogen.26

In this context, the merits of MnO2-based materials and technologies have recently been highlighted. For example, manganese is abundant in the soil, and its oxides generally possess higher redox potentials than iron.27 In addition, manganese oxides are highly involved in the humification, fixation, and degradation of toxic organic emissions or in the anchoring of hazardous heavy metal ions,28 while also playing an essential role in a variety of biogeochemical processes.29,30 Consequently, MnO2 can be considered an abundant and environment-friendly oxidant for the efficient and economical removal of pollutants via water treatment. MnO2 is composed of co-cornered or co-edged Mn–O octahedra MnO6 and crystallizes with layered (δ-MnO2) or tunneled (α(2 × 2/1 × 1), β(1 × 1), and γ(2 × 1/1 × 1)) structures.28 Among them, layered δ-MnO2 is the most active oxidizing agent due to its accessible active sites and desirable oxidation states, such as those between the 3+ and 4+ states, which can take part in synergistic cooperation.31 Considering these properties and also its significant roles in natural biogeochemical cycles and water treatment systems, investigation of the reaction pathways of MnO2 at the solid-water interface is essential in the optimization of heterogeneous catalytic oxidation processes to yield more efficient environmental remediation and energy conversion.32,33,34 However, the majority of reported MnO2 exists in the powder form, thereby constraining our understanding of the mechanisms involved in the immobilized states and presenting challenges in terms of both secondary pollution and recycling.

Membrane-immobilized MnO2 offers significant advantages over powdered MnO2, particularly in terms of its stability, reactivity, and environmental safety. Unlike powdered MnO2, which tends to aggregate and lose its reactivity, the uniform well-dispersed nature of the membrane-immobilized form ensures a consistent performance over time. This enhances the degradation kinetics of the organic pollutants (e.g., phenols) and reduces issues such as the auto-inhibition effect that can be caused by the reaction products. Moreover, membrane immobilization, which also allows customizability and mild synthetic conditions, is expected to minimize powder leakage, offer superior environmental safety, and eliminate the requirement for excessive MnO2 usage, thereby helping reduce expenditure for scalability and rendering it a more sustainable and efficient solution for environmental remediation. A more detailed analysis of the cost and immobilization advantages associated with manganese-based materials can be found in STAR Methods. Thus, in this study, δ-MnO2 was synthesized in situ on flexible self-supporting carriers, with phenol subsequently employed as a chemical probe to evaluate the oxidative activity of the synthesized material. The immobilized MnO2 patches functioned solely as catalytic oxidizers, independent of any transmembrane processes in this study. Phenol removal performance and reaction kinetics were comprehensively characterized through multiple control experiments, multi-model fitting, and physicochemical calculations. The oxidation pathway of phenol was elucidated by applying the state-of-the-art heterogeneous catalysis theory, which dominates the process. Furthermore, the proposed oxidative transformation mechanisms were analyzed, with radical-based pathways—commonly dominant in the homogeneous counterpart system—being qualitatively compared. This comparative approach broadens the relevance of the phenol degradation mechanism, extending its reference value beyond the specific system studied. Importantly, this work establishes a methodological prototype to advance environmental remediation applications of Mn-based materials.

Results and discussion

Characterization

Initially, the phase structures of the original membrane and the two prepared MnO2/M samples were determined by X-ray diffraction, as shown in Figure 1. It can be seen that the intensities of the peaks corresponding to the pristine membrane decreased without shifting after MnO2 loading (dashed line). In addition, it can be seen that the relative intensity (i.e., relative to the peak at 21.7°) of the diffraction peak at 23.98°, which coincides with the 002 crystal plane of δ-MnO2 (JCPDS 80-1098),35,36 increases after loading (as indicated by the bidirectional arrow), possibly due to the successful coverage of MnO2 on the original sample surface. All new emerging diffraction peaks can be attributed to δ-MnO2, which implies that the properties of the carrier membrane itself are preserved during the in situ synthesis of pure crystalline δ-MnO2. Notably, the diffraction peak observed for MnO2/M70-3 at 12.21° is significantly more intense than that for MnO2/M55-2, indicating that a temperature increase during loading/preparation can enhance the crystallinity of the loaded MnO2.

X-ray diffraction analysis of the original membrane carrier and the as-prepared MnO2/M samples

Subsequently, the specimens were subjected to X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy to determine the elemental compositions and chemical states of the materials. As shown in Figure 2B, the valence states observed at 643.2, 642.0, and 640.9 eV in the high-resolution Mn 2p3/2 spectrum could be attributed to Mn4+, Mn3+, and Mn2+,37 each accounting for 43%, 47%, and 10% of the total Mn species, respectively. Based on these results, the average valence of Mn was determined to be 3.33. The average oxidation state (AOS) of Mn was further calculated based on the Mn 3s multiplet splitting magnitude (ΔEs), as illustrated in Figure 2C. Based on the equation AOS = 8.95−1.13ΔEs,38 the AOS of Mn was determined to be 3.63, which is almost identical to the value of 3.64 reported by Huang et al.31 for δ-MnO2, and is close to that determined from the Mn 2p3/2 spectrum. Considering the abundances of the measured Mn(III) surface species, it appeared that the Mn(III) content was the most important factor in determining the activity of MnO2 in the presence of Mn(IV), i.e., the oxidative activity of MnO2 was found to be strongly positively correlated with the Mn(III) content. Furthermore, in the O 1s spectrum shown in Figure 2D, the peak at 532.4 eV was attributed to contributions from adsorbed molecular water and oxygen, whereas the peaks at 531.2 and 529.7 eV originated from the surface hydroxyl oxygen and surface bulk phase oxygen species, respectively.37

These results may suggest that submerging the membrane in solution A at 55°C for 2 h during preparation of the MnO2/M55-2 species did not cause significant reconstruction of the internal membrane structure and that the free volume within the polymer cross-linked network did not increase significantly. Consequently, the in situ synthesized MnO2 particles grew along the original pores, leading to a certain degree of pore plugging, which, together with the subsequent drying process, resulted in a smaller pore size, pore abundance, and pore capacity. On the other hand, during preparation of the MnO2/M70-3 system, thermostatic impregnation in solution A was performed at a higher temperature (70°C) for a prolonged time (3 h), leading to opening of the membrane structure (Figure S1E). As a result, the pore properties were optimized, resulting in a more uniform in situ loading of MnO2 and more abundant accessible reaction sites.

Oxidative treatment of an aqueous phenol solution

Initially, the effect of pH on the oxidative removal of phenol was evaluated. As illustrated in Figure 4A, the phenol reaction rate was maximized at pH 2, decreasing sharply at higher pH values, indicating that phenol removal by MnO2/M70-3 was minimal at pH 3 and beyond. In addition, upon increasing the pH, the phenol removal degree reached a plateau at an earlier time point (Figure 4B), indicating a reduction in the active site availability. More specifically, at pH 2, phenol removal continued beyond 180 min; however, at pH 3, the reaction plateau was reached within 30 min. Notably, when maintaining the pH at 3 during the process, the rate of phenol removal (Vt) remained relatively constant at 0.2 mg L−1 min−1. Considering that the essence of cyclic experiments is centered on continuous consumption of the loaded MnO2 and the free protons, kinetic interruption-re-excitation cycles were adopted instead of conventional cyclic experiments to determine the stability of the cyclic kinetics. For the prediction and demonstration of the final capacity of the system investigated herein, further details can be found in the “isotherm theories” section. The initial rate Vt0 in each cycle was determined to be ∼0.21 mg L−1 min−1 (Figure S2), indicating a good cyclic kinetic stability and oxidative interface renewal ability for the acidified heterogeneous system. Upon reducing the solution pH to 2.5 or 2, Vt increased, and the reaction plateau was delayed compared with that at pH 3. After the removal reactions were performed at initial pH values of 2, 2.5, and 3, all solution pH values were measured to be 7. This phenomenon was also observed by Ukrainczyk and McBride25,39 and suggests that maintaining an adequate proton concentration guarantees sustained reaction progression between the manganese species, i.e., Mn(III)/Mn(IV), and phenol, which may be facilitated due to Le Chatelier’s principle.40 The Nernst equation27 for the redox process (MnO2 + 4H+ + 2e− → Mn2+(aq) + 2H2O (E° = +1.29 V) can be expressed as follows:

The effect of the initial phenol concentration was then examined. As depicted in Figure 5A, with a fixed MnO2/M70-3 dosage, a final phenol removal degree of 97.3% was reached with a lower initial Vt of 0.45 mg L−1 min−1, whereas a final phenol removal degree of 55% was achieved with a higher initial Vt of 1.96 mg L−1 min−1. It should be noted that an increased initial concentration of phenol leads to a higher initial reaction rate due to the greater number of effective collisions per unit volume. However, this often leads to a sharp decrease in the number of active sites, and so in these experiments, it was observed that higher initial phenol concentrations led to a more pronounced decay of Vt. Importantly, even in the later stages of degradation, the Vt values tended to be identical for all experimental groups, regardless of Ct. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that as the initial concentration of phenol is increased, the number of accessible active sites per unit concentration decreases, as reported previously (Figure 5B).41

Adsorption isotherms

The Freundlich, Langmuir, Temkin, and Dubinin-Radushkevich (D-R) isotherm models were used to quantify the adsorptive capacity of the MnO2/M70-3 adsorbent, as detailed in Tables 2 and 3 and in the STAR Methods. Using the Langmuir model, a linear relationship was obtained, and the resulting Langmuir model parameter RL (i.e., (1+KLC0)−1) was determined to be 0.05–0.36 (i.e., <1),44 indicating that the phenol molecules show favorable adsorption on the prepared MnO2/M70-3 surface. The qmax,l predicted by the Langmuir model can be converted to 124.03 mg/gMnOx based on the increase in weight per unit load, as described in the preparation section. This is comparable to the values previously reported for thermal sewage sludge (158 mg/g) and Rattan sawdust (149.25 mg/g) but is greater than those of red mud (59.2 mg/g), date-pits (46.1 mg/g), and clay (30 mg/g).9 In addition, the excellent fit for the Freundlich model indicates that the non-homogeneous surface of MnO2/M70-3 exposes a greater number of unevenly distributed and high-energy non-ideal active sites, thereby favoring multilayer adsorption. The calculated Freundlich parameter n was equal to 3.11, implying a chemisorption tendency for phenol.45 Furthermore, the Sips equation is a three-parameter isothermal model that accurately and flexibly models non-interacting particle adsorption. At low Ce values, it reduces to the Freundlich model, whereas at high Ce values, it predicts the Langmuir monolayer homogeneous adsorption properties; so it is applicable at various concentrations. As a combination of both equations, the Sips equation indicated a high adsorption capacity and a moderate R2 value. The capacity predicted by the Sips model (qmax,s) was equal to 72.82 mg/g (i.e., 714.53 mg/gMnOx), thereby representing a value that is 2.2 times higher than that of commercial activated carbon (AC) (322.5 mg/g).9 According to qmax,s, the number of electrons required for oxidative removal of a unit of phenol (as calculated from the AOS of Mn 3s) is 2.55 (refer to the “Sips equation” in the SI for further details) or 2.09 (if calculated from the AOS of Mn 2p3/2). This value is close to 2, which is highly consistent with the calculated results of the direct oxidative transfer process (DOTP) of phenol in an acidified and heterogeneous MnOx system.32 It also reflects the advantageous application of the Sips model under similar conditions. Notably, the poor fit of the Tempkin model reflects the non-uniform force of the adsorbed molecules, i.e., the heat of adsorption appears to be nonlinearly correlated with the coverage. Moreover, it was found that the D-R model was a poor fit overall, although the fit was improved slightly at higher concentrations. Consequently, a segmented linear fit was employed for the D-R model. As the concentration was increased from 25 to 250 mg L−1, the adsorption capacity qmaxd-r increased along with the decreasing mean free energy E, suggesting that phenol adsorption transforms from chemisorption-dominated to multilayer adsorption at high concentrations in the near-surface layer. In addition, the R2 value of 0.9305 obtained at medium-to-high concentrations (i.e., 100–250 mg L−1) demonstrates a non-homogeneous mesoporous structure in MnO2/M70-3, indicating that the ameliorative thermoregulation process led to optimization of the original membrane carrier structure. Notably, the inhomogeneity of the surface layer produces abundant Mn(III/IV) active sites to facilitate synergistic adsorption and promote the reaction with phenol.

As shown in Table 5 for the pseudo-first-order model, the kinit parameter was comparable for the first 30 min at all concentrations, except at a low phenol concentration of 25 mg L−1, indicating that the initial rate was less affected by the concentration. Upon increasing the initial phenol concentration, k1 gradually decreased, indicating that a slower diffusion limitation follows the fast initial step.49 The fit of the pseudo-first-order model becomes worse with an increasing concentration, whereas the R2 value of the pseudo-second-order model remains almost constant at 0.999, and the average relative error is small. These results indicate that the chemisorption pathway dominates,50,51 so electron transfer within the generated surface complex is the rate-limiting step considering the fitness of the electron transfer control (ETC) model that supports a two-electron transfer process (R2 > 0.99, see Figure S4D; Table S2). The electron transfer rate constant k', which varied only within a limited range, was unaffected by the concentration of phenol. Although these experiments suggest that the number of available reactive sites (Srxn) is greatly enhanced with an increasing initial phenol concentration C0, the increment in the number of active sites (ΔSrxn) corresponding to the increase in C0 declined, likely due to limitations regarding the total available sites and the number of H+ ions. Furthermore, k″ was found to decrease with an increasing phenol concentration because of competition for limited surface-active sites. This resulted in a decrease in the total adsorption rate constant and the limited growth of Srxn.

Interestingly, the intra-particle diffusion model at each concentration can be divided into two distinct regions using a reaction cutoff of 90 min. At a concentration of 25–50 mg L−1, the adsorption transitioned from being controlled by intra-particle diffusion to being in the equilibrium phase. In contrast, at 100–250 mg L−1, the adsorption transitioned from being controlled by external surface diffusion to undergoing intra-particle diffusion control. With an increasing phenol concentration, kfd (diffusion rate) and C2 (representing the thickness of the boundary layer) both increased, suggesting an increased molecular exchange rate over a larger boundary area, thereby indicating a wider range of influence for the concentration gradient at the reaction interface. Moreover, the straight line fit using the liquid film model did not pass through the origin, implying that external diffusion control was not negligible in the adsorption reaction.52 Upon increasing the phenol concentration, the intercept deviated to a greater extent, whereas kfd converged toward 0.01, suggesting that the controlling effect of liquid film diffusion increased and the film diffusion rate tended to be stable. From these results, it is evident that the adsorption of phenol on MnO2/M70-3 is a complex process. Importantly, increasing the phenol concentration is sufficient to offset the correspondingly increasing resistance of the boundary layer, thereby significantly enhancing phenol consumption under the premise of a rapid contact reaction.

Extended physicochemical calculations

Physicochemical calculations were subsequently conducted to characterize the activity of the adsorption reaction from the perspective of a change in energy. Possible reaction mechanism

The TiO2-based heterogeneous photocatalytic system for phenol removal is generally considered to be based on hydroxyl radical-mediated degradation and valence band hole oxidation. The former is a typical advanced oxidation process (AOP, radical-based 1e− process). Therefore, it mainly involves hydroxyl intermediates, finally yielding various ring-opening oxidized products.57,58,59,60,61,62 Similarly, the MnOx/H+-mediated oxidative coupling system has also long been considered within the AOP framework; however, this classification is mechanistically insufficient and somewhat misleading for the latter. To investigate the actual reaction mechanism, potential intermediates were identified in reactions conducted with 150 mg L−1 phenol at 40°C and pH 2. The normalized ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorbance, shown in Figure S7A, was measured at 240, 247, 256, 289, and 510 nm, using the initial absorbance (t = 0 min) at each wavelength as the reference. The changes in absorbance at these characteristic wavelengths were mapped onto the full-spectrum curve and showed an excellent match, as shown in Figure S7B. The corresponding species are detailed in Table S8 and further discussed in the identification of soluble products section of the STAR Methods. The liquid-phase phenol transformation products are primarily p-benzoquinone, biphenylquinone, and oligomeric phenol derivatives. However, the absence of any free radicals detected by Electron Spin Resonance (ESR) during the reaction, the limited quantity of dissolved products relative to the total phenol removed, and the persistent presence of surface manganese-oxide complexes25 make it difficult to explain the mechanism solely within the framework of conventional AOPs. Although UV-vis characterization of liquid-phase transformation products in the MnOx/phenol system has been established since at least 1992,25 the dominant catalytic oxidation mechanism in this system was only recently confirmed through quantitative analysis.

Recently, through a hypothesis-deduction experiment, Elimelech et al.32 revealed that heterogeneous catalytic oxidation systems, such as the MnOx/phenol/H+ prototype, are dominated by DOTP (non-radical pathways), with the DOTP ratio for phenol ranging from 84% to 91%. This process involves cation-initiated coupling dimerization protocols and a cross-linking hyper-polymerization reaction, both of which are based on two-electron transfer steps that are confined within the solid-water interface. When a system undergoes a 100% DOTP process, the transformation products accumulate in situ on the catalyst surface, and the aqueous pollutants are, in theory, fully oxidatively transferred. This was further supported by thermogravimetric analysis, which showed that the weight loss of reacted MnOx upon heating closely corresponds to the Total Organic Carbon (TOC) removal of phenol from the solution. Therefore, it can be inferred that tracking liquid-phase products is not essential for capturing the dominant heterogeneous reaction mechanisms in MnOx/phenol systems and similar systems. However, in these heterogeneous catalytic oxidation systems, although the trends in solution TOC removal generally align with those of phenol removal, the latter consistently exceeds the former by a small margin. This gap gradually increases as the reaction progresses and may be attributed to the conversion of certain interfacial products into the bulk solution, similar to the dissolved substances observed in our experiments and reported in other studies.

As discussed previously, calculations based on the Sips model and the excellent fit achieved using the ETC model support a two-electron DOTP mechanism for phenol. Furthermore, prior studies have shown that the removal of organic substrates often correlates well with Mn(II) concentrations in the liquid phase.45,63,64,65 Taking into account previous investigations on the removal of functional ring-substituted counterparts,27 a comprehensive oxidative transformation mechanism regarding phenol transformation on the immobilized δ-MnO2 catalyst can be properly derived. In this mechanism, DOTP serves as the primary pathway, integrated with the partial dissolution of products as a secondary process and clearly distinct from conventional AOPs.

As outlined in Figure 8, it was rationalized that the organic species (phenol) binds to the Mn active sites via the deprotonated phenolic hydroxyl groups to form a surface complex. This is followed by electron transfer within the complex and the exfoliation of low-valent Mn species from the surface lattice into the solution, accompanied by the consumption of aqueous H+ to compensate for the release of coordinatively unsaturated oxygen species. As a result, the high-valent Mn species with strong oxidative capacity become exposed, regenerating the solid-liquid reaction interface. The resulting oxidized phenoxy intermediates are rapidly readsorbed onto this newly formed, more coordinatively active surface or preferentially coupled with adjacent reactive species to generate multipoint cross-linking precursors. This sustained process continuously drives the oxidative transfer and accumulation of phenol from the bulk solution to the interface. Under specific conditions, certain relatively stable intermediates are generated via specific pathways and escape from the interface with a measurable probability.

Mechanism of the oxidative transformation of phenol by MnO2/M70-3

Bold lines represent the typical DOTP processes, whereas dashed lines represent AOP and its derivative processes. Steps ix and ix′ serve as auxiliary steps to iv and vi, respectively. The dissolution of Mn(II) and the exposure of underlying high-valent sites are integrated into step x.

More specifically, in the oxidative transformation, phenol initially enters the MnO2/M70-3 boundary layer via diffusion, which is propelled by the concentration gradient (step i). In this system, Mn(IV) serves as a two-electron oxidant, whereas Mn(III) acts as a more reactive single-electron oxidant. Consequently, one phenol molecule is adsorbed onto two interface active sites, consistent with the pseudo-second-order model. Subsequently, phenol displaces the surface hydroxyl group and the phenol hydroxyl group is deprotonated to generate a phenoxy anion (ArO−) with high charge densities on one end, which forms a surface complex with Mn (step ii). Later, the charge is delocalized from the O atom to the benzene ring, resulting in an enhanced electron mobility that facilitates coordination and reduction.66 Since the pKa of phenol is approximately 10,67 the majority of phenol molecules in the liquid phase remain in an undissociated equilibrium state. When the pH is < 10, the oxidation potential of phenol increases linearly with decreasing pH, as depicted in Figure S3C. Given that the oxidation process examined herein occurs in an acidic environment, the rate of this initial oxidation process is highly reliant on the oxidation activity of the membrane-immobilized MnO2.

In the corresponding homogeneous system, the phenoxy anion transfers an electron within the surface complex to yield a phenoxy radical (ArO⋅) in step iii. Then, the phenoxy radical detaches from the surface (in step iv) and self-polymerizes (or reacts with phenol molecules) to generate a C–O–C-coupled dimer with a lower critical oxidation potential than phenol,68 which is susceptible to forming multimers, as shown in Figure 9.69,70 Notably, the phenoxy radical is an electrically neutral dipole whose charge density is mainly centralized at the O atom, rendering it susceptible to attack at the para position. However, the progression from step iii to step iv may occur in phenolic derivatives, as substituents on the aromatic ring can stabilize the radical to some extent. This step likely does not occur in unsubstituted phenol systems, as discussed earlier. The progression from step iii to step iv is extremely limited, as radical-based AOP processes involving single-electron transfer are largely suppressed in heterogeneous catalytic oxidation systems.32 Moreover, the number of monomers involved in the pure AOP-based polymerization was determined to be ≤ 3. This is likely because, without a densely packed active site at the solid-liquid interface, it is difficult to form high-molecular-weight cross-linked complexes.

Additionally, with regard to a homogeneous counterpart system, another electron is then lost from the phenoxy radical produced by step iii, resulting in its oxidation to generate the surface-adsorbed phenoxy cation (ArO+) via step v, a typical AOP-based one-electron process. In addition, if the pH of the reaction solution is lower than the isopotential point of curves A (for generation of the phenoxy radical from phenol) and B (for generation of the phenoxy cation from the phenoxy radical), which occurs at ∼4,71 the phenoxy radical will be in a highly metastable state. Consequently, upon its production, this radical will undergo disproportion into phenol and the corresponding phenoxy cation. In contrast, for a DOTP-dominated heterogeneous system, these two steps (iii and v) should be regarded as one inseparable, consecutive two-electron process in an interface-bound manner.

Subsequently, the phenoxy cations may lead to three types of key two-electron transfer reactions. It may promote the dissociation of the solvent water molecules (step vii), as presented in Figure 10. This is possible due to the robust electrophilic nature of the phenoxy cation, which is further oxidized to p-benzoquinone after the solvolytic reaction. Moreover, when molecules adsorbed on the upper layer react via p-C rather than through the phenoxide group, as delineated in step viii and Figure 11, C–C interfacial coupling reactions can occur. This is because ArO+ is capable of undergoing electrophilic substitution to form biphenol, particularly under acidic conditions.72 The resulting biphenol is then further oxidized to diphenylquinone.73 In addition to the above-described cross-coupling with phenol, ArO+ may also undergo self-coupling with another homolog in its original resonance form. Although many of these products remain bound to the dominant reaction surface and participate in subsequent multipoint cross-linking, a certain portion of the p-benzoquinone and diphenoquinone (the products of steps vii and viii, respectively) is expected to escape from the near-interfacial region into the bulk solution phase, where they can be detected by UV-vis. For instance, this may occur if the phenol bound to Mn(III/IV) sites is sterically or kinetically blocked at the ortho position, or if it is directly quenched by de-coordinated lattice oxygen and released into the solution along with the dissociation of metal sites. These dissolved quinone species are expected to undergo further oxidative coupling and contribute to the formation of dissolved organic matter precursors.39,74,75 ArO+ can also induce a polymerization reaction between carbonium ions (i.e., ortho/para resonators of ArO+) and phenolic hydroxyl groups, in step iv′ (Figure 12). This reaction is a defining characteristic of the DOTP mechanism, which facilitates the oxidative transfer of solution-phase phenol into unextractable, multi-point cross-linked networks anchored at the solid-liquid interface. This behavior is further supported by the fact that Fox Forest soil containing coupling-catalyzing minerals retains a substantial sorbed fraction even after elution and birnessite-amended systems exhibit a significantly enhanced solid-phase equilibrium concentration compared with any individual geosorbent tested.74

In terms of the catalytic cycle, step ix′ can occur with the transient de-coordination of phenoxy species (step vi) to generate the surface adsorption state of Mn(II) before dissolution. In step x, Mn(II) dissociates and is released into the solution, a process that is greatly accelerated in an acidified environment, since the solubility of MnO is three orders of magnitude higher than those of other manganese oxides.32 Thus, renewal of the reaction interface during phenol degradation can be expressed by Figures 13A and 13B, both corresponding to electron transfer, whereas Figure 13C involves Mn(II) exfoliation66 and regeneration of the active surface. Given that the pHpzc of δ-MnO2 lies between 1.5 and 3.7 (usually ∼2.2),35,41,76,77 and considering that the pH increased during the course of the reaction, the immobilized MnO2 can either be neutral or negatively charged. Therefore, in the later stages of the reaction, as the surface electrical properties change and H+ is consumed, the newly formed Mn(II) becomes unable to desorb. Meanwhile, the Mn(II) species present in the liquid phase may undergo re-adsorption, strongly competing for its homologous MnOx sites with organic compounds and subsequently lowering the redox potential of the Mn(IV)/Mn(II) system. This also accounts for the fact that Mn(II) exhibits the highest inhibitory efficiency among a group of metal ions,78 i.e., Co2+, Cu2+, Al3+, Mg2+, and K+, as these cations are unable to stabilize protonated phenols through chelation. Furthermore, cations with lower ionization energies tend to possess more stable hydration shells and cations capable of forming stable hydration shells are known to demonstrate stronger intrinsic inhibitory effects in the removal of organic compounds.79 Mn(II), with its moderate ionization energy and d-orbital electrons that are more likely to induce water polarization, is calculated to exhibit an intrinsic passivation efficiency on active surfaces, which should be comparable to those imparted by transition metal ions such as Cd2+, Cu2+, and Co2+ but weaker than those of Mg2+, K+, and Ca2+78; therefore, the role of a stable hydration shell represents a secondary factor.

Renewal of the reaction interface during the phenol degradation process

The obtained results suggest that the autoxidation of aqueous Mn(II) is highly likely to be hindered, as indicated by the original derivation and conclusion of the ETC/complex formulation control model. This is also implied by the simultaneous termination of the reaction, which is accompanied by the exhaustion of H+, and evidently contradicts the typical autocatalytic phenomenon (step xi). As previously reported,80 the Mn species can exist in the form of tunneled pyrolusite, whose microstructure differs significantly from the δ type. The autocatalytic reaction rate therefore remains continuous throughout the entire process until the organic substrate (methylene blue) is completely mineralized. It is also well known that the morphology and structural characteristics of the catalyst play a key role in its catalytic properties.60 Thus, the released Mn(II) could ideally be autoxidized to form a complete catalytic cycle and allow for a consistent reaction rate over an extended period.80,81 However, this procedure is known to be slow in the absence of a continuous influx of molecular oxygen (as a type of sacrificial agent) or the catalytic influence of highly crystalline pristine mineral surfaces or microorganisms.80 Moreover, for solution reactions, lattice oxygen is unlikely to be replenished by O2 due to the continuously increasing concentration of Mn(II) observed as the reaction progresses, which has been well documented during research into the oxidation of various organic compounds by δ-MnO2.3,31,45,61,70 Additionally, previous studies have shown that if the pH is < 3.5 (such as in the optimal current system), the dissolution of MnO2 could be accelerated, thereby further hindering replenishment by O2.

Finally, two key factors were considered regarding the oxidative degradation of phenol, namely, the abundant oxidation states of Mn and the role of lattice oxygen. Recent evidence has shown that the Mn(III) content is strongly correlated with the reactivity of the system.31,82 More specifically, the anti-bonding eg1 electron present in Mn(III) leads to weaker Mn–O bonds compared with those in Mn(IV) due to the Jahn-Teller effect.25 This enables smoother electron transfer in surface complexes without a necessity to alter the spin state, thereby imparting Mn(III) with a higher reduction potential (Mn(III) + e− → Mn(IV), E° = +1.51 V).29,30 Consequently, surfaces containing Mn(III) species are more catalytically and oxidatively active than pure Mn(IV). In addition, the lattice oxygen desorbed during the interfacial renewal may form surface peroxides78 that can directly oxidize volatile organic compounds and CO. Thus, a higher lattice oxygen content results in a higher oxidation efficiency,83,84,85 which is indispensable for water treatment and biogeochemical processes. Despite these advances, the self-catalytic cycling process of manganese, which is based on its valence state heterogeneity, has yet to be explored, and the ecological fate of various degradation intermediates requires further verification.

Conclusion

In the current study, the oxidative removal of phenol from aqueous solutions was conducted using δ-MnO2, which had been synthesized in situ and immobilized on a tailorable self-supporting membrane carrier (MnO2/M). Following this cost-effective synthetic process, the efficacy of the optimal MnO2/M70-3 species in oxidatively eliminating phenol was verified. During the initial 30 min of the reaction, the reaction rate (Vt) reached 0.45 mg L−1 min−1, giving 97.3% phenol removal after 180 min. Various isothermal models were employed to ascertain the adsorption properties and capacity, with the Freundlich model providing the best fit and the Sips model indicating a high adsorption capacity (72.82 mg g−1). These results suggest that the surface heterogeneity facilitates the exposure of abundant active sites, thereby enhancing molecular exchange and promoting phenol oxidation. Furthermore, several kinetic models were used to elucidate the adsorption mechanism, reaction pathways, and rate-controlling steps. Among these, the pseudo-second-order and ETC models were found to accurately describe the adsorption kinetics, indicating that the reaction predominantly involves chemisorption and that charge transfer within the surface complex is the rate-limiting step. Additionally, the intra-particle diffusion and liquid film models were used to elucidate sequential changes in the diffusion mechanism. Subsequent physicochemical calculations revealed the reaction to be exothermic and spontaneous, with a Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) of −31.65 kJ mol−1 at 298 K and an activation energy (Ea) ranging from 3.37 to 8.78 kJ mol−1. Finally, the reaction products were qualitatively analyzed and the detailed DOTP mechanism responsible for the removal of phenol by δ-MnO2 was elucidated and distinguished from AOP processes, underscoring the significance of manganese oxides as vital environmental oxidants. Future work should focus on exploring the long-term stability and regeneration of the MnO2-loaded membrane, particularly regarding fouling and pH adjustments, to enhance its practical applicability in water treatment. The experimental methodology and modeling approaches presented herein are expected to inspire future research on environmental remediation using advanced Mn-based materials.

Limitations of the study

This study presents a detailed model study on the degradation of phenol using membrane-supported MnO2, and, based on various validations, proposes a comprehensive reaction mechanism that provides valuable guidance for future research. However, the study has certain limitations. First, due to laboratory constraints, certain quantitative analysis results require further exploration. Additionally, the self-catalytic cycle of manganese, particularly its valence-state heterogeneity, has not been fully investigated. The ecological fate of degradation intermediates also needs further exploration. Finally, future research should focus on the long-term stability and regeneration capacity of membrane-supported MnO2, particularly regarding pollutant accumulation and pH adjustments, to enhance its practical applicability in water treatment.

Preparation of the membrane-immobilized MnO2

The immobilization of MnO2 was performed via a facile low-temperature hydrothermal method under atmospheric pressure. Initially, a 0.01 mol L−1 KMnO4 solution was prepared as solution A, and a 0.01 mol L−1 solution of Na2S2O3 was prepared as solution B; both solutions were prepared using deionized water as the solvent. The membrane was cut into 2×2 cm2 pieces and dried in a drying oven at 55 °C. Subsequently, the preheated membrane pieces were submerged in a beaker containing solution A (50 mL), which had been preheated to 55 °C. After maintaining the beaker in a water bath at 55 °C for 2 h, the resulting KMnO4-impregnated membranes were dried at 55 °C for 5 h, immersed in solution B for 12 h, and dried again in a drying oven at 55 °C to obtain the desired MnO2/M55-2. Alternatively, after initial submergence in solution A at 55 °C for 1 h, the beaker was heated to 70 °C and maintained for 3 h. After this time, the sample was dried at 70 °C, immersed in solution B (50 mL), and dried at 70 °C to obtain the sample denoted as MnO2/M70-3. Notably, maintaining the solution at 55 °C for 1 h before heating to 70 °C ensures that the solution completely replaces the gas present in the polymer carrier, thereby preventing the film from rising to the surface during subsequent heating. Upon prolonging the immersion time of the tailored membrane in solution A (70 °C, 1–4 h), the weight gain of the membrane after immersion in solution A was linearly proportional to the weight loss after immersion in solution B (Figure S8). Therefore, the net weight gain was almost constant. After loading, the average weight gain of the membrane was 45.02 mg, and the average mass was 0.44175 g for one piece (2×2 cm2) of membrane. Therefore, the load corresponding to the unit mass of the loaded membrane was determined to be 0.04502g/0.44175g, which is equal to 0.1019 g/g.

Phenol chromogenic method (PCM)

The phenol chromogenic method (PCM), proposed by Emerson in 1943,86 is a widely used technique for the detection of phenols. It involves the reaction of phenolic compounds with 4-aminoantipyrine at pH 9.4–10.2 in the presence of potassium ferricyanide,87 as shown in Figure S3. The alkaline environment facilitates the main reaction, and the use of K3[Fe(CN)6] as an oxidant was considered relatively safe. The red-brown chromogenic product exhibits an optimal absorbance peak at 510 nm,88 whose intensity is linearly correlated with the phenol concentration in the liquid phase (optimal linear range = 0.1–5 mg L-1). Thus, due to its safety and convenience, the PCM is the national standard for phenol detection in many countries.89 The PCM was performed as described and according to the mechanism presented in Figure S10. For a reaction system with an initial phenol concentration of 150 mg L−1, samples (1 mL) were taken periodically. Subsequently, deionized water (9 mL), a buffer solution (100 μL, 25 wt% ammonia solution (100 mL) containing NH4Cl (20 g)), a 2 wt% 4-aminoantipyrine solution (200 μL), and an 8 wt% potassium ferricyanide solution (200 μL) were added in sequence, shaken, and allowed to stand for 10 min at 25 °C. The resulting mixture was diluted to 50 mL using deionized water, UV-vis measurements were conducted as described above, and the liquid phase concentration was obtained by conversion according to the standard curve. Due to the possibility of some loose MnO2 powder being detached from the surface under stirring conditions to produce a slightly turbid solution, the chromogenic phenol solution was filtered through filter paper prior to analysis. Notably, such filtration had no detrimental effect on the UV-vis absorption intensity of the chromogenic phenol.

Benefit analyses of the carrier immobilization strategy and cost

Comparison with the powdered state : the advantage of carrier-immobilized MnO2, compared to other MnO2-based materials, mainly lies in the improved stabilization, superior long-term dispersibility, improved reactivity, enhanced flexibility, and customizable properties. In addition, the carrier-immobilized MnO2 system described herein features a mild synthesis method and theoretically lower levels of energy consumption, thereby rendering it more environmentally friendly and portable. Furthermore, it experiences lower amounts of powder leakage with a stable loading, which helps reduce the performance degradation typically caused by agglomeration in traditional particle degradation processes. Moreover, this membrane-loaded MnO2 system may offer valuable insights in the context of membrane permeation reactors. In this work, based on a calculation of the composite membrane mass, the qe of the reaction at 250 min was determined to be 7.7 mg g-1 for the acidified low concentration phenol system. Performing the same calculation based on the net MnO2 loading mass, the qe was determined to be 75.3 mg g-1. For the same acidified low concentration phenol system, Jiang et al.78 reported a qe of 3.2 mg g-1 at 250 min. Generally speaking, the lower the phenol concentration, the larger the rate constant for the first or second stage reaction. In the report of Jiang et al., the k1 rate constant for the first stage of the reaction was determined to be 0.0069 min−1, while in the current study it was determined to be 0.022 min−1. These results indicate that the MnO2 species that were dispersed and fixed on the carrier demonstrate significantly higher adsorption capacities and more efficient degradation kinetics compared to traditional powdered MnO2. Furthermore, according to a report.,32 the powdered MnO2 showed significant agglomeration following the degradation of phenol, thereby potentially affecting subsequent interface updates and the active site availability. Notably, the membrane-immobilized MnO2 described in the current study is comparable with previous MnO2 systems in terms of its organic catalytic oxidation performance, reaction times, and experimental/application conditions.

Brief comparison with membrane separation materials: Advanced hydrophobic pervaporation membranes used for the separation of aqueous phenol solutions continue to face significant challenges,91 such as membrane fouling, poor membrane durabilities, high membrane material costs, a trade-off effect between the permeation flux and the separation efficiency, and complex molecular-level selectivity designs. In addition, they tend to suffer from issues related to their long-term stability and operability, temperature and pressure dependence, difficulties in handling high-concentration solutions, thermodynamic control, and the trial-and-error protocol associated with the film formation process. Ultimately, these challenges restrict the large-scale application of such membranes. Regarding the cost issue, Khan et al.92 recently reported the use of MOF materials as selective functional layers for the pervaporation membrane separation of aqueous phenol solutions. However, the core structural unit, 2-ethylimidazole, costs 38.5 USD per 500 g, and the synthetic protocol requires long processing times (mechanical stirring and ultrasonic dispersion treatment). Furthermore, their approach employs large volumes of organic solvents for cleaning, and requires time-consuming baking treatments at temperatures >100 °C. Moreover, no anti-fouling evaluation was provided for the membrane. In contrast, the raw material for laboratory-prepared MnOx-based materials, i.e., potassium permanganate, has a significantly lower cost of 3.86 USD per 500 g, and the synthetic protocol requires only a low-temperature (i.e., 50 °C) routine drying stage. Furthermore, MnOx is found naturally in abundant sources such as continental soils and marine sedimentary minerals, thereby leading to significant economic and ecological advantages. Additionally, the treatment of organic compounds, including phenol, with MnOx-based materials involves the catalytic oxidation of phenol that allows its integration into the geophysical and chemical cycles of the Earth,29,30 whereas membrane separation emphasizes recovery and enrichment, requiring significant and specific instrumentation for its industrial application.

Isotherm theories

Adsorption isotherms are essential for calculating the equilibrium concentration of a solute. More specifically, an absorption isotherm curve relates the concentration of a solute on the surface of an adsorbent, qe, to the concentration of the solute in the liquid, Ce, with which it is in contact. Such models are widely used to predict adsorption behaviors and play an indispensable role in applied adsorption research.93 The isotherm models employed herein for fitting the experimental data are summarized in Table 3, and further details can be found in the Supporting Information.

The Langmuir model94 is widely employed to describe chemisorption systems involving covalent or ionic bonds between the adsorbate and the adsorbent. It predicts that adsorption capacity is linearly correlated with concentration at low concentrations and exhibits maximum coverage at high concentrations by addressing the equilibrium between adsorption and desorption. The Langmuir model assumes finite specific sites on a solid surface, in addition to equivalent sites, monolayer adsorption, and a lack of intermolecular forces. However, this idealized model often deviates from reality because it does not consider the surface roughness or lateral forces. This model is processed by fitting Ce/qe with Ce to define the Langmuir isotherm constant KL (mg·L−1) and the maximum capacity of MnO2/M70-3 for phenol qmax (mg·g−1), where Ce (mg·L−1) and qe (mg·g−1) are the phenol concentrations in the liquid and solid phases, respectively, after an infinite reaction time (3 h in the present study).

The Freundlich model95 considers the surface heterogeneity, introducing an exponential distribution of active sites with varying binding strengths. It was the first reported isothermal adsorption model and is purely empirical, disregarding Henry's law at low concentrations. In this model, n values between 1 and 10 indicate chemisorption, while n < 1 represents synergistic adsorption and n values close to 0 indicate inhomogeneous adsorption. The parameters for the Freundlich model can be obtained by plotting lnqe versus lnCe. This gives KF and n, which are the Freundlich isotherm constants related to the adsorption capacity and the adsorption intensity, respectively.

The Tempkin model55 is a modification of the Langmuir model, accounting for a uniform binding energy distribution, indirect adsorbate interactions, and a linear decrease in adsorption heat with increasing coverage. The Tempkin parameters were obtained by fitting qe versus lnCe,. In the corresponding equation, bT (J·mol−1) is the Temkin isotherm constant associated with the heat of adsorption, KT (L·g−1) is the equilibrium adsorption constant corresponding to the maximum adsorption energy, R is the ideal gas constant (8.314 J·mol−1·K−1), and T is the absolute temperature (K). If bT > 0, the adsorption is considered to be exothermic.

The Dubinin–Radushkevich (D-R) equation97 is a semi-empirical model that follows a pore-filling mechanism and assumes the presence of van der Waals forces in multilayer adsorption. It applies to physical adsorption processes, mostly describing non-homogeneous surfaces with Gaussian energy distributions. Initially applied to the microporous adsorption process of subcritical vapors, the D-R equation has been extended to study liquid phase adsorption, but is only suitable at medium-to-high concentration ranges. Upon fitting lnqe versus e2, the parameters of the D-R were obtained. In this equation, qd-r (mg·g−1) is the maximum sorption capacity, b (mol2·J−2) denotes the activity coefficient associated with the average sorption energy, e = RTln(1 + 1/Ce) is the Polanyi potential energy, and E = (2β)−1/2 is the apparent sorption energy.

The Sips equation96 is a three-parameter isothermal model that accurately and flexibly models non-interacting particle adsorption. It converges to the Freundlich model near equilibrium concentrations, and is applicable at various concentrations. In addition, at low Ce values, it reduces to the Freundlich model, while at high Ce values, it predicts the Langmuir monolayer homogeneous adsorption properties, representing a combination of both models. The Sips parameters were obtained by the non-linear fitting of 1/qe versus 1/Ce. In this case, KS (L·mg−1) is the Sips equilibrium constant, qmax,s (mg·g−1) is the maximum adsorption capacity, and n is the dimensionless heterogeneity factor used to determine the non-homogeneous adsorption characteristics.

Kinetic theories

Adsorption kinetics can be used to describe the time-dependent adsorption behavior of a system, and assist in identifying the rate-limiting steps to provide valuable predictive insights into the mechanism of the adsorption reaction.47 Several kinetic models were employed to fit the experimental data with the concentration as a variable to probe the adsorption mechanism of phenol and identify the potential rate-controlling process (Table 4). Further details regarding the theory and fitting methodologies can be found in the Supporting Information (as in Figure S11 and Table S9 for the pseudo first-order rate equation).

The pseudo-first order rate equation48 postulates that a phenol molecule adheres to one surface site, allowing characterization of the adsorption in a solid–liquid system based on the adsorption capacity of the solid. The parameters of the pseudo-first order rate equation were obtained by fitting log(qe−qt) versus 1/Ce, where qt is the phenol concentration in the solid phase at any reaction time t. To eliminate the uncertainty of long-term experiments, enhance the controllability of data processing, and simplify the experimental procedure, qe was obtained by extending the time t infinitely using trial-and-error methods. The first order rational function f(x) = (b + cx)/(1 + ax) was used to predict the phenol consumption over time. Thus, the predicted equilibrium adsorption capacity was denoted as qe,exp, and this value fell below the upper bound of the total phenol supply qe,upp (mg·g−1), as depicted in Figure S4 and Table S9.

The pseudo-second order equation99 assumes that a phenol molecule attaches to two surface sites, and this equation has been commonly employed to analyze the chemisorption kinetics of solid–liquid systems. By plotting t/qt versus t, the rate constant for the pseudo-second order adsorption (i.e., k2, g mg−1 h−1) can be determined from the intercept and the slope.

The ETC (electron transfer control) and CFC (complex formation control) equations are specific to MnO2-induced oxidation, as proposed by Zhang et al.41 They are applicable when precursor complex formation is significantly faster than electron transfer, or when precursor complex formation is significantly slower than electron transfer.

The intra-particle diffusion model52 is widely used to describe the mass transfer and adsorption behaviors of adsorbates at solid interfaces. According to this model, the total adsorption rate is mainly controlled by liquid film diffusion (outer surface or boundary layer diffusion) or intra-particle diffusion (pore diffusion or internal diffusion). If qt and t1/2 are linear across the origin, the latter represents the single rate-controlling step; if it is multilinear, it implies that the process contains multiple rate-controlling steps, which may involve chemical reactions or boundary-layer diffusion. By plotting qt versus t1/2, the intraparticle diffusion constant kid (mg g−1 min0.5) is obtained.

The liquid film diffusion model52 assumes that the adsorption resistance is centered in the boundary layer. If the fitted straight line passes through the origin, then intra-particle diffusion is the rate-controlling step; if not, liquid film diffusion is the rate-controlling step. By plotting ln(1 − qt/qe) versus t, the liquid film diffusion constant kfd (min−1) is obtained.

Identification of soluble products

As shown in Figure S7 and Table S8, p-benzoquinone and biphenylquinone are primarily detected in the solution during phenol oxidation. Moreover, Ukrainczy et al.25 also reported the gas chromatography/Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy/mass spectroscopy (GC/IR/MS) results of a reacted solution in which the initial feed phenol was fully transformed. Peaks at 1730 and 1642 cm-1 indicates the C=O and C=C stretching of quniol and benzene ring, respectively. MS (m/z scan range: 40–550) showed three prominent signals at m/z = 346, 390, and 390. Considering (1) the instability of biphenylquinone, whose reductive potential is slightly higher than that of phenol, and (2) that m/z 390 spans a relatively large window period of 160 near the upper detection limit, these MS signals could be ascribed to oligomers comprising four or three phenol molecular units, possibly with phenolic or quinoid structures.
