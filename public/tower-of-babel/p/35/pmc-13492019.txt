The gut-lung axis in ARDS: beyond microbial translocation
Authors: Xiaofeng Li, Xinyu Zhang, Shiying Yuan, Jiancheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut-lung axis has emerged as a pivotal pathway in the pathogenesis of acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Disruption of the intestinal barrier, a common event in critical illness, facilitates the systemic dissemination of live microbiota, their pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), and bioactive metabolites. This process critically depends on the integrity of the gut vascular barrier (GVB). The GVB is the endothelial layer underlying the gut epithelium. It serves as the final gatekeeper, restricting microbial products from entering the systemic circulation. Concurrently, intestinal immune cells, such as γδ T cells and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), migrate to the lungs and amplify the inflammatory cascade. Emerging evidence links regulated cell death, especially pyroptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis, to disruption of both gut and lung barriers, fueling a self-amplifying cycle of organ injury. This review synthesizes current evidence on the cellular, molecular, and metabolic mechanisms underlying gut-derived lung injury. Furthermore, we critically evaluate several emerging gut-targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring microbial homeostasis and mitigating ALI/ARDS, including fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), probiotics, synbiotics, and mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy. Deciphering the gut-lung dialogue holds promise for developing novel treatments for this devastating condition.

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) and its severe manifestation, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), are life-threatening clinical conditions. They are characterized by refractory hypoxemia, diffuse pulmonary inflammation, and disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier. Despite advances in supportive care, morbidity and mortality remain high, underscoring the need to elucidate underlying pathogenic mechanisms [1, 2]. Traditional research has predominantly focused on pulmonary-centric pathways. However, emerging evidence highlights the critical role of systemic and remote organ interactions in ARDS pathophysiology, notably through the gut-lung axis [3].

The gut-lung axis refers to the bidirectional crosstalk between the gastrointestinal and respiratory systems, mediated by microbial communities, their metabolites, and immune cells. In critical illness, intestinal barrier dysfunction—a common event in conditions like sepsis and trauma—becomes a pivotal driver of distant organ injury [4, 5]. This dysfunction compromises selective permeability, facilitating the translocation of gut-derived components into systemic circulation [6]. Critically, these components trigger multiple regulated cell death (RCD) programs in both intestinal and pulmonary compartments, amplifying barrier disruption and inflammatory injury.

Furthermore, gut dysbiosis, often induced by intensive care unit (ICU) interventions, alters the production of key microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). These metabolites play essential roles in modulating immunity [7]. Concurrently, intestinal immune cells, including γδ T cells and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), can migrate to the lungs under inflammatory conditions. This process is a significant mechanism in the gut-lung axis contributing to ALI/ARDS [8, 9].

This review aims to provide a comprehensive and mechanistic overview of the gut-lung axis in ALI/ARDS. We will first dissect the breach of the intestinal barrier as the initiating event. Subsequently, we will delineate the ensuing culprits fueling pulmonary inflammation. These include translocating bacteria, their immunogenic products (e.g., lipopolysaccharide [LPS]), and dysregulated microbial metabolites (e.g., SCFAs, succinate). The contribution of gut-originating immune cells to the pulmonary inflammatory milieu will be examined in detail. Finally, we will appraise the translational potential of interventions targeting the gut compartment, from microbiota manipulation to cellular therapies, and highlight pressing questions and future directions in this rapidly evolving field.

Intestinal barrier

Structure and function of the intestinal barrier

The intestinal barrier constitutes the body’s largest and most critical interface with the external environment. It functions not as a passive wall but as a highly integrated and dynamic ecosystem. This ecosystem comprises interdependent physical, chemical, microbial, and immunological layers that work in concert to maintain selective permeability and host homeostasis [6, 10].

At its core lies the physical barrier, formed by a single layer of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) intricately connected via tight junctions (TJs), adherens junctions, and desmosomes [11]. The integrity of this cellular sheet and its junctional complexes is paramount. Their disruption compromises barrier selectivity, which permits the translocation of microorganisms, PAMPs, and other luminal contents, thereby triggering gut inflammation and systemic sequelae [12].

This epithelial layer is shielded and modulated by the chemical barrier, which includes the mucus layer and a cocktail of antimicrobial substances such as lysozyme, defensins, and regenerating family member 3 (REG3) proteins. This chemical shield protects epithelial cells from digestive enzymes, toxins, and direct bacterial contact while facilitating nutrient absorption [6, 13].

Residing within and profoundly shaping this environment is the microbial barrier, primarily the gut microbiota. This dense community forms a living layer that competitively excludes pathogens and, through symbiotic interactions, is indispensable for maintaining barrier homeostasis [14]. A healthy microbiota upregulates the expression of tight junction and fimbrial proteins, thereby reinforcing mucosal structural integrity [15, 16]. Critically, its metabolic activity generates a stream of bioactive molecules that are essential for regulating barrier function and local immunity. Conversely, dysbiosis—an imbalance in this microbial community—can disrupt the mucosal barrier, precipitating inflammation and immune dysregulation [12, 15].

Orchestrating defense and tolerance is the sophisticated immune barrier, the densest concentration of immune cells in the body. This system operates at multiple levels: intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) patrol the epithelial layer; a diverse array of innate and adaptive immune cells (e.g., macrophages, dendritic cells, T cells, B cells) populate the lamina propria; and organized gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) coordinates regional immune responses. Specialized cells like Paneth cells secrete antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) for direct pathogen killing. T cells regulate immune tone and epithelial repair through cytokine production (e.g., interferon (IFN)-γ, interleukin (IL)-17 A), while B cells are pivotal for generating secretory IgA (sIgA), a key antibody for mucosal immune surveillance and maintaining commensal balance [11, 17]. The precise coordination among these four barrier components is fundamental to health, and their collective dysfunction represents a critical first step in the pathogenesis of gut-derived systemic diseases, including acute lung injury.

Beyond the four canonical barrier components lies a fifth, increasingly recognized layer: the intestinal endothelium, termed the gut vascular barrier (GVB) [18]. The GVB consists of a single layer of intestinal endothelial cells interconnected by tight and adherens junctions. This layer constitutes the final anatomical checkpoint that restricts luminal contents from entering the bloodstream [19]. In health, the GVB selectively permits the passage of small molecules while effectively barring the entry of bacteria and larger microbial products. The GVB is not merely a passive filter but a metabolically active interface: upon systemic inflammation, β-catenin-dependent signaling in gut endothelial cells is compromised, leading to GVB dismantling and increased accessibility of the vascular compartment for translocating bacteria. Importantly, GVB disruption often precedes or occurs independently of epithelial barrier damage, representing an earlier and more direct route for gut-derived PAMPs (e.g., LPS) to enter the portal and systemic circulations [20]. This hierarchical organization, with the GVB acting as the “second wall of defense” behind the epithelial barrier provides a more complete framework for understanding how gut-derived signals reach the lungs.

Common causes of intestinal barrier dysfunction in the ICU

Intestinal barrier injury is ubiquitous in the ICU, affecting approximately 50% of critically ill patients [21]. Its dysfunction arises from a convergence of insults, which can be conceptualized as a “triple-hit” model [8, 22].

Hit 1: Direct pathological insults

Primary conditions like sepsis and acute pancreatitis (AP) directly assault the barrier. Sepsis, with a high ICU mortality, suppresses IEC regeneration and promotes apoptosis. Inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor (TNF), IL-6) dysregulate tight junction protein expression (e.g., upregulating claudin-2, downregulating ZO-1) and activate myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK). Collectively, these effects increase intestinal permeability [23–27]. Cytokine-induced redistribution of claudins further disrupts barrier integrity [28]. Similarly, in severe AP, a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) triggered by a cytokine cascade severely damages intestinal barrier function, increases permeability, and causes gut dysbiosis and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) [29]. Management strategies like fasting and parenteral nutrition during severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) exacerbate the problem by creating an intestinal energy deficit, diminishing IgA production, and accelerating mucosal atrophy [30, 31].

Hit 2: Iatrogenic and supportive interventions

ICU therapies often have unintended gut-toxic effects (Fig. 1). Antibiotics are used in about 75% of ICU patients [32]. They induce dysbiosis, depleting beneficial commensals and promoting colonization by pathogens like Clostridioides difficile and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VRE). Consequently, this weakens barrier function and amplifying systemic inflammation [33]. Antibiotics also impair the immune tolerance of intestinal macrophages, leading to excessive inflammatory cytokine production upon bacterial stimulation [34]. While some microbial recovery occurs post-cessation, specific taxa may be permanently lost, leading to protracted dysbiosis [12, 34, 35]. Notably, the consequences of microbiota depletion are context-dependent, as antibiotic-induced alterations have been shown to paradoxically protect against LPS-induced ALI via the gut-lung axis in some settings [36]. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), used for stress ulcer prophylaxis, alter microbiota composition and diversity, increase intestinal permeability. They may also impair tight junction integrity by upregulating MLCK activity [37–39]. Catecholamines, vital for hemodynamic support, can reduce gut microcirculatory perfusion, risk intestinal ischemia, and directly stimulate intestinal pathogen proliferation, potentially exacerbating barrier failure and immune paralysis [40–42]. Parenteral nutrition (PN) disrupts the gut microbiota’s metabolic balance, reduces sIgA, and in the long term, suppresses immune activation, inducing mucosal atrophy and barrier dysfunction, thereby elevating the risk of bacterial translocation [43–45]. Beyond these, factors like mechanical ventilation, sedatives, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) further impair gut-lung homeostasis through multiple pathways [46].

Hit 3: Ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) and inflammatory feed-forward loops

In shock states (hemorrhagic, septic, traumatic), intestinal I/R injury is a critical determinant of barrier failure [47]. I/R degrades the mucus layer, diminishes absorptive capacity, and increases paracellular permeability [48, 49]. The resulting bacterial and endotoxin translocation can instigate ALI/ARDS [50]. Crucially, this initiates a vicious cycle: barrier damage allows translocation of PAMPs/damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which fuel systemic hyperinflammation, which in turn further damages the barrier through the mechanisms described in Hit 1 [51, 52]. This self-amplifying loop establishes the gut as a persistent driver of remote organ injury (Table 1).

Endothelial barriers in the gut-lung axis

In critical illness, the GVB is disrupted via cytokine- and LPS-induced glycocalyx shedding, β-catenin destabilization, and inter-endothelial junction disassembly, often preceding epithelial injury [20, 53]. This GVB failure permits systemic dissemination of PAMPs (e.g., LPS) and metabolites [54]. Concurrently, in the lung, pulmonary microvascular endothelial dysfunction directly drives ARDS pathology: loss of VE-cadherin-dependent junctions, endothelial cell death (apoptosis/pyroptosis), and proteolytic cleavage of junctional proteins widen paracellular gaps, causing protein-rich edema and neutrophilic inflammation [55, 56]. Thus, the gut-lung axis operates through a “two-endothelial-barrier” model: the GVB controls the magnitude of gut-derived signal entry into the circulation, while the pulmonary endothelium determines lung vulnerability to those signals. Dual dysfunction of both barriers is likely required for full expression of gut-derived ALI/ARDS.

Importantly, the integrity of both barriers is compromised not only by mechanical disruption of intercellular junctions but also by RCD pathways, most notably pyroptosis. Systemic endotoxemia originates from increased gut permeability. It triggers non-canonical inflammasome activation and caspase-11-dependent pyroptosis in the lung, including in alveolar macrophages and endothelium. This contributes to alveolar-capillary barrier disruption and ALI [57]. Cheng et al. demonstrated that caspase-11-mediated endothelial pyroptosis is a key driver of endotoxemia-induced lung injury, where pyroptotic endothelial cells undergo gasdermin D (GSDMD)-dependent membrane permeabilization, leading to barrier failure and protein-rich edema formation [58]. The Two-pore domain Weak Inwardly rectifying K(+) channel 2 (TWIK2) potassium efflux channel in macrophages mediates nod-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and subsequent caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis, thereby driving sepsis-induced lung inflammation [59]. Mechanistically, the TWIK2 potassium efflux channel in macrophages mediates NLRP3 inflammasome-induced inflammation, and IL-1β subsequent suppression of VE-cadherin transcription further compromises endothelial junctional integrity [59, 60]. Collectively, these findings establish pyroptosis as a central effector mechanism linking gut-derived endotoxemia to dual-barrier failure in ALI/ARDS.

Gut microbiota translocation aggravates lung injury

The gut microbiota, a complex community of ~ 10–100 trillion microorganisms dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, is pivotal for host health and lung function [61–63]. A dynamic equilibrium can alleviate lung injury and maintain immune balance [64]. Its early-life colonization is essential for the normal development of the immune and respiratory systems, and factors like delivery mode, feeding, and antibiotic use can diminish its diversity, exerting long-term detrimental effects on susceptibility to asthma, allergies, and severe infections [65–67]. Notably, pulmonary diseases like asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) frequently co-occur with GI disorders like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), highlighting systemic crosstalk [68].

Dysbiosis as a Precursor to Injury. In ALI/ARDS, gut dysbiosis manifests as a depletion of beneficial commensals (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium) and an expansion of pathobionts (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae, Clostridioides difficile). This shift weakens the gut barrier, impairs immunomodulation, increases intestinal permeability, and induces intestinal inflammation, allowing bacteria and PAMPs to translocate to the lungs [69].

Evidence for Translocation in ALI/ARDS. A growing body of evidence supports the translocation of gut-derived bacteria to the lungs during critical illness. However, a key challenge remains: distinguishing robust causal relationships from mere correlations.

Human observational studies

Consistent clinical evidence indicates that the lung microbiome of patients with ARDS is profoundly altered. Multiple cohorts have reported decreased alpha diversity in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and relative enrichment of gut-associated taxa, particularly Enterobacteriaceae and Pasteurellaceae. These taxa are typically rare in healthy lungs [70–72]. The pulmonary abundance of these taxa correlates positively with key inflammatory markers (e.g., TNF, IL-6) and disease severity scores [70, 71, 73]. Moreover, the ratio of putatively beneficial bacteria (e.g., butyrate-producing Lachnospiraceae) to potentially pathogenic bacteria in the lung predicts clinical outcomes, including ventilator-free days (VFDs) and survival [74]. Although these human data compellingly support an association, they cannot definitively prove that gut bacteria directly instigate lung injury, owing to confounding by shared host factors (e.g., antibiotics, mechanical ventilation) and the inability to distinguish translocation from cross-sample contamination or concurrent colonization from environmental sources.

Animal models

Experimental models provide the critical mechanistic evidence lacking in human studies. In neonatal mice, intestinal commensal bacteria translocate to the lung, where they paradoxically enhance resistance to pathogens while also triggering IL-6-mediated systemic inflammation [75]. More direct evidence derives from gnotobiotic and antibiotic-depletion models. For example, antibiotic-induced gut dysbiosis before an insult can either exacerbate [76] or, paradoxically, protect against LPS-induced ALI, depending on the specific microbial composition and context, underscoring that not all forms of dysbiosis are equivalent [36]. However, the most compelling causal evidence comes from mesenteric lymph duct ligation studies: surgical interruption of this lymphatic conduit, the primary route for gut-derived factors, markedly reduces the incidence of ALI in rat models of shock [77]. Conversely, direct infusion of septic mesenteric lymph (but not portal plasma) into healthy recipients is sufficient to induce acute lung injury [78]. These interventional studies firmly establish that gut-derived factors in the lymph are both sufficient and necessary for lung injury in these models.

The current evidence supports a causal role for the gut-lung axis in ALI in animal models, accompanied by strong associative data in humans (Table 2). Nevertheless, the translation from association to causation in patients remains incomplete. Thus, while the field has moved beyond mere speculation, any claim of causality must be tempered by acknowledgment of the existing evidence gaps, particularly the need for interventional human trials specifically targeting gut-derived translocation pathways.

The mesenteric lymphatic pathway

A Conduit and an Immunologic Processor. The route of translocation is pivotal. While the portal vein is a potential route [79], neither bacteria nor endotoxins were consistently detected in the portal blood of MODS patients [80]. Contemporary research identifies the mesenteric lymphatic system as the dominant pathway for gut-derived injurious factors [81]. Ligation or drainage of intestinal lymphatics in rats markedly reduces ALI incidence [77], and infusion of septic mesenteric lymph—but not portal plasma—induces lung injury in healthy recipients [78]. This pathway is not a passive pipe; it is an active immunologic compartment. Mesenteric lymph carries toxic factors, inflammatory mediators, and potentially immune cells. While resident macrophages may phagocytose some bacteria, bacterial fragments, proteins, and cytokines may evade clearance, activating immune responses in GALT or lymph nodes and contributing to ALI/ARDS [24, 61]. Thus, the mesenteric lymph delivers a “packaged inflammatory insult” from the gut directly to the pulmonary circulation via the thoracic duct, making the lungs a primary target [52].

Beyond the translocation of PAMPs and live bacteria, gut-derived inflammatory signals trigger multiple RCD pathways in both intestinal and pulmonary compartments, which profoundly shape the severity of ALI/ARDS. In the gut, IECs undergo not only apoptosis but also pyroptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis under critical illness [82]. Notably, GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis simultaneously impairs the gut barrier and the lung in LPS-induced ALI, and pharmacological targeting of GSDMD with disulfiram attenuates injury in both organs [83], highlighting a shared mechanistic node. In the lung, endothelial pyroptosis, driven by intracellular LPS-sensing inflammatory caspases (caspase-4/5 in humans or caspase-11 in mice) via GSDMD cleavage, releases IL-1β and disrupts the lung vascular barrier, thereby underlying endotoxemia-induced ALI [58]. Necroptosis, executed by RIPK1/RIPK3-mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL), drives lytic cell death and DAMP release, and its inhibition attenuates ALI [84]. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent lipid peroxidation pathway, has recently emerged as a key contributor to sepsis-induced ALI/ARDS [85]. The crosstalk among pyroptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis creates a self-amplifying inflammatory loop that sustains lung injury and hinders resolution [86].

Endothelial contribution to LPS systemic spread

The translocation of gut-derived LPS into the systemic circulation is not solely governed by epithelial permeability; the GVB plays a gatekeeping role equally critical. LPS crosses the intestinal epithelium via transcellular transport, either by lipid raft/caveolae-mediated or clathrin-mediated endocytosis [87]. Under dietary fat conditions, LPS is incorporated into chylomicrons and released into the mesenteric lymphatics for systemic dissemination [88]. However, for LPS to reach the pulmonary circulation, it must first cross the GVB. Thus, the GVB and pulmonary endothelium (controlling systemic entry and lung vulnerability, respectively) are critical, previously underappreciated nodes in the gut-lung translocation cascade.

Gut microbiota-derived metabolites regulate lung injury

The gut-lung axis operates not only through direct microbial translocation but also via a complex signaling network of microbiota-derived metabolites. These small molecules, including SCFAs, succinate, bile acids, amino acid derivatives, and structural components like LPS, function as remote endocrine and paracrine signals that critically regulate systemic and pulmonary immunity, inflammation, and tissue integrity [7]. Their collective balance—shifting from a homeostatic to a pro-inflammatory profile during dysbiosis—dictates the nature of the signal transmitted from the gut to the lung.

SCFAs, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are produced through microbial fermentation of dietary fiber and are indispensable for intestinal homeostasis [7]. In the gut, they exert multifaceted protective effects: butyrate serves as the primary energy source for colonocytes, promoting a hypoxic lumen that stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor-1alpha (HIF-1α) and upregulates barrier genes [89, 90]. Furthermore, SCFAs directly enhance the chemical barrier by stimulating goblet cells to secrete mucins (MUC2, MUC4) and trefoil factors [91–93]. Their immunomodulatory actions are mediated via two principal mechanisms: activation of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs, e.g., GPR41/43) and inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs) [94]. Binding to GPCRs on IECs can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to IL-18 release, which is crucial for epithelial integrity [95]. HDAC inhibition by SCFAs, particularly butyrate, suppresses nuclear factor kappaB (NF-κB) activation in macrophages and neutrophils, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine (TNF, IL-6, IL-1β) production [96, 97]. Concurrently, HDAC inhibition and modulation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)-S6K pathway promote the differentiation of anti-inflammatory regulatory T cells (Tregs) and increase IL-10 secretion, critically maintaining the Th17/Treg balance [98, 99]. Notably, butyrate also induces synaptopodin expression via HDAC inhibition, further consolidating barrier function [96].

Via systemic circulation, SCFAs exert direct protective effects on the lungs. They enhance macrophage phagocytosis and autophagy while reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production, through GPCR activation and HDAC inhibition, effectively lowering pulmonary bacterial load and inflammation [100]. In adaptive immunity, SCFAs modulate T cell differentiation, favoring IL-10+ Tregs under steady-state and promoting effector Th1/Th17 responses during active ALI/ARDS, while inducing anti-inflammatory IL-10 to prevent excessive inflammation [98–100]. SCFAs also inhibit Th2-mediated airway hyperresponsiveness and mucus hypersecretion, alleviating obstructive symptoms [101, 102]. This anti-allergic mechanism parallels the action of certain compounds that inhibit mast cell inflammation via phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt, mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK), and NF-κB pathways [103]. Systemic protection is further evidenced by GPR43 activation downregulating TNF and IL-6, preserving airway tight junctions, and mitigating viral pneumonia-associated injury [104].

In ALI models, SCFAs reduce disease incidence by suppressing excessive neutrophil activation and neutrophil extracellular trap formation (NETosis) via HDAC and NADPH oxidase inhibition [95, 105]. Butyrate attenuates sepsis-associated ALI by suppressing HMGB1/Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling and reducing nuclear NF-κB activity, IL-6, TNF, and neutrophil infiltration [81]. Crucially, dysbiosis-induced SCFA deficiency represents a double insult: depletion of SCFA-producing commensals (e.g., Lachnospiraceae, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii) coupled with overgrowth of SCFA-degrading pathobionts (e.g., Bacteroides) leads to decreased SCFA (butyrate, propionate) production [106]. This diminishes their anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective effects, while concurrently increasing gut-derived LPS, thereby exacerbating ALI [107, 108].

Beyond SCFAs, succinate, typically a transient metabolic intermediate converted to propionate by gut microbiota, can pathologically accumulate in the intestinal lumen following disturbances like I/R injury, ingestion of indigestible carbohydrates, or antibiotic use, which impair microbial cross-feeding [109–111]. This accumulated succinate translocates across a compromised barrier and accumulates in the lungs, where it acts as a powerful inflammatory signal [112]. In pulmonary tissue, succinate binds to its receptor SUCNR1 (GPR91) on alveolar macrophages, activating the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1α signaling cascade. This activation drives a pro-inflammatory metabolic reprogramming, promoting an M1 macrophage phenotype, stimulating the release of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF, and inducing alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis, thereby exacerbating ALI [113–115]. Genetic ablation or antibody blockade of SUCNR1 significantly attenuates lung injury, confirming its pivotal pathogenic role [112, 115].

Another class of immunomodulatory metabolites includes bile acids. Beyond their role in digestion, bile acids (BAs) modulate gut microbiota composition, suppress pathogens, and enhance energy metabolism [116]. They also fortify the intestinal barrier via activation of the Farnesoid X receptor (FXR), which stimulates IEC proliferation and upregulates tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin) [115]. Insufficient FXR signaling may increase intestinal permeability, allowing antigen translocation and triggering pulmonary immune-inflammatory responses [117].

Other microbiota-derived metabolites also contribute to pulmonary protection. Niacin, L-arginine-modified liposomes, and tryptophan derivatives exert anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, modulating macrophage polarization, and activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [69, 118, 119]. Gut microbiota-derived azelaic acid (AZA) translocates to the lung during sepsis, where it alleviates ALI by promoting MER proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase (MerTK)⁺ alveolar macrophage expansion and neutrophil apoptosis via peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) activation, thereby enhancing efferocytosis [120]. This highlights the diverse pharmacopeia of gut-derived molecules that can influence lung health.

LPS, a major component of Gram-negative bacterial outer membranes, is a primary driver of innate immune activation and a prevalent cause of ALI/ARDS with high mortality. The LPS molecule comprises lipid A (the endotoxic core), a core oligosaccharide, and an O-antigen. Lipid A acts as a PAMP recognized by host immune receptors [121].

Upon intestinal barrier compromise, LPS translocates systemically and engages TLR4 on alveolar macrophages and epithelial cells, activating two major downstream pathways:

The myeloid differentiation primary response gene 88 (MyD88)-dependent pathway leads to rapid TAK1 activation, triggering the IKK complex and MAPK cascade (p38, JNK, ERK). This results in IκBα degradation, NF-κB nuclear translocation, and transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF) [122, 123].The TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-beta (TRIF)-dependent pathway, initiated by CD14-mediated TLR4 endocytosis, involves the TRAM-TRIF complex. This activates TBK1-IRF3 to induce type I interferons (e.g., IFN-β) and indirectly activates NF-κB and MAPK via RIPK1, sustaining inflammation. Under severe conditions, TRIF can form a “pro-death TRIFosome” with RIPK1, FADD, and caspase-8, triggering caspase-8-mediated pyroptosis—a key event in sepsis-related lethality [124].

Additionally, LPS can activate the mTOR pathway to suppress autophagy, augmenting NF-κB signaling, and upregulate the janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of the transcription (JAK/STAT)/NF-κB axis, creating a pro-inflammatory positive feedback loop during LPS-induced ALI [125–127]. The coordinated activation of these pathways culminates in pulmonary neutrophil infiltration, alveolar epithelial apoptosis, and edema, characteristic of LPS-induced ALI (Fig. 2).

Notably, gut-derived metabolites not only activate TLR4 signaling but also directly modulate the susceptibility of lung cells to RCD. SCFAs, through HDAC inhibition, exert context-dependent effects on the NLRP3 inflammasome: under inflammatory conditions, SCFAs activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in human macrophages upon TLR stimulation, triggering hyperactivation and IL-1β release via epigenetic modulation [128]. Conversely, under sterile inflammatory conditions such as lung ischemia-reperfusion injury, SCFAs can diminish IL-1β release via free fatty acid receptor (FFAR)2/FFAR3, indicating a protective effect [107]. On the other hand, succinate accumulation in the lung primes alveolar macrophages for SUCNR1-dependent inflammatory responses. Elevated pulmonary SUCNR1 expression and serum succinate levels are observed in obese patients and mice, and exogenous succinate supplementation exacerbates LPS-induced acute lung injury. SUCNR1 is mainly expressed on lung macrophages, and its knockdown significantly inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokine expression [129].

Migration of intestinal immune cells induces ALI

The gut-lung axis also operates via a cellular highway. Intestinal immune cells, primed by the local dysbiotic and inflammatory milieu, migrate to the lungs and directly shape the pathological response.

γδ T cells function as IL-17 A storm troopers. Primarily residing in the small intestinal lamina propria, γδ T cells play a dual role [130]. Initially protective after epithelial injury, they can differentiate into a pro-inflammatory IL-17 A-producing subset (γδT17) upon severe barrier compromise [9]. In early sepsis, this differentiation is driven towards an IL-7Rhigh CD8low γδT17 phenotype [131, 132], which becomes the predominant population migrating to the lungs [9, 133]. Their migration is orchestrated by the C-C motif chemokine ligand (CCL)-1/CC chemokine receptor (CCR)-8 axis: lung macrophages produce CCL1, which binds to CCR8 upregulated on gut γδT17 cells, guiding them to the pulmonary battlefield [134–136]. Once there, they unleash large quantities of IL-17 A, a master regulator of neutrophilic inflammation. IL-17 A promotes the production of inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF, IL-1β, G-CSF, MCP-1) that drive neutrophil chemotaxis and NET formation [137, 138], becoming a principal driver of excessive pulmonary inflammation and lung injury post-sepsis [9, 139, 140].

ILCs serve as context-dependent sentinels. ILCs are key mucosal lymphocytes. ILC1s mediate early viral defense via IFN-γ and accumulate in inflamed intestinal mucosa [141, 142]. In neonatal mice, gut microbiota promotes ILC3 migration to the lung via CCR4/CCL17, providing early pneumonia defense through IL-22 [75]. ILC2s exhibit complex, context-dependent behavior. Intestinal ILC2s originate from postnatal lung migration and mature via the lung-gut axis under IL-33 [143]. Under inflammation, intestinal inflammatory ILC2s (iILC2s) can egress via sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P)-dependent mechanisms and migrate to the lung during helminth infection or in response to commensal protozoa (e.g., T. mu) via the microbiota-succinate-ILC2 and S1P-sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 4 (S1PR4) axes, potentially exacerbating asthma [144, 145]. Gut dysbiosis (e.g., increased Proteobacteria) can upregulate IL-33, activating the IL-33–CXCL16 axis to drive natural ILC2 (nILC2) migration from gut to lung for host defense [146]. In sepsis, pulmonary ILC2 expansion via IL-33/ST2 can be protective by producing IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13, with IL-9 inhibiting macrophage apoptosis [147, 148]. Conversely, in hemorrhagic shock, receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE)-mediated ILC2 expansion leads to harmful type 2 cytokine release and eosinophil infiltration [149].

The Th17/Treg balance is a critical therapeutic target for ALI/ARDS intervention [49]. Elevated Th17 cells coupled with Treg depletion instigate antibody-mediated ALI [150]. Gut dysbiosis disrupts this equilibrium [95]. Interventions like specific botanical agents (e.g., rhubarb) and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can reinstate the balance by augmenting Firmicutes abundance and facilitating gut metabolite generation [150, 151]. In LPS-induced ALI, the leukotriene B4 (LTB4)-leukotriene B4 receptor 1 (BLT1) axis specifically recruits Tregs to the alveoli, a process essential for timely inflammation resolution [152, 153]. Conversely, gut-derived Th17 cells can migrate via the CCL20/CCR6 axis, as shown with Houttuynia cordata polysaccharides, and upon reaching the lung, secrete IL-17 A causing parenchymal damage [154, 155].

Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy

MSCs, with self-renewal and multipotent capacity, show significant therapeutic potential [184]. MSCs have garnered substantial interest for ALI/ARDS based on compelling preclinical evidence. Mechanistically, MSCs alleviate LPS or Escherichia coli endotoxin-induced ALI through several actions. First, they restore endothelial barrier function via MSC-secreted hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), which activates the mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2)/Akt pathway. Second, MSC-derived microvesicles deliver keratinocyte growth factor mRNA. This reduces alveolar inflammation, lowers protein permeability, attenuates pulmonary edema, and promotes anti-inflammatory IL-10 production in macrophages [156, 157]. A key immunoregulatory mechanism is restoring the Th17/Treg balance. MSCs induce histone methylation in the forkhead box protein 3 (FOXP3) promoter and inhibit retinoid acid-related orphan receptor C (RORc) gene methylation in Th17 cells, promoting a Treg phenotype [158]. MSCs also inhibit naive CD4+ T cell differentiation into Th17 cells via contact-dependent programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) pathway [159]. In smoke inhalation models, MSCs augment transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, suppress IL-17 A, and reduce the Th17/Treg ratio [160].

However, clinical translation has encountered substantial challenges. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 48 studies with 1,773 patients reported that MSCs and their derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) significantly reduced all-cause mortality within one month (RR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.63–0.87) and was well tolerated in ARDS patients; however, the authors note that the limited sample size of the included phase 1/2 trials precludes definitive conclusions [161]. A recent Phase 2b multicenter randomised controlled trial (RCT) (STAT trial; N = 120) tested a single intravenous dose of bone marrow-derived MSCs (10 × 10⁶/kg) in ventilated ARDS patients, 84% of whom had COVID-19 ARDS. The trial found no difference in the primary endpoint of oxygenation index improvement over 36 h, nor any significant mortality difference at 14, 28, 60, or 180 days. Post-hoc analyses identified patient subgroups with differential treatment responses based on baseline plasma biomarkers, suggesting that patient selection may be key to future trial design [162]. A major challenge for MSC therapy in ARDS is biological heterogeneity across donors, tissue sources, and manufacturing batches, which may contribute to the mixed clinical outcomes observed to date. A 2024 head-to-head preclinical comparison demonstrated that umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) exhibited superior efficacy in improving survival and reducing lung inflammation compared to bone marrow- or adipose tissue-derived MSCs, underscoring that not all MSC sources are equivalent [163]. Moreover, manufacturing reproducibility, including donor variability and batch-to-batch consistency of critical quality attributes, remains a significant barrier to clinical translation, though novel microcarrier-microbioreactor platforms and good manufacturing practices (GMP)-compliant protocols are emerging to address these challenges [164, 165].

Beyond whole-cell therapies, MSC-derived EVs have emerged as a promising cell-free alternative. A preclinical systematic review of 51 in vivo studies reported consistent attenuation of inflammation, improved gas exchange, and enhanced survival across diverse ARDS models [185]. A network meta-analysis suggested that exosome-based therapy may match the benefits of parent MSCs without statistically significant differences, while offering practical and logistical advantages (e.g., no cell viability concerns, easier storage and handling); however, this finding requires confirmation in prospective trials [186]. Beyond direct lung effects, MSCs also modulate gut microbiota in sepsis (e.g., increasing Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio), enhancing survival and showing multi-organ protection [187]. MSC-based therapies for ARDS remain investigational, and further high-quality trials incorporating biomarker-based patient stratification are needed.

Probiotics and synbiotics

Administering probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics can modulate pulmonary immunity [188]. Probiotics enhance IgA secretion, promote IEC proliferation and mucus secretion, and stimulate butyrate production [189]. However, clinical evidence in critically ill populations has been mixed.

In a RCT of 72 mechanically ventilated sepsis patients, synbiotics (containing Bifidobacterium breve strain Yakult, Lactobacillus casei strain Shirota, and galacto-oligosaccharides) significantly reduced 28-day incidences of enteritis (6.3% vs. 27.0%, p < 0.05) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (14.3% vs. 48.6%, p < 0.05) compared with no synbiotics. Synbiotics also increased fecal Bifidobacterium/ Lactobacillus and acetate levels, but did not significantly affect 28-day mortality (8.6% vs. 10.8%, p = 0.84) [166]. A meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials involving 7,886 critically ill patients demonstrated that probiotic or synbiotic supplementation significantly reduced the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) (RR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.67–0.96; p = 0.021) and sepsis, shortened ICU and hospital length of stay, decreased ICU mortality, and reduced duration of mechanical ventilation. No statistically significant effects were observed on diarrhea, Clostridioides difficile infection, hospital-acquired pneumonia, or in-hospital mortality [167]. More strikingly, a 2026 umbrella review encompassing 24 meta-analyses of 92,711 mechanically ventilated patients reported that while probiotics were associated with a reduced risk of VAP (OR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.61–0.75), the methodological quality of included meta-analyses varied substantially, 4 were rated critically low, 12 low, 1 moderate, and 9 high. Moreover, the reduction in ICU mortality (OR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.79–0.94) did not consistently translate into a hospital mortality benefit, as the pooled OR was not statistically significant (OR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.84–1.01), although the RR analysis suggested a modest reduction [168]. Thus, probiotics/synbiotics may reduce infectious complications and ICU resource use without lowering mortality, but evidence quality is low-to-moderate. No large RCTs have specifically enrolled patients with ARDS, limiting generalizability. Future trials should focus on ARDS, strain selection guided by baseline gut microbiome, and standardized safety monitoring.

FMT

FMT involves transferring functional microbiota from healthy donors to restore dysbiosis [169]. While established as highly effective for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, FMT achieved the highest P-score of 0.9836 in this network meta-analysis, demonstrating superior efficacy over all other therapies [170]. However, the application of FMT in ARDS remains at an early, exploratory stage.

Mechanistic insights derive predominantly from animal models. In ALI rodent models, FMT may exert benefit by: (1) restructuring gut microbiota and reducing LPS translocation [171]; (2) Replenishing beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) and metabolites (SCFAs), enhancing gut homeostasis, upregulating TJ proteins (occludin, ZO-1), and reducing permeability [172]; (3) restoring the Th17/Treg balance via pathways like JAK/STAT to preserve immune homeostasis [151]. In an LPS-induced ALI rat model, FMT ameliorates lung injury and improves pulmonary function by modulating gut microbiota composition (e.g., increasing Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, restoring Muribaculaceae, Clostridia_UCG-014, Prevotella, and Adlercreutzia, while reducing Romboutsia) and restoring key metabolic and immune pathways, including lipid/amino acid metabolism and mTOR signaling [173]. However, well-designed RCTs in patients with ARDS are currently lacking. Two registered clinical trials are currently evaluating FMT specifically in patients with ARDS (NCT06970262, NCT07342205), albeit with small sample sizes.

A critical barrier to clinical translation is the safety profile of FMT in critically ill populations. FMT has been proven safe and effective in clinical use, with no infections or deaths related to FMT observed in long-term follow-up (5 years or more) [174]. Even for non-gastrointestinal diseases [175, 176] and immunodeficient patients [177], no serious adverse reactions related to FMT have been reported, supporting the feasibility of FMT in selected critically ill patients. Thus, while FMT represents a conceptually attractive approach to resetting gut ecosystem dysbiosis, its current evidence base in ARDS is preclinical/early translational rather than clinical. The field awaits the results of ongoing trials before any recommendation for clinical use can be made (Fig. 2).

Metabolite-targeted therapies

Beyond FMT, direct targeting of gut-derived metabolites represents a mechanistically rational but largely untested therapeutic avenue. Gut microbiota-derived succinate exacerbates ALI through SUCNR1-dependent alveolar macrophage polarization. These findings identify succinate as a novel target for gut-derived ALI in critically ill patients [112, 115]. In a cohort of 67 critically injured patients (21 of whom developed ARDS), elevated plasma succinate acts as the first event in ARDS by priming neutrophils via SUCNR1, leading to pulmonary sequestration and subsequent lung injury [178]. SUCNR1 inhibition has demonstrated efficacy in alleviating LPS-induced lung injury and decreased the HIF-1α expression and IκB-α phosphorylation [129]. Among gut-derived metabolites, AZA has progressed furthest toward translational relevance, gut-derived AZA translocates to the lung during sepsis and alleviates ALI by promoting MerTK⁺ alveolar macrophage expansion and enhancing efferocytosis via PPAR-γ activation [120]. This finding provides proof-of-concept that endogenous gut metabolites can be therapeutically harnessed. However, no metabolite-targeted agent has entered clinical testing for ARDS. Thus, while SCFA supplementation, SUCNR1 antagonists, and AZA hold theoretical advantages over whole-cell interventions, including defined pharmacokinetics, absence of infectious risk, and precise dose titration, their current evidence base is exclusively preclinical, and first-in-human trials are needed to translate these mechanistic insights into clinical practice.

Endothelial barrier as a therapeutic target

Beyond metabolite-based interventions, directly targeting endothelial barrier integrity represents a promising but underexplored strategy for gut-derived ARDS. This targeting could be directed at either the GVB or the pulmonary endothelium. In a murine model of gut-derived sepsis, terlipressin preserves GVB integrity through PI3K/β-catenin signaling, thereby reducing bacterial translocation and attenuating ALI [179]. FXR agonists have also been shown to strengthen both the muco-epithelial barrier and the GVB, reducing portal-venous bacterial translocation [180]. At the pulmonary side, Angiopoietin-1/Tie2 activation and glycocalyx-protective agents (e.g., human serum albumin, hydrocortisone) could stabilize lung microvascular endothelium in septic mice [181–183]. A dual-targeting approach, combining GVB-stabilizing agents with pulmonary endothelial protectants, may offer synergistic benefit in gut-derived ALI/ARDS, though this remains a largely theoretical construct requiring preclinical validation. Nevertheless, despite these promising preclinical findings, clinical evidence supporting endothelial barrier-targeted therapies for gut-derived ARDS remains critically lacking, and no such strategy has yet been validated in patients with ARDS.

Conclusions

The gut-lung axis exemplifies the systemic nature of critical illness, yet the field remains at a transitional stage, moving beyond association without having fully established causation in humans. This review delineates a plausible pathogenic cascade: intestinal barrier failure enables gut-derived inflammatory signals to propagate lung injury via humoral pathways (microbial metabolites, PAMPs) and cellular pathways (immune cell trafficking). However, much of this evidence derives from preclinical models, which are context-dependent and heterogeneous across injury types. Key unresolved issues include the following. First, how to define clinically meaningful gut-derived lung injury in patients. Second, how different RCD pathways (pyroptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis) interact to propagate injury across the gut‑lung axis. Third, how to disentangle causative gut-lung signaling from parallel dysbiosis driven by critical illness itself. Fourth, how ICU-specific factors (antibiotics, nutrition, ventilation, ecology) confound interpretation. Among the proposed mechanisms, intestinal barrier dysfunction and mesenteric lymphatic transmission represent relatively mature nodes supported by interventional animal data, whereas the causal role of specific microbial metabolites (beyond LPS) and immune cell subsets in human ARDS remains largely speculative.

Emerging gut-targeted therapies (e.g., FMT, MSCs, probiotics, and metabolite-based approaches) hold conceptual promise by aiming to restore ecological and immunological balance at the source. Their translation, however, faces substantial hurdles: weak evidence in human ARDS, lack of standardization, safety concerns in unstable ICU patients, and the need for biomarker-guided patient selection. Rather than overstating mechanistic certainty, we emphasize that future research must prioritize establishing causality through gnotobiotic models and longitudinal human cohorts, identifying translatable biomarkers of gut-driven lung injury, and designing precision interventions that account for the heterogeneity of critical illness. A sober appreciation of current limitations is essential to advance the gut-lung axis from a compelling hypothesis to a clinically actionable paradigm.
