HBsAg loss in recipients with chronic hepatitis B after allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation: a retrospective cohort study exploring kinetic dynamics
Authors: Qiannan Wang, Pan Huang, Qiao Zhang, Yushan Liu, Qijuan Zang, Chengbin Zhu, Yamin Wang, Yingli He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection poses a significant challenge in immunocompromised populations, particularly among hematologic malignancy patients undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). However, data regarding the mechanisms underlying this alteration have only been partially reported. This study aimed to explore the kinetics of HBsAg and the potential for achieving functional cure (HBsAg loss) after allo-HSCT by analyzing longitudinal quantitative data from a complete cohort in patients with chronic HBV and hematologic malignancies.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University from January 2013 to December 2023. Among the 622 consecutive allo-HSCT recipients, 15 patients with chronic HBV infection were included. Virological marker (HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and anti-HBc) and HBV DNA levels were monitored longitudinally before and after transplantation according to a standard protocol (baseline, weekly for the first month, monthly for 6 months, then every 3 months).The primary endpoint was HBsAg seroconversion, with exploratory outcome measures including HBV reactivation, cirrhosis decompensation, and mortality.ResultsAmong the 15 patients, two (13.3%) achieved HBsAg seroconversion at 6 months and 12 months post transplantation. Both patients were HBeAg-negative, had undetectable HBV DNA before transplantation, and maintained HBsAg clearance during extended follow-up, with one patient discontinuing antiviral therapy. The remaining 13 patients (86.7%) remained HBsAg positive. Comparative analysis of the entire cohort’s longitudinal quantitative HBsAg data revealed distinct kinetic patterns between the two subgroups, providing preliminary insights into differential clearance trajectories. Non-liver-related causes mortality was observed, although four patients (26.7%) died from nonliver-related causes, primarily severe pneumonia or underlying hematologic disease. Background

Chronic HBV infection represents a significant global public health challenge, affecting approximately 290 million individuals worldwide, particularly immunocompromised individuals [1, 2]. Despite the availability of effective antiviral therapies, such as nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs), achieving a functional cure—defined as sustained HBsAg loss with or without seroconversion to anti-HBs—remains rare [3, 4]. This highlights the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies. Patients with hematologic malignancies who receive allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) constitute a unique group. These patients are faced with two challenges: the risk of viral reactivation due to immunosuppression and the potential for immune-mediated viral clearance following transplantation. Most research on HBV and allo-HSCT has focused predominantly on the risk of HBV reactivation and prophylactic antiviral management [5, 6]. However, inspired by the cases where allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) using CCR5Δ32 donor cells successfully cured HIV, we hypothesized whether allo-HSCT could promote functional cure of chronic HBV [7, 8]. There are few studies addressing clinical cures of chronic hepatitis B post transplantation; notably, differing conclusions have been reported regarding success rates: 49.6% (62/125) [9] versus 11.1% (5/45) [10]. In this study, we aimed to move beyond reporting incidence rates by leveraging the unique opportunity to analyze complete longitudinal quantitative HBsAg data from all patients in our cohort. This allowed us to perform a comparative analysis of HBsAg kinetics between patients who achieved HBsAg loss and those who did not in chronic HBV patients undergoing allo-HSCT. We assessed the contributions of donor serostatus, antiviral therapy efficacy, and immune reconstitution to achieving HBsAg loss. Instead of just preventing HBV reactivation, we aim to generate hypotheses regarding potential predictors and mechanisms. This could provide long-term viral control for high-risk patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University from January 2013 to December 2023. Among the 622 consecutive patients who underwent allo-HSCT, 15 patients with chronic HBV infection and hematologic malignancies were included. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) chronic HBV infection defined by positive HBsAg for at least 6 months; (2) a diagnosis of hematologic malignancy; and (3) receiving allo-HSCT with available virological and immunological data from pre-transplantation and post-transplantation follow-ups. Patients who were lost to follow-up or whose clinical data were incomplete were excluded.

Data collection and viral surveillance

We retrospectively reviewed medical records and collected demographic, clinical, virological, and transplant-related data. We screened HBV-related markers (HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and anti-HBc) and HBV DNA in patients and donors before HSCT and evaluated them regularly post-HSCT according to a standard protocol: at baseline (1 week pre-HSCT), weekly during the first month, monthly for months 2–6, and every 3 months thereafter until the end of follow-up. Virological markers of HBV infection (HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and anti-HBc) were assessed via ARCHITECT i2000. HBV DNA was quantified using the Cobas TaqMan 96 real-time PCR (Roche), with a detection limit < 20 IU/mL. Donor screening included HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc, and HBV DNA testing to exclude occult HBV infection.

Transplantation protocol

All patients received allo-HSCT. To prevent graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), cyclophosphamide (CY)-based preparative regimens (cyclosporine A, mycophenolate mofetil, and short-term methotrexate) were used. Patients who were HBsAg-positive and/or had detectable HBV DNA were prophylactically treated with NAs before HSCT. Patients who had previously received antiviral therapy continued their original regimen, whereas patients who had not received treatment or had an unknown treatment history preferred entecavir (0.5 mg/day).

Definitions

Chronic HBV infection was defined as HBsAg positivity for ≥ 6 months. HBsAg loss was defined as two consecutive negative tests lasting at least six months post-transplantation. HBsAg negativity was defined as HBsAg < 0.05 IU/mL, and seroconversion was defined as HBsAg < 0.05 IU/mL and anti-HBs ≥ 10 mIU/L. The occurrence of HBV reactivation in HBsAg-positive, anti-HBc-positive patients was assessed according to the recommendation [11] of the APASL in 2021: (1) ≥ 2 log increase in HBV DNA levels from baseline levels, or (2) detection of HBV DNA with a level > 100 IU/ml in a person with undetectable HBV DNA at baseline.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the cumulative incidence of HBsAg seroconversion. The exploratory outcome measures included the incidence of HBV reactivation, cirrhosis decompensation, and mortality.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics summarizing demographic and baseline characteristics. Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are expressed as percentages. Paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to assess changes in virological and immunological markers.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between January 1, 2013, and December 1, 2023, a cohort of 17 patients were diagnosed with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and received allo-HSCT. Two patients were excluded from the analysis because of incomplete data, which included loss to follow-up and missing hospitalization records. Consequently, a total of 15 patients who completed at least 24 weeks of follow-up after allo-HSCT were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Tables 1 and 2 present the detailed characteristics of all patients prior to the initiation of transplantation treatment. All participants were recruited from Northwest China, comprising 12 males and 3 females, with a median age of 45 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 31–58 years). The hematological diagnoses included acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in 11 patients, acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) in two patients, myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) in one patient, and severe aplastic anemia (SAA) in one patient.

Antiviral therapy

All patients were HBsAg positive with chronic HBV infection at the time of allo-HSCT, and only one patient was HBeAg positive. At the initial diagnosis of hematologic disease, HBV DNA was detectable in eight patients. After antiviral treatment, the last test before transplantation revealed that, except for three patients who had detectable HBV DNA levels at diagnosis (60 IU/ml, 213 IU/ml, and 2240 IU/ml for P3, P4, and P9, respectively), the remaining patients had undetectable HBV viral loads.

All participants initiated antiviral therapy upon diagnosis of hematologic disease, with the exception of P3, who had received prior chemotherapy at other institutions. All patients received prophylactic antiviral treatment before transplantation, primarily with entecavir. The median duration of antiviral treatment before transplantation was 29.9 weeks (range: 8–76 weeks). Owing to an inadequate response to antiviral agents and persistently elevated HBV DNA levels, Patient 4 was switched to a combination regimen of tenofovir alafenamide fumarate (TAF), telbivudine (LdT), and entecavir (ETV) before undergoing HSCT. Antiviral therapy was maintained in all patients following transplantation. Patients 1 and 9 were prescribed TAF and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), respectively, in combination with entecavir due to persistent hypoviremia. Additionally, three patients (P2, P6, and P8; Fig. 2) required an increased dose of entecavir in response to HBV DNA replication. Only one recipient discontinued antiviral therapy after transplantation due to HBsAg loss. The remaining 14 HBsAg-positive patients continued antiviral treatment until their death or the endpoint of our study.

HBsAg clearance and functional cure of CHB

As of December 2024, we found that the majority of all patients had a decrease in HBsAg, while the decrease in HBsAg was not very obvious in AML, SAA or MDS patients (Fig. 2A, B). To determine whether there are differences among them, a larger sample size is still needed for statistical analysis. Comparative Analysis of HBsAg Kinetics: We analyzed the longitudinal quantitative HBsAg data of all 15 patients to compare patterns between the two patients who achieved HBsAg loss (P7, P15) and the 13 who did not. Both clearing patients showed a consistent downward trend in HBsAg post-HSCT until becoming undetectable (Fig. 3G, O). In contrast, among the non-clearing patients, HBsAg levels exhibited more heterogeneous trajectories, including stable low levels, gradual decline without clearance, or transient fluctuations (Fig. 3A–F, H-N). Two out of fifteen patients experienced HBsAg loss after treatment (6 and 12 months for P7 and P15, respectively). These two cured patients, aged 48 and 34 years, had acute myeloid leukemia, were asymptomatic and HBeAg-negative, had undetectable HBV DNA, and had HBsAg levels of 129.36 and 2418.05 IU/mL before transplantation (Table 3).

One of the patients was a 34-year-old male (P15). This patient was diagnosed with CHB in March 2014 and presented with HBsAg positivity and a significantly elevated HBV DNA plasma viral load of 16,500 IU/mL. These findings, in accordance with the national guidelines at that time, indicated the need for antiviral therapy. The patient received 0.5 mg of entecavir (ETV) daily, which resulted in sustained suppression of the plasma viral load (Fig. 3 O). In April 2014, he was diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) M2a. Following chemotherapy, including idarubicin, cytarabine induction therapy, and high-dose cytarabine consolidation, the patient achieved complete hematological remission. In September 2015, the patient received a transplant of 21.92 × 10^6 unmodified CD34+ peripheral blood stem cells per kg of body weight from a 10/10 HLA-matched sibling donor. Full donor chimerism was established and maintained for 17 days post-hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) (Table 1). On day 40 post-transplantation, transient mild elevation of liver enzymes (ALT 45.3 IU/mL) was observed, potentially indicative of drug-induced liver injury. Antiviral therapy with 0.5 mg of ETV daily was continued, and the HBV DNA viral load and HBsAg levels were monitored regularly. HBV DNA remained undetectable throughout the 24-week post-transplantation period. Notably, at the 30-week follow-up, the patient achieved HBsAg seroconversion accompanied by seroconversion (anti-HBs titer exceeding 1000 IU/mL). During subsequent follow-up for more than 200 weeks, HBsAg and HBV DNA remained undetectable. Additionally, there were no liver-related complications during follow-up.

Another patient (P7) who experienced HBsAg loss was a 48-year-old male diagnosed with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) concurrent with acute myeloid leukemia. The initial HBsAg level was 10.003 IU/mL, and the HBV DNA level was 6980 IU/mL (Table 3). Similar to the previous case, this patient received ETV treatment at 0.5 mg daily, achieving both complete remission of the bone marrow and undetectable HBV DNA levels before receiving an HLA 5/10 matched stem cell transplant from his son. Allo-HSCT was performed in April 2019, during which 6.53 × 10^6 CD34+ peripheral blood stem cells per kg of body weight were transplanted (Table 2). ETV administration continued post-transplantation, and HBsAg disappearance was confirmed by 48 weeks. Throughout the transplantation and follow-up periods, liver function remained stable without significant fluctuations (Table 2, Fig. 3G). Notably, the patient discontinued ETV in December 2021 but maintained HBsAg clearance during subsequent follow-up. This condition meets the criteria for a clinical cure for chronic hepatitis B.

Efficacy and survival

All patients were prospectively followed until December 2023. The overall survival (OS) rate for the entire cohort was 72.52%. Exploratory analysis of overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) comparing patients with or without chronic HBV infection is provided in Supplementary Fig 1. To further evaluate the impact of HBV infection on outcomes, we extended follow-up for the HBV-positive cohort (n = 15) until December 2024. Four patients (26.7%) died post-HSCT due to treatment-related complications (severe pneumonia, n = 3; underlying comorbidities, n = 1). No HBV-related liver mortality was recorded. The 6-month and 12-month post-HSCT survival rates were 93.3% (14/15) and 86.7% (13/15), respectively. The mean follow-up duration was 165 weeks (range: 27–353 weeks), with a median follow-up of 189 weeks among survivors (Table 1).

Discussion

Chronic HBV infection remains a major public health issue, affecting approximately 296 million people worldwide and contributing substantially to morbidity and mortality through liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [1, 12]. Although continuous antiviral therapy can effectively suppress viral replication and prevent liver-related complications, the inability to completely eradicate HBV from infected individuals continues to challenge current management strategies. The primary treatment goal for patients with chronic hepatitis B is to achieve a functional cure, which is defined as the loss of HBsAg, regardless of whether sustained anti-hepatitis B antibodies are produced and without the need for ongoing antiviral therapy [4, 11, 13].

In addition to available antiviral treatments such as NAs and interferon, the pursuit of a functional cure has spurred the development of novel therapeutic strategies, particularly for patients with comorbidities [13]. This study focused on chronic hepatitis B patients with malignant hematological diseases, who often require complex interventions such as allo-HSCT.

In patients with hematological malignancies, allo-HSCT not only offers a potential cure for the underlying malignancy but also may influence HBV outcomes. However, most previous studies on HBV in the context of allo-HSCT have concentrated on preventing or managing HBV reactivation [5, 14]. The mechanisms underlying HIV cure through CCR5Δ32 allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) differ from those required for achieving a functional cure of chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection [7, 8]. However, the profound immune reconstitution that follows allo-HSCT may hold relevance for chronic HBV. In individuals coinfected with HIV and HBV, the initiation of HBV-active antiretroviral therapy (ART) has been associated with high rates of early hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss. This outcome correlates with markers of immune activation, such as elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels and younger age—factors indicative of a robust host immune response [15]. Inspired by the observation that allo-HSCT and ART can ameliorate chronic viral infections through distinct mechanisms—such as CCR5Δ32 disruption or immune reconstitution—we propose exploring whether a functional cure for chronic HBV could be achieved via allo-HSCT. Although curing HBV necessitates the clearance of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA)—a distinct virological challenge—the post-transplant immune environment could favor functional cure through sustained immunological pressure. However, prior studies have often lacked detailed information on antiviral therapy and treatment for the primary disease. Because studies have shown that some tumor chemotherapeutic drugs and immunosuppressants can cause hepatitis B virus reactivation, it is necessary to understand patients’ past antitumor treatment regimens [16]. Our study offers a unique longitudinal perspective by tracking patients from the initial diagnosis of hematologic malignancies through chemotherapy, transplantation, and subsequent follow-up. In our study, all patients were HBsAg positive at baseline, and a subset exhibited detectable HBV DNA. They received antiviral therapy prior to chemotherapy and transplantation, with most patients achieving undetectable HBV DNA levels before the procedure. After transplantation, the continuation of antiviral therapy was maintained.

Notably, two patients (P7 and P15) achieved HBsAg loss after 6 and 12 months, respectively, with one patient maintaining HBsAg negativity even after discontinuing entecavir, suggesting a potential functional cure. Both patients had favorable baseline characteristics, including HBeAg negativity, undetectable HBV DNA levels before transplantation, and low HBsAg titers, which are known predictors of functional cure [9, 10, 17]. Our observed HBsAg clearance rate (13.33%) differs from that reported in some studies but is comparable to that reported in other studies, a discrepancy that may be attributable to the small sample size and consequent bias [9]. Beyond reporting the clearance rate, the core value of our study lies in the comparative analysis of longitudinal quantitative HBsAg data across the entire cohort. This analysis revealed distinct kinetic patterns: patients achieving clearance showed a consistent downward trend post-HSCT, whereas non-clearing patients exhibited more stable or variable trajectories (Fig. 3). This detailed kinetic comparison, rarely provided in larger cohort studies focused on incidence, offers preliminary clues to the dynamics of immune-mediated clearance post-HSCT. In addition to qHBsAg dynamics, significant changes in the HBV load were observed, with HBV DNA becoming undetectable (<10 IU/mL) in 13 patients at the final follow-up. These findings imply that HSCT, in the context of immune reconstitution, might induce durable viral control.

Furthermore, our patients were treated with antiviral drugs such as ETV and TAF before and after transplantation, which play key roles in inhibiting HBV DNA before and after transplantation to prevent viral replication and HBV-associated liver injury. We also depicted a detailed dot plot illustrating the dynamic changes in HBV DNA and HBsAg levels, thereby providing an intuitive visualization of hepatitis B outcomes in this patient population. Previous reports lacked comprehensive patient history data, making our longitudinal approach particularly valuable.

Interestingly, donor serological status also influences HBV outcomes. Grafts from donors who were HBsAg-negative but anti-HBs-positive appeared to confer passive immunity to recipients, aiding in the control of HBV posttransplant. This observation aligns with earlier studies suggesting that donor-derived anti-HBs antibodies may help prevent viral reactivation [9] (predicting the loss of hepatitis B surface antigen following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in patients with chronic HBV infection). Moreover, the genetic backgrounds of both recipients and donors likely contribute to the success of HSCT and the ultimate HBV outcome. Studies in HIV patients have demonstrated that T-cell depletion correlates with the degree of T-cell chimerism, suggesting that the successful reconstruction of HBV-specific T cells after transplantation could lead to the eradication of the viral reservoir [7, 18–20]. Unfortunately, this study lacked hematological samples from patients to explore immunological mechanisms further.

Despite these promising findings, HBV reactivation remains a significant concern following HSCT. In our cohort, one patient with undetectable HBV DNA at the time of transplantation experienced reactivation during follow-up, and another patient developed hepatocellular carcinoma. Although liver-related mortality is zero, severe liver injury or cancer remains a risk, particularly for patients with preexisting liver disease or those who are immunocompromised posttransplant [21].

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The small sample size is the primary limitation, restricting the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. The retrospective design introduces inherent biases in patient selection and data collection. However, the strength of our study lies in the completeness of longitudinal quantitative HBsAg data, which enabled a unique comparative kinetic analysis not feasible in larger but less granular datasets. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are necessary to validate these observations and to further assess the long-term outcomes of HBsAg loss after HSCT. Additionally, further investigations into the roles of genetic factors, donor-specific antibodies, and T-cell immunity in achieving HBV remission are warranted.

In conclusion, this exploratory study, utilizing complete longitudinal quantitative HBsAg data from all 15 patients, provides a comparative analysis of HBsAg kinetics post-allo-HSCT. We observed HBsAg clearance in 2 patients and identified differential kinetic patterns between clearing and non-clearing patients. Given the complex procedures and high risks associated with allo-HSCT, promoting this technique as a routine therapeutic approach remains challenging. However, our findings have opened new avenues for exploring HBsAg loss strategies in special populations with chronic hepatitis B virus infection.
