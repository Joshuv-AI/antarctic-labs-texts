Sonochemically assisted the synthesis and catalytic application of bismuth-based photocatalyst: A mini review

Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Introduction

The rapid development of urbanization and industrialization has caused human beings to worry about global environmental pollution and energy crisis [1], [2]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop efficient clean technologies for environmental remediation and energy regeneration. The discovery of TiO2 photocatalytic water splitting in 1972 provided a new direction for solving the above problems [3]. Since then, a large number of photocatalysts have been developed and applied in different fields, such as: environmental remediation [4], [5], [6], water splitting [7], [8], [9], CO2 reduction [10], [11], etc. Although photocatalytic technology has been initially developed, high carrier recombination rate, limited light absorption capacity and low reactive oxygen species (ROS) yield restrict the further application of photocatalytic technology. In order to achieve practical applications, the development of efficient new photocatalysts and collaborative technologies has become a hot spot.

In recent years, ultrasound, as an auxiliary technology, has received extensive attention in the fields of photocatalyst synthesis and photocatalysis [12]. Traditional synthesis methods usually have the disadvantages of too long preparation time, uncontrollable morphology, expensive reagents, high temperature and high pressure [13], [14]. However, ultrasound-assisted preparation has become a popular photocatalyst preparation method because of its short synthesis time, no external heating and pressure and controllable morphology [15], [16]. In terms of synergistic photocatalytic applications, ultrasonic waves>16 kHz will have a cavitation effect when periodically vibrating in the liquid medium, and the high temperature and high pressure (∼5000 K, ∼900 atm) generated by the collapse of cavitation bubbles in microseconds can form a local reaction center, thereby promoting the formation of ROS [17], [18]. In addition, ultrasound-induced photocatalyst deformation triggers piezoelectric effects, which can promote carrier separation [19]. Therefore, ultrasonic synergistic photocatalytic treatment of pollutants and energy conversion will become the general trend.

Recently, Bi-based photocatalysts have attracted widespread attention in the field of photocatalysis due to their moderate light absorption band gap, excellent chemical stability and efficient electronic structure [20], [21], [22], [23]. Metal Bi can produce localized Surface Plasmon Resonance (LSPR) similar to precious metals under light. In addition, the distortion of the Bi 6 s orbital of Bi-based semiconductors causes O 2p to overlap and cause hybridization [24], [25], [26]. These characteristics facilitate carrier migration and band regulation, making Bi-based semiconductors excellent candidates in the field of photocatalysis [27], [28]. Fig. 1 and Table 1 show the classification and band structure data of bismuth-based photocatalysts. From the bandgap data, it can be found that most Bi-based light semiconductors can be excited by low-energy visible light. However, due to the narrow band gap of Bi-based semiconductors, the separation efficiency of photogenerated carriers is low, which hinders its further application. Therefore, it is necessary to adjust the band structure and promote carrier separation through auxiliary technology and structural regulation. The deformation of Bi-based non-central symmetric materials under ultrasonic waves causes polarization effects, generating an internal electric field. It provides a strong driving force for the separation and migration of carriers, which greatly improves the photocatalytic efficiency. Therefore, ultrasonic synergy with Bi-based photocatalysts has become a future development trend.

In this paper, the auxiliary strategies of ultrasound in the preparation and modification of Bi-based photocatalysts are reviewed, and the influence of different factors (ultrasonic power, irradiation time and solvent) is discussed. Then, from the perspective of mechanism, the synergistic mechanism of ultrasonic waves in the whole process of photoacoustic catalytic reaction for various types of Bi-based photocatalysts is summarized, and the application of ultrasonic synergistic Bi-based photocatalysts in pollutant treatment and energy conversion is introduced. Finally, the development and application of ultrasonic synergistic Bi-based photocatalysts in the future are summarized and prospected.

Ultrasonic-assisted preparation of bismuth-based photocatalyst

In the past, it was difficult for traditional synthesis methods to effectively prepare bismuth-based photocatalyst with desired morphology or other physicochemical properties [50]. Therefore, there is a need to develop assistive technologies to couple with traditional synthesis methods. Many auxiliary technologies developed in the past often have the disadvantages of excessive energy consumption, long time consumption and easy to cause secondary pollution [51]. Until the ultrasound-assisted synthesis technology that overcomes the above shortcomings stands out. The advantages of ultrasound-assisted synthesis technology stem from acoustic cavitations (the formation, growth, and implosive collapse of the bubbles), which can provide extreme environments with very high temperatures (>5000 K), high pressures (>20 MPa), and cooling rates in excess of 109 K/s for the preparation of bismuth-based photocatalyst [52].

Morphology and structure control

In general, ultrasound mainly controls the morphology of Bi-based photocatalysts through two mechanisms: cavitation and acoustic radiation [53]. The cavitation phenomenon is caused by the pressure difference generated by the ultrasonic irradiation in the solvent and is a process of cavitation bubbles from generation to rupture [54]. The localized high temperature and pressure generated by the collapse of acoustic bubbles contribute to the dissolution of precursors [55]. Meanwhile, the high-speed collision among precursors driven by high intensity ultrasound irradiation promotes the collision of precursors and the crystallization of products, thereby forming nano-sized particles [55]. In addition, rapid changes in temperature and pressure can cause water splitting to produce H• and •OH, which can adjust the surface charge density of Bi-based photocatalysts, reduce the energy barrier formed by the material, and ultimately control the material size [56], [57].

Therefore, ultrasonic irradiation has emerged as a powerful tool for controlling the morphology and structure of bismuth-based photocatalyst. For example, Liu et al. obtained BiVO4 photocatalysts with different crystal forms and morphologies by changing different ultrasonic irradiation times [58]. XRD and SEM of the product showed that by extending the ultrasonic reaction time from 15 min to 120 min, the product gradually changed from a hollow sphere composed of thin sheets of pure tetragonal phase BiVO4 to monoclinic phase of BiVO4 hollow spindle-like particles (Fig. 2a and b). It is worth noting that the phase and morphology transformation process mainly includes three stages of dissolution, transport and recrystallization (Fig. 2c): (1) The hot pressure effect formed by a large number of cavities makes the internal channelization of the tetragonal phase BiVO4, and further internal dissolution occurs. (2) Due to the continuous high temperature and high pressure state inside, there is a nucleation resistance inside it, thereby transporting the melted tetragonal phase BiVO4 to the surface. (3) Recrystallization of monoclinic phase BiVO4 on the surface. Another study reported the effect of changing the ultrasonic power on the morphology and crystallinity of BiVO4 based on the sol–gel method [59]. Ultrasound during preparation can effectively improve the crystallinity and dispersion of BiVO4 and reduce the size. Among them, BiVO4 prepared at 80 W ultrasonic power has the most dispersed and smaller size. However, it should be noted that the extreme high temperature and high pressure generated by excessive ultrasonic power affect the optimal orientation of the material, and eventually reduce the crystallinity. Similarly, Long et al. tuned the morphology and compositions of BixOyCl formation by adjusting the sonication time. Their experiment showed that Bi12O17Cl2 gradually converted to BiOCl as the ultrasound time increases until the ultrasound time reached 40 min, pure BiOCl was prepared. This is mainly because ultrasonic cavitation can break the chemical bonds of the oxygen-rich [Bi12O17] layer, part of the Bi-O bond in [Bi12O17] layer was broken to form a part of [Bi2O2] layer and oxygen vacancy [60].

With the aim of manipulating the morphology of bismuth-based photocatalyst more precisely, some researchers supplement the ultrasound environment with surfactants. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is a commonly used non-ionic surfactant. It is a linear or branched, neutral polyether available in a variety of molecular weights. The ether oxygen on the fundamental chain is prone to adsorb positive ions and can interact with metal ions in the solution to form chain-like coordination complexes [61]. Shang et al. prepared nanosized BiVO4 particles with an average size of about 60 nm under ultrasonic irradiation with the assistance of PEG [52]. The coordination of Bi3+ ions with PEG can reduce the reaction rate between Bi3+ with VO3−, thereby avoiding the rapid formation of yellow precipitate BiVO4. Subsequently, under the ultrasonic irradiation, the complex will gradually decompose to release Bi3+ slowly, followed by the reaction with VO3−and the generation of amorphous BiVO4 sediment. Finally, PEG may adsorb on the surface of the sediment and act as the template and the protective agent so as to favor the formation of smaller granular grains during ultrasonic irradiation.

In order to further exploit the advantages of ultrasound and make the prepared materials more uniform, ultrasonic spray pyrolysis (USP) technology was developed. USP process is a material preparation method without template control morphology structure in recent years, and its main process is divided into three stages (Fig. 2d): (1) ultrasonic atomization of precursor solution; (2) induction of precursor atomization droplet reaction in tubular furnace; (3) solvent evaporation at high temperature to form reaction products [62], [63], [64]. Huang et al. prepared Bi2WO6 porous microspheres by USP method using a 1.7 MHz ultrasonic nebulizer (Fig. 2e) [63]. As shown in the Fig. 2f, compared with Bi2WO6 prepared by traditional methods, Bi2WO6 prepared by USP has higher crystallinity, more uniform size and larger specific surface area. However, the above-mentioned study does not fully explain the mechanism of USP method to control the morphology of materials. Dunkle et al. demonstrated the preparation of hollow BiVO4 using bubbles generated by the evaporation of precursors and solvent aerosols [65]. It is worth noting that the current research on the influence of ultrasonic atomization power and temperature on morphology is still blank.

Element engineering

The physical environment provided by ultrasonic irradiation promotes the dissolution of chemical bonds, collision of particles, exchange of atoms and ions, and the crystallization of amorphous semiconductors [66]. Furthermore, the transient acoustic cavitation bubbles in ultrasonic system, facilitating the uniform dispersion of doping ions [67]. Therefore, acoustic cavitation is expected to increase the efficiency of element engineering. In the synthesis of Cu-coupled BiVO4, the ultrasonic environment is the key strategy for Cu2+ doping and Cu loading [68]. As shown in the Fig. 3a and b, the addition of ultrasonic waves effectively replaces the V5+ ions in the lattice with Cu2+ ions and forms a virtual energy level. Thereafter, under ultrasound, part of Cu2+ is reduced to the surface of the material to form Cu nanoparticles. TEM also confirms this (Fig. 3c). Wang et al. transformed the crystal form of Sr2+ doped BiFeO3 (BFO) from diamond-shaped perovskite to pseudocubic phase structure by using ultrasonic hydrothermal strategy, and also improved the ferromagnetic properties of the material (Fig. 3d) [69]. This is mainly due to the introduction of O vacancies at the same time as the ultrasound transport of Sr2+ ions into the crystal lattice of the BFO, which inhibits the BFO's own spin structure, thus causing the generation of weak magnetism. More notably, the nanometer effect caused by the change of ultrasonic waves and doping amounts is an important factor in changing the crystal structure of materials.

Heterojunction construction

Sufficient contact area is a necessary condition for the construction of heterostructures, and the formation of interfacial chemical bonds can highly improve the effectiveness of heterojunctions [5]. As previously mentioned, when cavitation bubbles explode near the material, the high-speed microjets produced can change the surface morphology, composition, and surface energy of the material [70], [71]. Changes in surface energy can increase the affinity between interfaces, and changes in the surface phase of materials provide a prerequisite for bonding between interfaces. This means that during the preparation of bismuth-based photocatalysts, the spatial conditions required for heterostructure construction can be provided by morphology adjustment under ultrasonic irradiation. Meanwhile, ultrasonic effect can provide energy for the formation of interfacial chemical bonds. Therefore, it is a wise choice to prepare bismuth-based heterojunctions by ultrasonic method. For example, Wu et al. explored the preparation of BiOCl/MoS2 heterojunctions with O-vacancy based on pulsed ultrasound-assisted method [72]. As shown in the Fig. 4a, the authors used the high pressure of ∼ 500 atm and high temperature of ∼ 5000 K formed locally after liquid cavitation, and this hot pressure effect caused O atoms to fall off from the crystal lattice, further promoting the formation of Bi-S bonds, and finally induced the construction of BiOCl/MoS2 heterostructures[73]. In addition, it is worth noting that compared with the usual methods of chemical reduction or heat treatment to introduce vacancies, ultrasonic method has the advantages of high efficiency and low energy consumption. Another research reported that they prepared Bi2NbO5F/rectorite composites by ultrasonic synthesis [74]. The researchers revealed that cavitation bubbles, shock waves and shear forces generated by ultrasonic waves peel off the less intermolecular intermolecular sandwiches in Bi2NbO5F and rectorite. More importantly, the H• and •OH synergistic ultrasound generated during ultrasound causes a weak chemical bond between Bi2NbO5F and rectorite, thereby promoting the construction of heterojunctions (Fig. 4b). Wang et al. also formed a Br-Br bond between the interface of Cs2AgBiBr6 and Bi2WO6 by ultrasound-assisted method, which optimized the photogenerated electron transport pathway between the interfaces, thereby promoting the construction of S-scheme heterostructures [75].

It is worth noting that the choice of solvent is also a key factor in ultrasound-assisted synthesis of heterojunctions, and solvents with low vapor pressure can increase the temperature and pressure of cavitation effects. Zhu et al. prepared Bi2WO6/BiOCl heterojunctions by ionic-liquid assisted ultrasonic (BMIM) strategy using 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride as solvent [76]. The low vapor pressure, low boundary tension and high conductivity of BMIM make cavitation bubbles collapse rapidly, the nucleation rate is accelerated, and the conductivity is increased. The above method simplifies heterojunction preparation conditions.

In short, the ultrasonic process has great advantages in assisting the synthesis of Bi-based photocatalytic materials. In addition, it should be mentioned that in the process of controlling the morphology of Bi-based photocatalysts, part of the structure is often broken due to the huge energy generated by the cavitation effect. The research on ultrasound-assisted elemental engineering is still in its infancy, and the cavitation effect is local, which will lead to uncontrollable uniformity and concentration of doped ions and vacancies in Bi photocatalyst materials, which may make quantum efficiency impossible to achieve the best state. Ultrasound-assisted heterojunction construction is a promising synthetic method due to low vapor pressure and fewer solvents with good thermal stability. Therefore, it is necessary to study methods to solve the above problems by adjusting ultrasonic power, frequency, temperature, time, and solvent type.

Mechanism of the whole process of bismuth-based photocatalyst enhanced by ultrasound

In view of the powerful ultrasonic effect, the researchers found that ultrasound can not only promote the synthesis of Bi-based photocatalysts, but also play a beneficial role in the whole process of their photocatalysis. For the sake of further developing the beneficial role of the ultrasound, it is particularly important to fully understand the synergistic mechanism between ultrasound and photocatalysis. Therefore, we summarize the synergistic promotion mechanism of ultrasound outside and inside Bi-based photocatalysts, which provides an effective idea for further optimization of ultrasonic synergistic Bi-based photocatalysts in the future.

Piezoelectric effect

The piezoelectric effect is caused by mechanically coupled polarization [87]. As shown in the Fig. 5b, the piezoelectric material crystal deforms (compression, stretching and torsion) under ultrasonic force due to the lack of symmetry, resulting in the change of positive and negative charge centers with the change direction of the force, and finally forming a spontaneous polarization effect. At the same time, the positive and negative charge centers move in the opposite direction, indicating the formation of an internal electric field that induces the band bending of the semiconductor material, thereby promoting carrier migration [88]. It can be said that ultrasonic irradiation is the inducement of piezoelectric semiconductors to exert the piezoelectric effect. Thus, it is vital to clarify the relationship between ultrasound and piezoelectric effects. Zhong et al. demonstrated that the efficiency of piezoelectric photocatalysis is closely related to the frequency and power of ultrasound [89]. Low frequency (45 kHz) and high power (100 W) are beneficial to the piezoelectric photocatalysis. This is mainly because low frequency and high power will make the air bubbles grow to a larger size before the internal explosion, which can generate stronger pressure during implosion, thereby increasing the cavitation intensity and fracture pressure [90]. The resulting pressure can force on the piezoelectric photocatalyst dispersed in the aqueous solution and lead to deformation, inducing more polarization charge to improve the degradation efficiency [91]. In addition, the ultrasonic induced piezoelectric effect also presents special synergistic effects in different Bi-based photocatalyst systems, which will be discussed in detail in this section.

Co-catalyst synergistic with Bi-based photocatalyst

As cocatalysts, noble metal nanoparticles often use their electron capture capabilities or act as electron donors to reduce the recombination of photogenerated carriers, thereby improving the photocatalytic performance of Bi-based semiconductors [92]. Furthermore, direct contact between metals and piezoelectric semiconductors, in order to bring the Fermi energy level into equilibrium, usually causes electrons to flow between interfaces, bend the bands and form a Schottky barrier, reducing carrier recombination [93]. Along this line, the synergy between the piezotronics and schottky barrier would render massive merits for Bi-based photocatalyst. At the interface between metal and Bi-based photocatalyst, the piezoelectric potential induced by ultrasound can modulate the charge carrier transport and dictate the interfacial energy levels [94]. The COMSOL simulation results of Xu et al. showed that the piezoelectric potential generated in bare BiFeO3 nanofibers is complex and variable in different directions under periodic ultrasonic stimulation [95]. The introduction of Ag NPs can make the most of this disordered piezoelectric energy. As exhibited in Fig. 5e, when the piezoelectric polarization of BiFeO3 is pointed to the right, the Schottky barrier at the contact interface between Ag and BiFeO3 would increase. The hot electrons induced by the LSPR effect would transfer from Ag into the CB of the BiFeO3, and then the piezoelectric potential inside the BiFeO3 further actuates the separation of electrons and holes. Conversely, when the piezoelectric potential of BiFeO3 is pointed to the left, the piezoelectric potential would decrease the Schottky barrier and the photogenerated electrons of BiFeO3 would be injected into the Ag NPs [95]. Subsequently, the photo-generated electrons and holes would undergo redox reactions in different directions. It is proved that the formation of Schottky junctions and the piezoelectric effect synergistically improve the separation efficiency of electron-hole pairs [96]. In general, the metal-supported semiconductor photocatalyst produces LSPR effect and Schottky barrier under the synergistic action of ultrasonic waves and light, which ultimately promotes the separation and migration of carriers.

Elementally engineered modified Bi-based photocatalysts

In the process of elemental engineering to modify Bi-based photocatalysts, ion doping and defect introduction are usually carried out simultaneously. The two mainly enhance the light absorption efficiency and carrier separation by introducing intermediate energy levels and electron trap sites in Bi-based semiconductors. In addition, in the piezoelectric photocatalytic process, elemental engineering can further enhance the separation of improved carriers by inducing polarization [97], [98]. Liu et al. introduced Na and Sm into CaBi2Nb2O9 (NS-CBN) for piezoelectric photocatalysis [99]. The electroresponsive force microscopy shows that the surface potential of NS-CBN is 0.62 μV higher than that of pure CaBi2Nb2O9 (CBN), which is mainly caused by Na and Sm helping the crystal to expand in the 001 direction, and the dipole moves under ultrasound action, forming an internal electric field [100]. At the same time, the polarization–electric field (P-E) and current-electric field (I-E) curves showed that the maximum polarization of NS-CBN is significantly higher than that of CBN, which proves that NS-CBN has a stronger polarization effect and produces more charge per unit area (Fig. 5f). The total electron density results also revealed that there was a higher charge density in the atomic regions of Na and Sm after doping, which helped the separation of carriers during the ultrasonic piezoelectric process [101].

Defects play an important role in adjusting the ultrasonic piezoelectric effect of Bi-based semiconductors [102]. Defect engineering can change the band structure of materials, generate local electrons, enhance light absorption, and improve carrier efficiency [103]. At present, this modification strategy is mainly based on oxygen vacancies [104]. Tang et al. prepared O-vacancy-Bi4Ti3O12 nanosheets (BIT-OV1) [98]. Piezoelectric response microscopy and ± 10 V ramp voltage loop shows that BIT-OV1 has a strong piezoelectric response and has an amplitude voltage butterfly-type curve (Fig. 5c). In addition, the charge difference distribution, bader charge and adsorption energy calculation results also show that the charge interaction force of BIT-OV1 with oxygen/water molecules is significantly stronger than that of BIT, and the adsorption energy increases from 1.325 and 0.035 eV to 1.504 and 0.847 eV, respectively [98]. The bader charge also increases with the increase of OVs. Therefore, OVs contribute to charge transfer, as well as enhance adsorption, and ultimately enhance the ultrasonic piezoelectric effect of BIT-OV1. Zhu et al. also investigated the effects of O vacancies and Sr vacancies on ultrasonic piezoelectricity [105]. The DFT results showed that SVs had a negative effect on improving the polarization of (0 0 1) crystal planes, while OVs could promote polarization. This is mainly because OVs can promote the formation of titanium oxide polyhedral, while SVs can form electron traps [106]. Although vacancies have been shown to help improve the polarization effect and carrier separation of ultrasonic piezoelectricity. However, vacancy strategy still has its own instability, and uncertainty about the influence of the number and type of vacancies on the piezoelectric effect of the system [107].

Heterojunction of Bi-based photocatalysts

Bismuth-based semiconductors are promising photocatalysts with visible light response and controllable morphology. However, due to the low conduct band, low carrier separation rate and low stability of Bi-based materials, their practical catalytic performance is not ideal [108]. To solve these problems, the construction of heterojunctions has become an ideal modification direction. At present, binary and ternary heterojunctions are the focus of research in this field, and they can be divided into Type Ⅱ, p-n heterojunctions and Z-scheme heterojunctions by the transport path of electron-hole pairs and the characteristics of the internal electric field [109]. Type Ⅱ heterojunction redox potential difference promotes carrier separation, but inevitably leads to weakened carrier redox capacity [110]. The p-n heterojunction introduces an internal electric field on the basis of Type Ⅱ and further enhances the carrier separation efficiency [111]. In order to overcome the defect of low redox potential, Z-scheme heterojunctions using electrostatic gravity in adjacent energy bands to guide carrier transport were developed [112]. Similar to metal-loaded semiconductors, piezopolarization tends to tune the interfacial electronic behavior of heterojunctions as well as the band structure [113]. Usually, piezoelectric semiconductor contact interfaces with different Fermi energy levels generate potential barriers, and polarized electrons can be injected, and the band structure adjusted by piezopolarization [93]. Under ultrasonic vibration conditions, a large amount of microbubbles which can create local high pressure are generated in the water, and the microbubbles will explode on the surface of the catalyst, resulting in the bending of piezoelectric materials in the heterojunction. A negative piezoelectric potential is created on the curved surface of piezoelectric materials, and a positive piezoelectric potential is formed at the tensile surface, thereby creating a piezoelectric field which can provide a driving force in the heterojunction for the opposite migration of electron-hole pairs [114], [115]. The strategies for promoting carrier separation and migration in different heterojunctions with piezoelectric effects are described below:

Type II heterojunction photocatalysts, with bandgap interlacing properties, enable photogenerated electrons to transfer from high-CB semiconductors to low-CB semiconductors. Conversely, holes are transferred from low-VB semiconductors to high-VB semiconductors, eventually causing spatial separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs [110]. The construction of Type II heterojunction with piezoelectric effect can form a piezoelectric field at the interface, which can act as an accelerator for the separation and migration of photogenerated electron hole pairs [116]. For example, Tiron et al. used piezoelectric semiconductor Bi2Fe4O9 to construct Bi2Fe4O9/ε-Fe2O3 Type II heterojunction, when the photogenerated electron-hole pair reaches the interface, the internal electric field generated by the residual polarization effect can promote its rapid separation and migration, thereby improving the photocatalytic efficiency of the system [116].

Since Type II heterojunction has limited ability to facilitate electron-hole pair separation. Thus, the concept of p-n heterojunction was proposed. After contact between p-type semiconductors and n-type semiconductors, the difference in Fermi energy levels will lead to the transfer of electrons between interfaces, forming an internal electric field [111]. When photogenerated electron-hole pairs are generated, the rapid separation of photogenerated electrons is accelerated by the synergistic effect of the internal electric field. For example, Zhang et al. constructed BiOI/ZnO p-n heterojunctions for ultrasonic piezoelectric photocatalysis [114]. Since the electron holes generated by the p-n heterojunction have depletion regions during interfacial diffusion, the regulation of carriers by the internal electric field becomes particularly important [115]. As shown in Fig. 5d, BiOI/ZnO deforms under ultrasound, and positive and negative piezoelectric potentials are generated in bending and stretching, resulting in a piezoelectric field, providing a fast track for the separation of electron-hole pairs [117]. Liu et al. verified through theoretical calculations that when BiFeO3/TiO2 (BFO/TO) p-n heterojunction is subjected to ultrasonic stress along the Z axis, piezoelectric effects occur on both sides of BFO and cause the reverse movement of electron-hole pairs [118].

Although the above heterojunction can enhance the separation of electron-hole pairs, it also causes the reduction of redox potential, which affects the redox ability of Bi-based photocatalysts. Thus, Z-scheme heterojunctions with maximized redox potential were developed. The Z-scheme heterojunction migrates photogenerated electrons on the CB of a semiconductor with a low redox potential to the VB of a semiconductor with a high redox potential for recombination, while the remaining electron-hole pairs remain in place [119]. As a result, the system retains the maximum redox potential. Z-scheme heterojunctions based on Bi-based semiconductors have been applied to piezoelectric photocatalysis in recent years due to their efficient electron transport path and strong redox ability [120]. The introduction of piezoelectric polarization field into the Z-scheme heterojunction can modulate the band bending and promote the recombination of electron-hole pairs with low redox capacity, thus enhancing the formed Z-scheme charge transfer [121]. Xu et al. successfully prepared BiFeO3/TpPa-1-COF(BFO/COF) Z-scheme heterojunction piezoelectric photocatalyst [122]. DFT calculated the ΔG of O and H adsorption on diverse sites of BFO/COF to prove that the Fe site in BFO is the active center of OER and the O site in COF is the active center of H coordination. And the OER reaction performed in synergistic induction of BFO/COF by ultrasound and light has the lowest ΔG (1.57 eV). Therefore, the results of Gibbs free energy calculation can prove that the photocatalytic performance of BFO/COF can be effectively promoted under ultrasonic piezoelectric and photosynergy. In general, ultrasonic waves in heterojunction piezoelectric photocatalysis mainly promote carrier separation between heterojunction interfaces by generating a piezopolarized electric field, thereby improving piezoelectric photocatalytic performance. However, the catalytic role of ultrasonic waves itself in current piezoelectric photocatalysis research is often overlooked.

Other promotion mechanism

Bi-based photocatalysts often have insufficient quantum efficiency and low yield of reactive oxygen species, resulting that their efficient photocatalytic performance always relies on the assistance of other technologies [123], [124]. Ultrasound is a frequently used assistive technology. The high-energy vibrations of ultrasonic waves can transmit to the surrounding solvent molecules, resulting in periodic compression and expansion, and eventually cavitation [125]. Each bubble produced by cavitation can be regarded as a reactor, and three different reactions will be carried out from the inside to the outside (Fig. 5a): (1) the pyrolysis reaction of the high temperature generated instantaneously in the cavity core area, which reacts directly with volatile substances; (2) Reactive oxygen species are produced in the water vapor area; (3) ROS migration and reaction in the bubble-solvent interface region[19], [126], [127], [128]. The main reactions of the process are expressed by eqs. (1–4). Therefore, the ultrasonic process can effectively supplement the active oxygen species in the photocatalytic process of the Bi-based material.

More importantly, some studies have demonstrated the synergistic mechanism of acousto-optic catalysis. On the one hand, the existence of Bi-based photocatalysts contributes to the formation of cavitation bubbles. For example, Liang et al. found that Bi2WO6 powder can increase the rate of formation of cavitation bubbles in solution, which can increase the ROS concentration [129], [130]. On the other hand, ultrasound can induce the sonoluminescence of the Bi-based photocatalyst, which stimulates the electron transition and finally increases the quantum yield. The results of acousto-optic synergistic catalysis of Bi2WO6 showed that the acousto-photocatalytic performance was significantly higher than that of photocatalysis and sonocatalysis. This is mainly because the sonoluminescence of Bi2WO6 stimulates the electronic transition of Bi2WO6 and ultimately increases ROS production [131]. It is also worth noting that the physical action of cavitation bubbles can play a role in cleaning the surface of Bi2WO6 [132].

The exertion of acousto-optic synergistic effect is often affected by ultrasonic power, pH and other factors. The ultrasonic output power is related to the photocatalytic performance, because the cavitation bubbles generated by the high power are easy to separate from the photocatalyst. In addition, the increase in power represents more energy input, resulting in faster yield and collapse cycles of cavitation bubbles, resulting in improved degradation efficiency [133]. pH also affects the photoacoustic synergistic efficiency, and the results of Neppolian et al. showed that the ROS yield of cavitation effect is the highest in pH = 3 environment, and as the pH increases, the free radical quencher in the solution is gradually produced, so that the photocatalytic performance gradually decreases [134], [135]. Interestingly, the pKa value of the target molecule can affects the adsorption with cavitation bubbles [134]. Moreover, the choice of stirring speed also have an effect on acousto-optic synergistic effect. Xu et al. found that when the speed is lower than 500 r/min, the generation of ROS is affected by the lack of complete instantaneous collapse, while the cavitation bubble bursts before reaching the optimal size when the speed is higher than 600 r/min, which also leads to the degradation of ROS yield and ultimately affects the synergistic efficiency [136].

Contaminants treatment

Sonochemistry technology is widely used to improve the photocatalytic treatment performance of Bi-based semiconductors for contaminants. Ultrasound can not only directly form free radicals and excite Bi-based photocatalysts, but also induce the formation of polarized electric fields in materials, adjust the band structure to improve carrier separation efficiency [149], [150], [151], [152]. For example, 4-chlorophenol (4-CP) is a persistent and highly toxic organic pollutant with poor biochemistry and refractory degradation [153], [154]. However, ultrasound synergistic luminous light excitation of Bi2O3/TiZrO4 can efficiently degrade 4-CP [134]. HPLC spectra showed that the addition of ultrasonic waves not only increased the degradation rate of 4-CP, but also significantly reduced the intermediate products, and the TOC results also showed that the mineralization degree of 4-CP increased by 3 times under ultrasonic synergy. ROS from ultrasonic cavitation plays a crucial role in the degradation process. The introduction of a large amount of ROS strengthens the oxidation capacity of the system [155].

Piezoelectric photocatalysis has developed rapidly in pollutant treatment in recent years, and ultrasonic waves deform materials through their own energy and cavitation bubble collapse, form polarized electric fields, induce efficient separation of carriers, and finally greatly increase the ROS yield of Bi-based semiconductors [156], [157]. Hu et al. used the piezoelectric photocatalytic properties of Au-BiOBr to efficiently degrade carbamazepine (CBZ) [139]. As shown in the Fig. 6a, under the combined action of ultrasound and light source, the degradation efficiency of CBZ within 30 min is as high as 95%, which is 81% and 28.6% higher than that of photocatalysis and piezoelectric catalysis, respectively. This is mainly due to the SPR effect, which enhances light absorption and promotes interfacial carrier migration through the Schottky barrier generated by the piezoelectric effect [94], [158]. Although ultrasonic waves have made some progress in removing pollutants in synergistic Bi-based photocatalysis, there are still many research gaps, such as the design and development of Bi-based piezoelectric photocatalysts, and the combination of ultrasonic photocatalysis with other advanced oxidation technologies.

Contaminants treatment

With the increasingly serious environmental pollution, it is urgent to find technologies for efficient preparation of clean, high calorific value new energy [2], [159]. In recent years, ultrasonic synergistic Bi-based photocatalysts have been considered to be promising technologies for splitting water to produce H2 and H2O2 [51]. The key to water splitting is to regulate the carrier migration behavior and band structure between interfaces, and the piezopolarization effect of Bi-based semiconductors by using ultrasonic waves can significantly increase the water cracking rate [107]. Xu et al. prepared BiFeO3/TpPa-1-COF for piezoelectric photocatalytic water splitting [122]. As shown in the Fig. 6e, the piezoelectric photocatalytic production of O2 and H2 by BFO/COF20-C reaches 708 and 1416 μmol h-1g−1, respectively, which is mainly attributed to the piezoelectric potential generated by BFO/COF under ultrasound that causes the electron-hole pair to move in the opposite direction, which promotes carrier separation. In addition, the Gibbs free energy results of hydrogen and oxygen production showed that after the introduction of a polarized electric field, the ΔG of all reactions decreased and could be carried out spontaneously. Similarly, Tu et al. used the piezoelectric photocatalytic performance of Bi4W0.5Ti0.5O8Cl to generate water oxidation (WOR) to produce H2O2 [147]. As shown in the Fig. 6b and c, the efficiency of piezoelectric photocatalytic production of H2O2 in pure water is as high as 530 μmol h-1g−1.

Ultrasonic photocatalysis continuously reduces a large amount of CO2 into high-value products, which has become an effective way to sequester carbon and solve the greenhouse effect [160]. Due to the high stability of CO2 and the induction energy of C=O bonds up to 750 KJ/mol, photocatalytic reduction of CO2 has become a great challenge [161]. Yu et al. used O-vacancy-modified Bi3TiNbO9 for piezoelectric photocatalytic CO2 reduction, and the yield of CO was as high as 20.9 μmol h-1g−1 (Fig. 6f) [104]. This is mainly due to the introduction of OVs affecting the dipole alignment, strengthening the polarized electric field.

Summary and outlook

This article reviews the recent research progress of ultrasound on Bi-based materials in photocatalytic technology. The role and mechanism of ultrasonic waves in the preparation strategy, theoretical calculation and photocatalytic application of Bi-based semiconductors were comprehensively introduced. In the preparation process, ultrasonic waves can not only use cavitation effect to control the morphology and structure of materials, destroy the crystal lattice to complete the introduction of doping and vacancies, but also use the ultrasonic environment to promote the formation of weak chemical bonds between materials to achieve the purpose of heterojunctions construction. In addition, ultrasonic waves can use cavitation bubble collapse to produce a large amount of ROS, which plays a key role in promoting redox reactions. And there are also a large number of reports that ultrasonic waves can induce piezoelect ric effects on Bi-based semiconductors and regulate the energy band and carrier migration behavior through the resulting polarized electric field. Due to the above ultrasonic characteristics, the synergistic ultrasonic performance of Bi-based photocatalysts in pollutant degradation, water decomposition and carbon dioxide reduction is rapidly improved, which lays a good foundation for practical applications.

Up to now, although a large number of studies have been carried out and certain progress has been made, the research of ultrasound in collaborative Bi-based photocatalysis is still in its infancy, and there are still many questions and gaps in the mechanism research, as follows:

•In the ultrasound-assisted synthesis of Bi-based photocatalysts, due to the rapid and high-energy characteristics of cavitation effect, the material morphology is damaged, and it is difficult to ensure the consistency of the material morphology. In addition, ultrasound is still in its infancy in elemental engineering, and defects and doping concentrations are often the key to affect the photocatalytic performance, but the concentration and distribution characteristics and ultrasonic parameters of Bi photocatalysts have not yet emerged. Therefore, future research should not only focus on the development of solvents with different vapor pressures, but also study the effects of power, frequency, temperature, stirring rate and other factors on the morphology, doping and defects of Bi-based photocatalysts.•The mechanism of ultrasonic waves in synergistic Bi-based photocatalysts is not deep enough, and the piezoelectric effect is mainly the main one. The amount of ROS contribution generated by ultrasonic waves through cavitation is often overlooked. In addition, local thermal effects generated by ultrasonic waves may excite the photocatalyst. We need to further explore the types of ROS generated in the ultrasonic environment, the influence on the surface of Bi photocatalysts, and whether there is a synergistic effect between ultrasound, defects and vacancies.•In terms of ultrasound-induced piezoelectric photocatalytic effect of Bi-based semiconductors, it is necessary to develop more piezoelectric materials in the future, modify the high-entropy structure of existing piezoelectric materials, and explore the resonance frequency of the maximum piezoelectric potential to achieve the highest piezoelectric performance. In addition, the mechanism of piezoelectric effect of some Bi-based photocatalysts is not clear, and it needs to be discussed in depth in the mode of combining theoretical calculation and experiment.•Ultrasound synergistic Bi-based semiconductor photocatalysts are still in the laboratory simulation stage in terms of pollutant removal and energy conversion, and there are high energy consumption and costs. In the future, the research focus can be on the use of natural energy (sunlight, tides, wind, water flow difference, etc.) to induce ultrasonic synergistic photocatalytic reactions.
