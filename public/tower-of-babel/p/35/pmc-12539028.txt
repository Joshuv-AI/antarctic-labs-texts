Recurrent bilateral giant breast fibroadenomas with a somatic PIK3CA p.G106V mutation: case report and review of current evidence
Authors: Yuemei Zhang, Qixing Tan, Yugui Xiao, Wenhao Wang, Shengkai Huang, Qinguo Mo, Wenhai Zhang, Wei Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFibroadenomas are common benign breast tumors, but recurrent bilateral giant fibroadenomas are rare. Background

Fibroadenomas account for approximately 27.6% of benign breast lesions and are most prevalent in women under 30 years of age [1]. Giant fibroadenomas, defined as tumors larger than 5 cm or heavier than 500 g, account for under 4% of cases but often lead to prominent breast distortion and significant psychosocial impact [2, 3]. While their exact pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, most conventional fibroadenomas harbor MED12 mutations [4, 5], implicating this gene in tumor development. In contrast, PIK3CA alterations are uncommon in this setting and have been reported only sporadically. We describe here a young woman with recurrent, bilateral giant fibroadenomas carrying a PIK3CA p.G106V mutation, and we review relevant literature exploring the factors that may contribute to disease behavior.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old woman underwent bilateral excision of multiple fibroadenomas in 2019, the largest measuring 5.5 cm, with histology also showing intraductal papilloma and adenosis. She noted recurrence in 2021, with progressive growth by early 2024 (Fig. 1).

Clinical examination revealed multiple firm, mobile masses in both breasts, with the largest (right, 11 o’clock) measuring 7.5 cm, associated with breast asymmetry and ptosis. She had no personal or family history of breast cancer or syndromic disorders.

Laboratory testing revealed a serum prolactin level of 52.5 ng/mL (reference range: 5.18–26.53 ng/mL), an estradiol level of 71 pg/mL (reference range in the follicular phase: 21–251 pg/mL), and a progesterone level of 0.16 ng/mL (reference range in the follicular phase: <0.1–0.3 ng/mL). Ultrasonography revealed multiple solid lesions; MRI identified well-defined masses with BI-RADS 3–4 features (Fig. 2). Brain MRI excluded prolactinoma and neurofibromatosis.

She underwent bilateral mass excision combined with reduction mammoplasty and nipple–areolar reconstruction. Grossly, tumors were firm, encapsulated, and smooth (Fig. 3a-b). Microscopically, they showed epithelial hyperplasia, focal stromal cellularity, and no margin invasion(Fig.4a). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated CK5/6 patchy positivity (Fig.4b), diffuse ER positivity(>90%) (Fig. 4c), and a Ki-67 index of 5–10% (Fig. 4d).

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This patient presented with bilateral multiple breast giant fibroadenomas during adolescence, characterized by rapid tumor growth and postoperative recurrence, demonstrating the aggressive growth pattern typical of this type of breast fibroadenoma. The molecular mechanisms underlying these lesions may differ from ordinary fibroadenomas and require analysis of specific genetic and molecular abnormalities.

Previous studies have identified recurrent MED12 gene exon 2 mutations as fundamental to breast fibroadenoma formation, with mutations potentially regulated by estrogen pathways [5, 6]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that breast fibroadenomas are stimulated by estrogen and may grow rapidly during puberty and pregnancy [7]. Reproductive hormone stimulation, high sensitivity of breast tissue to estrogen, and relative deficiency of estrogen antagonists have also been considered potential pathogenic factors [8, 9]. Therefore, MED12 gene mutations cannot be excluded as potential triggers for disease development in this patient, since hormones may be important factors in tumor growth.

Notably, prolactin regulates mammary development through PRLR, and some patients with multiple breast fibroadenomas harbor heterozygous single nucleotide polymorphisms in exon 6 of the prolactin receptor (PRLR) gene (encoding Ile146→Leu substitution in the extracellular domain) [10]. The prolactin level of this patient is higher than normal, the potential role of PRLR gene mutations warrants attention, as such mutations may render breast tissue hypersensitive to normal hormone levels, leading to rapid tumor growth during pubertal hormonal fluctuations. This also provides theoretical basis for PRLR-targeted therapy.

Cowden syndrome (CS) is a cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline mutations in the PTEN gene tumor suppressor gene. Early occurrence of multiple breast fibroadenomas is one of its clinical features, with lifetime breast cancer risk estimated at 25–50% [11]. A previous report described an 18-year-old female with bilateral multiple breast fibroadenomas due to germline PTEN gene mutations [12], with clinical presentation similar to our patient. However, our patient showed no mutations affecting PTEN protein function, suggesting potential differences in pathogenic mechanisms.

PIK3CA gene-related overgrowth spectrum (PROS) disorders include CLOVES syndrome, megalencephaly-capillary malformation, and Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome [13]. Research has shown that some patients with diffuse capillary malformation with overgrowth (DCMO) harbor somatic PIK3CA p.G106V variants [14]. PIK3CA gene mutations are important drivers of solid tumor progression and, although rare in breast fibroadenomas [15], provide clinically significant implications worthy of exploration for this patient’s follow-up management.

From a molecular mechanistic perspective, mutations in PIK3CA gene, which encodes p110α lead to sustained activation of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway, promoting abnormal cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and enhancing tumor cell invasiveness—mechanisms proven central to tumor progression in various solid malignancies. The functional impact of the p.G106V mutation, which is located in the helical domain of p110α, remains relatively understudied [16, 17]. In breast fibroadenomas, Vorkas, P.A et al.‘s 2010 initial report suggested that PIK3CA mutations may disrupt the inherent growth balance of fibroadenomas, placing local tissue in a “potentially active state“ [18]. Although Nasir’s research indicated no difference in gene mutation profiles between recurrent and non-recurrent breast fibroadenomas [19], the presence of PIK3CA gene mutations in this case warrants vigilance regarding potential associations with breast cancer risk. Pivotal studies published have confirmed that complex fibroadenomas with cystic or sclerosing features are associated with an increased long-term risk of breast cancer. Aberrant activation of the PI3K–AKT pathway is also closely linked to malignant transformation of breast epithelial cells [20]. suggesting that such mutations could represent an additional risk factor. Furthermore, the prevalence of PIK3CA mutations among Chinese breast cancer patients has been reported to be 45.6% [21]. Accordingly, when such mutations are identified in breast fibroadenomas, it is important to consider whether they may indicate an “early molecular abnormality,” particularly in bilateral and multiple lesions. In this setting, such an underlying molecular profile may warrant closer clinical surveillance.

Regarding follow-up strategies, given this patient’s absence of family history but presence of PIK3CA gene mutations, individualized follow-up based on routine strategies is warranted. For conventional multiple breast fibroadenoma patients, breast ultrasound or mammography every 6–12 months is typically recommended, focusing on monitoring tumor size, number, and morphological changes. However, for this patient carrying a somatic PIK3CA mutation, it could be considered a follow-up every 3–6 months, as well as incorporating breast MRI for earlier detection of potential malignant transformation signs. Additionally, given cross-regulation between the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway and estrogen signaling [22], assessment of patient’s hormonal level changes may be necessary, as well as avoiding long-term exogenous estrogen exposure (such as estrogen-containing contraceptives) to reduce the potential risk of pathway overactivation.

Notably, current understanding of PIK3CA gene mutations’ clinical significance in breast fibroadenomas lacks large-sample studies. Whether these mutations directly increase cancer risk and whether synergistic effects exist with other driver genes (such as MED12) requires further exploration. Therefore, during follow-up, in addition to imaging surveillance, it can be considered performing biopsy on newly developed or significantly enlarged lesions, followed by molecular pathological analysis to track dynamic changes in mutation status, so that more precise evidence for risk assessment can be obtained. Simultaneously, patients should maintain regular breast self-examination and receive enhanced health education to fully understand their condition’s specificity and actively cooperate with long-term follow-up.

Limitations and future directions

This case report has certain limitations. First, MED12 and PRLR mutation analyses were not performed, which restricts a comprehensive understanding of possible multigene interactions. Second, the functional impact of the PIK3CA p.G106V variant remains unclear due to the absence of supporting data from in vitro or in vivo models, representing a critical gap in current knowledge. Future research will therefore focus on three main areas: (1) elucidating potential synergistic mechanisms between PRLR mutations and PIK3CA mutations; (2) validating, in larger patient cohorts, the causal relationship between PIK3CA mutations and excessive fibroadenoma growth; and (3) conducting functional studies to characterize the biological effects of the PIK3CA p.G106V variant.

Conclusion

Overall, PIK3CA gene mutation detection adds molecular-level considerations to this patient’s follow-up. Although current evidence cannot establish direct associations with breast cancer risk, combining multiple breast fibroadenoma features with specific biological pathway alterations may support a more intensive and individualized follow-up strategy. Future large-scale cohort studies are needed to clarify the prognostic value of such mutations and provide stronger scientific foundations for clinical decision-making.
