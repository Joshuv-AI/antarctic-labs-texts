Development of Power 4 a Healthy Pregnancy: reflective insights on cocreating an effective dietary program for pregnant women
Authors: Renske M. van Lonkhuijzen, Jeanne H. M. de Vries, Edith J. M. Feskens, Annemarie Wagemakers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While pregnancy is recognized as a “teachable moment” for lifestyle changes, many pregnant women struggle to meet dietary guidelines due to multiple barriers including limited nutritional support in antenatal care and challenges such as physical discomfort, food aversions, and conflicting information. Power 4 a Healthy Pregnancy (P4HP), a program that flexibly integrates four consultations into routine antenatal care, has demonstrated significant improvements in the quality of pregnant women’s diets. This article reflects on the program’s participatory development process, and examines how stakeholder engagement and empowerment principles shaped an effective program. We claim that the program’s success is rooted in its cocreation with midwives, dietitians, and pregnant women. The P4HP program offers valuable insights for developing other sustainable health promotion initiatives in antenatal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version of this article (10.1007/s12508-025-00473-1) contains supplementary material, which is available to authorized users.

Introduction

Maintaining a healthy diet during pregnancy is crucial for both mother and child, as it influences pregnancy outcomes, fetal development, and long-term health [1]. Pregnancy is often seen as a “teachable moment” for lifestyle changes, but challenges such as physical discomfort, food aversions, and conflicting information can hinder the adoption and maintenance of dietary changes [2]. The Health Council of the Netherlands (Gezondheidsraad) has established dietary guidelines for pregnant women [3]. These guidelines encompass daily fruit and vegetable targets, weekly fish consumption, limited intake of processed foods, and mandatory folic acid and vitamin D supplementation. However, many pregnant women, particularly those with a lower socioeconomic status (SES), struggle to meet these recommendations, potentially leading to adverse health outcomes for both mother and child [4].

In the Netherlands, midwives are ideally positioned as trusted sources of nutritional guidance during pregnancy [5]. Yet, they are rarely involved in promoting healthy nutrition, as current antenatal care primarily focuses on food safety and risk prevention, with limited attention to supporting women in establishing and maintaining healthy dietary patterns [6]. Despite the acknowledgment of their important role, midwives face barriers that hinder their ability to provide comprehensive dietary support, such as limited time and knowledge [5, 6]. This leads to an apparent paradox: Pregnancy provides an optimal opportunity for dietary intervention, but the healthcare system misses the opportunity to benefit from women’s increased motivation. Despite having limited knowledge and time, midwives are in a unique position to address this gap through improved nutritional support because of their trusted relationships and frequent contact. By recognizing the expertise of healthcare practitioners and encouraging women’s autonomy, an empowerment approach could lay the path for effective nutritional support.

Empowerment is a crucial factor in improving health outcomes and could play a promising and significant role in enhancing pregnant women’s self-reliance and capability to manage their nutrition [7–9]. It encompasses both external factors (such as facilitation of choices and access to resources) and internal factors (such as belief in one’s abilities and control over the situation) [7]. This dual focus is crucial, as both supportive environments and personal confidence are needed for making and sustaining healthy choices. An empowerment approach goes beyond traditional knowledge transfer, fostering respectful partnerships between women and healthcare providers, where power is shared equally [7, 10]. In this approach, healthcare professionals shift from being experts to facilitators, helping women to identify and work towards their own meaningful goals [10]. While healthcare providers offer their expertise, the woman’s priorities and readiness for change guide dietary improvements. This approach recognizes that supporting self-chosen goals, no matter how small, fosters openness for ongoing dietary changes. The healthcare provider’s role is to share knowledge with permission and engage in a dialogue that raises awareness, thereby ensuring women retain control over their dietary choices. Additionally, it acknowledges that dietary behaviors are embedded in women’s broader life contexts and that sustainable change occurs when choices align with both health goals and life circumstances. Given midwives’ trusted relationships with pregnant women and their integral role throughout pregnancy, program alignment with midwifery practice becomes crucial for effective implementation. However, existing programs aimed at changing health behavior during pregnancy often focus on education rather than empowerment and do not align well with Dutch midwifery care [9]. It is therefore recommended to implement programs that include opportunities for learning new behavior change skills.

Building on these empowerment principles, the Power 4 a Healthy Pregnancy (P4HP) program was developed and integrated into routine antenatal care to empower pregnant women to make healthier dietary choices. The program has demonstrated significant improvements in the quality of pregnant women’s diet and positive evaluations by pregnant women, midwives, and dietitians [11–14]. This underscores the importance of understanding how its development process shaped an effective program. This article describes the P4HP program, presents the development steps, and examines how the program’s participatory development process contributed to these outcomes, thereby offering insights for future program development in antenatal care.

Phase 1: exploratory research

In Phase 1, we investigated the perspectives of pregnant women, midwives, and dietitians using multiple research methods. We used focus groups with all stakeholders to investigate current practices, identify available or lacking tools in nutrition communication, and design interview methods for the interviews with pregnant women in the target group. Interviews with pregnant women, particularly those from lower SES groups, explored food-related experiences and needs, while interviews with midwifes examined nutritional communication practices and collaboration potential. Surveys assessed dietitians’ interest in expanding their role and measured women’s empowerment levels across socioeconomic groups. Eight systematic and nonsystematic literature reviews were conducted, covering eight topics including empowerment and empowerment programs, dietary factors and assessment tools for pregnant women with or with no low SES, midwives’ role and resources in nutritional guidance, interprofessional collaboration in prenatal care, and the effectiveness of dietary promotion tools. The methodology for these reviews followed systematic review guidelines with comprehensive database searches, defined inclusion/exclusion criteria, and quality assessment procedures. Full details of each review are available from the authors upon reasonable request, with titles and abstracts provided in Supplementary File 1.

Key findings were that pregnant women needed personalized information tailored to their specific situation, practical strategies for healthy eating, and support in managing challenges such as nausea and social pressure [15]. They expressed a desire not only for advice on foods to avoid, but also for positive guidance on healthy nutrition [15]. Women with a lower SES scored significantly lower on most empowerment subscales than those with a higher SES. The research in Phase 1 identified possibilities to improve nutrition communication in antenatal care at both the system level (structured midwife-dietitian collaboration) and individual level (women empowerment). While midwives acknowledged their crucial role in nutrition guidance and expressed interest in tools to facilitate nutrition discussions, they faced time and knowledge constraints [6]. Dietitians, who often see pregnant women late in pregnancy when problems are already present, advocated for a more preventive approach through closer collaboration with midwives [16]. Facilitating factors to improve the diet quality of pregnant women in antenatal care included integrated care pathways, empowerment-focused tools, emphasis on women’s autonomy, and enhanced professional training in nutrition counseling.

Phase 2: iterative program development

In Phase 2, we conducted “pressure cooker” (PC) sessions in which multidisciplinary stakeholders translated research findings into practical program components, thereby prioritizing professional implementation expertise and organizational knowledge to ensure feasible healthcare system integration. After reviewing the Phase 1 findings, participants identified key pregnancy moments for nutrition intervention and necessary tools. These sessions established four core principles: 1) early pregnancy intervention; 2) repeated nutrition communication; 3) a positive, empowering approach; and 4) combined expertise of midwives and dietitians. Based on this session and expert consultations, an initial concept for the P4HP program was developed and further refined during the second and third PC sessions.

This led to a revised second concept that was simplified and included a dietitian consultation. We evaluated this version through expert interviews, which resulted in several refinements: greater implementation flexibility across different practices, better integration with existing care pathways, clearer distinction between the roles of midwives and dietitians, and development of supporting materials. A notable addition was a visual conversation tool illustrating key food groups for pregnant women [3], designed to empower women to steer conversations toward nutrition topics most relevant to their individual situations (see Supplementary File 2 [in Dutch]). We used this refined third concept of the P4HP program in the pilot study.

Phase 3: pilot implementation and refinement

The third concept of the P4HP program was piloted in two midwifery practices, resulting in positive experiences across all stakeholder groups. Pregnant women reported increased motivation for dietary changes and valued the personalized approach and practical tips provided by midwives during the first consultation. Midwives were generally satisfied but encountered challenges integrating the consultation into their existing care routines. The second consultation with dietitians was particularly well-received, with women appreciating access to specialized nutritional expertise. Dietitians reported high participant motivation and observed early behavioral changes following the first consultation. The visual conversation tool was well-received by both women and professionals, facilitating more targeted discussions. This pilot evaluation resulted in only minor adjustments to the content of the final P4HP program.

Final program

The development process resulted in the P4HP program, which focuses on improving nutrition from an empowerment perspective. The unique aspect of the P4HP program is the integration of empowerment principles with practical support facilitated by midwives and dietitians during consultations. In these consultations, empowerment principles are reinforced using motivational interviewing (MI), a counseling method that recognizes women as experts in their own lives. MI is used to enhance motivation, self-efficacy, and personal control [17]. The implementation of the P4HP program is supported by a comprehensive manual for professionals, which includes example questions, case studies, information about MI, and a visual conversation tool (see Supplementary File 2).

The P4HP program consists of four nutrition-focused consultations, designed to be integrated into routine antenatal care. The midwife’s consultations can be combined with existing prenatal check-ups, ensuring efficient use of both the midwife’s and woman’s time. These consultations are complemented by one dedicated session with a dietitian. Empowerment is the core principle of the P4HP program [15]. By fostering equal partnerships between healthcare providers and women, the program integrates both external factors (facilitating choices and meaningful connections) and internal factors (women’s belief in their abilities and control over their circumstances) [7]. These principles are put into practice during consultations in which women analyze their situations and create their own solutions. Although the program includes visual tools, the potential of these tools is not automatically realized. Their effectiveness rather depends on how they are used to facilitate women’s autonomy in decision-making.

The P4HP program incorporates several key approaches to operationalize empowerment. It shifts the focus of nutritional support from traditional expert-driven care to facilitative partnerships, thereby placing women’s self-identified goals and readiness for change at the center. Midwives and dietitians take a holistic approach to nutrition, by recognizing that factors such as women’s daily challenges and conflicting priorities significantly influence their dietary choices. The program intentionally adopts a strength-based approach, emphasizing women’s potential rather than focusing solely on nutritional issues. Pregnant women reported sustained motivation for dietary changes, and both women and healthcare providers appreciated the positive framing, the focus on women’s context, and the collaborative dynamic.

Phase 4: implementation and evaluation

The P4HP program was implemented on a larger scale in midwifery practices and evaluated using a cluster randomized controlled trial (C-RCT) and process evaluations. The program was delivered at no cost to pregnant women, with participating midwives and dietitians receiving reimbursement for their consultations through the research project.

The C‑RCT demonstrated significant improvements in pregnant women’s diet quality [11]. Process evaluations revealed high satisfaction among pregnant women, who particularly appreciated the personalized advice from dietitians, who could answer specific questions about nutrition, while midwives helped them stay motivated throughout their pregnancy [14]. In addition, the P4HP program enhanced interprofessional collaboration between midwives and dietitians [12]. A key takeaway was the importance of implementation flexibility. While the P4HP program’s core elements remained unchanged (empowerment principles, repetition, and interprofessional collaboration), its integration into existing care routines needed to be adaptable. This flexibility was achieved by allowing healthcare professionals to determine consultation timing that suited their practice routines. Additionally, practices could opt for dietitians to deliver the first consultation when this was preferred, which two practices chose for. This adaptable approach enabled the program to accommodate different practice realities while maintaining its essential components.

Discussion

The P4HP program has been developed in close collaboration with key stakeholders to enhance the diet quality of pregnant women through empowering consultations provided by midwives and dietitians. This article reflects on the participatory development process of the program. The participatory development approach employed in the P4HP program aligns with implementation science principles demonstrating that stakeholder engagement enhances program adoption and sustainability [18]. The empowerment focus resonates with global woman-centered care recommendations recognizing women’s expertise while providing professional support [19]. We claim that the success of the P4HP program is rooted in its cocreation with midwives, dietitians, and pregnant women.

The P4HP program’s success lies in how stakeholder engagement shaped both the program’s content and implementation approach. The comprehensive stakeholder engagement embodied the empowerment principles we aimed to integrate into the program itself. The development process demonstrates that meaningful stakeholder engagement requires the creation of multiple opportunities for voice and influence [7]. The extensive research in Phase 1 proved to be crucial not just for gathering information, but also for building relationships and trust that carried through subsequent development phases. This comprehensive understanding enabled us to translate empowerment from an abstract concept into concrete, actionable program components that addressed the multifaceted influences on prenatal dietary choices. Phase 2’s PC sessions demonstrated the value of true cocreation, with multidisciplinary group dynamics facilitating program translation from research to practice. This cocreation approach reflects evidence that interprofessional collaboration improves maternal health outcomes [20].

While midwives and dietitians were generally enthusiastic to implement the P4HP program and recognized its importance, we faced some implementation challenges including the recruitment of pregnant women with a low SES and heavy workloads that sometimes limited midwifery practices’ participation. Nevertheless, we successfully matched local dietitians with midwifery practices for which no existing collaboration had been established. The iterative development process revealed that successful implementation required balancing standardization with local adaptation. While maintaining core elements was essential for program integrity, the ability to adjust the timing and delivery to local contexts proved crucial for sustainable adoption in real-world settings. The successful outcomes of the P4HP program demonstrate that integrating nutritional support into routine antenatal care through an empowerment-based approach is both feasible and effective when supported by the appropriate structures [11, 12, 14].

Engaging stakeholders early and maintaining their involvement throughout the development process were essential in ensuring the P4HP program was practical and aligned with real-world requirements and preferences. However, time constraints in midwifery practices and financial barriers to dietitian consultations pose challenges to the integration of the P4HP program into routine antenatal care. While larger-scale research is needed to determine the cost-effectiveness of the P4HP program, our research offers valuable insights for developing other sustainable health promotion initiatives in antenatal care through participatory development and empowerment principles. This study underscores the necessity of consistent investment in preventative care to reduce downstream healthcare costs associated with adverse health outcomes.
