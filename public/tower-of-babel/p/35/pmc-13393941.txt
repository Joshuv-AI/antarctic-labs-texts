Feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of electronic medication monitoring system to improve antipsychotic adherence: a type 1 effectiveness-implementation hybrid pilot study
Authors: Run Sherry Wang, Huanyu Hu, Mengjun Zhang, Wei Li, Mingsong Li, Guangyu Tong, Saimei Luo, Hui Wang, Birong Yang, Zhaojun Zuo, Chenyun Wang, Zhengxu Li, Xintian Long, Le Xu, Julius Niyibizi, Rui Zhao, Yingqi Wang, Bohan Li, Yaoming Tian, Yu Tang, Teng Zhang, Dong Roman Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchizophrenia is a severe mental disorder characterized by widespread poor medication adherence, which impacts treatment outcomes and quality of life. In rural China, despite the national Severe Mental Illness Management System, day-to-day adherence monitoring still relies largely on family supervision and self-report, creating gaps in early detection of non-adherence.ObjectiveTo assess the preliminary effectiveness, feasibility, and acceptability of an electronic Medication Event Monitoring System (MEMS) pillbox to improve antipsychotic adherence among community-dwelling adults with schizophrenia in rural China.MethodsWe conducted a two-month pilot randomized wait-list controlled trial in two townships in Yunnan Province. Twenty-eight patients were identified via purposive sampling and then individually randomly assigned (1:1) to either the intervention group (active MEMS pillbox) or the control group (deactivated pillbox for Month 1, then activated for Month 2) via a computer-generated random sequence. The primary outcome was medication adherence, secondary outcomes included symptom severity, functional status, adverse effects and attitudes toward medication. We also conducted rapid qualitative research, analyzed using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), to assess feasibility and acceptability.ResultsAt month 1, the intervention group showed higher daily and dose adherence rates than the control group, although these differences did not reach statistically significant. In contrast, self-reported adherence (measured by BARS) and medication attitudes (measured by DAI-10) showed significant improvements over time in both groups. No serious adverse events, psychiatric rehospitalizations, or clinical relapses occurred in either group during the study. Qualitative findings showed high technical acceptance but revealed declining provider motivation over time and systemic performance appraisal pressures as key implementation barriers.ConclusionsThis pilot study provides preliminary evidence of the technical feasibility of MEMS for schizophrenia care in rural area; however, given the limited sample size, the findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. While technically feasible, the broader applicability of MEMS is constrained by organizational and systemic barriers. Future larger-scale trials with extended follow-up are necessary to evaluate long-term sustainability and scalability.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2500102022. Introduction

Mental disorders have become an increasingly urgent global public health concern, contributing substantially to disability and mortality. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2019, the overall burden of mental illnesses has increased sharply over recent decades [1]. From 1990 to 2019, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to mental disorders rose by 55%, increasing from 80.8 million to 125.3 million, and accounting for approximately 4.9% (95% UI: 3.9%–6.1%) of the total global DALYs [1]. Among all mental health conditions, schizophrenia is one of the most severe and disabling psychiatric disorders, affecting an estimated 20.9 million people worldwide [1]. In China, the direct healthcare cost of schizophrenia, largely driven by recurrent hospitalizations and sustained antipsychotic use, have been increasing annually [2]. More critically, indirect costs—including loss of productivity, unemployment, and the burden of family caregiving—are estimated to be two to three times greater than direct healthcare costs [2]. These combined pressures often lead to prolonged economic hardship and even chronic poverty in households affected by schizophrenia [3].

Antipsychotic medications remains the cornerstone of schizophrenia treatment, offering proven benefits in controlling positive symptoms (e.g., hallucinations and delusions), negative symptoms (e.g., apathy and social withdrawal), disorganized behavior, and cognitive impairments [4]. However, the effectiveness of these medications largely depends on patients’ adherence to prescribed regimens, which refers to the extent to which patients take their medications at the correct dosage and at prescribed intervals [5]. Medication non-adherence—ranging from occasional missed doses to intentional discontinuation—remains a persistent issue in schizophrenia care [4–6]. It is strongly associated with symptom relapse, clinical deterioration, and the so-called “revolving door” phenomenon, where patients repeatedly cycle through hospitalization, discharge, and re-hospitalization [7, 8].

Despite the well-established benefits of sustained antipsychotic treatment, medication adherence among individuals with schizophrenia remains suboptimal. Studies report a wide range of non-adherence rates—from 20% to 89%—with many indicating that roughly 40% to 55% of patients do not follow their medication regimens as recommended [9–11]. In China, where an estimated 90% of individuals with schizophrenia reside in community settings, the government has implemented the Serious Mental Illness Management System (SMIMS) to support community-based care. This system provides outpatient follow-ups, quarterly home visits, free antipsychotic medications, and financial assistance [12]. Although SMIMS has facilitated access to treatment for millions of individuals, gaps remain in ensuring medication adherence. In rural areas, village doctors—who are often tasked with providing comprehensive health services to all residents within their jurisdiction—face considerable constraints in delivering adequate follow-up and home-based care for individuals with schizophrenia [13]. Consequently, the medication management of schizophrenia depends heavily on family caregivers or patients’ self-monitoring [14]. This arrangement constrains clinicians’ ability to detect early signs of non-adherence, which is often only recognized after symptom exacerbation is observed during home visit consultations [14]. Moreover, adherence assessment is frequently based on self-reported or caregiver-reported data, which may be prone to recall bias or inaccuracies [15], thereby further complicating efforts to ensure effective long-term pharmacological management.

Digital health interventions, particularly mobile health (mHealth) technologies, such as short message service (SMS) reminders, mobile health applications, and electronic medication monitoring system (e-monitors) have emerged as promising strategies for improving medication adherence in the management of chronic conditions [8, 16]. Among those, medication event monitoring system (MEMS) demonstrated efficacy in enhancing adherence among patients with diseases such as tuberculosis [16], hypertension [17], and HIV/AIDS [18]. For example, a randomized controlled trial conducted in Tibet reported that the use of MEMS reduced poor adherence by 29.2% and increased treatment success rates by 21% [16].

However, improving medication adherence in individuals with schizophrenia poses more complex challenges. In addition to cognitive impairments—such as memory deficits and executive dysfunction—non-adherence is often exacerbated by poor insight into illness and concerns regarding medication side effects, leading many patients to discontinue treatment voluntarily [19, 20]. MEMS can address these specific barriers by providing audio-visual cues that help compensate for neurocognitive impairment. More importantly, since many patients lack insight and may unintentionally or discreetly skip doses, MEMS offers an objective record of medication-taking behavior. By bypassing the biases of self-reporting and sharing real-time adherence data with community providers, this technology allows for early clinical intervention before minor lapses escalate into full symptomatic relapse. Although MEMS have been applied in schizophrenia care, existing studies have yielded mixed findings, and high-quality evidence remains scarce, particularly in low- and middle-income country (LMIC) settings [21, 22]. Notably, to our knowledge, no randomized controlled study to date has evaluated the effectiveness of MEMS in promoting medication adherence among individuals with schizophrenia in China. To address these gaps, the present pilot randomized controlled trial was designed to evaluate a multifunctional MEMS—a smart pillbox equipped with audio-visual reminders, automatic medication-taking detection, and real-time data sharing via a smartphone platform (WeChat). The primary aim of this study is to assess the preliminary effectiveness, feasibility, and acceptability of this MEMS-based intervention in promoting antipsychotic adherence within a Chinese community context.

Methods

Study design

This study used a Type 1 effectiveness–implementation hybrid design. In a Type 1 hybrid, the primary focus is to evaluate the preliminary clinical effectiveness of an intervention, while simultaneously collecting data on implementation processes and outcomes (e.g., feasibility and acceptability) in real-world settings. This approach is intended to accelerate translation by assessing not only whether the intervention shows benefit, but also whether it can be delivered in routine care. We conducted an individually randomized, two-arm wait-list controlled trial to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention.

Participants and sample size

Participant screening occurred on 17 January 2025, followed by participant contact and invitation by village doctors on 17–18 January 2025. Written informed consent was obtained on 19–20 January 2025, prior to baseline assessment and randomization, in two rural townships located within Baoshan Municipality, Yunnan Province, China. Baoshan, situated on the western border of Yunnan Province and adjacent to Myanmar, served as the study location. Final follow-up visits for benefit and harm outcomes were completed by 22 March 2025. The trial ended on schedule after reaching the target sample size (n = 30); no interim analyses or stopping rules were applied. The intervention was implemented across eight village health stations distributed within the two selected townships.

The sample size for this pilot study was determined based on recommendations for feasibility trials, which suggest that a sample of 25–30 is sufficient to estimate the variance of primary outcomes and to assess implementation feasibility. Moreover, assuming the most conservative scenario of 50% medication adherence, a sample size of 30 would allow us to estimate the adherence rate with a 90% confidence interval and a margin of error of approximately ± 15%. This level of precision is adequate to inform the parameters for a future definitive randomized controlled trial without subjecting participants to unnecessary burden.

Individuals with schizophrenia were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) residing in the community; (3) having a primary diagnosis of schizophrenia based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), as applied by local psychiatric services in Baoshan; (4) currently receiving oral antipsychotic medication; (5) physically residing in one of the two designated rural townships of Baoshan Municipality; and (6) registered in the national Serious Mental Illness Management System. Exclusion criteria included: (1) being hospitalized for schizophrenia at the time of recruitment; (2) having missed to collect medication refills for three consecutive months; and (3) having serious physical impairments (e.g., severe hearing or visual impairments) that would hinder the use of a MEMS.

Village doctors were eligible to participate in the implementation component of the study if they: (1) Were currently responsible for community management and medication follow-up of patients with schizophrenia under the local Severe Mental Illness Management System in the study township; (2) Were able to communicate with patients and caregivers in Mandarin or the local dialect. Village doctors were excluded if they reported that they would retire, relocate, or leave their post during the two-month study period.

Randomization and masking

Participants were identified by public health physicians at the rural township hospitals and selected using a purposive sampling approach. To examine the potential effectiveness of MEMS across different adherence profiles, 30 participants were deliberately sampled based on their prior medication adherence status and caregiver availability. Following enrollment, a research team member independent of the recruitment process assigned participants in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or wait-list control group using a computer-generated random sequence.

Both verbal and written informed consent were obtained from all participants and their caregivers prior to enrollment. After generating the participant roster, individuals were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or control group by a designated research team member. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and healthcare providers was not feasible. The statistician was also not blinded to group allocation, as direct access to the MEMS cloud platform was required to extract and calculate granular adherence metrics (daily and per-dose adherence). This technical necessity resulted in unavoidable unblinding during data processing. To mitigate potential bias, all analyses were conducted according to a pre-defined Intention-to-Treat (ITT) framework, and this constraint has been acknowledged in the Limitations section.

Procedures

Participants assigned to the intervention group were provided with a MEMS pillbox for 30 days medication storage. The device offered two primary functions: first, it issued musical reminders—using instrumental tones without lyrics—to prompt patients to take their medication at scheduled times; second, it digitally recorded each instance when the box was opened, with the data automatically transmitted to a cloud-based server. Village doctors could access patients’ medication adherence data either through a secure, password-protected WeChat-based smartphone application or via a web-based computer interface in real time. Participants in the control group were provided with an identical but deactivated MEMS pillbox, which functioned solely as a medication storage container. To ensure that control participants were also exposed to the MEMS device — and to meet the required sample size for the qualitative component — the devices were activated after the 1-month follow-up. Participants then continued using the fully functional MEMS devices for an additional month. Adherence data from both the first and second months were analyzed to assess usage patterns and intervention effects over time, providing both between-group comparisons (Month 1) and within-group longitudinal insights (Month 2). During the trial all participants continued to receive usual care under the national Serious Mental Illness Management System: free oral antipsychotics dispensed monthly by village doctors and quarterly home visits; no additional psychosocial interventions or medication changes were introduced by the study.

Both patients and their cohabiting family members received standardized training on the use of the MEMS pillbox at the time of device distribution, with particular emphasis on incorporating the device into their daily medication routines. The reminder times were individually tailored to align with each patient’s habitual medication schedule, as some patients had fixed times for taking their medication. Although the MEMS devices recorded each opening event and automatically transmitted the data to a cloud-based server, neither patients nor their family members—regardless of group allocation—were granted access to the adherence data, to minimize participant burden. Access to adherence information was restricted exclusively to village doctors in intervention group. If a patient refused to continue medication, missed more than two consecutive doses or a full day’s medication, or if a village doctor identified any concerns about a patient’s adherence, the doctor was required to promptly initiate a phone call to remind the patient and verify their medication status.

Furthermore, in Baoshan, all individuals diagnosed with mental health disorders are eligible to receive antipsychotic medications free of charge. Village doctors are responsible for retrieving a monthly supply of these medications from township hospitals and notifying patients to collect their prescriptions from local village health stations. In addition, under the framework of the national Serious Mental Illness Management System, village doctors are mandated to conduct follow-up visits every three months for patients with severe mental disorders. In our study, to ensure continuous medication supply, patients were asked to return to the village health stations to replenish their 30-day medication stock, starting from the day of enrollment. Additionally, participants were instructed to bring back any unused medications during their follow-up visits. Village doctors conducted home visit pill counts at day 24 and day 54, while trained investigators performed independent pill counts and collected self-reported adherence data from patients at day 30 and day 60. These assessments allowed to capture both overall treatment coverage and the stability of adherence over time, we analyzed the data at four distinct time points: (1) Cumulative adherence for Month 1 and Month 2, calculated as the average adherence over the first 30-day period (Days 1–30) and the second 30-day period (Days 31–60), respectively; and (2) End-of-week adherence at Week 4 and Week 8, representing the average adherence during the final seven days of the first month (Days 24–30) and the second month (Days 54–60), respectively.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was adherence to antipsychotic medications, defined as the proportion of scheduled doses actually taken. Adherence was assessed using data recorded by the MEMS devices via the cloud-based server, supplemented by pill counts conducted during home visits. Two indicators were used to calculate adherence based on MEMS data: (1) Medication Adherence–Daily Rate (MA-DR), which is the number of days on which all prescribed doses were taken as scheduled, divided by the total number of days in the monitoring period; (2) Medication Adherence–Dose-based Rate (MA-DoR), which means the total number of correctly recorded dosing events divided by the total number of scheduled dosing events. To further validate adherence estimates, we also administered two validated rating scales: the Brief Adherence Rating Scale (BARS) and the Drug Attitude Inventory–10 (DAI-10).

Secondary outcomes included symptom severity, functional status, adverse effects. Symptom severity was measured using the Clinical Global Impression scale for schizophrenia, including both the Severity (CGI-S) and Improvement (CGI-I) subscales. Functional status was assessed using the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0). Patient-reported side effects of antipsychotic medications were evaluated using the Glasgow Antipsychotic Side-effect Scale (GASS). All above outcome assessments were conducted at baseline (T0), one month (T1), and two months (T2) following enrollment.

A complementary process evaluation was conducted to assess the implementation of the intervention, employing rapid qualitative analysis guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and group concept mapping (GCM) methodology. We conducted individual semi-structured interviews (aligned with CFIR domains) and group interviews (informed by GCM procedures) with patients, their caregivers, village doctors, and public health physicians from township hospitals across both intervention and control groups after the trial’s completion. The semi-structured interview guides were purpose-built for this study in Chinese, translated into English, and tailored to three respondent groups (staff, patients, caregivers); no previously published version exists (English interview guides provided as Supplementary File 1). Interviewees were invited to share their experiences with the intervention, including their perceptions of the design, delivery, and usability of the electronic medication monitoring system. In addition, they were asked to provide recommendations for improving the intervention and facilitating its future implementation and scale-up in rural community settings.

Routine patient-level demographic and clinical information was extracted from the national Serious Mental Illness Management System, in accordance with national reporting requirements. Collected data included name, age, sex, residential address, education level, occupation, psychiatric diagnosis, and standardized treatment outcomes. Each participant was assigned a unique study identification number to ensure de-identification for research purposes. Additional patient outcomes tracked through the public health reporting system included outpatient appointment attendance, relapse events (defined as a clinically assessed worsening of symptoms based on interviews with patients and caregivers), psychiatric rehospitalizations, and adverse events such as all-cause mortality, episodes of wandering, interpersonal violence, property damage, and suicide.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

All analyses followed the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomized participants in the groups to which they were originally assigned, regardless of their level of adherence or continued participation. As the dropout rate was 0% throughout the study period, all 28 randomized participants provided complete data; therefore, no statistical imputation methods were required. For continuous quantitative variables, data were summarized using mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, and between-group comparisons were conducted using independent-sample t-tests. For non-normally distributed data, the median and interquartile range [P25, P75] were reported, and comparisons between groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages were reported, and between-group comparisons were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For outcomes measured at multiple time points, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were applied using the geepack package in R. An exchangeable working correlation structure was specified, and baseline values of the corresponding outcomes were included as covariates in the models. Estimated within-group changes and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and between-group differences were estimated accordingly. Longitudinal trends, including estimated means or mean differences across time points, were visualized using line plots.

The qualitative component employed a rapid qualitative analysis approach, guided by the CFIR. Semi-structured interview notes were analyzed using a deductive coding framework based on CFIR’s five domains: Intervention Characteristics, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Characteristics of Individuals, and Implementation Process. To enhance analytical efficiency, real-time structured notetaking and matrix-based analysis were used in place of full verbatim transcription. Emergent themes were organized by CFIR domains and presented in tabular format to highlight perceived barriers, facilitators, and implementation strategies.

Although pill counting and group concept mapping was initially included as a complementary method to measure medication adherence and capture stakeholder perspectives, these procedures imposed substantial cognitive burden for participants, particularly among those with limited formal education. Consequently, the quality and amount of usable data collected during the study period was insufficient for analysis. Therefore, findings from the pill counting and GCM component are not presented in the results section but are discussed in the discussion section to reflect on methodological challenges and implications for future implementation research.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of the Southern Medical University in Guangzhou, China [SMU Ethical Approval 2024 No.104] and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients and their family members in this study gave their written informed consent before taking part in the study.

Results

Recruitment and the flow of participants

A total of 30 individuals were screened, of whom 2 declined participation. The remaining 28 participants were enrolled and randomly assigned to the intervention (n = 14) or control group (n = 14). No participants were lost to follow-up during the two-month study period (Fig. 1). Regarding clinical safety, the rates of psychiatric rehospitalization and clinical relapse (defined as a clinically assessed worsening of symptoms) were 0% in both groups throughout the trial. Furthermore, no serious adverse events, including all-cause mortality, episodes of wandering, or interpersonal violence, were recorded. Detailed reporting of mild adverse events was measured by the GASS scale.

Primary outcome: medication adherence

Adherence outcomes for the first follow-up period are summarized in Table 2. At Month 1 (representing the cumulative 30-day average), the intervention group showed numerically higher adherence rates than the control group, with a Medication Adherence–Daily Rate (MA-DR) of 0.51 (SD = 0.10) versus 0.40 (SD = 0.09), and a Medication Adherence–Dose-based Rate (MA-DoR) of 0.52 (SD = 0.11) versus 0.48 (SD = 0.10). Further analysis of Week 4 (the final seven days of the first month, Days 24–30) revealed that the intervention group maintained a stable adherence level (MA-DR = 0.66, SD = 0.11; MA-DoR = 0.66, SD = 0.12) relative to the control group (MA-DR = 0.42, SD = 0.10; MA-DoR = 0.48, SD = 0.10). Although these differences did not reach statistical significance, the intervention group demonstrated consistently higher medication adherence than the control group across all time points. At the Month 2 follow-up (Days 31–60), adherence in the intervention group remained stable (MA-DR = 0.58, SD = 0.11). Notably, after the MEMS reminder and upload functions were activated for the control group at the start of the second month, their MA-DR showed a slight upward trend, increasing from 0.40 (SD = 0.09) at Month 1 to 0.45 (SD = 0.11) at Month 2, while their MA-DoR remained relatively stable (0.48 vs. 0.47). Secondary outcome: self-reported adherence and attitudes

As shown in Tables 3 and 4, not statistically significant between-group differences were observed for most secondary outcomes; however, significant longitudinal time effects were identified across several domains over the two-month period (T0, T1, and T2). Regarding medication-related attitudes and self-reported adherence, scores on the BARS (χ² = 6.266, p = 0.044) and the DAI (χ² = 144.719, p < 0.001) improved significantly over time in both groups, although no significant group-by-time interactions were found. Regarding clinical safety, mild adverse events were monitored using the GASS. While the GASS scores showed a significant time effect (χ² = 9.560, p = 0.008), there were no significant group or interaction effects, indicating that the MEMS intervention did not disproportionately increase the burden of side effects compared to the control group (Table 4). For clinical symptoms, a marginally significant group-by-time interaction was observed on the CGI-S scale (χ² = 5.971, p = 0.051). Post hoc analysis indicated that the control group experienced a greater reduction in symptom severity at the 1-month follow-up (β = − 0.86, 95% CI: − 1.58 to − 0.14, p = 0.020); however, this difference was not sustained at the 2-month mark. No significant changes were observed for the CGI-I scale. Finally, functional status, as assessed by the WHODAS 2.0, showed a significant deteriorated over time in both groups (χ² = 88.459, p < 0.001), with no statistically significant difference between groups.

Qualitative components: feasibility and acceptability of the MEMS

Qualitative analysis based on the CFIR framework revealed key barriers and facilitators to implementation, as shown in Table 5, which presents the results of the qualitative study. Participants reported usability challenges with the MEMS pillbox, including weak reminders, limited capacity, technical malfunctions, and difficulty navigating the mobile app. Despite this, many found it helpful for medication organization. Cost concerns and increased workload were noted, but acceptance remained high if the device was free or low-cost. Village doctors cited time constraints and integration challenges with existing public health tasks, though community support and leadership endorsement facilitated uptake. Low health literacy, competing demands, and misaligned reminder times limited patient engagement. While some providers questioned its necessity for stable patients, most users expressed positive attitudes. Over time, provider motivation declined due to added workload. Suggested improvements included simplifying procedures, targeting non-adherent patients, and offering small incentives.

Disscussion

This Type 1 Effectiveness-Implementation Hybrid Pilot Study evaluated the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of a MEMS-based intervention to enhance antipsychotic adherence among community-dwelling individuals with schizophrenia in rural China. TheAlthough the primary adherence outcomes did not reach statistical significance, participants in the intervention group consistently demonstrated higher adherence scores than those in the control group across all measured time points. Despite these observed gains, the adherence rates measured via MEMS in this pilot study were lower than the approximately 0.70 mean adherence rate reported for oral antipsychotics in a previous meta-analysis [23]. Given the pilot nature of this study and its limited statistical power, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. primary value of these preliminary signals lies in providing essential variance estimates to inform the power calculations and design of future, more robust trials.

Interestingly, adherence rates estimated from pill counts — which are not reported in the Results section-were significantly higher than those recorded by MEMS, occasionally exceeding 100%. This discrepancy highlights limitations in the feasibility and accuracy of pill counts, particularly in rural settings. In our study, overestimation from pill counts may have been partly due to inadequate training on pill count procedures provided to village doctors as part of the research project, leading to inconsistent or inaccurate documentation. This reflects broader challenges in using pill counts as an adherence measure in resource-constrained rural areas of China, where the primary care workforce faces systemic barriers—including an aging workforce, high turnover rates, low educational attainment, and a shortage of qualified physicians [24]. Many rural doctors have not received full-time academic training and are not eligible to take formal licensing exams. Combined with heavy clinical workloads, these factors make it difficult to perform accurate and consistent pill counts for patients with schizophrenia. Additionally, qualitative interviews uncovered further behavioral challenges in adherence monitoring. Some patients and caregivers reported either taking additional doses voluntarily or withholding medication from loved ones who were unwilling to take it. Caregivers also emphasized the need for lockable MEMS devices to prevent unauthorized access to medication.

Adherence measured by BARS were also higher that the adherence measure by MEMS, which consistent with prior studies that highlight the tendency of self-reported adherence measures to overestimate actual adherence. According to existing literature, the drug adherence rate among patients with schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorders estimated by objective measures was 65.5%, 60% to 81% by a combination of subjective and objective measures, and 34% to 80% by subjective measures alone, revealing a large degree of difference among results. When using various measures within a single study, the drug adherence rate estimated by self-report was 92% [21].

The absence of significant group-by-time interaction effects across primary and secondary outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as it likely reflects the inherent short-term variation in schizophrenia symptoms rather than a definitive lack of intervention impact. Clinical global ratings, such as the CGI-S may be less sensitive to subtle improvements within an eight-week window, given that symptomatic stabilization often lags behind behavioral changes. Despite the lack of statistical significance, the temporal trajectories observed reveal patterns worth noting. Both groups exhibited improvements in DAI during the first and second months following the intervention, which may be consistent with previous findings that participants tend to modify their behavior when aware of being monitored [23]. Interestingly, this improved attitude coincided with the unexpected deterioration in self-reported WHODAS scores. This paradoxical dissociation may because patients’ drug attitudes improve and they become more engaged with their treatment, they may develop a sharper insight into their functional limitations, leading to higher disability reporting. Meanwhile, the divergent GASS (side effect) profiles—showing a transient reduction in the intervention group but a delayed exacerbation in the control group—further highlight the complex temporal dynamics of patient-reported outcomes. The discrepancy between these patient-reported gains in attitude (DAI) and the lack of clinician-rated symptom superiority (CGI-S) suggests that symptomatic stabilization may lags behind attitudinal shifts. In addition, WHODAS, as a patient-reported measure, captures limitations in daily activities and social participation that may not be apparent to clinicians. In contrast, the CGI-S focuses mainly on observable symptom severity and may overlook more subtle functional impairments. Such discrepancies between measurement tools are well-documented in schizophrenia and mood disorders, where patient- and clinician-reported outcomes often exhibit low concordance.

The qualitative process evaluation, guided by the CFIR framework, provided additional insights into the contextual determinants influencing the implementation process. While many patients and caregivers expressed interest in and acceptance of the intervention, several barriers—such as low health literacy, misalignment with daily routines, and limited technological familiarity—hindered effective engagement. Low educational attainment among participants likely posed a barrier to the effective use of the MEMS system and the associated mobile application. As noted in our qualitative findings, participants with limited formal education experienced greater difficulty navigating the technology and understanding digital reminders. This underscores the need for more inclusive, low-literacy designs in future interventions. Second, village doctors’ attitudes toward the MEMS system evolved over the course of the project. The requirements to set up individualized medication schedules, conduct pill counts, and regularly log in to the WeChat mini program to remind patients significantly increased their workload. As a result, most village doctors did not follow up with patients who missed doses on certain days. Furthermore, some village doctors questioned the added value of electronic pillboxes, believing that medication adherence could be sufficiently managed through routine approaches such as self-reported logs. Third, at the administrative level, concerns emerged regarding integration of MEMS data into the national severe mental illness management system. A key concern was that the performance appraisal criteria for medication adherence—set at 90–95%—would be difficult to meet if adherence were measured using MEMS data rather than self-reports. Since MEMS provides a more accurate reflection of real-world adherence, its use could result in lower performance ratings, which may in turn affect salaries. These systemic constraints must be addressed in future studies. In subsequent phases, we will focus on supporting village doctors in actively using the MEMS system and leveraging its data to provide differentiated care, with increased attention to patients demonstrating poor adherence.

Implementation for practice

In rural China, schizophrenia case management is often undertaken by village doctors. While “formal” physicians also practice in these areas, their licensure and scope of practice differ from those of tertiary-hospital specialists. Functionally, village doctors’ responsibilities resemble those of community health workers or community nursing roles in other countries. Notably, China does not have a standardized community health care workers cadre, and community nursing remains concentrated in urban settings. Against this backdrop, our findings are directly transferable to psychiatric and community nursing in resource-limited settings. Nurses can operationalize MEMS-generated adherence signals to to risk-stratify patients and set follow-up intensity; tailor adherence counseling and psychoeducation for patient–caregiver dyads; coordinate support across primary care and families; and detect and escalate emerging non-adherence earlier. Collectively, these practices position nurses as the hub of data-informed, family-engaged adherence management in rural care.

Limitations

This study has several strengths, including a mixed-methods approach, real-time adherence tracking, and successful integration within routine rural health services. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, although randomization was employed to ensure internal validity, the purposive sampling approach limits the generalizability of findings. Recruitment was based on predefined fit profiles, including prior adherence status and caregiver availability. Consequently, the sample may not fully represent the broader rural schizophrenia population, particularly those with minimal social support. Additionally, while we adhered strictly to the intention-to-treat principle, the lack of blinding for participants and the statistician—due to limited field resources—may introduce a risk of analysis bias. Second, the structural complexity of the delayed-intervention design limits long-term controlled comparisons. The control group’s transition to the intervention after one month shifted the study from a parallel-group RCT to a feasibility-focused longitudinal observation. While this ensured all participants received the intervention, it reduces the interpretability of second-month outcomes as strictly controlled experimental findings. Third, the intervention was a multi-component integrating MEMS monitoring with responsive phone reminders from village doctors. While this pragmatic approach reflects real-world primary care, the observed effects stem from the synergy between technology and human support. Therefore, the standalone impact of the MEMS hardware cannot be isolated from the behavioral influence of reminders. Fourth, the two-month follow-up period is insufficient to evaluate long-term sustainability. Finally, the poor data quality of manual pill counts, and the failure of the Group Concept Mapping component underscore the heavy workload of primary care staff and the cognitive burden placed on patients by complex research tasks. These challenges resulted in unusable data for certain secondary measures.

Conclusion

This pilot study demonstrates that a MEMS-based intervention is technically feasible and acceptable for monitoring medication adherence among rural patients with schizophrenia. However, the qualitative evidence reveals that systemic applicability remains a significant challenge. Factors such as increased workload for village doctors, declining provider motivation, high cognitive demands on patients, and the lack of integrated performance metrics within the healthcare system pose substantial barriers to routine implementation. Given the pilot nature of this study, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. While our results suggest a potential for behavioral improvement, they primarily serve to identify the critical individual and organizational adaptations required for future large-scale trials. The transition from a pilot research setting to sustainable clinical practice will require structural adaptations, including automation of data feedback loops, targeted training for primary care providers, and alignment with existing performance appraisal systems. Future research should prioritize these implementation strategies to ensure the long-term viability of digital health solutions in resource-constrained environments.
