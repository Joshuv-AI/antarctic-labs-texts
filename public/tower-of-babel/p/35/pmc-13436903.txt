Erector spinae plane block versus transversus abdominis plane block on postoperative analgesia among patients undergoing hysterectomy: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Ahmed Goda Ahmed, Saeed Baradwan, Wael Mohamed Elmahdi Ibrahim, Othman Saadeldien Yahia, Doaa Mohamed Almonayery, Asmaa Ibraheim Tolba Gad, Amira Ibrahim Lashin, Mona Salah Eldin Sayed, Sherif Kamal Arafa, Ahmed Mohamed Abdelhakim, Amr Ahmed Magdy, Ahmed Abdelaziz Shama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:While the transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) is commonly used for postoperative analgesia after abdominal procedures, the erector spinae plane block (ESPB) has recently gained attention as an alternative regional anesthesia technique with potential advantages in managing visceral pain. We aimed to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the analgesic efficacy of ESPB compared to TAPB under ultrasound guidance following hysterectomy.Materials and Methods:A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science, and Scopus to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing ESPB versus TAPB for pain relief in patients undergoing hysterectomy through January 2026. Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan software: Review Manager (RevMan), version 5.4.0, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark. The primary outcome was postoperative pain intensity assessed by the 10-cm visual analog scale (VAS), with clinical significance evaluated using minimal clinically important difference (MCID) analysis (threshold 1.0 cm). Secondary outcomes included postoperative opioid consumption and incidence of nausea and vomiting.Results:Six RCTs involving 447 patients were included. ESPB demonstrated significantly lower VAS pain scores compared to TAPB at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h postoperatively (P < 0.001). MCID analysis revealed that clinical benefit was most pronounced in the early postoperative period, particularly at 2 h. Patients receiving ESPB had significantly lower 24-h opioid consumption (P < 0.001) and reduced incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) (P = 0.004).Conclusion:ESPB demonstrates superior analgesia compared to TAPB following hysterectomy, particularly in the early postoperative period, with reduced opioid consumption and PONV incidence. Further research with standardized protocols is warranted.

Introduction

Hysterectomy is one of the most commonly performed gynecological procedures and ranks as the second most prevalent surgery after cesarean section.[1] Patients undergoing hysterectomy typically experience moderate to severe postoperative pain.[2] Inadequate pain management can lead to delayed recovery, prolonged hospitalization, reduced patient satisfaction, and increased risk of chronic pain and thromboembolic complications.[34]

Patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with opioids is commonly used for postoperative pain management.[5] However, opioid use is associated with adverse effects, including nausea, vomiting, constipation, respiratory depression, and the risk of dependence.[6] Consequently, opioid-sparing strategies have become increasingly prioritized. Multimodal analgesia, which combines various analgesic techniques to achieve effective pain control while reducing opioid consumption, has emerged as a cornerstone approach.[7] Among these techniques, regional anesthesia has gained considerable attention for its efficacy and safety profile.[8]

The transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) is a regional anesthesia technique used for pain control in abdominal surgeries.[9] It involves injecting local anesthetic into the fascial plane between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles using either anatomical landmarks or ultrasound guidance.[10] This plane contains the spinal nerve branches that innervate the abdominal wall muscles and parietal peritoneum.[11] Studies have demonstrated that TAPB reduces both acute and prolonged postoperative pain, decreases opioid consumption, and delays time to first rescue analgesia in patients undergoing hysterectomy compared to no block.[1213] However, while TAPB effectively manages somatic pain, it has limited efficacy in blocking visceral pain.[1415]

The erector spinae plane block (ESPB) is a novel regional anesthetic method first described by Forero et al.[16] in 2016. Unlike traditional truncal blocks, ESPB has the potential to address both somatic and visceral pain through blockade of the dorsal and ventral rami, as well as the rami communicantes of spinal nerves.[1718] The technique utilizes easily identifiable ultrasound landmarks, enhancing its safety profile.[19] Clinical evidence has demonstrated that ESPB reduces postoperative opioid consumption and improves pain control compared to placebo in patients undergoing hysterectomy.[20]

Research comparing the postoperative analgesic efficacy of ESPB and TAPB following hysterectomy has yielded conflicting results. Some studies have reported superior pain management with ESPB compared to TAPB,[2122] while others have found no significant difference between the two techniques.[2324] To address these inconsistencies, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of ESPB versus TAPB for postoperative pain management in patients undergoing open or laparoscopic hysterectomy. Our primary objective was to evaluate postoperative pain intensity, while secondary objectives included assessment of opioid consumption and the occurrence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV).

Materials and Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The review was not prospectively registered.

Search strategy

A comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted across four electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science, from inception to January 28, 2026. Only English-language publications were included. The search strategy utilized combinations of the following terms: (Erector spinae plane block OR Erector spinae plane nerve block OR ESP block OR ESPB) AND (Transversus abdominis plane block OR TAP block OR TAPB) AND (Hysterectomy OR Abdominal hysterectomy OR Laparoscopic hysterectomy). The literature search was carried out independently by two authors, and any differences were resolved through mutual agreement. Detailed search strategies tailored to each database are available in the supplementary materials [Supplementary File S1].

Study selection

We chose studies that conformed to the following population, intervention, control, outcomes, and study design (PICOS) criteria: (1) Population: women undergoing elective hysterectomy (open or laparoscopic) for benign or malignant conditions, (2) Intervention: ultrasound-guided ESPB, (3) Control: ultrasound-guided TAPB, (4) Outcomes: postoperative pain levels, cumulative opioid consumption, and instances of PONV, and (5) Study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs). We imposed no restrictions on the type, dosage, volume, or concentration of local anesthetics, or the use of adjuvant agents, provided both groups received ultrasound-guided block techniques.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) mixed surgical populations or combined gynecological procedures (such as hysterectomy with other concurrent procedures) where hysterectomy-specific outcomes could not be separately extracted or analyzed; (2) studies that did not report any of our predefined primary or secondary outcomes of interest; and (3) studies that lacked full-text availability or were published only as conference abstracts without sufficient methodological detail.

Two independent reviewers systematically screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to identify potentially eligible studies. Articles that appeared to meet the inclusion criteria based on title and abstract screening underwent full-text review by the same two reviewers to confirm eligibility. Disagreements at any stage of the selection process were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized, pre-piloted data extraction form to ensure consistency and accuracy. Extracted data were subsequently cross-checked among the two reviewers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The following data were systematically extracted from each included study: first author and year of publication, country of origin, sample size, baseline patient characteristics (mean age, body mass index (BMI), and american society of anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification), surgical approach (open vs. laparoscopic), operative duration, details of the regional anesthesia technique (type, dose, volume, and concentration of local anesthetic agents used), and main findings in both ESPB and TAPB groups. The primary outcome was postoperative pain intensity, assessed using the 10-cm visual analog scale (VAS), at predefined time points: 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h after surgery. Secondary outcomes included cumulative opioid consumption and the incidence of PONV within the first 24 h postoperatively.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included RCTs were independently assessed by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB 2) tool.[25] Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion. The ROB 2 tool evaluates five distinct domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (5) bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain was assessed using signaling questions, and an overall risk of bias judgment was assigned for each study as “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias.” Risk of bias assessments were visualized using the risk-of-bias visualization (robvis), R package version 0.3.0, developed by McGuinness LA and Higgins JPT, University of Bristol, Bristol, United Kingdom.[26]

We utilized the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to systematically assess the quality of evidence.[27] This assessment considered factors such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Evidence quality was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low by two review authors working independently, with disagreements resolved through discussion.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4.0, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) by one author and independently verified by a second author to ensure accuracy. Meta-analyses were conducted using random-effects models for all outcomes to account for both within-study and between-study variability, providing more conservative effect estimates. Continuous data were pooled and reported as mean differences (MD) or standardized mean differences (SMD), while dichotomous data were summarized as odds ratios (OR), all accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CI). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess clinical significance alongside statistical significance for pain intensity outcomes, we calculated the proportion of the 95% CI exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). The MCID for VAS pain scores was defined as 1.0 cm on a 10-cm scale, based on published literature for acute postoperative pain.[28] When the CI overlapped the MCID threshold, we calculated the proportion Pδ using the formula: Pδ = (ΔU − δ)/(ΔU − ΔL), where ΔU and ΔL represent the upper and lower limits of the 95% CI, respectively, and δ represents the MCID.[29] Values of Pδ close to 1 indicate that most of the CI lies above the MCID, suggesting strong clinical benefit; values close to 0.5 indicate uncertainty regarding clinical significance; and values close to 0 suggest minimal clinical benefit. This analysis was performed for pain scores at each time point (2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h postoperatively).

Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Chi-square test and quantified using the I2 statistic. Sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses were planned to explore sources of substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 50%) if present. Assessment of publication bias using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test was not performed, as fewer than 10 studies were included in each meta-analysis, which limits the reliability of these methods.[3031]

Results

Results of the literature search and characteristics of the included studies

The study selection process is depicted in the PRISMA flow diagram [Figure 1]. Initially, a total of 70 studies were identified through the database search. After removing 25 duplicate records, 45 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 28 records were excluded as they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. The remaining 17 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 11 were excluded for specific reasons [Supplementary File S2]. Ultimately, six studies satisfied the criteria for both qualitative and quantitative analysis.[212223243233]

Three studies[232432] performed ESPB and TAPB before the initiation of surgery, whereas the remaining three studies[212233] applied both blocks following the surgical procedure. Three studies[212232] included patients undergoing total abdominal hysterectomy (TAH), in which the uterus is removed through an abdominal incision via laparotomy, while three studies[232433] involved total laparoscopic hysterectomy (TLH), a minimally invasive technique using laparoscopic instruments. The sample sizes for the included RCTs ranged from 30 to 154 participants, with an overall total of 447 individuals involved; 223 were assigned to the ESPB group and 224 to the TAPB group. There was variability in the dosages, types, drug combinations, and adjuvants used with local anesthetics across the studies; however, all studies administered the same dose and type of medication for both blocks. Bupivacaine was the primary local anesthetic utilized in four trials,[21233233] while ropivacaine was used in two trials.[2224] The included studies took place in several countries, including China,[22] the United States,[23] Egypt,[21] Italy,[24] Turkey,[33] and India.[32] Table 1 outlines the characteristics of the studies involved.

Risk of bias of included studies and quality of evidence

The risk of bias assessment for the included RCTs is summarized in Figure 2. Of the six included studies, four were rated as having low overall risk of bias,[21222333] while two had a high risk of bias.[2432] The high-risk ratings were primarily attributed to concerns regarding deviations from intended interventions and bias in outcome measurement. According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence varied across outcomes, with low certainty for postoperative pain intensity and moderate certainty for postoperative opioid consumption and PONV. Detailed results of the GRADE assessment are presented in Supplementary File S3.

Outcomes

Postoperative pain intensity

The temporal pattern of postoperative pain intensity is illustrated in Figure 3. ESPB demonstrated significantly lower pain scores compared to TAPB at all assessed time points (P < 0.001 for all comparisons), with MD of –0.95 cm (95% CI [–1.50, –0.40]) at 2 h, –0.45 cm (95% CI [–0.59, –0.32]) at 4 h, –0.52 cm (95% CI [–0.72, –0.32]) at 6 h, –0.40 cm (95% CI [–0.58, –0.22]) at 12 h, and –0.31 cm (95% CI [–0.42, –0.21]) at 24 h.

Temporal pattern of postoperative pain intensity comparing erector spinae plane block (ESPB) versus transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB). The line chart displays mean differences in Visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores with 95% confidence intervals at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h postoperatively

Assessment of clinical significance using the MCID threshold of 1.0 cm revealed that at 2 h postoperatively, the Pδ ratio was 0.45, indicating uncertainty regarding clinical benefit due to overlap of the CI with the MCID. At subsequent time points (4, 6, 12, and 24 h), the entire CIs fell below the MCID threshold, suggesting that although pain reductions were statistically significant, they did not reach clinically meaningful levels in the later postoperative periods.

Statistical heterogeneity was moderate at 2 h (I2 = 47%) and low at subsequent time points (I2 = 9-25%). The magnitude of pain reduction was greatest at 2 h postoperatively and gradually diminished over time, though statistical significance was maintained throughout the 24-h period. Detailed forest plots for each time point are provided in Supplementary Figures S1a-e.

Postoperative opioid consumption

Five trials examined postoperative opioid usage, revealing that the ESPB group significantly reduced opioid consumption 24 h after hysterectomy compared to the TAPB group (SMD = –0.48, 95% CI [–0.73, –0.22], P < 0.001), as illustrated in Supplementary Figure S2. Statistical heterogeneity was low (P = 0.23, I2 = 28%).

Postoperative nausea and vomiting

Five trials assessed the occurrence of PONV 24 h post-hysterectomy, finding that the ESPB group had a lower incidence of PONV compared to the TAPB group (OR = 0.35, 95% CI [0.17, 0.72], P = 0.004), as shown in Supplementary Figure S3. Moderate heterogeneity was observed among studies (P = 0.17, I2 = 38%).

Discussion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that ESPB provides superior analgesia compared to TAPB, with significantly lower pain scores at multiple postoperative time points (2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h). Assessment of clinical significance using the MCID of 1.0 cm on the VAS scale revealed that only the early postoperative period (2 h) approached this threshold with considerable uncertainty, while subsequent time points fell below the MCID despite maintaining statistical significance. Additionally, ESPB was associated with reduced 24-h opioid consumption and lower incidence of PONV.

Our findings are consistent with recent meta-analytic evidence comparing ESPB to TAPB across various abdominal procedures. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of laparoscopic cholecystectomy found that ESPB significantly reduced pain scores at rest and during movement, decreased morphine equivalent consumption, and prolonged time to first analgesic request, though PONV rates were comparable between groups.[34] Similarly, another meta-analysis in abdominal surgery found that ESPB reduced 24-h opioid consumption, improved pain scores, prolonged block duration, and decreased PONV incidence compared to TAPB.[35] Our results in hysterectomy patients extend this evidence, showing consistent benefits in pain reduction and opioid-sparing effects with the additional advantage of reduced PONV. However, our MCID analysis provides a nuanced interpretation: while ESPB demonstrates statistical superiority, the clinical meaningfulness of this benefit appears most pronounced in the immediate postoperative period, with uncertain clinical relevance at later time points. These findings collectively suggest that ESPB may be preferred for early postoperative pain management in patients undergoing hysterectomy.

The potential superiority of ESPB over TAPB in managing postoperative pain stems from fundamental differences in their anatomical targets and mechanisms of action.[36] ESPB involves injecting local anesthetic into the fascial plane deep to the erector spinae muscle, allowing the anesthetic to spread to the paravertebral space where it blocks both dorsal and ventral rami of spinal nerves.[37] This extensive distribution enables ESPB to provide comprehensive analgesia for both somatic and visceral pain by blocking multiple dermatomes and autonomic fibers involved in visceral pain transmission.[38] In contrast, TAPB specifically targets nerves within the transversus abdominis fascial plane, primarily addressing somatic abdominal wall pain with limited efficacy against visceral pain.[39]

Our research had several significant advantages. To begin with, we implemented an extensive search strategy that covered multiple databases. Additionally, we restricted our analysis exclusively to RCTs to ensure the credibility of our results. It is important to emphasize that our study complied with the PRISMA guidelines, demonstrating its methodological soundness. Importantly, this is the first meta-analysis specifically comparing ESPB and TAPB for postoperative analgesia in hysterectomy patients, addressing a critical gap in the literature and providing evidence-based guidance for clinical practice.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this systematic review was not preregistered in PROSPERO or other registries, which may introduce potential selection and reporting bias. Second, the number of eligible studies was limited. Some trials lacked adequate blinding, introducing potential bias into the results. In addition, the included studies had relatively small sample sizes and short follow-up periods, precluding assessment of long-term outcomes such as chronic pain development. Pain reductions, while statistically significant throughout the 24-h period, fell below the established MCID of 1.0 cm at most time points, which may limit the clinical significance of these findings. Our restriction to English-language publications may have introduced language bias and excluded relevant non-English studies. Furthermore, we did not systematically search gray literature sources, which may have introduced publication bias if unpublished studies with null or negative findings were missed. Finally, heterogeneity among the included studies may affect the robustness of our findings. Several factors contributed to this variability, including differences in local anesthetic type, volume, and concentration, as well as the use of adjuvants. Variations in perioperative analgesic protocols, timing of block administration (preoperative versus postoperative), and surgical approach (open versus laparoscopic hysterectomy) further contributed to heterogeneity across studies.

Future research should prioritize several key areas to advance the evidence base. First, studies should investigate whether cumulative analgesic benefits translate into meaningful clinical outcomes such as earlier mobilization, improved functional recovery, and reduced hospital stay. Second, research should focus on underreported outcomes, including patient satisfaction, quality of recovery scores, and chronic postsurgical pain development. Third, subgroup analyses examining ESPB versus TAPB across different surgical approaches (laparoscopic vs. open hysterectomy), patient populations (varying BMI, age, comorbidities), and technical variations (local anesthetic regimens) would provide valuable clinical guidance. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses evaluating both direct and indirect costs would inform clinical and policy decision-making.

Conclusion

ESPB demonstrates significant reductions in postoperative pain, opioid consumption, and PONV incidence following hysterectomy compared to TAPB. However, clinical significance assessment using MCID analysis reveals that pain reductions are most meaningful in the early postoperative period, with uncertain clinical relevance at later time points. Additional high-quality studies with standardized protocols and comprehensive outcome reporting are needed to confirm these findings and clarify long-term benefits.
