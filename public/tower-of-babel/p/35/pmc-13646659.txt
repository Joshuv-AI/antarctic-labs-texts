Enhancement of Arabidopsis Growth by Enterobacter sp. SA187 Under Elevated CO2 Is Dependent on Ethylene Signalling Activation and Primary Metabolism Reprogramming
Authors: Amina Ilyas, Caroline Mauve, Céline Oury, Stéphanie Pateyron, Christine Paysant‐Le Roux, Jean Bigeard, Michael Hodges, Axel de Zélicourt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary statementThe beneficial bacterium Enterobacter sp. SA187 increases Arabidopsis thaliana growth in an elevated CO2 atmosphere through ethylene‐dependent signalling and a deep reprogramming of plant gene expression and primary metabolism.

The Anthropocene is characterised by a significant increase in atmospheric CO2 levels, from around 280 ppm in the 17th century to more than 430 ppm today, with projections reaching 800–1000 ppm by 2100, if nothing is done to reduce emissions (Lee et al. 2021). Elevated CO2 (eCO2) is known to improve plant growth by stimulating photosynthetic CO2 assimilation and reducing photorespiration. However, the eCO2 fertilisation effect is often less important than expected, mainly because of acclimation, including reduction of plant tissue nitrogen (N) content and photosynthetic activity. This acclimation is linked to complex changes in gene expression and metabolism, including a decrease of Rubisco content and lower nitrogen uptake (Ainsworth and Long 2021; Leakey et al. 2009). Plant growth‐promoting bacteria (PGPB) represent a promising solution to reduce such negative effects. Such bacteria like the strain Enterobacter sp. SA187 (SA187), although its precise taxonomy needs to be confirmed (Andrés‐Barrao et al. 2017), has been repeatedly shown to enhance growth and stress tolerance of Arabidopsis and crops, in part by maintaining primary metabolic processes, nitrogen status and activating ethylene‐dependent pathways (Andrés‐Barrao et al. 2021; Ilyas et al. 2026). In this study, SA187 improved Arabidopsis growth in eCO2, and transcriptomics and metabolomics were used to understand the mechanisms involved.

To assess the impact of SA187 on plant growth in eCO2, Arabidopsis was cultivated in ambient CO2 (aCO2, ~420 ppm) or eCO2 (1000 ppm), with or without SA187 inoculation. Fresh weight (FW), dry weight (DW) and total carbon (C) and N contents were measured 10 days after germination. Under eCO2, noninoculated plants showed a 59% increase in FW (Figure 1A) and a 71% increase in DW (Figure S1) compared to aCO2, consistent with the CO2 fertilisation effect in C3 plants (Leakey et al. 2009). When SA187 was added, growth was further enhanced in eCO2, with a 51% increase in total FW (Figure 1A), a 43% increase in shoot FW and a 26% increase in total DW compared to mock‐inoculated plants (Figure S1). Additionally, SA187 inoculation enhanced root FW in aCO2 and eCO2 conditions resulting in an altered shoot/root ratio that was similarly affected in both conditions (Figure S1C, S1D). Furthermore, SA187 inoculation in eCO2 reduced shoot C (Figure S2) while increasing N content, leading to a lower shoot C/N ratio compared to mock eCO2 conditions and resembling that found in aCO2 (Figure 1B,C). This suggested a synergetic effect between SA187 and eCO2 on biomass accumulation.

To check if growth promotion by SA187 under eCO2 was linked to an increase in bacterial proliferation, SA187 was cultured in liquid medium in aCO2 and eCO2. Optical density measurements did not show any significant difference between the two conditions (Figure S3A), indicating that eCO2 did not directly influence SA187 growth in vitro. Moreover, bacterial colonisation of Arabidopsis was quantified, and no differences were found between CO2 conditions. SA187 mainly colonised roots, with densities of 1.26.106 CFU/mg FW and only 3.16 104 CFU/mg FW in shoots (Figure S3B). Then, the positive effects of SA187 on plant growth in eCO2 were not due to an increased bacterial density. Therefore, improvements were likely associated with intrinsic changes in plant metabolism and physiology, although potential contributions from changes in bacterial physiology cannot be ruled out.

RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in shoots and roots of Arabidopsis between the tested conditions. This revealed 4048 DEGs in shoots and 1256 DEGs in roots when comparing the effect of SA187 and eCO2, alone or in combination, to mock conditions. The clustering of shoot transcriptome data highlighted several groups of genes responding to SA187 under eCO2 (Figure 1D,E and Table S1). Cluster 2 and 7 showed similar patterns and included genes that were not affected by eCO2 alone but were strongly induced by SA187 under eCO2. These clusters were enriched in genes related to stress phytohormones, including salicylic acid, jasmonic acid and ethylene and nitrogen metabolism genes. The activation of stress‐related phytohormone pathways suggests that SA187 could enhance plant resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses under eCO2, which is particularly relevant in the context of climate change (Xu et al. 2015). Cluster 5 presented a synergistic effect (upregulated by eCO2 and further induced by SA187) with genes involved in primary metabolism, including the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) cycle and C metabolism, as well as defence responses and ethylene signalling. Cluster 3 genes linked to carbohydrate metabolism, such as starch and sucrose pathways, were highly induced by eCO2 alone but repressed by SA187 in eCO2. In roots, five clusters were identified, with cluster 5 being the largest (Figure S4 and Table S2). These genes were strongly induced by eCO2 but downregulated by SA187 under both CO2 conditions. This cluster was enriched in carbohydrate metabolism, flavonoid biosynthesis and sulfur compound metabolism genes. Cluster 2 grouped genes consistently upregulated by SA187, independently of CO2 levels and it was enriched in defence and hypoxia‐related genes.

GC‐MS metabolite profiling indicated 20 differentially accumulated metabolites in shoots in response to SA187. Nine were more abundant in SA187‐inoculated plants, including TCA cycle intermediates (pyruvate, 2‐oxoglutarate) and amino acids (asparagine, tryptophan). The less abundant metabolites included citrate and proline (Figure S5, Table S4). In roots, 18 of the 20 differentially accumulated metabolites were significantly reduced in SA187‐inoculated plants, including TCA cycle intermediates (succinate, fumarate) and amino acids (beta‐alanine, proline, glycine) (Figure S6, Table S5). Therefore, SA187 modified plant primary metabolism, including the TCA cycle and amino acid metabolism, as suggested by the transcriptomic data.

Consequently, both transcriptomics and metabolomics datasets suggest that SA187 modified primary metabolism in both shoots and roots. This was supported in shoots by the accumulation of pyruvate and 2‐oxoglutarate (Figure S5, Table S4), two key TCA cycle metabolites, and increased expression of genes involved in the TCA cycle and carbon metabolism, as shown by the transcriptome analysis (Figure 1E, cluster 5). Conversely, starch metabolism genes were downregulated (Figure 1E, cluster 3), indicating that SA187‐inoculated plants may allocate more carbon to energy production and amino acid biosynthesis rather than storage. Indeed, the TCA cycle is central for energy production and amino acid biosynthesis and its stimulation likely contributes to the observed growth enhancement. Nitrogen metabolism was also stimulated by SA187 in eCO2, as shown by the enrichment of N‐related genes and the induction of the high‐affinity nitrate transporters including NRT2.5 (Figure 1G), as seen under low N conditions (Ilyas et al. 2026). This might help explain the increased shoot N‐content, helping to maintain a balanced C/N ratio under eCO2. This is perhaps important since eCO2 often causes N‐dilution in plant tissues due to faster growth (Terrer et al. 2019). By improving N‐content, SA187 could help sustain the CO2 fertilisation effect, which is increasingly recognised to depend on adequate N‐availability (Terrer et al. 2019).

A role for ethylene signalling in SA187‐induced plant growth in eCO2 was inferred by transcriptomics. To test this, the response of the ethylene‐insensitive mutant ein2‐1 to SA187 was compared to wild‐type Col‐0 plants in eCO2. Again, SA187 increased the FW of wild‐type seedlings in eCO2. However, while no change in bacterial density were observed (Figure S3C), SA187 inoculation of ein2‐1 led to a decrease in seedling FW compared to controls, showing that without functional ethylene signalling, SA187 shifted from promoting to inhibiting growth (Figure 1F). This underlined the crucial role of ethylene in SA187 growth effects in eCO2. Moreover, SA187‐responsive genes, including the nitrate transporter NRT2.5 and the wound‐responsive gene WR3/NRT3.1 were not induced in ein2‐1 (Figure S7). This reinforced the idea that ethylene signalling was necessary for SA187 to be beneficial. Additionally, genes related to carbohydrate metabolism, SBE2.1 and FBA6, did not respond to SA187 or eCO2 in ein2‐1, suggesting that ethylene signalling regulated primary metabolic pathways in response to eCO2, independently of SA187 (Figure S7).

In summary, this work provides evidence that SA187 enhances early Arabidopsis growth in eCO2 by acting on a deep reprogramming of gene expression and primary metabolism, particularly through ethylene‐dependent pathways (Figure 1G), suggesting similar mechanisms relying on KMBA production as previously observed (Andrés‐Barrao et al. 2021). Further research is needed to clarify how beneficial microbes influence plant responses to eCO2, especially during the life cycle of the plant. Such knowledge could help develop new bio‐based solutions to mitigate the negative impacts of eCO2 on food security.
