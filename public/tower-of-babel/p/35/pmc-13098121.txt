Temporal Assessment of Pressure and Stiffness in Compression Therapy for Venous Leg Ulcers: Implications for Clinical Optimization
Authors: Juan Francisco Jiménez García, María Piedad García Ruíz, Mercedes Muñoz Conde, Ana Belén Fernández Ramírez, Francisco González Jiménez, María Gutiérrez García, José Luís Jiménez Laínez, Francisco Pedro García Fernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo measure and compare the temporal variations in sub‐bandage pressure compression systems in the Andalusian Health System (SAS). Additional objectives included assessing the relationship between pressure and healing, analysing the influence of the healthcare professional applying the bandage, and determining bandage stiffness. This prospective observational and multicentre study included 140 patients with active VLUs in Andalusia. Sub‐bandage pressures were measured at three anatomical points in the leg for 96 h, under different positions and activities. The bandage application technique was standardised through specific training provided to advanced practice nurses. The initial pressures were higher than those recommended by guidelines, but showed a notable reduction within the first 24 h, stabilising within therapeutic ranges for the remainder of the 96‐h study period. Most systems showed low dynamic and static stiffness. No significant pressure differences were found attributable to the nurses or the location of the injury. The observed pressure dynamics, initially high, with a subsequent drop and final stabilisation, suggest a high material settlement or application to compensate for the expected loss. The sustained pressure stability confirms the effectiveness of the systems over 96 h.

Introduction

The use of compression systems in the treatment of venous ulcers (VUs) and other pathologies related to chronic venous insufficiency has been extensively investigated in both clinical and biomechanical research [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Effective compression therapy (CT) not only improves venous circulation, but also promotes the reduction of edema [6, 7, 8], promotes healing, and prevents recurrences [1, 4]. However, despite its widespread use, there are still questions about how the pressure applied varies over time, depending on the type of CT system used [1, 9, 10], and how these variations influence therapeutic effectiveness [11].

One of the fundamental aspects in evaluating these systems is understanding the changes in the pressure generated during their use [3]. The pressure exerted by the different CT systems is not static; it evolves over time due to factors such as material stiffness, the application technique, patient mobility and the biomechanical properties of the system itself [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. The characterisation of these pressure changes is essential to optimise clinical practice, guarantee adequate therapeutic pressure and be able to prevent risks associated with excessive or insufficient pressures. In this line, the problem has been addressed from different perspectives. Previous studies have analysed pressure variations in compression systems, highlighting the relevance of the application technique and the quality of the materials [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

The study of how pressure changes over time with different compression systems has shown that material properties, such as rigidity and elasticity, have a strong influence on the stability of applied pressure. Maintaining stable pressures is essential to ensure a sustained compressive effect and reduce the risk of therapeutic ineffectiveness or related clinical complications [2]. For CT to reach its maximum effectiveness, it must deliver an adequate and sustained interface pressure over time. Continuous, individualised CT, adjusted to the specific needs of each patient and applied with precisely controlled pressure, has been shown to significantly improve venous circulation, as well as microcirculation, positively influencing the reduction of venous stasis and the promotion of a favourable hemodynamic environment [18, 22].

Research indicates that higher stiffness in compression materials, quantified by the static stiffness index (SSI) and the dynamic stiffness index (DSI), is associated with higher working pressures during muscle contraction, which enhances the reduction of venous hypertension during walking and contributes to a significant improvement in venous function [12, 23, 24]. In this context, the classification of bandages not only takes into account their extensibility (ability to stretch) and elasticity (ability to recover their original length), distinguishing between short and long elasticity bandages, but also considers stiffness as a determining property in vivo, understood as the resistance of the material to changes in muscle volume, especially at the calf level [23, 25].

The right choice of materials, their combination and the correct application technique are essential aspects to ensure a uniform and sustained pressure over time. The rigidity of the bandage is a determining factor, since its variability can have a direct impact on the evolution of healing, while the professional's precision in positioning has a significant influence on the effectiveness and uniformity of CT [26, 27]. The training and experience of the healthcare professional play a decisive role in the quality of the dressing placement and in the final pressure reached, a critical aspect, since improper application can not only decrease the effectiveness of the compression system, but also cause iatrogenic complications [6, 10, 13, 28].

Given the great diversity of compression systems available and the uncertainty about whether they are all equally effective in the treatment of VUs, and if they are effective, it remains to be determined which method offers greater therapeutic efficacy. It is also a critical question to establish precisely what pressure these systems actually exert during use, whether this pressure remains stable over time and whether it is within the ranges considered optimal from a therapeutic point of view. Answering these questions is essential to move towards more standardised, safe, and personalised protocols in the management of chronic venous insufficiency and VLUs, allowing therapeutic results to be optimised.

Methodology

Study Design

This prospective observational and multicentre study included, was carried out across four provinces of Andalusia, involving six Health Districts (DS) and/or Health Management Areas (AGS): (DS Almería, DS Metropolitano de Granada, DS Jaén‐Jaén Sur, AGS Norte de Jaén, AGS Serranía de Málaga and AGS Este de Málaga Axarquía). This study is part of a broader project evaluating the clinical efficacy of different types of venous compression systems carried out by Advanced Practice Nurses in Complex Chronic Wounds (EPA‐HCC) in Andalusia (Spain).

Study Population

The target population consisted of patients with active VLUs referred to the EPA‐HCC during the study period, which ran from January 2024 to July 2025.

Sample

A conceptual sample was used. All patients referred to the EPA‐HCC during the study period and who met the eligibility criteria were included until the planned sample size was reached.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

People with VLUs, whose ankle–brachial index (ABI) was between 0.9 and 1.3, with exudate and scarring that would allow two weekly cures, ambulant and over 18 years of age belonging to the same DS and/or AGS, were included. Patients with other types of wounds, with an inability to give informed consent, at the end of life, and under 18 years of age were excluded.

Sample Size

To achieve an accuracy of 5% for estimating a proportion using a bilateral 95% normal asymptotic confidence interval, assuming a 10% rate of pressure loss at 96 h based on current scientific literature data [29, 30, 31], a sample of 139 patients was required. Participants who could not complete follow‐up were replaced and not considered dropouts.

Data Collection Methods and Instruments

Prior to the start of the study, the collaborating companies (Farmaban, Urgo, Medi, and Essity) were asked to provide compression materials and specific training to standardise techniques and minimise bias. All companies except Essity contributed to the training, accreditation and certification of participating professionals. Although Essity did not provide training or materials, its system was included to maintain the study's independence, as no external funding was received. Mölnlycke supplied the tubular Tubifast bandages (7.5 and 10.5 cm × 10 m), which were used under all systems. Prior tests confirmed that this tubular mesh did not alter the final compression results.

Pressure monitoring was performed using the Tight Alright system (Feeltect Limited, Ireland), a Class I, CE‐marked medical device (Figure 1). This system consists of a portable rechargeable transmitter, a sensor with disposable adhesive sleeves and associated software installed on the EPA‐HCC iPads. different sized sensors were used to measure, according to the patient's size, at three points on the leg (B, B1 and C) to obtain a full compression profile. The sensors were placed on Tubifast mesh (7.5 year 10.5 cm × 10 m), avoiding direct contact with the skin and any possible injuries.

Procedure

Once the dressing was complete, the procedure consisted of applying the tubular bandage (7.5 and 10.5 cm × 10 m) based on the patient's leg circumference, positioning the sensors, applying the compression system, which uses visual tension or stretch indicators according to the manufacturer's instructions, with the leg in a physiological position, and recording the pressures in real time.

For the low‐elasticity CPK Compress system, a modified technique based on Fisher was used. With the patient supine and the ankle dorsiflexed to 30°, after placing the same tubular mesh (from the root of the fingers to the popliteal hollow), the CPK Compress 8 cm × 7 m bandage was applied in a herringbone pattern with 50% stretch and overlap. A second CPK Compress 10 cm × 7 m bandage was then added, starting on the malleoli and ending under the tibial tuberosity, using the same technique.

The equipment was tested before each use and measurements were made at rest and activity to assess the patient's functional response.

Each EPA‐HCC applied local wound care protocols based on the TIMERS framework (Tissue, Infection/Inflammation, Moisture, Epithelialization, Repair/Regeneration and Support), adapting interventions to the baseline healing phase. This approach allowed for individualised care, ensuring the effectiveness of CT and patient safety.

A specific questionnaire was designed to collect all the variables for the study.

Data Analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed for each variable. Qualitative variables were summarised using frequency and percentages, while quantitative variables were described with means and standard deviations, assuming normal distribution. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with Gustav Liliefors' correction.

Parametric tests (Student's t‐test and analysis of variance [ANOVA]) were used to compare means. The qualitative variables were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test when applicable.

Pressure changes were calculated as the percentage decrease from baseline (time 0) to 24, 48, 72 and 96 h.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Coordinating Committee on Research Ethics. The risk/benefit ratio was carefully considered, avoiding unnecessary inconvenience to patients, adapting data collection to their needs, preferences, and prioritising their well‐being. Participants were informed of their right to decline any questions.

Before enrolment, all participants received an information leaflet about the study that was also explained verbally before signing the informed consent. They were assured that participation was voluntary, that declining would not affect the quality of their care, and that confidentiality would be maintained. They could withdraw at any time in accordance with Organic Law 3/2018 on the Protection of Personal Data, and all related ARCO rights were upheld.

Results

A total of 140 participants were included in the study, of whom 59.3% were women and 40.7% were men. The sample was relatively homogeneous in age, with a mean of 71 years (95% CI 69–73 years). The participants had a high mean body weight of 93 kg (95% CI 88–97 kg) and an average height of 1.67 m (95% CI 1.65–1.69 m). The calculated mean body mass index (BMI) was 32.79 (95% CI 31.56–34.01).

Weight of patients included in the study.

As shown, none of the patients had a normal weight. Most presented Grade 1 obesity, followed by Grade 2 and 3 obesities. This parameter was analysed in relation to sociodemographic variables. No significant differences were observed by sex (p = 0.43). However, differences were found by age. Patients with Grade 3 and Grade 2 obesity were younger (62 and 67 years, respectively) than those with overweight or Grade 1 obesity (mean age of 74 years) (p < 0.001).

Of the participants, 97.2% were treated in a primary care setting, 68.5% at health centres and the rest at home. The remaining 2.9% were institutionalised in social and healthcare residences.

All patients underwent an ABI assessment before starting the bandaging process, with a mean ABI of 1.05 (95% CI 1.03–1.06), indicating a highly homogeneous profile. Across all 140 participants, ABI values ranged from 0.9 to 1.3.

Regarding lesion characteristics, 80.0% of patients had a single VLU, 12.9% had two, and 7.1% had three lesions. The mean lesion size was 5.45 cm2 (95% CI 4.16–6.74 cm2). Lesion severity was assessed using the RESVECH 2.0 Wound Healing Assessment Scale (which evaluates surface area, depth/affected tissues, wound borders, tissue type in the wound bed, exudate, and signs of infection or inflammation). The mean severity score was 8.2 points (95% CI 7.6–8.7) on a scale ranging from 0 to 35, indicating that all patients had lesions of similar severity.

Stiffness Indices

Another relevant parameter in CT is the dynamic and static stiffness indices (Figure 5). The DSI is defined as the difference, in mmHg, between the pressure recorded in the supine position (rest) and the maximum pressure detected after six dorsiflexion, reflecting pressure variation during movement. On the other hand, the SSI corresponds to the pressure difference, also in mmHg, between the supine position and standing, reflecting pressure variation between rest and upright posture.

Evolution of dynamic (A) and static (B) stiffness indices over time with confidence interval of 95%.

Dynamics (A): All DSI values at all three sensor positions and time points were well below 20 mmHg, starting from baseline values of approximately 2–2.5 mmHg and showing a progressive decline. Note how dynamic stiffness (the ability to massage while walking) drops to almost zero on Day 4 (96 h). This confirms that the bandage loses its primary “massage” property over time.

Static (B): All SSI values remained below 10 mmHg, starting from baseline between 5.2 and 8.7 mmHg and further decreasing over time. It remains somewhat better, but also decreases. Interestingly, on the upper sensor, it rises slightly at 24 h (perhaps due to edema or readjustment) and then drops sharply.

Values well below the suggested thresholds corroborate that the material or technique used guarantees low stiffness. This stiffness is appropriate for patients requiring minimal restraint. The downward trend of both indices also suggests a rapid adaptation and possible need for readjustment or replacement of the bandages depending on the clinical course.

There are three factors that may influence the recorded pressures and act as potential confounding variables: the type of bandage used, the healthcare professional applying the bandage, and the location of the sensors (left or right lower limb) depending on the site of the lesion.

Regarding the CT systems, all six types of products were analysed on the day of application and every time a measurement was taken, and no statistically significant differences between systems were found.

By Day 4, significant differences were observed at Sensors 1 and 2 in the supine position, specifically with Urgo K2 (Sensors 1 and 2) and with Circaid Juxtalite (Sensor 2), with pressures higher than the rest of the systems exceeding 40 mmHg. At Sensor 3, pressures remained more homogeneous, with no significant differences between systems. A similar pattern was observed after six repetitions of dorsiflexion, as well as in the sitting and standing positions: all systems showed pressures above 40 mmHg at application, and by Day 4, Urgo K2 (Sensors 1 and 2) and Circaid Juxtalite (Sensor 2) continued to record significantly higher pressures. This supports the hypothesis that all systems initially generate pressures above 40 mmHg, likely to compensate for the expected pressure loss over the following days.

Analysis of interface pressure across activities confirms a significant loss of therapeutic compression after 96 h (p < 0.05). While typical short‐stretch bandages (CPK) showed pressure drops exceeding 30% in the standing position, the dual‐component system (Urgo K2) demonstrated superior pressure retention, maintaining therapeutic levels (> 40 mmHg) even at the distal sensor. This suggests that for patients requiring prolonged wear times without re‐application, multicomponent systems offer a more stable hemodynamic profile compared to traditional cohesive bandages.

Figure 7 presents the DSI and SSI for the different compression systems on Day 0 and Day 4.

Dynamic (DSI) and static stiffness index (SSI) per system on Day 0 and Day 4.

The longitudinal analysis of stiffness indices over 96 h reveals a striking heterogeneity in system performance, confirmed by statistical significance (p < 0.001 for SSI at the calf level).

Regarding Static Stiffness (SSI), while adjustable Velcro devices (Circaid) and multicomponent systems (Urgo K2) maintained a consistent stiffness profile (suggesting reliable containment), the CPK Compress 2 system defied the typical trend of stress relaxation. Instead of loosening, it exhibited a paradoxical and sharp increase in rigidity at the calf (sensor B), rising from an initial SSI of 8.13 to 13.65 by Day 4. This behaviour suggests a ‘material compaction’ or interlocking phenomenon between bandage layers over time, potentially creating a tourniquet‐like effect that, while high in containment, differs significantly from its initial application properties.

Conversely, the DSI analysis uncovered a functional collapse in the Jobst Compri2 system at the distal level. By Day 4, the DSI plummeted to negative values (−1.50), a finding that is statistically distinct (p = 0.001) from the resilience observed in Circaid and Urgo K2. A negative DSI is physically indicative of bandage slippage or complete loss of contact during muscle contraction, confirming that the system had ceased to provide any effective hemodynamic ‘massage’ effect well before the 96‐h endpoint.

Influence of Operator and Sensor Placement

The effect of the person applying the bandage was analysed using ANOVA. No significant differences were found when compression systems were placed in the supine position on Day 0 or at subsequent daily measurements in all positions (p > 0,05). A slight, non‐significant increase was observed from one of the researchers in Sensor 3 (supine: p = 0.10; sitting: p = 0.06; walking: p = 0.11 on Day 0). No significant differences were found on any day or in any position when bandages were applied by the advanced practice nurse.

Similarly, pressure differences related to sensor placement on the left or right lower limb were analysed using Student's T, showing no statistically significant differences across days or positions (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study investigated how the pressure applied by different compression systems varies over time in patients with active VLUs. It was designed as a prospective, observational, multicentre study conducted in real clinical settings by EPA‐HCC across six health districts in Andalusia. The standardisation of the application technique was ensured through specific training and the provision of materials by manufacturers to guarantee consistency in the initial pressure.

Our findings indicate that, although the initial pressure of the bandages was considerably higher than the values recommended by the manufacturers, these pressures dropped significantly during the first 24 h. After this initial reduction, the pressure stabilised and remained within adequate therapeutic ranges throughout the four‐day follow‐up period. In general, the compression systems evaluated proved to be of low dynamic and static stiffness. However, some specific systems were capable of maintaining statistically significant higher pressures by the end of the study period. This suggests a dynamic behaviour inherent to these compression systems.

This initial drop could be interpreted as a settling of the compressive material in response to the limb morphology and early activity of the patient, and it could reflect an intentionally higher initial application pressure by manufacturers to ensure that the pressure remains within the desired therapeutic range over time, compensating for the expected loss of tension. The subsequent stability of pressure over subsequent days is a crucial indicator of the sustained effectiveness of these systems, which is critical for oedema reduction, promotion of healing and venous return.

Another relevant observation for clinical practice is that no significant differences were observed in the pressures applied by different researchers, despite some systems lacking pressure indicators. Many studies have previously highlighted the importance of the professional's training and experience, the tension exerted, the number of layers, and the final pressure reached [3, 34] in CT application. Our study found that the standardised training received by the EPA‐HCC professionals was effective in minimising variability and ensuring consistency of application. This could be a positive indication that rigorous training programs are very valuable in CT [9, 12, 19].

In terms of stiffness, all bandages analysed were classified as systems of low dynamic (DSI < 20 mmHg) and static stiffness (SSI < 10 mmHg), offering limited resistance to changes in muscle volume and gravitational forces. This feature makes them suitable for situations where minimal restriction is required or where patient comfort is a priority. None of the bandages had a mean DSI value above 20 mmHg, showcasing their low rigidity classification. However, some systems, such as CPK Compress and Jobst Compri2, initially showed higher SSI values, comparable to high rigidity systems, but they then decay over time. This variability in biomechanical properties between the different products could influence their initial compressive behaviour and the patient's hemodynamic response.

Extensive research on compression systems for VLUs underscores the need to understand pressure variation over time [1, 6, 35]. The study results align with extensive prior research showing that the pressure exerted by compression systems tends to decrease over time [1, 3]. The need to maintain adequate and sustained interface pressure is a widely accepted principle to maximise therapeutic efficacy, reduce edema, and promote healing [36]. Our findings confirm that despite the initial drop, these systems manage to maintain pressures within therapeutic ranges for at least 96 h.

The finding that the initial pressures of bandages are higher than those declared by manufacturers is a recurrent concern in the literature [6, 37]. While high compression levels are often advocated for maximising hemodynamic improvement, our data revealed interface pressures significantly exceeding the standard consensus of 40 mmHg, reaching > 50 mmHg even in the supine position (Day 0). Physiologically, sustained pressures exceeding capillary perfusion pressure pose a risk of cutaneous ischemia and iatrogenic pressure ulcers, particularly during recumbency. Furthermore, pressures significantly surpassing the 40 mmHg ‘Gold Standard’ have been associated with reduced patient tolerance and compliance due to pain [38]. Therefore, the excessively high initial pressures observed in some systems in this study, while hemodynamically potent, may be counterproductive regarding safety and long‐term adherence, underscoring the need for strict pressure monitoring to avoid the risks of ‘overcompression’ [38].

This observation raises questions about the accuracy of current product information and suggests a need to adjust the application guidelines in clinical practice to avoid excessive initial compression.

Interestingly, while increased stiffness is associated with elevated working pressures during muscle contraction, which potentiates the reduction of venous hypertension during walking and contributes to a significant improvement in venous function [1], most of the bandages analysed in our study were classified as low stiffness. This divergence could be due to the intrinsic properties of the materials [9, 12, 15, 28, 39, 40] or to the standardised application technique used in the study [2, 3, 10, 17, 26, 27, 41, 42]. However, the identification of some systems that do show an initially higher SSI suggests that there may be options within the low‐stiffness category that temporarily behave more like inelastic bandages, offering greater stiffness at the time of application.

This study provides robust empirical evidence to optimise management protocols for chronic venous insufficiency and VLUs [6, 13, 22]. Demonstrating that the pressures stabilise within therapeutic ranges for 4 days has direct implications for clinical practice, suggesting that, for many patients, dressing changes every 96 h may be adequate to maintain effective compression and reduce the burden of care [23, 43].

From a scientific perspective, this study advances in the characterisation of temporal pressure changes and the biomechanical properties of compression systems, crucial information for selecting materials tailored to individual patients' pathophysiology and activity levels to optimise clinical interventions [9, 16, 19, 25, 44]. The predominance of low‐stiffness compression systems highlights the need for further research exploring the correlation between stiffness levels and therapeutic effectiveness in different stages of venous pathology [9, 40]. From a professional development perspective, this study underscores the value of standardised training. The demonstrated consistency of bandage application across multiple health districts is a testament to the importance of education and continuing professional development in nursing [27, 35, 45]. This will not only improve the quality of patient care but also promote equity in access to effective treatments and can serve as a model for standardising other clinical procedures [23, 28, 35].

Limitations

The study's multicentre cohort design without a control group may limit its ability for establishing causality and generalising the findings. The sample size (n = 140) may not fully represent the entire Andalusian population. Moreover, most systems tested were low‐rigidity; including inelastic compression systems or more rigid systems would have provided a more comprehensive comparison. Finally, as most applications were performed by highly trained professionals, the findings may not fully reflect real‐world variability. Future studies should examine outcomes when bandages are applied by professionals with varying levels of training.

Future research should focus on how sustained pressure influences healing and recurrence, comparing different systems (including high‐stiffness or inelastic ones) using controlled trials. Another line of future research should be aimed at measuring the functional stiffness index, which captures cumulative static stiffness over time, allowing us to measure the pressure that the bandage exerts on the leg during movements and thus see how it performs under real‐life conditions.

Conclusions

The compression systems evaluated showed dynamic behaviour, with an initial interface pressure drop of 17%–23% within the first 24 h, followed by stable therapeutic pressure levels maintained up to 96 h. This highlights the importance of continuous pressure monitoring to enable timely adjustments or dressing changes, ensuring optimal pressure is maintained throughout CT and reducing the risk of complications.

The bandages analysed were mostly classified as low rigidity systems, both dynamic (DSI < 20 mmHg) and static (SSI < 10 mmHg). The biomechanical variability between products influences their initial compressive behaviour and should be considered when selecting products.

A crucial finding for clinical practice is that standardised training, expertise, and certification of EPA‐HCCs led to consistent application techniques. This highlights the importance of rigorous training programs to ensure the uniformity and efficacy of CT.

Finally, the finding that initial pressures of the bandages may be higher than those declared by the manufacturers suggests the need to review and potentially adjust clinical application guidelines to avoid excessive initial compression. However, the ability of the systems evaluated to maintain adequate therapeutic pressures for 96 h suggests that, in many patients, a frequency of dressing change every 4 days could be sufficient to maintain effective compression, which could reduce care workload while maintaining therapeutic efficacy.
