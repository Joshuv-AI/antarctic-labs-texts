INTRODUCTION

Seizures are common and clinically complex neurological events ranging from brief lapses in awareness to full‐body convulsions. 1 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 1 in 10 individuals will experience at least one seizure during their lifetime. 2 While a single seizure may be provoked by an acute event, such as hemorrhage, hypoglycemia, or alcohol withdrawal, it can also be the first sign of epilepsy, a chronic disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures. 3 , 4 As of 2015, approximately 3 million U.S. adults were living with epilepsy. 5

Seizures that do not meet the criteria for epilepsy or nonepileptic (functional) seizures, including febrile, post‐traumatic, and unspecified seizures, are often coded and documented in health records as unclassified. 6 These episodes often lack a clear initial etiology or classification but prompt similar clinical evaluations, including imaging, laboratory workups, and specialist consultations. 7 Collectively, seizure‐related healthcare spending in the U.S. exceeds $44 billion annually, but the extent to which unclassified seizures contribute to this burden remains unclear. 8

Compared to epilepsy, which is typically managed through outpatient care with established diagnostic and treatment pathways, individuals with unclassified seizures are more likely to seek care in emergency or acute care settings and may lack access to consistent follow‐up or neurology care. 9 , 10 This fragmented and episodic care, along with diagnostic uncertainty, can lead to inefficient healthcare use, delays in appropriate treatment, and unmet clinical needs. These gaps may also increase vulnerability to stigma and negatively affect their quality of life, further compounding the overall burden on both patients and the healthcare system.

Despite the clinical prevalence and potential psychosocial consequences, unclassified seizures as a collective diagnostic group have received limited attention in health services research. The objective of this study is to compare healthcare utilization and spending between U.S. adults with epilepsy and those with unclassified seizures, using nationally representative data. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to directly compare these diagnostic groups using nationally representative data, including the COVID‐19 pandemic period.

This study aims to systematically characterize disparities in healthcare use and spending between individuals with epilepsy and those with unclassified seizures. By contributing to the limited body of research on this collective diagnostic group, we hope to inform future efforts to improve care coordination and equitable resource allocation in seizure‐related care. These insights are critical to understanding real‐world challenges in managing epilepsy and related conditions, particularly among patients whose diagnosis remains uncertain.

METHODS

The Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) is a nationally representative survey of the civilian, non‐institutionalized U.S. population that collects detailed, self‐reported information on health status, healthcare utilization, insurance coverage, and associated costs over a 2‐year period. 11 We pooled MEPS data from 2016 to 2022 and analyzed responses from 1034 U.S. adults (aged 18 years and older) diagnosed with epilepsy or unclassified seizures, each with a positive person‐level survey weight. Using the unique person identifier, we linked the MEPS Household Component medical conditions file to the full‐year consolidated file. For individuals who participated across two calendar years, each year's data were treated as an independent observation.

As this study used publicly available, de‐identified data, it was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval. The STROBE Reporting Guidelines were followed (Table S1).

Identification of epilepsy and seizure cases

In the MEPS, medical conditions are reported by respondents during interviews and then professionally coded into the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10). In the present study, we classified individuals based solely on ICD‐10 codes, not the self‐reported condition labels. To maintain confidentiality, the MEPS only reports the three‐character category level, collapsing subcodes into the main category (e.g., G40.xx becomes G40). 12

To ensure coding consistency, we restricted our analysis to 2016 onward, following the nationwide transition from ICD Ninth Edition (ICD‐9) to ICD‐10 in late 2015. Epilepsy cases were identified using ICD‐10 code G40, while unclassified seizure cases were identified using R56 (“Convulsions not elsewhere classified”), a code commonly used in acute or undifferentiated contexts. 6 , 13 The R56 code includes febrile convulsions, post‐traumatic seizures, and other unspecified seizure events. We refer individuals coded with R56 as having “unclassified seizures” to reflect diagnostic uncertainty and distinguish them from epilepsy (G40), dissociative seizures (F44.5), and neonatal seizures (P90). 6

Respondents with both G40 and R56 codes (n = 51, 3.9%) were categorized under epilepsy (ICD‐10 G40) due to the greater diagnostic specificity and clinical relevance of the code. Both G40 and R56 fall under the same Clinical Classification Software Refined (CCSR) category, NVS009 (“Epilepsy; Convulsions”), reflecting the interchangeable use of the terms “seizure” and “convulsion” in administrative coding. 14

Variables of interest

The primary independent variable was the diagnosis of epilepsy or unclassified seizures. Outcomes included healthcare utilization and expenditure, both overall and by service category: office‐based provider care, outpatient visits, acute inpatient care, emergency department visits, prescription medication fills, antiseizure medication (ASM) use, and home health and other services. The MEPS prescription medications file was filtered to identify ASMs based on specific drug names and therapeutic classes. These outcomes reflect common points of contact with the healthcare system for individuals with seizures and may help identify care gaps and inefficiencies. Expenditure values were adjusted to 2023‐dollar values using the medical care component of the Consumer Price Index from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. 15

Covariates included age, sex, race and ethnicity, U.S. Census region, comorbidity burden, marital status, education level, family income, employment status, insurance coverage, and the COVID‐19 pandemic period. To capture underlying health status, we calculated the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) using a claims‐based algorithm adapted from Quan et al. 16 Each condition was weighted based on its mortality risk, and scores were summed to generate a total CCI score. 17 We then grouped comorbidity burden as none (CCI = 0), mild (CCI = 1–2), moderate (CCI = 3–4), or severe (CCI ≥5), following prior literature. 18 To account for the pandemic, we included an indicator for pandemic (2020–2022) versus pre‐pandemic (2016–2019). 19

Statistical analysis

Respondents in the epilepsy cohort were matched allowing replacement, as the unit of analysis was person‐year. Respondents observed in two calendar years contributed two records. Propensity scores were estimated via logistic regression, incorporating all previously described covariate, and then we performed nearest neighbor matching at the record level. Matching was performed to reduce confounding and improve comparability between cohorts. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using chi‐square (χ 2) tests.

Differences in healthcare utilization (e.g., hospital visits, medical office‐based care events, prescription fills) were examined using zero‐inflated negative binomial regression models. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were reported to quantify relative event rates between groups. Differences in healthcare expenditures were evaluated using a two‐part model, which accounted for the typical right‐skewed distribution of expenditure data. The first part used logistic regression to estimate the probability of incurring any expenditure. The second part used a generalized linear model (GLM) with a Gamma distribution and log‐link to estimate costs among those with nonzero expenditures. Cost ratios were used to quantify spending differences. Adjusted means and group differences were estimated using recycled predictions, holding covariates constant while varying the independent variable. 20 These marginal effects provide interpretable estimates of the healthcare burden associated with each condition.

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design using person‐level weights provided by the MEPS. Analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 4.4.1, R Core Team) and Stata (version 17.0, StataCorp LLC), and a p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Population characteristics

The initial data pool included 1034 adults with epilepsy or unclassified seizures, representing approximately 11 million U.S. adults (4.5 million with epilepsy; 6.6 million with unclassified seizures) (Table 1). Across both groups, respondents were generally middle‐aged, female, non‐Hispanic White, low‐income, and unemployed. Compared to those with epilepsy, individuals with unclassified seizures were more often publicly insured (n = 388 (52%) vs. n = 189 (42%); p < 0.001) and married (n = 222 (38%) vs. n = 124 (34%); p < 0.001), whereas adults with epilepsy were more likely to be privately insured (n = 169 (52%) vs. n = 250 (47%); p < 0.001) and never married (n = 162 (47%) vs. n = 206 (31%); p < 0.001).

After 1:1 matching, the analytical sample comprised 824 adults, representing 9.3 million people nationally. Post‐matching covariate balance improved, although some differences remained in age, race/ethnicity, and comorbidity burden (Table 1).

Unadjusted trends in healthcare utilization and expenditure

From 2016 to 2022, trends in healthcare utilization and spending differed by diagnosis. Among individuals with unclassified seizures, utilization increased initially, plateaued until 2020, and declined thereafter, coinciding with the COVID‐19 pandemic (Figure 1A). However, stratified analysis revealed a rise in acute care utilization, specifically emergency department and inpatient visits, during the pandemic period, despite the overall decline (Figure S1). Spending in this group dropped sharply from 2016 to 2017, increased steadily through 2020, and then steadied (Figure 1B). Inpatient and emergency department expenditure increased during the pandemic, diverging from the steady overall spending trend (Figure S2).

Expenditure remained relatively stable before increasing in 2021, coinciding with the peak of the pandemic (Figure 1B). Adjusted healthcare utilization and expenditure

Adjusted models revealed that individuals with unclassified seizures had significantly higher healthcare utilization, with a 38% increase in total encounters (IRR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.11–1.72) compared to those with epilepsy, corresponding to an average of approximately 13 additional visits per person (95% CI: 4.1–23) (Table 2). While overall healthcare spending was higher among individuals with unclassified seizures, the cost difference was not statistically significant (cost ratio = 1.06; 95% CI: 0.861–1.31), with an estimated average difference of $1362 per person (95% CI: −$3441 to $6166) (Table 2).

By service category, individuals with unclassified seizures had 41% more inpatient visits (IRR = 1.41; 95% CI: 1.05–1.88) and 37% more emergency department visits (IRR = 1.37; 95% CI: 1.11–1.70), both translating to modest but significant increase in use (Table 2). Use of home health and other services was more than four times higher among this group (IRR = 4.86; 95% CI: 2.87–8.22), although the associated costs were not significantly different between the two cohorts. Despite higher overall prescription use, the unclassified seizure group had 12% lower ASM fills (IRR = 0.876; 95% CI: 0.856–0.896) and 43% lower spending (cost ratio = 0.570; 95% CI: 0.510–0.637), averaging $26 less per person than those with epilepsy (95% CI: −$46 to −$6) (Table 2).

Among adults with unclassified seizures, those unemployed all year had 37 more total healthcare encounters (95% CI: 25–48) and $6384 higher total spending (95% CI: $39–$12 729) compared to those employed all year (Table 3). In addition, insurance coverage was also associated with significantly higher expenditures. Public insurance coverage corresponded to $16 741 higher average spending (95% CI: $9558–$23 924) and private insurance coverage with $13 715 more (95% CI: $6657–$20 774), relative to being uninsured (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the healthcare burden associated with epilepsy and unclassified seizures using a nationally representative sample of community‐dwelling U.S. adults. We found that approximately 4.5 million adults had a diagnosis of epilepsy, while 6.6 million reported unclassified seizures. The annual direct healthcare expenditures for these populations were substantial, totaling $21.3 billion (95% CI: $15.3 billion–$27.3 billion) for epilepsy and $38.9 billion (95% CI: $32 billion–$45.6 billion) for unclassified seizures. A previous analysis using 2010 to 2018 data reported expenditures of $14.8 billion on epilepsy‐related healthcare and $44.7 billion on seizure‐related healthcare (inflated to 2023 USD). 8 Our findings extend these prior estimates by leveraging more recent data, adjusting for socioeconomic factors such as income, and differentiating between epilepsy and unclassified seizures. While few studies have examined healthcare trends specifically for individuals with unclassified seizures, our unadjusted per‐person epilepsy spending estimate ($20 556) aligns with previous estimates ranging from $2648 to $25 573 (inflated to 2023 USD). 21 , 22

A key finding of this study is the disproportionately higher healthcare burden among adults with an unclassified seizure diagnosis. Nonspecific or ambiguous seizure presentations may lead to frequent, low‐value care and delays in appropriate treatment. The elevated use of acute care services, particularly hospital‐based care, may reflect inefficiencies and fragmented care rather than more intensive or effective management, thereby contributing to higher overall costs. 23 The significantly greater number of inpatient admissions and emergency department visits in the unclassified seizure group underscores the acute and uncertain nature of initial seizure presentations. However, persistent reliance on emergency and inpatient care, rather than timely outpatient follow‐up, could indicate gaps in care coordination, diagnostic delays, or limited access to neurology services. Pandemic‐related disruptions may have further intensified this burden; COVID‐19 has been hypothesized to trigger seizures even in individuals without preexisting epilepsy, leading to longer hospitalizations and increased mortality risk. 24 Inaccurate patient or caregiver self‐report, especially in vulnerable populations, can further compromise treatment decisions and contribute to suboptimal care and excess healthcare use. 25

Medication use patterns further illuminate differences in management. While ASMs are commonly prescribed following first seizures, particularly when the recurrence risk is high, clinical guidelines recommend judicious initiation that balances benefit against potential harm. 26 , 27 In line with expectations, epilepsy patients demonstrated greater ASM use and spending, reflecting their central role in epilepsy management. By contrast, lower ASM use in the unclassified seizure group may reflect more cautious prescribing in the absence of diagnostic certainty. This group is heterogenous and likely includes individuals with provoked seizures, single events, or nonepileptic episodes, for whom long‐term ASM use is often inappropriate. Delayed diagnosis and limited follow‐up may also contribute to more conservative prescribing patterns. From 2016 to 2022, total annual ASM spending reached nearly $2 billion in the epilepsy cohort and $744 million in the unclassified seizure group. Rising ASM costs, driven by branded product prices and possibly polypharmacy in drug‐resistant epilepsy, have contributed to a doubling of median per‐person spending between 2006 and 2021, with an additional 24% rise during the pandemic. 28 , 29 We did not assess temporal trends in specific ASM prices in the present work, which may have influenced observed spending patterns. Future research could examine price trajectories of individual ASMs over time to better understand their contribution to cost trends.

Geographic disparities also influenced observed utilization and spending for both diagnostic groups. A large proportion of seizure‐affected adults resided in the South, often referred to as the “Epilepsy Belt” due to high disease burden and low neurologist density. 30 Limited access to specialty care in rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged regions has been linked to lower rates of epilepsy specialist visits, greater reliance on emergency departments, and suboptimal diagnostic practices. 31 These factors may delay diagnosis, hinder seizure control, and contribute to avoidable acute healthcare utilization rather than coordinated outpatient management.

Socioeconomic vulnerabilities were also common across both groups. More than half of the individuals reported family income below 200% of the federal poverty level, and unemployment was prevalent. These findings reinforce prior evidence that seizures are disabling and socially disruptive. 32 For individuals with unclassified seizures, the absence of a formal diagnosis may further limit access to workplace accommodations and seizure safety planning. The resulting loss of income and insurance coverage may exacerbate financial vulnerability and lead to delays in routine care, potentially leading to episodic, high‐cost healthcare encounters, such as emergency department visits or hospitalizations. 33 Unemployed individuals with unclassified seizures had significantly more healthcare encounters and higher spending than their employed counterparts, likely reflecting a complex relationship between illness severity, unemployment, and insurance access. However, many individuals were covered by public insurance, which may have enabled continued care, despite the high rates of joblessness. These findings underscore the intertwined roles of health status, disability, and insurance in shaping utilization patterns.

These challenges, including socioeconomic barriers, diagnostic uncertainty, and inconsistent access to neurological care and highlight the need for more integrated, patient‐centered seizure care models. Our findings emphasize how diagnostic clarity and social determinants jointly influence outcomes and costs for seizure‐affected adults. Reducing the financial and clinical burden of seizure‐related conditions will require scalable strategies that improve timely diagnosis, continuity of care, and equitable access to essential services. Multidisciplinary care pathways have shown promise. In Zambia, a national epilepsy program delivered effective care for under $25 per patient per year ($33 in 2023 USD), by training primary care providers and implementing the World Health Organization (WHO)‐recommended guidelines, offering a template for high‐value, low‐cost care in resource‐limited settings. 34 Similar approaches to standardizing outpatient care and improving access could benefit underserved populations in the U.S. Tailoring these models to local healthcare may reduce preventable utilization and improve outcomes for individuals with seizure‐related disorders.

Limitations

Interpretation of the study findings should consider some limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design of the publicly available MEPS dataset restricts the ability to draw causal inferences and limits longitudinal tracking. Outcomes were measured from the date of the first qualifying diagnosis code during the study period. As a result, estimates may not fully capture patients' clinical histories or chronic disease burden. The dataset captures annual healthcare spending rather than lifetime costs, which may underrepresent the long‐term economic burden of a chronic condition like epilepsy. Despite matching, residual confounding may persist. Post‐match differences in age, race/ethnicity, and comorbidity burden suggest potential unmeasured differences in clinical complexity. For instance, the older average age in the unclassified seizure group may reflect greater comorbidity burden, which could partially explain their higher healthcare utilization.

The MEPS dataset lacks detailed clinical information, such as seizure type, frequency, severity, and treatment response, which limits the ability to fully characterize patients' healthcare needs. 35 Additionally, because MEPS relies on self‐reported data and survey‐linked provider records, there is a risk of recall bias, nonresponse, and social desirability bias. Individuals diagnosed in outpatient settings who do not report epilepsy or whose care is not captured may be missed, potentially leading to underestimation of epilepsy cases. The broad R56 code captures heterogeneous conditions, such as single seizures, syncope, or nonepileptic events, raising misclassification concerns. 6 Respondents with both epilepsy and unclassified seizure codes were classified as having epilepsy; however, symptom overlap and diagnostic uncertainty may still affect comparability between groups. Future studies leveraging claims‐based or electronic health record (EHR) data may allow for better patient tracking, more precise case definitions, and longitudinal designs that can support causal inference.

Patients with unclassified seizures may be more likely to appear in the MEPS during years of acute, high‐cost events, potentially inflating spending estimates. This study included the COVID‐19 pandemic period, which disrupted healthcare access and utilization. While we adjusted for calendar year effects, we were unable to account for specific, pandemic‐related changes in healthcare‐seeking behavior and service delivery. Future studies focusing exclusively on the post‐pandemic period could provide clearer insights.

The MEPS excludes institutionalized individuals, defined as those in hospitals or long‐term care facilities for more than 45 days. 36 Given the higher prevalence of seizures in these settings, this exclusion is likely to lead to an underestimation of inpatient care use and associated costs. Lastly, the MEPS only captures spending on prescription medications, excluding over‐the‐counter (OTC) drugs and potentially underrepresenting total medication burden. 37 Although our study identified significant differences in ASM fills and spending, the dataset's incomplete capture of newer, often costlier ASMs during the study period, may limit the accuracy of medication‐related expenditure estimates.

CONCLUSION

In this nationally representative analysis of U.S. adults with epilepsy or unclassified seizures, we observed significantly higher acute care utilization, particularly emergency and inpatient care, as well as greater total expenditures among those with unclassified seizures. While cost differences alone may not indicate system inefficiencies, the high prevalence of undiagnosed seizures and greater reliance on acute care settings suggest potential missed opportunities for earlier diagnosis and more continuous care. Interventions aimed at facilitating earlier specialist evaluation and accurate seizure classification may reduce acute care use and improve long‐term outcomes for this high‐need population.
