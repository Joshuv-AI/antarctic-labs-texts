Continuous glucose monitoring reveals distinct clinical implications of glucose levels at the start and end of hemodialysis in people with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Akinori Hayashi, Rihito Takaesu, Haruna Sogabe, Yuiko Tani, Nagisa Aoki, Yoshiki Ko, Satoshi Tsuji, Ibuki Moriguchi, Naoyuki Kobayashi, Takeshi Miyatsuka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical significance of glucose levels at the start and end of hemodialysis (HD) sessions in people with type 2 diabetes remains insufficiently explored.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 116 individuals with type 2 diabetes undergoing HD (age 61 ± 12 years; 69% male; HbA1c 6.5 ± 1.3%; glycated albumin 21.1 ± 6.9%). Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data were collected from two dialysis institutions to evaluate sensor glucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions and their associations with CGM metrics and hypoglycemia.ResultsGlucose levels were significantly higher at the start than at the end-of-HD (175.6 ± 55.4 mg/dL vs. 118.7 ± 43.3 mg/dL, P < 0.0001), with no correlation (r = 0.095, P = 0.31). Start-of-HD glucose was independently associated with dialysis duration (β = −0.184, P = 0.015) and time above range > 180 mg/dL (TAR180; β = 0.576, P < 0.0001), whereas end-of-HD glucose was associated with glycemic variability (%CV; β = −0.412, P < 0.0001) and TAR180 (β = 0.360, P < 0.0001). HD-induced hypoglycemia occurred in 25 participants (21.6%), including 14 (12.1%) during HD and 22 (19.0%) after HD. Lower end-of-HD glucose was significantly associated with all-cause hypoglycemia (OR 0.97; 0.95–0.98), HD-induced hypoglycemia (OR 0.96; 0.94–0.98), hypoglycemia during HD (OR 0.93; 0.90–0.97), and post-dialysis hypoglycemia (OR 0.95; 0.93–0.98; all P < 0.0001). An end-of-HD glucose level < 90 mg/dL was linked to increased all-cause (76.9% vs. 17.8%) and HD-induced (61.5% vs. 10.0%) hypoglycemia, demonstrating clinically meaningful discrimination for hypoglycemia risk stratification.ConclusionGlucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions demonstrate distinct clinical relevance. End-of-HD glucose level serves as a useful marker for hypoglycemia risk, highlighting the potential importance of tailored monitoring strategies to support glycemic risk assessment in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02261-7.

Introduction

Glucose management in people with diabetes and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) presents unique challenges, making it difficult to achieve optimal glucose profiles [1–6]. People with diabetes undergoing hemodialysis (HD) face an increased risk of complications and metabolic abnormalities, further exacerbating blood glucose level fluctuations, although the critical importance of meticulous glucose management is often understated [3–8]. Recent studies utilizing continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) have provided detailed insights into glucose profiles in people with diabetes on HD, reporting a high incidence of HD-induced hypoglycemia and post-dialysis meal-induced glucose excursions [9–11]. Furthermore, HbA1c, a conventional glycemic marker, tends to underestimate glycemic control in people with both diabetes and ESKD [12], partly due to renal anemia and erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA), which may alter red blood cell turnover. This has led to updated recommendations advocating the use of CGM in these people [10, 13–15].

Hypoglycemia can result in severe complications, such as loss of consciousness and seizures, whereas hyperglycemia increases the risk of cardiovascular events and other microvascular complications [16]. In people with diabetes undergoing HD, increased glycemic variability and the combined effects of glycemic variability and hypoglycemia have been reported to contribute to heightened mortality risks [17]. However, detailed evaluations of glucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions, along with their clinical significance, remain unexplored. Although people with diabetes undergoing HD are prone to glucose fluctuations and hypoglycemia owing to the effects of HD [9, 10], the precise glucose profiles before and after HD sessions and their clinical significance remain unclear.

In this study, we hypothesized that glucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions reflect distinct glucose profiles, particularly the risks associated with hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia. While previous CGM studies have mainly described intradialytic glucose trends and overall glycemic variability in patients undergoing HD, the specific clinical relevance of glucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions has not been systematically evaluated. We examined the relationship between glucose levels and glucose profiles obtained from CGM. These findings may provide novel insights into glucose management in patients with diabetes undergoing HD. An improved comprehension of glucose profiles in these populations can potentially inform the development of more effective treatment strategies, ultimately enhancing their quality of life.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant

We conducted a retrospective analysis of data from 116 people with type 2 diabetes undergoing maintenance HD who underwent professional CGM at either Kitasato University Hospital or Sohbudai Nieren Clinic until February 2019. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kitasato University Hospital (B23-108). All study methods adhered to the relevant guidelines and regulations of Kitasato University Hospital, as well as the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Participants in Japan and under the Code of Ethics of the Helsinki Declaration. Type 2 diabetes was defined according to the American Diabetes Association criteria, including measurements of blood glucose levels, HbA1c, and glycated albumin (GA) (Lucica; Asahi Kasei Pharma, Tokyo, Japan). People with malignancies, liver cirrhosis, hematologic disorders, active thyroid dysfunction, pregnancy, or hemoglobinopathies were excluded. Participants were required to demonstrate stable glycemic control for at least three months, maintain a consistent dosage of ESA, and avoid blood transfusions during the study period. During the CGM monitoring period, the prescribed dietary energy intake, dialysis parameters, and insulin regimens were not intentionally modified.

Device description

CGM was performed on all 116 participants using either the CGMS® System Gold (Medtronic Minimed, Northridge, CA, USA) in 41 cases or the Medtronic iPro2® CGM (Medtronic Minimed, Northridge, CA, USA) in 75 cases. Although two types of recording devices (CGMS® System Gold and iPro2®) were used, both systems utilized the same Enlite® sensor; therefore, the accuracy of interstitial glucose measurement is considered comparable. Each CGM device was used and calibrated according to the manufacturer-recommended procedures. Monitoring was performed for two consecutive days, including both HD and non-HD days, in accordance with previous studies [10, 18]. The full dataset of sensor glucose level (SGL) readings was analyzed from CGM records collected between 0:00 and 24:00 over the 2 days on both HD and non-HD days. Hypoglycemia was defined as SGL readings below 70 mg/dL for a duration exceeding 15 min [19, 20]. Hypoglycemia during HD and post-dialysis hypoglycemia were defined as episodes of low SGL occurring during the HD session and in the period following HD completion and prior to the subsequent meal, respectively. HD-induced hypoglycemia was defined as any occurrence of hypoglycemia during and/or after HD sessions [10]. To assess CGM metrics, we calculated multiple metrics using the SGL data collected over the entire 48-h recording period, including mean SGL and coefficient of variation (%CV). In addition, we computed the glucose management indicator (GMI) based on the 48-h mean SGL and determined the percentage of time that SGL readings fell within specific ranges: 70–180 mg/dL (termed ‘time in range’ or TIR), < 70 mg/dL (‘time below range’ or TBR70), < 54 mg/dL (TBR (54)), > 180 mg/dL (‘time above range’ or TAR180), and > 250 mg/dL (TAR250) for the 2 consecutive days: HD and non-HD days [19].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5.02 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and JMP Pro 16 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified. Continuous variables were compared using unpaired t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, and categorical variables using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with SGL obtained from CGM at the start and end of HD sessions. Variables with clinical relevance and/or statistical significance in univariate analyses were included in multivariable models. Logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate associations between clinical variables, peri-dialysis glucose levels, and hypoglycemia risk, with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals calculated. Multivariable nominal logistic regression models included age, sex, HbA1c, GA, and SGL at the start and end of HD sessions. The difference in SGL during HD (start-of-HD minus end-of-HD glucose) was also examined as a predictor of hypoglycemia and evaluated in a separate multivariable model to avoid multicollinearity. ROC curve analyses were used to assess the discriminative ability of end-of-HD glucose for predicting hypoglycemia, with AUC calculated as a measure of discrimination. Optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index, and sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and likelihood ratios were calculated. Multivariable discrimination was evaluated using ROC curves based on model-predicted probabilities. Comparisons of AUCs were descriptive, and no formal statistical testing between ROC curves was performed. In additon, internal validation of the ROC model was performed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to estimate optimism-corrected AUC. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

One hundred and sixteen participants were included in this study; 80 (69.0%) were males, and the average age was 61 ± 12 years, with HbA1c at 6.5% ± 1.3% and GA at 21.1% ± 6.9% (Table 1). All participants consumed meals prior to the dialysis session. All participants underwent thrice-weekly 4-h maintenance HD and consumed their next meal within 0.5–1 h after completing HD. Ninety-five participants started their HD in the morning, 13 in the afternoon, and eight in the evening. The dialysate glucose concentrations were 100 mg/dL in 64 participants, 125 mg/dL in 42 participants, and 150 mg/dL in 10 participants.

Glycemic profile and CGM metrics

The CGM data and metrics are presented in Table 2. The mean and %CV of the 48-h SGL were 152.3 ± 32.3 mg/dL and 26.7 ± 7.7%, respectively. The TIR was 77.9% (3.6–100%), and 79 participants (68.1%) achieved a TIR of > 70%. TBR70 and TBR54 were both 0% (0–26.0%) and 0% (0–6.4%). A total of 101 participants (87.1%) had TBR70 < 4%, 88 participants (75.9%) had TBR70 < 1%, and 107 participants (93.0%) had TBR54 < 1%. TAR180 and TAR250 were 20.1% (0–96.4%) and 0.5% (0–76.0%), respectively. On HD days, the mean and %CV of SGL were 150.8 ± 34.9 mg/dL and 28.7 ± 10.3%, respectively. The TIR was 74.0% (1.0–100%), and 68 participants (58.6%) achieved a TIR of > 70% on HD days. TBR70, TBR54, TAR180, and TAR250 were 0% (0–34.7%), 0% (0–12.8%), 18.6% (0–99.0%), and 0% (0–55.6%), respectively. On non-HD days, the mean and %CV of SGL were 153.9 ± 42.0 mg/dL and 24.9 ± 8.8%, respectively. TIR, TBR70, TBR54, TAR180, and TAR250 were 78.6% (0–100%), 0% (0–23.3%), 0% (0–1.7%), 19.3% (0–100%), and 0% (0–95.5%), respectively.

Thirty-six participants (31.0%) experienced all-cause hypoglycemia during the 48-h CGM monitoring period. Specifically, 14 participants (12.1%) exhibited hypoglycemia during HD sessions, 22 participants (19.0%) experienced post-dialysis hypoglycemia, and 25 participants (21.6%) had HD-induced hypoglycemia.

Glucose levels at start and end of HD sessions

The SGL at the start and end of the HD sessions were 175.6 ± 55.4 mg/dL and 118.7 ± 43.3 mg/dL, respectively. There was no significant correlation between SGL at the start and end of HD sessions (r = 0.095, P = 0.3097). SGL were significantly lower at the end of the HD sessions than at the start (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1).

Factors associated with SGL at the start and end of HD sessions were examined using univariate linear regression (Table 3). SGL at neither time point were associated with sex, age, dry weight, body mass index, or diabetes duration. Dialysis duration was negatively correlated with the SGL at the start of HD (r = − 0.22, P = 0.0017) but not with end-of-HD glucose. Insulin-related variables, including basal and bolus doses, total daily dose, insulin dose on HD days, and pre-HD bolus dose, showed no significant associations with SGL at either time point. Prescribed daily energy intake per ideal body weight was positively correlated with start-of-HD glucose (r = 0.24, P = 0.0107) but not with end-of-HD glucose. Hemoglobin concentration was negatively correlated with end-of-HD glucose (r = − 0.20, P = 0.0309), whereas albumin showed no association with glucose levels at either time point. HbA1c was positively correlated with start-of-HD glucose (r = 0.25, P = 0.0065) but not with end-of-HD glucose, while GA was positively correlated with the SGL at both the start and end of HD. Among dialysis-related parameters, only dialysis fluid flow showed a modest but statistically significant negative correlation with the SGL at the start of HD. CGM metrics demonstrated distinct associations. Mean SGL were positively correlated with the SGL at both time points, whereas %CV was negatively correlated only with end-of-HD glucose. TIR was negatively correlated with the SGL at both the start and end of HD. TBR70 and TBR54 was negatively correlated only with end-of-HD glucose. In contrast, TAR180 and TAR250 showed positive correlations with the SGL at both the start and end of HD sessions. Additionally, the difference in sensor glucose levels during HD was significantly correlated with both start-of-HD glucose and end-of-HD glucose.

In the multivariate analysis of SGL at the start and end of HD sessions, variables including age, sex, dialysis duration, HbA1c, GA, mean SGL, %CV of SGL, TIR, TBR70, and TAR180 were selected based on the results of the univariate linear regression analyses. Multiple linear regression analyses demonstrated that TAR180 and dialysis duration were independently associated with the start-of-HD glucose (r² = 0.3458, β = 0.576, F = 60.19, P < 0.0001; and r² = 0.3795, β = −0.184, F = 6.13, P = 0.0148, respectively). In contrast, %CV and TAR180 were independently associated with the end-of-HD glucose (r² = 0.1428, β = −0.412, t = − 5.11, F = 26.13, P < 0.0001; and r² = 0.2710, β = 0.360, t = 4.46, F = 19.86, P < 0.0001, respectively). We examined the association between glycation markers, SGL at the start and end of HD sessions and the risk of hypoglycemia using logistic regression analyses. Among all evaluated variables, only end-of-HD glucose level was significantly associated with an increased risk of hypoglycemia, including all-cause hypoglycemia (OR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95–0.98; P < 0.0001), HD-induced hypoglycemia (OR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.94–0.98; P < 0.0001), hypoglycemia during HD (OR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.90–0.97; P < 0.0001), and post-dialysis hypoglycemia (OR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.93–0.98; P < 0.0001) (Table 4). Because of collinearity, the difference in SGL during HD was evaluated in a separate multivariable logistic regression model including age, sex, HbA1c, and GA. The difference in SGL during HD was significantly associated with all-cause hypoglycemia (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02, P = 0.0009), hypoglycemia during HD (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02, P = 0.0453), and post-dialysis hypoglycemia (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02, P = 0.0112), but not HD-induced hypoglycemia (OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.99–1.02, P = 0.0564) (Supplementary Table 1).

To assess the clinical utility of end-of-HD glucose levels for predicting hypoglycemia, ROC analysis was performed. Univariate ROC analysis using end-of-HD glucose as the sole discriminator yielded an AUC of 0.791, with an optimal cutoff of 89 mg/dL based on the maximum Youden index. Given the minimal numerical difference and the need for pragmatic clinical application, a threshold of 90 mg/dL was adopted for risk stratification. ROC analysis based on predicted probabilities from the multivariable model showed slightly improved discrimination (AUC 0.797), indicating that incorporating multiple clinical variables modestly enhances predictive performance beyond end-of-HD glucose alone. All-cause hypoglycemia occurred more frequently in patients with end-of-HD glucose < 90 mg/dL than in those with levels ≥ 90 mg/dL (76.9% vs. 17.8%), yielding a sensitivity of 55.6% and a specificity of 92.5%, with a positive likelihood ratio of 7.4. Similarly, HD-induced hypoglycemia was more common among patients with end-of-HD glucose < 90 mg/dL (61.5% vs. 10.0%), with a sensitivity of 64.0%, specificity of 89.0%, positive likelihood ratio of 5.8, indicating good discriminative performance. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). The optimism-adjusted AUC was 0.776 for all-cause hypoglycemia and 0.789 for HD-induced hypoglycemia, indicating moderate discrimination with minimal overfitting. In multivariable nominal logistic regression analysis including age, sex, HbA1c, GA, and glucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions, end-of-HD glucose remained the only independent predictor of HD-induced hypoglycemia (β = −0.0365, P = 0.0003), whereas no other covariates were significant. The model demonstrated good overall fit and acceptable calibration. Baseline characteristics stratified by end-of-HD glucose (< 90 mg/dL vs. ≥90 mg/dL) are shown in Supplementary Table 2. There were no significant differences in HbA1c, GA, start-of-HD glucose levels, or dialysis-related parameters between groups, whereas the proportion of insulin users was higher in patients with end-of-HD glucose ≥ 90 mg/dL.

Discussion

In this study, we clarified the clinical relevance of glucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions using CGM in people with type 2 diabetes undergoing HD. While previous studies have examined differences in glycemic profiles between HD and non-HD days and overall glycemic variability in this population [10, 18, 21–23], the specific clinical significance of glucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions has not been systematically evaluated. Patients receiving HD are at high risk for hypoglycemia; however, routine CGM for all patients is often impractical, underscoring the need for simple and reliable risk markers. The lack of correlation between glucose levels at the start and end of HD sessions may reflect dynamic intradialytic glucose changes. Plasma glucose tends to equilibrate toward the dialysate glucose concentration during HD, while erythrocyte glucose consumption may further lower glucose levels. In addition, intradialytic hypoglycemia and subsequent counter-regulatory responses may lead to rebound increases in glucose levels, weakening the relationship between them. Our findings demonstrate that glucose levels at both the start and end-of-HD are clinically informative, with end-of-HD glucose emerging as a variable associated with both HD-induced and all-cause hypoglycemia. In contrast, conventional markers such as HbA1c, GA, and start-of-HD glucose were not significantly associated with hypoglycemic events, highlighting the potential clinical utility of end-of-HD glucose as a timely and actionable indicator of hypoglycemia risk. Previous CGM-based studies have shown a progressive decline in glucose levels during HD sessions [9, 10, 18, 21, 23–27], consistent with our findings. A gradual decrease was observed in most cases, even when glucose levels were relatively low at the start, sometimes resulting in HD-induced hypoglycemia, which has been reported in more than 20% of patients [10, 27, 28]. We also evaluated the magnitude of glucose decline during HD as a potential indicator of hypoglycemia. Although greater declines were associated with several hypoglycemia outcomes, the effect size was smaller than that of end-of-HD glucose. This may partly reflect rebound increases in glucose toward the end-of-HD after marked intradialytic declines and hypoglycemia. Therefore, end-of-HD glucose may better represent the immediate glycemic status and subsequent hypoglycemia risk. Since HD-induced hypoglycemia diminishes the quality of life and may increases the risk of cardiovascular events and atherosclerosis progression [14, 16], it is important to identify patients at increased risk of hypoglycemia. Further analyses are warranted to identify the clinical parameters and CGM profiles that can refine risk prediction.

Linear regression models and multivariate analysis demonstrated that glucose levels at the start-of-HD correlated with the time of hyperglycemia, while those at the end-of-HD correlated with both time of hyperglycemia and glycemic variability. These findings suggest that addressing hyperglycemia at the start and end-of-HD may reduce hyperglycemia. However, the correlation between glucose levels at the end-of-HD and glycemic variability implies that glucose-lowering interventions for individuals with high glucose levels at the end-of-HD may be associated with the risk of HD-induced hypoglycemia. Hence, careful and intensive strategies are required to minimize hypoglycemia risk while preventing hyperglycemia in these cases.

People with diabetes and ESKD face a significantly higher risk of severe hypoglycemia, which is often asymptomatic during HD. This underscores the importance of accurate detection and prevention [7, 8]. HD-induced hypoglycemia may result from impaired counter-regulatory hormone secretion and autonomic responses [9, 29, 30]. Studies have documented that people with type 2 diabetes on HD frequently experience HD-induced hypoglycemia [9, 24], and these episodes do not correlate significantly with HbA1c, GA, or glucose levels at the start of HD sessions [10]. While CGM data for people with type 2 diabetes undergoing HD have detected both HD-induced hypoglycemia and HD-associated glucose excursions, the characteristics of those who experience these glucose irregularities remain unclear [9, 21, 24]. This study demonstrated, for the first time, that lower glucose levels at the end of HD were closely associated with the risk of hypoglycemia. This finding suggests that assessing glucose levels at the start of HD may be insufficient for detecting and preventing HD-induced hypoglycemia, highlighting the clinical importance of monitoring glucose levels at the end of HD sessions. Notably, glucose levels at the end of HD sessions can be readily assessed using conventional glucometers, making this approach highly feasible in routine practice. End-of-HD glucose may serve as a simple and practical screening marker to identify patients at increased risk of hypoglycemia. Patients with end-of-HD glucose < 90 mg/dL may warrant closer monitoring, such as CGM, and consideration of individualized treatment adjustments, including modification of insulin dosing, intradialytic nutritional intake, or dialysis-related settings. This approach may be particularly useful in settings where CGM is unavailable, providing a practical strategy to support hypoglycemia risk management in daily dialysis care.

ROC analysis demonstrated that end-of-HD glucose has moderate discriminative ability for hypoglycemia risk, with a cutoff of approximately 90 mg/dL identifying patients at markedly increased risk of both all-cause and HD-induced hypoglycemia. This threshold showed high specificity and a strong positive likelihood ratio, suggesting potential utility as a practical rule-in marker. However, its moderate sensitivity indicates that glucose levels ≥ 90 mg/dL do not exclude the risk of hypoglycemia. Therefore, this cutoff should be interpreted as a pragmatic clinical threshold rather than a strict diagnostic boundary. Given the minimal difference between the statistically optimal cutoff (89 mg/dL) and the proposed value, 90 mg/dL was selected to facilitate clinical use. Because formal statistical comparisons between ROC curves were not performed, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than definitive comparisons of discriminative performance. Moreover, the improved discrimination achieved with multivariable logistic regression models suggests that incorporating additional clinical factors alongside end-of-HD glucose may further refine risk prediction. Together, these findings suggest that end-of-HD glucose may serve as an initial screening tool, with supplementary clinical information considered as needed. People with low end-of-HD glucose also showed lower mean glucose levels and greater glycemic variability, suggesting a phenotype prone to hypoglycemia. Recent evidence suggests that CGM-derived hypoglycemia metrics may have limited ability to predict severe hypoglycemia when used alone [31]. Nevertheless, identifying non-severe hypoglycemia may remain clinically relevant, as such episodes could precede more severe events.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of our study lies in its sample size, which is the largest among previous studies utilizing CGM in populations with diabetes undergoing HD. However, our study had some limitations. As an observational study, it remains unclear whether evaluating and managing glucose levels at the start and end of HD contributes to the improvement of complication outcomes and mortality. The CGM device used was an older model with lower accuracy in the hypoglycemic range; however, its reported accuracy in HD populations is comparable to that of newer devices, supporting the validity of our findings [32–37]. Although prescribed dietary intake and insulin regimens were available, the actual dietary intake and the precise dose and timing of insulin administration were not captured. In addition, dialysis sessions were conducted at different times of the day, and circadian or meal-timing effects may have influenced glucose levels. These factors may represent unmeasured confounders influencing end-of-HD hypoglycemia. Furthermore, although internal validation using bootstrap resampling was performed, the proposed cutoff value for end-of-HD glucose was not externally validated in an independent cohort. Therefore, the generalizability of this finding should be interpreted with caution and requires confirmation in future prospective studies. In addition, CGM accuracy is known to be lower in the hypoglycemic range, which could affect the detection of hypoglycemic events. Glucose data were collected over a limited 48-h period, which may affect generalizability, although this timeframe included both HD and non-HD days and allowed assessment under different glycemic conditions. Future prospective studies using contemporary CGM devices are needed to determine whether end-of-HD glucose–guided interventions can reduce hypoglycemia and improve clinical outcomes in this high-risk population. The long-term clinical implications of using the end-of-HD glucose threshold of < 90 mg/dL to identify patients at high risk of hypoglycemia remain unclear and require validation in prospective studies. Recent advances in CGM-derived dynamic indices and machine learning–based approaches may further enhance risk stratification and enable more precise prediction of hypoglycemia by integrating dynamic glucose patterns with clinical variables [38, 39].

Conclusions

This study demonstrated for the first time that glucose levels at the end of HD sessions strongly predicted all-cause hypoglycemia, including HD-induced hypoglycemia, whereas HbA1c, glycated albumin, and start-of-HD glucose did not. End-of-dialysis glucose is a simple and practical marker that can be assessed using conventional glucometers, enabling clinicians to identify high-risk patients and intervene appropriately, even in settings where CGM is not routinely available. In particular, an end-of-HD glucose threshold of 90 mg/dL showed good discriminative performance for identifying patients at an increased risk of hypoglycemia, suggesting its potential utility as a clinically relevant cutoff value for hypoglycemia risk stratification in routine dialysis care. These findings provide a strong rationale for incorporating end-of-dialysis glucose monitoring, potentially using the 90 mg/dL threshold, into routine practice to optimize glycemic management and improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
