Systematic evaluation of Tox21 compounds that target β-adrenergic receptors and their role in cardiotoxicity
Authors: Xi Luo, Li Zhang, Srilatha Sakamuru, Precious A. Adesina, Jinghua Zhao, Menghang Xia, Tuan Xu, Deborah K. Ngan, Jameson Travers, Savannah Wood, Ray Sukhawanit, Anthony Garrison, Carleen Klumpp-Thomas, Stephen S. Ferguson, Hu Zhu, Matthew D. Hall, David M. Reif, Ruili Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
β-adrenergic receptors play important roles in heart failure and drug-induced cardiotoxicity (DICT). The Tox21 10 K library of drugs and environmental chemicals have been tested for their activity against β-adrenergic receptor subtypes 1 and 2 (ADRB1 and ADRB2), as well as inhibition of the human ether-à-go-go-related gene (hERG) in a quantitative high-throughput screening (qHTS) format. In this study, the Tox21 compound activity profiles in the ADRB1/2 and hERG assays were compared in relation to their DICT potential. The results showed that compounds that acted as ADRB1 agonists, ADRB2 antagonists, or hERG inhibitors were more likely to exhibit DICT. The ADRB1 and ADRB2 assays shared similar compound activity profiles, while the hERG inhibition assay identified a distinct set of active compounds. In addition, we identified structural features that may differentiate the cardiotoxic and non-toxic ADRB1 agonists. Finally, machine learning models were developed for ADRB1 activity prediction based on chemical structure. The models were used to virtually screen a collection of approximately 360 K diverse compounds, with the highest-ranked compounds selected for experimental validation. This work represents the first systematic study of drugs and environmental chemicals against ADRB1/2, providing important insights into β-adrenergic receptor-related cardiotoxicity mechanisms. By clarifying how specific pharmacological interactions contribute to cardiac risk, it provides a framework for early cardiotoxicity prediction and the design of safer therapeutics through integrated profiling and modeling.

Introduction

Drug-induced cardiotoxicity is a major safety concern in drug development and clinical practice, contributing to significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs (Destere et al., 2024; Mladěnka et al., 2018; Cheang et al., 2024). Clinically relevant cardiotoxic events can occur across diverse therapeutic classes, including oncology, respiratory, and metabolic agents, and may manifest as arrhythmias, myocardial injury, or heart failure (Herrmann, 2020; Ewer and Ewer, 2015). In drug-treated populations, cardiotoxicity not only worsens existing cardiovascular disease but can also precipitate new-onset cardiac dysfunction, leading to hospitalizations and long-term treatment complications (Mladěnka et al., 2018; Ashfield and Ojha, 2025). High-profile examples—such as the market withdrawal of cisapride due to fatal arrhythmias or black-box warnings for doxorubicin-related cardiomyopathy—underscore the clinical and regulatory consequences of inadequate cardiovascular safety assessment (Wysowski and Bacsanyi, 1996; Smalley et al., 2000). These adverse events frequently emerge late in development or post-marketing, resulting in costly late-stage attrition and restricted patient access to otherwise effective therapies.

Among the molecular determinants of drug-induced cardiotoxicity, β-adrenergic receptors (β-ARs) (Lorton and Bellinger, 2015) and the human ether-à-go-go-related gene (hERG) potassium channel (Vandenberg et al., 2012) are of particular interest. The β1-adrenergic receptor (ADRB1), highly expressed in the heart, regulates chronotropy and inotropy through G-protein-coupled signaling (Zhang et al., 2021), while the β2-adrenergic receptor (ADRB2), more widely distributed, contributes to vasodilation, bronchodilation, and fine-tuning of cardiac contractility (Romano et al., 2021). Pharmacological modulation of these receptors, whether by agonists or antagonists—can influence cardiac remodeling, arrhythmogenesis, and myocardial survival pathways, with both beneficial and detrimental effects (Zhao et al., 2020; Parichatikanond et al., 2024). The hERG potassium channel mediates the rapid delayed rectifier K+ current (I_Kr), essential for cardiac repolarization, the inhibition of which is a well-established cause of QT interval prolongation and torsades de pointes (Hancox et al., 2008; Witchel and Hancox, 2000). Together, ADRB1, ADRB2, and hERG represent key pharmacological nodes where therapeutic intervention intersects with potential cardiotoxic risk. Despite their importance, current research and safety screening practices often address cardiotoxicity as a broad, non-specific endpoint, without dissecting the relationship between precise pharmacological target activity profiles and clinical risk. Many drugs cause cardiotoxicity through non-hERG mechanisms (Force et al., 2007; Wu et al., 2020), yet early-stage screening still heavily emphasizes hERG inhibition alone. Furthermore, the influence of target modulation type (agonist vs antagonist) and receptor subtype selectivity on cardiotoxic outcomes remains poorly defined (Raschi et al., 2010). There is also a lack of integration between target activity profiles, chemical structure information, and predictive modeling in preclinical cardio safety evaluation, limiting opportunities for rational drug design aimed at minimizing cardiovascular liability (Tosca et al., 2021).

To help bridge this gap, the Tox21 10 K compound library of drugs and environmental chemicals (Richard et al., 2021) was screened against ADRB1 and ADRB2, as well as hERG in a quantitative high throughput screening (qHTS) format (Krishna et al., 2022; Huang, 2022; Zhao and Xia, 2022). Additionally, a reference list of compounds with known drug-induced cardiotoxicity (DICT) potential was compiled by integrating in vivo toxicity data from a variety of literature sources (Ye et al., 2022). These datasets enabled a systematic analysis of how β-AR modulation and hERG inhibition relate to cardiotoxicity. We further analyzed the chemical structures of compounds exhibiting different modes of activity against the β-ARs and hERG to identify distinctive chemical features linked to cardiotoxicity. Finally, we developed machine learning models for predicting ADRB1 modulators, which we applied to virtually screen the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS) in house collection of ~360 K diverse compounds, with selected candidates undergoing experimental validation. By systematically examining compound activity profiles against these targets in relation to reported cardiotoxicity, we aim to clarify how distinct pharmacological interactions contribute to cardiac risk. By integrating pharmacological profiling with structural information and predictive modeling, this work provides a framework for early identification and mitigation of cardiotoxic liabilities in drug discovery for safer therapeutics.

Methods

Compound library

The Tox21 10 K compound library consists of compounds mostly procured from commercial sources by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS), and the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS), for more than 10,000 plated compound solutions consisting of 8599 unique chemical substances including environmental chemicals such as pesticides, industrial chemicals, food additives, and drugs (Richard et al., 2021). Moreover, the Tox21 10 K compound library contains the NCATS Pharmaceutical Collection (NPC) (Huang, 2019), which comprises roughly 3000 small molecule drugs approved for clinical use or under investigational evaluation by regulatory authorities in the U.S., Europe, Japan, Australia, and Canada.

Chemical structure analysis

For structure-based analysis, chemical structures were converted to ToxPrint fingerprints. These fingerprints encode publicly accessible ToxPrint chemotypes (v2.0_r711), which were generated using the associated ChemoTyper application (https://github.com/mn-am/chemotyper). The ToxPrint chemotypes encompass 729 distinct chemical features (Wei et al., 2024). Structural features significantly associated with ADRB1/ADRB2 activity were identified using the Fisher’s exact test, with a p-value threshold of <0.05 considered statistically significant. t-SNE (t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) was used to reduce the ToxPrint of different molecules to 2D and visualize how different compounds are grouped together, identifying patterns, outliers, or clusters based on their structural similarity.

ADRB1/ADRB2 and hERG qHTS data and selectivity method

The qHTS data used in this study were obtained through screening the Tox21 10 K compound library against the ADRB1/ADRB2 and hERG assays (https://tripod.nih.gov/pubdata/). This dataset includes both agonist and antagonist activities for the β-AR subtypes ADRB1 and ADRB2, as well as activities from the hERG channel inhibition assay. The qHTS data processing pipeline involving raw plate-level data processing, concentration-response curve fitting to determine potency and efficacy, classification of compound activity, evaluation of assay reproducibility, and assignment of activity outcomes can be found in a previous study (Huang, 2022). Briefly, the Tox21 compound activity in each assay was measured by first assigning a class based on the concentration–response curve observed (1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, and 3 for actives; 4 for inactive). The curve class was then converted to a curve rank, an integer between −9 and 9, where the higher rank corresponds to a higher curve quality, potency, and efficacy according to previously described criteria (Huang, 2022). Inhibitors were assigned negative curve ranks, and activators were assigned positive curve ranks. A curve rank of 0 indicates inactivity. The compound was then assigned an activity outcome as previously described, based on the average curve rank and reproducibility from the triplicate assay runs, as one of the following: active agonist/antagonist, inconclusive agonist/antagonist, inconclusive, or inactive.

Machine learning models and virtual screening of compound libraries

Four machine learning (ML) classification algorithms, including Naïve Bayes (NB) from the “MultinomialNB” package, Support Vector Machine (SVM) from the “SVC” package, Random Forest (RF) from the “RandomForestClassifier” package, and XGBoost from the “XGBClassifier” package in Python, were applied to build models to predict compound activity against ADRB1. In these models, compounds classified as active agonists (agonist mode assay) or antagonists (antagonist mode assay) in the qHTS assays were assigned a value of 1 and inactive compounds were assigned a value of 0. Chemical structures were converted to ECFP4 (Extended Connectivity Fingerprints, version 4) fingerprints, generated in KNIME, which were used to train the classifiers. For each dataset (ADRB1 agonist or antagonist), the data was randomly split into 70 % training and 30 % testing sets, and the models were trained over 10 iterations. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) scores across all iterations. Models with high AUC-ROC scores were then applied to virtually screen an NCATS in-house collection of approximately 360 K compounds for ADRB1 agonist and antagonist activity, based on their ECFP4 fingerprints. Around 10,000 compounds with predicted probabilities higher than the threshold for each machine learning model were selected, and around 2000 compounds from the consensus predictions of four models were further selected for experimental validation for both ADRB1 agonists and antagonists.

Experimental validation

The model predicted ADRB1 actives were selected for experimental validation based on the predicted probabilities from four ML models (consensus model). A total of 1385 model predicted ADRB1 actives, including 689 predicted agonists and 771 predicted antagonists (75 compounds predicted to function as both agonists and antagonists), were selected based on availability of physical samples in house. These compounds were tested in the ADRB1 assay in both agonist and antagonist modes. The performance of the ADRB1 model was evaluated using the positive predictive value (PPV = TP/(TP + FP)), where TP (true positive) is the number of compounds that were tested active in the ADRB1 assay and FP (false positive) is the number of compounds that were tested inactive in the ADRB1 assay.

Example concentration-response curves from experimental follow-up assays were fit using the four-parameter logistic regression method, with the % assay activity as the response and log10 compound concentration as the independent variable, within the “drc” statistical package in R. Plots were generated using the “ggplot2” package in R. Representative chemical structures were drawn using the ChemDraw Professional software (version 17.1).

Results

Compound activities in the ADRB1/ADRB2 and hERG assays

A total of 7872 compounds in the Tox21 10 K compound library, which includes drugs and environmental chemicals, were screened for ADRB1 and ADRB2 activity in both agonist and antagonist modes, as well as for hERG inhibition. Compounds were categorized into “active agonist”, “inconclusive agonist”, “active antagonist”, “inconclusive antagonist”, “inconclusive”, and “inactive” based on their assay activities. The numbers of active and inactive compounds for each target are listed in Table 1. In each assay, the active compounds are outnumbered by the inactive compounds. The percentage of active compounds in the hERG inhibition assay (9.50 %) is higher than the ADRB1/2 assays (1.66 % for ADRB1-agonist, 1.11 % for ADRB1-antagonist, 0.57 % for ADRB2-agonist and 1.94 % for ADRB2-antagonist), suggesting that the compounds in the Tox21 10 K compound library are more likely to inhibit hERG and less likely to be modulators of ADRB targets.

To compare the ARDB1 and ADRB2 assays in terms of compound activities, we calculated the numbers of active and inactive compounds for the ADRB1 and ADRB2 assays in both agonist and antagonist modes (Fig. 1). For the agonist mode assays, 27 (0.55 %) compounds were active against both ADRB1 and ADRB2, while 30 compounds (0.61 %) were only active against ADRB1 and 6 compounds (0.12 %) were only active against ADRB2. For the antagonist mode assays, 44 (0.62 %) compounds inhibited both ADRB1 and ADRB2, while 27 compounds (0.38 %) inhibited only ADRB1 and 76 compounds (1.07 %) inhibited only ADRB2. These results show that, of the 63 compounds that acted as an agonist of either ADRB1 or ADRB2, 27 compounds (43 %) were active against both. Similarly, of the 147 compounds that inhibited either ADRB1 or ADRB2, 44 (30 %) inhibited both receptors according to the antagonist mode assay results. Taken together, while most of the active compounds showed differential activities toward ADRB1 and ADRB2, there was still a substantial overlap suggesting the similarities between the two β-AR subtypes in their modulation.

Comparison of cardiotoxicity potential between β-AR modulators and hERG inhibitors

We have previously compiled a list of drugs with known cardiotoxicity potential including negative controls (Ye et al., 2022). Each compound on this list was assigned a DICT (drug induced cardiotoxicity) score (a value between 0 and 1, where 1 means cardiotoxicity was reported by all sources and 0 means no cardiotoxicity reported) based on the frequency it was reported in the literature as cardiotoxic. There are 1001 compounds in the Tox21 10 K library that have a DICT score assigned, which were also screened in the ADRB and hERG assays. We compared the distributions of the DICT scores for compounds that fall into different assay activity categories (Fig. 2). Among the 1001 Tox21 compounds with DICT scores available, compounds that acted as ADRB1 agonist, ADRB2 agonist, ADRB2 anagonist, and hERG inhibitor have mean DICT scores larger than 0.40. The group of ADRB2 antagonists showed the largest mean DICT score of 0.47. In comparison, compounds that were inactive in all the ADRB and hERG assays have DICT scores below 0.40 presenting a lower potential for inducing cardiotoxicity. In addition, the group of ADRB1 antagonists showed the lowest mean DICT score of 0.32. Therefore, the compounds that fall into the categories of ADRB1 agonist, ADRB2 agonist, ADRB2 anagonist, and hERG inhibitor are more likely to exhibit cardiotoxicity, while the ADRB1 antagonists are unlikely to be cardiotoxic.

To further investigate the role that each target plays in cardiotoxicity, we calculated the active rates (i.e., the fraction of active compounds in an assay) of the compounds that have been reported to be more (DICT score > 0.50) or less (DICT score < 0.20) likely cardiotoxic in the ADRB1/ADRB2 and hERG inhibition assays (Fig. 3). For each assay, the active rates between the cardiotoxic and non-toxic compounds were compared to see if there was an enrichment of assay activity among the cardiotoxic compounds, i.e., if hitting the assay target was likely to induce cardiotoxicity, and the significance of difference was determined utilizing the Fisher’s exact test (p-values are shown in Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 3, the order of active rates for the five assays, in descending order regardless of cardiotoxicity potential, is as follows: hERG inhibition, ADRB1 agonist, ADRB2 antagonist, ADRB2 agonist, and ADRB1 antagonist. This shows that the hit rate for hERG was higher than those of ADRB1/2. When comparing the active rates between the cardiotoxic and non-toxic compounds within each assay, the p-values derived from the Fisher’s exact test for hERG inhibition, ADRB1 agonist, ADRB2 antagonist, ADRB1 antagonist, and ADRB2 agonist assays are <0.01, 0.14, 0.20, 0.79, and 0.76, respectively. Only the hERG inhibition assay showed a statistically significant difference with p < 0.01 in the active rates between the cardiotoxic and non-toxic compounds, indicating that hERG inhibitors are more likely to cause cardiotoxicity than ADRB1/2 modulators. Though not significant at p < 0.05, probably due to a relatively small sample size, the ADRB1 agonist assay had the second smallest p-value of 0.14 with cardiotoxic compounds showing a higher active rate in this assay than non-toxic compounds. This suggests that ADRB1 agonists are also likely to induce cardiotoxicity. Another assay that showed some level of significance is ADRB2 antagonist with p = 0.2, indicating a role for ADRB2 antagonism in cardiotoxicity.

Assay activity profiles of cardiotoxic compounds in the Tox21 10 K library

We analyzed the activity profiles of 589 cardiotoxic compounds (DICT score > 0.2) in the Tox21 10 K library across various targets, including hERG inhibition, ADRB1/2 agonism and antagonism (Fig. 4). Of the 589 compounds, 27 compounds (4.58 %) demonstrated ADRB1 agonist activity, 7 (1.19 %) showed ADRB1 antagonist activity, 7 (1.19 %) exhibited ADRB2 agonist activity, 25 (4.24 %) were ADRB2 antagonists, and 116 (19.69 %) were hERG inhibitors. As shown in Fig. 4, the hERG assay had the largest number of active compounds (blue sections), and a large fraction of the hERG inhibitors (73, 62.93 %) were not active in any of the ADRB assays (white sections). In comparison, the ADRB1 and ADRB2 assays had more active compounds in common. For example, among the 27 ADRB1 agonists and 25 ADRB2 antagonists, 7 compounds (26 % of ADRB1 agonists and 28 % of ADRB2 antagonists) overlapped in activity profiles, whereas only 7 ADRB1 agonists (26 %, constitutes 6 % of hERG inhibitors) and 14 ADRB2 antagonists (56 %, constitutes 12 % of hERG inhibitors) showed activity in the hERG assay. These findings suggest that ADRB modulation and hERG inhibition are largely independent mechanisms for cardiotoxicity with a small portion of ADRB modulators also showing hERG inhibition, while ADRB1 agonism and ADRB2 antagonism appeared to be interconnected mechanistically on some level. Also of note is that nearly half (254 compounds, 43.12 %) of the 589 cardiotoxic compounds did not show activity in any of the five assays investigated here, suggesting that the cardiotoxicity mechanisms of these compounds involve targets and pathways other than ADRB1/2 modulation or hERG inhibition. Overall, the compound activity profiles derived from the ADRB and hERG assays provide insights into the underlying mechanisms of drug-induced cardiotoxicity.

Chemical structure analysis of compounds acting through different cardiotoxicity mechanisms

We evaluated the structural characteristics of cardiotoxic compounds that act through different cardiotoxicity mechanisms to see if they could be distinguished by chemical structure. Fig. 5 shows the structure distribution, visualized using a t-SNE 2D projection of the chemical structures encoded in ToxPrint, of 182 cardiotoxic compounds in the Tox21 10 K library with DICT score > 0.2 that were also active in at least one of the ADRB or hERG assays. Overall, the hERG inhibitors were mostly separated from the ADRB modulators, which tend to cluster together in the upper left corner of the t-SNE plot. Some of the ADRB1 agonists and ADRB2 antagonists co-clustered with the hERG inhibitors in the lower right portion of the plot. These results show that the hERG inhibitors are structurally distinct from the ADRB modulators, while the ADRB1 and ADRB2 modulators share a high degree of structural similarity.

Agonists of the β1-AR (ADRB1) can potentially induce cardiotoxicity, especially when used chronically or at high doses (Biswal et al., 2025). However, we found that not all ADRB1 agonists identified from the Tox21 assay have documented cardiotoxicity. To find out if there are specific chemical features that contribute to the cardiotoxicity of ADRB1 agonists, we applied the Fisher’s exact test to identify chemical structures significantly enriched in cardiotoxic, as opposed to non-toxic, ADRB1 agonists (Table 2). The analysis revealed three significant chemical features with p-value <0.05, which include “bond: COC_ether_aliphatic__aromatic” (p = 0.006), as observed in pindolol, along with “bond: COH_alcohol_aromatic” (p = 0.026) and “bond: COH_alcohol_aromatic_phenol” (p = 0.026), present in isoprenaline and rotigotine, respectively. These findings suggest that aromatic ether and aromatic alcohol functionalities may contribute to cardiotoxicity for some compounds exhibiting ADRB1 agonist activities.

Virtual screening of ~ 360 K diverse compounds and experimental validation

The machine learning models trained on the Tox21 10 K compound library were applied to virtually screen the NCATS in-house collection of ~360 K diverse compounds for ADRB1 activities. The four ML models trained on the Tox21 10 K compound library for the prediction of ADRB1 agonists performed well as measured by AUC-ROC: 0.88 ± 0.03 (RF), 0.89 ± 0.02 (XGB), 0.83 ± 0.04 (NB), and 0.89 ± 0.03 (SVM); while the AUC-ROCs of the four ADRB1 antagonist prediction models are: 0.82 ± 0.05 (RF), 0.83 ± 0.04 (XGB), 0.74 ± 0.08 (NB), and 0.88 ± 0.04 (SVM). The results underscore these models’ robustness in identifying ADRB1 agonists and antagonists. Supplementary Table 1 presents the virtual screening results of the in-house collection of 360 K diverse compounds for ADRB1 activities from four ML methods including RF, XGB, NB, and SVM. The table lists the compounds predicted as potential ADRB1 agonists and antagonists. The final consensus results, which included 2860 ADRB1 agonists and 2750 ADRB1 antagonists, were prioritized for further testing.

A total of 1385 ADRB1 actives predicted by the models, including 689 agonists and 771 antagonists (75 were predicted to function as both agonists and antagonists), were further tested for experimental validation using the ADRB1 assay run in both agonist and antagonist modes, assay results were shown in Supplementary Table 2. The predicted compounds had significantly higher hit rates compared to the hit rates of the Tox21 ADRB1 assay data that were used to train the models. The ADRB1 model training datasets contained 1.99 % ADRB1 agonists, while 208 (30.19 %) of the model predicted agonists were experimentally confirmed as true ADRB1 agonists, corresponding to around a 15-fold increase in hit rate. Similarly, for ADRB1 antagonists, the model training datasets contained 0.91 % of actives while 34 (4.41 %) of the model predicted antagonists were tested active in the ADRB1 antagonist mode assay, corresponding to a 5-fold enrichment of actives in the ADRB1 antagonist model predictions.

The 256 compounds, including 208 agonists and 52 antagonists, tested active against ADRB1 in the first round of experimental validation were cherry-picked and retested in the ADRB1 assay for further confirmation, assay results were shown in Supplementary Table 3. 194 out of the 208 ADRB1 agonists remained active in the ADRB1 confirmation assay, yielding a confirmation rate of 93.3 %. Of the 194 confirmed ADRB1 agonists in the ADRB1 follow-up assay, 121 (62.4 %) compounds showed potent ADRB1 agonism in the assay with IC50 < 5 μM, with 81 (41.8 %) ADRB1 agonists showing IC50 < 1 μM. Among which, more than 20 compounds, including ractopamine, norepinephrine, dobutamine, fenoterol, and clenbuterol, have documented cardiotoxic potential with the risks ranging from tachycardia and arrhythmia to myocardial damage or hypertrophy (Yaeger et al., 2012; Lee, 2015), and a few compounds, such as ritodrine, prenylamine, and deterenol have been withdrawn in some regions due to safety concerns (Yonaga and Ito, 2021; Cohen et al., 2021). Out of the 194 confirmed ADRB1 agonists, 16 compounds (i.e., dobutamine, prenalterol, xamoterol, r(−)-denopamine, isoprenaline, norepinephrine bitartrate, dopamine, dopexamine, fenoterol, isamoltan, mirabegron, clenbuterol, terbutaline, metaproteranol, and levalbuterol) are known ADRB1 agonists (Delini-Stula and Hunn, 1988; Bergh et al., 2010), either selective or non-selective, while other agonists are diverse compounds without previously reported ADRB1 activity. The 16 known ADRB1 agonists are known to cause cardiovascular toxicity or show cardiotoxic potential (Kumari et al., 2023), while others showed minimal or no significant cardiovascular toxicity. Fig. 6 shows the structures and concentration response curves of representative novel potent ADRB1 agonists (panels A-C) and antagonists (panels D—F) in the ADRB1 confirmation assays. The most potent novel ADRB1 agonists were NCGC00018295–07 (EC50 = 0.047 ± 0.013 μM), NCGC00598758–01 (EC50 = 0.068 ± 0.032 μM), and NCGC00015825–05 (EC50 = 0.104 ± 0.018 μM) (Fig. 6 A-C). Similarly, 46 out of the 52 ADRB1 antagonists remained active in the ADRB1 confirmation assay, yielding a confirmation rate of 88.5 %. Of the 46 ADRB1 antagonists confirmed in the ADRB1 follow-up assay, eight (17.4 %) compounds showed potent ADRB1 antagonism in the assay with IC50 < 5 μM, with three (6.5 %) ADRB1 antagonists showing IC50 < 1 μM. Among which, five compounds (10.87 %), including landiolol, metoprolol succinate, atenolol, sotalol, and lisuride maleate, were previously reported to have cardioprotective effects, while the rest have no evidence of cardio protection (Takahashi et al., 2007). Out of the 46 confirmed ADRB1 antagonists, four compounds (landiolol, metoprolol, sotalol and atenolol) are known ADRB1 antagonists, while other antagonists were diverse compounds without previously reported ADRB1 activity. All four known ADRB1 antagonists are clinically validated for cardio protection, especially in managing arrhythmias, reducing cardiac workload, and preventing complications post-heart attack (Kleinbongard, 2020). The most potent novel ADRB1 antagonist was NCGC00597474–01 (IC50 = 0.944 ± 0.125 μM). Other examples of confirmed novel ADRB1 antagonists include NCGC00428192–01 (IC50 = 1.460 ± 0.562 μM), NCGC00427697–01 (IC50 = 4.146 ± 1.213 μM), and NCGC00426977–01 (IC50 = 6.105 ± 3.065 μM) (Fig. 6 D–F). The experimental validation results demonstrate the capacity of the ML models in identifying potential ADRB1 targeting compounds.

Discussion

Both ADRB1 and ADRB2 play essential roles in regulating heart function through the β-adrenergic signaling pathway, with ADRB1 primarily affecting heart rate and myocardial contractility, while ADRB2 influences vascular tone and cardiac function. Aberrant activation or inhibition of either receptor by cardiotoxic compounds can disrupt normal cardiovascular functions, leading to potential cardiotoxic effects. The overlapping roles of ADRB1 and ADRB2 in maintaining heart function suggest that compounds targeting either receptor could have similar cardiotoxicity effects, particularly in terms of arrhythmias and disturbances in myocardial contractility (Battipaglia and Lanza, 2014; Gordan et al., 2015). The current study found a substantial overlap between the compounds that modulate the two β-ARs based on the screening results of the Tox21 10 K compound library against the ADRB1 and ADRB2 assays. Specifically, among the 131 ADRB1 agonists and 153 ADRB2 antagonists in the Tox21 10 K compound library, 31 compounds (23.66 % of ADRB1 agonists and 20.26 % of ADRB2 antagonists) had overlapping activity profiles. Moreover, by comparing the ADRB activities of cardiotoxic and non-toxic compounds, we found that some ADRB1 agonists or ADRB2 antagonists are likely to induce cardiotoxicity. These results are consistent with literature reports (Baker, 2014).

The hERG (human ether-à-go-go-related gene) encodes a potassium channel responsible for the repolarization phase of the cardiac action potential. Inhibition of the hERG channel can lead to prolonged repolarization, resulting in QT interval prolongation, which increases the risk of arrhythmias such as Torsades de Pointes (Sanguinetti and Tristani-Firouzi, 2006). hERG inhibition is another common mechanism leading to cardiotoxicity. ADRB modulation and hERG inhibition can independently contribute to the development of arrhythmia. ADRB receptor antagonists or agonists can disrupt normal heart rhythm, while hERG inhibitors can lead to prolonged repolarization and arrhythmic potential. While ADRB1 activation can influence heart rate and contractility, the direct interaction with hERG channels is less pronounced, resulting in a more limited overlap in their cardiotoxic mechanisms. In contrast, both ADRB2 antagonism and hERG inhibition can contribute to arrhythmias, and compounds targeting both mechanisms may have a higher potential for exacerbating cardiac toxicity, particularly in terms of altered repolarization and rhythm (Wang et al., 2015). Some β-blockers like carvedilol have hERG channel blocking effects and can regulate hERG channels via both direct channel binding and β-receptor antagonism (Kawakami et al., 2006). In this study, we compared the activity profiles of cardiotoxic compounds in the Tox21 10 K library against the ADRB receptors and hERG to investigate the relationship between these cardiotoxicity targets. We found that the hERG inhibitors were largely distinct from the ADRB modulators, 62.93 % of which did not show any activity against the ADRB targets. The hERG inhibitors are structurally distinct from the ADRB modulators as well. Among the small fraction of hERG inhibitors that were also active in the ADRB assays, more compounds acted as ADRB2 antagonists than ADRB1 agonists. These findings suggest that while some ADRB modulators may inhibit the function of the hERG channel, hERG inhibition and ADRB modulation are most likely independent mechanisms that lead to cardiotoxicity, and this consideration aligns with previous reports in the literature (Kawakami et al., 2006).

In addition, we identified chemical features of ADRB1 agonists that are more likely to induce cardiotoxicity. The most significant feature found is “bond: COC_ether_aliphatic__aromatic” (p = 0.006). Example compounds in the Tox21 library that contain this feature include oxyfedrine, pindolol, celiprolol, and carteolol. Oxyfedrine, a partial β-AR agonist, is used in the treatment of angina pectoris, heart failure, conduction defects, and myocardial infarction. It is non-selective between β1- and β2-ARs and, while intended to support cardiovascular health, can cause paradoxical effects, such as exacerbation of angina or arrhythmias in rare cases (Kaski et al., 1991). Some β-blockers, including pindolol, celiprolol, and carteolol, possess intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA), allowing them to partially stimulate β receptors while blocking them, leading to a mild stimulating effect (Ishikura et al., 2012). Pindolol, a β-receptor blocking agent, is used to manage heart rate and blood pressure during submaximal and maximal exercise, with no reported deaths from overdose, though isolated cases of tachycardia and hypertension have been noted. Celiprolol, a β1-selective AR blocker, is primarily used for hypertension and chronic stable angina, with off-label uses in arrhythmias and heart failure. Carteolol, an ophthalmic solution, is used to treat open-angle glaucoma and other causes of high intraocular pressure; its overdosage can lead to bradycardia, bronchospasm, congestive heart failure, and hypotension (Gundersen et al., 2021).

Two other chemical features, i.e., “bond:COH_alcohol_aromatic” (p = 0.026) and “bond:COH_alcohol_aromatic_phenol” (p = 0.026), were also found significantly enriched in ADRB1 agonists that induce cardiotoxicity. Example compounds containing these features include isoprenaline, dopexamine, and isoxsuprine (Charlton and Thompson, 2022). Isoprenaline is a selective β-AR agonist that can activate both β1- and β2-receptors, which increases heart rate and myocardial contractility. However, isoprenaline has been shown to cause ischemic myocardial necrosis in animal models due to its exaggerated cardiovascular effects (Ojha et al., 2011). Dopexamine, a synthetic catecholamine, predominantly acts on β2-receptors with lesser effects on β1 and dopaminergic sites, leading to smooth muscle relaxation in the renal and mesenteric arterial beds. It enhances cardiac output by increasing heart rate and inotropy, while reducing afterload through β2-mediated vasodilation, and is often used to mitigate ischemia in sepsis and heart failure. Despite its beneficial effects in improving cardiac function and reducing ischemia, dopexamine can have cardiotoxic effects, especially when used in high doses or in certain patient populations (Bilir et al., 2006). Isoxsuprine, a β-adrenergic agonist used for treating cerebrovascular insufficiency and peripheral vascular diseases, also increases vasodilation in peripheral arterioles. Despite its clinical benefits in conditions like Raynaud’s disease and arteriosclerosis obliterans, isoxsuprine has been linked to an increased risk of cardiotoxicity, highlighting its potential adverse cardiovascular effects (Evron et al., 1983).

There were also compounds that acted as ADRB1 agonists without pronounced cardiotoxicity, such as dobutamine, droxidopa, epinephrine, and dopamine (Lessing et al., 2024). Dobutamine, a β1 agonist, is used to treat cardiac decompensation in patients with organic heart disease or after cardiac surgery and is associated with a much lesser increase in heart rate compared to dopamine (Cottin et al., 2000). Droxidopa is a prodrug that is taken up by sympathetic neurons and converted to norepinephrine, which then binds to α1 ARs to increase vasoconstriction and blood pressure; however, it may also increase tachycardia as it is converted to epinephrine, activating β-ARs (Kulshreshtha et al., 2020). Epinephrine, a sympathomimetic hormone and drug, binds to both α- and β-ARs with dose-dependent effects; low doses primarily activate β-receptors to enhance bronchodilation and cardiac activity, while higher doses engage α-receptors to induce vasoconstriction and increase vascular tone (Lou et al., 2024). Dopamine exhibits positive inotropic activity and binds to α1- and β1-ARs. Through β1 receptor activation in the myocardium, dopamine increases heart rate and contractility, enhancing cardiac output (Aslanoglou et al., 2021).

Finally, we applied four ML models including RF, SVM, NB, and XGB to virtually screen a diverse library of ~360 K compounds for the prediction of ADRB1 agonists and antagonists. The ML models trained on the Tox21 10 K compound library performed well with AUC-ROCs ranging from 0.83 to 0.89 for ADRB1 agonist prediction, and 0.74 to 0.88 for ADRB1 antagonist prediction. Previous studies have also identified potential small chemical entities through computational screening of ~99 million chemical compounds, which can possibly and strongly interact with both β1- or β2-AR, or used ML models to predict ADRB actives (Islam et al., 2021; Chang et al., 2025). The model predicted actives were then experimentally validated using the ADRB1 assay. Compared with the qHTS assays used for constructing the ML models, the optimal models increased the assay hit rates by 15-fold for ADRB1 agonist and 5-fold for ADRB1 antagonist. The ADRB1 follow-up assay had a confirmation rate of 93.3 % for agonists and 85.5 % for antagonists. Overall, the models identified 194 ADRB1 agonists and 46 ADRB1 antagonists that were experimentally confirmed including a few novel potent ADRB1 agonists/antagonists. Among the confirmed compounds, 13.92 % of the ADRB1 agonists have documented cardiotoxic potential and 10.87 % of the ADRB1 antagonists are known to have cardioprotective effects. The experimental validation results show that ML models were able to effectively identify potential ADRB1 targeting compounds. The results from this study are consistent with literature reports that ADRB1 agonists and antagonists are likely to show opposite cardio effects. In addition, clinical evidence from the literature confirms the cardiotoxicity potential of 37 of the 141 experimentally validated ADRB1 actives identified by our ADRB1 prediction model (Supplementary Table 4). However, a key limitation of this study is that while the predicted ADRB1 activities were experimentally confirmed, no additional in vitro cardiotoxicity assays were performed to directly validate the predicted safety profiles of these compounds due to resource limitations. The cardiotoxicity assessments made in this study relied on literature reports rather than new toxicological experiments. Future studies should aim to experimentally validate cardiotoxicity predictions using relevant cellular or animal models to strengthen the translational potential of these findings. Lastly, this study focused only on ADRB1/2 and hERG-dependent cardiotoxicity, while future investigations should explore cardiotoxic effects arising from other mechanisms or pathways.

Conclusion

This study presents the first large-scale, systematic analysis of compound activity profiles against β-ARs ADRB1 and ADRB2 in relation to cardiotoxicity. The results indicate that both hERG inhibition and ADRB modulation are associated with cardiotoxicity, while ADRB1 agonists and ADRB2 antagonists are more likely to cause cardiotoxicity compared to ADRB1 antagonists and ADRB2 agonists, which may have cardioprotective effects. The Tox21 10 K compound library contains more hERG inhibitors than ADRB modulators, and the hERG inhibitors are structurally distinct from the ADRB modulators. There was substantial overlap between the ADRB1 and ADRB2 activity profiles, while the activity overlap between the β-ARs and hERG was minimal. In addition, we identified chemical features of ADRB1 agonists that are more likely to induce cardiotoxicity. Finally, machine learning models were developed for the prediction of ADRB1 modulators. The models were applied to predict the ADRB1 activity of approximately 360 K compounds, with the highest-ranked compounds selected for experimental validation. Notably, 43.12 % of the cardiotoxic compounds in the Tox21 library did not show any activity in either the hERG or ADRB assays, suggesting other mechanisms for these compounds and the complexity of cardiotoxicity. Nevertheless, the findings from this study can aid the further understanding of the molecular mechanisms behind drug induced cardiotoxicity and help with the design of safer therapeutic agents.
