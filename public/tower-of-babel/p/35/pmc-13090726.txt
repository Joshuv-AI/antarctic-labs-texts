Recent advances in immunoregulatory biomaterials for tendon healing: From immune remodeling to functional regeneration
Authors: Jinbo Zhang, Qing Han, Xue Zhao, Xingzhen Li, Aobo Zhang, Yang Liu, Jincheng Wang, Hao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTendon injury is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder characterized by delayed healing, functional impairment, and diminished quality of life. Tendon repair is a dynamic process driven by immune regulation of tissue remodeling.This review summarizes the immunological mechanisms underlying tendon healing. In the early inflammatory phase, neutrophils and M1 macrophages trigger tissue degradation through TNF-α and IL-1β mediated NF-κB and MAPK pathways.As healing progresses, M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells promote inflammation resolution and extracellular matrix reconstruction via IL-10 and TGF-β signaling, thereby driving the transition from inflammation to regeneration. Moreover, this review highlights biomaterial-based immunomodulatory strategies for tendon repair, focusing on how material composition, surface topography, and physical cues regulate immune responses, and how bioactive molecules modulate macrophage polarization and T-cell homeostasis to promote inflammation resolution and regeneration. Furthermore, emerging strategies including chemical modification, physical stimulation (electrical, mechanical, magnetic), and intelligent responsive systems (pH, ROS, inflammatory cues) highlight the potential of integrating multiple immunomodulatory mechanisms into biomaterial design to synergistically regulate inflammation resolution, matrix remodeling, and collagen organization for functional tendon regeneration.Keywords: Tendon repair, Immunomodulation, Biomaterials, Macrophage polarization, Intelligent responsive systems

Introduction

Tendons are critical musculoskeletal tissues that connect muscles to bones and transmit muscle-generated forces [1,2]. However, their hypovascularity, low cellularity, and limited metabolic activity confer poor intrinsic healing capacity [3]. Mild or partial tears typically heal through a fibrotic response in which newly formed collagen, predominantly type III, remains disorganized, resulting in reduced mechanical strength and incomplete functional recovery [4]. In contrast, large or complete ruptures rarely heal spontaneously and generally require surgical repair [5]. Current treatment options including direct suturing, autografting, and allografting possess characteristic advantages and limitations intrinsic to their application [6,7]. Direct suturing is effective only for small defects, as large-gap repairs frequently lead to re-rupture [8]. Autografts offer superior biocompatibility but are restricted by donor-site morbidity and limited tissue availability, whereas allografts can alleviate material shortages but pose risks of immune rejection and disease transmission [[9], [10], [11]]. Consequently, biomaterials such as synthetic polymers, tissue-engineered scaffolds, and composite constructs have been extensively investigated to provide mechanical support and structural templates that facilitate vascularization and tissue regeneration [12]. Nevertheless, conventional biomaterials are often perceived as foreign bodies by the host immune system, leading to excessive inflammatory responses that hinder their integration with surrounding tissues. As a result, early biomaterial designs prioritized immune-evasive bioinertness, which minimized acute reactions but offered limited capacity to actively modulate the immune microenvironment required for functional regeneration [13,14].

Growing insights into regenerative immunology have revealed that well orchestrated and timely immune activation is indispensable for initiating tissue repair [15]. Tendon healing is classically divided into three highly overlapping phases-inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling-although recent studies emphasize a more continuous and dynamic progression. A robust early inflammatory phase facilitates the clearance of necrotic tissue and triggers subsequent repair, whereas the transition to the proliferative and remodeling phases determines the ultimate quality of tendon regeneration [16,17]. Macrophages, broadly categorized into pro-inflammatory M1-like and pro-regenerative M2-like phenotypes, play central roles in directing the progression of tendon healing [18]. During the early phase, M1 macrophages predominantly produce pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), along with upregulated inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and nitric oxide (NO) production. These signals shape the local inflammatory milieu and coordinate the recruitment and activation of immune effector cells, thereby supporting the clearance of damaged tissue and the initiation of repair; however, prolonged M1 activation may sustain inflammation and promote fibrotic scar formation [19]. Subsequently, M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative factors including interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which attenuate inflammatory signaling and modulate stromal and vascular responses to promote collagen organization and angiogenesis, ultimately facilitating tendon regeneration [20]. Emerging evidence suggests that regulatory T cells (Tregs) help restrain excessive inflammation partly via IL-33 related signaling and indirectly support M2-like macrophage maintenance and functional tissue regeneration [21].This mechanistic understanding has motivated the development of biomaterials capable of actively modulating local immune responses to establish a pro-regenerative microenvironment [22]. Consequently, the concept of immunomodulatory biomaterials has emerged, aiming to shift the paradigm from passively reducing immune rejection toward actively guiding immune-mediated tissue regeneration and functional tendon restoration.

Building on this concept, increasing research has focused on immunomodulatory biomaterials that can actively guide immune responses through the rational design of material composition, surface topography, and functional modifications [23,24]. Accumulating evidence indicates that these physicochemical cues substantially influence immune-cell behavior and phenotype transitions [25,26]. In the context of tendon healing, representative strategies include engineering biomimetic scaffold architectures to modulate macrophage adhesion/activation and bias the immune milieu toward pro-regenerative remodeling [27], incorporating immunoregulatory ions (e.g., Mg2+) to attenuate excessive inflammation and support more organized matrix reconstruction [28], and using hydrogel-based delivery platforms to provide sustained local presentation of bioactive cargos (exosomes or growth factors) that help maintain a reparative immune microenvironment conducive to tendon repair [29]. Collectively, these approaches highlight the central premise of this review: biomaterials can be designed not only as structural supports but also as immune-instructive interfaces to coordinate the inflammatory-to-remodeling transition required for functional tendon regeneration.

This review aimed to delineate the key immunoregulatory mechanisms during tendon healing, emphasizing the roles and interactions of immune cells including neutrophils, macrophages, T cells, and B cells in inflammation resolution and tissue remodeling. We also summarize recent advances in immunomodulatory biomaterials, covering material selection and compositional modulation, structural and topological optimization, bioactive molecule loading and delivery, physical stimulation regulation, and intelligent responsiveness, with discussions on underlying mechanisms and future directions toward functional tendon regeneration.

Tendon injury and repair process

Tendon injuries are primarily caused by acute mechanical overload or traumatic events such as excessive stretching, sudden acceleration, or direct impact [30]. Under conditions of overuse or repetitive microtrauma, collagen fibers within the tendon may rupture and become disorganized, accompanied by local inflammation that compromises the structural integrity and mechanical continuity of the tissue [31]. Following injury, tendon healing generally occurs through two distinct but sometimes overlapping mechanisms: intrinsic healing and extrinsic healing. The relative contribution of each depends on the injury site, severity, and postoperative loading conditions. Intrinsic healing typically occurs in mild injuries when the epitenon remains intact, with repair cells mainly originating from the endotenon and tendon proper. This process often results in better mechanical strength and fewer adhesions [32]. In contrast, extrinsic healing takes place when the epitenon is disrupted, and the repair cells are derived from the paratenon, surrounding sheath, and circulating blood cells [33,34]. Because most severe complications, such as adhesion formation, are closely associated with extrinsic healing, research has primarily focused on this process, which progresses through three overlapping phases: inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling (Fig. 1B).

Inflammation phase

Following tendon injury, the inflammatory phase is immediately initiated, representing the earliest stage of the repair process and typically lasting several days [35]. The injury leads to vascular rupture and hematoma formation, which triggers local platelet aggregation and the rapid release of various chemotactic and growth factors, including PDGF, TGF-β, and VEGF [36]. These molecular signals increase vascular permeability and create chemotactic cues for immune cell recruitment, promoting the rapid migration of neutrophils and monocytes to the injury site [37]. Neutrophils arrive within hours after injury and clear necrotic tissue and cellular debris through the release of reactive oxygen species, proteases, and cytokines, while the inflammatory mediators they produce further amplify the local inflammatory response [38]. Recent studies have shown that neutrophils not only participate in the early clearance of necrotic cells and pathogens but also play a complex role in modulating later immune responses and tissue repair. They regulate macrophage polarization by releasing lysosomal enzymes and cytokines, promoting the transition toward an M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype, which is crucial for effective removal of necrotic tissue [39]. In addition, neutrophils interact with vascular endothelial cells to influence local angiogenesis and support new blood vessel formation, ensuring sufficient perfusion for subsequent repair [40,41]. As inflammation progresses, monocytes migrate from the circulation into the injured tendon and differentiate into macrophages upon recognizing damage-associated molecular patterns [42]. These macrophages display an M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype and secrete cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 to sustain local immune activation and enhance tissue clearance [43]. Meanwhile, the activation of matrix metalloproteinases accelerates the degradation of the damaged extracellular matrix, creating space for cell migration and deposition of new ECM [44]. In the later stage of inflammation, as necrotic tissue is gradually removed, the inflammatory response stabilizes and a subset of fibroblasts is recruited to the injury site to synthesize limited ECM components, forming a preliminary scaffold that establishes a microenvironment conducive to the subsequent proliferative phase [45]. This transition from inflammatory clearance to matrix deposition marks the progression of tendon repair into a new stage and provides essential conditions for neovascularization and tendon cell migration.

Proliferation phase

As inflammation gradually subsides, tendon repair enters the proliferative phase, which typically occurs within one to several weeks after injury and represents a critical stage determining the quantity and quality of the regenerated tissue [46,47]. During this period, tenocytes, fibroblasts, and tendon-derived stem cells (TDSCs) rapidly proliferate and migrate into the injury site, producing large amounts of extracellular matrix (ECM) predominantly composed of disorganized type III collagen that forms a temporary scar-like tissue to fill the defect [48,49]. The newly formed tissue exhibits a fibrotic appearance characterized by thin, loosely arranged collagen fibers that markedly differ from the highly aligned, parallel bundles of native tendons [50]. Meanwhile, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and fibroblasts recruited from surrounding tissues secrete various growth factors such as TGF-β, IGF-1 and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), which activate the tendon repair microenvironment [48]. These signaling cues induce the expression of tendon-lineage-related genes, including Scleraxis and Tenomodulin, thereby driving MSC differentiation into mature tenocytes and progressively establishing tendon-specific cellular architecture [51]. Angiogenesis also plays an essential role during this stage. Newly formed capillaries develop within the repair tissue, improving oxygen and nutrient supply while secreting pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF to further regulate vascular formation [52]. ECM synthesis and angiogenesis occur in a coordinated manner, as increased vascular density is often accompanied by enhanced collagen deposition [53]. Concurrently, the dynamic balance between MMPs and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) governs ECM degradation and redeposition, maintaining a plastic matrix that facilitates the subsequent conversion of type III collagen into type I collagen and its ordered alignment [54]. In rat tendon injury models, the upregulation of VEGF correlates with increased ECM protein expression during early repair, suggesting a close relationship between angiogenesis and matrix reconstruction [55]. Both angiogenesis and type III collagen deposition peak around two to three weeks post-injury, showing temporal consistency. Overall, ECM synthesis and angiogenesis during the proliferative phase occur as tightly coupled processes whose interplay directly influences the mechanical properties and functional restoration achieved during the subsequent remodeling phase.

Remodeling phase

As the repair process progresses, tendon healing enters the remodeling phase, which can last for several months or even longer and represents the key period determining the ultimate quality and mechanical performance of the repaired tissue [47]. During this stage, type III collagen is gradually replaced by type I collagen, accompanied by an increase in collagen fibril diameter, enhanced crosslinking, and the alignment of fibers into parallel bundles, all of which contribute to improved tissue strength and resilience [35]. As cellular synthetic activity declines, the extracellular matrix (ECM) undergoes gradual reorganization under mechanical loading and cellular traction, resulting in a more uniform fiber orientation that restores the hierarchical structure and mechanical integrity of the tendon. The balance between MMPs and TIMPs remains crucial in this phase, ensuring the appropriate degradation of old ECM while sustaining new matrix deposition, thereby preventing structural abnormalities and excessive fibrosis [56]. If early inflammation is not adequately resolved, the newly formed tissue often exhibits disorganized collagen deposition, scar formation, and irregular fiber arrangement, ultimately compromising the mechanical performance of the repaired tendon [57]. Notably, tendons in infants and young children often display a near-scarless regenerative healing pattern, where both tendon and skin tissues can achieve almost complete structural and functional restoration under a relatively regenerative immune environment [58]. This phenomenon is closely associated with their immature immune system, attenuated inflammatory response, and ECM remodeling patterns favoring regeneration [59]. In contrast, adult tendon healing typically follows a reparative rather than a regenerative trajectory. The scar-like ECM formed during the proliferative phase is never fully replaced; even during the remodeling phase, the conversion of type III collagen to type I collagen remains incomplete, resulting in tissue that is structurally and mechanically inferior to native tendon [60]. This limitation is primarily attributed to a more intense and prolonged inflammatory response, excessive early scar deposition, reduced regenerative potential of fibroblasts and tendon-derived stem cells with age, and insufficient efficiency in collagen crosslinking and fiber reorganization [61]. Consequently, tendon healing in adults rarely restores the native architecture and function, leading to a scar-mediated repair rather than true regeneration.

Immune cells related to tendon repair

During tendon injury and repair, immune cells exert regulatory functions at different stages, with their effects broadly classified into the innate and adaptive immune systems [62]. The innate immune response is rapidly activated within hours to days after injury, serving as the first line of defense in the repair process [63]. Neutrophils are the earliest responders that infiltrate the injury site, where they phagocytose necrotic debris and release ROS and proteolytic enzymes to clear damaged tissue; however excessive or prolonged neutrophil activity can aggravate tissue injury [64]. Subsequently, circulating monocytes migrate to the lesion area and differentiate into macrophages, which initially exhibit a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, secreting cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β to amplify inflammation and activate downstream repair pathways. As the repair progresses, macrophages gradually shift toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype that secretes IL-10, TGF-β, and VEGF, facilitating inflammation resolution, angiogenesis, and collagen organization, thereby establishing a microenvironment favorable for the proliferative and remodeling phases (Fig. 2A) [65,66]. In contrast, the adaptive immune response becomes engaged after the initiation of innate immunity, exerting more refined control over the amplitude and duration of inflammation. Among adaptive immune cells, T cells play a pivotal role. CD4+ helper T cells regulate macrophage polarization and fibroblast activity through the secretion of multiple cytokines [67], while Tregs suppress excessive immune activation via IL-10, IL-33, and PD-1 signaling pathways, maintaining homeostasis within the repair microenvironment and indirectly promoting the organized reconstruction of ECM [21,68]. Furthermore, B cells have been implicated in chronic tendinopathies, where they contribute to persistent inflammation and immune complex deposition, potentially leading to repair failure and fibrosis [69,70]. Collectively, innate immunity initiates inflammation and clears necrotic tissue to pave the way for repair, whereas adaptive immunity fine-tunes inflammation resolution and tissue remodeling in later stages. The coordinated interplay between these two immune systems ultimately determines the quality and outcome of tendon healing (Table 1).

Neutrophils

Neutrophils, which develop from myeloid progenitor cells in the bone marrow, are the first immune cells to arrive at injured tendon tissue following injury [64]. Platelets and chemotactic factors released from the damaged matrix, such as members of the CXCL family and IL-8, together with complement fragments (C3a and C5a), rapidly recruit and activate neutrophils, which then recognize damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) through Toll-like receptors (TLRs) to initiate downstream inflammatory cascades [38]. Recent studies have further highlighted the multifaceted roles of neutrophils in tissue repair. Beyond their early function in clearing necrotic debris and pathogens, neutrophils contribute to immune regulation, tissue repair, and angiogenesis. They promote neovascularization through interactions with vascular endothelial cells, ensuring an adequate blood supply for the repair process [72,86]. In addition, neutrophils modulate macrophage polarization through direct interactions, influencing subsequent immune responses and the progression of tissue repair [71,73]. The interplay between neutrophils and macrophages not only drives the local immune response but also shapes the immunotolerant characteristics of the healing microenvironment. Key neutrophil effector functions include phagocytosis of necrotic fragments, release of reactive oxygen species and proteases, secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and, when required, the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [87]. In the early stage of tendon injury, neutrophils and NETs typically exhibit a transient peak, followed by a rapid decline in neutrophil numbers, which facilitates the recruitment of monocytes and the initiation of the repair process [74]. However, excessive or persistent NET formation can sustain pro-inflammatory signaling, maintain M1 macrophage polarization, increase the risk of fibrosis, and delay healing [88,89]. Therefore, the spatial and temporal regulation of neutrophil activity and NET formation is critical, ensuring efficient short-term action while preventing detrimental overactivation. Excessive NETs can impede repair, leading to chronic inflammation and fibrosis, and recent evidence suggests that their interaction with wound inflammation under mechanical load may trigger prolonged immune responses, indicating that an early peak followed by rapid resolution is the optimal trajectory for repair [71]. Furthermore, neutrophils continue to influence later stages of healing by modulating the local immune environment. Their interactions with macrophages, T cells, and other immune cells affect the duration and intensity of immune responses, thereby regulating tissue regeneration. In the later phase of inflammation, neutrophils contribute to immune cell polarization, further shaping the immune microenvironment and promoting tendon repair. Overall, neutrophils play a central role in the immune response following tendon injury, not only initiating immunity through the clearance of damaged tissue and pathogens but also facilitating repair by precisely regulating immune activity, promoting angiogenesis, and interacting with other immune cells. The regulation of neutrophils and NETs is essential for controlling fibrosis and optimizing healing, and future research should focus on strategies to finely tune these responses to enhance tendon repair outcomes.

Macrophages

Macrophages originate from circulating monocytes and participate in all stages of tendon injury repair, including inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling, through directed recruitment and differentiation [90]. Studies have shown that selective depletion of macrophages in Achilles tendon suture models reduces cell proliferation and the formation of new extracellular matrix, impairing the mechanical properties of the healing tissue, which underscores the indispensable role of macrophages in the early repair phase of tendon injury [65]. A defining feature of macrophages is their phenotypic plasticity, allowing them to dynamically switch between the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in response to microenvironmental cues. This phenotypic transition is critical for collagen deposition, fiber alignment, and ultimately the mechanical integrity of the repaired tendon [75]. Macrophage plasticity not only determines their function during the inflammatory response but also directly influences the quality and rate of tendon healing by modulating immune responses and guiding tissue remodeling throughout the repair process.

Macrophage plasticity

Macrophages exhibit a high degree of phenotypic and functional plasticity, enabling rapid and dynamic responses to diverse microenvironmental cues. During different stages of tendon repair, the polarization and relative proportions of macrophages adjust in accordance with changes in the local microenvironment [91]. Their functional heterogeneity is influenced not only by cytokines but also by physical and chemical signals within the injury site [75]. Local hypoxia, mechanical stimuli, and alterations in extracellular matrix (ECM) composition can modulate macrophage polarization through specific signaling pathways, thereby affecting their role in tissue repair [76]. In the early phase of tendon injury, M1 macrophages dominate, exerting pro-inflammatory effects, clearing necrotic tissue, and regulating local immune responses through the secretion of cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β.

This early inflammatory activity facilitates the initiation of repair and establishes a microenvironment conducive to subsequent regeneration. However, excessive or prolonged activation of M1 macrophages can lead to over-degradation and fibrosis, impeding normal tissue repair and delaying the proliferative and maturation phases [92]. Therefore, the early response of M1 macrophages is critical, but its intensity and duration must be precisely controlled to prevent adverse outcomes. As repair progresses, particularly during the late inflammatory phase, M2 macrophages gradually increase and play a key role in the proliferative and remodeling stages. M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β, which suppress excessive inflammation, promote angiogenesis, facilitate ECM reconstruction, and support the resolution of inflammation, thereby enabling tissue repair and regeneration [77]. Phenotypic conversion to M2 macrophages is essential for tendon healing, as these cells promote type III collagen deposition and neovascularization, providing a stable microenvironment for tissue remodeling. During the proliferative phase, M2 macrophages also regulate the balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), facilitating the transition from type III to type I collagen and ultimately restoring tendon structure and mechanical function [93]. Thus, the dynamic plasticity of macrophages is driven not only by external signals but also by the specific demands of each repair stage. While M1 macrophages establish the foundation for early repair, the role of M2 macrophages becomes increasingly critical as healing advances. Microenvironmental factors such as local hypoxia, mechanical load, and ECM composition directly influence macrophage polarization, making the precise regulation of macrophage plasticity and the balanced transition from M1 to M2 essential for accelerating functional tendon repair and regeneration.

Pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages

The pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype typically predominates during the early inflammatory phase of tendon repair and is driven by the NF-κB/STAT1 signaling axis. Upon recognition of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), M1 macrophages release TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and nitric oxide, while upregulating MMP-9 and MMP-13 to cooperatively clear damaged extracellular matrix (ECM) [94]. A brief but robust M1 response facilitates the removal of necrotic material and prepares the tissue for subsequent repair [95], however, excessive duration or intensity of pro-inflammatory activity can disrupt inflammation resolution, leading to over-degradation, disordered collagen deposition, fibrosis, and impaired efficiency of later proliferative and maturation phases [78]. In Achilles tendon transection models, early interventions that reduce mature M1 macrophages compromise the mechanical properties of the healing tissue, highlighting that although M1 activity is pro-inflammatory, its moderate activation is essential for initiating repair and ECM remodeling, and excessive suppression may delay healing [96]. During tendon injury and repair, M1 macrophage function exhibits high plasticity, with polarization closely influenced by the local microenvironment. Factors such as hypoxia, mechanical stress, and ECM composition modulate macrophage polarization, and the timely transition from M1 to M2 is critical [97]. Failure to appropriately regulate M1 activity or delayed conversion to the M2 phenotype can result in fibrosis and disorganized collagen deposition, negatively affecting functional tendon recovery [98]. Therefore, precise regulation of the dynamic balance between M1 and M2 phenotypes, ensuring effective macrophage plasticity, represents a key strategy for promoting functional tendon regeneration.

Anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages

During tendon repair, anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages gradually increase, primarily in the late inflammatory phase and after the tissue enters the proliferative stage, with their differentiation driven by cytokines such as IL-4, IL-13, and IL-10 [66,79]. These macrophages typically secrete anti-inflammatory and reparative factors, including IL-10, TGF-β, VEGF, and IGF-1, which suppress excessive inflammation, promote angiogenesis, and facilitate extracellular matrix (ECM) reconstruction [80,99]. In the early proliferative phase, M2 macrophages inhibit pro-inflammatory signals while promoting type III collagen deposition and neovascularization, thereby establishing a stable microenvironment for subsequent tissue remodeling [100]. During the late proliferative and remodeling phases, they maintain low inflammatory homeostasis and regulate the balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), enabling the replacement of type III with type I collagen and the organized alignment of collagen fibers, gradually restoring tendon structure and mechanical function [93,101]. M2 macrophages are not a homogeneous population and can be further classified into subtypes, including M2a, M2b, M2c, and M2d. M2a macrophages, induced by IL-4/IL-13 signaling, promote angiogenesis and ECM synthesis; M2b macrophages participate in immunoregulation, maintaining a balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signals; M2c macrophages, regulated by IL-10, primarily mediate inflammation resolution and tissue remodeling; and M2d macrophages are closely associated with VEGF expression and angiogenesis [18,102] (Fig. 2B). The dynamic interconversion of these subtypes constitutes a continuous immunoregulatory process, enabling a smooth transition from inflammation to regeneration. Experimental studies have demonstrated that enhancing M2 macrophage polarization is closely linked to improved tendon repair outcomes. In flexor tendon injury models, COX-targeting siRNA induced M2 polarization, resulting in significantly increased tensile strength and reduced adhesion of the healing tendon [81]. In rotator cuff and tendon–bone interface repair studies, mechanical stimulation activating the IL-4/JAK/STAT pathway promoted tissue integration and functional recovery [82]. Collectively, these effects facilitate the structural and functional reconstruction of newly formed tendon tissue (Fig. 3). Therefore, accelerating the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages can shorten the inflammatory phase, enhance angiogenesis and matrix remodeling, and improve the mechanical properties of the repaired tendon.

Natural killer cells

Natural killer (NK) cells are a critical component of the immune system with broad immunoregulatory functions, maintaining immune surveillance by recognizing and eliminating abnormal or damaged cells [83]. During tendon repair, NK cells play a dual role, contributing to tissue regeneration while potentially promoting excessive immune responses. NK cell activation typically occurs through interactions between their surface receptors and stress-induced ligands on target cells. Specifically, receptors such as NKG2D, NKp46, and NKp30 enable NK cells to recognize stress signals on the surface of injured cells, including molecules upregulated during tissue damage or infection, such as MICA/B and ULBP [84,85]. These signals trigger NK cells to release cytotoxic molecules, including perforin and granzymes, as well as cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, thereby clearing damaged cells and modulating the local immune environment. In the context of tendon repair, NK cells participate in the regulation of inflammation by modulating local immune responses [103]. They are first activated and recruited to the injury site, where, in addition to removing damaged cells, they secrete IFN-γ to promote macrophage polarization from the M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype toward the M2 reparative phenotype, which helps mitigate excessive inflammation and supports tissue repair and regeneration [104,105]. Furthermore, NK cells interact with T cells to further regulate immune responses, maintaining immune tolerance and balancing inflammatory activity [106,107]. However, excessive activation of NK cells can lead to an overly strong inflammatory response, impair tendon repair, and contribute to scar formation. Therefore, the immunoregulatory role of NK cells during tendon repair is a double-edged sword, and its effects depend on the precise control of the local immune response.

T cells and B cells

T cells, as key adaptive immune effectors, differentiate into CD4+ or CD8+ lineages. CD4+ T cells include Th1, Th2, Th17, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), with distinct subtypes exerting either beneficial or detrimental effects on tissue repair and regeneration [108,109]. CD4+ lineages modulate local immune and repair balance through cytokine networks of Th1/Th2/Th17 subgroups; while IFN-γ and IL-17 contribute to pathogen and debris clearance, excessive activity prolongs inflammation [68]. Tregs play a central role in resolving inflammation and establishing repair homeostasis by secreting IL-10 and IL-33 and via co-inhibitory pathways such as CTLA-4 and PD-1, suppressing overactivation of effector T cells and antigen-presenting cells [110,111]. Tregs also indirectly support M2 macrophage maintenance and enhance type I collagen synthesis and organized ECM deposition by tenocytes, bridging immune homeostasis and tissue remodeling [63]. Recent studies in rotator cuff healing highlight that the timing and magnitude of T cell responses correlate with clinical outcomes. Single-cell transcriptomics reveal that chronic tendinopathy is associated with reduced Treg numbers and persistent Th1/Th17 pathway activation, correlating with fibrosis severity [112]. CD8+ T cells, traditionally cytotoxic, also contribute to tendon repair: early activation can release IL-10 and granzyme, participating in immune resolution and tissue remodeling, indicating multilayered adaptive immune regulation during tendon healing [113]. Collectively, T cell phenotypic balance and temporal activation are critical for modulating inflammation, controlling fibrosis, and promoting functional regeneration, representing promising targets for immunomodulatory interventions in tendon repair. B cells are activated by antigen and helper T cell signals, differentiate into plasma cells, and participate in antibody production, immune complex clearance, and complement activation [114]. In tendons, B cells are more prominent during subacute to chronic phases, suggesting involvement in persistent low-grade inflammation [16]. Histological and systematic analyses in chronic tendinopathy samples show lymphocyte (including B cell) infiltration coexisting with macrophages and mast cells, consistent with an inflammatory microenvironment [115]. Compared with macrophages and T cells, experimental evidence for B cells in tendon repair remains limited, highlighting the need for further in vivo studies to clarify their roles in fibrosis and immune homeostasis.

Immunoregulatory biomaterial strategies for promoting tendon regeneration

Upon implantation of biomaterials into injured tendon tissue, the host recognizes the material as a foreign signal and initiates a complex and dynamic immune response. This process occurs through multiple stages, including protein adsorption, cellular recognition, inflammation, and immune regulation, ultimately determining the compatibility of the implanted material with host tissue and the outcome of repair. Immediately after implantation, the material surface is exposed to blood and tissue fluids, and plasma proteins such as albumin, fibronectin, immunoglobulins, and complement components C3 and C5 rapidly adsorb onto the surface, forming a dynamically evolving protein adsorption layer [116]. This layer not only defines the biointerface properties of the material but also exposes new binding epitopes through conformational changes in the adsorbed proteins, thereby triggering activation of the complement system and generating cleavage fragments such as C3a and C5a. These fragments strongly chemotactically recruit neutrophils and monocytes to the implantation site and induce vascular endothelial cells to express adhesion molecules, including ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, promoting immune cell extravasation and accumulation [117] (Fig. 4).

The design of immunomodulatory biomaterials aims to optimize tendon repair by precisely controlling immune responses. The physical properties of these materials, including surface topography, pore size, and porosity, play a critical role in shaping the immune response during tendon regeneration [118]. Materials with defined microstructures can modulate macrophage phenotype upon interaction with immune cells, promoting a shift from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [119]. M1 macrophages sustain local immune defense by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, but prolonged activation can inhibit tenocyte proliferation and promote scar formation. In contrast, M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory factors, including IL-10 and TGF-β, which support tissue repair and regeneration and facilitate the recruitment of tendon-derived stem cells (TDSCs) as well as the deposition of collagen types I and III [43]. Therefore, designing biomaterials with specific pore sizes and surface morphologies can effectively regulate macrophage polarization and optimize the repair microenvironment [120]. Chemical composition also represents an important strategy for immunomodulatory biomaterials. Surface modifications or intrinsic chemical adjustments allow materials to influence immune cell recognition and response. For example, functionalization with amino, hydroxyl, or negatively charged groups can enhance immune cell adhesion and interaction [121]. Loading bioactive molecules such as growth factors or exosomes enables precise regulation of local immune responses and promotes the restoration of immune homeostasis [122]. Growth factors like VEGF and TGF-β, which are critical for angiogenesis and tissue repair, can be incorporated into materials to stimulate vascularization and cell migration at the injury site, accelerating tendon regeneration [123]. In addition, physically stimulated and smart responsive materials provide further opportunities for immunomodulatory design. Materials responsive to external physical cues, including magnetic fields, mechanical forces, or electrical stimulation, can guide local immune cell recruitment and modulate their function. Mechanically responsive materials mimic the physical environment of tendons, influencing interactions between immune and reparative cells, promoting macrophage polarization, and accelerating tissue repair [124]. Smart responsive materials can adjust their properties in response to local environmental changes, such as pH or inflammatory factor concentrations, to meet dynamic immune demands [125]. The integration of these strategies enables immunomodulatory biomaterials to finely tune immune responses during tendon repair, achieving a coordinated balance between inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration.

Immunomodulatory roles of intrinsic properties of biomaterials

The intrinsic properties of biomaterials refer to their inherent physicochemical characteristics prior to any additional functional modification, including chemical composition, surface energy, charge state, morphology, stiffness, and degradation behavior. These factors collectively determine how biomaterials initially interact with the immune system, influencing protein adsorption, immune cell recognition, and downstream signal transduction, thereby governing the initiation of inflammation and the remodeling of the immune microenvironment [126]. Consequently, different classes of biomaterials with variations in chemical composition, degradation products, and interfacial compatibility with host tissues can elicit markedly distinct immune response profiles following implantation [127].

Composition and type dependent immunomodulation

Different types of biomaterials, owing to variations in their chemical composition, degradation behavior, and interactions with host tissues, can elicit markedly distinct immune response profiles after implantation [24]. The surface chemical functional groups, charge states, and degradation products of these materials influence protein adsorption and immune cell recognition, thereby modulating both the intensity and trajectory of the inflammatory response [128]. In general, natural polymer-based materials, owing to their intrinsic bioactivity and compositional similarity to native extracellular matrix (ECM), tend to induce mild immune reactions that favor tissue repair. Synthetic polymers, with tunable chemical structures and mechanical properties, enable precise modulation of immune responses through rational molecular design. Meanwhile, inorganic or ion-releasing materials regulate cellular signaling pathways via ion dissolution, thereby influencing macrophage polarization and remodeling of the immune microenvironment [129,130].

Natural polymer-based biomaterials

Scaffolds derived from natural polymers possess unique advantages, offering superior cell viability, adhesion, biocompatibility, and controlled degradability compared with other material types [130]. Consequently, these biomaterials hold great promise for tendon repair and regeneration. Common natural polymers employed in scaffold fabrication include collagen, alginate, hyaluronic acid, and silk fibroin [131].

Collagen

Collagen is the main component of tendon extracellular matrix (ECM), with type I collagen playing a central role in maintaining tissue structural integrity and mechanical properties, which has led to the widespread use of collagen-based scaffolds in tendon repair [132]. Collagen fibers exhibit excellent biocompatibility and support cell adhesion, making them a common foundational material for various tissue engineering scaffolds [133]. Upon implantation into injured tendon sites, collagen-based scaffolds elicit a mild immune response and demonstrate good host integration [134]. In addition, collagen can serve as a carrier for bioactive molecules or immunomodulatory agents [135]. For example, porous type I collagen scaffolds fabricated via 3D printing and loaded with exosomes have been shown in Achilles tendon injury models to significantly suppress excessive inflammation during the repair phase, enhance tenocyte proliferation, and promote type I collagen deposition, thereby accelerating tissue regeneration [136]. Beyond serving as a delivery platform, collagen itself possesses immunomodulatory properties. Its triple-helical structure contains sequences such as GFOGER and DGEA, which are recognized by α1β1 and α2β1 integrins, mediating immune cell adhesion and activating downstream signaling pathways that regulate macrophage polarization and influence the local inflammatory microenvironment and tissue regeneration [137]. The three-dimensional network and surface chemistry of collagen materials can directly affect macrophage polarization. Compared with two-dimensional coatings, 3D type I collagen gels not only promote M2 macrophage polarization and upregulate anti-inflammatory factors IL-10 and TGF-β, but also suppress the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β, thereby modulating M1 macrophage activity and establishing an immune microenvironment favorable for tissue repair [138]. In a rat Achilles tendon defect model, 3D collagen-rich composite scaffolds further demonstrated in vivo immunoregulatory potential. These scaffolds enhanced M2 macrophage infiltration, upregulated ARG-1 and CD163 expression locally, and significantly reduced CD68 and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, indicating both promotion of M2 polarization and inhibition of M1-mediated inflammation, which reduced scar formation and improved healing quality [139]. Moreover, collagen-based composite hydrogels also exhibited effective immunomodulatory and reparative effects in tendon injury models by suppressing early inflammatory responses and apoptosis while activating the AKT/GSK-3β signaling pathway to regulate collagen remodeling, thereby improving the mechanical properties of the repaired tissue [140].

Collagen-based biomaterials, owing to their excellent biocompatibility and integrin-binding motifs, can modulate inflammatory responses in a mild manner and induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby establishing an immune microenvironment conducive to tendon repair. Meanwhile, when employed as delivery platforms for bioactive molecules, they can synergistically enhance cell proliferation and extracellular matrix remodeling. However, the intrinsic limitations of natural collagen including poor mechanical strength, rapid degradation, and insufficient precision in immune regulation remain inadequate to meet the demands of functional tendon regeneration under high-load conditions. Future efforts should focus on molecular modification and composite design strategies to achieve synergistic optimization of immunomodulation and mechanical performance, ultimately advancing collagen-based systems toward intelligent regenerative platforms.

Alginate

Alginate, a naturally occurring anionic polysaccharide derived from brown algae, is composed of β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G) units and can form three-dimensional gel structures through egg-box ionic crosslinking in the presence of divalent ions such as Ca2+ [141]. This material exhibits excellent biocompatibility, injectability, and tunable mechanical properties, making it widely used for constructing biomimetic hydrogel scaffolds [141]. Due to its high water content and engineerable characteristics, alginate can serve as a carrier for cells or bioactive factors [142], providing a suitable regenerative microenvironment for injured tissue while modulating immune cell adhesion and polarization to achieve a dynamic balance between inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration during tendon repair [143]. The immunoregulatory effects of alginate-based scaffolds are multi-layered and temporally sequenced. Injectable alginate hydrogels can initially induce M1 macrophage activation during the early phase of tendon injury, which is a normal component of the inflammatory repair process, followed by promotion of M2 macrophage polarization to establish an anti-inflammatory microenvironment, enhance angiogenesis, and improve tissue continuity and mechanical properties. In Achilles tendon and rotator cuff tendon-bone interface repair models, alginate hydrogels have been shown to promote early M1 activation, subsequent M1 suppression, and enhanced M2 polarization, thereby facilitating tissue remodeling and repair [144]. Building on this, composite alginate scaffolds incorporating graphene oxide further strengthen early immunomodulation, accelerating the transition from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase, significantly suppressing excessive inflammation, reducing scar formation, and promoting organized reconstruction and mechanical reinforcement of the interface tissue. These findings indicate that alginate-based scaffolds can temporally regulate both M1 and M2 macrophage activity [145]. Alginate can also serve as a delivery platform for sustained release of bioactive molecules, effectively downregulating M1-associated pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-1β, upregulating IL-10 and TGF-β expression, and promoting type I collagen deposition and tendon-like tissue regeneration during the proliferative phase [146]. By modulating the immune microenvironment and delivering biological cues, alginate achieves a dynamic balance between inflammation and regeneration in tendon repair. However, limitations remain in terms of mechanical matching, cell adhesion, and degradation kinetics, and its full potential in functional tendon regeneration can only be realized when immunomodulation and tissue mechanics are synergistically optimized.

Silk fibroin

Silk fibroin (SF), produced by silkworms such as Bombyx mori, consists of alternating β-sheet crystalline and amorphous domains, which endow the material with tunable mechanical properties, excellent processability, and controllable degradability [147]. After degumming to remove sericin, SF exhibits low immunogenicity and induces only a mild inflammatory response upon implantation, demonstrating outstanding biocompatibility [148]. The surface morphology and micro/nanostructure of SF are highly designable, allowing its roughness, topography, and degradation behavior to act as programmable biophysical cues that regulate immune cell adhesion, migration, and polarization [149]. Silk fibroin films with defined micro-groove structures not only induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, upregulating anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, but also suppress M1 macrophage (CD68-positive) activity during the early phase of tendon repair, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and establishing an immune microenvironment conducive to tissue remodeling. In vivo Achilles tendon injury models demonstrated that these silk fibroin films significantly decreased M1 macrophage infiltration while increasing the proportion of M2 macrophages. Furthermore, by modulating cell–matrix interactions and cell adhesion molecule (CAM) signaling, the films promoted organized collagen fiber deposition and facilitated tendon regeneration (Fig. 5A) [150]. Patterned SF films containing microgrooves or micropillars further promote tendon-derived stem cell differentiation toward the tenogenic lineage through the α2β1/FAK signaling axis, resulting in enhanced cellular alignment, increased type I collagen deposition, and improved tissue continuity in vivo (Fig. 5B) [150,153]. Moreover, gradient or triphasic composite scaffolds based on SF, when combined with tendon-derived stem cells for tendon–bone interface reconstruction, facilitate the formation of more mature interface-like tissue and significantly enhance mechanical strength, highlighting the multifaceted synergistic role of silk fibroin in structural support, immune modulation, and tissue regeneration [154].

Synthetic polymer-based biomaterials

Synthetic polymeric materials widely used in tendon repair include poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL), poly(lactic acid) (PLA), and their copolymers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), as well as poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) based systems [111]. Although these polymers exhibit notable advantages in tissue engineering applications such as controllable degradability, favorable mechanical properties, and processability, they also present inherent limitations, including low bioactivity and insufficient cell recognition sites [137]. Therefore, strategies such as composite fabrication and surface functional modification are often employed to enhance their bioactivity and cell-interactive performance.

Polycaprolactone

Poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) is a semi crystalline and biodegradable synthetic polyester characterized by excellent processability, a moderate elastic modulus, and a slow degradation rate, making it a commonly used structural support material for tendon repair [155] PCL fibers can be fabricated into anisotropic architectures via electrospinning or melt-printing techniques to mimic the hierarchical alignment of native collagen bundles in tendons. Such anisotropic PCL nanofiber scaffolds have been shown to induce directional alignment of tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) in vitro, upregulate the expression of tenomodulin (TNMD) and type I collagen (COL I), and promote collagen remodeling and tissue regeneration in vivo [156,157]. Despite these structural merits, pristine PCL remains biologically inert, lacking sufficient cell adhesion motifs and exhibiting limited capacity to modulate immune responses; therefore, it is often combined with other materials or subjected to surface modification to enhance its immunoregulatory performance [158,159]. Among surface functionalization strategies, polydopamine (PDA) has gained attention due to its excellent self-polymerization and adhesion properties. PDA coatings introduce catechol and amine functional groups on PCL surfaces, improving cell adhesion and enabling the anchoring of bioactive molecules [160]. Composite strategies further integrate PCL with bioactive agents to achieve synergistic effects between mechanical support and immune modulation. For instance, PCL scaffolds loaded with type I collagen and exosomes derived from tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) enhanced tendon regeneration, improved tissue continuity, and increased mechanical strength in a rabbit rotator cuff tear model [161].

Magnesium-doped polycaprolactone (Mg/PCL) scaffolds upregulate IL-10 while downregulating IL-1β and IL-6, thereby inhibiting macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype and preventing excessive inflammatory stimulation [28]. Although the anisotropic architecture of PCL provides an excellent structural mimicry of native tendon, its intrinsic bioinert nature remains a major limitation for functional regeneration. Future design strategies should therefore focus on immuno-instructive approaches that enable PCL to transition from a passive structural scaffold into an active regulator of the tendon healing microenvironment.

Poly(lactic acid)

Poly(lactic acid) (PLA) and its copolymers, including poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) and poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA), are widely used biodegradable polyesters featuring excellent biocompatibility, degradability, and processability, and have been extensively employed as scaffold materials in tissue engineering [162]. PLA is synthesized through ring-opening polymerization of cyclic lactide monomers and can be fabricated into porous structures by melt extrusion, electrospinning, or composite printing, providing initial mechanical support for tendon repair while enabling cell migration and nutrient exchange [163,164]. Multilayered PLGA scaffolds have been shown to promote mesenchymal stem cell proliferation and tenogenic differentiation, with significant upregulation of TNMD and COL I expression, suggesting that PLA-based materials can support tendon tissue formation to some extent (Fig. 5C) [151]. However, the lactic acid byproducts generated during degradation lower the local pH, leading to acidic stress and sustained inflammation, which can interfere with immune cell phenotype transitions and impair tissue regeneration [162]. In terms of surface modification, polydopamine (PDA) coatings not only enhance cell adhesion but also, due to their phenolic hydroxyl buffering properties, partially neutralize acidic degradation products and mitigate local pH fluctuations [165]. Similarly, grafting ECM-mimetic peptides or chitosan rich in basic groups can improve surface affinity and suppress excessive early M1 macrophage-mediated inflammation [166]. In composite materials, scaffolds combining PLGA with hydroxyapatite (HA) nanosheets not only alleviate lactate accumulation through the Ca2+/PO43− buffering system but also enhance macrophage efferocytosis, thereby modulating the inflammatory microenvironment. During the early phase of tendon repair, this scaffold inhibits M1 macrophage activity, downregulates pro-inflammatory factors associated with the IL-17/NF-κB pathway, and simultaneously induces M2 polarization, increasing IL-10 and TGF-β levels. These effects improve local inflammation, promote collagen remodeling, and support tendon stem cell differentiation toward tenogenic lineages. In vivo experiments demonstrated that the scaffold significantly reduced the M1/M2 ratio, favoring M2 macrophages and establishing an immune microenvironment conducive to tissue repair [167]. The key advancement of PLA and its copolymers thus lies not merely in improving biocompatibility but in integrating chemical modification with immune modulation to counteract degradation-induced acidification, reestablish immune homeostasis, and create an optimal microenvironment for functional tendon regeneration.

Poly(ethylene glycol)

Poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) and its derivatives, such as PEG diacrylate (PEGDA) and PEG methacrylate (PEGMA), represent a class of hydrophilic polymers widely utilized in hydrogel systems or as functional components within composite scaffolds [168]. PEG-based polymers can be crosslinked via photochemical or chemical reactions to form three-dimensional network structures capable of encapsulating cells or bioactive molecules while providing a controlled release environment [169]. Due to their intrinsic immune-inert nature and resistance to protein adsorption, PEG-based materials often function as immune-buffering layers in tendon repair, mitigating early post-implantation inflammation and maintaining local immune homeostasis to create a favorable microenvironment for tissue regeneration [170]. Incorporating immunomodulatory factors or natural matrix components into PEG hydrogels enables active regulation of the immune microenvironment. For instance, PEGDA hydrogels incorporating bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) and periosteum-derived stem cells have been shown to promote fibrocartilage-like tissue formation and enhance interfacial mechanical strength in tendon–bone interface injury models [171]. Although PEG-based systems can transiently attenuate inflammation during tendon repair, their excessive immune inertness limits biointeractive signaling between the material, cells, and extracellular matrix, resulting in insufficient immune engagement for effective regeneration.

Inorganic ion-based biomaterials

Inorganic ion-based materials, as an important component of biomaterial systems, exhibit unique immunoregulatory potential in tissue repair due to their controllable dissolution and bioactive signaling properties [172]. Unlike natural or synthetic polymers, inorganic ions do not rely on specific sequence recognition or macromolecular structures, but instead regulate immune cell behavior through soluble signaling molecules via a release–receptor/ion channel–intracellular signaling pathway [173]. Ion release can influence macrophage M1/M2 polarization, modulate ROS levels and NF-κB/Nrf2 signaling, and intervene in NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby controlling the intensity and duration of inflammatory responses [174]. In tendon repair, an ideal immune environment should effectively suppress excessive M1 macrophage-mediated inflammation in the early phase, reducing pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α, while promoting timely M2 macrophage establishment and polarization, increasing anti-inflammatory mediators including IL-10 and Arg-1, and achieving a balanced transition from inflammation resolution to tissue remodeling [175]. Against this backdrop, ions such as magnesium (Mg2+), zinc (Zn2+), silicon (Si), copper (Cu2+), and manganese (Mn2+) have attracted attention for their prominent roles in immunomodulation. Mg2+ is considered a key immunoregulatory ion, as its sustained release enhances anti-inflammatory signaling through the TRPM7/PI3K-AKT pathway and promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype under appropriate conditions, thereby suppressing early inflammation. Mg2+ can also partially limit M1 activation and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), although rapid dissolution may shorten the duration of this effect [176]. Zn2+ exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects by inhibiting NF-κB signaling and activating Nrf2 pathways, supporting M2 maintenance and restraining overactive M1 macrophages, which reduces local inflammation and fibrosis risk [177]. Cu2+ and Mn2+ in nanozyme systems, remove ROS via SOD-like activity, mitigating oxidative stress-induced pro-inflammatory signaling and limiting M1 activation, although their effective therapeutic window is narrow due to cytotoxicity at higher concentrations [178]. Therefore, single-ion approaches are often insufficient to simultaneously regulate M1/M2 polarization for complete repair. Li and colleagues developed a layered flap-like scaffold (dtECM-TAZn/Mg) to achieve stage-specific immunoregulation: the upper layer combines Mg2+ and polyphenols (TA) to scavenge ROS, enhance M2 polarization, and inhibit M1 activation, reducing early inflammation, while the lower Zn2+ layer maintains anti-inflammatory signaling and promotes tendon structure and tendon–bone interface reconstruction (Fig. 5D) [152]. Such synergistic strategies indicate that inorganic ions coupled with spatial layering can be a key approach for constructing temporally controlled immunomodulatory scaffolds. Additionally, borate ions (B(OH)4-) have been reported to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation, contributing to the suppression of sterile inflammation and promotion of tissue regeneration [179]. In summary, the role of inorganic ion-based materials in tendon repair extends beyond simple anti-inflammatory support; through controlled ion release and coordinated multi-pathway signaling, they enable dynamic reconstruction of immune homeostasis and facilitate the sequential progression from inflammation resolution to tissue regeneration. Future studies should focus on precise regulation of ion concentration, release kinetics, and multi-ion synergistic effects to develop immune interventions tailored to specific repair stages, achieving temporally precise coordination between immune responses and tissue remodeling.

Physicochemical cues mediating immune modulation

When biomaterials are implanted into the body, they typically trigger a cascade of complex immune responses. The surface properties of these materials determine the type, concentration, and conformation of adsorbed proteins, which in turn directly influence the activation and function of immune cells [180]. Therefore, tailoring the surface physical characteristics of biomaterials to modulate immune responses represents a promising strategy for improving host integration and tissue regeneration [181]. Current research on the physical attributes of biomaterial surfaces primarily focuses on aspects such as surface topography, mechanical stiffness, pore size, and porosity, which will be discussed in detail in the following sections.

Surface topography

The surface topography of implanted biomaterials serves as a key physical cue that determines the trajectory of the host's early immune response. Immune and stromal cells can sense the micro- and nanoscale geometric features of the underlying substrate through adhesion receptors and mechanosensitive proteins on their membranes. This perception is typically accompanied by cytoskeletal tension remodeling and reorganization of focal adhesion complexes, which activates the integrin–FAK–Src signaling axis [182]. The subsequent mechanotransduction cascades not only regulate cell morphology and migration patterns but also modulate intracellular pathways such as NF-κB, MAPK, and YAP/TAZ in macrophages, enabling fine control over the magnitude and duration of inflammation [183]. Unlike conventional chemical induction, topography-mediated regulation relies purely on physical stimulation to drive immune cell phenotype transitions, thereby endowing biomaterial surfaces with intrinsic immunoregulatory capability. This physical immune coupling aligns well with the native geometry of tendon tissue, which consists of hierarchically aligned collagen fibers and periodic crimps oriented along the tensile axis. Such anisotropic architecture not only imparts mechanical resilience but also provides a morphology-based niche that supports a low-inflammatory immune state [184,185]. Studies have demonstrated that biomimetic aligned topographies can direct tendon stem cell tenogenic differentiation while simultaneously modulating the mechanical forces and adhesion patterns of immune cells, attenuating pro-inflammatory signaling and promoting a pro-resolving immune response during the early inflammatory phase (Fig. 6A) [184]. On aligned groove structures, macrophages adopt an axially elongated morphology and upregulate markers such as Arg-1 and CD206 (Fig. 6B and C), whereas disordered rough surfaces enhance IL-1β and TNF-α expression, promoting M1 macrophage activation [186]. Further investigations using three-dimensional biomimetic collagen nanofiber bundles confirmed that physical topography exerts a coordinated influence on immune cell polarization and stem cell fate, with anti-inflammatory efficacy comparable to exogenous IL-4 stimulation [187]. It is noteworthy that both the scale and the degree of order in surface morphology dictate the direction of immune modulation: moderate nanoscale roughness favors M2 polarization, whereas excessive roughness above 100 nm tends to activate M1-type pro-inflammatory responses [188]. Therefore, constructing anisotropic nanotopographies with well controlled dimensions enables stage-specific modulation of inflammation without relying on exogenous cues, thereby promoting tissue remodeling and scaffold integration. In essence, surface topography is not merely a passive structural feature but an actively designable immunoregulatory parameter its scientific essence lies in reprogramming the balance between inflammation and regeneration through the coupling of mechanical signaling and cellular mechanoperception.

Stiffness

The mechanical stiffness of biomaterials not only determines the structural support capacity of scaffolds but also serves as a crucial physical signal perceived by immune cells to sense their microenvironment [189]. Immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils can detect variations in substrate elasticity through integrin-FAK signaling and mechanosensitive ion channels, including Piezo1 and TRPV4, which subsequently activate downstream RhoA/ROCK and YAP/TAZ pathways to regulate cytoskeletal tension and inflammatory cytokine expression profiles [189,190]. Across various experimental models, an intermediate stiffness range (approximately 5–20 kPa) has been shown to balance immune homeostasis and structural support [191]. Materials with lower stiffness can maintain the neurogenic differentiation potential of MSCs even under pro-inflammatory conditions [192], whereas excessive stiffness (>100 kPa) induces macrophage rounding, hyperactivation of NF-κB signaling, and upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes [193]. Ye et al. designed a gradient-stiffness scaffold that validated this mechanical–immune balance: the low-stiffness regions enriched M2 macrophages and enhanced MSC expression of Scx and Tnmd, thereby promoting tendon–bone interface regeneration [194]. It is noteworthy that materials with fixed stiffness cannot fully accommodate the temporal dynamics between inflammation and regeneration during tendon healing. Recently, dynamically tunable stiffness scaffolds have emerged as an innovative strategy for immune modulation [195]. For example, photoresponsive hydrogels with light-induced softening maintain a high modulus during early implantation to resist mechanical loading, while gradually softening during the remodeling phase to promote M1-to-M2 transition and attenuate fibrosis, thereby achieving temporally orchestrated immune regulation [196]. Overall, material stiffness finely regulates immune cell polarization and the rhythm of inflammatory resolution through integrin-FAK-YAP/TAZ mediated mechanotransduction. Current evidence suggests that an intermediate and dynamically adaptable mechanical environment most effectively supports immune homeostasis while providing structural reinforcement, thus enabling a smooth transition from acute inflammation to tissue regeneration.

Pore size and porosity

The pore architecture of biomaterials (pore size and porosity) plays a pivotal role in directing immune responses. Pore size primarily governs cellular infiltration and morphology, whereas porosity determines scaffold permeability and metabolic exchange; together, they shape the local immune microenvironment [197,198]. Studies have demonstrated that scaffolds with pore sizes ranging from 500 to 600 μm facilitate deep immune cell infiltration and uniform distribution within the scaffold, leading macrophages to adopt an elongated morphology with reduced cytoskeletal tension. This mechanical state activates STAT6 and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways and upregulates anti-inflammatory mediators such as Arg-1 and IL-10 [199]. In contrast, small-pore scaffolds restrict cell migration and often induce local hypoxia, which enhances glycolytic metabolism via HIF-1α activation and sustains M1-type inflammatory responses [200]. Porosity and pore interconnectivity also critically influence the magnitude and duration of immune reactions. Highly porous structures enhance oxygen and nutrient diffusion, enabling rapid inflammation resolution and promoting M2 macrophage polarization, whereas dense scaffolds with low porosity tend to cause cellular retention and ROS accumulation, thereby prolonging the inflammatory phase [201]. In scaffolds with varying porosity, high-porosity groups exhibited greater infiltration of CD206+ M2 macrophages and lower TNF-α expression, indicating pronounced anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic effects [202]. Therefore, in the context of tendon repair, the design of pore architecture should balance immune modulation with mechanical stability. An optimal combination of pore size and porosity can achieve synchronized regulation of immune responses and tissue regeneration.

Bioactive substance-based materials

Bioactive substances play a pivotal role in modulating the microenvironment of tendon repair, promoting tissue regeneration through direct or indirect immunoregulatory mechanisms. Various bioactive agents (decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM), growth factors, exosomes, and stem cells) have been extensively explored to regulate the local immune microenvironment [203]. These substances modulate macrophage polarization, T-cell responses, and cytokine secretion, thereby facilitating the transition from pro-inflammatory to reparative phenotypes and accelerating tendon regeneration and remodeling. Collectively, these strategies highlight the intricate coupling between immune responses and tissue regeneration during tendon healing, and they also provide valuable insights for the rational design of immunomodulatory biomaterials.

Decellularized extracellular matrix

dECM serves as both a structural and signaling foundation for constructing regenerative microenvironments. Owing to its low immunogenicity and tissue specificity, dECM has become one of the most representative biomaterial scaffolds for tendon repair [204]. By removing cellular components while preserving the native collagen fiber network and matrix-bound signaling molecules, dECM significantly reduces host immune rejection and maintains biological activity, thereby suppressing excessive neutrophil and monocyte infiltration during the early inflammatory phase and promoting the restoration of tissue homeostasis [205,206]. In xenogeneic flexor tendon transplantation models, decellularized scaffolds showed markedly reduced infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages, improved structural integrity, and enhanced tensile strength [207], indicating that the mere reduction of immunogenicity can strengthen repair outcomes at the structural level.

Building on this, researchers have focused on conferring active immunomodulatory capacity to decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) scaffolds through functional modification. Tannic acid-modified decellularized tendon scaffolds significantly reduce M1-promoting factors, including IL-1β, IL-6, and ROS, while upregulating IL-4 and increasing the proportion of M2 macrophages, thereby mitigating inflammation and accelerating organized collagen reconstruction [208]. Decellularized tendon matrix membranes also demonstrate dual effects by suppressing early inflammation and preventing excessive fibroblast migration, effectively reducing postoperative peritendinous adhesions and improving functional recovery [209]. These findings indicate that decellularization and surface modification are not merely additive but synergistically achieve anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic immunoregulation through structural and chemical coordination. dECM scaffolds retain tissue-specific biochemical cues and structural integrity, serving as endogenous carriers of regenerative and immunomodulatory signals. Tendon-derived dECM scaffolds enriched with VEGF and SDF-1 not only promote stem cell adhesion and tenogenic differentiation but also induce M2 macrophage polarization [210]. Notably, photo-crosslinked porcine Achilles dECM maintains native collagen structure and mechanical properties while interacting with THP-1 cells without triggering the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, and IFN-γ [211]. These results suggest that dECM scaffolds support M2-mediated regenerative responses while suppressing M1-driven pro-inflammatory activation, thereby establishing an immune-friendly microenvironment for tendon repair. Emerging composite strategies further expand the immunoregulatory potential of dECM. Enzyme–nano hybrid-loaded decellularized tendon scaffolds achieve ROS scavenging, promote M2 polarization, and enhance type I collagen deposition, significantly reducing fibrosis and inflammation while accelerating mechanical recovery [98]. In summary, decellularized matrices dynamically shift the local microenvironment from inflammation control to regeneration promotion by reducing immunogenicity, suppressing excessive M1 macrophage-mediated inflammation, and inducing M2 polarization. Their combined anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and homeostasis-maintaining properties provide an ideal platform for tendon repair that integrates structural support with immunomodulation and lay the foundation for subsequent strategies combining growth factors or exosomes.

Growth factors

The role of growth factors in tendon repair has been redefined, extending beyond the promotion of cell proliferation and matrix synthesis to function as key modulators of the immune microenvironment. Different growth factors collectively contribute to the dynamic balance between inflammation resolution and tissue reconstruction by regulating inflammatory cascades, macrophage polarization, and immunoregenerative signaling pathways [212]. Transforming growth factor β3 (TGF-β3) is a prototypical immunoregulatory growth factor that activates Smad and downstream TGF-β/IL-10 signaling, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine release and inducing macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype [213]. Similarly, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), present at high levels in platelet-rich plasma (PRP), is considered a key mediator of PRP's anti-inflammatory effects [214]. Connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), when combined with stem cells, significantly reduces pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-6 while increasing IL-4 and IL-13 expression. In canine flexor tendon repair models, this combination inhibits M1 macrophage-mediated scar formation and enhances the recruitment of CD146+ tendon stem cells [215]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), beyond its role in promoting angiogenesis, mitigates excessive inflammatory stress in aged tendon stem cells [216]. Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), an immunomodulatory bioactive peptide, binds to VPAC1/2 receptors to activate the cAMP-PKA pathway and inhibit NF-κB signaling, thereby preventing excessive M1 polarization and maintaining M2 homeostasis [217]. Recent studies also indicate that growth factor-mediated JNK and STAT3 signaling plays a central role in tendon stem cell inflammatory responses, maintaining stem cell homeostasis and suppressing excessive adipogenic differentiation under inflammatory stimulation, thus providing a mechanistic link between growth factor activity and immune regulation [218]. Studies using degradable scaffolds to deliver pre-activated mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) further demonstrate that sustained release of immunomodulatory molecules stabilizes the M1/M2 balance and enhances tissue integration, enabling a smooth transition between inflammation resolution and regenerative initiation [219]. Collectively, growth factors in tendon repair transcend their traditional roles in promoting growth, functioning instead to suppress NF-κB, activate IL-10/TGF-β signaling, and maintain stem cell immunological homeostasis, thereby orchestrating both inflammation control and tissue regeneration.

Exosomes

Exosomes (Exos), belonging to the family of extracellular vesicles (EVs), are essential mediators of intercellular communication. These nanosized vesicles, typically 30–150 nm in diameter, originate from the exocytosis of intraluminal vesicles within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) [220] (Fig. 7A). Compared with microvesicles and apoptotic bodies, exosomes possess membranes enriched in sphingolipids, cholesterol, and phosphatidylserine, while their lumen contains heat shock proteins, ESCRT complexes, and a wide range of mRNAs, miRNAs, and lipid signaling molecules. Through the transfer of genetic and protein information between cells, they play a vital role in immune modulation and tissue remodeling [221] (Fig. 7B). As pivotal carriers of intercellular signaling, exosomes exhibit remarkable immunoregulatory potential during tendon repair. By delivering miRNAs, proteins, and lipid mediators, they induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, regulate the Th17/Treg balance, and promote angiogenesis and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, thereby establishing a coordinated equilibrium between inflammation resolution and tissue reconstruction [222].

Exosomes derived from different cellular sources exhibit distinct immunomodulatory properties. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell derived exosomes (BMSC-Exos) are enriched with miR-23a-3p, which suppresses IRF1 and downregulates NF-κB signaling, thereby promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and attenuating pro-inflammatory responses [223]. Tendon-derived stem cell exosomes (TDSC-Exos) demonstrate stronger local regulatory capacity, being rich in miR-145-3p that activates the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway to enhance cell migration and collagen synthesis. When incorporated into GelMA-based hydrogel delivery systems, these exosomes effectively modulate T-cell activity and reduce fibrotic tendon regeneration [224]. Adipose-derived exosomes (ADSC-Exos) upregulate IL-10 and downregulate IL-6 through the TGF-β/Smad pathway, thereby preventing excessive M1 macrophage polarization and improving the inflammatory microenvironment [225]. Additionally, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes can stabilize inflammation by modulating the Th17/Treg balance, preventing overactivation of the inflammatory response [226]. Functionalized MSC exosomes (BG-EVs) further expand the immunomodulatory potential of exosome-based therapies. Xu and colleagues reported that MSC-EVs induced by bioactive glass become enriched with miR-199b-3p and miR-125a-5p, significantly suppressing the expression of iNOS+ pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages [122]. In a rat Achilles tendon injury model, these functionalized exosomes promoted the co-localization of M2-CD206+ macrophages and CD31+ vasculature within ring-like spatial arrangements, ultimately enhancing tissue mechanical recovery and collagen maturation (Fig. 7C). Regarding delivery strategies, composite material-based systems are commonly employed. Encapsulating tendon-derived stem cell exosomes (TDSC-Exos) within decellularized tendon ECM (tdECM) hydrogels reduces levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 during the inflammatory phase, suppresses M1-CD86+ macrophages, and synergistically promotes collagen deposition and vascularization [227].Likewise, hydrogels loaded with human umbilical vein endothelial cell–derived exosomes (HUVEC-Exos) simultaneously activated VEGF-mediated angiogenic signaling and STAT1-dependent anti-inflammatory pathways, maintaining a balance between vascular formation and inflammation suppression [228]. These findings collectively indicate that integrating exosomes with hydrogel-based delivery systems enables precise spatiotemporal regulation of the immune microenvironment and extends their pro-regenerative effects [229]. Notably, exosomes also exhibit regenerative potential in aging-related tendinopathy. Exosomes derived from young tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) suppress M1 macrophage–driven NF-κB activation, restoring the stemness and immunoregulatory capacity of aged TSPCs and mitigating inflammatory degeneration [230]. Moreover, purified plasma-derived exosomes (PEPs) display dual anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative effects in rat rotator cuff injury models, demonstrating favorable clinical safety and accessibility [231]. Collectively, exosomes achieve synergistic regulation of immune homeostasis, angiogenesis, and tissue regeneration through miRNA-mediated signaling and material-assisted delivery. Their cell-free nature, controllable activity, and long-term biofunctionality highlight exosomes as promising candidates for the development of immunoregulatory tendon regeneration strategies.

Stem cells

The role of stem cells in tendon repair has evolved beyond simple tissue regeneration; their most significant value lies in orchestrating the resolution of inflammation and the reestablishment of regenerative immune homeostasis through multifaceted immunomodulation. As upstream regulators of immune responses, stem cells coordinate a dynamic network of macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells by secreting growth factors, exosomes, and contact-dependent signaling molecules, thereby forming a regenerative immune circuitry that spans the entire healing process [203]. MSCs, TDSCs, and amniotic epithelial cells (AECs) have all been demonstrated to facilitate inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration through immunoregulation. Their shared mechanism involves the modulation of macrophage polarization, suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, and promotion of the M2 immunophenotype via paracrine signaling and cell-cell interactions, ultimately creating a regenerative immune microenvironment conducive to tendon healing.

MSCs as prototypical immunoregulatory stem cells, secrete prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), TGF-β, and IL-10, which suppress NF-κB–mediated pro-inflammatory signaling and promote anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization [203]. In vitro coculture studies have shown that adipose-derived MSCs significantly reduce IL-1β and TNF-α secretion by M1 macrophages, thereby alleviating macrophage-induced inflammatory activation of tenocytes [92]. Beyond macrophage regulation, MSCs also modulate adaptive immunity by secreting HGF and PD-L1 to inhibit dendritic cell maturation, decrease antigen-presenting activity, promote regulatory T-cell (Treg) expansion, and suppress Th1/Th17 responses, thereby maintaining immune homeostasis [232]. TDSCs as tissue-specific adult stem cells, display comparable immunomodulatory capacity. In rat Achilles tendon defect models, TDSC implantation markedly improved the mechanical properties of the repaired tendon and reduced adhesion formation, associated with macrophage polarization from the M1 to M2 phenotype, as evidenced by reduced CD68 and upregulated CD163 and ARG-1 expression [139]. Healthy TDSCs also attenuate chronic inflammation by reprogramming the pro-inflammatory activity of M1 macrophages in aged tendon tissue, thereby improving tendon healing quality in elderly rotator cuff tear models [233]. Furthermore, TDSCs modulate adaptive immune responses through IL-10 and CXCL12 signaling, which suppress excessive T-cell activation and recruit Tregs, establishing a cooperative immune homeostasis among immune cell populations [234]. Amniotic epithelial cells (AECs) have also been shown in large-animal studies to promote tendon regeneration through immunoregulation. AECs possess low immunogenicity and a highly anti-inflammatory secretory profile; their transplantation into sheep Achilles tendon defects significantly reduced CD86 and IL-12 expression while increasing CD206 and IL-10 levels, elevating the IL-10/IL-12 ratio and decreasing immune cell infiltration, ultimately restoring near-normal tendon architecture within 28 days [235]. Undifferentiated epithelial AECs (eAECs) exert even stronger immunomodulatory effects, inducing M2 macrophage accumulation and angiogenesis during the early phase, whereas tendon-induced AECs (tdAECs) primarily promote extracellular matrix remodeling and collagen fiber alignment, enhancing mechanical integration during late-stage repair [236]. Collectively, MSCs, TDSCs, and AECs contribute to the immunological reprogramming from inflammation toward regeneration by regulating macrophage polarization and T-cell proliferation, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory responses and strengthening anti-inflammatory signaling to reconstruct a regenerative immune microenvironment conducive to tendon healing.

Chemically modulated biomaterials

Unlike strategies that rely on exogenous bioactive signals, chemically tuned materials achieve endogenous regulation of host immune responses through their intrinsic chemical properties. The chemical composition and structure of these materials determine their initial interactions with the biological environment, including protein adsorption, cell recognition, and the characteristics of degradation products, all of which collectively influence immune homeostasis and the repair process [93]. Surface chemical modifications primarily modulate protein corona formation and immune recognition through functional groups, charge, and polarity, shaping early immune responses at the interface level, whereas intrinsic chemical structure modifications, such as adjustments to molecular chirality, backbone/side-chain chemistry, and degradation product profiles, maintain long-term immune homeostasis at the bulk material level [25]. The synergy between these strategies provides a critical chemical foundation for constructing immunocompatible scaffolds for tendon repair. Moreover, during tendon healing, spatiotemporal regulation of immune responses and dynamic changes in immune cell phenotypes are key determinants of repair outcomes. Macrophages, as highly plastic immune cells, dynamically adjust their polarization state in response to the local injury microenvironment. Early-infiltrating M1 macrophages participate in necrotic cell clearance and initiate the inflammatory response, whereas the subsequent shift toward the M2 phenotype facilitates inflammation resolution and tissue remodeling, a process tightly regulated by local cues. Consequently, chemically tuned materials that create a favorable microenvironment can selectively guide immune cell phenotypes at different stages, optimizing inflammation resolution, reducing fibrotic tendencies, and promoting functional regeneration.

Surface chemical modification

Surface chemical modification reshapes the chemical environment of biomaterial surfaces without altering the bulk material structure by introducing functional groups, adjusting surface polarity, or immobilizing bioactive molecules [25,237]. The chemical properties of the material surface determine the composition and orientation of adsorbed proteins, which in turn influence macrophage recognition and immune signaling, making it a critical factor in early inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration [128]. Through precise design of interfacial chemistry, it is possible to achieve accurate regulation of immune responses, promoting the transition from pro-inflammatory to regenerative phenotypes and establishing an immune-friendly microenvironment for tendon repair.

In terms of functional group and surface polarity regulation, Li and colleagues developed a three-layer composite coating composed of polydopamine (PDA), chondroitin sulfate (CS), and BMP-2, demonstrating that dual control of charge and hydrophilicity synergistically promotes anti-inflammatory responses and tenogenic differentiation. The catechol/amine groups of PDA provide a highly reactive interface and enhance hydrophilicity, enabling stable covalent binding of CS to form a negatively charged polysaccharide layer that inhibits M1 macrophage-mediated fibrosis [238]. Simultaneously, MSC-derived ECM pre-stimulated with IFN-γ significantly enhances remodeling of the local immune microenvironment by activating multiple immune signaling pathways, including B cells, T cells, and Toll-like receptors [239]. These findings indicate that functional group density and interfacial polarity are not merely physical modification parameters but key factors that determine protein adsorption conformation and immune phenotype at the molecular recognition level. Surface chemical modification not only regulates interfacial activity through physicochemical properties but also shapes the local immune microenvironment by modulating immune cell polarization [240,241]. Functionalization of surfaces can induce the conversion of M1 to M2 macrophages, a transition that is critical for reducing inflammation, inhibiting fibrosis, promoting angiogenesis, and facilitating extracellular matrix reconstruction [242]. Therefore, surface chemical modification techniques not only influence the phenotypic balance of immune cells but also drive tissue remodeling and functional recovery during tendon repair through precise immunomodulation.

Building on this, chemical functionalization strategies are progressively evolving from simple surface property modulation toward precise delivery of bioactive signals. Covalent immobilization of Wnt3a protein on polycaprolactone nanofiber surfaces enables localized and sustained release, driving early-stage macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and activating β-catenin signaling [243]. Similarly, He and colleagues treated autologous tendon grafts with Kartogenin (KGN), inducing tendon-derived stem cells (TDSCs) to differentiate along the tenogenic lineage via chemical adsorption, resulting in tendon-like tissue enriched in type I collagen [244]. These findings suggest that surface chemical modification serves not only to activate interfacial properties but also as an effective tool for reconstructing cellular signaling networks. Notably, combining surface chemical modification with molecular functionalization can provide greater regulatory depth. Shang and colleagues modified scaffolds with anthocyanin polyphenols, whose hydroxyl–aromatic structures enable hydrogen bonding and antioxidant activity, significantly reducing ROS levels and activating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, thereby mitigating inflammatory damage and promoting ECM reconstruction in tendon regions [245]. Likewise, Yao and colleagues developed a dual-dynamic crosslinked network within a hyaluronic acid–o-phthaldialdehyde–Fe3+ system to create a Janus hydrogel patch with antibacterial, antioxidant, and immunoregulatory functions. This patch modulates the ROS/NF-κB pathway to promote macrophage polarization from M1 to M2, markedly reducing peritendinous inflammation and adhesions while enhancing tendon regeneration in vivo (Fig. 8A) [246]. Collectively, these studies indicate that functional interface design based on reversible chemical bonds and radical scavenging represents an important trend in the development of immune-friendly biomaterials.

Overall, current research on surface chemical modification is undergoing a paradigm shift from static surface alteration toward the construction of dynamic signaling interfaces. On one hand, the modulation of functional groups and surface polarity indirectly governs inflammatory responses by altering protein adsorption patterns and immune recognition. On the other hand, the chemical immobilization and delivery of specific bioactive cues directly regulate immune regenerative signaling pathways. Future studies should focus on establishing quantitative correlations between interfacial chemical parameters and immune phenotypic outcomes, as well as elucidating the role of interfacial chemical kinetics in the temporal coordination of immune modulation. Such efforts will advance surface chemistry from empirical modification toward a predictive and programmable immune-engineering strategy.

Intrinsic chemical structure modification

Intrinsic chemical structural modification regulates immune recognition and biological responses at the molecular level by tuning the chemical composition and stereochemical configuration of materials [248] [230]. Unlike surface modification, which primarily influences interfacial reactions, these intrinsic adjustments arise from the internal chemical framework of the material, enabling continuous regulation of immune cell behavior throughout degradation and tissue remodeling, thereby dynamically modulating tendon healing [249,250] [231,232]. The underlying mechanisms mainly involve: (1) modulation of stereochemical conformation to influence membrane receptor recognition and downstream signal transduction; and (2) alteration of backbone chemistry and degradation product characteristics to reshape local pH, ion balance, and redox state, guiding immune responses from pro-inflammatory to regenerative phases.

In terms of molecular chirality regulation, chiral materials possess asymmetric L- or D-configurations that selectively interact with chiral biomolecules and receptors within natural systems, thus exhibiting directionally specific immunomodulatory effects [250]. Chiral interfaces alter macrophage polarization through differential protein adsorption and integrin recognition, where L-configured surfaces generally promote anti-inflammatory M2 polarization, while D-configured counterparts tend to favor M1 activation [251]. Furthermore, chiral materials can modulate cytoskeletal tension and receptor conformation, activating FAK/Src, NF-κB, or STAT6 signaling pathways to establish immune homeostasis between neutrophils and macrophages [252]. For instance, electrospun membranes functionalized with L-arginine side groups significantly enhanced integrin-mediated adhesion and activated the FAK/YAP axis, thereby promoting both TDSC tenogenic differentiation and anti-inflammatory responses, whereas D-Arg modification elicited mild pro-inflammatory effects [247] (Fig. 8B). These findings suggest that molecular chirality is not merely a geometric attribute but an encodable immunochemical signal language. Inorganic chiral systems also demonstrate comparable immunoregulatory potential; for example, L-configured calcium silicate membranes modulate integrin-FAK signaling to enhance angiogenesis and restore immune equilibrium, thereby providing a favorable microenvironment for tendon regeneration [253]. Similarly, Luo et al. reported that L-chiral hydroxyapatite (L-CHAP) could enhance macrophage efferocytosis, suppress IL-17/NF-κB axis activation, and ultimately alleviate inflammation while improving mechanical properties in a rat Achilles tendon model [167]. Beyond chirality, the chemistry of the polymer backbone and side chains, as well as the nature of their degradation by-products, plays a decisive role in immune regulation. The polarity, bond type, and side-group composition of polymer chains determine degradation kinetics and product profiles, which subsequently influence local acidity and redox balance. Acidic degradation products tend to prolong inflammation, whereas neutral species containing hydroxyl, carboxyl, or ester groups suppress pro-inflammatory signaling and maintain immune homeostasis [248]. For instance, incorporation of polyglycerol sebacate units into PCL backbones improved hydrophilicity and degradation uniformity, reducing acidic accumulation and restoring the IL-10/IL-12 balance [254]. Likewise, citrate-based polymers released low-molecular-weight citric acid during degradation, which acted as a metabolic signal to activate the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, decrease ROS levels, and enhance M2 macrophage polarization [255]. In addition, PDO/PCL composite patches elicited minimal chronic inflammation upon long-term implantation, further demonstrating the profound impact of molecular-level chemical design on immune tolerance [256]. Collectively, these findings highlight that intrinsic chemical structure—encompassing stereochemistry, backbone composition, and degradation behavior-serves as a central determinant in orchestrating immune homeostasis and promoting functional tendon regeneration.

In summary, intrinsic chemical structural modification achieves spatiotemporal regulation of the immune microenvironment through the synergistic interplay of molecular chirality and backbone side chain chemistry. Chiral conformation primarily determines the selective recognition and signaling bias of immune cells, whereas the backbone chemistry and its degradation products govern the maintenance of immune homeostasis during the subsequent stages of tissue repair.

Physically Stimuli–Responsive biomaterials

The process of tendon repair depends not only on chemical and biological signaling but is also profoundly influenced by physical cues such as electrical, mechanical, and magnetic stimuli. In recent years, biomaterials capable of modulating the local immune microenvironment and promoting tissue remodeling through external physical stimulation have attracted considerable attention [257,258]. These materials regulate macrophage polarization and inflammatory resolution via mechanisms including electrical signal transmission, mechanical stress transduction, or magnetic field responsiveness, thereby facilitating the structural and functional restoration of tendons [259]. Among them, electrically responsive, mechano-responsive, and magneto-responsive materials have emerged as the most intensively studied systems, representing key directions in the development of physical-stimuli-mediated immunomodulatory strategies for tendon regeneration.

Electrical stimulation biomaterials

Electrically responsive biomaterials refer to functional biomaterials capable of generating electrical potential changes under external electric fields or mechanical deformation, thereby activating bioelectrical signaling pathways. Native tendon tissue inherently exhibits piezoelectric properties, in which polarized collagen fibrils generate charge distributions upon mechanical loading; this endogenous electrical signal is considered a crucial biophysical cue guiding cell migration, alignment, and tissue repair [259]. Appropriately regulated electrical stimulation not only directly enhances tenocyte proliferation and collagen deposition but also promotes inflammatory resolution through immune modulation [260]. Previous studies have demonstrated that electrical cues can regulate macrophage membrane potentials and Ca2+ influx, subsequently activating the FAK/Src, STAT6, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways to induce M2 macrophage polarization while suppressing NF-κB mediated pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and iNOS [261,262]. Concurrently, these stimuli enhance the secretion of anti-inflammatory mediators, including IL-10 and Arg-1, thereby reconstructing a pro-regenerative immune microenvironment favorable for tendon healing.

In tendon tissue, electrical stimulation not only directly activates the membrane potential and ion channels of tenocytes but also indirectly promotes regeneration by modulating immune cell behavior. Dai et al. developed a coaxial piezoelectric nanofiber membrane composed of PCL/BaTiO3 that could autonomously generate a stable electric field under mechanical stretching without external power input. This local electrical stimulation markedly inhibited M1 macrophage activation and enhanced M2 phenotype maintenance by downregulating the IL-17A/NF-κB inflammatory pathway [263]. Building on this, Wang et al. introduced a T-SrTiO3@PCL composite nanofiber system, in which polarizable SrTiO3 nanocrystals embedded within electrospun scaffolds reconstructed an electrical microenvironment analogous to that of native tendons (Fig. 9A). This scaffold upregulated tendon-related genes such as SCX, EGR1, and COL I, while suppressing MAPK and Wnt signaling to restore immunological homeostasis during the remodeling phase, confirming that electrical signal reestablishment acts as a pivotal regulator of inflammatory resolution and collagen reorganization [264] (Fig. 9B). Notably, the concept of electrically responsive systems is evolving from externally powered devices toward self-adaptive and injectable modalities. Luo et al. designed a piezoelectric injectable hydrogel (PE-IAH) composed of PLLA short fibers that generated localized piezoelectric effects under ultrasound stimulation, thereby enabling in situ electrical activation. This hydrogel modulated macrophage polarization and tenogenic lineage differentiation of TDSCs, suppressing exogenous fibroblast adhesion and inflammation while promoting endogenous tendon regeneration [266]. Subsequently, Ge et al. developed a highly stretchable polyester-based piezoelectric elastomer (PETRR) capable of sustaining continuous piezoelectric stimulation to enhance collagen remodeling and mechanical recovery, while simultaneously enabling real-time monitoring of tendon stress and temperature, integrating immunoregulation with smart feedback [267]. Furthermore, Wang et al. reported a Y-doped ZnO piezoelectric biosensor that utilized a piezotronic transistor effect for dual-mode monitoring of tendon stress and motion signals, and combined with deep learning algorithms, accurately recognized movement patterns and predicted re-injury risks [265] (Fig. 9C). Collectively, these studies demonstrate that electrically responsive materials can reconstruct the bioelectrical microenvironment characteristic of native tendons under physiological loading, thereby guiding immune-mediated tissue regeneration. Moreover, electroactive biomaterials are advancing toward intelligent systems capable of both monitoring and modulating the healing process.

Mechanically responsive biomaterials

Mechanically responsive biomaterials are capable of undergoing reversible deformation or stiffness modulation under external loading, translating mechanical cues into biologically recognizable signals that dynamically regulate immune responses. Macrophages are considered the most mechanosensitive immune cells, capable of sensing substrate stiffness, tensile strain, and shear stress through integrin-mediated Piezo/NFAT1/LIF signaling, thereby modulating their polarization states [257]. Moderate cyclic strain promotes M2 polarization, enhances the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, and facilitates inflammatory resolution, whereas excessive or irregular loading tends to maintain an M1-dominant phenotype, prolonging the healing process. Wang et al. demonstrated that macrophages exposed to appropriate mechanical stimulation polarized toward the M2 phenotype and secreted TGF-β1 to promote tendon repair, while these effects were abolished following macrophage depletion [268]. Compared with tenocytes, macrophages exhibit a higher mechanosensitivity, underscoring their central role in mediating crosstalk between mechanical microenvironments and tissue repair. McKenzie et al. further established a mechano-inflammatory coupling model showing that M1 macrophages suppress the mechanosensitivity of tendon fibroblasts by activating the NF-κB and JAK/STAT pathways, thereby downregulating mechanoresponsive genes such as IL-6 and MMP-13 [269]. The magnitude and pattern of mechanical strain exert bidirectional effects on macrophage behavior: low strain levels (5%–7%) generally induce M2 polarization, whereas high strain (12%–15%) activates the M1 phenotype [270,271]. Additionally, Adams et al. found that cyclic compression simultaneously upregulated M2 markers and osteogenic genes, suggesting that dynamic loading exerts a beneficial influence on the repair process [272]. Notably, strain direction and matrix topography also affect macrophage responses; even under identical strain amplitudes, disordered substrates can induce pro-inflammatory phenotypes, indicating that microstructural organization and mechanical parameters jointly determine the directionality of immune modulation [273]. Moreover, macrophages of different origins (tendon-derived versus tissue-resident) may exhibit heterogeneous responses under the same mechanical stimuli, emphasizing the need for source- and context-specific investigations. Current mechanical stimulation strategies encompass a broad range of approaches, from modulating matrix stiffness and surface microstructure to applying exogenous loading systems or multiphysical-field hybrid stimulation. Variable-stiffness hydrogels and shape-memory polymer scaffolds have been shown to maintain low inflammation levels under cyclic strain and promote tenogenic cell alignment and collagen deposition [274]. However, conventional loading devices often suffer from uneven stress distribution and limited reproducibility, restricting precise micro-scale control. Recent advances in micro/nanorobotic systems offer new solutions; under magnetic or electric fields, microrobots can generate localized mechanical oscillations that suppress Piezo1-mediated mechanotransduction and induce M2 polarization [275]. Similarly, oscillating magnetic nanoparticles produce localized mechanical cues whose frequency-dependent effects can selectively induce either M1 or M2 phenotypes, providing a promising route for spatiotemporally controlled immune modulation [275]. Looking forward, the integration of programmable stiffness regulation with self-feedback monitoring in intelligent mechanoadaptive scaffolds may enable precise tendon regeneration and long-term functional restoration.

Magnetically responsive biomaterials

Magnetically responsive biomaterials are functional materials capable of generating magneto-mechanical forces or localized thermal effects under external magnetic fields, typically achieved by incorporating ferrite-based magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) into natural or synthetic polymer matrices [258,276]. Magnetic stimulation can influence cell membrane receptors, calcium ion channels, and integrin clustering, thereby modulating intracellular signaling and immune responses. Owing to their remote, reversible, and noninvasive controllability, magnetic materials have recently shown unique advantages in reconstructing the tendon repair microenvironment. External magnetic fields not only induce macrophage polarization and facilitate inflammatory resolution but also promote tenocyte alignment and collagen deposition, offering a novel strategy for magnetically guided immunoregenerative coupling [277]. In terms of immune regulation, Vinhas et al. developed a Fe3O4 nanoparticle–incorporated magneto-responsive scaffold subjected to low-frequency electromagnetic stimulation. The magnetic field markedly suppressed the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and COX-2 in IL-1β–conditioned tenocytes while upregulating IL-10 and TIMP-1, thereby establishing an anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative milieu. Further analysis revealed that magnetic exposure directly induced M2 macrophage polarization and enhanced their reparative function, suggesting that magnetic-responsive systems could coordinate inflammation resolution through immune cell modulation [278]. Similarly, magnetic stimulation modulated macrophage tenocyte crosstalk by promoting the M2 phenotype and inhibiting NF-κB activation, while magnetic nanocomposites attenuated pro-inflammatory gene expression in IL-1β treated tenocytes, confirming that magnetic cues can regulate local immune homeostasis across multiple cell types [279]. From a structural remodeling perspective, magnetic nanoparticles have been employed to achieve magnetic alignment of collagen hydrogels, orienting both the extracellular matrix and adipose-derived stem cells along the magnetic field direction and upregulating scleraxis and tenomodulin, thereby reconstructing tendon-like anisotropic architectures [280]. Subsequently, Bianchi et al. fabricated a poly(3-hydroxybutyrate)/gelatin scaffold doped with Fe3O4 nanoparticles, which, under a 155 mT static magnetic field, significantly enhanced stem cell proliferation, type I collagen secretion, and integrin/cytoskeletal protein expression while reducing inflammatory cytokine levels [281]. Notably, the immunomodulatory effects of magnetic materials are not limited to macrophages; magnetic fields can also regulate mast cell degranulation and histamine release, attenuating inflammatory cascades and indirectly optimizing the reparative environment [282]. Collectively, magnetically responsive biomaterials achieve dual functions of immune homeostasis modulation and structural orientation via magnetic field–cell interactions, thereby integrating anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative mechanisms in tendon healing.

Intelligent stimuli-responsive materials

Smart responsive materials are designed to achieve on-demand release and precise regulation of the local immune microenvironment by responding to physicochemical cues specific to the injury site, such as pH, ROS, and inflammatory mediators [283]. Following tendon injury, inflammation and oxidative stress mutually amplify each other, leading to elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) accompanied by ROS accumulation. These signals disrupt collagen metabolism and promote degeneration, providing clear trigger cues and intervention targets for responsive systems [284]. The design of smart responsive materials fundamentally relies on their ability to sense microenvironmental changes and precisely modulate immune responses, thereby shaping a regenerative microenvironment. During tendon injury and repair, immune responses are not static but undergo temporally sequenced phenotypic transitions in accordance with the dynamic cytokine niche. Macrophages, as highly plastic immune cells, integrate complex microenvironmental signals including cytokines, oxidative stress, and small-molecule metabolites, predominating as M1 macrophages during early inflammation and gradually transitioning toward the M2 phenotype. This phenotypic conversion dictates the efficiency of inflammation resolution, tissue remodeling, and regeneration [285,286]. Therefore, surface chemical modification not only regulates material physicochemical properties to influence immune cell phenotypic balance but also, through precise control of immune cell polarization within the local cytokine niche, actively drives immunomodulation and tissue remodeling during tendon repair.

More specifically, the mildly acidic environment characteristic of the early inflammatory phase provides a natural trigger for pH-responsive biomaterials. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), particularly zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), remain stable under physiological pH but degrade in acidic conditions, thereby enabling stage-specific drug release when incorporated into hyaluronic acid based injectable hydrogels [287]. A composite scaffold employing ZIF-8 as the pH-sensitive framework achieved enhanced local drug availability during the fibroblast proliferation phase, leading to downregulation of IL-9R and the p-MEK/p-ERK pathway, reduced type III collagen deposition, and improved macroscopic and histological outcomes [288] (Fig. 10A). Thus, pH-responsive systems precisely confine antifibrotic and anti-adhesion signaling to the critical pathological window, lowering the probability of scar-mediated healing without impeding endogenous regeneration. However, phase-targeted drug delivery alone cannot completely interrupt the positive feedback loop between oxidative stress and inflammation. Therefore, subsequent strategies have evolved toward the ROS axis, aiming to regulate the pathological inflammatory circuitry [290]. A dual-network PVA/PEG hydrogel loaded with ZIF-8@CeO2 nanozymes utilizes the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle to provide both superoxide dismutase (SOD)- and catalase (CAT)-like catalytic activity for localized ROS scavenging (Fig. 10B). Meanwhile, the hydrophilic OH-rich interface minimizes nonspecific adsorption of fibrin and fibronectin. Under oxidative stress, this system markedly reduces ROS accumulation, DNA damage, and apoptosis in TDSCs, while maintaining the expression of COL-I, SCX, and TNMD. Concurrently, RAW264.7 macrophages transition from a CD86+ M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype to a CD206+ M2 reparative phenotype [289]. Through targeted ROS elimination, this strategy accelerates the transition from the inflammatory to proliferative phase, suppressing excessive type III collagen deposition while establishing a stable microenvironment for tenogenic differentiation. Once oxidative-inflammatory coupling pathways are attenuated, the key determinant of regenerative quality shifts toward fine-tuning the cytokine niche. To this end, small-molecule-loaded hydrogels with immunoregulatory microcavities have been developed. For example, rhein-loaded microcavity hydrogels achieve spatially confined release and localized signaling, reducing M1 macrophage ratios in vitro, suppressing PI3K-Akt and IL-1β/CXCL10 pathways, and in vivo decreasing inflammatory infiltration while enhancing type I collagen deposition and tissue mechanics [291]. Likewise, functionalized GelMA microspheres, modified via dopamine–heparin conjugation, enhance adhesion and antioxidative performance while continuously releasing hepatocyte growth factor (HGF). This dual-action system simultaneously suppresses oxidative stress and inflammation, restores the COL-I/COL-III balance, and in a rat tendinopathy model, significantly reduces ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels while promoting ECM remodeling and degeneration suppression [292]. With an increasingly sophisticated understanding of the dynamic immune microenvironment, the design of smart responsive systems is advancing toward finer levels of regulation. Smart microcavity hydrogels can respond to specific threshold concentrations of inflammatory cytokines and locally release immunoactive small molecules, thereby further optimizing the cytokine niche and promoting the progression of immune phenotypes toward regenerative states. In this process, a closed-loop feedback system integrating physical, chemical, and biological cues is established between the material and the host immune system. Through this system, the material not only responds to the injury microenvironment but also effectively guides immune cell polarization, enabling precise immunoregulation within the local immune microenvironment and creating favorable conditions for tendon repair.

Clinical translation

The immune-mediated repair process plays a critical role in tendon healing, and with the advancement of clinical translation, immunomodulatory biomaterials have gradually become an integral component of repair strategies. Following tendon injury, prolonged or excessive inflammation often leads to scar tissue formation, adversely affecting tendon structure and functional recovery. During the inflammatory phase, activation of M1 macrophages and their secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β contribute to the clearance of damaged tissue; however, sustained high expression disrupts collagen metabolism and degrades the extracellular matrix, ultimately resulting in the mechanical properties of the healing tissue being substantially lower than those of native tendon [293,294]. As repair progresses, if the immune response does not effectively transition to an anti-inflammatory and regenerative phase, scar-mediated healing can significantly impede functional recovery. Therefore, modulation of the immune environment, particularly through the regulation of macrophage polarization, has emerged as a key focus in recent tendon repair research.

In this field, stem cells and their derivatives have demonstrated remarkable immunomodulatory capabilities. Studies have shown that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) not only promote angiogenesis through the secretion of various growth factors but also mitigate local inflammation by regulating immune cell phenotypes. In tendon injury models, MSCs facilitate the polarization of M1 macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, significantly reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and thereby enhancing effective tissue regeneration [295,296]. Research on adipose tissue-derived MSCs (AT-MSCs) indicates that these cells can efficiently induce M2 macrophage accumulation in vitro, resulting in higher-quality tendon repair in small animal models. The application of immunomodulatory biomaterials further supports this polarization process by improving the local immune microenvironment and promoting tissue repair [297]. In recent years, hydrogel-based, nanocarrier-based, and surface-functionalized material strategies for immunomodulation have achieved significant progress. These materials effectively attenuate post-injury inflammation in tendons and, through sustained release of immunoregulatory factors, enhance immune homeostasis during the repair process [291]. Although these materials have shown promising biological effects in vitro and in vivo, most remain at the preclinical stage, necessitating further investigation into their interactions with immune cells, long-term impacts on repair, and potential synergy with conventional repair approaches. In clinical translation, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy, a widely used immunomodulatory treatment, has demonstrated reparative potential [298]. Despite variability in outcomes across clinical trials, PRP is generally considered to accelerate tendon healing by promoting growth factor release and modulating the local immune environment. Notably, studies in patients with partial rotator cuff tears report that PRP treatment significantly improves recovery scores, although its long-term efficacy requires further validation through high-quality clinical studies.

Nevertheless, the clinical application of immunomodulatory materials still faces significant challenges. Variations in patients’ immune status and the nature of tendon injuries can lead to differential responses to these materials, making personalized immunoregulation a critical yet unresolved issue. In addition, concerns regarding the safety of biomaterials, batch-to-batch consistency, and standardized large-scale production remain major bottlenecks in the translational process. Therefore, future research should focus on systematic analyses of immune phenotypes, multicenter validation in preclinical animal models, and the design of precise clinical trials to advance the clinical implementation of immunomodulatory materials for tendon repair. With the continued development of this research framework, immunomodulatory biomaterials are poised to play an increasingly important role in tendon regeneration and hold promise as novel clinical therapeutic options.

Conclusions and prospects

Tendon repair is a dynamic biological process initiated by inflammation and orchestrated through cellular remodeling and extracellular matrix regeneration. Traditionally, research has primarily focused on optimizing the mechanical compatibility and biocompatibility of scaffolds, while immune responses were regarded mainly as adverse reactions to be minimized. However, accumulating evidence now indicates that the immune response is not merely detrimental but rather a decisive determinant of repair quality and regenerative outcome. Early, moderate immune activation facilitates debris clearance and the release of pro-healing cues, whereas subsequent resolution and homeostatic reconstruction depend on the temporal balance among macrophages, T cells, and other immune subsets. Excessive or prolonged inflammation leads to fibrosis and scar formation, whereas dynamically regulated immune phenotypic transitions can promote angiogenesis, ordered collagen deposition, and mechanical recovery. Consequently, immunomodulation has evolved from a passive adjunct to a central regulatory mechanism shifting the paradigm of tendon healing from structural repair toward functional regeneration.

Guided by this concept, biomaterial design has progressed from immunoinertness toward active immunoregulation. The intrinsic physicochemical properties of materials including chemical composition, surface topography, porosity, and stiffness directly influence immune cell adhesion, migration, and polarization, thereby determining the magnitude and duration of inflammation. Meanwhile, functional strategies such as exosome loading, growth factor delivery, and decellularized matrix incorporation promote inflammation resolution and tissue reconstruction through the controlled release of immunoregulatory signals. Furthermore, the integration of chemical modification, physical stimulation, and intelligent stimuli-responsive systems endows materials with temporally controlled and multi-signal synergistic immunoregulatory capabilities, enabling a seamless transition from anti-inflammation to pro-regeneration. Future research should further elucidate the coupling mechanisms between immune modulation and tendon tissue regeneration and develop monitorable, intervenable, and adaptive immunoregulatory biomaterial systems to achieve dynamic coordination between immune homeostasis and structural remodeling. Overall, the emergence of immunomodulatory biomaterials has redefined the research paradigm of tendon repair and provided an engineering framework for soft tissue regeneration. The ultimate goal is no longer the passive closure of defects, but the precise orchestration of the immune microenvironment to achieve true functional regeneration.
