Bioinspired and living multiscale composites for regenerative medicine in the treatment of surgical site infections
Authors: Fengshou Chen, Limei Wang, Hao Liu, Yutao Wang, Bing Tang, Naren Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surgical site infections are a major postoperative complication because microbial contamination may destabilize healing and undermine the effectiveness of regenerative biomaterials. Conventional approaches tend to focus on infection control and tissue regeneration in isolation, thereby restricting their efficacy in complicated surgical procedures. Recent advancements in bioinspired and living multiscale composite materials offer new methods of antimicrobial functionality and regenerative support through hierarchical material design and bioinspired strategies. This review presents an extensive discussion on bioinspired and living multiscale composites in regenerative medicine to address surgical site infections. This review discusses the principles of biology, design of multiscale architectures, and provides an overview of how inert implants have evolved into bioactive, antimicrobial, and adaptive multiscale material systems. Most important material classes and fabrication strategies are addressed, with literature on the modes of incorporating structural hierarchy, antimicrobial strategies, and biological integration through the composite platform. The existing uses in preventing infections, regeneration of contaminated defects, delivering infection-responsive drugs, and biosensing have been critically evaluated. Translational, industrial, and regulatory issues, as well as the problems of scale-up, manufacturing obstacles, biosafety, standardization, and clinical integration specifically for living and hybrid systems, are also discussed here. This review summarizes the prospects and shortcomings of multiscale composite strategies through the synthesis of materials science, biology, and translational research to identify key directions for creating effective, safe, and clinically viable anti-infective regenerative materials.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are among the most frequent and serious postoperative complications, particularly when surgical procedures are central to modern healthcare [1]. SSIs are known to cause delayed healing, implant failure, extended hospitalization, and increased health care expenses, especially when tissues like bones, skin, or soft tissues that undergo structural regeneration are infected [2, 3]. Traditional wound dressings and inert implants solely provide mechanical support and usually lack a hierarchical structure and biological signaling required to favor regeneration and inhibit infection. Consequently, they frequently fail to inhibit bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation, thereby prolonging inflammation and leading to chronic infection [4].

Regenerative medicine aims to repair injured tissues via scaffolds, bioactive materials, and developed biological systems [5]. The majority of existing regenerative materials lack multiscale grouping, active responsiveness, and antimicrobial properties, which restricts their application in contaminated/infection-susceptible surgical settings [6]. This has developed a clinical need for multifunctional biomaterials that combine regenerative ability with active infection containment [7, 8]. The relationship between the surgical site infection, impaired tissue regeneration, and the role of bioinspired, living multiscale composite materials is schematically displayed in Fig. 1.

The existing methods of SSI prevention rely primarily on the utilization of systemic antibiotics, antimicrobial coatings, and topical antiseptics [9]. Though these approaches are effective in the short term, systemic antibiotics exhibit low penetration into the damaged tissues and lead to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance. Antimicrobial surfaces may also suffer from burst release, instability, or a short lifespan [10, 11]. The majority of traditional regenerative scaffolds are passive matrices that do not have any intrinsic antimicrobial or immunomodulatory capabilities [12]. This leads to easy bacterial adhesion, biofilm formation, and impaired cell recruitment to these inert surfaces.

The microenvironment at infected surgical sites becomes enzymatic, inflamed, hypoxic, and acidic [13]. Most conventional biomaterials undergo structural degradation or mechanical weakening at infected surgical settings, which are characterized by low pH, inflammation, enzymatic activity, and harbor a high bacterial burden [14]. Consequently, these materials fail to provide coordinated signals that result in effective immune control and tissue remodeling [15]. These limitations point to the necessity of materials that will be able to respond to infection, as well as regenerate. Such limitations are presented here as a basic framework or will be referenced in later sections in terms of advanced bioinspired and living multiscale material techniques.

The bioinspired materials are designed based on natural tissue principles [16, 17]. These principles have hierarchical organization on multiple length scales to achieve mechanical stability, biological functionality, and adaptability [18, 19]. These concepts can be recreated in artificial materials to improve tissue integration and cellular control [20]. The living and hybrid composite have been developed. It contains engineered cells or microorganisms that are able to sense infection-related indicators and release antimicrobial or immunomodulatory factors [21, 22]. The formation of biofilm has been prevented using bioinspired mineral phases, antimicrobial peptides, and nanoscale engineering strategies without restraining regenerative activities [23]. These directions represent the transition toward multiscale composites that can be integrated into one platform, providing structural support, biological activity, and infection control [24].

This review aims to summarize the latest developments in bioinspired and living multiscale composites for regenerative medicine in infectious surgical units. It focuses on the concepts of material design, operational strategies of incorporating antimicrobial and regenerative performance, and critical considerations of translatability.

The review discusses the fundamentals, classes of materials, fabrication approaches, assessment methods, and future outlooks with a focus on strategies to combine infection prevention with tissue regeneration.

Scope and terminologies

Throughout this review, key terms are defined to ensure conceptual clarity. Living composites are material systems that incorporate metabolically active biological components, such as engineered bacteria or mammalian cells, capable of sensing environmental cues and performing therapeutic functions within the host environment.

Microorganism-derived composites represent a subset of living composites in which bacteria or other microbes act as the primary functional elements, typically contributing to antimicrobial production or environmental sensing.

Cell-laden composites refer to scaffolds containing mammalian cells that primarily support tissue regeneration. While biologically active, these systems do not necessarily possess intrinsic antimicrobial or sensing capabilities.

Hybrid composites are multiscale material systems that integrate living or cell-mediated components with bioinspired, non-living structural elements to achieve both biological functionality and mechanical stability.

In contrast, bioinspired multiscale composites are non-living materials designed to replicate the hierarchical organization and dynamic behavior of natural tissues without incorporating metabolically active components.

The scope of this review focuses on living, bioinspired, and hybrid multiscale composites developed for regenerative anti-infective applications, while purely cellular systems and conventional tissue engineering approaches without antimicrobial relevance are included only for contextual comparison.

Foundations of multiscale and bioinspired materials

The bioinspired and multiscale composites are created with reference to natural tissues, in which the hierarchical structure of various length scales facilitates the acquisition of the mechanical stability, biological functionality, and functional adaptability simultaneously [26]. Understanding these natural systems, along with engineering approaches to recapitulate their key aspects, underlies the design of materials that are biodegradable, regenerative, and capable of inhibiting or responding to infection in surgical practice [27]. However, it is important to note that the hierarchical organization of both natural tissues and engineered materials often reflects trade-offs among conflicting mechanical, biological, and functional demands, as opposed to a continuous global optimization of all properties.

Overview of hierarchical biological structures

Natural tissues like bone, skin, and nacre are multiscale composite structures containing nanoscale structural units assembled into microscale units. These are, lastly, developed into macro-scale structures that are engineered to be mechanically resilient and biologically active [28, 29]. The example of bone can be seen in the combination of hydroxyapatite crystals with collagen fibrils on a nanoscale into lamellae and osteons, which render the bone its stiffness, toughness, and the ability to remodel [30]. The nacre structure is a layered architecture made of aragonite plates, with superior fracture strength through controlled interfaces and hierarchical assembly [31]. These biological structures depict the benefits of multiscale arrangement in improving mechanical integrity and biological compatibility. It may be used as a guide in designing synthetic regenerative composites [32]. Bioinspired principles for antimicrobial regenerative composites

Bioinspired composites are also recapitulating natural hierarchical assemblies and are prepared by assembling hard and soft phases, graded interfaces, and anisotropy [1]. The features distribute mechanical loads, reduce stress concentrations, and facilitate cellular adhesion and tissue ingrowth [34]. Synthetic systems incorporating mineral phases, collagen-mimetic structures, or nanoparticle reinforcements improve osteogenesis or dermal regeneration by presenting biologically relevant nanoscale cues [35]. Multiscale design may enable the incorporation of antimicrobial nanoparticles, ion-releasing minerals, or drug-containing domains into composites for infection-prone settings, although such additions may influence mechanical properties, degradation behavior, or cytocompatibility [36, 37]. In practice, the addition of antimicrobial agents can affect mechanical properties, degradation kinetics, or cytocompatibility based on dose, spatial distribution, and material chemistry, and these effects should be carefully weighed in the design of composites. The porosity is hierarchical, and the interfaces are regulated, rendering it feasible to release antimicrobials locally and reduce bacterial adhesion, which improves surgical reconstruction [38]. Other sections discuss a non-living, but adaptive alternative to these bioinspired multiscale strategies, which are engineered living materials. Additionally, dose-dependent, long-term structural performance under infected conditions, and possible cytotoxic or inflammatory outcomes are also inadequate elements that have not yet been explored and deserve methodical research.

Living materials and engineered microbial/cellular systems

Living materials incorporate engineered cells, microbial systems, or cell-mimicking components that can sense and respond to infection-related stimuli; beyond nonliving bioinspired composites, living material systems extend these principles by introducing active sensing and response capabilities [39]. Changes in pH, bacterial signaling molecules, or inflammatory markers can be detected by engineered bacteria or mammalian cells, and antimicrobial peptides or metabolites can be released [40, 41]. When combined with a multiscale scaffold, such living systems are dynamic, with adaptive antimicrobial responses, immune-modulating, and regenerative factor-releasing properties [42, 43]. This aids in developing a new generation of multipurpose composites that can work well in the surgical sites that pose high risks of infection.

Combining these ideas, the basic design principle of highly regenerative composites is a hierarchical structure permitting multifunctionality. Interface control that regulates mechanical and biological behavior, and responsive or living components that provide adaptive infection control [44, 45]. Biological hierarchies and bioinspired design principles can offer a framework for creating multiscale composite materials that address both infection control and tissue regeneration. Through structural mimicry, incorporation of functional gradients, and adaptive behavior of natural systems, these strategies incorporate antimicrobial activity with regenerative support across a variety of length scales. These starting ideas provide the basis for further advances in anti-infective biomaterials, with a shift toward more bioactive, responsive, and living composite systems.

Historical and technological evolution of regenerative anti-infective materials

The development of biomaterials for surgical wound repair and infection prevention has gone through specific phases. The stages were shaped by limitations of previous methods and raised new problems [50]. Overall, this development reflects a transition from passive structural support to adaptive and reactive material systems.

Inert and bioactive materials for surgical repair

Initial surgical implants were primarily inert materials like metals, silicones, and polymethyl methacrylate. Their primary role was mechanical replacement and structural stability [51]. These materials were not designed to be biologically active and exhibited limited controlled interactions with host tissue and the immune system. The term inert here refers to the lack of designed biological activity and effective immunomodulatory activity, rather than an absence of host immunological response, although materials like metals and poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) are known to cause inflammatory and foreign-body reactions. Then, the bioactive materials were developed to enhance the integration of tissues [52]. Calcium phosphate ceramics and bioactive glass stimulate osteointegration and bone attachment to the surface. Although these materials improve the biological compatibility, they offer limited protection against infection. As a result, systemic antibiotics have remained crucial for infection control [53].

Development of antimicrobial and immunomodulatory composites

Antimicrobial strategies are added to biomaterials to mitigate the risk of infection. Polymeric or ceramic scaffolds have been functionalized with antibiotics, antimicrobial ions, peptides, and surface-engineered topographies [54, 55] to reduce bacterial adhesion and premature biofilm development. Immunomodulating properties were subsequently added to reduce excessive inflammation and promote healing [56]. Yet, these methodologies predominantly exhibit a passive characteristic, and their inherent limitations, such as burst release, brief functional lifespan, and the development of antimicrobial resistance, have been thoroughly discussed in Sect. "Scope and terminologies". These shortcomings led to the development of more sensitive material systems.

Emergence of living materials for infection detection and response

Engineered living materials represent a shift towards adaptive biomaterial systems. These materials use engineered bacteria, mammalian cells, or cell-mimetic materials [57]. They have been engineered to detect infection-related changes in the pH, quorum-sensing molecules, or inflammatory mediators [58]. In response to these signals, living systems secrete antimicrobial peptides, biofilm-disrupting enzymes, or regenerative factors. When incorporated into scaffold matrices, they create hybrid, bi-functional scaffold composites with structural and biological applications [43]. Material classes and fabrication

The bioinspired and living multiscale composites can be classified by their materials and functional design strategies. Such classes vary regarding their complexity, responsiveness, and translational preparedness [58, 69]. This part focuses on material concepts and fabrication logic, rather than the metrics of the occurrence of specific clinical outcomes. Table 3 provides a comparative summary of the key material classes and their design-related functions.

Bioinspired structural composites with antimicrobial properties

Bioinspired structural composites normally involve the association of polymeric matrices and inorganic mineral phases [70]. Common materials include hydroxyapatite, bioactive glass, and nanoclays. These stages confer stiffness, osteoconductivity, and polymers afford toughness and processability [71]. Antimicrobial functionality is incorporated either using ions, nanoparticles, or surface chemistry. A few examples include silver, copper, zinc, or chitosan-based systems [71]. These mechanisms are designed to inhibit bacterial adhesion and early biofilm formation. The antimicrobial effect tends to be inactive and temporary. Here, inactive and temporary mainly refer to passive and non-responsive antimicrobial systems whose activity is limited to short-term release or surface action and does not include the long-term interaction with regenerative or immunomodulatory mechanisms. When compared with living composite systems, these bioinspired designs are clinically appealing because they are more stable, have less complicated regulatory routes, and can be scaled. Nanocomposite hydrogels are a related category of materials, which are polymers that include nanofillers in natural or synthetic polymers [18, 45]. These systems have cell-friendly environments and moderate antibacterial activity; however, there are trade-offs in porosity, mechanical strength, and antimicrobial longevity. Moreover, dose-dependent cytotoxicity, short-term effects of antimicrobials, and the compatibility of antibacterial activity with the host cell are the major design limitations that must be carefully considered when designing effective anti-infective composites.

Living and hybrid composites for infection sensing and response

Living and hybrid composites make use of biological elements in scaffold matrices. These materials are engineered cells or microorganisms. The scaffold supports the construction, and the living part serves sensing and response functions [72]. Engineered living materials can sense infection-related information and cues, such as pH variations, bacterial/metabolite responses, or inflammatory indicators [73]. These systems, in turn, release antimicrobial peptides or immunomodulatory factors and are in the preclinical phase [74]. As described in Sect. "Challenges and limitations", these challenges need precise material and biological design. Hybrid mineral-living systems demonstrate sustained cell differentiation capabilities and also facilitate responsive behavior [75]. Despite their promise, such systems face several challenges, such as the loss of cell viability, immune interactions, and biosafety concerns. They are the emerging material platforms [76]. Multiscale fabrication technologies

Multiscale composites must be fabricated using methods capable of regulating structure at multiple length scales. These methods can be fabricated materials in a way that macroscopic geometry and pore architecture can be controlled [77, 78]. Freeze-casting produces aligned porous materials at a microscale, whereas electrospinning creates fibrous networks that recapitulate key characteristics of an extracellular matrix [79]. Inorganic phases can be manipulated at the nanoscale through sol-gel processing and mineralization. Combining these techniques enables the use of hierarchical architectures [80]. Both fabrication methods contribute to certain structural or functional attributes, though greater architectural control tends to raise fabrication complexity.

Electrospinning of polycaprolactone/silk fibroin tissue engineering mats with enhanced structural stability and desirable cell compatibility, using emulsion-based electrospinning, has also been demonstrated as a suitable approach for the fabrication of regenerative scaffolds [81]. Broadly, various material types and methods of fabrication provide different tradeoffs among the structural support, antimicrobial activity, and responsiveness. These differences define the suitability of each material class to particular regenerative and infection-relevant applications, which are discussed in the next section.

Applications in surgical infection management

Bioinspired and living multiscale composites have been studied over a wide range of applications to prevent or manage SSIs and to support tissue regeneration [86]. These applications indicate the necessity to combine antimicrobial activity and structural support. They also require biological compatibility and regulated contact with the host in the complex surgical setting. Placental extracellular material (ECM)-functionalized hydrocolloid wound dressing has been shown to improve wound healing and tissue regeneration in animal models, demonstrating the importance of ECM-functionalized regenerative materials [87]. Table 4 summarizes representative applications of multiscale composite materials in the management of surgical infections. Nonetheless, these experimental results should not be considered definitive proof of clinical safeguards against infection at the surgical site, even when they show decreased bacterial adhesion and biofilm production in regulated circumstances.

Prevention of surgical site infections

The prevention of SSIs is a major objective of advanced regenerative materials, especially for implants and wound dressings that are at high risk of being contaminated with large numbers of bacteria [88, 89]. Bioinspired composites based on antimicrobial surface, ion-releasing phase, or nanoscale topography have been shown to minimize bacterial adhesion. They also reduce premature biofilm development [90]. These strategies work best in the early postoperative phase, when the risk for an infection is the greatest. Antibiotic-loaded electrospun fiber-coated decellularized skin scaffolds show synergistic antibacterial effects and have the ability to promote tissue regeneration in an infected wound model [91]. Many passive antimicrobial methods rely on a finite reservoir of active agents, which decreases efficacy with time [92]. Besides, protein adsorption or tissue encapsulation can also undermine surface-based antimicrobial effects [93, 94]. The limitations indicate that materials with sustained or adaptive protection are required. Purely antibacterial performance in a short-term scenario is insufficient. Despite their postulated in vitro antimicrobial activity, most of these strategies fail to demonstrate sustained in vivo efficacy because of actual reasons like rapid clearance, dilution by wound exudates, immune-based neutralization, and limited penetration in diseased tissues. These inconsistencies point to the significance of assessing antimicrobial composites under clinically important conditions and not depending only on simplified laboratory models. Patient-specific aspects that are not adequately represented in preclinical studies, including immunological state, comorbidities, surgical methods, and bacterial diversity, also affect clinical outcomes.

Regeneration in infected or contaminated defects

Infection has a major effect on tissue regeneration by inhibiting vascularization, immune homeostasis, and cell recruitment [95]. Multiscale composites have been created to simultaneously provide a solution to structural repair and infection control of contaminated tissues, which is especially important in bone and soft tissue reconstructions [96, 97]. Biologically inspired mineral polymer composites have the potential to offer mechanical stability while supporting cell adhesion and differentiation even under hostile conditions. Antimicrobial components are incorporated to prevent bacterial proliferation in the early stages of healing, allowing regenerative processes to occur [6]. Regardless of promising preclinical outcomes, regeneration at infected sites is not uniform. The behavior of the materials is highly dependent on the defect size, type of bacterial species, and the immune system of a patient [98, 99]. These aspects highlight the role of the context-dependent material design instead of universal solutions. Infection-responsive drug delivery and adaptive systems

To address the limitations of passive antimicrobial release, infection-responsive materials have been engineered to release therapeutics in response to the local environmental stimuli [100]. Antimicrobial or immunomodulatory releasing composites can be stimulated by acidic pH, bacterial enzymes, or other inflammatory mediators [101]. Biological systems that detect and act on infection signals are also further extended by living and hybrid systems, that harbor biological components [102]. The systems provide an opportunity for local, need-based intervention and reduce unnecessary exposure to antimicrobial agents. Currently, the majority of infection-responsive platforms are at the proof-of-concept, early preclinical phase. Issues related to response reliability, safety, and long-term stability need to be resolved before translating to the clinic [103].

Biosensing and diagnostic integration

Multiscale composites, also known as therapeutic functions, are explored as infection detection and monitoring platforms [104]. Responsive optical, electrical, or biochemical components can be incorporated into materials to give real-time insights into the presence of bacteria. They also show an inflammatory response at surgical locations [105]. The diagnostic possibilities can facilitate prompt intervention and better management of infections [106]. Incorporation of sensing capabilities into regenerative materials introduces additional complexities, such as signal stability, specificity, and compatibility with clinical practices [107]. Therefore, diagnostic applications are now seen as complementary rather than as an independent solution.

The management of surgical infections needs materials to achieve an efficient balance between antimicrobial effect and regenerative support. Biological compatibility must also be maintained in dynamic and heterogeneous settings. Multiscale, living composites inspired by biology provide a way forward in terms of dealing with these challenges. To be successful, they must maintain functionality, control host interactions, and maintain performance stability across a variety of clinical situations. Furthermore, even though the development of local antimicrobial and regenerative approaches, systemic antibiotics continue to play a central role in surgical infection treatment, highlighting that biomaterial-based technologies are not only imagined to be used as complementary, but as independent clinical tools.

Characterization and evaluation of antimicrobial and regenerative performance

The bioinspired and living multiscale composites need a robust assessment of bioinspired and living composites, which is to be implemented through combined characterization strategies covering both material properties and biological functioning of bioinspired and living composites under infection-relevant conditions [114, 115]. Since these systems are intended to integrate structural, antimicrobial, and regenerative functionality, no single assay can adequately assess their efficacy [116]. It is necessary to have such integrated evaluation strategies that help prioritize material systems with balanced performance profiles, enabling them to advance to preclinical and clinical testing.

To facilitate a more systematic evaluation of these multifunctional materials, an integrated assessment framework can be considered. In such an approach, structural and physicochemical characterization is first used to confirm hierarchical organization and mechanical integrity, followed by antimicrobial testing to determine the ability to inhibit bacterial growth and biofilm formation. Subsequently, biological evaluation is conducted to assess cytocompatibility, regenerative potential, and immune response. Finally, in vivo models and translational considerations are used to validate performance under clinically relevant conditions. This stepwise yet interconnected evaluation strategy helps ensure that antimicrobial efficacy is achieved without compromising regenerative function or material stability. Structural, chemical, and mechanical characterization

Structural characterization gives the necessary understanding of the process of achieving and sustaining multiscale architecture [118]. The methods applied to the resolution of features at nanoscale to macroscale levels include scanning and transmission electron microscopy, micro-computed tomography, and atomic force microscopy [119, 120]. These techniques prove the pore architecture, phase distribution, and the integrity of the interface, all of which are essential for both mechanical activity and the biological interactions [121].

Mineral phases, antimicrobial components, and surface chemistry are assessed by chemical and compositional analysis such as X-ray diffraction, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, and elemental mapping [122]. Mechanical testing under compression, tension, and fatigue is of particular use in surgery, where infectious environments may accelerate material degradation [123]. However, mechanical data between studies can often be difficult to compare because the sample geometry, loading protocols, and test environment are different [124]. In vitro antimicrobial evaluation

In vitro antimicrobial testing is popular for determining the capacity of materials to hinder bacterial growth or the formation of biofilms [125]. The most popular ones are colony-forming unit counting, zone-of-inhibition tests, and live-dead staining. Although such tests provide quick, quantitative data, they mostly reflect relatively short-term or simplified conditions [126, 127]. The findings of planktonic bacterial cultures cannot always be applied to biofilm-based infections, which are often seen in surgical areas [128]. Biofilm is a more clinically relevant behavior in the sense that it is much more varied in terms of bacterial strains, incubation time, and measures [129]. Consequently, antimicrobial performance documented in different studies must be viewed relatively, but not as the definitive measure of efficacy.

In vitro regenerative and immunological assessment

Cytocompatibility, proliferation, differentiation, and immunomodulatory effects are assessed by cell-based assays [130]. The use of osteogenic, fibroblastic, or endothelial cell models mostly depends on the target tissue. These tests help establish the fact that antimicrobial strategies undermine regenerative potential, which is often of concern when using multifunctional materials [7, 131]. Single-cell cultures cannot fully replicate the complexity of infected surgical microenvironments. The immune responses, vascularization, and host-pathogen interactions are not fully replicated in vitro [132, 133]. Therefore, positive cellular responses must be regarded as necessary but not adequate leaders of in vivo performance.

In vivo models and translational relevance

Animal models will remain important for determining the aggregate antimicrobial and regenerative properties of advanced composites [134]. Typically, infection control, tissue integration, and material degradation are evaluated using small-animal models under regulated conditions [135]. Full-body animal models are better anatomical and mechanical models, but are used less commonly due to cost and ethical reasons [136]. The absence of a standardized model of infection is one of the primary problems. Differences in bacterial load, inoculation procedures, and timing of evaluation render cross-study evaluations tricky [137]. Decreases in bacterial load are not always accompanied by functional tissue regeneration. Hence, microbiological and regenerative endpoints should be used to evaluate in vivo outcomes and provide an in-depth assessment [138].

The bioinspired and living multiscale composites must be evaluated in terms of structural, antimicrobial, and biological roles, in a coordinated manner at multiple scales and model systems. The lack of standardized testing procedures remains one of the biggest obstacles to meaningful comparison and translation, necessitating systems of integrated and clinically relevant assessment. Comparative studies indicate that antimicrobial systems lacking adaptive/multiscale capability tend to exhibit lower in vivo efficacy levels despite demonstrating good antibacterial performance in vitro. This emphasizes that multiscale design approaches that incorporate immunological regulation, mechanical strength, and prolonged antimicrobial activity are required to achieve significant translational outcomes. In reality, the effective analysis of multifunctional composites should be conducted by combining structural, mechanical, biological, and antimicrobial data rather than sequentially interpreting discrete measurements. Structural and mechanical tests determine the capacity of a substance to withstand physiological and infested environments, whereas local infection control is determined through antimicrobial tests. These results should be compared with biological data, including immune response and tissue integration, to understand whether the antimicrobial efficacy is achieved without interfering with regeneration. Collectively, this combined system of assessment facilitates functional benchmarking and informed translational decision-making.

Translational and clinical integration

The translation of bioinspired and living multiscale materials from laboratory designs to clinically relevant surgical applications requires coordinated advances in preclinical validation, manufacturing scalability, regulatory compliance, and integration into surgical workflows [143, 144]. These materials should demonstrate good performance in infection-prone environments and meet the safety standards, which permit their use in actual clinical settings.

From lab models to preclinical infection models

The initial testing of composite scaffolds is generally conducted through in vitro tests, followed by evaluation in small animals. However, infection-prone disorders require more realistic preclinical models that can recreate contaminated bone defects, infected soft-tissue wounds, or surgical site conditions [6, 19]. The rodent and rabbit models of osteomyelitis and various models of infected wound healing are increasingly used to study the effects of antimicrobial release, biofilm reduction, immune responses, and regenerative outcomes [96]. These models offer crucial data regarding the behavior of materials with respect to bacterial load, inflammation, and physiological stress [145]. It is noteworthy that a number of bioinspired multiscale composites have now been shown to replicate equivalent performance in preclinical infection models, a fact that underscores their translational potential for living systems.

New living composites still require further preclinical evaluation, such as long-term retention of engineered cells or microbes, in vivo stability of engineered biocircuits, and biosafety in infected and non-infected hosts [146]. Replacement of laboratory tests with sophisticated models of infection is thus important for establishing or refuting the ability of a composite to act adequately and safely in applied surgery situations [147]. Additionally, in preclinical infectious studies, bioinspired multiscale composites that integrate immunomodulatory signals, regulated release, or hierarchical porosity have shown better effectiveness than single-scale or exclusively antimicrobial designs. The above observations indicate that structural and functional incorporation among length scales is a determinant of translational success.

Clinical-scale manufacturing of anti-infective living composites

The production of bioinspired composites at the clinical scale needs to be reproducible in terms of material synthesis, porosity, mechanical properties, and the stability of antimicrobial or bioactive functions [70]. For mineral-polymer composites, freeze-casting using 3D printing, extrusion-based methods, and sol-gel mineralization are being scaled and optimized to satisfy regulatory and clinical production requirements [72, 75].

Scale manufacturing is necessary in clinical applications, but it cannot guarantee clinical consent and legal compliance by itself. In addition, a consistent distribution of nanoparticles or ions must be ensured to achieve consistent antimicrobial activity. As presented in Sect. "Challenges and limitations", manufacturing and sterilization demands show significant translational barriers for the living composite framework.

Living composites add additional complexity, since entrenched cells or engineered microbes need to be viable and functional over the course of production, storage, and delivery [148]. To standardize the large-scale production of living scaffolds, bioprocessing techniques, sterile closed-system production, cryopreservation approaches, and genetic stabilization are being investigated [149]. The most significant impediment to translation is achieving clinical-grade reproducibility [150]. Beyond just technical optimization, regulatory compliance also calls for substance traceability, verified sterility, quality assurance processes, good manufacturing practice compliance, and consistent batch-to-batch performance. Integration with surgical practice

To implement new types of composite biomaterials, surgeons require that the biomaterials be compatible with existing procedures, sterilizable without compromising functionality, and easy to manipulate during implantation [151]. A pre-existing bioinspired mineral-polymer scaffold is a good fit for orthopedic and soft-tissue surgery, as its mechanical properties and structural stability are familiar to clinicians [49, 152]. They offer greater antimicrobial benefits in contaminated or high-risk surgical sites. Surgical integration is particularly difficult in living composites. They might require special storage, altered sterilization methods, or timely implantation to ensure appropriate preservation of live biological elements in them [55]. Their capacity to detect infection or discharge antimicrobial agents in vivo has high clinical potential. Nonetheless, to gain acceptance by the surgical teams, intraoperative usability and postoperative reliability need to be demonstrated [154].

In summary, bioinspired and living multiscale composites cannot be successfully translated without the simultaneous approach to material design, preclinical proof, scalable manufacturing, and compatibility with surgical workflow. Non-living bioinspired materials are more readily translatable for clinical use, but living and hybrid systems are still associated with additional risks related to stability, safety, and regulatory classification. Solving these problems will involve a prior coordination of material development and evaluation models with clinical restrictions. These considerations characterize the realistic route by which more sophisticated anti-infective composites can be transferred out of the laboratory to surgery.

Challenges and limitations

The bioinspired and living multiscale composites offer significant opportunities to treat infected surgical defects; however, several biological, material, manufacturing, and regulatory challenges still exist that restrict the safe and effective clinical translation of these composites [57, 158]. Importantly, these difficulties largely vary and extend between bioinspired non-living composites and the living antimicrobial systems, with regulatory and ethical limitations in addition to material and design factors. These challenges need to be recognized to help develop and implement safe and realistic regenerative anti-infective technologies. Biological and material stability in infected environments

The conditions at infected surgical sites are acidic pH, proteolytic enzymes, inflammatory cytokines, and high bacterial load, which may degrade polymeric phases, dissolve mineral components, or destabilize nano-reinforced interfaces in composite scaffolds [6, 19]. Such severe conditions can either decrease mechanical integrity, change drug or ion release profiles, or affect bioactive surface responses. Regenerative composites in bones can also lose stiffness or fracture resistance if the porous, multiscale structure is destroyed [159].

The living composites encounter further instability because cells attached or engineered bacteria could be subjected to cytotoxic stress, nutritional depletion, or immune response within invaded tissue [160]. This may lead to the disruption of antimicrobial secretion, decreased sensing ability, or cell death [21]. This means that bioinspired, as well as living, composites, have to be designed to resist hostile inflammatory and microbial environments during clinically significant times [161].

Safety, ethics, and control of living antimicrobial systems

Special safety issues are associated with living antimicrobial systems, as once implanted, engineered bacteria or mammalian cells can behave unpredictably [162]. The risks include uncontrolled proliferation, horizontal gene transfer, and unwanted immune and cytotoxic metabolites [163]. To ensure the safe use of the living components, the living components need to be separated from the scaffold and not absorbed by the body once the therapeutic window is completed [164]. The inoculation of genetically modified organisms into patients is also an ethical concern, as the organisms’ lifespans and potential release into the environment are possible [165].

Current approaches to prevent uncontrolled survival or mutation of engineered organisms are the genetic kill-switches, self-limiting circuits, and auxotrophic strains [166]. However, whether these mechanisms of control will be stable in vivo in the long term is questionable [148]. These risks cannot be considered purely technical, which can be solved by engineering approaches; in fact, they are significant regulatory obstacles that, in certain instances, can even exclude the possibility of clinical translation of living antimicrobial systems altogether. Significantly, some investigations indicate neutral or inconclusive therapeutic advantages of living antimicrobial systems in vivo, especially when immune clearance, loss of engineered cell activity, or lack of persistence develops. Therefore, when assessing the clinical potential of living materials, biological intricacy should be carefully considered as it may mitigate theoretical benefits. Biosafety and ethical governance are therefore critical concerns that need to be addressed before the development of living antimicrobial material beyond the experimental [167].

Scalability and manufacturing barriers

The bioinspired multiscale composite synthesis in clinical scale strictly depends on porosity control, the distribution of the mineral, the loading of nanoparticles, and the addition of antimicrobial agents [18]. Batch-to-batch variations can influence, while insignificantly affecting changes in these parameters, including mechanical strength and antimicrobial performance [168]. The other issue is the sterilization, because heat or radiation may kill polymers, inactivate bioactive molecules, and kill engineered cells in hybrid systems.

Living composite adds even more complexity to manufacturing due to sterile bioprocessing requirements, regulated culture environments, maintenance of cell vitality, and constant manifestation of designed functions [169, 170]. The storage and means of transport must not result in a loss of functionality, and the circumstances should comply with clinical-grade manufacturing requirements [171]. These barriers to production are the current impediments to rampant adoption.

Regulatory and clinical translation bottlenecks

The regulatory framework for advanced biomaterials, particularly those with living or genetically modified constituents, is still in its developmental stages [144]. The authorities should assess the efficacy of antimicrobials, their long-term effects, the products of material degradation, genetic stability, and associated environmental risk [172]. Particularly, living antimicrobial systems have been subject to ambiguous approval routes, falling under drug, device, and biologics policies, which greatly increase the time to translation [173].

The other criteria that are crucial for clinical translation are good performance in large-animal models, reliability and durability, ease of handling by the surgeon, and lastly, economic [174]. In the absence of standardized guidelines, developers are left wondering what kind of preclinical evidence is needed to make commercialization challenging, despite some good laboratory findings [175]. Emerging and future directions

The rapid evolution of biomaterials engineering, synthetic biology, and nanotechnology enables consideration of ideas beyond traditional antimicrobial scaffolds [44]. The future perspectives covered in this section have both practically feasible advancements that utilize recent technological possibilities or theoretical ideas meant to direct ongoing studies, instead of immediate clinical applications. As highlighted in Sect. "Challenges and limitations", future advances must tackle the remaining problems of biosafety, stability, and regulatory hurdles. Future composite systems will be designed to work autonomously, recreate the immune system thinking, and interact with the current microbiome. They will also be able to improve the multi-layered regenerative condition [69]. In the present context, terms like autonomy or immune-like behavior have been employed primarily to describe limited detecting-behavior abilities rather than to refer to actual biological autonomy or immune system equivalency. Instead of living systems, the future development of bioinspired multiscale composites and engineered living materials is likely to provide the necessary advancement. The innovations are most likely to transform the simultaneous solution of surgical infection and tissue repair. Autonomous self-sterilizing and self-healing living materials

Self-sterilizing composites are designed to react to early infection warning, such as pH, quorum-sensing molecules, or oxidative stress. They can self-deliver antimicrobials [179]. Biological composite systems can produce antimicrobial peptides, bacteriocins, or biofilm-degrading enzymes without the need for external signals [180]. Concurrently, self-healing networks of minerals and polymers are also emerging as a new design. These designs can be utilized to repair micro-cracks or structural damage in polluted environments [181]. When these autonomous functions are combined, systemic antibiotics can be reduced, and scaffold life can be prolonged in the course of long-term healing. Regulated control of the microbiome is much more complex than basic conceptual frameworks because microbial communities in surgical environments are strongly patient-specific and actively affected by host immunity, antibiotic exposure, and surgical factors.

Self-healing behavior can also be generated by intrinsic populations of living microorganisms or stem cells. They can generate a new matrix and reestablish mechanical or microbial damage [182]. The combination of self-sterilizing behavior and self-repair mechanisms is a promising approach for long-term implants in high-risk surgical sites [183].

Immune system–inspired bioactive composites

New composite designs draw inspiration from innate immune functions, which incorporate receptor-mimetic molecules, antimicrobial peptide analogs, and immunomodulatory signals [184]. The Surface of materials can be altered to instruct macrophage behavior as well, converting inflammation into a pro-healing form, improving tissue integration, and limiting bacterial survival [185]. Some systems release chemokines or cytokine-like factors to enhance the recruitment of immune cells and the rapid elimination of contamination [186]. It is possible to reproduce neutrophil activity using scaffolds with the ability to generate reactive oxygen species or local ionic shifts, providing wide-spectrum antimicrobial protection and minimizing resistance development [187].

Microbiome-inspired and immune-modulatory material design

There is growing interest in using microbiome-derived processes to control infection and promote regeneration [188]. The benefits of these microorganisms or their metabolites could be to compete with pathogens, regulate inflammation, or influence wound healing processes [13]. The introduction of microbiome-inspired signaling agonists or artificial probiotic strains into composite scaffolds would create competitive habitats that deter colonization of pathogens.

Better results can be obtained by optimizing the ratio of pro-inflammatory to pro-generative effects by using immune-modulatory composites [189]. These composites can establish immune conditions by regulating surface chemistry, stiffness, or bioactivity, thereby regulating the activities of the macrophage or T-cells to prevent further progression of an infection and tissue regeneration [190, 191]. Industrial and translational perspectives

The future industrial use of bioinspired and living multiscale composites relies on whether these materials can be processed reliably, inexpensively, and following regulatory and clinical standards [193]. The successful deployment of new technologies depends on industry-driven considerations, as the laboratory prototypes become commercial products [194]. For implanted biomaterials, industrial decisions are influenced not only by technical efficiency and cost-effectiveness, but also by regulatory requirements, safety considerations, and clinical validation.

Industrial adoption of anti-infective multiscale composites

Industries involved in orthopedic implants, wound-care products, and regenerative scaffold development have begun to appreciate the importance of composites with strong structural support and antimicrobial capabilities [195]. Firms are considering mineral polymer hybrid scaffolds, nanocomposite hydrogels, and ion-releasing biomaterials to lower the rate of infection and enhance healing processes [196]. Some of the factors include scalability, compatibility with sterilization processes, and integration with the existing clinical workflow [197].

Living composites have attracted high research interest but with limited industrial application because of biosafety issues, storage, and viability concerns [198]. Fields in which cell therapy and engineered probiotics are active are beginning to consider hybrid living systems as a potential platform for infection-responsive biomaterials [199, 200].

Cost–benefit and economic considerations

In large-scale industrial applications, materials must demonstrate clear economic benefits, including fewer postoperative infections, fewer revision surgeries, less hospitalization, and higher healing rates [200, 201]. In infections, conventional implants incur high downstream costs; therefore, infection-resistant composites can offer long-term economic benefits despite high manufacturing costs [202]. Smart-responsive materials, such as living materials, may require specialized production, cold-chain storage, or other safety tests, which are expensive [203]. Industry must, therefore, balance these costs with estimated clinical gains, viability of reimbursement, and possible reductions in antibiotic usage, providing global public health benefits [204].

Regulatory frameworks and approval pathways

The regulatory processes governing advanced antimicrobial composites are evolving, especially for materials that include living cells, engineered microbes, or genetically encoded antimicrobial products [60]. Traditional biomaterials are regulated as devices; however, living or hybrid systems may fall under combination product regulation, including device, biologic, or gene therapy standards [148]. This increases documentation requirements, preclinical evidence requirements, and long-term monitoring requirements.

Regulators must also assess the risks of antimicrobial resistance, genetic stability, environmental containment, and biocompatibility in accordance with ISO and GMO standards [205]. Commercialization of a developed material depends on a number of other considerations, like business risk aversion, exposure of future liability, financial uncertainty, and the potential for administrative exclusion. Insufficient classification slows commercialization and needs proper early engagement between developers and regulatory organizations to efficiently navigate the approval pathways [206, 207]. The development of multiscale composites needs collaboration between educational laboratories, industrial collaborators, regulatory bodies, and clinical facilities to develop them [209]. Future roadmap (2025–2035)

Although engineered living materials are a promising new frontier, bioinspired multiscale composites are also important for developing anti-infective regenerative strategies that can be translated to clinical use. The next decade will see bioinspired and living multiscale composites take a forward step to the next stage of becoming not only a highly anticipated experimental system but also a regenerative solution to infection-prone surgical settings. As described in Sect. "Challenges and limitations", challenges in overcoming biological, developmental, and regulatory hurdles will be significant for successful clinical translation. The roadmap should include achievable milestones that enhance the scientific basis, address translational gaps, and align with clinical and industrial requirements, with defined decision points based on safety, stability, and feasibility. At each stage, go/no-go decisions should be guided by these criteria.

Short-term goals (2025–2027)

Short-term objectives emphasize enhancing basic knowledge of the stability and performance of composites in an infected microenvironment. Studies need to focus on improving material compositions for enhanced resistance to enzymatic, inflammatory, and microbial growth. New models of in vitro-in vivo correlation, such as standardized infected-defect platforms, will be necessary to forecast the behavior of materials in early surgical healing. For living composites, short-term objectives involve improving the genetic stability of engineered cells or microbes, kill-switch validation, and establishing biosafety profiles in conditions that are relevant to infection. Cooperation with industry will also be significant to start pilot-scale production and early regulatory consultations.

Mid-term objectives (2028–2031)

The composites should demonstrate clinically relevant performance during the mid-term, such as the ability to perform sustained antimicrobial activity and consistent regeneration of different types of defects. Recent progress in 3D biomanufacturing and automated mineral-polymer processing should enable uniform, reproducible fabrication of multiscale scaffolds in clinical-grade settings. This stage may see the initiation of living composites into controlled clinical feasibility studies, should there be clear evidence of biosafety. Compared with nonliving bioinspired composites, living composite systems are more regulated and pose biosafety risks, thus potentially requiring a longer validation period. Still, integration with surgical processes, the development of the long-term storage approach, and the initial involvement of reimbursement and health-economic models will also remain significant mid-term goals.

Long-term vision (2032–2035)

In the early 2030s, clinically approved anti-infective regenerative composite implants are expected to be developed that integrate structural integrity, adaptive antimicrobial response, and guided tissue repair. Next-generation surgical biomaterials may evolve toward more autonomous living or hybrid systems capable of detecting pathogens, modulating immune responses, and supporting self-repair, although such advances depend on overcoming current safety, stability, and regulatory challenges.

Mass production systems, standardized international regulations, and automated computerized surveillance systems will likely facilitate large-scale clinical use. Alternative approaches built around nonliving bioactive materials may offer quicker therapeutic options when living or hybrid systems cannot meet health or scalability requirements. The final aim is, ultimately, to reduce the level of surgical infections, avoid the use of systemic antibiotics in most surgeries, and provide long-term regenerative results even in patients who are at risk and in the contaminated surgical environment.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Bioinspired and living multiscale composites present a significant advantage in the treatment of surgical infections and tissue defects. Recent developments in hierarchical architecture, adaptive antimicrobial behavior, and regenerative functionality offer a holistic approach that conventional implants could not offer. This conclusion summarizes all the main insights, gaps, and implications presented in this review.

In all material classes, hierarchical design principles bioinspired by natural tissues offer mechanical strength, biological compatibility, and tunable porosity necessary for regeneration. Sustained control of pathogens is enabled by the incorporation of antimicrobial cations, nanoparticles, or responsive chemistries, whereas dynamic sensing and feedback functions–previously unattainable with inert scaffolds–are made possible through living composites. Multiscale fabrication methods, such as 3D printing and nanocomposite engineering, enable a high degree of control over architecture to fit the various requirements of surgery. These findings indicate increased opportunities for multifunctional composites, which can combine tissue restoration with infection control. Nevertheless, the majority of the treated material systems are still in preclinical or early translational development, and their routine clinical applications are not yet established.

Despite significant progress in the development of anti-infective and regenerative composites, gaps in knowledge still remain. The long-term capability and biological stability of such composites in an infected or inflamed environment need to be studied further. In the case of living antimicrobial systems, genetic stability, immune clearance, and biosafety are all formidable challenges. In vivo performance data, particularly of clinically relevant infection models, are still very limited. The actual degradation behavior, antimicrobial durability, and incorporation with host tissues during prolonged healing phases are poorly defined. Critical constraints associated with long term safety, manufacturing scalability, regulatory consent, and performance in challenging infection settings remain to be addressed.

In the future, standardized infection models to assess the results of regeneration and the durability of antimicrobials should be the focus of research. The research on materials should also aim at the enhancement of resistance to enzymatic and microbial breakdown, mechanical performance enhancement of defects under loads, and immunomodulatory capabilities. For living composites, it will be necessary to develop resilient genetic defenses, improve long-term viability, and develop clinically compatible storage and sterilization approaches. Close collaboration among materials scientists, clinicians, and regulatory specialists will further enhance safe translation.

Integrating antimicrobial activities with regenerative capabilities has the potential to transform clinical wound care, the management of infected fractures, contaminated implants, and chronic defects of mucosal tissue. Multifunctional composites can decrease antibiotic dependence and the risk of developing infection at the surgical site, and enhance the outcomes of patients under high-risk. Thus, these strategies should still be studied in an interdisciplinary manner and with cautious translational assessment before they can be widely used in clinical practice. The development of autonomous, immune-inspired, and self-healing materials in the long run may revolutionize the way clinicians handle contaminated surgical settings and present new opportunities for robust and personalized tissue repair.
