Introduction

Hemophilia A is the most common inherited bleeding disorder caused by a deficiency or dysfunction of coagulation factor VIII (FVIII), typically transmitted in an X-linked recessive pattern, affecting approximately 1 in 10,000 males. Female Hemophilia A is extremely rare and often associated with lyonization or chromosomal abnormalities [1]. Factor XII (FXII) deficiency, also known as Hageman factor deficiency, is a rare autosomal recessive condition characterized by markedly prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) but typically not associated with clinical bleeding [2]. Although FXII deficiency is considered asymptomatic, its co-occurrence with other factor deficiencies may complicate clinical manifestations and laboratory diagnosis [3].

The coexistence of Hemophilia A and FXII deficiency is exceptionally uncommon. Only a few case reports in the literature have described individuals, particularly females, harboring both conditions, either through compound inheritance or extreme lyonization of the normal X chromosome [1,4]. Such overlap can pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly when aPTT prolongation may be misleading in identifying the primary coagulopathy [2]. Moreover, in patients with concurrent hemorrhagic and thrombotic risks, such as those presenting with both bleeding diathesis and arterial thrombosis, a thorough evaluation of coagulation factors and inhibitors becomes essential to guide appropriate management [3,4].

In this report, we describe a rare case of a patient presenting with acute lower limb ischemia in the context of acquired Hemophilia A and concurrent FXII deficiency. This case highlights the importance of integrating clinical, laboratory, and molecular findings for accurate diagnosis and emphasizes the need for a multidisciplinary approach in managing conflicting hemostatic disorders. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 checklist [5].

Case presentation

A 70-year-old male with a history of hypertension and remote tibial fracture surgery 30 years ago and a 1 pack-year smoking history, with no history of illicit drug use or prior blood transfusions, presented with progressive bilateral leg claudication over three months, associated with paresthesia from toes to lower legs. He was diagnosed with severe peripheral arterial disease (PAD) at a provincial hospital via CT angiography, revealing left common iliac and superficial femoral artery occlusion, severe left internal iliac stenosis, moderate right iliac stenosis, and atherosclerotic changes in bilateral lower limb arteries. According to the medical records at the provincial hospital, conservative medical management was initiated, including Aspirin 81 mg once daily after breakfast, Rosuvastatin 10 mg once daily after dinner, and Paracetamol 500 mg three times daily after meals for pain control. Two days before current admission, he developed acute worsening of left leg ischemia with pain and cyanosis, followed by uncontrollable bleeding at the IV site after fluid infusion at a local district health center, leading to hypovolemic shock. He was urgently transferred to Cho Ray Hospital.

On arrival (Feb 28, 2025), he was alert but pale and tired. According to the local hospital records, his initial vital signs were stable (blood pressure 130/80 mm Hg, pulse 90 bpm, temperature 37 °C). However, during uncontrolled bleeding at the IV site, his condition deteriorated with hypotension (blood pressure 80/60 mm Hg) and tachycardia (pulse 120 bpm), consistent with hypovolemic shock. Vital signs at our hospital after resuscitation were: BP 140/80 mm Hg, HR 90 bpm. Examination revealed a 3 × 5 cm hematoma with active oozing at the right forearm IV site and severe ischemia in the left leg with gangrene of the foot, consistent with Rutherford category III acute limb ischemia (Fig. 1). Laboratory tests showed severe anemia (Hb 52 g/L), leukocytosis (WBC 21 G/L), thrombocytosis (PLT 529 G/L), and markedly prolonged APTT (79.9 s), with normal PT. Corticosteroid therapy was initiated immediately upon suspicion of acquired Hemophilia A, and a multidisciplinary team subsequently convened to confirm the diagnosis and establish a definitive management plan.

Ischemic necrosis of the left foot on admission, with gangrenous changes and signs of infection.

The patient was diagnosed with chronic bilateral iliofemoral artery occlusion with severe ischemia and suspected coagulopathy due to isolated prolonged APTT. Emergency limb amputation was planned due to gangrene but postponed pending hematology consultation. Repeat APTT was >120 s. Mixing studies revealed time-dependent inhibition, and FVIII activity was low (1.5 %). Lupus anticoagulant (LA) was positive (LA screen 1.94, LA confirm 1.17, LA ratio 1.65). Bethesda assay detected high-titer FVIII inhibitor (84.4 BU), confirming acquired hemophilia A (AHA). Factor XII activity was also low (16.5 %), suggesting concurrent congenital FXII deficiency.

Autoimmune markers (ANA, anti-dsDNA) were negative. Anticardiolipin and β2-glycoprotein 1 antibodies were mostly negative, indicating that positive LA likely reflected non-specific autoimmunity or inflammation. Von Willebrand factor activity was elevated. The final diagnosis included AHA on a background of bilateral PAD and congenital FXII deficiency.

Multidisciplinary consensus, including specialists from hematology, intensive care, anesthesiology, hepatology, cardiology, and orthopedic surgery, planned: (1) bypassing agent (rFVIIa) perioperatively, (2) urgent left transfemoral amputation, (3) immunosuppressive therapy with corticosteroids and rituximab, and (4) comprehensive postoperative care. Above-knee amputation was selected instead of below-knee amputation because of left iliac artery occlusion and poor collateral circulation around the knee, which would have made healing unlikely. Considering the patient's age and comorbidities, this approach minimized the risk of reoperation. The patient received two units of packed red blood cells preoperatively, five doses of rFVIIa intraoperatively, and four units of packed red blood cells postoperatively, together with FEIBA, Tranexamic acid, corticosteroids, and rituximab for hemostatic support. rFVIIa was initiated preoperatively and continued postoperatively. Surgery was successful, with moderate intraoperative bleeding (Fig. 2). The estimated intraoperative blood loss was approximately 300 mL, mainly from diffuse oozing without a clear bleeding point, which made hemostasis difficult. Postoperatively, the surgical site continued to ooze with blood soaking the dressings but without a specific bleeding focus. A total of 9 transfusions of packed red blood cells were required during hospitalization. No complications beyond persistent bleeding were recorded during the postoperative course.

Discussion

This case represents a rare and diagnostically challenging scenario: an elderly patient with critical limb ischemia due to atherosclerotic disease, coinciding with acquired Hemophilia A (AHA) and congenital factor XII (FXII) deficiency, further complicated by the presence of lupus anticoagulant (LA). Timely diagnosis and effective multidisciplinary coordination were pivotal to achieving a favorable outcome.

Diagnostic challenges

The patient presented with spontaneous bleeding at an IV site and an isolated, markedly prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), raising initial suspicion for an intrinsic pathway defect. According to international guidelines, unexplained isolated APTT prolongation with bleeding should prompt investigation for acquired Hemophilia, most commonly due to FVIII inhibitors [[6], [7], [8]]. Although LA was also detected, LA is generally associated with thrombotic complications rather than bleeding and can prolong aPTT in vitro without a bleeding phenotype [6,7]. Therefore, clinical bleeding alongside high FVIII inhibitor titers (84.4 BU) and low FVIII activity (1.5 %) confirmed acquired Hemophilia A. Concurrently, the detection of FXII deficiency (16.5 %) added further complexity, as this congenital deficiency typically does not cause bleeding but may paradoxically predispose to thrombosis by impairing fibrinolysis [3,4].

The simultaneous occurrence of LA and FVIII inhibitors is exceptionally rare. López-Villegas et al. (2019) described a case of systemic lupus erythematosus with co-existing FVIII and FXI inhibitors, leading to both deep vein thrombosis and severe bleeding, a pattern comparable to our patient's presentation [9]. Immune dysregulation in aging or occult autoimmunity may underlie this dual pathology. In our patient, no systemic autoimmune disease was diagnosed, but LA positivity suggests a pro-autoimmune state, potentially triggering FVIII inhibitor development [6,7].

The congenital FXII deficiency further complicated the diagnosis, as it causes marked APTT prolongation without clinical bleeding and may mask the severity of concurrent acquired bleeding disorders [1,4]. Some studies even propose a link between FXII deficiency and increased thrombotic tendency due to impaired fibrinolysis [10].

Management strategies

Successful perioperative management was achieved with bypassing agents. Recombinant activated factor VII (rFVIIa) is considered first-line therapy in AHA due to its rapid onset and lower thrombogenicity [7]. In our case, initial bleeding control with rFVIIa was suboptimal, necessitating a switch to activated prothrombin complex concentrate (aPCC), which has proven efficacy in patients with high-titer inhibitors [8].

Immunosuppressive therapy is the cornerstone of inhibitor eradication. High-dose corticosteroids were started immediately, and rituximab was added early due to the high inhibitor titer. This approach aligns with recent consensus guidelines that recommend early combination therapy in patients with high-titer inhibitors or life-threatening bleeding [7,8]. Our patient responded well, with FVIII levels recovering and inhibitor becoming undetectable after one month.

Close infection monitoring was essential, especially in elderly patients receiving dual immunosuppression. Prophylactic hepatitis B treatment and nutritional support were provided, which may have contributed to avoiding infectious complications, a key cause of mortality in AHA [7].

Multidisciplinary collaboration

This case illustrates the critical importance of timely hematologic evaluation, surgical planning, anesthetic support, infectious disease screening, and pharmacy consultation in managing AHA with complex coagulopathy. Adhering to national and international guidelines, while tailoring decisions to the individual case, allowed for optimized care and a successful outcome.

Potential triggers and underlying factors

This case also illustrates a rare clinical scenario in which acquired Hemophilia A developed in association with severe peripheral arterial disease and critical limb ischemia. While acquired Hemophilia A is itself an uncommon disorder, the search for underlying causes and potential triggers is crucial for patient management. Recent studies have emphasized that psychological stress, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and genetic predisposition can act as initiating or exacerbating factors in autoimmune diseases [11,12]. These findings provide a broader context for acquired Hemophilia A and underscore the importance of investigating potential stress-related and environmental contributors when assessing such patients.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic complexities of managing acquired Hemophilia A coexisting with congenital factor XII deficiency and lupus anticoagulant in an elderly patient presenting with acute limb ischemia. The markedly prolonged APTT in the absence of prior bleeding history or anticoagulant use necessitated prompt evaluation for acquired inhibitors and rare coagulation factor deficiencies. Despite the inherent paradox, bleeding in the presence of a known thrombophilic factor deficiency, the patient benefited from timely diagnosis, effective use of bypassing agents, and early immunosuppressive therapy. Multidisciplinary collaboration among hematology, vascular surgery, and critical care teams played a pivotal role in achieving a favorable clinical outcome. Clinicians should maintain high vigilance for atypical coagulation profiles in vascular emergencies and consider rare hematologic conditions when standard explanations fall short.
