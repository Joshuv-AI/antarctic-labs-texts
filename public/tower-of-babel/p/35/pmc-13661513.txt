Individualised progesterone receptor modulator prevention strategies for triple-negative breast cancer in BRCA1 pathogenic variant carriers

ABSTRACT
AbstractWomen with germline BRCA1 pathogenic variants face a very high risk of early-onset triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), with risk-reducing mastectomy currently the only established preventive option. Emerging evidence suggests aberrant progesterone signalling contributes to TNBC risk, while preclinical studies indicate progesterone receptor modulators such as mifepristone may substantially reduce cancer development. DNA methylation-based markers reflecting field cancerization and replicative age could enable real-time monitoring of preventive efficacy and support an individualised primary prevention approach.Subject terms: Cancer, Genetics, Oncology

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the leading cause of cancer-related death among young women worldwide, and its prevalence is increasing1. Pathogenic variants (PVs) in the BRCA1 gene are responsible for approximately 1 to 3% of BCs globally, with 60 to 70% of these cancers being of basal cell origin and lacking expression of estrogen, progesterone, and HER2 receptors, making them triple negative breast cancers (TNBC)2. Among all BC subtypes, TNBC has the worst prognosis3 and treatment options remain limited due to the absence of therapeutic targets. BRCA1 PV carriers have a 40% lifetime risk for TNBC, compared to a 1% lifetime risk in women without PVs; notably, this risk rises sharply from 25–30 years onwards2. Additionally, BRCA1 PV carriers have a 45% lifetime risk of ovarian cancer (OC), compared to 1.8% risk for women without PVs and the risk starts to rise from the age of 35 onwards4.

Options for management of TNBC risk for BRCA1 PV carriers are rather scarce. Primary prevention strategies (aimed at preventing cancer development) are mostly limited to risk reducing bilateral mastectomy, which can lower BC risk by 90-95%5 and remains the gold standard for breast cancer prevention. However, the procedure shows only a tendency towards improved survival6, has a higher risk of failure in women who smoke or have a high body mass index (BMI), carries a risk of post-surgical complications, and has negative consequences on self-perception and physical and mental well-being. Risk reduction through selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs; e.g. tamoxifen and raloxifene) and aromatase inhibitors7,8 has shown efficacy in reducing estrogen receptor positive BC risk, but not mortality, and these drugs do not prevent TNBC.

Secondary prevention strategies (aimed at early cancer detection) are currently limited to enhanced imaging-based surveillance (i.e. mammograms and breast MRI) and these remain a core aspect of the follow-up management of BRCA1 PV carriers. Blood-based early detection strategies (e.g. detection of circulating tumor DNA) have not shown great promise so far due to limited sensitivity9,10.

Individual BC risk for BRCA1 PV carriers varies considerably, making risk stratification an important component of both primary and secondary prevention. These risks have been substantiated in recent years by comprehensive cohort studies. In particular, age-dependent prospective incidence rates have been empirically determined and allow age-dependent risk prediction11,12. This has significantly improved risk communication and informed decision-making on the uptake of preventive measures. CanRisk, an online tool based on personal, family and genetic risk factors, is now fully embedded in many cancer genetics services and implementation studies using CanRisk with other risk factors [i.e. polygenic risk score (PRS) and non-genetic risk factors] are underway in tertiary care (e.g. Precision HBOC, CanRisk-Clingen) and primary care (e.g. CanRisk-GP). Such comprehensive risk prediction has been shown to also modify individual breast cancer risk in BRCA1 PV carriers that might provide additional information for decision-making regarding prophylactic bilateral mastectomy13. Although mammographic density is a risk factor for BC, it is currently not well integrated into risk stratification, and – in particular for BRCA1 PV carriers – might be unsuitable due to lower mammographic density observed in TNBC14.

Progesterone – a key risk factor for TNBC

Elevated steroid hormone levels are one of the most striking recently discovered cell non-autonomous factors mediated by a pathogenic BRCA1 PV. These findings point to elevated progesterone levels during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle17–20. Beside several other effects, progesterone stimulates Receptor Activator of Nuclear factor Kappa-B Ligand (RANKL) secretion21 in hormone-sensitive mature luminal cells, which triggers the proliferation of luminal progenitor (and basal) cells via a paracrine mechanism22–25. These estrogen- and progesterone-receptor-negative cells are the cells of origin for TNBC.

Additional evidence supporting progesterone as the most important steroid driving the formation of BCs with poor prognosis includes findings from the Women’s Health Initiative study26 where the combination of estrogen with medroxyprogesterone acetate for hormone replacement was associated with an increased BC risk, while estrogen alone was linked to a reduced risk. In addition, long-term use of oral contraceptive pills, mainly due to the progestin component27, increased the TNBC risk28. Higher parity, which corresponds to prolonged exposure to progesterone, was associated with higher TNBC risk among black women28. Most recent evidence further supports progesterone’s key role in the formation of TNBC also in young women: Estrogen plus progestin hormone therapy was associated with an increased incidence of young-onset breast cancer, with a particularly strong association observed for TNBC (HR 1.50; 95% CI, 1.02–2.20) whereas estrogen alone decreased the breast cancer risk significantly (HR 0.86; 95% CI 0.75–0.98)29.

The observation that risk-reducing bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy consistently reduces BC risk in BRCA1 PV carriers but has a less marked effect in BRCA2 PV carriers30,31, strongly supports the view that aberrant high progesterone levels in BRCA1 PV carriers play a central role in the development of TNBC.

In addition to the proliferative effects of progesterone on the cells of origin for TNBC (i.e. basal and luminal progenitor cells)24,25, progesterone may exert its pro-carcinogenic potential via the modulation of immune surveillance given that immune tolerance mechanisms are shared by both cancer and pregnancy. The progesterone induced expression of B7-H4 (VTCN1), an onco-fetal immune tolerance checkpoint, contributes to progesterone-induced BC progression via CD8 + T cell exhaustion32. Progesterone may also be involved in the development and progression of metastatic high-grade serous ovarian cancer33–35, at least in part via progesterone-mediated suppression of natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity at the Fallopian Tube fimbriae, which are exposed to ultra-high concentrations of progesterone due to their close proximity of the ovaries19.

Current modalities for assessing and monitoring TNBC risk

Primary cancer prevention implies that in the organ at risk (i.e. breast) there are neither macroscopic nor microscopic signs of cancer or pre-cancer. Significant amounts of research focus on the detection of the earliest signs of cancer, utilising circulating cells36,37 or circulating cell-products (e.g. cancer-specific altered DNA9,38–40, RNA41 or proteins42,43). However, none of these efforts have so far convincingly demonstrated a clinical benefit over existing imaging-based strategies, such as mammography or breast MRI. These imaging-based methods often lead to overdiagnosis44,45 and do not effectively detect TNBC and other BCs with poor prognosis, which have higher mortality rates compared to screen detectable cancers46,47.

PRS, which can be used to individualise BC risk prior to clinical manifestation, also have substantial limitations. A commonly used PRS, consisting of 313 common genetic variants preferentially predicts risk for estrogen-receptor positive BCs48. A new PRS, based on analysis of a more diverse cohort led by the US National Cancer Institute (Confluence), may help to overcome this issue49. PRS is being used in conjunction with other risk factors, such as age, to measure changing risk over time. Nevertheless, due to the nature of single nucleotide polymorphisms, a PRS per se cannot be used to monitor individual risk in response to an intervention.

Two parameters that directly assess breast tissue and may reflect cancer risk have gained some attention: (i) mammographic density is associated with BC risk, however, the association is weaker and has not been evaluated extensively in women 35 years of age or younger50. Mammographic density also depends on menstrual cycle51. Most importantly, women with TNBC tend to have lower mammographic density, less MRI-assessed fibroglandular tissue, and lower background parenchymal enhancement compared to non-TNBC14. Therefore, although suitable for monitoring risk modulation in older women on SERMs52,53, imaging-based risk monitoring in young BRCA1 PV carriers at risk for TNBC is unlikely to be safe and feasible. (ii) Ki67, a marker for cell proliferation, has been used to assess SERM efficacy54,55. However, Ki67 expression in normal breast tissue is weakly associated with BC risk56. Additionally, it provides only a snapshot of current proliferation in breast tissue, is dependent on the menstrual cycle, and is difficult to assess in small breast biopsies with limited numbers of epithelial cells, leading to limited interlaboratory reproducibility57.

Progesterone Receptor Modulators for prevention

Progesterone Receptor Modulators (PRMs; also called antiprogestins), such as mifepristone, play a pivotal role in reproductive health through their effects on the cervix and uterus58. PRMs have also demonstrated effects on breast epithelial cell proliferation20,59–61 and show BC preventive effects in mice61,62.

PRMs are currently in use for emergency-63 or on-demand contraception and are well-established for use in medical abortion64–66. Their efficacy for treating symptoms related to uterine fibroids67 and other reproductive health conditions68 was demonstrated many years ago. When administered at appropriate dosages and suitable delivery methods, PRMs like mifepristone offer a woman-centred contraceptive option with additional health benefits, supporting autonomy, while offering added non-contraceptive health benefits. Based on existing data, long-term treatment with mifepristone has found to be safe69,70 and is undergoing further exploration71.

One particular aspect of safety relates to the endometrium. PRMs have previously been observed to impact the endometrium but effects differ across PRM type, dose, timing of administration within the menstrual cycle, duration, and possibly also ethnicity72. Most of the evidence has been generated for mifepristone with less evidence for the other PRMs such as ulipristal acetate (UPA; now discontinued as a treatment for fibroids) and others that were under clinical development. Follicular phase administration has little effect on the endometrium, but depending on the dose used ovulation may be delayed or inhibited. Continuous administration of PRMs in low doses that do not affect ovulation can induce endometrial effects that are potentially contraceptive73. Prolonged use of doses that inhibit ovulation has led to concerns about endometrial hyperplasia due to unopposed estrogen. However, the endometrial changes that occur are a distinct histological entity known as PR modulator-associated endometrial changes (PAEC)74. Specifically, PAEC reveals an antiproliferative state and the endometrial glandular epithelium shows reduced mitotic activity compared to the proliferative phase although extensive cystic glandular dilatation is seen. Evidence indicates that PAEC regresses upon cessation of treatment75. Recent evidence demonstrates that three months of mifepristone exposure does not induce molecular changes associated with endometrial carcinogenesis76.

Although UPA is also highly effective60,77, its association with liver injuries78 precludes its use for BC risk reduction.

A downstream effector of progesterone – RANKL – can be targeted with antibodies such as denosumab (Amgen), which is in clinical use to treat bone loss79. Unlike mifepristone68, denosumab is not known to be lipophilic and, hence, would not be enriched in the fat-containing breast tissue. Targeting RANKL has been shown to prevent mammary gland cancer in mice22,80 but has no effect on contralateral breast cancer risk in postmenopausal BC patients81. The BRCA-P study is currently evaluating the BC risk reducing effect of denosumab in women carrying a germline BRCA1 pathogenic variant (EudraCT 2017-002505-35 and NCT04711109). The acceptance rate is, however, considerably impaired by potential participants’ wishes to become pregnant and the fear of side effects.

Compared with denosumab, mifepristone has several features that may make it considerably more acceptable to young BRCA1 PV carriers in the context of a prevention trial. First, mifepristone is taken orally at home and does not require clinic-based injections, conferring a meaningful practical advantage over denosumab (given as a subcutaneous injection every six months) that is likely to improve both recruitment and long-term adherence. Second, because low-dose mifepristone has well-documented contraceptive efficacy, it could simultaneously serve as the required contraception during the trial period - a prerequisite in any prevention trial involving reproductive-age women - thereby reducing the perceived burden of participation compared to alternative prevention drugs, which necessitates separate contraception. Third, unlike denosumab, mifepristone is anticipated to reduce the risk of both breast cancer and ovarian cancer in BRCA1 PV carriers, given accumulating evidence that elevated or heightened progesterone responsiveness contributes to ovarian cancer risk in this population33–35. The prospect of a single oral agent that may simultaneously lower risk across both organs is a compelling patient-relevant argument that denosumab cannot offer. Fourth, short-term hormonal modulation with mifepristone has recently been shown not to induce oncogenic endometrial changes in BRCA1/2 PV carriers76, providing direct reassurance about safety in this specific population.

The role of the epigenome in assessing and monitoring TNBC risk

The role of the epigenome in cancer development

Historically, cancer was considered a purely genetic disease, with the presence of somatic structural DNA alterations considered nearly conclusive evidence for the presence of a cancer82. It is now clear that DNA mutations including pathogenic driver mutations are present in normal tissues and are particularly evident in hormone-sensitive tissues like the endometrium83,84 and breast85, even at a very young age. Cancers can develop in the absence of DNA mutations, purely triggered by epigenetic alterations86 and epigenetic modifiers like those suppressing the activity of the polycomb repressor complex 2 component EZH2 frequently drive cancer formation87. Indeed, DNA methylation (DNAme) at promoter regions occupied by the polycomb repressor complex 2, belonging to the so called polycomb group target genes (PCGTs), is one of the most prevalent findings across all cancer entities88–90. Together data indicating that PCGT gene expression is essential for cellular differentiation91 and that methylation of promoter CpG islands leads to an almost irreversible gene suppression92, suggest DNAme at PCGT gene promoters is an essential factor in cancer development as it locks stem/progenitor cells in an undifferentiated state93.

The most unifying model of cancer development available today is that both exogenous factors and factors triggered by genetic predispositions drive the division of epithelial (stem/progenitor) cells and thereby increase cancer risk. For BC, exogenous factors include progesterone-containing hormone therapy26, which is associated with development of BCs with poor prognoses. Importantly, known genetic risk factors indirectly influence exposure to factors driving proliferation in specific at-risk cells. BRCA1 deletion in granulosa cells in mice94–96 and BRCA1 pathogenic variants in humans alter hormone levels (notably elevating luteal phase progesterone)17,18,20, immune function19 and microbiome composition97. These exogenous and genetically-triggered factors collectively promote proliferation of certain cell types and may ultimately lead to cancer formation21,98. They are integrated at the level of the epigenome, where integration depends on individual genotypes (i.e. single nucleotide polymorphisms; SNPs) which impact on DNAme patterns (i.e. methylation quantitative trait loci; meQTLs) in a cell-type-specific manner99. Most importantly, the epigenome is dynamic and malleable over time20,93,100.

Monitoring cancer field defects

Tumors arise from a field of molecularly altered epithelial cells of a specific type – a “field defect” comprising DNAme and somatic DNA mutation – that creates a permissive environment for malignant evolution. Field defects mostly occur without visible, morphological changes. Quantifying and monitoring cancer risk could be achieved through identification of these field defects.

Field defects for cervical, ovarian, breast and lung cancers have been identified. Cervical cancer field cancerization has been studied most extensively due to accessible at-risk tissue prior to disease onset, allowing prediction of (pre)cancer development years in advance through DNAme- tests101–103. Most recently, DNAme markers assessed in samples with normal cytology were shown to predict 80% of invasive cervical cancers within six years following sample collection102.

Breast field cancerization has also been described: expression of the retinoic acid receptor beta (RAR-β) was absent in normal adjacent breast tissue104 due to DNAme of the RARB gene105,106. A large component of field cancerization is a consequence of stem/progenitor cell proliferation triggered by certain conditions17,20,21 which drive the accumulation of DNAme at PCGTs88,91. A DNAme-based mitotic clock, which reflects replicative age by focusing on promoter CpG sites that localize to PCGTs and are unmethylated in 11 different foetal tissue types, is universally accelerated in cancer, including pre-cancerous lesions (both of the breast and lung), and is also accelerated in normal breast tissue adjacent to cancers20,107,108. Importantly, this process appears to be reversible up to a certain stage, by removing the primary proliferative stimulus (e.g. with PRMs)20.

An example of field cancerization is illustrated in Fig. 220: high progesterone levels from combined hormone therapy26 or a BRCA1 germline PV17,18,20 trigger progesterone-receptor positive luminal mature cells to release RANKL and other factors that, in a paracrine fashion, activate the proliferation of estrogen- and progesterone-receptor negative luminal progenitor cells eventually leading to formation of TNBC21,22,25,80,109. Mechanisms to reduce the luminal progenitor cell population are not yet fully established, but treatment with PRMs may exert a direct effect20 and an indirect effect by enhancing NK cell cytotoxicity19.

Cancer risk signatures in epithelial cells

As discussed above, DNAme signatures in normal epithelial at-risk tissue are indicative of cancer field defects and are associated with the development of cervical102,103, breast20,110–112, ovarian113,114 and endometrial115 cancers. Breast cancer field defects can be assessed in fine needle aspirates (FNA). These field defects can also be identified in easy-to-access epithelial surrogate tissues which have similar features to the epithelial cells from which the actual cancer is derived. These features could include cell-type (i.e. epithelial), embryological origin and hormone sensitivity. Epithelial cells from the cervix or buccal mucosa (inner cheek) are therefore relevant surrogate tissues in the case of breast cancer.

Utilizing DNAme signatures, there is evidence that the field defects can be identified in cervicovaginal and buccal samples, making them suitable for assessing risk for breast116,117, ovarian118, endometrial119 and lung cancers120. For example, the WID-BC, a BC risk test based on DNAme markers measured in cervicovaginal samples116 had an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.76–0.86) in an independent external validation dataset with 113 cases and 225 controls. In comparison, the PRS313121, analysed in 314 cases and 816 controls, had an AUC of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.64–0.71). Preliminary evidence suggests that the WID-BC can predict BC development up to 5 years before diagnosis116.

Monitoring cancer risk with malleable markers

Small proof of principle studies have demonstrated that the disease-associated DNAme signatures can change dynamically due to specific interventions such as exposure to PRMs20 or smoking cessation120. Data from a window trial shows that mifepristone reduces both (i) DNAme markers reflective of stem/progenitor cells’ replicative age as well as (ii) the proportion of luminal progenitor and basal cells in normal breast tissue of healthy women without a BRCA1/2 PV and in a proportion of BRCA1/2 PV carriers20. These DNAme markers are higher in normal breast tissue adjacent to TNBC compared to normal breast tissue from age-matched cancer-free women and increase in almost all TNBCs when compared to normal adjacent tissue indicating that these markers are good intermediate surrogate markers. Whether risk indicating DNAme signatures measured in an epithelial surrogate sample, such as the WID-BC test, reflect the efficacy of mifepristone in reducing cancer risk over time has not yet been studied.

Serial breast biopsies are not standard of care and are not a practical solution for biomarker monitoring in a prevention trial. Circulating tumour-derived nucleic acids - whether cell-free DNA (cfDNA) or microRNAs - depend critically on shedding from (pre-)malignant cells and are inherently insensitive in a true primary prevention context where no tumour has yet formed. Fine needle aspiration (FNA) of the breast, whilst less invasive than a core biopsy, requires a clinic visit and trained personnel and is not yet standard of care in asymptomatic BRCA1 PV carriers. Accessible epithelial surrogate tissues - cervicovaginal and buccal cells - share cell type, embryological origin, and hormone sensitivity with breast epithelium and can be obtained through simple non-invasive self-sampling at home. The WID-BC test, a DNAme signature measured in cervicovaginal samples, achieved an AUC of 0.81 in independent external validation and can predict breast cancer development up to five years before diagnosis116. Proof-of-principle from a mouse model demonstrates that mifepristone-induced DNAme changes in the cervix parallel the reduction in mammary tumour incidence62, directly validating the surrogate tissue approach as a pharmacodynamic readout. We therefore propose the following hierarchy of biomarker approaches for a mifepristone prevention trial in BRCA1 PV carriers: (i) cervicovaginal and/or buccal DNAme signatures as the primary non-invasive endpoint; (ii) breast FNA-derived DNAme in a subset of willing participants to validate surrogate tissue signals against tissue-of-origin; and (iii) circulating liquid biopsy approaches (cfDNA methylation, miRNA panels) as exploratory endpoints, acknowledging their current sensitivity limitations in a prevention setting.

Recent findings from a mouse model using medroxyprogesterone acetate and 7,12-dimethylbenzanthracene to induce mammary tumours provide compelling evidence that epigenetic signatures can reflect cancer risk and serve as surrogate markers for monitoring preventive interventions. Treatment with mifepristone reduced mammary cancer incidence by more than 50%, a reduction that was paralleled by decreased epigenetic alterations both in the tissue at risk (normal mammary gland, indicating field cancerization) and in distant, non-target tissues such as the cervix, indicative of field defect62. These results strongly support the continued investigation of epigenetic biomarkers in accessible tissues to monitor the efficacy of cancer prevention strategies – particular of mifepristone - in humans.

The vision: personalised TNBC prevention in BRCA1 PV carriers

Our vision for personalised TNBC prevention includes predicting TNBC-risk in real time through the analysis of DNAme signatures in breast tissue (obtained through biopsy or more fine-needle aspiration which is more acceptable) or, eventually, in a more easily accessible epithelial surrogate sample. These markers would enable the ongoing assessment of primary prevention efficacy, for example, with mifepristone. They would also allow for personalised adjustments, such as changes in dosage, frequency or even medication choice which in addition to mifepristone includes drugs which have shown potential for breast cancer prevention (e.g. selective estrogen receptor modulators or thiazolidinediones122 or which are specifically active in BRCA1 PV carriers (e.g. PARP inhibitors)123. Consensus from the scientific and regulatory communities on the extent of evidence supporting a biomarker’s effectiveness would be needed before embarking on a prevention study.

In light of the accrual challenges experienced by the BRCA-P trial and the current state of evidence for mifepristone in breast cancer prevention, a randomised phase IIB proof-of-concept trial with a short-term biomarker endpoint is a more immediately feasible first step than a large-scale randomised controlled trial (RCT) powered for TNBC incidence. Such a phase IIB trial would serve to assess drug acceptability, safety, and preliminary pharmacodynamic response, and could inform the design of a subsequent definitive prevention trial. The duration of drug intervention in such a trial would ideally be biomarker-guided - adapted in response to the individual’s risk trajectory as measured by intermediate surrogate markers - rather than fixed at a predetermined timepoint.

An important consideration in selecting the biomarker endpoint for such a phase IIB trial is its biological relevance to the specific cancer subtype at risk. Background parenchymal enhancement (BPE) on breast MRI has been proposed as a potential short-term endpoint given that annual breast MRI is standard of care in BRCA1 PV carriers. However, a recently published large pooled analysis of the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC) demonstrated that mammographic density (percentage density) shows a markedly attenuated - effectively null - association with breast cancer risk specifically in BRCA1 PV carriers (OR per SD: 1.02, 95% CI 0.78–1.34), in contrast to BRCA2 carriers (OR per SD: 1.55, 95% CI 1.08–2.24), and attributes this finding to the predominance of the triple-negative subtype in BRCA1-associated cancers124. Because BPE and mammographic density are inter-related imaging surrogates of fibroglandular tissue, and because women with TNBC characteristically have lower BPE and mammographic density compared to women with non-TNBC, BPE is likely to be insensitive - or even directionally misleading - as a pharmacodynamic endpoint for monitoring TNBC risk reduction in BRCA1 PV carriers. For this reason, DNA methylation (DNAme)-based biomarkers in accessible surrogate tissues (see below) are preferred as intermediate endpoints in this population.

Challenges of mifepristone

Due to fact that mifepristone has been shown to have an acceptable safety profile without liver toxicity, it is the most suitable PRM for consideration in the context a future breast cancer prevention trial.

Despite evidence from mouse models and a very limited number of window trials, the evaluation of mifepristone20,59 for breast cancer prevention has not been extensive. Data generated with mifepristone are supported with trials using other PRMs such as ulipristal acetate20,60. Large-scale evidence from adjuvant trials similar to that which exists for SERMs – agents that are central components of adjuvant breast cancer treatment – are unlikely to become available for PRMs. Data from ongoing contraceptive trials with mifepristone add to its long-term safety profiles71.

Evaluation of mifepristone’s effectiveness could start within the context of a randomised clinical trial amongst very high-risk women. To walk towards this already challenging starting point, additional hurdles have to be overcome. Chiefly, these revolve around the negative political and cultural perceptions of mifepristone, which is primarily known as an abortion drug, and the limited access to it in countries where abortion laws are restrictive. Additionally, funding and regulatory challenges exist. The interest from the pharmaceutical industry is low, in part because of the association of mifepristone with abortion, but also because mifepristone is off-patent and prevention trials can be multi-national, long and expensive. Who will fund such a trial in the absence of pharma interest? Consensus from regulators on the design of clinical trials, and the biomarkers used within them, would also be needed to ensure results can be used to support implementation.

Nevertheless, when compared with denosumab (the agent being tested in the BRCA-P trial), mifepristone has several important potential advantages for acceptability and feasibility. These are elaborated in the context of a possible stepwise trial approach described in the previous section.

An additional topic requiring clarification is which drugs, when applied at low doses for short periods, could affect mifepristone-resistant cells (see Fig. 3 panel A). Several options exist and could potentially be applied in the same trial. These include intermittent fasting, a ketogenic diet/supplement (unpublished data), PARP inhibitors, which have particularly high antineoplastic activity in BRCA1 PV carriers compared to non-carriers125, and – still very speculatively – thiazolidinediones, which may help prevent breast cancer by activating PPARγ, which reduces cell proliferation, promotes cellular differentiation, and improves insulin and inflammatory signalling pathways122. The effectiveness of these approaches, particularly if applied in combination, will need to be considered.

New, personalised approaches to primary breast cancer prevention are urgently needed and could be developed by building on the last 15 years of research into the pathogenesis of TNBC in BRCA1 PV carriers and methods to monitor risk. However, a community effort is needed to reach this goal of a new personalised cancer prevention approach. A consensus workshop, held in Innsbruck in September 2024, began this community effort.
