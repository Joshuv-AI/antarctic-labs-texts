GABA produced by multiple bone marrow cell types regulates hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells
Authors: Cesi Deng, Adedamola Elujoba-Bridenstine, Ai Tang Song, Rylie M. Ceplina, Casey J. Ostheimer, Molly C. Pellitteri Hahn, Cameron O. Scarlett, Sahitya Saka, Xuan Pan, Owen J. Tamplin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) maintain homeostasis of the blood system by balancing proliferation and differentiation. Many extrinsic signals in the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment that regulate this balance are still unknown. We report gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) metabolite produced in the BM as a regulator of HSPCs. Deletion of the genes encoding the glutamate decarboxylase enzymes (Gad1 and Gad2) that produce GABA in either B lineages or endothelial cells (ECs) led to a slight reduction in BM HSPCs but not GABA levels. However, simultaneous blockade of GABA production from both hematopoietic cells and ECs resulted in a greater reduction of HSPCs and a significant reduction of BM GABA levels. Lower GABA levels in the BM altered the gene expression profile of HSPCs, with expression reduced for proliferation-associated genes and increased for B lineage genes. Our findings suggest GABA from multiple sources coordinates to regulate HSPC activity.

Introduction

Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) replenish the entire blood system throughout life (Pinho and Frenette, 2019). The balance between HSPC proliferation and differentiation is tightly regulated by extrinsic signals in their microenvironment, also known as the niche. In adults, bone marrow (BM) is the primary site of hematopoiesis and is made up of a highly complex array of different niche cell types. Although research has focused on the cytokines, other growth factors, receptor ligands, and adhesion molecules produced by these different niche cells, there are also understudied factors such as metabolites and neurotransmitters that play a role in regulating HSPC activity. For example, peripheral nerves can provide adrenergic signals that act on stromal cells to regulate HSPC mobilization (Méndez-Ferrer et al., 2008), and dopamine can act directly acting on neuroreceptors on the HSPC surface (Liu et al., 2021). The full range of secreted immune metabolites are reviewed elsewhere (Zhang et al., 2022).

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is a major neurotransmitter in the central nervous system (CNS). GABA binds to two classes of receptors, ionotropic GABAA receptor and metabotropic GABAB receptor. Interestingly, both types of GABA receptors have been found on HSPCs. GABA type A receptor subunit rho 1 (GABRR1) marks a subset of HSPCs and its deletion inhibited megakaryocyte differentiation and reduced blood platelets (Zhu et al., 2019). In our previous work, we showed that loss of GABA type B receptor subunit 1 (GABBR1) reduced proliferation and reconstitution capacity of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), and impaired B cell production (Shao et al., 2021). While these findings suggest GABA has a pleiotropic role in regulating HSPC activity, the sources of GABA in the BM remain unclear. We examined the spatial distribution of GABA in the BM using imaging mass spectrometry and found it was enriched in the endosteal region of the mouse femur (Shao et al., 2021). GABA production is catalyzed by glutamate decarboxylases 1 and 2 (GAD1 and GAD2) (Erlander et al., 1991). Work by us and others showed GAD1 but not GAD2 is expressed in human and mouse B cells that are also enriched for GABA metabolite (Shao et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). We sought to block GABA production from various BM niche cell types. We show that B cells and endothelial cells (ECs) produce GABA, which is required for the maintenance of the HSPC pool. HSPCs are primed to commit to the B cell lineage when GABA level in the microenvironment is low. We propose that GABA serves as an environmental cue to regulate the decision between HSPC proliferation and B cell production to maintain homeostasis of the B cell pool.

Results

B cells are a source of GABA in the bone marrow niche

To determine if B cells are an autonomous source of GABA, we tracked GABA production throughout B cell development using the OP9 stromal cell co-culture system (Holmes and Zuniga-Pflucker, 2009; Shao et al., 2021). In brief, HSPCs sorted from wild-type (WT) mice were co-cultured with stromal cells and examined at 3-day intervals (Figures 1A and 1B). GABA level in the co-culture media was determined by mass spectrometric analysis, and B lineage cell production was determined by flow cytometry. We found Cd19+ cells emerged on day 9 and underwent rapid expansion in the following three days (Figures 1A–1C). GABA level in co-culture media was normalized against the trace amount of GABA in fetal bovine serum (Figure 1C). During B cell differentiation GABA level remained low until day 9, then increased dramatically at day 12 (Figure 1C). To confirm the in vivo functional requirement of GABA production by B cells in the BM we generated mouse models with B cell-specific deletion of Gad1 and Gad2 (Gad1/2). We crossed Cd19-Cre (Rickert et al., 1997) and Gad1/2fl/fl;Rosa26tdTomato mouse lines (Meng et al., 2016) to generate Cd19-Cre;Gad1/2fl/fl;Rosa26tdTomato conditional knockout (CKO) (Cd19-CreCKO; Figure 1D). Cd19-Cre activity was shown by the percentage of double-positive Cd19+/tdTomato+ cells (Figure S1A). We isolated HSPCs from Cd19-CreCKO mice and induced their differentiation into B cells using the OP9 co-culture system (Figures 1B–1E). While we observed no significant change in B cell output, there was significant reduction of GABA level in co-cultures seeded with HSPCs from Cd19-CreCKO mice (Figure 1E).

We used mass spectrometry to measure GABA levels in femurs from Cd19-CreCKO mice but did not find a significant difference compared to controls (Figure 1F). Consistent with previously published results that used the B lineage-specific conditional Gad1 knockout model Mb1-Cre;Gad1fl/fl (Zhang et al., 2021), we also observed no change in BM HSPC numbers (Figure 1G). However, we did observe a decrease in the proportion of BM HSPCs (Figure 1G), a phenotype that was not tested previously (Zhang et al., 2021), suggesting B cell-derived GABA may directly regulate HSPCs. We found no difference in BM cellularity, number or percentage of B220+ cells, common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs), committed B lineage progenitors, or PB B220+ cells (Figures S2A–S2E).

Endothelial cells are a source of GABA in the bone marrow niche

Data from our group and others found BM endothelial cells (BMECs) express Gad1 and are therefore a potential source of GABA (Shao et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2019). Notably, published mouse BM stroma single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets do not show Gad1 expression in any populations (Baryawno et al., 2019), while quantitative PCR and bulk RNA-seq do show expression in B cells and BMECs (Shao et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2019), suggesting Gad1 transcript may be expressed at low levels. To functionally test if ECs contribute to GABA production in BM, we generated an endothelial-specific Gad1/2 deletion by crossing Cdh5-CreERT2 (Sörensen et al., 2009) and Gad1/2fl/fl;Rosa26tdTomato lines to generate Cdh5-CreERT2;Gad1/2fl/fl;Rosa26tdTomato mice (Cdh5-CreERT2CKO; Figure 2A). Tamoxifen was administered at 5–6 weeks and experiments were performed at 8–12 weeks. Cdh5-CreERT2 activity was confirmed by the presence of tdTomato+ BMECs (Figure S1B). There was no significant decrease in GABA levels in Cdh5-CreERT2CKO BM (Figure 2B). However, there was a reduction in both number and proportion of HSPCs (Figure 2C). No change was detected in BM cellularity, number or percentage of B220+ cells, CLPs, committed B lineage progenitors, or PB B cells and myeloid cells (Figures S2F–S2J). Hematopoietic and BMEC-derived GABA synergize to regulate HSPC activity

To understand the contribution of multiple sources of GABA in the BM, we generated a Tie2-Cre;Gad1/2fl/fl;Rosa26tdTomato mouse model (Tie2-CreCKO; Figure 3A). Tie2-Cre is expressed in all ECs during embryonic development and into adulthood (Kisanuki et al., 2001). In the embryo, the hemogenic endothelium gives rise to all definitive HSPCs, meaning in adult Tie2-CreCKO mice all ECs and hematopoietic cells have undergone Cre excision (Tang et al., 2010). Therefore, Tie2-CreCKO enabled us to evaluate the effect of Gad1/2 deletion in all ECs and hematopoietic cells, inclusive of both B cells and BMECs. Cre activity was confirmed by detection of BM tdTomato+/CD45+ hematopoietic cells and BMECs (Figure S1C). We measured GABA levels in the BM of Tie2-CreCKO mice and detected a ∼60% reduction compared to control mice (Figure 3B). Considering that deletion of Gad1/2 from B cells (Figure 1F) or BMECs alone (Figure 2B) did not lead to a significant reduction of BM GABA, our results using Tie2-CreCKO suggest B cells and BMECs synergize to sustain homeostatic BM GABA levels. BM cellularity, number or percentage of B220+ cells, CLPs, and PB myeloid cells were unchanged (Figures S3A–S3D). As in Cdh5-CreERT2CKO (Figure 2C), we found a decrease in HSPC percentage and number (Figure 3C). We found a significant increase in pre-B cells in Tie2-CreCKO (Figure 3D), while other committed B lineage progenitors were unaffected (Figure S3E). We did observe a slight reduction in percentage of PB B220+ cells (Figure 3E). Although Cd19-CreCKO, Cdh5-CreERT2CKO, and Tie2-CreCKO all showed a decrease in the proportion of BM HSPCs, only the combined hematopoietic and EC-specific deletion of Gad1/2 with Tie2-CreCKO resulted in a significant reduction in BM GABA levels and HSPC abundance (Figure 3F). Reduced BM GABA levels induced a shift in gene expression profiles of HSPCs

To determine if attenuated BM GABA levels result in intrinsic molecular changes in HSPCs, we isolated HSPCs from Cd19-CreCKO, Cdh5-CreERT2CKO, and Tie2-CreCKO and performed bulk RNA-seq. Among the total 21,114 genes detected, there were differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in HSPCs of each model when compared to WT: 494 in Cd19-CreCKO (367 upregulated, 127 downregulated); 284 in Cdh5-CreERT2CKO (131 upregulated, 153 downregulated); 200 in Tie2-CreCKO (73 upregulated, 127 downregulated; false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.1, log2FC > 1 or log2FC < -1); Figures 4A and 4B; Figure S4A). Comparison of DEGs in each mutant showed 21 genes were commonly upregulated upon GABA level reduction, including HSC genes Hlf, Emcn, and Fgd5 (Figures 4A–4C) (Calvanese et al., 2022; Engelhard et al., 2024; Gazit et al., 2014; Holmfeldt et al., 2016; Matsubara et al., 2005; Reckzeh et al., 2018; Ueno et al., 2001). Our previous analysis of GABA receptor Gabbr1 null HSPCs found they were less proliferative, less responsive to poly(I:C), and had defective B cell differentiation (Shao et al., 2021). Intriguingly, phenotypic Gabbr1 null HSPCs exhibited a shift in transcriptional profiles from undifferentiated progenitor to more B lineage committed progenitor (Shao et al., 2021). We hypothesized loss of Gabbr1 in HSPCs would simulate attenuated GABA signaling and therefore mimic HSPCs from a low GABA microenvironment. To test this, we considered DEGs separately in Cd19-CreCKO, Cdh5-CreERT2CKO, and Tie2-CreCKO models and performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for HSPCs from each model (Figures 4D and 4E; Figures S4C–S4E; supplementary tables). Like Gabbr1 null HSPCs, we found B cell activation and receptor signaling were enriched in all mutant models compared to WT (Figures S4C–S4E). Notably, immunoglobulin heavy constant alpha (Igha) was strongly upregulated in all CKO models (log2FC > 6) (Figure 4C). During B cell development Igha expression occurs at pro-B cell stage and induces pre-B cell development (Meffre et al., 2001), and we did observe an increase in pre-B cell fraction in Tie2-Cre model (Figure 3D). Together, this suggests reduced GABA level can promote B cell lineage commitment. We also found interferon signaling pathways were highly upregulated in all CKO models (Figure 4D). Consistently, our previous analysis showed a positive correlation between Gabbr1 expression in HSPCs and interferon signaling-related genes (Shao et al., 2021). Although E2F and MYC targets and G2M checkpoint genes were under-represented in all CKO HSPCs (Figure 4E), suggesting reduced proliferative capacity, we did not observe changes in cell cycle status in Tie2-CreCKO HSPCs (Figure S3F). Together, this shows phenotypic HSPCs from a low GABA microenvironment have a similar intrinsic profile to HSPCs that lack GABBR1 receptor signaling.

There were similarities between Cd19-CreCKO, Cdh5-CreERT2CKO, and Tie2-CreCKO models; however, we wanted to consider if there were differences in HSPC regulation dependent on the cellular source of GABA. The hierarchical clustering of DEGs from all models showed clusters that were unique to B lineages, BMECs, or both (Figure 4A). Intriguingly, a curated list of B lineage genes (Miyai et al., 2018) found some of these markers were only strongly upregulated in Cd19-CreCKO (Cd79a, Cd79b, and Vpreb1; Figure S4B; supplementary tables). Mecom, an important regulator of HSCs (Voit et al., 2023), was only upregulated in models that deleted Gad1/2 from BMECs (Cdh5-CreERT2CKO and Tie2-CreCKO). These findings suggest that HSPCs may be differentially regulated by GABA depending on their location in the BM niche, and their proximity to B cells or BMECs. Our data show reduced GABA production in BM promotes HSPC priming toward B cell lineage commitment, while suppressing HSPC proliferation genes. Overall, our results reveal a role for GABA as a molecular switch to regulate HSPC activity and B lineage priming.

Discussion

GABA is well-known as a neurotransmitter in the CNS but has also been detected in the peripheral nervous system, gastrointestinal track, pancreas, and liver (Zhang et al., 2022). In these peripheral tissues, GABA has diverse functions beyond transmitting neural signals, such as regulating immune cell activity in lymph nodes. We demonstrate GABA serves as an environmental cue in BM to regulate HSPC differentiation into B cells and maintain the stem cell pool. Despite detectable GABA in peripheral tissues, the sources of GABA remain largely unknown. We show that B cells and BMECs synergize to maintain homeostatic levels of GABA in the BM; however, GABA was not completely absent in Tie2-CreCKO BM, suggesting other unknown GABA sources. Single-cell RNA-seq datasets from mouse BM stromal cells did not identify cells expressing Gad1 or Gad2 (Baryawno et al., 2019), possibly because mRNA expression levels are low. While GAD1 is considered the primary pathway for GABA biosynthesis, other mechanisms have been found, including catalysis of putrescine by aldehyde dehydrogenases (Kim et al., 2015; Li et al., 2025). Further investigation is needed to identify all GABA sources in the BM.

Although there were similarities in HSPCs from all CKO models, some B lineage-specific genes (Cd79a, Cd79b, and Vpreb1) were uniquely upregulated in Cd19-CreCKO. These transcriptional differences likely arise from blockade of GABA production in different niche cell types. Spatial mapping of lymphopoiesis in the sternum revealed CLPs reside in an arteriolar niche, surrounded by pre-pro-B, pro-B, and pre-B cells (Wu et al., 2024). Within these B cell production lines, lineage-committed cells are located further away from CLPs. While both B lineages and BMECs are GABA sources, lymphoid progenitors are proximal to arterioles and more distant from pro-B and pre-B cells (Wu et al., 2024). Therefore, HSPCs may be differentially impacted by GABA sources depending on their spatial relationships and location in microdomains of the BM.

Downstream-differentiated hematopoietic cells have been recognized as regulators of their own upstream HSPCs through feedback mechanisms (Kirouac et al., 2010). For example, platelet-biased vWF+ HSCs associate with megakaryocyte niches and are regulated by CXCL4 produced by megakaryocytes (Pinho et al., 2018). We demonstrate that GABA-producing B lineages and BMECs regulate GABBR1+ HSPCs, emphasizing the importance of feedback mechanisms in the BM niche to maintain homeostasis. Our findings suggest clinical neuromodulators could be repurposed to target hematopoietic and immune systems for therapeutic benefits.

Methods

Mice

All mice were housed in the breeding core at UW-Madison. Protocols were approved by UW-Madison Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Details of lines in supplemental methods.

Flow cytometry

Data were collected on BD LSRII Fortessa or Thermo Fisher Attune Next Flow Cytometers and data were analyzed using FlowJo v.10.10. HSPCs were purified using BD FACSAria and BD FACSDiscover S8 cell sorters.

Flow cytometry of hematopoietic populations

Femurs and tibias were harvested from mice (8–12 weeks old) and crushed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Corning, 21-040-CV) containing 2% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum with mortar and pestle. PB was collected by cardiac puncture. RBCs were removed by lysis with BD PharmLyse Lysing Buffer (BD Biosciences, 555899). For HSPC isolation, BM cells were pre-enriched for c-Kit+ cells using c-Kit microBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-091-224) and a Midi MACS separator (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-042-301). BM, PB, or c-Kit-enriched cells were then stained for 30 min at 4°C with combinations of antibodies (see supplemental methods).

Flow cytometry for bone marrow stromal cells

Femurs were cut at the end and the BM was flushed out using digestion buffer (HBSS, Corning, 21-023-CV; 2 mg/mL Dispase II, Sigma, D4693; Collagenase, Sigma, C9891) and incubated in digestion buffer for enzymatic dissociation for 30 min at 37°C. Cells were washed with staining buffer and filtered through 70 μM strainer. Red blood cells were lysed using BD PharmLyse Lysing Buffer (BD Biosciences, 555899). Cells were stained for 1 h at 4°C with antibodies (see supplemental methods).

Mass spectrometric detection of GABA

For liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis of GABA, mouse bones were prepared by homogenization, precipitation, and filtration (see supplemental methods).

In vitro B cell differentiation assay

OP9 stromal cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (CRL-2749) and were grown as previously described (Shao et al., 2021) (see supplemental methods).

Bulk RNA-seq

LSK (Lin-/Sca-1+/c-Kit+) cells from 8 to 12-week-old WT or Cd19-CreCKO, Cdh5-CreERT2CKO, and Tie2-CreCKO (triplicates for each group) were sorted using BD FACSAria Cell Sorter or BD FACSDiscover S8 Cell Sorter and collected in Trizol LS (Invitrogen). For RNA isolation, library preparation, sequencing, and data analysis (see supplemental methods).

Statistics

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparison between single experimental and control groups, Welch’s t test was used unless otherwise specified. For comparison between multiple groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, and Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) was used as the post-hoc test to determine differences between groups. p < 0.05 is considered as statistical significance. Mouse experiments combined biological replicates from 2 to 3 independent experiments, except Figure S2I that was one experiment. GraphPad Prism 10.4 was used for all analyses.
