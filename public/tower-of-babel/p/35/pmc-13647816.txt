Between fate and fault: a qualitative study on the presence, absence and ambivalence of guilt in subjective theories of illness among patients with advanced cancer in palliative care
Authors: Anna Kitta, Nestor D Kapusta, Franziska Ecker, Joachim Bär, Feroniki Adamidis, Lea Kum, Eva K Masel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatients’ personal beliefs about illness shape how they experience the disease. This study explores how individuals with a terminal cancer diagnosis understand the nature and causes of their illness and whether their explanations include feelings of guilt or personal responsibility.MethodsThis study employs a qualitative methodology. It comprises 23 semi-structured interviews with patients living with cancer admitted to the palliative care unit of a tertiary hospital in Austria. The interviews were recorded digitally and transcribed verbatim. The data were analyzed using thematic analysis and MAXQDA 2022 software.ResultsThree main themes could be identified within the interviews: Patients shared their (1) understanding of the disease and its origin and their (2) self-evaluation in the context of guilt—being non-guilty or guilty or avoiding judgment when applying the possible causes to their individual situation. Finally, the interviews showed the participant’s (3) agency and adaptation in response to causal attributions.ConclusionSome patients with advanced cancer at the end-of-life experience guilt and blame themselves. In palliative care settings, understanding beliefs on illness causation is essential for meaningful clinician–patient communication. Additionally, more emphasis needs to be placed on providing patients with information on the origins of oncological diseases early in the disease trajectory. Addressing patients’ subjective theories of illness sensitively and being aware of them will enable clinicians to improve end-of-life care by adopting patient-centered strategies of care.Keywords: Palliative care, Guilt, Subjective theories of illness, End-of-life care, Qualitative research

Introduction

Subjective theories of illness (STOIs) refer to the individual cognitive frameworks through which patients interpret the origin, meaning, and trajectory of their disease. These personal explanatory models are shaped by lived experience, social context, cultural narratives, and different information sources, and they do not necessarily align with medical or scientific explanations [1, 2]. STOIs are therefore particularly relevant in cancer care, where patients frequently confront existential questions concerning causality, responsibility, and meaning. Patients’ attempts to understand why illness occurs are neither unique to cancer nor to contemporary medicine. Medical anthropologist Kleinman described explanatory models as the beliefs and understandings that patients and clinicians hold regarding the causes, course, and treatment of illness, emphasizing that these interpretations may differ substantially from biomedical explanations [3, 4]. More broadly, anthropological research has shown that people rarely seek explanations for disease in general but rather for why a particular illness occurred to a particular person at a particular time. Evans-Pritchard’s classic work among the Azande illustrated how individuals search for meaning in experiences of misfortune and suffering through culturally shared explanatory frameworks [5]. Likewise, the concept of lay epidemiology emphasizes that people draw on personal observations, social experiences, and culturally shared beliefs when constructing explanations for illness and health risks [6]. Together, these perspectives suggest that patients’ causal attributions are not simply misunderstandings of biomedical knowledge but meaningful attempts to explain illness, locate responsibility, and restore coherence in the face of uncertainty.

Attributions about illness causation are closely linked to self-conscious emotions such as guilt and shame. Guilt arises when individuals perceive that they have violated moral or social standards, and it often motivates reparative or corrective action as it is linked to feelings of remorse and a sense of responsibility [7]. This has been used by the public health domain, and guilt appeals have been shown to be a powerful tool to promote health-related attitudes and intentions through describing negative consequences of a risky behavior [8]. However, feelings of guilt may also contribute to distress, concealment, and stigma [9]. Cancer-related stigma has been associated with higher distress, reduced quality of life, depressive symptoms, social isolation, and barriers to help-seeking and treatment engagement [9–11]. Quantitative studies further suggest that self-blame is associated with greater perceived stigma and poorer psychosocial adjustment among cancer patients [12].

Cancer-related guilt has been documented across diagnostic groups. Early psycho-oncological work already described guilt as a prominent reaction to cancer [13, 14]. While studies of patients with hematological malignancies undergoing curative treatment have not identified guilt as a central theme [2, 15], other cancer contexts demonstrate stronger associations. In particular, guilt and stigma are frequently reported in lung cancer, especially when smoking is implicated [16–18] and shame has been linked to depressive symptoms in individuals with lung cancer [20]. Patients who attributed their cancer to their own behavior reported higher levels of guilt than those who did not endorse behavioral causation. In particular, smoking status was significantly associated with heightened guilt among individuals with breast, prostate, and lung cancer [19].

Beyond individual patient experiences, public perception studies further demonstrate that lung cancer is more strongly associated with personal responsibility and blame compared to other malignancies [20]. Social media analyses reveal that public discourse around cancer frequently contains emotional language reflecting fear, sadness, and self-blame [21]. Women with cancer may additionally encounter socially mediated moral judgments concerning lifestyle, femininity, and care responsibilities [22].

Beyond behavior-related guilt, a qualitative study conducted in Iran suggest that patients may experience existential forms of guilt, which encompasses regrets about life choices, unrealized potentials, or perceived burdensomeness [23]. Spiritual and religious frameworks can further shape interpretations of suffering and self-blame at the end of life [24].

Despite growing recognition of stigma and guilt in oncology [9–11, 19], little is known about how patients with advanced cancer in palliative care settings construct causal explanations of their illness and whether guilt forms part of these subjective theories. Most existing studies focus on earlier treatment phases or specific tumor entities. The end-of-life context, however, represents a distinct existential and relational situation in which questions of responsibility, regret, and meaning may acquire particular urgency.

The present qualitative study therefore explores STOIs among patients with advanced cancer in a palliative care unit (PCU), with special attention to experiences of self-responsibility and guilt. This study aims to contribute to a nuanced understanding of how causal attributions shape emotional experience, coping, and clinical communication at the end of life.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative design using semi-structured interviews to explore how patients in a PCU understand the origin of their oncological disease and whether these explanations are associated with feelings of guilt.

Interviews were conducted in the PCU of a university hospital in Austria. Due to participants’ physical frailty, interviews were primarily conducted at the bedside and, when feasible, in the PCU meeting room to minimize interruptions. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Vienna (Approval no. 1206/2020) and adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [25].

Participants and data collection

Participants were eligible if they were 18 years of age or older, able to communicate verbally, and capable of providing informed consent. Participants were recruited by AK, a physician trained in qualitative research who was employed at the PCU at the time of data collection but not involved in participants’ clinical care. She provided written and verbal study information materials in person, explicitly emphasising voluntariness and the absence of any impact on treatment; and obtained consent during a follow-up visit. Post-interview support was ensured through access to ward-based nursing, psychological, and medical staff as well as the interviewer’s contact details for any further concerns. All eligible patients were approached consecutively, and included upon providing written informed consent, including explicit permission for audio recording of the interview.

Interviews were conducted by the first author (AK). A semi-structured interview guide (see Table 1) was used to elicit narratives regarding participants’ understanding of their illness and its origin. Additionally, baseline data were assessed using the clinician-rated “Potsdamer Belastungsscreening bei Krebspatienten – Kurzform” (Po-Bado-KF), a standardized instrument that systematically captures socio-demographic and medical information as well as overall psychosocial distress, physical symptoms, and limitations in daily life [26].

All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Field notes were taken to document contextual factors, non-verbal expressions, and post-interview reflections. Relevant content shared after formal recording was documented immediately following the interview and incorporated into the analytic process. The interviewer also recorded reflexive notes after each interview to enhance transparency regarding positionality and interpretive influence.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis [27–29]. Analysis was supported by MAXQDA software [30]. Coding was primarily conducted by AK. To enhance analytic rigor, the first three interviews were independently coded by AK and EKM, followed by discussion and refinement of the coding framework. Coding and theme development proceeded iteratively and inductively, with codes evolving throughout the analytic process. Themes were generated through interpretive engagement with the data and organized in relation to the research question: How do patients explain their oncological disease, and is this accompanied by feelings of guilt? Data collection concluded when sufficient depth and richness of data had been achieved to meaningfully address the research question [31].

During the preparation of this work the authors used the artificial intelligence programs DeepL, Grammarly, and ChatGPT for language support, including the translation of German quotes, language correction, and grammar refinement, but not for idea generation or content creation [32–34]. All text was reviewed and edited by the authors, who take full responsibility for the manuscript.

Results

A total of 23 participants with advanced, incurable cancer were interviewed (see Fig. 1). Interviews lasted between 9 and 76 min (mean 32 min). The sample comprised 12 women (52%) and 11 men (48%), aged 23 to 85 years (mean age 64). The mean ECOG performance status was 2 (median 3).

Consort diagram of included patients

Primary diagnoses included cancers of the upper gastrointestinal tract (stomach, esophagus, pancreas, liver) (n = 7), lower gastrointestinal tract (colon, rectum, anal carcinoma) (n = 4), breast cancer (n = 3), lung cancer (n = 3), and hematological malignancies (n = 3). Additional diagnoses included otolaryngological tumor, urothelial carcinoma, and soft tissue sarcoma (n = 1 each) (see Table 2). Participants are referred to as P1–P23.

Three overarching themes were identified through reflexive thematic analysis (see Table 3). The interviews revealed that each participant had considered the cause of their illness and asked themselves “Why me?”. Theme 1 describes how participants constructed and negotiated explanatory models of cancer causation. Theme 2 captures how participants connected certain causal explanations to their individual cases, highlighting processes of guilt, rejection of responsibility, and ambivalence. Theme 3 outlines how causal interpretations informed relational, psychological, and behavioral coping practices.

Theme 1: understanding the disease and its origin

Communicated knowledge

Biomedical anchoring of illness narratives

Theme 1 captures how participants constructed and negotiated understandings of their illness and its origin. Most participants began the interviews by describing their illness in biomedical terms, referring to tumor size, localization, metastases, diagnostic findings, and previous treatments. These accounts often included precise medical terminology and reflected information conveyed by healthcare professionals.

Without being directly prompted, many participants also referred to the life-limiting nature of their illness and their awareness of approaching death. Some described drawing conclusions about disease progression based on bodily sensations or interpretations of diagnostic results. However, most reported that their understanding was primarily based on information provided by physicians and other healthcare professionals.

Negotiating uncertainty and informational insufficiency

Despite demonstrating detailed biomedical knowledge, participants also emphasized uncertainties and gaps in understanding. One participant stated, “sometimes I don’t even know what to think about that” (P06). Another described the difficulty of navigating medical information and formulating appropriate questions during brief clinical encounters:

“I’m rather the one asking questions. ‘I can imagine it this way and that way, am I imagining it correctly? Am I right here? How can I visualize this?’ A few open questions on a ward round. There will be five minutes (…). The question is whether you can manage with the three questions. Or whether you won’t stumble hopelessly into the next three to nine [questions]. And whether the thing [clinical matter] won’t get too big for you. Without feeling stupid or uneducated. Simply, I’m not a doctor.” (P10).

Some participants reported hesitating to ask questions, while others described actively seeking clarification, particularly regarding the origin of their disease. When clear explanations were not provided, this occasionally resulted in mistrust. For example, one participant described asking about possible causes and receiving the response that the cause was unknown. She interpreted this as withholding information and expressed distrust toward medical professionals (P06).

Several participants reported disappointment regarding insufficient discussions about the etiology of their disease. One stated, “I was never properly informed about this disease. I was only told that they didn’t know whether I would survive. That’s all I ever heard from the doctors” (P02).

In some cases, initial medical explanations appeared to shape patients’ own causal narratives, even when subsequent diagnostic findings contradicted earlier assumptions. One participant (P02) initially presented to the emergency department with severe gastric pain and was told it was likely gastritis related to stress and lifestyle factors. She remembered being told by the physician, “No, Mrs [P02], there will be no examination now, it’s gastritis, I’m sure of it, drink less Red Bull, you have a lot of stress, work, the children, it comes from that. If you have time in the course of the year, [you] should have a gastroscopy.” After later being diagnosed with gastric cancer and liver metastases, she incorporated the earlier stress-related explanation into her personal account of the cancer’s origin: “I only thought it might be the Red Bull (…) and the stress. I worked too much, (…). And I thought due to this. But lots of other people do the same and don’t get cancer. So, I can’t say what the cause is. But that’s my guess, stress and Red Bull.”

Socially mediated explanatory models

Participants described receiving explanatory models not only from healthcare professionals but also from family members, colleagues, and acquaintances. While most denied being directly blamed for their illness, they reported encountering various interpretations of cancer causation within their social environments. These perspectives were generally described as supportive or meaningful. For example, one participant (P01) referred to a spiritually oriented acquaintance who attributed the illness to ancestral energy. P04 mentioned colleagues who associated cancer with trauma or burnout. Only one participant (P23) reported experiencing explicit accusations. She described being asked in a reproachful manner what she had done “20 years ago” to cause her cancer, which she experienced as distressing.

Patient explanations of possible causes of oncological disease

Participants articulated multiple hypotheses regarding cancer etiology, distinguishing between uncontrollable and modifiable factors.

Attributions to structural and uncontrollable factors

Several participants contextualized their diagnosis in relation to genetic predisposition, referring to cancer among family members. Age and statistical probability were also mentioned.

Environmental influences were frequently discussed. Participants referred to nuclear power plants, the Chernobyl nuclear disaster, electromagnetic radiation (e.g., mobile networks, DECT phones), environmental pollution, toxins, and broader technological or political developments perceived as harmful to health. Urban living conditions, reduced contact with nature, and diminished community structures were also described as potentially detrimental.

Stressful or traumatic life events were repeatedly mentioned as possible contributing factors. Participants associated stress with excessive work demands, lack of sleep, caregiving responsibilities, bereavement, accidents, experiences of violence, depression, burnout, relationship difficulties, financial strain, migration-related stressors, and previous illness. These experiences were described as cumulative burdens potentially linked to cancer development.

Attributions to lifestyle and personal responsibility

Participants also discussed modifiable lifestyle factors. Nutrition was frequently mentioned, ranging from specific childhood memories (“we ate moldy jam on bread as children,” P04) to general dietary patterns and beliefs about sugar consumption. Balanced diets rich in vegetables and low in meat and fat were commonly described as protective.

Smoking was widely recognized as a risk factor, particularly for lung cancer. Alcohol consumption, insufficient physical activity, and lack of physical fitness were also identified as potential causes.

In addition to behavioral factors, participants described “invisible” personal characteristics as contributing to illness. Traits such as perfectionism, excessive self-imposed pressure, overinvolvement, and negative attitudes or thinking patterns were framed as potentially harmful and causally relevant.

Theme 2: self-evaluation in the context of guilt

Theme 2 captures how participants evaluated their own responsibility in light of the causal explanations outlined in Theme 1. Following the discussion of possible etiologies, participants positioned themselves along a spectrum ranging from explicit rejection of guilt to self-blame and regret. These positions were rarely static and often marked by ambivalence.

Rejecting personal responsibility: external forces and uncontrollability

Most participants did not attribute responsibility for their cancer to themselves. Instead, they framed the illness as resulting from forces beyond personal control, including coincidence, bad luck, fate, or spiritual causation.

Several participants emphasized pure coincidence. One participant with a scientific background described cancer occurrence as largely coincidental:

“It’s usually a bit of a coincidence as to whether you’ll get it or not. (…) I’m a math teacher in my first subject and have a slightly different approach to probabilities from physics. (…) I don’t want to say that I didn’t always care [about resolving the question of whether cancer was caused by chance or by a specific reason]. But I was able to combine the two, to put it that way.” (P18).

The participants also shared spiritual explanations. They could be specifically ascribing a distinct responsible spiritual concept (e.g., energies from ancestors or God) or be diffuse (fate). Some referred to fate as a means of accepting their diagnosis. As one participant explained: “You just accept fate as it comes. Because I can’t change it, it’s not in my power” (P11). When spiritual interpretations were articulated, some referenced to God’s will in a Christian sense, while others used diffuse formulations such as “Only the good Lord knows” (P20, 22).

A further dimension concerned perceptions of injustice. Several participants described cancer as unrelated to moral deservingness or behavior. One participant contrasted lifestyle-related cardiovascular disease with cancer, which she described as “insidious” and beyond control:

“I don’t feel guilty. (…) I’ve always believed that life is fair. (…) And life is not fair. (…) I don’t think you choose anything. (…) But with cancer, which is so insidious, I don’t think there’s anything you can do. (…) Well, what helped me was to accept the most important thing: the world is not fair.” (P22).

Across these accounts, rejection of guilt was closely linked to acceptance of limited agency and acknowledgment of existential unpredictability (Fig. 2).

Self-blame, guilt and regret: lifestyle, prevention, and life conduct

In contrast, other participants articulated explicit feelings of guilt. When present, these were only for a few patients linked to lifestyle behaviors or missed preventive measures. For example, one participant regretted unhealthy eating and alcohol consumption (P10). Another expressed self-blame for not attending recommended cancer screening examinations:

“We would probably have discovered all this earlier if I had gone for a mammogram. I actually have to partly blame myself for that. (…) Yes, one of the reasons was that I was sloppy and didn’t go for a mammogram. That was my mistake, and the colonoscopy was actually my first, where they say you should have it from the age of 50. And I was already 61 at the time anyway and didn’t do it. And again, I can only blame myself. (…) If I had gone regularly, they would have taken it away as a polyp. And I could have spared myself everything.” (P12).

More frequently, however, guilt and regret were framed less in biomedical terms and more in relation to broader life conduct and psychological disposition. Participants reflected critically on past conformist behaviors and inner attitudes that they seemed to see differently now, such as excessive ambition, neglect of self-care, overcommitment, or remaining too long in certain relationships.

One participant described the illness as a consequence of not caring adequately for herself: “I should have looked after myself a bit better (…) not be so terribly ambitious and dutiful (…) You have to take really good care of yourself, because nobody takes responsibility for you” (P05).

Another participant explicitly attributed causation to internal tension and unresolved grief, stating:

“And I’m just of the opinion that, to put it bluntly, you cultivate it yourself. Nobody wants to hear that, not even a psychologist, when you say it’s your fault. (…) In my opinion, it’s very much to blame, something like that, when you get so tense inside (…) and you just can’t get away from it. I just didn’t understand that the time was over, that it was over [referring to the mourning phase after the death of the parents]. (…) So, I have brought about a bit of a change in myself, or yes, the illness, which in the end I ultimately caused myself anyway.” (P03). (Fig. 3)

The interviews also revealed that the distinction between considering oneself guilty and not guilty was not always clear. On the contrary, most conversations unveiled deep ambivalence.

Ambivalence and suspension of moral judgment

For many participants, the distinction between guilt and innocence remained unresolved. Rather than adopting a definitive position, they described uncertainty, disengagement from the question, or recognition of multifactorial causation.

Some explicitly stated that they had not reflected extensively on the origin of their illness: “To be honest, I don’t think about it that much” (P09) and “I haven’t dealt with it (…) I do what the doctor tells me” (P20).

Others avoided moral positioning by emphasizing complexity and the interaction of multiple factors. One participant described cancer as the result of multiple influences:

“There are several [origins], simply due to environmental influences, heredity, and genetics. What else is there, in my opinion? Yes, perhaps a small percentage is also psychological, due to worry. I don’t think it’s just one factor. It’s a combination of several factors. But it’s clear that anxiety also influences the immune system, so there’s no point in getting upset.” (P13).

In these narratives, uncertainty functioned as a means of suspending moral evaluation. For some, the question of causation had become less relevant over time, replaced by a pragmatic focus on treatment and coping (Fig. 4).

Theme 3: agency and adaptation in response to causal attributions

Theme 3 describes how participants translated causal interpretations into relational, cognitive, and behavioral coping strategies. Causal attributions were not only explanatory but also action-guiding, shaping how participants engaged with others, themselves, and the healthcare system.

Relational negotiation: seeking support, setting boundaries and selective disclosure

Importance of relationships and conversations

Many participants described conversations with others as central to processing questions about the origin of their illness. Friends, family members, and fellow patients supported reflection and meaning-making.

Peer exchange with other patients was frequently described as particularly supportive due to shared experiential knowledge. These interactions occurred both during treatment and in online communities. Conversations with friends and family were also perceived as helpful. As one participant noted: “You can talk to friends. You have time to explore it in peace and quiet, and then you come up with your own explanation” (P01). Participants who had healthcare professionals within their personal networks described these relationships as especially valuable for interpreting medical information.

Boundary-setting and relational adjustment

Supportive engagement also involved selective withdrawal and boundary-setting. Several participants described becoming more assertive in communicating personal limits. As one participant stated, “I also say what I don’t want. I never used to do that” (P17).

Others described deliberately withholding personal explanatory models to maintain relational harmony. For example, one participant chose not to share his own views about illness causation with hospital staff whose religious framework differed from his own, in order to avoid conflict and preserve trust.

Managing the experience alone

In contrast, some participants emphasized solitary processing. For a few, conversations with friends were perceived as emotionally burdensome because others could not fully understand the experience. Others described intentionally carrying the question of causation alone in order to protect family members from additional distress. In these accounts, solitude functioned as a protective strategy.

Focus on the self

Deliberate reflection and cognitive processing

Time and psychological space were frequently described as necessary for engaging with questions of causation. Participants valued periods of quiet reflection away from everyday routines. As one explained: “I am a person who lives better with answers to a why (…) and I was naturally curious as to whether there was a why” (P10).

Another emphasized the importance of distance from work routines in order to reflect and develop a personal explanation: “you have time, you are away from this office routine, you can think in peace and quiet, (…). You have time to explore it (…), and then you come up with this explanation for yourself” (P01).

Mindset shifts and restoration of agency

Participants described shifts in mindset as central to coping. Psychological attitude was framed as influential in shaping illness experience. As one participant noted, “the psyche of a person is also very important, how you think about it” (P02).

Several described rediscovering personal agency through increased attention to their own needs and values. This was particularly visible in narratives of prior self-neglect and among female participants, suggesting that the experience of self-criticism or neglecting one’s own needs may be more prevalent among women. One participant reflected on earlier difficulties in expressing personal wishes and described self-reflection as transformative: “When I deal with myself, I get the right answers” (P23).

Professional psychological support was described by some as helpful in addressing persistent self-doubt and questions such as “why me?” One participant reported temporary relief from these concerns following therapy sessions focused on self-worth:

“I did therapy again and again in between. And it was actually always about self-worth, increasing my self-esteem, and feeling valued, and this really annoying feeling of ‘why me?’ And that was actually gone every time after the sessions. And then it always came back at some point, but it’s not there now.” (P22).

Acceptance and pragmatic orientation

Over time, several participants described a shift from active searching toward acceptance. Continued rumination about causation was often framed as unproductive or emotionally draining. As one participant stated: “Well, I had seen again, nothing comes out of it. I can blame myself now for whatever was, but I can only try to do better for the rest of the time, to see the good moments. To take more care of myself, even more. It’s no use, it just ruins the remaining time” (P05).

Similar sentiments emphasized letting go of unanswerable questions or adopting a pragmatic stance: “As it is, it is” (P20). Another participant summarized this orientation as living with the uncertainty and “making the best of it” (P11).

Across narratives, acceptance did not necessarily imply resolution of causal questions, but rather a recalibration of focus toward remaining life, recovery, and self-care.

Discussion

Negotiating responsibility for illness

Participants articulated diverse causal explanations for their cancer, ranging from lifestyle behaviors to environmental exposures and fate. While some expressed clear self-blame, others rejected personal responsibility or oscillated between competing explanations. Reflections often extended beyond concrete earlier behaviors toward broader existential concerns, regrets, and moral self-evaluations. In response to their causal attributions, participants reported several adaptive strategies aimed at maintaining agency, such as drawing on social support, establishing personal boundaries, and engaging in self-reflection.

A central finding was that guilt was most often associated with specific behaviors that participants perceived as potentially contributing to their illness, such as dietary habits, stress, or particular lifestyle choices. This demonstrates that guilt often attaches to tangible actions rather than abstract notions of responsibility. Janoff-Bulman differentiates between behavioral self-blame (“I did something wrong”) and characterological self-blame (“I am bad”). Participants in our study primarily engaged in behavioral self-blame, attributing illness to particular actions rather than to negative personal characteristics. Such attributions may serve a psychological function: by locating causality in past actions, participants could imagine, at least hypothetically, reparative measures and thereby preserve a subjective sense of control. In this sense, guilt may have a reparative dimension, motivating attempts to regain influence over one’s remaining life circumstances, for example through lifestyle adjustments, complementary practices, or changes in personal priorities [7].

At the same time, however, the patients’ reflections revealed ambivalence. While some considered whether their behaviors warranted guilt, others questioned whether causality could be established at all, particularly when peers with similar lifestyles remained unaffected, and still others rejected personal responsibility for the development of their illness. These processes demonstrate the ongoing dialogic negotiations patients engage in when dealing with the causes of their illnesses.

Individual responsibility in contemporary prevention discourse

Participants’ interpretations must also be understood within broader cultural and structural contexts. Social scientists have long emphasized the process of medicalization, in which various aspects of human life are increasingly defined by biomedical frameworks [35]. Contemporary health discourses emphasize prevention, risk management, and individual responsibility for health outcomes [36]. While such approaches encourage health-promoting behavior, they may also contribute to the moralization of illness [37]. Within this framework, disease can be interpreted as the consequence of insufficient personal responsibility rather than as the outcome of complex biological and social processes [38, 39]. Several participants in this study appeared to internalize such narratives, attributing responsibility primarily to their own behavior.

Structural determinants and the limits of individual responsibility

Only a small number of participants explicitly referred to broader structural or environmental factors, such as pollution or historical environmental disasters. This pattern mirrors previous sociological observations that contemporary health discourse increasingly positions individuals as responsible health actors [40]. Such narratives are connected to economic interests [38] and risk obscuring systemic influences, thereby fostering what Coughlan described as a “blaming the victim” dynamic [41]. When responsibility for illness is framed primarily at the individual level, structural determinants of health, including socioeconomic inequalities, occupational exposures, or environmental risks, may become less visible [42–45]. The Lancet Commission on the Value of Death further emphasizes that non-communicable diseases cannot be cured; instead, interventions should prioritize prevention, harm reduction, and self-management while addressing the complex social determinants of these chronic conditions [46].

For palliative care, these dynamics are particularly relevant. End-of-life care ideally fosters compassion, solidarity, and relational presence. Excessive emphasis on personal responsibility risks undermining these values and may intensify isolation.

Implications for clinical communication in palliative care

These findings also have implications for clinical communication in palliative care. An important finding was that patients’ STOIs often emerged gradually over the course of conversation, suggesting that eliciting these perspectives benefits from attentive listening and sufficient space for dialogue. Creating such opportunities within clinical encounters may strengthen doctor-patient communication and support more patient-centred care in oncology and palliative settings.

Patients’ STOIs represent meaningful attempts to interpret and integrate illness experiences. However, healthcare professionals may be tempted to evaluate such explanations primarily in terms of biomedical accuracy. Doing so risks what Fricker has described as epistemic injustice, in which patients’ experiential knowledge is dismissed or devalued [47, 48]. Respecting patients’ subjective interpretations, while gently contextualizing them within the scientific understanding of cancer, may therefore be important for maintaining trust and supporting therapeutic relationships.

Participants’ reflections on causality were often embedded in broader processes of life review. At the end of life, questions about the origins of illness can become intertwined with reflections on past decisions, regrets, and relationships. Feelings of guilt, shame, or stigma may therefore contribute to psychological distress and social withdrawal. At the same time, discussing these concerns can open opportunities for meaning-making and connection. Clinicians may support this process by providing space for open conversations about patients’ interpretations of illness, validating emotional experiences, and offering psychological or supportive resources when needed. Interventions addressing guilt may also extend to family members, as caregiver guilt is common in oncology contexts [49].

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The study was conducted at a single PCU in Austria, and participants were receiving specialized palliative care. Experiences and interpretations may therefore differ in other cultural or healthcare contexts. In addition, the interviewer was both a physician and researcher within the clinical environment. Although the interviewer was not directly involved in patient care and efforts were made to conduct non-directive interviews, participants may nonetheless have shaped their responses in relation to perceived medical expectations. Social desirability and reluctance to discuss potentially stigmatized beliefs may therefore have influenced some narratives. The interviewer was aware of, though not specifically trained in the principles of the psychoanalytic free associative narrative interview method [29, 50], which takes into account that each interviewed subject’s responses are guarded by defenses against anxiety, thus emphasizing the need to provide a secure and interested space during interviews with a minimum of interruptions.

Finally, the exploratory qualitative design focused on patients’ experiences at a specific point in the disease trajectory. STOIs are likely to evolve over time as individuals process new information and adapt to changing health circumstances. Longitudinal research could therefore provide further insight into how illness explanations and associated emotions develop throughout the course of advanced disease [15].

Despite these limitations, the qualitative approach enabled in-depth exploration of patients’ lived experiences and provided insight into the complex ways individuals interpret illness and responsibility in the context of life-limiting disease, underscoring the importance of subjective illness narratives in both clinical care and public health discourse [51].

Conclusion

This study highlights how patients with advanced cancer engage in complex interpretive processes when reflecting on the origins of their illness. While some patients do not actively seek explanations, others test out ideas and connect their illnesses to lifestyle or past decisions, occasionally holding themselves responsible. The search for explanations appeared less oriented toward identifying definitive causes than toward negotiating meaning and agency in the face of serious illness. Within this process, STOIs function as tools for sense-making, allowing patients to integrate their diagnosis into their personal life narratives.

For palliative care practice, these findings underscore the importance of acknowledging patients’ explanatory models and emotional responses without reducing them to questions of biomedical accuracy. Conversations that respectfully engage with patients’ interpretations may help reduce feelings of isolation, support meaning-making, and strengthen therapeutic relationships. Providing clear information about the multifactorial nature of cancer while validating patients’ experiences may contribute to more compassionate and patient-centered care for individuals facing life-limiting illness. Acknowledging patients’ subjective explanatory models and emotional experiences can create opportunities for meaningful connection and contribute to more humane, patient-centered oncology and palliative care.
