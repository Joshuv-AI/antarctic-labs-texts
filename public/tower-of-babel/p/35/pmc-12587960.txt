Association of modified primary efficacy response rates at 1 year and outcomes in a cohort of lupus nephritis in Jamaica
Authors: Lori Ann Fisher, Joel Wright, Mahiri Bromfield, Rebecca Thomas-Chen
Published: 2025

Introduction

An estimated 50% of patients with SLE will develop lupus nephritis in their lifetime, with people of African descent more likely to have adverse kidney outcomes and reduced response to therapy.1 2 Early response to therapy increases renal survival, with a modified primary efficacy response (mPERR) at 2 years, associated with up to a 75% and 67% reduced risk of impaired kidney function, end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) and death, respectively.1 Little is known of the clinical and pathological associates of kidney outcomes and response rates in the Anglo-Caribbean, a region with a high prevalence of SLE. We aimed to describe the response rates and outcomes in a cohort of lupus nephritis in Jamaica.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study of adult and paediatric patients who underwent a medical renal biopsy and met clinical criteria for SLE based on Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics criteria, based on physician assessment, was conducted. Medical renal biopsies were performed during the period 1 July 2013–30 June 2020 at the University of the West Indies (UWI), Mona. Institutional ethics approval was obtained from the UWI Ethics Committee, and informed consent for chart review was waived. Inclusion criteria were an adequate biopsy sample (≥10 glomeruli), complete follow-up data, immunofluorescence and light microscopic evaluation. Clinical and laboratory data for persons meeting criteria were reviewed. The outcome of interest was treatment response, defined as meeting mPERR at 1 year (see online supplemental table 1). We used the creatinine and urine excretion measurements at 12 months (±3 months) following the time of biopsy. Secondary outcomes included ESKD (dialysis ≥3 months) and death. Data were analysed using STATA software (V.SE 17.1; StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA). Differences between the primary outcomes were compared using hypothesis testing (Mann-Whitney U test). Cox proportional analysis was performed to determine the association of lack of response with the development of ESKD or death. A complete case analysis was performed.

Results

Analyses included 54 patients with a median (IQR) follow-up time of 3.6 (2.3–5.9) years, a median age of 32.5 years (age range 12–58 years) and 92.6% (50) females (see online supplemental table 2). Median (IQR) haemoglobin was 103 (88–122) g/L. 47% (25) had reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR) (estimated GFR <60 mL/min/1.73m2). Median (IQR) serum albumin was 29 (24–35) g/L, and urinary protein excretion was 1.8 (1–3.2) g in 24 hours. Hypertension was present in 87% (47), haematuria in 60.4% (32), nephrotic syndrome in 48% (25) and acute kidney injury in 52% (28). Most (83%) had class III or IV lupus nephritis, mild interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, 67% (36) had features of chronicity on biopsy, and 83% (45) and 54% (29) had activity. Complete response at 1 year was 54% (27), while mPERR occurred in 36 (72%). 17% (nine) died, four before 1 year. Dialysis occurred in 33% (18/54). Of those who needed dialysis, two had a recovery of kidney function, while one died within 1 year. ESKD occurred in 27.8% (15/54), with a median (IQR) time to dialysis of 1 (0.4–3.5) year. A lower proportion with a complete response (11.1% vs 43.4%, p=0.009) or mPERR (11.1% vs 64.8%, p<0.001) at 1 year progressed to ESKD. A lack of mPERR at 1 year was associated with an almost 9.5-fold increased risk of ESKD (HR 9.47, 95% CI 2.89 to 31.08, p<0.001) and a ninefold increased risk of the composite outcome of death or ESKD (HR 9.18, 95% CI 3.16 to 26.63, p<0.001). Discussion

In this first cohort of lupus nephritis with biopsies using the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society classification from the Anglo-Caribbean, we found a high rate of proliferative nephritis, chronicity on biopsy and rate of ESKD (~28.7%). Just over 70% had an mPERR to treatment at 1 year. Lack of mPERR at 1 year was associated with a 9.5-fold increased risk of ESKD and a ninefold increased risk of ESKD or death.

Our findings are consistent with mPERR rates of 58%–75% and the association of 2-year mPERR with the development of chronic kidney disease and/or death. Although we used the 1-year cut-off, the consistency of these findings enhances the evidence base of use of mPERR as a surrogate for long-term renal survival among diverse ethnicities, resource settings and the argument for earlier application of therapeutic endpoints in clinical trials.

Rates of ESKD remain unchanged compared with prior rates of 25%–30% and are higher than international reports of 10%–15%.13 Direct comparisons of treatment responses are difficult due to heterogeneity in response definitions and treatment regimes. Our rates of ESKD are higher than those reported from other Caribbean countries, however. Data from the French Caribbean reported ESKD rates of 14% at 5 years, despite similar complete response rates at 1 year of 53% vs 54%.2 This may reflect differences in health systems and access to care. In the French Caribbean healthcare, is sponsored through the government, whereas in Jamaica, it is mixed public-private funding, with predominant out-of-pocket payment for immunosuppressant medications and biopsies. Therefore, high rates of ESKD despite similar complete remission rates may reflect a lack of sustainability of care due to costs and/or medication compliance. Furthermore, delays in care may be due to both costs and limitations in access to subspeciality care. This may explain increased chronicity on biopsy and high rates of ESKD. Efforts to enhance early screening of kidney disease in lupus in middle-income countries like Jamaica, facilitate early biopsy and improve medication access are needed.

Our study has some limitations; first, this was a single-centre retrospective study, resulting in selection bias and limiting the representativeness of the findings. There was the possibility of left censorship bias, as deaths occurring outside the institution may not have been captured. Finally, our sample size was small, resulting in lower precision of associations.

Among a cohort of lupus nephritis in Jamaica, treatment response rate at 1 year was associated with renal survival. ESKD rates were higher than recent international reports. Further longitudinal studies identifying determinants of progression to ESKD and rates of renal relapse, coupled with implementation studies targeting early detection and healthcare access inclusive of kidney biopsy, are needed to improve outcomes.
