CircLRIG1 inhibits the malignant phenotypes of colorectal cancer cells by inactivating the NF-κB signaling through interaction with FUS
Authors: Qi Liu, Pei Chen, Lei Liu, Menghui Wang, Zhenjia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCircLRIG1 has been demonstrated to inhibit the progression of bladder cancer. However, the expression profile and function of circLRIG1 in colorectal cancer (CRC), the third most prevalent malignancy globally, remain to be explored.MethodsCircLRIG1 (circBase ID: hsa_circ_0003266) expression was first analyzed in the GSE142837 dataset and subsequently validated in CRC tissues and cell lines. HT-29 and LoVo cells were subjected to gain- and loss-of-function manipulations to delineate the impact of circLRIG1 on the proliferation, migration, and apoptosis of CRC cells. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) interacting with circLRIG1 were predicted by bioinformatic analysis, and verified by RNA pull-down and RNA immunoprecipitation assays, subcellular fractionation, and rescue experiments. Western blot and immunofluorescence were employed to assess the effects of circLRIG1 and FUS on total and phosphorylated p65 levels as well as p65 nuclear translocation. The NF-κB inhibitor PDTC was applied to circLRIG1-silenced CRC cells, and the malignant phenotypes were assessed. Finally, the tumor-suppressive capacity of circLRIG1 was evaluated in mouse subcutaneous xenograft models.ResultsCircLRIG1 was lowly expressed in CRC tissues and cell lines. Its expression was inversely correlated with advanced pathological features of CRC patients. Overexpression of circLRIG1 inhibited CRC cell proliferation, migration, and induced apoptosis. Conversely, silencing of circLRIG1 revealed the opposite effects. Mechanistically, circLRIG1 binds to FUS and inhibits its nuclear export, leading to the inactivation of NF-κB signaling. In vivo, circLRIG1 upregulation suppressed tumor growth, reduced the levels of Ki-67 and MMP9, and inhibited p65 phosphorylation.ConclusionsCircLRIG1 inhibits CRC progression through interaction with FUS to inactivate the NF-κB pathway. This may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05259-8.

Introduction

According to the latest GLOBOCAN estimates, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, responsible for more than 1.9 million new cases and 903,859 deaths annually [1]. Growing evidence indicates that numerous factors such as aging populations, tobacco use, alcohol intake, consumption of red or processed meat, obesity, and sedentary lifestyles contribute to an elevated risk of CRC [1, 2]. Advances in endoscopic diagnosis and minimally invasive therapy have markedly improved survival for patients with early-stage CRC [1, 3, 4]; nevertheless, more than 30% of those who undergo curative resection ultimately relapse [5]. And the 5-year survival rate plummets to ~ 14% once distant metastases appear [6]. Consequently, elucidating the molecular mechanisms that drive CRC carcinogenesis is urgently needed to generate insights for more effective therapeutic strategies.

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are a type of non-coding RNAs that arise from back-splicing to form covalently closed loops. The absence of a 5′ cap and 3′ poly (A) tail renders circRNAs impervious to exonuclease digestion, endowing them with greater stability than their linear counterparts [7]. An increasing body of evidence has demonstrated that circRNAs participate not only in normal physiology but also in pathological processes, including cancer [8–10]. They function by sequestering miRNAs, regulating transcription, and interacting with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) [11]. Multiple circRNAs are frequently dysregulated during CRC tumorigenesis, functioning as either oncogenic drivers or tumor-suppressors. For example, circRBM33 is increased in CRC tissues and cells and it boosts CRC development partly by regulating the miR-512-5p/SLC1A5 axis [12]. Elevated hsa_circ_0005571 expression in CRC promotes tumor cell proliferation and migration through the miR-520f-3p/LRP8 axis [13]. Hsa_circ_0001766 is downregulated in CRC tissues and its upregulation suppresses CRC progression via the miR-1203/PPP1R3C/mTOR/Myc axis [14]. However, the functions of most circRNAs in CRC remain to be explored.

LRIG1 has been demonstrated to function as a tumor suppressor in multiple cancers [15, 16]. Hsa_circ_0003266 (referred to as circLRIG1) is generated from the exons 2 and 3 of the LRIG1 gene via back-splicing. It is located on chr3:66501982–66,512,933, with a length of 147 nucleotides. Cheng et al. [17] have reported that circLRIG1 expression is reduced in bladder carcinoma tissues and cell lines, where it suppresses tumor progression through the miR-214-3p/LRIG1 axis. In this study, bioinformatic analysis of the GSE142837 dataset revealed that circLRIG1 is notably decreased in CRC tissues compared with corresponding controls. However, its role in CRC has not been previously investigated. Therefore, we further explored the function of circLRIG1 in CRC through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Additionally, the molecular mechanisms by which circLRIG1 regulates CRC progression were investigated.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analysis and human tissues samples

The GSE142837 dataset, which contains circRNA expression data from 5 CRC tissues and the corresponding non-cancerous tissues, was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Subsequently, we screened the differentially expressed circRNAs using the online analyzing tool GEO2R with the criteria (P < 0.05 and ∣logFC∣>1). RBPsuite 2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/RBPsuite/index.html#start), catRAPID omics v2.1 (http://service.tartaglialab.com/page/catrapid_omics2_group), Starbase v2.0 (https://rnasysu.com/encori/rbpClipRNA.php?source=circRNA), and CircAge (https://circage.kiz.ac.cn/; accessed on August 5, 2025) online databases were used to identify potential RBPs interacting with circLRIG1. The selection criteria were: (ⅰ) presence of binding sites in circLRIG1 (CircAge), (ⅱ) Interaction_Propensity > 2.0 (catRAPID omics v2.1), and (ⅲ) score > 0.5 (RBPsuite 2.0).

51 pairs of CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were acquired from CRC patients receiving radical resection between January 2025 and October 2025 at Songjiang Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. These patients were without preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy and the diagnosis of all specimens was ascertained by a pathologic examination. Fresh specimens were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at − 80 °C until analysis.

Cell culture, plasmid construction, and transfection

HT-29, LoVo, and SW480 CRC cell lines were procured from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. The normal colon epithelial cell line FHC was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and grown in DMEM: F12 medium with 10% FBS. All cells were maintained under a humidified atmosphere (37 °C, 5% CO₂).

CircLRIG1 overexpressing and silencing plasmids, as well as FUS overexpressing plasmid, were generated by HANBIO (Shanghai, China). FUS siRNA (5’-CCAGAGCAGCTATTCTTCTTA-3’) was acquired from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). A scramble sequence (5’-GATCCTGCTTGCAGTCGACGT-3’) was used as the control. These constructs were transfected into HT-29 or LoVo cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) based on the manufacturer’s instructions.

5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay for cell proliferation

Cell proliferation was measured by BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, HT-29 or LoVo cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and transfected with specific constructs for 48 h. Then, cells were incubated with 10 µM EdU for 2 h, followed by fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X100. A Click reaction solution was added and incubated for 30 min. Subsequently, the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo). EdU-positive cells display green fluorescence and the nuclei are stained in blue. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell migration

Both HT-29 and LoVo cell lines can be utilized for Transwell and scratch assays. In order to minimize experimental bias inherent to a single method, we employed both techniques. The migration of HT-29 cells was evaluated by 24-well Transwell chambers with 8-µm pore size (Corning, NY, USA). Transfected cells were suspended in serum-free medium (1 × 105 cells/mL). 500 µL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled up with 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS. After 24 h incubation at cell culture incubator, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Migrated cells on the lower surface were imaged under a microscope and counted in five randomly chosen fields per insert.

Scratch assay was used to examine the migration of LoVo cells. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well. After reaching approximately 90% confluent monolayer, a wound was created using a sterilized 200 µL pipette tip. Detached cells were removed through washing with PBS. And the medium was changed to FBS-free medium. Images were captured at 0 h and 24 h to measure the distance of the wound. Cell migrated rate (%) = (width of the wound at 0 h-width of the wound at 24 h)/width of the wound at 0 h ×100%.

Apoptosis assay

Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) was used to measure cell apoptosis. CRC cells were planted into 6-well plate and transfected with indicated constructs. At 48 h following transfection, the cells were collected and resuspended in 100 µL 1 × Binding Buffer. Subsequently, the suspension was incubated with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI in the dark for 10 min. After addition of 400 µL 1 × Binding Buffer and gentle mixing, apoptosis was quantified by flow cytometry (Beckman, FL, USA).

Real-time PCR and RNase R treatment

Total RNA was extracted from HT-29 and LoVo cells using the Trizol reagent. RNA concentrations and quality were examined by Thermo Scientific NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometers. To quantify circLRIG1 expression, high-quality RNA was reverse-transcribed and subjected to real-time PCR with the GENESEED® circRNA qRT-PCR Kit (Geneseed, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The relative expression levels of circLRIG1 are calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. GAPDH was used as an internal control.

To detect the stability of circLRIG1, 1 µg of total RNA was incubated with 4 U RNase R (Geneseed) at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 70 °C for 10 min. The treated RNA was then reverse-transcribed and analyzed by real-time PCR for both circLRIG1 and its linear counterpart, LRIG1, as described above. Nucleus/cytoplasm separation for RNA and protein analysis

The nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions of HT-29 and LoVo cells were prepared with the PARIS™ kit (Invitrogen™, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, RNA or protein was isolated from each fraction. RNA expression was quantified by real-time PCR, with GAPDH as the cytoplasmic reference and U6 for the nuclear fraction. Protein samples of nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were subjected to Western blot assay.

RNA pull-down and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays

Biotin-labeled circLRIG1 and negative control probes were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. RNA pull-down assay was performed using the RNA-Protein Pull-Down Kit with Streptavidin Agarose (P1811S, Beyotime). Cells were lysed on ice for 10 min in lysis buffer containing 1 mM PMSF, followed by centrifugation at 13,000×g for 10 min at 4℃. Subsequently, cell lysates were collected and incubated overnight with probe-conjugated Streptavidin Agarose at 4℃. After washing three times, RNA-protein complexes were eluted with 60 µL Biotin Elution Buffer. The bound proteins were analyzed by Western blot.

To perform RIP assay, BeyoRIP™ RIP Assay Kit with Protein A/G Agarose (P1801S, Beyotime) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed and incubated for 4 h at 4℃ with Protein A/G Agarose coated with anti-FUS antibody (#67840, Cell signaling technology, CST, Danvers, MA, USA) or anti-IgG antibody (#2729, CST). After elution and purification, immunoprecipitated RNAs were analyzed by real-time PCR.

Immunofluorescence assay

HT-29 and LoVo cells were seeded on glass slides and transfected with designated constructs for 48 h. Then, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100, followed by incubation with nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) p65 antibody (#8242, CST) overnight at 4 °C. Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (ab150077, Abcam) secondary antibody was used to label protein for 30 min at room temperature. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

In vivo experiments

4-6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were purchased from SLAC laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and raised in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. After adapting to the environment for a week, mice (n = 4 per group) were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 106 HT-29 cells stably overexpressing circLRIG1 (OE-circ) or control (OE-NC). Tumor sizes were measured with calipers every three days and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. 21 days after injection, all mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia (50 mg/kg) and tumors were excised and weighted. Then, one half of tumor was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent Ki-67 and MMP9 immunohistochemistry using standard procedures, and the other half was preserved at − 80 °C for future analysis.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from CRC cells and tumors using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Lysates were collected after centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were measured by a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gels, followed by transferred onto PVDF membrane. 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was utilized to block nonspecific binding sites. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against FUS (1:1000, #67840, CST), p65 (1:1000, #8242, CST), p-p65 (1:1000, #3033, CST), Histone H3 (1:1000, #9715, CST), and GAPDH (1:50000, A19056, ABclonal, China). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, the protein bands were visualized by an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit and imaged on Tanon 5200 chemiluminescence image analysis system (Tanon Science & Technology, Shanghai, China).

Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP)

Briefly, cell lysates were collected after lysis with RIPA buffer. Subsequently, the lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-FUS antibody (#67840, CST), using anti-IgG (#2729, CST) as a negative control. Protein A + G Agarose (Beyotime) was then added to the mixture and incubated for 3 h at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 min, the supernatant was carefully removed. The proteins were eluted and analyzed by Western blot.

Statistical analysis

The data in bar graphs are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test using GraphPad Prism 8.0. P < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

CircLRIG1 was lowly expressed in CRC tissues and cell lines

By analyzing the GSE142837 dataset with the screening criteria (P < 0.05 and ∣logFC∣>1), we found a significantly downregulated circRNA circLRIG1 (circBase ID: hsa_circ_0003266) (Fig. 1A, B). To further validate circLRIG1 expression across multiple centers [18, 19], we collected 51 pairs of CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues and examined its expression using real-time PCR. The results showed that circLRIG1 was lowly expressed in CRC tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, its expression was negatively correlated with CRC patients’ tumor size, TNM stage, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis (Table 2). CircLRIG1 expression was further examined in CRC cell lines. The results showed that compared with normal human colonic epithelial cell line FHC, circLRIG1 expression was decreased in CRC cell lines, including HT-29, SW480, and LoVo (Fig. 1D). The back-splicing junction site of circLRIG1 was examined by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1E). Moreover, we found that circLRIG1 exhibited strong stability, as evidenced by its significant resistance to RNase R digestion compared with its linear counterpart LRIG1 mRNA (Fig. 1F). Nucleus/cytoplasm separation revealed that circLRIG1 was predominantly located in the cytoplasm of CRC cells (Fig. 1G).

CircLRIG1 inhibited the growth and migration of CRC cells

To explore the function of circLRIG1 in CRC, both loss- and gain-of-function strategies were employed. Effective downregulation and overexpression of circLRIG1 was demonstrated by real-time PCR (Fig. 2A). EdU assay was conducted to assess CRC cell proliferation. The results revealed that downregulation of circLRIG1 markedly promoted the proliferation in HT-29 cells, whereas its overexpression inhibited the proliferation of LoVo cells (Fig. 2B). The migration capability was increased after circLRIG1 silencing, but decreased by circLRIG1 upregulation (Fig. 2C and D). Additionally, elevated circLRIG1 markedly promoted apoptosis in LoVo cells (Fig. 2E). Taken together, these data suggest that circLRIG1 suppressed the growth and migration of CRC cells.

CircLRIG1 regulated the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells through interaction with FUS

Given that circLRIG1 was mainly localized to the cytoplasm of CRC cells (Fig. 1F), we speculated that circLRIG1 might function via an RBP-dependent manner. Four online databases (RBPsuite, catRAPID, Starbase, and CircAge) were employed to identify the potential RBPs that interacted with circLRIG1. Our analysis identified FUS as the potential RBP across all databases (Fig. 3A). The known motif of protein FUS and its binding score with circLRIG1 were shown in Fig. 3B and C. Using biotinylated probes, we captured the circLRIG1-FUS interaction (Fig. 3D), and RIP assay using the anti-FUS antibody further validated their direct association (Fig. 3E). It has been demonstrated that RBPs participate in the regulation of circRNA formation [20]. Therefore, we explored whether FUS modulates circLRIG1 expression in CRC cells and found that FUS depletion had no discernible effect on circLRIG1 levels (Fig. 3F and G). Additionally, we examined whether circLRIG1 can regulate FUS expression. The results showed that although modulation of circLRIG1 did not affect FUS proteins in whole-cell lysates, circLRIG1 downregulation decreased FUS protein levels in the nucleus but elevated its levels in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3H). Conversely, circLRIG1 overexpression produced opposing changes in the nuclear and cytoplasmic levels of FUS (Fig. 3H). This indicates that circLRIG1 suppresses the nuclear export of FUS.

CircLRIG1/FUS interaction suppressed the growth and migration of CRC cells through inactivation of the NF-κB signaling

NF-κB signaling pathway is a key promotor of CRC progression [24]. Studies have demonstrated that many circRNAs can regulate NF-κB signaling [25–27]. The interaction between FUS and circ_0002456 has been shown to inhibit NF-κB pathway [28]. We speculated that circLRIG1/FUS interaction might mediate NF-κB signaling. Uranishi et al. [29] have demonstrated that FUS interacts with NF-κB p65 through the C-terminal transactivation domain and activates NF-κB-mediated transcription. We also found that FUS interacted with p65 in CRC cells, and that FUS overexpression promoted the transcription of NF-κB target genes, IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 6A, B). Our experimental findings showed that circLRIG1 downregulation increased, whereas its overexpression decreased p-p65 protein levels (Fig. 6C). Immunofluorescence assay confirmed that circLRIG1 silencing markedly promoted, while its upregulation notably suppressed, p65 translocation to the nucleus (Fig. 6D). Moreover, the mRNA levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were increased after knockdown of circLRIG1, but decreased after circLRIG1 overexpression (Fig. 6E and F). Notably, the p-p65 levels and p65 nuclear translocation as well as IL-6 and TNF-α mRNA levels affected by circLRIG1 overexpression were both reversed upon FUS downregulation (Fig. 6C and F). Then, we evaluated whether circLRIG1 functions through inhibiting the activation of NF-κB signaling using the NF-κB inhibitor PDTC. As shown in Fig. 6G and I, PDTC not only notably suppressed proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis in sh-NC-transfected CRC cells, but also significantly reversed the malignant phenotypes caused by circLRIG1 silencing, restoring them to levels close to those of the sh-NC control. These data indicate that circLRIG1 functions by inactivating NF-κB signaling via competitive binding to FUS.

The suppressive role of circLRIG1 was validated in xenograft mice by injecting HT-29 cells that stably overexpressed circLRIG1 (OE-circ) or OE-NC. Compared with the OE-NC group, tumor volume and weight were both reduced in the OE-circ group (Fig. 7A, C). Moreover, the protein levels of Ki-67 and MMP9 were lower in OE-circ group than in OE-NC group (Fig. 7D). Additionally, the phosphorylation of p65 was decreased by circLRIG1 (Fig. 7E). These results demonstrate that overexpression of circLRIG1 inhibits CRC tumorigenesis and NF-κB activation in vivo.

Discussion

With the development of high-throughput sequencing and microarray technologies, an increasing number of circRNAs have been identified and shown to play pivotal roles in cancer biology [30–32]. In this study, we found that circLRIG1 expression was reduced in CRC and it inhibited the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells by inactivating NF-κB signaling through interaction with FUS.

CircLRIG1 has been reported to be lowly expressed and inhibit the progression of bladder cancer [17]. In this study, we also found that circLRIG1 expression was reduced in human CRC tissues and was inversely correlated with advanced pathological features, suggesting its involvement in CRC progression. Our further study confirmed the decreased expression of circLRIG1 in CRC cell lines. It was mainly located in the cytoplasm of CRC cells. Functional experiments proved that circLRIG1 upregulation inhibited the proliferation and migration, while induced apoptosis of CRC cells in vitro. Furthermore, in xenograft mice, overexpression of circLRIG1 reduced tumor growth and the expression of MMP9, a key promoter of tumor cell metastasis [33, 34]. These results suggest that circLRIG1 inhibits CRC progression.

An increasing body of research has focused on the vital role of RBP in circRNA function [35–37]. Considering the predominant cytoplasmic localization, we hypothesized that circLRIG1 exerts its function through an RBP-dependent mechanism. Through bioinformatic analysis, RNA pull-down, and RIP assays, we confirmed that FUS is a binding protein of circLRIG1. FUS is a nucleocytoplasmic shuttling protein that primarily localizes in the nucleus to play crucial roles including RNA regulation and maturation, and DNA damage repair [38]. We further evaluated whether circLRIG1 can influence the expression and subcellular localization of FUS. Our study confirmed that circLRIG1 did not affect FUS expression but inhibit the nuclear export of FUS. Additionally, we found that knockdown of FUS significantly abrogated the effects of circLRIG1 overexpression on the proliferation, migration, and apoptosis of CRC cells. These data demonstrate that circLRIG1 inhibits the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells through binding to and suppressing nuclear export of FUS.

NF-κB, a ubiquitously expressed transcription factor, governs a broad spectrum of cellular processes. Initially characterized as a pivotal regulator of inflammation, it is now increasingly recognized as a central driver of tumor initiation and progression [24, 39, 40]. Constitutive activation of NF-κB is seen in the majority of cancers [41, 42]. Studies have demonstrated that many circRNAs can regulate the NF-κB signaling pathway [25–27]. The interaction between FUS and circ_0002456 has been revealed to inhibit NF-κB pathway [28]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the interaction between circLRIG1 and FUS might regulate the NF-κB pathway. In resting cells, inactive NF-κB is retained in the cytoplasm by forming a complex with NF-κB-inhibitor (IκB). Upon activation, IκB is phosphorylated and degraded in a proteasome-dependent manner, allowing NF-κB to translocate rapidly into the nucleus. Within the nucleus, NF-κB-often in its phosphorylated form-binds to promoter sequences of target genes to regulate their expression [43, 44]. As expected, circLRIG1 downregulation augmented both p65 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, whereas its upregulation suppressed these events in CRC cells. CircLRIG1 overexpression also decreased p65 phosphorylation in the tumor tissues of xenograft mice. Furthermore, FUS downregulation partially reversed the influences of circLRIG1 overexpression on p65 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation in vitro. These data indicate that circLRIG1 inhibits the activation of NF-κB pathway through interaction with FUS. We also found that both circLRIG1 and p65 interacted with FUS in CRC cells. As FUS activated NF-κB-mediated transcription by interacting with p65 [29], and given that FUS overexpression mimicked circLRIG1 knockdown on the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells and circLRIG1 inhibited NF-κB signaling through FUS, these data suggest competitive binding between circLRIG1 and p65 for FUS. To further analyze whether circLRIG1 functions via regulating NF-κB pathway, a NF-κB inhibitor PDTC [45] was employed. Our results showed that PDTC alleviated, at least partially, the effects of circLRIG1 downregulation on the proliferation, migration, and apoptosis of CRC cells. These results demonstrate that circLRIG1 inhibits the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells at least partially through inactivation of the NF-κB pathway. However, this study has some limitations. First, we were unable to evaluate the relationship between circLRIG1 expression and patient survival due to insufficient follow-up time. Second, we only conducted animal experiments with a limited sample size (n = 4 per group) and did not perform CRC orthotopic xenograft models to directly assess tumor metastatic capacity. Additionally, besides the NF-κB pathway, other CRC-related signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin [46], PI3K/AKT [47]) may interact with the circLRIG1-FUS complex, as PDTC only partially restored the malignant phenotypes elicited by circLRIG1 silencing. Therefore, our future study will focus on analyzing the correlation between circLRIG1 expression and patient survival, investigating CRC metastasis using orthotopic models in vivo, and further elucidating whether circLRIG1 interacts with additional pathways.

Anyway, the present study unmasks that circLRIG1 inhibits the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells through interaction with FUS to inactivate the NF-κB pathway. This study provides a potential novel therapeutic strategy for CRC.
