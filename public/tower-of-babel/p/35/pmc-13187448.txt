Numerical analysis and engineering application of bolt support technology for controlling coal body sliding
Authors: Chengwu Wang, Shuangwen Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With increasing coal mining depth, coal bumps—disasters that severely compromise mine safety—have garnered growing attention in the mining industry. Among these, coal bump sliding-type coal bumps cause significant structural damage and pose serious safety hazards. Analysis of damage patterns indicates that such sliding typically originates from insufficient strength at the coal-rock interface. To address this issue, bolt support technology has been proposed to enhance the reinforcing capacity of roadway support systems at this critical interface. The arrangement angle of the bolts significantly affects the reinforcement of the interface. This study uses ABAQUS software to simulate and analyze the stress-strain and bearing characteristics of bolts at support angles of 30°, 45°, 60°, and 90°. The simulation results show that at support angles of 30° and 45°, the bolt fails primarily due to tensile forces, while at 60° and 90°, failure is mainly caused by shear forces. The higher load-carrying capacity of the bolt under tensile damage, compared to shear damage, indicates stronger reinforcement of the interfaces. The research findings were implemented in the return airway of a working face at a coal mine. Field observations during roadway excavation in the test section revealed relatively minimal roof separation. Compared to the conventional support system, the cumulative sidewall displacement was reduced by 28.6% during excavation and by 51.9% after mining commenced, while roof separation remained consistently low. These results confirm that the proposed bolt support technology significantly improves control of the surrounding rock mass in roadways.

Introduction

China initiated studying and applying bolt support technology since 1956, and gradually recognizing its significant advantages in the control of roadway surrounding rock, such as active support, cost effectiveness, improved construction efficiency and safety, strong adaptability, and superior support performance.

However, in mines with complex geological conditions—such as great depth, high in-situ stress, hard roof strata, and dynamic disturbances—the coal body along roadway sidewalls is prone to sliding at the coal-rock interface. Improper application of bolt support technology may lead to bolt failure, which directly compromises support functionality and undermines the stability of the surrounding rock. This can trigger large-scale coal body sliding during impact events, posing a severe threat to mine safety.

In order to better study bolt support, many scholars employ numerical simulation software to propose management strategies for the coal bump from different perspectives. He1-3 verified that NPR cables offer better support under strong shock and vibration through in-situ blasting tests and numerical simulations. Batugin4 proposed a staggered split-level panel layouts combined support technology, utilizing simulations to study support parameters under dynamic loads and analyze the support effect in gob-side entries. Nguyen et al.5 analyzed the Superbolt performance under dynamic loading through tests and simulations, verifying that plastic extension and energy absorption occur. Roth6 studied the synergistic action of the support system under dynamic loading and found that the bolts initially take the load and transfer to the metal mesh, which together absorb energy. Ansell7 developed the energy absorbing bolt, equipped with ribbed anchoring section and nut, which can plastically extend and absorb energy under dynamic loading. Marulanda8 simulated the bolt behavior under dynamic loading and found that the debonding of the anchored section leads to bolt failure, with the bolt parameters affecting energy absorption. St-Pierre9 established a dynamic model of the conical bolt and found that the bolt absorbs energy through sliding in resin and the plastic deformation of the rod. Brandle et al.10 verified the applicability of support system that combines high-strength steel mesh and bolts under dynamic conditions, analyzing its load-bearing behavior. Yokota11 developed energy-absorbing bolts and confirmed high bearing capacity and deformation ability by simulations and experiments. Tahmasebinia12 simulated the bearing capacity of bolts with varying diameters and strengths under shear, finding that both diameter and yield strength positively correlate with force and displacement. Garza-Cruz13 showed that support systems fail due to shear under high stresses and proposed using bolts with high compatibility and shear capacity, emphasizing the importance of support spacing and secondary support. Neshchadymenko14 proposed a support system combining shelf sheds and bolts, optimized by simulations to enhance the roadway stability and safety. Kirmaci15 analyzed the failure mechanism of the roadway sidewall using simulations and proposed that combined support at the sidewall, and increased support density. Siddiqui16 confirmed that resin bolt support provides better stability than concrete bolts by 3D roof modeling to simulate the load response of beams with different thicknesses.

In addition, the sliding of the coal body is primarily caused by insufficient interface strength. Some scholars use experiments and numerical simulations to study the interface reinforcement issue. Ferrero17 used experiments and simulations to analyze the effects of parameters such as rock type and bolt type on the rock joint strength, aiming to optimize bolt design. Pellet et al.18 proposed a model to predict the strengthening effect of bolts on rock joints under shear and analyzed the impact of support parameters on reinforcement. Grasselli19 analyzed the deformation behavior of various bolt by tests and simulations under shear, and found that the inclination and diameter of the bolts affected interfacial reinforcement. Li20 found that the combination of bolts, shotcrete, and TSL is the most effective in preventing interface sliding in double shear tests. Saadat et al.21 showed that increasing the joint roughness enhances its shear strength, which is further improved by bolt support. Marenče et al.22 developed numerical models to predict the damage and shear response of bolts at the joint and analyzed the effects of bolt support parameters on shear resistance. Srivastava et al.23 found that bolts reinforce the shear strength of joint through experiment, which is closely related to support area, interval, block size, and material strength. Spang et al.24 studied the effect of bolts on the shear resistance of rock joints, considering factors such as interface friction, bolt inclination angle, and rock deformation capacity. Indraratna25 studied the shear strength of rock discontinuity joints and found that the thickness and saturation of the filling material significantly affect the strength. Srivastava et al.26 established correlation model to study the effect of bolts on rock joint strength and found that bolts improved the failure mode and strength of the rock joint. Boon et al.27 studied the design parameters of support system comprising bolts, liners, or their combination to strengthen rock joint using numerical simulations. Deb et al.28 developed the DEFE program to simulate bolt behavior at rock joints, finding that appropriately designed bolt system enhances shear resistance. Li29 found that bolts can change the slip mode of hard rock structural planes from stick-slip to stabilized slip under high stresses, thereby delaying dynamic damage induced by shear. He30, Srivastava31 and Deb32 showed that bolts increase rock strength by limiting the sliding of rock joints and enhancing the internal locking within the rock mass. Meng33 analyzed the shear-slip damage mechanism of the surrounding rock and proposed comprehensive support measures, including grouting, bolts, and high-strength concrete. Xing et al.34 revealed the influence of rock residual strength and fault characteristics on roadway stability by simulations, noting that delayed support can enhance stability. Boon35 investigated the support mechanism of bolts in horizontally stratified rock by simulations, finding that bolt force decreases with distance in thick rock strata, while thin rock strata exhibit beam or suspension characteristics.

Although extensive research has been conducted in-depth research on bolt support for coal bump prevention and interface reinforcement, most studies focus on reinforcing rock joint surfaces, whereas investigations coal-rock interface reinforcement remain comparatively limited.

This study focuses on the coal-rock interface to investigate how varying bolt support angles influence stress-strain behavior and load-bearing capacity under lateral coal body displacement. The primary objective is to determine the optimal support angle for effectively restricting coal body movement. Field validation of these findings provides both theoretical insights and practical engineering solutions for coal bump mitigation, while confirming the efficacy of optimized bolt support systems.

Bolt support mechanism for controlling coal body sliding

Large-scale sliding-type coal bumps are characterized by wholesale displacement of the coal body. During such events in mining roadways, the entire coal body undergoes sliding failure, generating extensive damage zones along the roadway. Notably, the support system of the roadway is less damaged, and the roof integrity is better, leaving sliding traces on the roof surface. These phenomena stem from multiple reasons. In deeper strata, elastic energy gradually accumulates due to the interaction of various factors such as geological formations and mining activities. In specific conditions, the sudden release of this accumulated energy disrupts the horizontal stress equilibrium at the coal-rock interface adjacent to roadway sidewalls. This energy release triggers horizontal sliding of the coal body along the interface36, which leads to the coal bump.

The coal body sliding stems from inadequate strength at the coal-rock interface to resist horizontal stresses. Consequently, bolt support systems must be engineered with both high load-bearing capacity and effective interface reinforcement capabilities to ensure the stability of the surrounding rock.

The supporting mechanism of bolt reinforced interface includes two aspects. On the one hand, the shear strength and anchorage extrusion of the bolt increase the frictional resistance of the interface and improve the strength of the coal-rock interface, as shown in Fig. 1. On the other hand, when the coal body displaces, bolts deform to develop restraining forces that resist further movement and transfer load to the surrounding rock, consequently enhancing the structural integrity of the interface, as shown in Fig. 2.

Design and construction of reinforced support model for interface

Design of the numerical simulation model

This study investigates the stress-strain behavior, failure modes, and energy absorption capacity of bolts under varying installation angles (30°, 45°, 60°, and 90°) during interfacial slip between coal seams and rock strata. Numerical simulations were performed using ABAQUS to evaluate bolt mechanical responses at these specific angles. Comparative analysis of the simulation results establishes a theoretical foundation for optimizing roadway support system.

Establishment of numerical simulation model

Model gridding

The model is built on coal-rock dislocation, with attributes assigned based on typical mine rock properties. During meshing, the grid is refined around the bolt area to ensure higher accuracy in calculating bolt forces, displacement changes, damage states, and energy absorption capacity. The finite element model configuration is presented in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4Interface enhancement model.

Interface enhancement model.

Attribute assignment for the model

In the parameter settings, the X-axis represents the horizontal stress direction, the Y-axis indicates the mining roadway direction, and the Z-axis is the vertical direction. The model size is 200 mm × 200 mm × 210 mm. The model is constrained in the Y-axis direction and has fixed displacements on the bottom and top surfaces along the Z-axis, while movement is allowed freely along the X-axis. The coal-rock interface strength is temporarily disregarded in the analysis of bolt bearing capacity at various support angles. The coal body is applied to the displacement in the X-axis direction at the rate of 50 mm/s with a time step of 1, causing the bolt to gradually break. Load is not applied in the Z-axis direction.

Eight node hexahedron linear reduced integration solid element is adopted for bolt, coal body and rock mass. The final number of numerical model grids is 99,451, and the number of bolt, rock mass, and coal body grids is 6000, 46,799 and 46,652 respectively. General contact is adopted by the two ends of the bolt. The normal contact relationship is set as hard contact, and the tangential contact relationship is based on coulomb friction model with the penalty function. In numerical simulations, the anchoring effect is simplified by increasing the friction coefficient.

Modeling process

A 10 mm gap is reserved between the coal and rock bodies to analyze the effect of bolt support under frictionless interface condition. A multi-angle bolt model is constructed and assigned parameters. Displacement boundary conditions are applied to the coal body, and geometric nonlinear analysis is used for conducting simulation calculations. The stresses, strains, plastic deformations and fracture of the bolt are monitored when the model calculations satisfy the preset conditions. The load-bearing capacity and energy absorption characteristics of bolts are systematically evaluated across different installation angles to determine optimal support configurations.

Simulation results of reinforced support for interface

Stress analysis of bolt

Based on the simulation result, the bolt is analyzed for the stress variation at different support angles.

Stress analysis of bolt at a 30° support angle

Fig. 5Maximum principal stress distribution of bolt at 30°.

Maximum principal stress distribution of bolt at 30°.

The breaking process and maximum principal stress distribution of the bolt are shown in Fig. 5 at a 30° support angle. Failure initiates at the coal-rock interface, with the damage zone exhibiting a distinct ‘S’-shaped deformation pattern. Within the fracture region, the bolt demonstrates significant axial elongation accompanied by necking deformation—characteristic features confirming tensile failure as the dominant mechanism.

The maximum principal stress in the bolt reaches 553 MPa during the elastic deformation phase and peaks at 650 MPa prior to fracture. Within the necking zone, progressive reduction of the cross-sectional area induces stress concentration. Stress concentration not only reduces the bolt bearing capacity but also accelerates the breaking process. The spatial distribution of stress fields reveals both the global mechanical response of the bolt throughout the loading sequence and the stress transmission mechanisms across different structural domains.

Stress analysis of bolt at a 45° support angle

At a support angle of 45°, as shown in Fig. 6, the bolt failure pattern is closely resembles that observed at 30°. However, both the necking deformation and the spatial extent of the fracture zone exhibit more pronounced characteristics.

Fig. 6Maximum principal stress distribution of bolt at 60°.

Maximum principal stress distribution of bolt at 60°.

The maximum principal stress in the bolt reaches 570.8 MPa during the elastic deformation phase and peaks at 680.4 MPa prior to fracture. Compared to the 30°, this elevated stress demonstrates enhanced load-bearing capacity and improved resistance to failure under interfacial sliding conditions.

Stress analysis of bolt at a 60° support angle

At a support angle of 60°, as shown in Fig. 7, the bolt exhibits pronounced bending deformation within the fracture zone, with a predominantly flat fracture surface characteristic of shear failure mechanisms.

Fig. 7Maximum principal stress distribution of bolt at 45°.

Maximum principal stress distribution of bolt at 45°.

The maximum principal stress of the bolt reaches 531.2 MPa in the elastic phase and peaks at 635.7 MPa prior to fracture. This stress value is notably lower than those recorded at 30°and 45°, indicating reduced load-bearing capacity. Under shear condition, the bolt is more likely to be damaged by shear at a 60° support angle.

Stress analysis of bolt at a 90° support angle

The breaking process and the distribution of the maximum principal stress in the bolt are shown in Fig. 8 at a support angle of 90°. The failure morphology closely resembles that observed at 60°, exhibiting a smooth fracture surface with an inclined cross-section—characteristic features confirming shear-dominated failure mechanisms. During the elastic deformation phase under shear loading, the maximum principal stress reaches 513.2 MPa, peaking at 575.5 MPa prior to fracture. The overall values are relatively low, indicating that the bolt’s ultimate bearing capacity is more limited under shear stress.

Fig. 8Maximum principal stress distribution of bolt at 90°.

Maximum principal stress distribution of bolt at 90°.

Bolts exhibit significantly higher ultimate load-bearing capacity under tensile loading conditions compared to shear stress states. Correspondingly, tensile-dominated failure mechanisms prevail at angles of 30° and 45°, while shear-dominated failure modes dominate at 60° and 90°.

The relationship between maximum principal stress and displacement is shown in Fig. 9. At 30° and 45° support angles, bolts demonstrate optimal load-bearing capacity in tensile-dominated conditions, attributable to stress distribution patterns and minimized stress concentration effects. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing roadway support systems, where angular configuration enhances structural integrity and mitigates coal-rock interface failure risks.

Analyzing plastic strain and force

Force and strain are analyzed in the breaking region of the bolt, and bearing capacities of the bolt are compared.

Strain analysis of bolt at a 30° support angle

During the loading process, bolts progressive strain accumulation until reaching a failure threshold, at which point fracture occurs. As shown in Fig. 10, the strain is mainly concentrated in the damage area, and the accumulative value reaches 0.7056 before breaking. With the continuous increase of the strain, the accumulated value reaches 1.24 after the bolt breaks.

Fig. 10Plastic deformation distribution of bolt at 30°.

Plastic deformation distribution of bolt at 30°.

Strain analysis of bolt at a 45° support angle

As Fig. 11 shows, the bolt strain is similarly concentrated in the damage area.

Fig. 11Plastic deformation distribution of bolt at 45°.

Plastic deformation distribution of bolt at 45°.

Specifically, the cumulative strain reaches 0.75 before breaking and increases to 1.814 afterward. Compared to the 30°, bolts installed at 45° demonstrate superior strain tolerance before fracture onset, confirming enhanced load-bearing capacity at this optimal orientation.

Strain analysis of bolt at a 60° support angle

As depicted in Fig. 12, the maximum strain reaches 1.013 prior to fracture at 60°, with post-fracture strain value of 1.05. Compared to at 30° and 45°, fracture occurs at significantly lower strain under this angle. At 60°, the bolt experiences strong shear, with more pronounced stress concentration, reducing its ability to withstand large strain and making it more prone to damage.

Fig. 12Plastic deformation distribution of bolt at 60°.

Plastic deformation distribution of bolt at 60°.

Strain analysis of bolt at a 90° support angle

Figure 13 demonstrates that at a 90° angle, the maximum strain attains 0.99 prior to fracture, with post-fracture strain value of 1.05. The strain values at 90° and 60° support angles are comparable, indicating similar strain bearing capacities under shear. Under this shear state, bolts exhibit significantly reduced ductility and fracture at lower strain compared to tensile-dominated angles (30° and 45°), indicating diminished deformation capacity.

Fig. 13Plastic deformation distribution of bolt at 90°.

Plastic deformation distribution of bolt at 90°.

Force analysis of bolt

Figure 14 presents the comparative failure load and bolt axial force evolution curves across different installation angles. Initially, the load-displacement curves for 30° and 45° exhibit a more gradual ascending phase compared to the steeper initial response observed at 60° and 90°. However, the 30° and 45° orientations achieve significantly higher peak loads, indicative of superior load-bearing capacity. The axial force, as a component of the load along the rod, follows a trend similar to the load curve, but its value is typically lower than the total load. Under 30° and 45°, bolts primarily experience tensile-dominated loading conditions, which facilitate more efficient stress transfer and result in higher peak load. This tensile-dominant behavior directly correlates with enhanced structural performance and greater ultimate load-bearing capacity in practical roadway support applications.

Bolt failure load and axial force curve.

Analysis of bolt energy absorption

The energy-absorbing capacity of bolt is an important indicator of their impact resistance. In the numerical simulation, displacement-controlled boundary conditions were applied to the coal body at a constant loading rate. Under same conditions, the optimal support angle is determined through comparative analysis.

As shown in Fig. 15, total strain energy and bearing capacity are higher at 30° and 45° support angles. The bolt strain energy gradually accumulates after the coal body begins to displace. The curve initially rises sharply, and then the growth rate slows until the bolt fails. This avoids stress concentrations and ensures that the bolt maintains good energy absorption over a long period of time.

Fig. 15Total strain energy of each angle.

Total strain energy of each angle.

In practice, the support system requires better energy absorption. Under the same conditions, the roadway adopts bolt arrangement of excellent bearing capacity, which enhances its impact resistance and energy absorption capacity, thereby ensuring roadway safety and stability.

Application of bolt support technology in engineering

Background of engineering

The working face, located in the No.7 coal seam, is the third working face in the east. This working face is bordered by an adjacent working face to the west, separated by a 6 m wide protective coal pillar. The working face dimensions are characterized by a depth of 718–848 m, strike length of 1104 m, face dip direction of 220 m, average seam thickness of 3.10 m, and mean dip angle of 23°. The roadway excavation layout for the working face are illustrated in Fig. 16.

Fig. 16Working face roadway excavation layout.

Working face roadway excavation layout.

The immediate roof of the coal seam consists of mudstone and sandy mudstone. The mudstone thickness ranges from 0.77 to 3.84 m, averaging 2.07 m, while the sandy mudstone ranges from 0.48 to 1.58 m, with an average thickness of 0.96 m. The main roof is hard fine sandstone, ranging from 2.77 to 9.70 m in thickness, with an average of 6.38 m. The floor is mudstone, varying between 0.93 and 6.12 m, with an average thickness of 4.32 m.

The working face is characterized by a large depth, a thick coal seam, and high stress. Ground pressure behavior is obvious in the mining roadway. The main roof consists of a hard rock layer, and the coal seam, roof, and floor exhibit impact tendencies. The existing support system fails to control surrounding rock deformation and is prone to coal bumps under dynamic load disturbances.

Bolt support technology

To prevent coal body sliding from the roadway sidewall, the support configuration for the return airway has been systematically optimized to enhance coal-rock interface stability, effectively constrain interfacial displacement, and improve the energy absorption capacity of the support system. A representative 100 m segment of the roadway was designated as the field test zone to validate support performance under actual mining conditions. The test section was divided into two segments for comparative analysis: the first segment maintained the original support scheme, whereas the second segment employed an optimized support arrangement. Both segments exhibited comparable geological conditions, characterized by the absence of significant geological structures and negligible variations in coal seam depth. Comparative illustrations of conventional support design and the optimized support are presented in Fig. 17, highlighting key modifications to bolt installation patterns and supplemental reinforcement measures.

Fig. 17The existing support and the optimized support.

The existing support and the optimized support.

In the field test section, the focus is to strengthen the support and control of the coal body in the roadway sidewall, while the roof support method remains unchanged. Specifically, 5.5-meter cables were horizontally installed at mid-height of the coal pillar-side. The upper inclination angle of bolts was systematically increased from 15° to 45° with the lower inclination angle maintained at 15°, creating an optimized bolting pattern. Both bolts and cables feature anchorage zones fully embedded within stable roof and floor strata to ensure effective load transfer. Large pallets are used to expand the anchorage control area. The coal body exposure is small on the working face side of the roadway, and the support method remains unaltered. The comprehensive support layout for the test roadway is presented in Fig. 18, illustrating the spatial arrangement of reinforcement elements and interface stabilization measures.

Fig. 18Bolt support of roadway sidewall.

Bolt support of roadway sidewall.

Analysis of support effectiveness

Monitoring devices are installed in both the test and existing support sections to compare the displacements of the sidewalls and roof, as well as surface displacement and roof abscission layer data.

Roadway monitoring results during excavation

Continuous monitoring of surrounding rock deformation was conducted throughout the complete roadway excavation cycle. These datasets are subsequently analyzed to characterize the evolution patterns of roadway deformation. The monitoring results are presented as deformation-time curves in Fig. 19.

Fig. 19Monitoring results of roadway displacement during excavation.

Monitoring results of roadway displacement during excavation.

Field monitoring data indicates that while the temporal evolution patterns of roof-to-floor convergence are similar between the optimized support test section and the conventional support section, the magnitude of displacement exhibits a significant reduction with the improved design. Specifically, the maximum cumulative roof convergence displacement in the test section stabilizes at 20 mm, representing a 28.6% reduction compared to the 28 mm recorded in the conventional support section.

Roof separation monitoring was conducted in parallel across the optimized support test section and conventional support section, with deformation profiles presented in Fig. 20. The monitoring data reveal an inverse correlation between separation monitoring displacement and anchorage depth, where increased anchor depth corresponds to reduced separation monitoring. The test section exhibits less displacement than the existing support section.

Fig. 20Roof separation monitoring results during excavation.

Roof separation monitoring results during excavation.

The deep displacement monitoring result for the coal body in the roadway sidewall is presented in Fig. 21. Significant differences are observed between the two sections, with lower displacement values recorded in the test section.

Fig. 21Monitoring results of deep displacement of sidewall during excavation.

Monitoring results of deep displacement of sidewall during excavation.

Roadway monitoring results during mining

Throughout the mining period, displacement and roof separation development are continuously monitored in both roadway sections. The monitoring data are presented in Fig. 22. Although cumulative roof deformation does not differ significantly between the two sections, roof displacement is lower in the test roadway section. Compared with the existing support section, cumulative displacement in the sidewall of the test roadway is reduced by 55 mm, representing a 51.9% reduction.

Fig. 22Monitoring results of roadway displacement during mining.

Monitoring results of roadway displacement during mining.

Monitoring results for roof separation during the mining period are presented in Fig. 23. Measurement points are classified as deep or shallow based on anchorage depths. While displacements at shallow points remained low in both roadway sections, measurements from deep points revealed larger separation magnitudes and accelerated growth rates in the existing support section. Comparative analysis indicates that the test roadway section maintained minimal roof separation values throughout the monitoring period, confirming its superior support performance.

Fig. 23Roof separation monitoring results during mining.

Roof separation monitoring results during mining.

Numerical simulations and engineering applications demonstrate that bolt support technology effectively controls roadway deformation. This technology not only provides broader support coverage but also significantly mitigates coal mass displacement, thereby ensuring roadway structural integrity and personnel safety.

Conclusion

Based on coal bump prevention support technology principles, numerical simulation determines the optimal support angle for bolt bearing capacity. The bolt is mainly tensile-damaged at support angles of 30° and 45°, exhibiting high bearing capacity. In contrast, the bearing capacity is relatively low at 60° and 90°, with shear failure mechanisms dominating the bolt failure patterns. At the coal pillar sidewall’s upper corner, the bolt support angle is optimized to 45°, with the anchorage zone embedded in both the roof and floor strata. Supplemental cable reinforcement is installed at the mid-height of the roadway sidewall, anchored to deeper floor strata. These modifications effectively reinforce the coal-rock interface structural integrity, enhance mine operational safety, and mitigate potential coal body displacement tendencies through improved load transfer mechanisms.Analyzing the bolt support effect, the roof abscission layer displacement is small during the test roadway excavation. Moreover, the cumulative sidewall displacement is reduced by 28.6% compared to the existing support. During the mining period, the cumulative sidewall deformation is reduced by 51.9%, and the values of the roof abscission layer are relatively low. These facts demonstrate that impact support technology is highly effective in roadway surrounding rock control applications. The control measures developed in this study are specifically tailored to large-scale sliding-type coal bumps. Their applicability and effectiveness for other coal bump types warrant further empirical validation through future investigations.
