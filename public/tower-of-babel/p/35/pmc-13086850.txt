Bioinspired nanofluidic iontronic device with integrated photoreceptor and photosynaptic functions
Authors: Wenchao Liu, Lian Duan, Xiangyu Zhang, Xinyi Zhu, Yongxin Ge, Guoheng Xu, Zhixiao Si, Jiqing Dai, Wenbo Chang, Miliang Zhang, Ronghua Lan, Zhijun Hu, Ruotian Chen, Kowit Hengphasatporn, Yasuteru Shigeta, Kai Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biological vision acquires external information through light-induced transmembrane ion transport, generating electrical impulses. Emulating the dual visual functions of photoreceptors and photosynapses through light-modulated ion transport presents a significant challenge. Herein, we present a bioinspired light-regulated nanofluidic iontronic device that can mimic biological visual functionalities, realized through an engineered carbon nanotube and molybdenum disulfide (CNT/MoS2) heterostructure. This bioinspired device combines two key functionalities of photoreceptor-like optical sensing and photosynaptic signal processing with dynamically adjustable polarity-switching behavior, achieved via bias voltage–modulated transient photoresponse speeds. Both theoretical and experimental results prove that light-modulated ion transport originates from the heterointerface-induced asymmetric photovoltage generation across CNT/MoS2 nanotube. Furthermore, we demonstrate its implementation for both accurate orientation recognition and reliable fingerprint detection under varying incident light angles. The device’s bidirectional photoresponsiveness highlights its unique advantages for adaptive visual simulation systems.

Introduction

In biological systems, ~80% of external information is processed primarily through the visual system1. The retina, a sophisticated neural tissue lining the posterior eye, serves as the gateway for visual perception, owing to its anatomically integrated and intricately organized structure2 (Fig. 1a). Among these, the photoreceptor cells and photosynaptic layers serve as the most critical components, with the former specializing in light reception and the latter mediating information storage and interlayer communication3. Photoreceptor cells rapidly capture light stimuli and convert them into electrical signals via photosensitive protein channel, which precisely regulate ion transport, inducing membrane hyperpolarization4 (Fig. 1b). The resulting light-dependent electrical signals propagate via synaptic connections to downstream retinal neurons, enabling visual information processing5 (Fig. 1c). Inspired by biological vision systems, researchers have developed solid-state optoelectronic devices that leverage semiconductor-based electron-hole separation mechanisms to emulate both photoreceptor sensing and synaptic preprocessing functions6–11. However, the mechanisms behind how light triggers localized interactions between electrons, holes, and ions at interfaces are still not fully understood. Over the past 5 years, advances in nanofluidic memristors have exploited nonlinear ion transport through spatially confined systems in nano- and sub-nano channels and membranes to emulate biological synaptic information processing, enabling brain-like computation through electrical-to-ionic and ionic-to-electrical signal transduction mechanisms12–16. Meanwhile, recent advances in optically controlled ion transport based on ion-conductive hydrogels and nanofluidic channel materials have partially achieved bioinspired visual functionalities17,18. However, current research primarily focuses on biological photosynaptic functions, which are limited by slow light-response speeds, thus failing to replicate the rapid detection of high-frequency visual signals characteristic of photoreceptors. Given these limitations, creating a bioinspired visual system within a single nanofluidic device that integrates photoreceptor-like optical sensing, photosynaptic processing, and polarity-switching is still a major challenge.

Herein, we present a light-controlled nanofluidic iontronic device based on a carbon nanotube and molybdenum disulfide (CNT/MoS2) heterostructure that emulates biological visual functions through a sequential “light stimuli-electrical signal-electron/ion coupling-ionic signal” transduction process (Fig. 1d). This platform achieves dual photoreceptor- and photosynaptic-like functionalities within a single device via bias voltage-controlled modulation of photoresponse dynamics. In a two-terminal model without bias voltage, the device replicates photoreceptor cell operations with a rapid photocurrent responsive speed and responsive specificity of light power density and wavelength. In a three-terminal configuration with bias voltage, the device demonstrates photosynaptic characteristics, including short-term plasticity, long-term plasticity, and experience-dependent learning behaviors. The mechanism arises from the coupled effect between bias voltage and photogenerated potential (Ephoto) across the heterostructure nanotube (Fig. 1d), which together precisely control ion transport across the CNT/MoS2 nanotube. This is facilitated by the one-dimensional coaxial architecture, which establishes a continuous and enlarged heterointerface that not only enhances photogenerated charge separation and strengthens the built-in electric field, but also gives rise to a heterointerface-induced asymmetric photovoltage. This photovoltage directly drives ion transport in a manner distinct from conventional photothermal or surface-charge modulation schemes19–21. The resulting ion photocurrent reaches 526 nA, exceeding the performance of state-of-the-art nanofluidic opto-ionic systems based on established material platforms like metal organic framework (MOF)17, covalent organic frameworks (COFs)22, two-dimensional transition metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes)19, and layered graphene oxide (GO)23,24. Furthermore, this iontronic device realizes accurate orientation recognition and robust fingerprint detection across a wide range of incident light angles, highlighting its potential for biointerfaced artificial retina.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of nanotube heterostructure

The CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructure was fabricated via a two-step synthesis process. Initially, CNT was synthesized using a porous anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) membrane template by chemical vapor deposition (CVD). Subsequently, (NH4)2MoS4 was thermally decomposed within the CNT to form MoS2 nanotubes. Structural characterization using scanning electron microscope (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) confirmed the CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructure with a pore diameter of ~90 nm (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1). The MoS2 layer and CNT layer exhibited a thickness of ~5.9 and ~11.6 nm, respectively. A well-defined lattice fringe spacing of 0.623 nm was observed, corresponding to the (002) interplanar distance of MoS225. In comparison, MoS2 nanotubes with a small pore diameter of ~35 nm was characterized using SEM (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, the resulting heterostructure nanotubes were also systematically characterized using Raman spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements. Mechanism verification of the nanofluidic iontronic device

To investigate the light-induced interfacial band bending and charge separation mechanisms in the CNT/MoS2 heterostructure, we performed first-principles density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional with empirical van der Waals correction by D3(BJ) scheme26,27(Supplementary Fig. 6a). Ground-state electrostatic potential difference analysis reveals spontaneous charge redistribution at the CNT/MoS2 heterointerface, in which electron depletion was observed on the outer surfaces of the MoS2 nanotubes, while electron accumulation occurred on the inner side of the CNT (Fig. 2b). This indicates that the MoS2 nanotubes act as electron donors, transferring electrons to the inner surface of the adjacent CNT. This interfacial charge redistribution establishes a built-in potential at the heterostructure interface, which can effectively separate photogenerated electron-hole pairs upon photoexcitation, reducing recombination probability and enhancing photoelectric conversion efficiency. As shown in Fig. 2c, in the excited states, the hole-electron analysis shows that electrons are predominantly localized on the CNT. In contrast, the holes remain concentrated on the Mo atoms of the MoS2 nanotubes. This directional electron transfer from the photoexcited MoS2 phase to the conductive CNT matrix is driven by the inherent band bending at the interface, which is consistent with our experimental findings.

To directly probe the Ephoto arising from charge separation, we employed Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) to quantitatively track the evolution of contact potential differences (CPD) and surface photovoltage (SPV) of CNT/MoS2 heterostructure nanotubes28. KPFM measurements revealed reversible CPD modulation that precisely tracked the optical switching sequence, with distinct on/off states confirming light-controlled interfacial potential generation (Fig. 2d). The SPV demonstrated a linear amplification from 3.8 to 7.7 mV across 0.1–0.5 mW cm−2 illumination intensity (Fig. 2e). These results provide direct experimental evidence of Ephoto generation through efficient electron-hole separation at the CNT/MoS2 heterointerface (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8).

A theoretical model of finite element method (FEM) simulations developed by Poisson–Nernst–Planck (PNP) equations to further validate the proposed mechanism of electrophoretically modulated ion transport mediated by Ephoto in the heterostructured nanotubes (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Under unilateral illumination, the CNT/MoS2 heterostructure nanotubes generated an asymmetric electric field through Ephoto establishment (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 9), which can drive continuous ion migration throughout the CNT/MoS2 heterostructure nanotubes, generating sustained ion photocurrent. As shown in Fig. 2g, the interfacial regions exhibit enhanced current densities due to the convergence of the ion transport pathway. Furthermore, a pronounced concentration polarization phenomenon induced by ion accumulation was observed at the illuminated interface of the nanotube heterostructure (Supplementary Fig. 9). This region showed significant enrichment of K⁺, with their concentration substantially exceeding that of Cl⁻ (Fig. 2h). This asymmetric spatial distribution of ions generates a measurable photovoltage (Supplementary Fig. 10). Both theoretical simulations and experimental results confirm that the ion selectivity originates from the heterostructure’s inherent negative surface charge, as demonstrated by zeta-potential characterizations and drift diffusion experiments29–31 (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12), which facilitates preferential electrostatic attracting cations while repelling anions.

Photosynaptic function of the nanofluidic iontronic device

Building upon the nanofluidic iontronic photoreceptor capabilities, we implemented synergistic optoelectrical modulation to emulate photosynaptic behavior. A three-terminal ionic phototransistor was constructed, consisting of a pair of Ag/AgCl electrodes immersed in two electrolyte reservoirs (Fig. 4a). This configuration enabled simultaneous (i) external bias voltage application and (ii) monitoring of photoinduced ion currents through the CNT/MoS2 nanotube under pulsed illumination. Different from photoreceptor characteristics, the photoinduced ion currents exhibited persistent temporal response characteristics under 0.5 V bias potential, similar to biological photosynaptic behavior (Fig. 4b, c). Meanwhile, the peak ion current demonstrated power density-dependent gain from 1.5 to 6.1 µA under an illumination intensity range of 10–94 mW cm−2 (Fig. 4b), and voltage-dependent gain from 1.0 to 15.7 µA over a bias voltage range of 0.1–0.9 V (Fig. 4c). We systematically analyzed the relationship between excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC) dynamics and optical pulse stimulation in modulating short-term plasticity (STP) of the photosynaptic device. When stimulated by two consecutive optical pulses at 0.1 V bias, the device exhibited paired-pulse facilitation (PPF), which is recognized as a hallmark of short-term synaptic plasticity, with the second pulse generating significantly higher EPSC amplitude (A2) than the first (A1) (Fig. 4d). The facilitation magnitude was quantified by the PPF index = (A2 − A1)/A1 × 100%, which follows a biexponential decay with increasing interpulse intervals17. This dynamic behavior closely mimics the short-term plasticity characteristics observed in biological photosynaptic systems13 (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, long-term plasticity (LTP), which converts transient photosynaptic events into persistent memory traces through repeated activation, was experimentally modeled using sequential optical pulse trains35. The spike-number-dependent plasticity emerges with increasing pulse counts (10 to 80), exhibiting both progressive EPSC amplitude enhancement and prolonged decay kinetics (Fig. 4f).

The transition from STP to LTP can be mediated through pulse-duration optimization (spike-duration-dependent photosynaptic plasticity) (Fig. 4g). Short optical pulses of 1 s induced rapid EPSC responses that decayed to baseline. While prolonged optical stimulation from 1 to 20 s resulted in progressive augmentation of EPSC amplitude with extended signal attenuation, signifying the LTP establishment. This transition mechanism can be mechanistically attributed to optical stimulation-induced nonlinear ion carrier accumulation. Meanwhile, Fig. 4h systematically characterizes the pulse-frequency-dependent facilitation elicited by sequential photostimulation across a physiological frequency range, which reveals a progressive enhancement of incremental facilitation effects of EPSC amplitudes as the stimulation frequency increases from 0.05 to 1.00 Hz. To emulate synaptic learning-memory functions, we implemented three consecutive training cycles of optical pulse stimulation (Fig. 4i). Each protocol mimicked repetitive cognitive learning, demonstrating progressive efficiency gains. The initial learning phase necessitated 40 training cycles to achieve full proficiency (100%), followed by a reduced demand of 20 cycles in the second learning session, and further decreased to merely ten cycles in the tertiary learning phase following an equivalent forgetting period. The progressive reduction in training cycles across successive sessions demonstrates enhanced learning efficiency through periodic memory consolidation, similar to biological memory consolidation processes36.

Applications of the nanofluidic iontronic device

Orientation recognition represents a fundamental visual function essential for organizing complex spatiotemporal information37,38. Notably, spatiotemporal correlation within the receptive fields of orientation-selective ganglion cells enables the initial neural computation of moving object trajectories39. Drawing inspiration from orientation-selective ganglion cells, we developed a bioinspired orientation recognition system based on our light-regulated nanofluidic iontronic device, implemented through an artificial neural network (ANN) architecture (Fig. 5a). Here, three optoelectrically modulated nanofluidic devices were arranged at 120° intervals on a circular platform, forming an orientation-sensitive detection array. The three devices, operated at distinct bias voltages (Device#0.1 V, Device#0.3 V, and Device#0.5 V), exhibited characteristic EPSC response profiles to angular illumination. This differential response enables reliable orientation recognition. The dependence of EPSC on bias voltage and illumination power density was systematically investigated across orientation angles. EPSC results exhibited distinct synaptic weight modulation as a function of orientation angles (Fig. 5b). Within polar coordinates spanning 0°–360° (in 30° increments), orientation-induced EPSC demonstrated voltage-dependent amplification across a 0.1–0.5 V bias range (Fig. 5c). The synergistic integration of orientation-selective and illumination power density-dependent synaptic weight modulation in artificial vision systems enables multidimensional control of imaging information. By integrating our light-regulated nanofluidic iontronic device into an orientation-selective artificial vision system, we demonstrate enhanced extraction of contrast and spatial resolution from landscape images captured across multiple angles. Notably, images acquired at 90° exhibit superior contrast enhancement, confirming the exceptional performance of our orientation-selective, light-controlled nanofluidic iontronic device for high-resolution imaging applications (Fig. 5e). To identify complex spatiotemporal patterns, we developed a three-layer artificial neural network (ANN) architecture capable of omnidirectional orientation recognition (Fig. 5a). The input layer received flattened one-dimensional vectors representing the time-dependent currents from each device. The network architecture also incorporated a hidden layer with 16 neurons and an output layer comprising 12 neurons, corresponding to 12 distinct angular directions. To exploit the intrinsic memory properties of the devices, we constructed a specialized training dataset comprising orientation-dependent EPSC recordings over a 50-s post-illumination period. Remarkably, the model achieved a high recognition accuracy of 98.5% after only 40 training epochs (Fig. 5e). In contrast, the instantaneous pattern recognition system also achieves 98.8% recognition accuracy, together with response to light incident at various angles, confirming the device exhibits strong robustness in complex pattern recognition capability (Supplementary Fig. 19). The confusion matrix for the test dataset demonstrates exceptional classification accuracy across all angles, with the majority of samples predicted correctly, as evidenced by the strong concentration along the matrix diagonal (Fig. 5f). Moreover, the nanofluidic iontronic device enables high-fidelity extraction and recognition of complex visual features, such as fingerprint patterns via convolutional neural networks (CNNs) employing single fully connected hidden layer architectures for classification (Supplementary Fig. 20a). The 90° image demonstrates optimal contrast and achieves the highest recognition accuracy, attaining 96.13% (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 20b). The system maintained recognition accuracy (>95%) across multiple angles (0°–330°) after 10 epochs, demonstrating high-fidelity feature extraction and reliable detection efficiency for practical imaging applications (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 20c).

Discussion

In summary, we have developed an artificial nanofluidic iontronic device that successfully emulates key biological visual functions, including photoreception and photosynaptic processing. The photoresponsive functionality of the nanofluidic iontronic device arises from a sequential “light stimuli-electrical signal-electron/ion coupling-ionic signal” transduction mechanism, enabled by photogenerated electron-hole separation at the CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterointerface. Operating as a photoreceptor, it demonstrates rapid temporal response characteristics, effectively emulating biological optical sensing under zero-bias voltage conditions. When configured as an ionic phototransistor under applied bias voltage, the device demonstrates voltage-tunable persistent photoconductivity with weight-adjustable photosynaptic behavior. These performance metrics enable light signal processing in the artificial nanofluidic iontronic device, demonstrating robust performance in accurate orientation recognition and fingerprint detection under variable incident light angles. The artificial nanofluidic iontronic device, capable of integrated visual sensing and preprocessing, holds promising potential for machine vision or biointegrated vision.

Methods

Materials and reagents

Acetonitrile (ACN) was purchased from J&K Scientific China. Ammonium tetrathiomolybdate ((NH4)2MoS4), sulfur powder (S), and dimethyl formamide (DMF) were purchased from Aladdin, Shanghai, China. The chemicals include LiCl, KCl, NaCl, MgCl2·6H2O, and CaCl2·2H2O were purchased from Aladdin, Shanghai, China. All aqueous solutions were prepared with Milli-Q water (18.25 MΩ·cm−1). The AAO membranes with a thickness of 60 μm, and a pore size of ~50 and ~100 nm were purchased from Puyuan Nano, China.

Fabrication of CNT

The CNTs were synthesized through a CVD equipment (BTF-1200 C-Ⅱ, AnHui BEQ Equipment Technology Co., Ltd., China) employing AAO membranes as nanostructured templates and ACN as the carbon feedstock. The fabrication protocol was implemented in a horizontal tube furnace configured at atmospheric pressure. Prior to growth initiation, the AAO substrate underwent thermal pretreatment at 1090 °C for 30 min under a flowing gas mixture (Ar: 100 sccm, H2: 50 sccm) to facilitate in situ reduction and surface activation. The CNT growth was subsequently conducted by introducing ACN vapor through a carrier gas mixture stream at the optimized synthesis temperature for 20 min. Post-synthesis cooling to ambient temperature was performed under continuous argon purge (100 sccm) to prevent oxidative degradation, yielding vertically aligned CNT arrays within the AAO template architecture.

Fabrication of MoS2 nanotubes

The synthesis of MoS2 nanotubes was achieved through the AAO template-assisted CVD approach. Initially, a precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 0.05 g of (NH4)2MoS4 in 5 mL of DMF under continuous magnetic stirring. The AAO templates were subjected to vacuum-assisted infiltration with the precursor solution, followed by thermal treatment in a convection oven at 80 °C for complete solvent evaporation. Subsequently, the dried AAO template was positioned in a quartz boat at the central heating zone of a horizontal tube furnace under atmospheric pressure, while 0.2 g S was placed in a separate quartz boat 20 cm upstream from the furnace center to prevent premature S evaporation prior to thermal processing. The system was rapidly ramped to 800 °C with a mixed carrier gas (Ar: 100 sccm, H2: 50 sccm) throughout the thermal treatment. Upon reaching the target temperature, S powder moved quickly towards the tube furnace through an externally controlled magnetic manipulation system that translated the sulfur-containing boat into the heating zone, which was maintained at 800 °C for 60 min to obtain MoS2 nanotubes.

Fabrication of CNT/MoS2 nanotubes

The synthesis process of CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterojunctions essentially combines the aforementioned two-step fabrication methodology. Briefly stated, the pre-synthesized CNTs are initially impregnated with (NH4)2MoS4 precursor solution through vacuum-assisted filtration, followed by drying. Subsequently, the dried CNT was positioned in a quartz boat at the central heating zone of a horizontal tube furnace, while 0.2 g S was placed in a separate quartz boat 20 cm upstream from the furnace center. The system was rapidly ramped to 800 °C with a mixed carrier gas (Ar: 100 sccm, H2: 50 sccm). Upon reaching the target temperature, S powder moved quickly towards the tube furnace, which was maintained at 800 °C for 60 min to obtain CNT/MoS2 nanotube.

Fabrication and measurement of devices

The fabricated MoS2 and CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructure membrane-based devices were initially encapsulated using a perforated polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold. Subsequently, these packaged devices were mounted between a custom-designed electrochemical cell architecture with a fixed transparent area, featuring dual symmetric reservoirs. Both compartments were filled with equal volumes of electrolyte solution. Photogeneration was induced through lateral-side illumination using a xenon lamp (CEL-S500-T5, Beiiing China Education Au-light Co., Ltd., China) or monochromatic laser (CEL-LED100HA, Beiiing China Education Au-light Co., Ltd., China) wavelength (365, 405, 520, 590, and 660 nm). A bipotentiostat configuration was implemented using Ag/AgCl electrodes immersed in the reservoirs for transmembrane current signal acquisition. Photocurrent was measured using a Keithley 6487 picoammeter (Keithley Instruments, Cleveland, OH).

KPFM measurements

Surface photovoltage characterization was performed using a state-of-the-art KPFM integrated with an AFM system (Bruker Dimension Icon). The measurements were carried out using Pt/Ir-coated silicon probes (Bruker SCM-PIT series) with resonant frequencies between 60 and 100 kHz and spring constants between 1 and 5 N·m−1 in ambient conditions. To successfully reduce capacitive coupling artefacts between the probe and sample surface, the amplitude-modulated KPFM technique was applied with optimal parameters: an AC modulation voltage of 0.5 V and a dual-pass lift mode configuration using a minimum lift height of 20 nm. This configuration achieved exceptional energy resolution (<5 mV) in mapping CPD variations. Photogeneration was induced through front-side illumination using a xenon lamp with different incident power densities.

Quantum-mechanical simulation of CNT/MoS2 nanotubes

We systematically investigated the structural optimization and electronic excitation properties of CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructures through advanced materials modeling techniques. First-principles calculations were performed using the CP2K 2023.1 software package40 within the framework of DFT and TD-DFT using the PBE functional with empirical van der Waals correction by the D3(BJ) scheme27. The CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructures were constructed employing the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) package. The MoS2/CNT composite model consists of a MoS2 nanotube and a CNT. Both the MoS2 nanotube and the CNT were individually optimized under periodic boundary conditions. After constructing the nested composite structure (Supplementary Fig. 6, using a supercell whose length along the z-axis corresponds to the least common multiple of the minimal periodic units of both components), an additional geometry optimization was performed for the combined system. Structural optimizations and electronic band structure analyses were conducted using the PBE exchange-correlation functional, while long-range dispersion interactions were accounted for through the DFT-D3(BJ) empirical correction scheme27. The electronic wavefunctions were expanded using double-ζ DZVP-MOLOPT-SR basis sets in combination with Goedecker-Teter-Hutter (GTH) norm-conserving pseudopotentials. Given the substantial unit cell dimensions of the CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructures, Brillouin zone sampling was restricted to the Γ-point, with a plane-wave cutoff energy of 400 eV employed to ensure computational accuracy while maintaining reasonable computational efficiency. For post-processing analysis, electrostatic potential difference maps were generated through a subtractive methodology using the Multiwfn 3.8 (dev) wavefunction analysis program41. Specifically, the electrostatic potentials of isolated MoS2 and CNT fragments were subtracted from those of the coupled CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructures to visualize interfacial charge redistribution. Furthermore, hole-electron analysis was performed utilizing the same computational package to elucidate the fundamental mechanisms of interfacial charge transfer processes.

Material characterization

The morphological characteristics of CNT, MoS2, and CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructures were investigated using SEM (Hitachi SU8220) at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV. The structural and elemental composition were subsequently performed through TEM and EDX using a FEI Talos F200X system operating at 300 kV. Raman spectroscopic characterization was conducted on a Horiba Xplora Plus spectrometer with a 532 nm excitation laser source to analyze the vibrational modes of CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructures. Crystallographic properties were examined by XRD using a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) under operational parameters of 45 kV and 200 mA, collecting data in the 2θ range of 5–80°. Surface chemical states were analyzed through XPS measurements (PHI 5000 VersaProbe system) employing monochromatic Al Kα radiation, with binding energy calibration referenced to the adventitious carbon C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. The Zeta Potential of MoS2 and CNT/MoS2 nanotube heterostructure was measured by a Malvern Nano-ZS90 analyzer. Optical absorption properties were evaluated by ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV-Vis) recorded on a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer.
